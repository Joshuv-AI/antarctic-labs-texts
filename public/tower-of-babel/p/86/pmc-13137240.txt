A Biomimetic Micro‐Nano System Maximizing ‘Active Transport and Retention’ Effect in Solid Tumors
Authors: Kaiyun Yang, Jiajia Su, Alan J Davidson, Zimei Wu, Wenli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Active Transport and Retention (ATR) principle offers a new strategy to enhance tumor entry of nanodrugs via transcytosis. However, its application is limited by poor tumor‐homing, endothelial polarized efflux, and uncontrollable transcytosis of the nanodrug. Herein, we construct a bio‐mimetic micro‐nano system (PG@BAM‐LRC) comprising nanoliposomes (LRC) and berbamine (BAM) within platelet‐derived microcarrier (PG), leveraging PG's tumor‐homing ability and MMP‐9 responsive remodeling for tumor‐specific cargo release. Thereafter, BAM selectively promotes basal transendothelial transport of LRC toward the tumor by modulating apical recycling endosomes. While R8 promotes the cellular uptake, the arginine‐lysine‐lysine‐arginine‐cysteine (Cys) ligand facilitates Golgi‐targeted transendothelial transport of LRC, bypassing the endo‐lysosome pathway. Once inside tumor cells, furin‐mediated Cys‐cleavage halts transcytosis, yielding superior intracellular drug retention compared to the non‐cleavable Cys counterpart (LRC’). Four murine tumor models are established, demonstrating high heterogeneity in collagen density, vascularity, and EPR effects. An orthotopic pancreatic tumor, characterized by minimal EPR effect, is selected to demonstrate the ATR effect of PG@BAM‐LRC. PG@BAM‐LRC loaded with BAY‐872243 exhibits exceptional tumor accumulation and therapeutic outcome compared to LRC, PG@LRC without BAM, and PG@BAM‐LRC. Collectively, this study establishes PG@BAM‐LRC as a robust tumor‐targeting system leveraging the ATR mechanism while addressing tumor heterogeneity.Keywords: active transport and retention (ATR), biomimetic micro‐nano system, golgi apparatus, intracellular retention, oriented transendothelial transport

Introduction

In the past decades, nanodrugs have been extensively explored as ‘magic bullets’ in oncology by exploiting passive targeting transport through the ‘Enhanced Permeability and Retention’ (EPR) effect [1]. However, mounting evidence shows that the EPR effect is inconsistent across tumor types, and the clinical translation of nanodrugs has fallen short of expectations, only offering modest reductions in adverse effects [2, 3]. Recently, the ‘Active Transport and Retention’ (ATR) concept has emerged as a promising strategy, leveraging active endothelial transcytosis to facilitate the delivery across the impermeable blood‐tumor barrier, as demonstrated in various inorganic nanocarriers [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Despite promising preclinical outcomes, the ATR strategy remains in its infancy, with significant challenges yet to be addressed [9]. First, the poor tumor‐homing property of nanodrugs limits their interaction with tumors. Second, while basolateral transendothelial transport into the tumor across the tumoral vascular endothelial barrier is desirable, apical exocytosis that returns nanodrugs back to the bloodstream remains unavoidable [10]. Thirdly, once inside the tumor, excessive and undifferentiated transcytosis across multiple tumor cells reduces the local drug retention and cellular bioavailability, thus limiting the therapeutic outcome [11, 12]. Additionally, large endothelial gaps in some normal organs, such as the liver and spleen, lead to sequestration of nanodrugs and thus inevitable toxicity [13]. Artificial nanocarriers, such as liposomes, are also prone to phagocytosis by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) [14]. Moreover, heterogeneous tumor microenvironments (TME) pose additional uncertainties [15], further complicating the clinical translation of ATR strategies.

To address these challenges, in this study, we propose a biomimetic micro‐nano platform PG@BAM‐LRC in which nanoliposomes (LRC) and berbamine (BAM) are co‐loaded within the platelet membrane (also known as platelet ghost; PG) to serve as a tumor‐homing camouflage (Figure 1a). Like platelets, PGs (1–2 µm) have high expression of CD47, a ‘don't eat me’ signal, and are less likely to extravasate into normal tissues [16] or be cleared by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) [17]. Instead, PG naturally accumulates at sites of vascular injury and inflammation in tumors, facilitated by interaction between P‐selectin and CD44 [18] (Figure 1b). Furthermore, matrix metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP‐9) is overexpressed in tumors [19, 20], which could cleave a variety of substrates on platelets at metalloproteinase‐sensitive cleavage sites [21]. Consequently, PG is proposed to undergo MMP‐9–responsive deformation [21], enabling rapid release of LRC and BAM in the tumor (Figure 1c).

LRC are functionalized with a cell‐penetrating peptide (CPP) octaarginine (R8), alongside a Golgi‐targeting cysteine ligand (Cys) [22]. R8 promotes the uptake by endothelial cells [23] while Cys directs intracellular transport via the endosome‐to‐Golgi pathway for effective exocytosis [24], bypassing the endo‐lysosome degradation. Moreover, BAM, a bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid with anticancer property [25], inhibits the apical exocytosis of LRC back to blood by modulating Rab8, a GTPase associated with apical recycling endosomes [26], facilitating the transendothelial transport to the tumor side (Figure 1c,d).

Once cross the endothelial barrier, it is crucial to terminate further transcytosis of nanodrugs to ensure their retention in tumor cells. Furin, a protease ubiquitously expressed in the Golgi apparatus and often upregulated in cancer cells, cleaves substrates at their polybasic motifs, such as R‐X‐R [27]. Thus, a tumor‐specific Cys cleavage mechanism is designed by introducing arginine‐lysine‐lysine‐arginine‐cystine motif (RKKR‐Cys) to the terminal end of polyethylene (PEG) on phospholipids. This design enables furin‐mediated Cys cleavage to prevent excessive intra‐tumoral transcytosis of LRC. Potential endosome entrapment is inhibited by protonation of the remaining cell‐penetrating motif RKKR and R8 on liposomes at acidic endosomal pH [28], promoting the liposome disruption and efficient cargo release to exert effects (Figure 1c,e).

Next, we performed in vitro and in vivo proof‐of‐principle studies to critically validate the PG@BAM‐LRC system and its potential to leverage the ‘ATR’ effect for enhancing tumor drug delivery. BAY‐872243 (BAY), a potent and selective hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1) inhibitor [29], was used as a model drug for the efficacy study. Results from a Transwell model illustrated BAM‐oriented transcytosis of nearly all LRC across the bEnd3 endothelial cell monolayer. In contrast, the resulting transcytosed LRC achieved nearly full retention in downstream 4T1 breast tumor cells. Tumor penetration and antitumor effect were further assessed in a 3‐D tumor spheroid consisting of 4T1 tumor cells co‐cultured with 3T3 fibroblasts. The biodistribution of PG@BAM‐LRC and control formulations was compared in four breast and pancreatic tumor models, including both subcutaneous and orthotopic types, validated to exhibit different vascular permeabilities and microenvironments as well as EPR effects. Lastly, the orthotopic PANC‐1 pancreatic tumor, which displays minimal EPR effect, was selected to assess the ATR effect of PG@BAM‐LRC versus control formulations to elucidate the mechanisms. PG@BAM‐LRC elicited the most potent therapeutic response while showing minimal accumulation in normal tissues.

Results

Characteristics of Liposomal Nanocarriers

To engineer liposomes with various ligands, DSPE‐PEG‐R8, DSPE‐PEG‐RKKR‐Cys (Cys‐cleavable), and DSPE‐PEG‐Cys (uncleavable Cys) were synthesized, and their structures were confirmed by 1H‐NMR (Figure 2a).

Putative peak assignments are p1: DSPE, p2: PEG, p3: R8, p4: RKKRCys, p5: Cys, p6: DMSO, p7: H2O. (b) Liposomal size, PDI, and zeta potential. (c) Morphology observed by Cryo‐TEM (scale bar: 100 nm). Four liposomes functionalized with different ligand(s), LR, LC, LRC, and LRC’ were constructed with a backbone consisting of SPC, DPPC, and cholesterol at a 6:3:1 molar ratio. The addition of bile salt sodium cholate at a weight ratio of 4:10 relative to SPC facilitated the formation of small‐sized liposomes [30]. LR and LC (Cys‐cleavable) were modified with DSPE‐PEG‐R8 and DSPE‐PEG‐RKKR‐Cys (5% mol), respectively. Dual functionalized LRC were engineered with R8 and RKKR‐Cys at a 3:1 molar ratio (5% mol in total). LRC' were prepared by replacing DSPE‐PEG‐RKKR‐Cys with DSPE‐PEG‐Cys(Cys‐uncleavable) as a control liposome formulation.

All liposomes prepared with the thin‐film hydration method were approximately 90 nm in size with a PDI of ∼0.2 and a near‐neutral surface charge (1–5 mV). Cryo‐TEM confirmed that they were mostly spherical, resembling small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) (Figure 2b, c). Encapsulation efficacy (EE) and drug loading (DL) of BAY were consistent across formulations, achieving nearly 80% and 1.6% (w/w), respectively (Figure 2d). The comparable physical characteristics of liposomes allowed for fair comparison of the intrinsic effects of the ligands on their in vitro and in vivo properties.

Ligand Effects on Cell Entry and Transcytosis of LRC

The cellular uptake of R8 functionalized liposomes, including LR, LRC, and LRC’ by bEnd3 endothelial cells over 2 h was 1.5‐fold higher than that for LC as measured by intracellular Nile Red (µg/104 cells), confirming the role of R8 in promoting the cellular uptake (Figure 3b). The same cellular uptake trend was observed in 4T1 tumor cells for both the original liposomes (Figure 3c), as well as for the transcytosed liposomes that had crossed the endothelial monolayer, including LCt, LRC’ t, and LRCt (Figure 3d).

We further evaluated liposome distribution in these Transwell models following a 2‐h exocytosis phase (Figure 3a, Step 2). Notably, 97% of LR remained within endothelial cells, with only 3% being expelled into the upper chamber (blood side). In contrast, merely 20% of LC, LRC, or LRC’ were retained in endothelial cells while over 70% being exocytosed, primarily into the upper chamber, likely due to Cys‐mediated exocytosis (Figure 3e). A similar phenomenon was observed in tumor cells treated with original liposomes (Figure 3f). Notably, transcytosed liposomes that had crossed endothelial cells exhibited a distinct distribution pattern in tumor cells (Figure 3g); While LRC’ t with un‐cleavable Cys exhibited only 23% cellular retention, LRCt with cleavable Cys achieved 80% retention. Moreover, removal of R8 from liposomes (LCt) reduced retention to 55%. The results underscore the critical roles of both the cleavage of Cys and R8 in promoting tumor cell retention.

Role of BAM in Directing Basal Transport of LRC Across Polarized Endothelial Monolayer

At the corresponding dose, BAM did not affect the uptake of LRC by endothelial cells, or the uptake of LRC and LRCt by tumor cells (Figure 3b–d). However, BAM dramatically altered the direction of LRC transcytosis across the endothelial cell monolayer, resulting in apical–to‐basal polarized transport with the majority of liposomes detected in the lower chamber (tumor side) (Figure 3e). In contrast, in the absence of BAM, LRC were predominantly expelled back into the upper chamber, 5 times more than into the lower chamber. In comparison, in the non‐polarized 4T1 Transwell model, BAM barely affected the distribution of either LRC or LRCt in three compartments (Figure 3f, g).

Intracellular Trafficking in Endothelial versus Tumor Cell Lines

The intracellular trafficking of Nile red‐labelled liposomes was investigated via colocalization analysis of confocal images. To verify the liposome integrity, the lipid membrane was co‐labelled with Cy5 and Nile red. The colocalization degree was determined by the Pearson correlation coefficient, ranging from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating random colocalization and 1 indicating perfect colocalization [31].

In endothelial cells, Nile red in LR initially exhibited high colocalization with early endosomes (EE), which declined over time. This was accompanied by low colocalization with the Golgi apparatus and recycling endosomes (RE), and a gradual increase in colocalization with lysosomes (Figure 4a, b; Figure S1, Supporting Information). In contrast, Cys‐modified LC and LRC (with or without BAM) initially showed low colocalization with EE, followed by substantially increased co‐localization with Golgi and RE over time, markedly higher than that observed for LR. Moreover, a rapid decrease over time in colocalization between Nile red and Cy5 implicated degradation of LR. In contrast, a stable correlation (close to 1) was observed in LC and LRC, suggesting their structural integrity.

Each original liposome showed a similar intracellular trafficking pattern in tumor cells as it was observed in endothelial cells (Figure 4c, d; Figure S2). Conversely, after transport across endothelial cells, LRCt (either with or without BAM) predominantly co‐localized with lysosomes, rather than Golgi and RE, likely due to the loss of Cys from the ligand, RKKR‐Cys. Meanwhile, rapid disassociation of LRCt was observed as indicated by reduced co‐localization between Nile red and Cy5, possibly due to lysosomal degradation.

Characteristics of Transcytosed Liposomes

We further characterized the liposomes collected after transcytosis through bEnd3 in Transwells.

Morphology

TEM revealed that transcytosed liposomes (LCt, LRC’ t, LRCt) all retained a spherical nanoscale morphology (Figure 5a). Consistent with our previous study [32], LRCt was partially incorporated in extracellular vesicles (EVs) while LCt and LRC’ t showed no association with EVs. LR primarily remained within endothelial cells; therefore, the so‐called LRt that appeared in the lower chamber is presumably large EVs (>200 nm).

Cys Cleavage in Endothelial Cells

Cys cleavage efficiency was determined by comparing the amount of Cys on liposomes before and after transcytosis across endothelial cells (LRCt, LRC’ t). Cys was detected via 5,5‐dithiobis‐2‐nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) assay based on quantification of free thiol groups (‐SH) on Cys. LRCt retained 23% Cys in contrast to 75% remaining on LRC’ t (Figure 5b).

Intracellular Drug Release

To predict BAY release of LRC and LRCt in their corresponding intracellular compartments, drug release was monitored at pH 6.5, representative of EE, Golgi, and RE before transcytosis, and pH 5.5 mimicking the endo‐lysosomal environment post‐transcytosis. Favorably, minimal release from LRC (∼20%) occurred over 12 h at pH 6.5, whereas ∼80% of BAY was released from LRCt within 4 h at pH 5.5 (Figure 5c).

Biocompatibility and Cytotoxicity

BAY‐loaded liposomes showed high compatibility with bEnd3 cells, maintaining >80% viability with up to 100 µm BAY even following 24 h exposure. Similarly, BAY showed low cytotoxicity to fibroblasts (Figures S3–S4). In contrast, both original and transcytosed liposomes containing BAY showed significant cytotoxicity to 4T1 tumor cells, and were more potent than free BAY (Figure 5d, e). Among original liposomes, LR showed the lowest IC50 (∼13 µm). Among transcytosed liposomes, LRCt showed significantly lower IC50 over LCt@BAY and LRC’ t@BAY (p < 0.001), implying the significance of R8 and cleavable Cys in prompting the cytotoxicity. Notably, compared to original LRC@BAY, transcytosed LRCt@BAY produced 2‐fold lower IC50, which was further reduced by the addition of BAM to around 5 µm.

All blank liposomes, either before or after transcytosis, showed high biocompatibility across three investigated cell types at the concentrations corresponding to the highest BAY‐dose tested (Figure S5).

In Vitro Evaluation of Tissue Penetration and Cytotoxicity in Tumor Spheroids

To validate the ATR effect, LRC was assessed against various control formulations using 3D tumor spheroids composed of 4T1 tumor cells cocultured with 3T3 fibroblasts at 1:3 ratio [33].

Confocal Z‐stack imaging was used to measure the penetration of Nile red‐labelled liposomes into tumor spheroids (Figure 6a), with penetration ability expressed as the ratio of fluorescent radius (r) to the total spheroid radius (R). The ligands exhibited a significant impact on liposome penetration. For liposomes without BAY, tumor penetration was ranked in the order of LRC’> LRC> LC. Transcytosed liposomes were also ranked as LRC’ t> LRCt> LCt (Figure 6b, c). Notably, the penetration of LRCt was improved when anticancer drug BAY was loaded in liposome (p< 0.05) due to the cell‐killing action. BAM at the tested concentration had a negligible effect on the penetration of LRCt@BAY, likely due to the non‐polarized nature of tumor cells and fibroblasts.

BAY‐loaded liposomes exhibited concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity to the tumor spheroids (Figure 6d). The IC50 of LRC@BAY was 3‐fold lower than that of LC@BAY, consistent with the findings that R8 enhanced the cellular uptake and spheroid penetration. Despite relatively low spheroid penetration validated on bank liposomes, IC50 of LRC@BAY was 2‐fold lower than that of LRC’@BAY (Figure 6e). This suggests that Cys cleavage during the early‐stage transcytosis facilitated endosomal escape in downstream cells, thereby promoting intracellular drug release and increasing cytotoxicity. The same trend was noted in transcytosed liposomes (LRCt@BAY< LRC’ t@BAY) but not sufficient to reach statistical significance (Figure 6f). Consistent with the penetration efficiency, BAM was less potent in promoting the cytotoxicity of LRCt@BAY.

Construction of a Platelet‐based Switchable Nanocarrier (PG@BAM‐LRC)

Optimization of LRC and BAM Loading Into PG

Platelet ghost (PG) was obtained following hypotonic elution, evidenced by the absence of dark granules observed in the platelets (Figure 7a). Cryo‐TEM of PG@BAM‐LRC confirmed that LRC was successfully incorporated into PG without compromising the integrity of PG or liposomes. Following the reported method [16], LRC (10 mg) was mixed with 106 PG suspended in 1 mL of hypotonic solution (0.5% NaCl), which induced PG swelling and increased membrane permeability [34]. With ultrasound, the EE of LRC reached 75% after 10 min. In contrast, the EE was only 25% in a 0.1 M PBS (isotonic) (Figure S6). This difference in EE may also be reflected in DiO labeling, as indicated by the slightly stronger DiO fluorescence signal in PG systems prepared with 0.5% NaCl. Nevertheless, the confocal microscope revealed strong Nile red intensity of LRC (Figure 7b), colocalized with DIO‐labelled PG (green). These results confirmed that hypotonicity facilitated LRC loading, likely via pores formed on PG membranes.

BAM was incubated with PG at a fixed incubation ratio of 50 µg per 106 PGs, while varying BAM concentrations (100, 250, and 500 µg mL− 1) were tested to determine their effects on EE and DL of BAM in PGs. Both EE and DL increased with BAM concentration (Figure 7c).

BAM is known to alter the rigidity of bio‐membrane [35]. This can be assessed by fluorescence polarization (FP), with higher FP values implying greater structure rigidity. Compared to the original PG membrane, FP decreased with increasing BAM loading (Figure 7d). To balance the PG stability and effective BAM dose, a DL of 3.4 µg/106 PG was selected in the final formulation. This was achieved by incubating PG with 250 µg mL−1 BAM at a ratio of 5 µg BAM per 106 PG.

Physicochemical Characteristics of PG@BAM‐LRC

DLS revealed that PG, PG‐LRC, and PG@BAM‐LRC were all negatively charged microcarriers with an average size of approximately 1.7 µm (Figure 7e). This indicates loading with BAM and LRC had minimal impact on the physicochemical properties of PG. Western blotting revealed that PG, PG‐LRC, and PG@BAM‐LRC exhibited comparable levels of CD47 and P‐selectin (CD62) to those of fresh platelets (Figure 7f).

MMP‐9 Responsiveness of PG@BAM‐LRC

In the presence of MMP‐9 (50 ng mL−1), which is in the reported concentration range in tumor (around 50–130 ng mL−1) [19, 20], 80% of LRC@BAY and 50% of BAM were released in 2 h (Figure 7g), more than twice the amount released from media lacking MMP‐9. Notably, liposomal LRC@BAY was more rapidly and completely released compared to BAM. Negligible release of free BAY suggested high integrity of LRC@BAY and PG@BAM‐LRC.

To further confirm the rationale for MMP‐9 responsive release, membrane fluidity of PG@ BAM‐LRC versus native platelets was compared in the presence of MMP‐9 (50 ng mL−1) using fluorescence polarization (FP). As shown in Figure 7h, PG@BAM‐LRC exhibited three‐fold lower FP value than native platelets (1.3 vs 3.3 × 106 mp), indicating higher membrane fluidity. Upon exposure to MMP‐9, the FP of PG@BAM‐LRC decreased markedly over time, whereas only a slight reduction was observed in platelets. This pronounced change in fluidity suggests enhanced membrane responsiveness of PGs, which is essential for cargo release at the tumor site. This MMP‐9 responsiveness is further verified by TEM (Figure 7i). PG@BAM‐LRC displayed apparent structural deformation following exposure to MMP‐9 (10–50 ng mL−1) for 2 h. A complete cargo release was further observed upon exposure to 50 ng mL−1 MMP‐9 for 4 h.

Pharmacokinetic Study of BAM and BAY in PG@BAM‐LRC@BAY

Prior to the pharmacokinetic study, HPLC methods for BAY and BAM analysis in plasma samples were developed and validated. First, the external standard curves for BAY and BAM were established and validated (Figure S7). The HPLC assay for BAY was linear (R2> 0.999), with over 93.6% extraction recovery of plasma samples across all concentrations (0.78, 3.13, and 12.5 µg mL−1). The HPLC method for BAM was linear (R2 >0.998) with highextraction recovery (>98%) from plasma samples at all tested concentrations (1, 20, and 100 µg mL−1) (Table S1).

Pharmacokinetics of BAY and BAM in various delivery systems were evaluated following i.v administration into rats at BAY (2 mg kg− 1), BAM (4 mg kg− 1), and 1.17 × 109 PG kg− 1 with an injection volume of 1.0 mL. The final PG@BAM‐LRC@BAY formulation contained 0.4 mg mL−1 BAY, 0.8 mg mL−1 BAM and 2.35 × 108 PG mL− 1. Compared to free BAY, LRC@BAY and PG@BAM‐LRC@BAY showed 2 fold longer half‐life, alongside higher AUC by 1.5 and 2.8 fold, respectively (Figure 8a, Tables S2–S3). Compared to BAM, PG@BAM and PG@BAM‐LRC@BAY achieved 2.7 fold longer half‐life and 2 fold higher AUC. The results proved that PG camouflaging effectively extended the systemic circulation and reduced the clearance of BAY and BAM from the bloodstream.

Selection of an Appropriate Tumor Model for Evaluating the ATR Effect

To identify a suitable model for evaluating the ATR effect of the macro‐nano system, four murine tumor models were developed and characterized, each displaying distinct vascular permeability and EPR effect. These include a subcutaneous breast tumor (small size), the medium and large orthotopic breast tumors alongside the orthotopic PANC‐1 pancreatic tumor. Tumor accumulation of nanoparticles was estimated at 5 h following i.v infusion of Evans Blue (EB) (Figure 8b). EB forms a nanocomplex with serum albumin, which mimics nanoparticle accumulation in tumors [6], a process driven by both EPR and ATR effects.

The pancreatic tumor and large breast tumor showed limited EB accumulation (0.3 and 0.6 µg g−1, respectively), corresponding to 0.01% and 0.1% of the administered dose (ID), respectively. In contrast, small (subcutaneous) breast tumors showed significantly high EB accumulation (13.3 µg g−1), followed by the medium breast tumors (10.8 µg g−1), corresponding to 0.37% and 0.67% of ID, respectively (Figure 8c, Table S4). These data demonstrated profound heterogeneity across tumors.

To further isolate the contribution of the EPR effect, EB‐albumin nanocomplexes were perfused in ‘zombie’ mice, in which active transcytosis (i.e., ATR) was deactivated while vascular architecture was preserved, allowing the passive extravasation via the EPR effect without interference of ATR [6] (Figure 8b). The contribution of the EPR effect was subsequently quantified by comparing EB accumulation in tumors of zombie mice versus that in the corresponding living tumor models. Moreover, the vascular density and extracellular matrix (ECM) component were characterized by measuring the vascular marker CD31 [36] and ECM collagen‐I [37], respectively.

Among all tested tumor types, pancreatic tumors showed the least EB accumulation, which was correlated to their dense ECM, the lowest CD31 expression, and the lowest EPR effect contribution (35.5%). In contrast, the medium‐sized breast tumor exhibited high EB accumulation, due to low ECM density, despite relatively low EPR effect contribution (36.3%) and low CD31 level (Figure 8c, Tables S4–S5). As breast tumors increased to larger sizes, EB accumulation was impeded by enriched ECM, even though the vascular density and EPR effect contribution were high (64.7%). Notably, small subcutaneous breast tumors—commonly used in preclinical nanomedicine studies—displayed the highest EB accumulation and EPR effect (62.6%), consistent with their low ECM content and high CD31 expression. Overall, high EB accumulation in tumors is strongly correlated with low ECM level [38], while a profound EPR effect is associated with rich vascularization. Based on its poor EPR effect and vascularization, the pancreatic tumor was selected to assess the ATR effect.

Heterogeneity of Biodistribution Among Various Tumor Models

The biodistribution of selected formulations, LRC, PG‐LRC, PG@BAM‐LRC, and PG@BAM‐LRC, was investigated in four tumor models, using Cy5‐labeled LRC to validate the contribution of each formulation element, including PG encapsulation, BAM incorporation, and potential modifications following transcytosis across endothelium.

Organ images collected at 24 h post‐injection are shown in Figure 8d. In the small subcutaneous breast tumors, which exhibited a high EPR effect of EB, LRC produced a stronger Cy5 signal than free Cy5. In contrast, in the medium‐sized breast tumor with low EPR effect, Cy5 intensity was comparable between free Cy5 and LRC. In the large breast tumors that showed low EB accumulation, a negligible Cy5 signal was observed for both free Cy5 and LRC, and PG coating showed no effect. In contrast, in pancreatic tumors, PG@BAM‐LRC significantly enhanced Cy5 intensity, reaching the highest level among all tested formulations (p<0.05). Despite a strong Cy5 signal in the liver and kidney, PG camouflage indeed reduced Cy5 levels in the spleen and lung in most investigated models (Figures S8–S10). In addition, in the most permeable small‐ and medium‐sized breast tumor models, PG@BAM‐LRC did not show an obvious increase in tumor accumulation compared to PG@BAM‐LRC'.

The biodistribution of PG@BAM‐LRC was further monitored using DIR‐labelled PG. DIR is a fluorescent lipophilic dye that could be integrated into the PG membrane. As shown in Figure 8e, DIR signal was more rapidly distributed in Balb/c mice bearing breast tumors than in C57 mice bearing pancreatic tumors. Moreover, DIR notably infiltrated the small breast tumor with high vascular density and low ECM level, in contrast to the marginal distribution observed in the other three tumor types, which showed either a less developed vascular network or a dense ECM.

Safety Profiles and Antitumor Effects Against PANC‐1 Pancreatic Tumors

Consistent with favorable in vivo distribution profiles, the safety and superior antitumor effect of PG@BAM‐LRC@BAY were demonstrated following i.v treatment in the PANC‐1 pancreatic tumor‐bearing mice (Figure 9a). The treatment doses were BAY (2 mg kg− 1), BAM (4 mg kg− 1), and 1.17 × 109 PG kg− 1 with an injection volume of 0.1 mL.

To assess the compatibility of endogenous platelets, plasma 5‐HT, an essential component in platelets [39], was monitored within 1 h post injection. No significant difference was noted across treatment groups and the saline control (Figure S11).

No abnormal behaviors were observed in the animals throughout the experiments. Moreover, no animal death or significant body weight loss was noted in any of the treatment groups compared to the saline control (Figure S12). Histological examination by H&E staining of heart, liver, spleen, and kidney, excised 15 days after treatment, revealed no evidence of pathological alterations (Figure 9b). Notably, pulmonary abscesses and alveolar lesions were detected in the saline group. The pathological changes were alleviated by BAY and LRC@BAY and further reduced with encapsulation of LRC@BAY in PG.

Antitumor effect of various formulations was assessed in PANC‐1‐Luc model on mice models by measuring tumor growth and changes in tumor luciferase intensity after treatment, and image of excised tumors at the study endpoint (15 days). Tumor suppression effect was ranked as BAY <LRC@BAY <PG@BAM‐LRC’@BAY <PG‐LRC@BAY (Figure 9c–e). The results are collectively attributed to the EPR and ATR effects of LRC, PG‐driven tumor homing, BAM facilitated transcytosis, as well as enhanced tumor retention via Cys cleavage.

Excited tumors were further histologically examined via TUNEL staining (Figure 9f), which detects DNA fragmentation in both apoptotic and necrotic cells. The saline group showed the most substantial TUNEL‐positive area (∼60%), reflecting spontaneous apoptosis and necrotic regions commonly found in hypoxic tumors [40]. In contrast, treatment with all formulations containing BAY markedly reduced the TUNEL‐positive area to 10–20%, indicating alleviation of necrotic cores.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the successful design and validation of a biomimetic micro‐nano delivery platform, PG@BAM‐LRC, which exploits the ATR mechanism to overcome critical barriers in tumor drug delivery. By integrating tumor‐homing platelet‐derived microcarrier (PG) with a dual‐functional nano‐liposome system (LRC) and berbamine (BAM), we achieve coordinated enhancement of tumor targeting, reduced off‐target distribution, efficient endothelial transcytosis, and intra‐tumoral retention.

PG Overcoming Poor Tumor‐Homing and off‐Target Sequestration

Platelet ghosts (PG) served as an effective biomimetic camouflage for LRC, exploiting natural platelet‐tumor interactions via P‐selectin/CD44 recognition and inflammatory site tropism. PG camouflage also reduced the RES clearance and thus extended the circulation by over 2 fold (Figure 8a), enabling long‐distance delivery to the tumor site.

Beyond tumor homing nature, PG minimized nonspecific liposome extravasation into healthy tissues, as evidenced by markedly reduced spleen and lung accumulation compared to non‐camouflaged formulations. However, hepatic and renal accumulation persisted, likely due to leakage of small LRC fragments (<50 nm) from PG, which were prone to uptake by Kupffer cells in the liver [41], and retention in the kidneys during circulation. This is supported by the in vitro release of LRC from PG even without MMP‐9, representative of a physiological condition (Figure 7g).

PG membrane deformation in response to MMP‐9 (50 ng mL−1) ensured the on‐site release of LRC and BAM in TME characterized by elevated MMP‐9 levels (50–130 ng mL−1) relative to clinical tumors [19, 20], enabling spatially controlled activation of the ATR cascade. The mechanisms for PG deformation involve MMP‐9‐induced shedding of GP Ib, a key determinant of platelet membrane rigidity [16], leading to payload release in tumors. It was found that metalloproteolytic release of glycocalicin might result in reduced GP Ib density, a key determinant of platelet membrane rigidity [21]. Reduced GP Ib density was reported to increase PG fluidity and thus cargo release [16], which is proposed to be associated with MMP‐9‐responsive release.

It is worth noting that a discrepancy between the in vitro release and in vivo pharmacokinetics of BAM and BAY (Figure 8a) was observed. MMP‐9‐responsive pore formation enables rapid release of LRC@BAY, while electrostatic interactions between positively charged BAM and the negatively charged PG membrane result in slower BAM release. In vivo, similar half‐lives of BAM and BAY (12 h vs. 11 h) were observed, reflecting the combined effects of release kinetics and systemic metabolism.

Ligands Ensuring Transendothelial Transport and Intra‐Tumoral Retention

Building upon previous work on intestinal endothelium [22], this study demonstrated efficient transcytosis of LRC across the tumor endothelial barrier. The R8 modification enhanced endothelial uptake, while the Golgi‐targeting Cys ligand guided LRC through the endosome‐to‐Golgi pathway, circumventing endo‐lysosomal degradation.

Interestingly, the collected transcytosed liposomes from the Transwells (LRCt, LRt) were found to be wrapped in a spherical structure >200 nm, most likely extracellular vesicles (EVs) (Figure 5a). This is consistent with previous reports where EVs are involved in the transcytosis of nanoparticles [32, 42]. This may also explain that LRCt achieved more efficient uptake by tumor cells (Figure 3 c,d) and penetration into 3D tumor spheroid (Figure 6 b,c), than the original LRC, possibly due to the facilitation by EV membrane. EVs play an important role in cell‐to‐cell communication and exhibit superior tissue penetration and cellular uptake [43].

A key novelty of our design is the tumor‐specific furin‐cleavable RKKR‐Cys motif, which halts transcytosis in tumor cells, capitalizing on elevated furin activity in tumor cells to cleave the Cys ligand and terminate Golgi‐mediated recycling. The resultant exposure of cationic residues (RKKR) as well as R8 at acidic endosomal pH further triggers liposome disruption and rapid release, enhancing cytosolic delivery. The dual‐trigger mechanism, spatial (tumor‐specific furin cleavage) and temporal (acidic endosome protonation), maximizes the retention and therapeutic payload availability. Compared to liposomes with non‐cleavable Cys (LRC’),LRC improved the tissue penetration and doubled the cytotoxicity of BAY‐containing liposomes on tumor spheroids (Figure 6e), demonstrating efficient ATR and a subsequent transcytosis‐retention switch.

BAM Facilitating Directional Transcytosis

BAM played a pivotal role in biasing endothelial transcytosis toward the basolateral direction (tumor‐facing) by inhibiting Rab8‐mediated apical recycling, addressing the efflux barrier for conventional nanocarriers where significant fractions are returned to the blood. In the endothelial barrier, apical recycling is reported to exceed the basal exocytosis by 3‐fold [44]. In this study, including BAM resulted in 2‐fold higher basal exocytosis relative to apical recycling in vitro. In murine tumor models, a combination of BAM (4 mg kg− 1) and ligand effects yielded efficient and preferential delivery across tumor vasculature, even in the tumor models with poor EPR effect, as shown in the orthotopic pancreatic tumor (Figure 8d). The BAM dose in the PG‐based system is well below its reported anticancer dose (60 mg kg− 1) [25].

Inter‐tumor Heterogeneity

Four preclinical tumor models were established, demonstrating high heterogeneity in collagen density, vascularity, and EPR effects through monitoring EB accumulation. Overall, validation across tumor models revealed widespread systemic distribution of EB nanoparticles, with <1% tumor accumulation even with a distinct EPR effect (Figure 8c). This is consistent with a previous report that only 0.7% of the injected nanodrug dose was delivered to tumors, and even less (0.0014%) managed to reach tumor cells [7]. These findings addressed the ongoing challenge of reducing off‐target effects for achieving efficient tumoral accumulation.

Of note, subcutaneous tumor models showed the highest EB nanoparticle accumulation (0.67% of ID at 5 h post‐injection), mainly achieved by EPR effect (62.6%), in contrast to the pancreatic tumor with only 0.01% of ID and low EPR effect (35%). These results demonstrate that tumor accumulation and EPR efficiency are both highly variable across tumor types, highlighting the importance of selecting appropriate models for clinical translation of nanodrugs. Compared to frequently used subcutaneous tumor models [3], orthotopic pancreatic tumors exhibited features particularly relevant to clinical tumors, characterized by poor vascularization, low EPR effect, and enriched peritumoral tissues against penetration [45].

PG@BAM‐LRC Addressing Inter‐tumor Heterogeneity via ATR

Conventional ATR strategies have faced uncertainty in heterogeneous TMEs where vascular permeability, stromal density, and ECM composition can differ markedly [15]. By testing across four tumor models with varying EPR characteristics, including a poorly vascularized, collagen‐rich, orthotopic pancreatic tumor, we demonstrated that PG@BAM‐LRC maintains superior delivery and therapeutic efficacy irrespective of low vascular leakiness. This suggests that an ATR‐based platform, when coupled with biomimetic targeting and directional transport control, can overcome limitations of EPR dependency and perform robustly across tumor types.

Despite this major proof of concept, our ex vivo biodistribution results indicate that, while ATR is clinically relevant and effective, the EPR effect remains the dominant pathway for nanodrug entry in highly permeable tumors, such as the small and medium‐sized 4T1 models. In these tumors, PG@BAM‐LRC showed comparable accumulation to LRC or PG@BAM‐LRC’ (Figure 8d), emphasizing that ATR effect provides benefit primarily in tumors with low vascular permeability, whereas the EPR effect remains the dominant mechanism in leaky tumors.

Therapeutic Implications

Consistent with the favorable biodistribution profile, the biomimetic micro‐nano system PG@ BAM‐LRC@BAY exhibited superior antitumor effect in low‐EPR pancreatic tumors, compared to free BAY, LRC@BAY, and PG@BAM‐LRC’@BAY, demonstrating successful implementation of the ATR strategy.

Loading BAY‐872243, a HIF‐1 inhibitor, into PG@BAM‐LRC yielded significant tumor suppression with minimal systemic toxicity in an orthotopic pancreatic tumor model. The observed therapeutic advantage is attributed to the synergistic interplay of improved tumoral localization, enhanced transendothelial trafficking, and sustained tumoral retention. Moreover, reduced accumulation in normal vascularized organs indicates a favorable safety margin, a major consideration in clinical translation of ATR‐based nanomedicines.

The model drug, BAY, has been validated with significant antitumor activity against several tumor types. However, following a Phase I clinical trial, the development was terminated due to dose‐limiting side effects in patients [46]. By enhancing tumor specificity and minimizing off‐target exposure, PG@BAM‐LRC enables the safe administration of higher, otherwise intolerable doses, further improving the therapeutic efficacy. At the dose tested, free BAY (2 mg kg− 1) produced only modest tumor suppression, whereas PG@BAM‐LRC treatment achieved a 4‐5 fold greater reduction in tumor size. BAY inhibits mitochondrial complex I and suppresses HIF‐1α accumulation; its therapeutic efficacy depends on efficient delivery into the hypoxic tumor core and cancer cells [29]. PG@BAM‐LRC enabled deep tumor penetration through transcytosis, effective remodeling of the hypoxic microenvironment, resulting in reduced necrotic fractions, as evidenced by the markedly decreased TUNEL‐positive area (Figure 9f).

It is worth noting that PG‐based systems preferentially accumulated in highly perfused organs such as the liver (Figure 8d, e). The liver sinusoidal endothelium is uniquely permeable and slow‐flowing, making it a natural site for platelet patrolling [47]. Importantly, this hepatic accumulation did not induce histological damage (Figure 9b). Furthermore, removal of internal granules from PG is expected to reduce the risk of acute coagulation reactions [16], further supporting the biosafety of the PG‐based delivery system.

Conclusion

This study underscores the pronounced heterogeneity of preclinical tumors and confirms that the ECM could be a major barrier to both EPR‐ and ATR‐mediated nanoparticle accumulation in solid tumors. Avascular pancreatic tumors, characterized by a dense ECM and minimal EPR effects, are selected to evaluate the ATR strategy using PG@BAM‐LRC. This biomimetic micro‐nano platform addresses key barriers in nanodrug delivery, including poor tumor homing, the blood‐tumor endothelial barrier, and limited cellular accumulation. By combining the tumor‐homing property of PG, directional transcytosis of BAM, and tumor‐specific retention via Cys cleavage, PG@BAM‐LRC effectively leveraged the ATR effect to enhance tumor drug delivery, even in tumors with low EPR effect.
