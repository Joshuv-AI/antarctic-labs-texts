Effect of paternal resveratrol intake on mitochondria and telomere length in mouse embryos and offspring
Authors: Noko TERAMOTO, Nanako KOBAYASHI, Kokoa MITSUI, Asuka KAMIO, Komei SHIRASUNA, Hisataka IWATA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced paternal age affects embryonic development and offspring phenotypes. We previously demonstrated that resveratrol prevents age-associated declines in the mitochondrial DNA copy number (mt-cn) and telomere length (TL) in embryos. The present study was performed to investigate the effects of paternal resveratrol intake on the mt-cn and TL in pups and embryos produced either via in vitro fertilization (IVF) or IVF using sperm preincubated in epididymal fluid (EF). C57BL/6N male mice were administered drinking water containing either the vehicle (ethanol, 1/2500) or 0.1 mM resveratrol. When these males were mated with young ICR females, paternal resveratrol treatment did not affect the mt-cn or TL in offspring derived from young fathers (16–25 weeks of age). In contrast, in offspring of aged fathers (41–51 weeks of age), the mt-cn and TL in heart tissue were altered in a sex-dependent manner. Moreover, continuous resveratrol administration to males until an advanced paternal age resulted in sperm TL elongation. However, resveratrol treatment did not affect the TL in embryos but significantly increased the mt-cn and reduced the lipid content in blastocysts produced via IVF using oocytes from young females (4 weeks of age). RNA–sequencing revealed that resveratrol treatment affected metabolic pathways, including lipid metabolism. The preincubation of sperm from young untreated males (11 weeks of age) in EF derived from resveratrol-treated males did not affect the mt-cn or TL in blastocysts but reduced the lipid content. In conclusion, paternal resveratrol intake modulates embryonic and offspring phenotypes, potentially through alterations in sperm TL, sperm epigenetic modifications, and bioactive components in EF. Resveratrol intake may exert long-term effects on offspring.Keywords: Embryos, Mitochondria, Paternal aging, Resveratrol, Telomere length

Aging is a decisive factor affecting fertility in mammals, and age-associated declines in pregnancy outcomes represent a major global societal challenge. Among the cellular hallmarks of aging, telomere length (TL) and mitochondrial function have been extensively studied. Numerous studies have demonstrated age-associated reductions in the TL and mitochondrial DNA copy number (mt-cn), particularly in oocytes [1,2,3].

One report indicating that paternal age at birth affects the offspring TL [4] suggests sperm-mediated paternal inheritance. In humans, paternal aging affects the sperm TL [5], and in mice, the sperm TL influences the embryonic TL [6]. In contrast, almost all mitochondria in early embryos are predominantly derived from oocytes, and limited studies have investigated the effects of paternal aging on mitochondria in embryos. A previous study demonstrated that paternal aging affects the mt-cn and TL in embryos as well as in the hearts of offspring [7]. Furthermore, RNA-seq analyses of blastocysts derived from the same father at young and advanced ages revealed differential gene expression patterns enriched in mitochondria-related pathways [8]. However, the mechanisms underlying these paternal age-associated changes in embryos remain largely unclear.

Several studies have investigated interventions that may attenuate aging-associated alterations in oocyte and sperm quality [9,10,11]. Resveratrol is a widely occurring natural phenolic compound and phytoalexin found in grapes, peanuts, cocoa, and some berries [12]. Moreover, its effects on mitochondria in oocytes and embryos have been demonstrated [13, 14]. Notably, a previous study reported that male mice administered resveratrol from 8 to 58 weeks of age produced embryos with an increased mt-cn and elongated TL after mating with young female mice [15]. These findings suggest that resveratrol exerts a protective effect against paternal aging-induced declines in the mt-cn and TL in embryos. In that study, several potential mechanisms were proposed, including the elongation of sperm TL, resveratrol-induced epigenetic modifications of the sperm genome, alterations in molecular components of sperm and seminal plasma, and changes in the uterine microenvironment.

In the present study, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the protective effects of resveratrol on paternal aging-associated embryonic changes. Specifically, we examined (i) the effect of resveratrol intake on the sperm TL and the TL and mt-cn in pups; (ii) the effect of resveratrol intake on the mt-cn and TL, as well as gene expression, in embryos derived from in vitro fertilization (IVF); and (iii) the effects of epididymal fluid (EF) from resveratrol-treated mice on the embryonic mt-cn and TL, following sperm preincubation in the EF.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Unless otherwise indicated, all chemicals used in the present study were purchased from Nacalai Tesque (Kyoto, Japan).

Medium

Human tubal fluid (HTF) medium was used for sperm and oocyte handling, as previously reported [16]. Potassium simplex-optimized medium containing amino acids (KSOM-aa) was used for embryo culture after fertilization [17].

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee for Laboratory Animals of Tokyo University of Agriculture (No.250067). C57BL/6N male mice (Japan SLC Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) were housed individually, and female ICR mice (Japan SLC Inc.) were housed in groups (four mice per cage). All mice had ad libitum access to food and water and were maintained at a controlled temperature of 23 ± 2°C with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Body weight was measured weekly, and the total amount of water consumption per 7 days was measured. Resveratrol was diluted in ethanol (0.25 M) and added to water at a final concentration of 0.1 mM (1/2,500). For the control group, ethanol was added to the water at 1/2500 (v/v). All animals were used for experiments after 1 week of acclimation.

Experimental design

As summarized in Fig. 1, control (n = 10) and resveratrol-treated (n = 10) male C57BL/6N mice (8 weeks of age) were provided with water containing the vehicle (ethanol) or resveratrol. During the young period (16–25 weeks of age), four males were randomly selected and mated with ICR female mice (8–15 weeks of age) to obtain offspring. Vaginal plugs were confirmed 0.5 days post coitum (dpc), and offspring were collected at 19.5–20.5 dpc. Within 12 h after birth, the litter size and birth weight were recorded, and the sex was determined based on an examination of the external genitalia (males were identified based on a longer anogenital distance, whereas females had a shorter anogenital distance). Heart tissue was then collected from the pups. The control and resveratrol-treated groups yielded 29/22 and 16/23 male/female offspring, respectively. After measurements, heart tissues were harvested and stored at −20°C until DNA extraction.

During the aged period (41–51 weeks of age), four males were randomly selected and mated with ICR female mice (8–17 weeks of age), and offspring were collected using the same procedures described previously herein. The control and resveratrol-treated groups yielded 28/16 and 30/25 male/female offspring, respectively. Males (48–51 weeks of age) were euthanized and subjected to sperm and EF collection (EF samples collected from control and resveratrol-treated males were referred to as EF–C and EF–R, respectively). The sperm was used for IVF or DNA extraction, and the EF was used for sperm pre-incubation. Second, three young males (11 weeks of age) were euthanized, and sperm were collected from the epididymis and used for pre-incubation in EF–R or EF–C, followed by IVF.

For IVF, female mice (4 weeks of age) housed from 3 weeks of age were used for superovulation.

Collection of oocytes

Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were collected from superovulated female mice. Superovulation was induced via the intraperitoneal administration of 10 IU CARD HyperOva, followed by the administration of 7.5 IU human chorionic gonadotropin 48 h later. Female mice were euthanized, and COCs were collected from the ampullae of the oviducts. Cumulus cells were removed via treatment with hyaluronidase (final concentration, 4.2 U/ml) in HTF medium, and oocytes were randomly divided into control and resveratrol groups.

Preparation of sperm suspension

Sperm was collected from the cauda epididymis by incising the tissue in a 500 µl drop of HTF medium and incubating it for 5 min to allow sperm to swim-out. The sperm suspension was used for IVF, EF collection, and the preparation of sperm pellets.

EF collection

A 400 µl aliquot of the sperm suspension was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to collect the sperm pellets. The supernatant was collected as HTF containing EF (EF–C or EF–R). The sperm pellets were used for DNA extraction. EFs from two males were equally mixed and stored at −80°C until use.

Sperm used for IVF

Motile sperm were carefully collected from the sperm suspension using a pipette and then transferred to 700 µl of fresh HTF medium and incubated for 1 h. Motile sperm accumulating at the edge of the drop were collected and added to IVF medium containing oocytes.

Preincubation of sperm with EF-C or EF-R from aged males

The sperm suspension of a young male was prepared as described previously herein, and the suspension was centrifuged at 800 × g for 3 min at 35°C. The sperm pellet was resuspended in stored EF-C or EF-R and incubated for 1 h prior to IVF. EF-C, and EF-R samples were randomly selected from five lots.

Embryo production

At 4.5 h after insemination, presumptive zygotes were cultured in 10 µl droplets of KSOM-aa for 72 h (4.5 dpc) at 37.0°C in an atmosphere of 5% O2, 5% CO2, and 90% N2. Blastocysts exhibiting a clear, typical blastocyst morphology (not hatched) were selected for subsequent experiments.

DNA extraction from embryos

Prior to DNA extraction, blastocysts were incubated with 1 µg/ml Hoechst 33342 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min at 37°C, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (IX71; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) to determine the total cell number per blastocyst. Each blastocyst was then transferred to 20 µl of DNA extraction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.4 mg/ml proteinase K, 0.9% Nonidet P-40, and 0.9% Tween-20) and lysed at 55°C for 30 min, followed by heat inactivation at 98°C for 10 min. The lysate was diluted with DNase-free water to a final volume of 70 µl and used for mt-cn and TL determinations.

DNA extraction from sperm

The sperm pellets obtained via centrifugation of a sperm suspension (described previously herein) were washed in PBS containing 0.1% polyvinyl alcohol. The sperm pellets were then resuspended in DNA extraction buffer. In the first step, samples were incubated for 30 min at 55°C to remove contaminated somatic cells. The sperm suspension was centrifuged to collect sperm pellets, which were resuspended in the second DNA extraction buffer containing 0.1 M dithiothreitol and incubated for 3 h at 55°C. DNA was purified using the MagExtractor-PCR & Gel Clean Up Kit (Cosmo Bio Co., Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and diluted tenfold with DNase-free water. The total number of sperm cells contained in the samples was determined via real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting a single-copy gene, described as follows.

DNA extraction from heart tissue

Offspring heart samples were crushed into a powder and incubated in DNA extraction buffer for 3 h at 55°C. DNA was purified using the MagExtractor-PCR & Gel Clean Up Kit (Cosmo Bio Co.) as described previously herein, followed by DNA concentration measurement. All DNA samples were adjusted to equal DNA content using DNase-free water. The number of cells contained in the samples was measured via RT-qPCR targeting a single-copy gene, described as follows.

PCR for single-copy gene

qPCR was conducted targeting the single-copy gene ACTB (Mus musculus strain C57BL/6N, NC_000071.7, 67 bp) using a primer pair (forward: 5ʹ-GATCGATGCCGGTGCTAAGA-3ʹ; reverse: 5ʹ-CCTTCTGACCCATTCCCACC-3ʹ). The PCR program was 95°C for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of 98°C for 8 sec and 60°C for 10 sec. Standard curves were generated using tenfold serial dilutions of standard plasmids into which PCR products had been cloned. Cloned products were confirmed via sequencing prior to use. DNA copy numbers were calculated based on the DNA concentration, Avogadro’s number, and the molecular weight of the plasmid.

Evaluation of mt-cn and TL

The mt-cn and TL were quantified via qPCR according to a previously described method [15]. For mt-cn quantification, primers (Mus musculus mitochondrion complete genome, NC_005089.1, 82 bp; forward: 5ʹ-ACCCACGATCAACTGAAGCA-3ʹ; reverse: 5ʹ-AGTACGATGGCCAGGAGGAT-3ʹ) were used. The TL was measured using telomere-specific primers (forward: 5ʹ-CGGTTTGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTGGGTTTGGGTT-3ʹ; reverse: 5ʹ-GGCTTGCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCTTACCCT-3ʹ). PCR conditions for mt-cn analysis were 95°C for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of 98°C for 8 sec and 60°C for 10 sec. PCR conditions for TL analysis were 95°C for 3 min, followed by 31 cycles of 98°C for 5 sec and 59°C for 60 sec. For the standard, PCR products cloned into a plasmid vector (for mt-cn) or synthetic TTAGGG repeats (15 repeats; for TL) were used. The calculation of the copy number was conducted as described previously herein. All measurements were performed in duplicate. Blastocyst data were normalized based on the total cell number determined and sperm and heart tissue data were normalized based on the total cell number determined via qPCR targeting the single-copy gene. Notably, measurements performed to compare control and resveratrol samples were conducted using one plate (96-well) to mitigate inter-plate variation. Therefore, all data were presented as one data set (control vs. resveratrol), with the value of the control group set at 1.0.

Assessment of the lipid content in embryos

Embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Nile Red (0.314 µM; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) for 10 min. After staining, they were mounted on glass slides, and an image of the equatorial plane was captured using a fluorescence microscope (SCR-038447; Dianova, Hamburg, Germany). The acquired images were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RNA-seq

Total RNA from blastocysts was extracted using the Quick-RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). RNA quality was determined using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent RNA 6000 Nano kit; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The average RNA integrity number of the RNA was 9.78 ± 0.06. Library preparation was conducted using the NEBNext Single Cell/Low Input RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB: #E6420L). The concentration of the cDNA libraries was determined using an Agilent TapeStation D1000 kit and a TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technology). The concentrations of the cDNA libraries were reassessed using a Kapa Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosystems). The multiplexed samples were sequenced based on single-read 100-bp reads using the NextSeq 1000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using P2 XLEAP-SBS Reagent Kit 100 Cycles (#20100987). Raw data were generated using bcl2fastq2 software (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Raw sequence processing, reference genome mapping, and differential gene expression analysis were performed using CLC Genomics Workbench ver. 23.0.2 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The remaining sequence data were aligned to the M musculus genome sequence (M musculus: GRCm39.115) to count the sequence reads. Gene expression values were evaluated as transcripts per million. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined using transcriptomic tools from the CLC Genomics Workbench with a threshold FDR Q value < 0.05.

Pathways based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enriched among DEGs were predicted using a functional annotation tool (DAVID, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), where M musculus was used as the background species. The fold-enrichment score was calculated using the geometric mean based on the –log scale of the member’s P value in the corresponding annotation. P values were calculated using Fisher’s exact test.

The data have been deposited with links to BioProject accession number PRJDB40322 in the DDBJ BioProject database.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, parametric data were analyzed by performing Student’s t-test and nonparametric data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Body weights of pups were analyzed via two-way ANOVA to determine the interaction between age and experimental groups. The developmental rate was analyzed based on a chi-square (χ2) test. Values less than 0.05 were considered as significant difference.

Results

Resveratrol intake by the father, from 8–51 weeks of age, did not affect their body weights (Supplementary Fig. 1) and water consumption (Supplementary Fig. 2). Moreover, the litter size did not differ between young and aged mice and between control and resveratrol groups (Fig. 1B). The weights of pups (g) did not differ between the control and resveratrol groups, whereas the body weights of pups sired by aged fathers were lower than those of pups sired by young males, regardless of the sex of the pups (Fig. 1C). In pups derived from a young father, resveratrol did not affect either the TL or mt-cn in the heart (Figs. 2A–D). However, in pups derived from an aged father, resveratrol reduced the mt-cn in the hearts of male pups (Fig. 2E), whereas it increased this in female pups (Fig. 2F). Further, resveratrol significantly elongated the TL in male pups (Fig. 2G) but not in female pups (Fig. 2H).

Discussion

A previous study showed that resveratrol intake in aged males, but not young males, increases the mt-cn and TL in in vivo fertilized embryos [15]. Here, we demonstrated that resveratrol intake in aged males, but not young males influences the mt-cn and TL in the heart cells of offspring. Furthermore, resveratrol intake affected the sperm TL, as well as the mt-cn and lipid contents, in in vitro fertilized embryos. Moreover, the preincubation of sperm with EF-R reduced the lipid content in embryos.

Resveratrol intake affects the mt-cn and TL in pups

A previous report showed that the age-associated decline in the mt-cn and TL in embryos can be attenuated by paternal resveratrol intake; however, resveratrol treatment had no effect during the young period [7]. In the present study, we demonstrate, for the first time, that paternal resveratrol intake does not affect the mt-cn or TL in the hearts of offspring at a young life stage, but significant effects were observed at an aged life stage. Notably, these effects were dependent on the sex of the offspring: changes in the TL were observed only in male pups, and differential changes in the mt-cn were detected between male and female pups. Taken together, these findings suggest that paternal resveratrol intake can influence embryonic characteristics; however, the resulting changes are likely modulated by the sex-specific biological background of the offspring. Alternatively, the resveratrol-induced effects may be mediated by multiple factors, including alterations in the sperm (e.g., TL and DNA methylation), sperm-associated bioactive molecules, and changes in the maternal reproductive tract induced by seminal plasma, which differentially affect pups.

Mitochondria in cardiomyocytes are highly responsive to environmental stimuli, particularly in a sex-dependent manner [18, 19]. This sex-specific differential response to stress may be attributed, for example, to greater mitochondrial biogenesis, enhanced mitochondrial quality control, and stronger antioxidative capacity in females [20, 21]. In addition, the TL differs between males and females [22, 23], and sex hormones such as testosterone and estradiol have been reported to influence the TL [24, 25]. Further studies are required to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of paternal resveratrol intake on the mt-cn and TL in pups. We next removed the maternal effects using IVF and examined the contribution of EF.

Possible causative factors of a differential mt-cn and lipid content in embryos

An advanced paternal age reduces the TL and mt-cn in mouse embryos [7]. In our previous study, however, we demonstrated that resveratrol intake by aged males increases SIRT1 expression in the liver and enhances antioxidative capacity in the plasma, thereby ameliorating the age-associated decline in the mt-cn and TL in embryos [15]. In the present study, embryos generated via IVF exhibited increased mt-cn and decreased lipid content. In contrast, embryos produced using sperm incubated in EF-R showed a reduction only in lipid content. These findings strongly suggest that the EF contains small bioactive molecules that are incorporated into sperm and subsequently influence lipid metabolism in embryos (discussed further in the Possible events in the epididymis section). Conversely, the genetic and epigenetic characteristics of sperm, including the TL and DNA modifications, may primarily affect mitochondrial function in embryos.

Notably, unlike the results of our previous report using in vivo-developed embryos [15], IVF-derived embryos in the present study did not exhibit differences in TL between the control and resveratrol intake groups. It has been reported that in vitro culture conditions shorten the TL in both embryos and offspring [26]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the IVF–in vitro culture conditions used in this study may have influenced the embryonic TL, potentially masking subtle differences between groups.

How sperm DNA or TL influences mitochondrial regulation in embryos

Aging has been reported to alter DNA methylation patterns in sperm [27, 28]; however, how such epigenetic changes translate into embryonic phenotypes remains unclear. Moreover, in the present study, we did not directly examine the effects of resveratrol intake on sperm DNA methylation or DNA methylation patterns in the resultant embryos. Numerous studies have demonstrated a close association between mitochondrial function and TL [29, 30]. In the present study, we show that aged male mice receiving resveratrol from a younger age exhibit a longer sperm TL compared with that in aged males not administered resveratrol. Previous studies using mice have reported that the sperm TL influences the embryonic TL [6]. In addition, Inoue et al. [31] reported that the treatment of embryos with a telomerase inhibitor shortened the TL and reduced the mitochondrial number in bovine embryos. Therefore, our results suggest that the observed increase in the mt-cn in embryos may be attributable, at least in part, to the longer TL of sperm derived from resveratrol-treated males. Previous reports showed that the shortened TL activates the p53–PGC1 signaling network to reduce mitochondria in oocytes [32], and bovine blastocysts treated with telomerase inhibitor exhibited a shortened TL and low expression levels of PPARGC1A [31]. However, whether a long sperm TL or certain epigenetic changes in sperm DNA influence the mitochondria in embryos remains unclear.

Possible events in epididymis

The present study did not directly determine when and where the sperm TL is influenced by resveratrol. During spermatogenesis, active telomerase protects sperm from telomere shortening; however, the TL has been reported to increase during epididymal maturation, and an age-associated decline in the epididymal sperm TL has been observed in two rat strains [33]. These findings raise the possibility that resveratrol affects sperm TL during epididymal maturation rather than during spermatogenesis.

It has been reported that sperm take up tsRNA during the maturation process [34]. The epididymis is a major target of metabolic disorders, and the mitochondrial tRNA-derived fragment is increased under oxidative stress conditions [35]. Mitochondrial tsRNAs that accumulate in sperm have been shown to influence embryonic metabolism, including oxidative phosphorylation [36]. In addition, sperm-borne tsRNAs contribute to the intergenerational inheritance of metabolic disorders [37]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that paternal physiological conditions alter the abundance of sperm miRNAs; notably, the overexpression of Pgc1a changes sperm miRNA profiles [38]. Furthermore, adding increases oxidative stress and inhibits mitochondrial quality control in embryos [39]. It has been reported that the treatment of oocytes with resveratrol improves mitochondrial quality [40] and reduces lipid content in embryos [41]. Given the broad range of molecular targets of resveratrol, it could improve mitochondria in epididymal cells, modify the epididymal environment and alter the composition of sperm-associated small non-coding RNAs, thereby affecting embryonic phenotypes, including lipid content. In this context, resveratrol-associated miRNAs represent important targets for future investigation.

RNA-seq analysis of blastocysts derived from a resveratrol-treated father

Functional annotation analysis revealed that the mitochondrion was a significantly enriched cellular component among the DEGs. Previous RNA-seq analyses of blastocysts derived from young and aged fathers demonstrated that mitochondrial functions, particularly oxidative phosphorylation, were among the top pathways enriched among DEGs [8]. In contrast, although the present DEGs included genes encoding mitochondrial membrane proteins (Tomm5, Tomm20, Tomm34, and Tomm40), genes directly involved in oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial biogenesis, and mitochondrial fusion and fission were not identified among the DEGs (Supplementary Tables 1–5).

Because in vitro culture has been reported to affect mitochondrial function and number in mouse and bovine blastocysts [42, 43], the current gene expression profiles may suggest that the embryo culture conditions partially masked the differences induced by paternal resveratrol intake. Alternatively, resveratrol supplementation may not fully reverse the effects of paternal aging on mitochondria.

A pathway analysis further showed the enrichment of metabolic pathways. The present RNA-seq data demonstrated downregulation of the expression of genes involved in lipid synthesis (Acly and Acaca), which may underlie the reduced lipid content observed in embryos. Furthermore, expression levels of genes related to lipid oxidation (Acsl5 and Hadh) were also downregulated. In addition, expression levels of genes associated with glycolysis (Pfkl, Aldoa, Eno1, Pkm, Pdk1, and Hif1a) and glutamine metabolism (Slc1a5, Idg2, Ogdh, and Acly) were downregulated in these embryos. Consistent with the “quiet embryo” hypothesis [44], which proposes that lower metabolic activity is associated with higher developmental competence, these findings suggest that paternal resveratrol intake may reprogram embryonic metabolism toward a more dormant or metabolically restrained state. However, the mechanisms by which epigenetic modifications or alterations in TL induce these metabolic changes need to be elucidated.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that paternal resveratrol intake exerts broad effects on embryonic quality, potentially through alterations in the EF composition, sperm epigenetic alterations, and/or the sperm TL. Furthermore, resveratrol intake in aged males influences mitochondrial characteristics and the TL in the offspring. These findings provide new insight into the role of paternal nutritional interventions in modulating embryonic development and offspring health.
