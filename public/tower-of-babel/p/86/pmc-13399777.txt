Combined Lateral Patellofemoral Ligament and Lateral Patellotibial Ligament Reconstruction for Medial Patellar Instability
Authors: Napatpong Thamrongskulsiri, Logan D Moews, Felipe Casanova, Thibault Marty‐Diloy, Benjamin Kerzner, Jorge Chahla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedial patellar instability is an uncommon but debilitating condition that may arise following excessive lateral retinacular release. The lateral patellofemoral ligament and lateral patellotibial ligament function as key static stabilizers that resist medial patellar translation throughout knee flexion. This technical note describes a reproducible open technique for combined lateral patellofemoral ligament and lateral patellotibial ligament reconstruction using a single semitendinosus allograft to restore both proximal and distal lateral restraints. This technique provides anatomic reconstruction while minimizing the risk of residual instability.

Medial patellar instability is an uncommon yet challenging condition that can lead to significant functional impairment, anterior knee pain, and recurrent subluxation episodes. 1 , 2 Although lateral instability from injuries to the medial patellofemoral ligament has been extensively investigated, 3 , 4 , 5 the understanding and management of lateral‐sided ligamentous insufficiency remain poorly defined. The lateral patellofemoral ligament (LPFL), lateral patellotibial ligament (LPTL), and lateral patellomeniscal ligament constitute the principal static soft‐tissue restraints on the lateral aspect of the patella, preventing excessive medial translation and maintaining central patellar tracking throughout the range of knee motion. 6 , 7

The LPFL originates anterior and distal to the lateral femoral epicondyle. The measurement from the lateral femoral epicondyle to the center of the femoral attachment is 13.5 ± 3.2 mm and inserts with an osseous and soft‐tissue attachment onto the superolateral border of the patella, constituting about 45% of the osseous lateral surface of the patella. 6 , 8 The LPFL functions predominantly during early knee flexion (0°‐30°) to counteract medial patellar displacement. 7 In contrast, the LPTL extends from the distal one‐third of the lateral patellar margin to the proximal tibia, located proximal and medial to Gerdy's tubercle, at a mean distance of 8.1 ± 2.1 mm. Furthermore, the lateral patellomeniscal ligament has a tibial origin on the anterolateral aspect of the lateral meniscus with its insertion on the patella that is confluent with the LPTL. 7 Both the LPTL and lateral patellomeniscal ligament provide greater stabilization particularly during mid to deep flexion. 7 Collectively, these structures act in a complementary manner to maintain patellar stability, facilitating the balanced distribution of lateral restraining forces along the proximal‐distal axis of the patella (Figure 1).

Iatrogenic medial patellar instability, secondary to lateral retinacular release or over‐release, has been reported in 57% to 91% of cases. 9 , 10 , 11 Isolated LPFL reconstruction techniques have shown encouraging outcomes in selected patients; however, persistent distal instability or abnormal patellar tilt often remains when the LPTL is deficient. 12 Anatomical and biomechanical investigations have helped define physiological patellar kinematics in order to guide anatomic‐based reconstruction for medial patellar instability. 7

This technical note describes a reproducible open technique for combined reconstruction of the LPFL and LPTL using a single semitendinosus allograft. The procedure is designed to anatomically restore proximal and distal lateral stabilizers, thereby reestablishing physiological patellar tracking while minimizing the risk of overconstraint (Video 1).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning

After induction of anesthesia, an examination under anesthesia is performed for all patients undergoing patellar instability surgery as apprehension preoperatively may limit the physical examination. Findings of the examination under anesthesia should be compared with the contralateral extremity and should include the number of quadrants of translation both medially and laterally, firm or soft endpoint, degree of patellar eversion, and instability resolution angle of knee flexion. After the examination under anesthesia, the patient is placed in the supine position with a lateral post, and a nonsterile tourniquet is applied proximally. After standard prep and draping, the operative extremity is exsanguinated, and a pneumatic tourniquet is inflated. A standard diagnostic arthroscopy is performed through anterolateral and anteromedial portals to evaluate the patellofemoral joint, cartilage surfaces, menisci, and ligaments. Any loose bodies or chondral flaps are addressed, and patellar tracking is carefully assessed to confirm medial subluxation before proceeding with reconstruction.

Exposure

A midline longitudinal incision is made extending from the 2 cm above the superior pole of the patella distally to the tibial tubercle (Figure 2A, Video 1). Superficial flaps are developed, and a laterally based soft‐tissue window is created to the palpable lateral epicondyle. The lateral epicondyle is palpated (Figure 2B, Video 1), and the incision proceeds through the iliotibial band (layer 1) at the level of the lateral epicondyle to expose the lateral retinaculum and capsule (layers 2 and 3, respectively) (Figure 3A, Video 1).

Right knee. (A) The incision is carried through the ITB at the level of the lateral epicondyle to expose the underlying lateral retinaculum and capsule. (B) The periosteum of the patella and paratenon of the patellar tendon are incised longitudinally in the midline, and the flap is elevated laterally to expose the lateral patellar border. (ITB, iliotibial band.)

Patellar Preparation

The paratenon of the patellar tendon is longitudinally incised in midline with a 15 blade taking care to create a single large soft‐tissue flap. The flap is elevated laterally to expose the lateral patellar border (Figure 3B, Video 1).

Femoral Preparation

The native femoral LPFL origin is identified approximately 12 mm anterior to the lateral epicondyle and 14 mm posterior to the anterior cartilage margin. These distances are confirmed intraoperatively using a sterile ruler, guided by direct visualization and previously described anatomic landmarks. 7 , 13 The overlying soft tissue is cleared, and a Beath pin is drilled at this femoral attachment site, directed proximally and anteriorly to avoid breaching the intercondylar notch with the pin end out of the medial distal thigh (Figure 4A, Video 1). The tunnel is then over‐reamed with a 7‐mm reamer over the Beath pin to a depth of 30 mm, and a passing Ethibond suture is placed for subsequent graft passage (Figure 4B, Video 1).

Right knee. (A) A Beath pin is drilled at the femoral attachment site, directed proximally and anteriorly to avoid breaching the intercondylar notch. (B) The tunnel is over‐reamed with a 7‐mm reamer to a depth of 30 mm, and a passing suture is placed for subsequent graft passage.

A semitendinosus allograft, approximately 22 cm in length, is prepared on the back table. The terminal 3 cm of each end is whipstitched using high‐strength No. 2 suture. This is the senior author's similar preparation for a medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction procedure. The semitendinosus allograft is then passed through the prepared femoral bone tunnel to a depth of 30 mm and secured with a 7 × 30 mm biocomposite interference screw (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) (Figure 5A, Video 1).

Right knee. (A) The semitendinosus allograft is passed through the prepared femoral bone tunnel and fixed with a 7 × 30‐mm biocomposite interference screw. (B) A soft‐tissue tunnel is created deep to the iliotibial band, for semitendinosus allograft passage. The graft is directed through this plane, coursing extracapsularly toward the patella and beneath the paratenon layer.

Graft Passage

A soft‐tissue tunnel is created deep to the iliotibial band, to allow graft passage. One limb of the graft is passed medially through this plane, coursing extracapsularly toward the patella and beneath the paratenon layer (Figure 5B, Video 1).

Patellar Fixation

The graft is then sutured to the patellar periosteum using high‐strength No. 2 sutures in two figure‐of‐eight stitches with the knee in full extension (Figure 6A, Video 1). The suture knots are passed laterally through the lateral paratenon flap and trimmed flush with the flap to conceal the knots (Figure 6B, Video 1). This recreates the native pull and stability of the LPFL.

Right knee. (A) The graft is sutured to the patellar periosteum using high‐strength No. 2 sutures in 2 figure‐of‐eight configurations with the knee in full extension. (B) The suture knots are passed laterally through the lateral paratenon flap and trimmed flush with the flap to conceal the knots.

Tibial Fixation

To restore the inferior stabilizer, the semitendinosus allograft limb is extended distally along the lateral patellar tendon and anchored to the proximal tibia, replicating the native anatomic orientation of the LPTL. A Q‐Fix all‐suture anchor (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is placed at the tibial insertion site between Gerdy's tubercle and the lateral patellar tendon (Figure 7A, Video 1). With the knee in full extension, the position where the LPFL is at its maximal length and the LPTL remains nearly isometric, the graft is tensioned, and a marker pen is used to mark the desired fixation point (Figure 7B, Video 1). Using a free needle, the sutures from the anchor are passed and tied to the marked location on the graft to secure it to the tibia (Figure 8A, Video 1). The knee is then cycled several times to verify appropriate anisometry, ensuring that the graft gradually tightens with flexion while maintaining smooth, central patellar tracking throughout the range of motion. The remaining graft end is folded back and sutured to the lateral patellar tendon using 1 suture limb from the anchor in Krakow locking fashion, eliminating the need for independent high‐tensile sutures (Figures 8B, and 9, Video 1).

Closure

The paratenon are then closed, followed by subcutaneous closure with 2‐0 Monocryl and skin closure with 3‐0 Monocryl in the standard fashion. A sterile dressing and a hinged knee brace locked in full extension are applied at the end of the procedure.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, the patient is placed in a hinged knee brace locked in full extension and allowed to bear weight as tolerated with the brace in place. During the first 2 weeks, the range of motion is limited to 0° to 30°, emphasizing full knee extension and gentle quadriceps activation. From weeks 2 to 4, the range of motion is gradually increased to 0° to 60°, and quadriceps strengthening exercises are initiated. By weeks 4 to 6, knee flexion is advanced to 90°, maintaining proper patellar tracking. The brace is discontinued at approximately 6 weeks, and progressive closed‐chain strengthening, squats, leg presses, and controlled agility drills are introduced between weeks 6 and 14. Forward running and light plyometric exercises may begin around 14 to 22 weeks postoperatively once the patient shows adequate quadriceps control and can perform an 8‐inch step‐down without pain or instability. Return to sport‐specific activities is typically permitted after 22 weeks, contingent on clinical assessment and restoration of strength, motion, and stability comparable to the contralateral limb.

DISCUSSION

Reconstruction of the lateral patellofemoral complex remains technically demanding due to its intricate anatomy and limited clinical evidence compared with medial‐sided procedures. The present technique emphasizes restoration of both the LPFL and LPTL, which act as complementary static stabilizers of the lateral patellar restraint. Addressing both components allows for restoration of physiological patellar tracking and tilt control throughout flexion, particularly in cases where iatrogenic medial instability follows an excessive lateral release.

Anatomic studies have revealed that the LPFL originates near the lateral femoral epicondyle and contributes to stability during early flexion, whereas the LPTL extends distally toward the tibia and functions primarily in mid to deep flexion. 6 , 7 Reconstruction of only the LPFL may therefore fail to correct distal instability or abnormal patellar tilt when the LPTL is incompetent. By utilizing a single semitendinosus allograft, this technique replicates the native orientation and anisometric behavior of both ligaments in a single, efficient construct.

There is limited clinical long‐term outcome data for LPFL reconstruction in isolation and with concomitant LPTL reconstruction with much of the literature looking at biomechanical analysis and technique papers. 1 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Moatshe et al. performed a case series of 13 patients who underwent isolated LPTL reconstruction for prior lateral release with associated medial patellar instability. They found that Lysholm scores improved from 45.6 to 71.9 postoperatively, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index improved from 38 preoperatively to 6 postoperatively, and overall mean satisfaction was 8.2. 9 Additionally, Beckert et al. retrospectively looked at 19 knees in 17 patients who underwent isolated LPFL and found no patients had postoperative instability with an overall increase in knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score (34.39 vs 69.54; P < .0001). 19 Although these studies are encouraging on the importance of reconstruction, both comparative and long‐term clinical outcome studies are needed for lateral patellofemoral complex reconstruction.

Technical nuances are critical for optimal outcomes. The femoral tunnel should be directed proximally and anteriorly to avoid the intercondylar notch, 20 , 21 while the tibial fixation must be positioned between the Gerdy tubercle and the lateral patellar tendon to reproduce the native LPTL vector. 7 Graft tensioning in full extension ensures restoration of physiological patellar alignment while preventing overconstraint, a known risk in lateral reconstructive procedures. Verification of smooth, central tracking through repeated flexion‐extension cycles is essential before final fixation 7 (Table 1).

The advantages of this technique include anatomical restoration using a single graft, avoidance of additional graft harvest, and maintenance of an extra‐articular course that preserves future surgical options. The stepwise exposure and layered approach also enhance reproducibility, making it adaptable for both primary and revision settings. However, several disadvantages should be considered. The procedure requires meticulous identification of anatomical landmarks, and small errors in tunnel placement can significantly alter graft isometry and patellar tracking. The open approach may lead to greater soft‐tissue dissection compared with arthroscopic‐assisted techniques, potentially increasing postoperative discomfort and recovery time. In addition, the use of allograft tissue may not be universally available and carries potential concerns regarding cost and biologic incorporation. Finally, as with all reconstructive procedures of the patellofemoral joint, overtensioning of the graft can result in patellofemoral overload and stiffness, underscoring the importance of careful intraoperative tension assessment (Table 1).

In summary, combined LPFL and LPTL reconstruction using a single semitendinosus allograft offers an anatomic and reproducible method to restore balanced lateral restraint and physiological patellar tracking. With precise tunnel placement and appropriate graft tensioning, this technique provides a practical solution for managing medial patellar instability after excessive lateral release.
