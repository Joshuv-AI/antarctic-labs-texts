Laser Interstitial Thermal Therapy as Surgical Approach for Unresectable Glioblastoma: Hope is on the Horizon?
Authors: Philipp Karschnia, Arthur Wagner
Published: 2026

While maximal safe resection represents the first management step for any patient with a newly diagnosed glioblastoma,1 about 20–30% of individuals initially present with imaging findings deemed as not meaningfully resectable by the treating neurosurgeon due to proximity to functional brain areas.2 In these cases, biopsy for neuropathological confirmation is typically scheduled to guide further non-surgical treatment; however, such patients do not experience the presumed oncological benefits from cytoreductive surgery.3 Acknowledging that patients triaged to undergo biopsy frequently also identify with less favourable clinical characteristics including a high level of frailty, individuals treated with standard-of-care radiochemotherapy in the absence of prior open resection face a poor prognosis with a median overall survival rarely exceeding ten months.2,4

In this issue of The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, Dr. Neutel and colleagues publish the results from the prospective, randomised, seven-centre, non-blinded EMITT trial (NCT05318612) conducted in the Netherlands.5 Recruiting patients with good clinical functioning and presumed non-resectable glioblastoma, the trial intended to show superiority of Laser Interstitial Thermal Therapy (LITT) plus biopsy over biopsy alone with regard to improving overall survival and quality of life; hypothesizing that a LITT-induced ablation of at least 70% of the contrast-enhancing tumour mass may translate into better outcome. Given premature trial closure due to funding being terminated as only 29 patients were enrolled between 2022 and 2024, Dr. Neutel and colleagues herein report on the outcomes of 26 patients from the intention-to-treat subgroup. While it is tempting to speculate that the median overall survival of 7.8 months in LITT-treated patients supports the trial’s hypothesis compared to 4.8 months in the control cohort, the presented EMITT dataset is prone to confounding: less patients from the control cohort successfully completed the standard-of-care radiochemotherapy, and tumours in the intervention cohort were substantially smaller in size. As baseline tumour volume has been established as a key predictor for outcome independent of adjuvant treatment allocation,4 the intervention arm from the EMITT trial likely started from a more favourable oncological baseline; and any observed survival difference cannot be causally attributed to LITT.

What the EMITT trial shows with unusual clarity is that there was no convincing signal for a delay in post-operative standard-of-care treatment by LITT (with 58.3% in the intervention group and 50% in the control group starting adjuvant therapy), and also procedure-related morbidity remained below the pre-specified threshold for DSMB review. An increasing number of surgical procedures has become available for minimally invasive cytoreduction over the last decade, not only including LITT but also focused ultrasound or photodynamic therapy.6,7 It appears exciting to envision a world where such innovative surgical approaches might provide an oncological benefit in patients not traditionally considered surgical candidates for open resection, without hampering standard-of-care. At present, however, those approaches must be viewed as experimental for the treatment of glioblastomas and application should be limited to prospective trials. With the EMITT trial not delivering any reliable conclusions on the primary survival endpoint, a salient point for further discussion remains whether minimally invasive cytoreduction (or partial resection) targeting only a portion of the newly diagnosed glioblastoma improves outcome compared to biopsy alone when assessed in a prospective, randomised manner.

Highly emphasized should be the fact that accrual fell short in the EMITT trail despite a preceding feasibility pilot and a nationally coordinated multi-centre infrastructure.8 In a disease like ‘unresectable’ glioblastoma where recurrence virtually always occurs within months, the problems faced during the EMITT trial are problems generally endemic to surgical trials in neuro-oncology: deteriorating clinical condition at referral, heterogeneity between neurosurgeons in resectability assessment, and reluctance of patients to participate in a trial potentially jeopardizing standard-of-care. Accordingly, those problems can also be observed in other European countries as exemplified by the French ANOCEF trial which aimed to compare open resection against biopsy only in patients with glioblastoma over the age of 70 years9; and the trial took eleven years to recruit a total of 109 patients (in a country with more than 2000 newly diagnosed glioblastoma cases per year).10 With most health systems across Europe providing de-centralized care to glioblastoma patients, it appears unlikely that neither the EMITT trial nor the ANOCEF trial would have achieved better recruitment rates when conducted in other countries in the absence of reliable national (or even European) referral systems for timely evaluation of trial eligibility. While it is, therefore, to be assumed that the observations from the EMITT trial are not yet practice changing, the message from this prematurely closed surgical trial is clear: a call to the surgical community in Europe to foster a collaborative trial environment to improve care for glioblastoma patients.
