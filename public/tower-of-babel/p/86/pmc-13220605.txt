ADH4 deficiency activates the NRF2-CSF1 axis by increased lipid peroxidation in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ping Yan, Yunhai Luo, Dadi Peng, Tong Mou, Dengliang Lei, Shanshan Li, Huarong Yu, Zuotian Huang, Zhongjun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is frequently associated with serious metabolic disorders, resulting in a bleak prognosis. Although ADH4 is implicated in the metabolism of fatty acids, its exact function in tumourigenesis is still unclear.MethodsOur study initially examined proliferation potential in vitro and in vivo using various approaches, such as CCK8, EdU incorporation, colony formation assays, and a tumourigenicity assay in mice, which included orthotopic injection into the left liver lobe. Next, we conducted a quantitative analysis of oxylipins to investigate the correlation between ADH4 and lipid peroxidation in hepatoma cells. Additionally, we examined the intracellular ratio of NADP+/NADPH and measured the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) in other hepatoma cells. Furthermore, we employed mRNA sequencing to identify the downstream targets of ADH4 in hepatoma cells.ResultsThe results indicate that there is a significant association between ADH4 levels and lipid peroxidation in hepatoma cells. It has been shown that there is a downregulation of ADH4 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma, and that ADH4 deficiency is a contributing factor in tumour progression and overall poor prognosis. Both gain- and loss-of-function studies have demonstrated that ADH4 has an anti-proliferative effect on hepatocellular carcinoma both in vitro and in vivo. Inhibition of ADH4 leads to the accumulation of oxylipin metabolites and an increase in the NADP+/NADPH ratio, ROS and MDA production, ultimately resulting in the activation of the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway and the nuclear translocation of NRF2. Furthermore, the results of mRNA sequencing indicate a close correlation between ADH4 expression and the cytokine-cytokine receptor pathway, with the strongest negative association found with CSF1 expression. This has been validated in other hepatoma cells and clinical patient samples. Flow cytometric analysis has confirmed ADH4’s ability to suppress CSF1-mediated polarization of M2-type macrophages in an NRF2 signaling-dependent manner. In conclusion, ADH4 plays a significant role in the regulation of macrophage polarization.ConclusionThese findings suggest that downregulation of ADH4 may exert protumourigenic effects on hepatocellular carcinoma by increasing lipid peroxidation levels, NADP+/NADPH ratio, ROS and MDA levels, leading to activation of the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway, upregulation of CSF1 and M2-type polarisation of tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for hepatocellular carcinoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03940-6.

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma is the sixth most common cancer globally and the third leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. In China, hepatocellular carcinoma is the third most prevalent cancer and the second leading cause of death, accounting for more than 50% of global cases [2]. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major pathogenic factor behind the high rates of morbidity and mortality associated with hepatocellular carcinoma worldwide [3]. The tumourigenic process initiated by HBV typically occurs in conjunction with chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis that persists for long periods [4]. Furthermore, HBV antigens can interfere with host cell lipid metabolism and evoke mild oxidative stress in vivo [5].

Dysregulated metabolism significantly impacts the development of liver disease. Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) metabolism, which is most active in the liver, breaks down fatty acids into CO2 and H2O, releasing a significant amount of energy. Therefore, dysregulated FAO is associated with the incidence and progression of tumours, and reduces the effectiveness of clinical therapies in cancer patients [6]. Deregulated redox homeostasis is an additional characteristic of cancer cells. While cancer cells initially experience elevated ROS levels due to rapid proliferation, they often rewire their metabolism (e.g., via the Warburg effect) to maintain ROS at optimal levels, preventing apoptosis while supporting tumourigenesis [7]. This leads to the acceleration of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) oxidisation, resulting in lipid peroxidation (LPO) and oxylipin accumulation in cell membranes and lipoproteins [8]. Consequently, cancer cells have developed an efficient system to regulate ROS accumulation by antioxidation [9]. Nuclear factor (erythroid-derived 2)-like 2 (NRF2) performs crucial functions in antioxidant response, anti-inflammatory reactions, and other vital cellular processes [10]. Nevertheless, overactivation of NRF2 has been identified as a protumour agent, facilitating proliferation, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy [11]. Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) is a widely recognized NRF2 negative regulator that mediates its degradation, facilitating the dissociation of NRF2. This enables its nuclear translocation and the subsequent induction of its target genes [12].

Alcohol dehydrogenase families (ADHs), which predominantly exist in the livers of humans and animals, serve as essential enzymes in the metabolism of short-chain alcohols and ω-oxidation of certain fatty acids, playing crucial roles in numerous physiological processes. A cluster of genes consisting of seven alcohol dehydrogenase-encoding genes (ADH1A, ADH1B, ADH1C, ADH4, ADH5, ADH6 and ADH7) is situated on chromosome 4q and encodes ADHs. Loss of Chromosome 4q is a frequent occurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma, leading to the loss of an entire cluster of seven genes. As a result, there is typically a decrease in the expression of ADHs (ADH1A, ADH1B, ADH1C, ADH4 and ADH6) in hepatocellular carcinoma, as observed in the TCGA database (GEPIA2 website; http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index; Fig. S1, 2 and Fig. 1B, C). Amongst these, ADH4 is the most substantially downregulated gene, which affects both overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in hepatocellular carcinoma patients. Furthermore, a thorough investigation was previously conducted to identify possible biomarkers for predicting hepatocellular carcinoma recurrence [13]. The results showed that in hepatocellular carcinoma, ADH4 was significantly downregulated and this downregulation indicated a higher likelihood of recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma patients. Afterward, we performed additional bioinformatic analysis to develop a prognostic signature based on fatty acid metabolism genes associated with hepatitis B in hepatocellular carcinoma [14]. ADH4 was also found to play a significant role in the development of hepatocellular carcinoma. As a consequence, further research was conducted on the role of ADH4 in hepatocellular carcinoma progression.

In this study, our aim was to explore the role of ADH4 in hepatocellular carcinoma and uncover the underlying mechanisms. We evaluated the impact of ADH4 on hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation and analyzed the levels of lipid peroxidation, NADP+/NADPH ratio, ROS and MDA production, as well as KEAP1/NRF2 signaling via loss and gain of ADH4 function in vitro. Our findings collectively suggest that ADH4 may function as a suppressor of tumour in hepatocellular carcinoma and may represent a potential therapeutic approach for this condition.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

Forty-five pairs of hepatocellular carcinoma tumour tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. The samples included 30 HBV-infected and 15 HBV-non-infected cases. Patient consent was obtained for sample collection and associated clinical information. All experiments were approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (TFAHCQMU). All diagnoses were verified by two skilled pathologists. A total of 322 hepatocellular carcinoma patients in the TCGA database with defined clinical data were chosen for correlation study. Mice

C57BL/6 Adh4-KO mice (strain no. T001724) and BALB/c Nude mice (strain no. D000521) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) and maintained in the animal breeding facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Chongqing Medical University under specific pathogen-free conditions. For orthotopic injection, 4- to 6-week-old C57BL/6 or nude mice (20–25 g) were anesthetized with tribromoethanol (200 mg/kg, i.p.), and 25 µL of a suspension of 3 × 106 tumour cells mixed with Matrigel (356237, Corning) was injected into the left liver lobe. For subcutaneous injection, 100 µL of 3 × 106 tumour cell suspension was injected into the nude mice. The tumour volume was measured and calculated using the formula [(small diameter)2x(large diameter) x0.5]. The experimental procedures for the use and care of animals were approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University.

Cell culture

HepG2, HepG2.2.15, Huh7, Hepa1-6, HEK293T and THP1 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Other hepatoma cell lines (Hep3B, PLC/PRF/5, SK-Hep1) and normal human hepatocytes L02 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HepG2 and HepG2.2.15 human hepatoma cells were cultured in Minimum Essential medium (MEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Rockville, MD, USA), 100 IU penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin (15140122, Gibco), and culture dishes were coated with collagen (354236, Corning) to increase the generation of cell-specific morphology. THP1 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco), and the other cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco). All cell lines were confirmed to be mycoplasma free and were used for less than 50 passages.

Chemicals and reagents

A Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (BB-31122) was obtained from BestBio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), ELISA Kits for human CSF1 (MM-2022H1) from Meimian Industrial (Jiangsu, China), ELISA Kits for human 5,6-EET (ml063590) from Mlbio (Shanghai, China), brusatol (MB7292) from Meilunbio Biotechnology (Dalian, China) and TAT-14 TFA (T295042) from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). The NRF2 antagonist brusatol and the NRF2 activator TAT-14 TFA (TAT in short) [15] was dissolved in 100% DMSO to a concentration of 10 mM to generate stock solutions. For in vitro experiments, cells were treated with 50 nM brusatol or 100 µM TAT alone or with vehicle control. For in vivo experiments, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 10 µmol/kg TAT or vehicle control twice weekly until sacrificed.

Lentiviral particle production and lentiviral transduction

Lentiviral particles were generated by cotransfecting HEK293T cells with the shuttle plasmid pLVX-puro or pLKO.1-puro, envelope plasmid pMD2.G, and packaging plasmid psPAX2 using PEI MW40000 (40816ES02, Yeasen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. For stable transfection, the lentiviral supernatants were collected and used to infect target cells for 24 hours, and the cells were recovered in fresh culture medium for 24 hours and then selected for 1 week with the appropriate antibiotics. The full-length cDNA of human ADH4 (NM_001306171.2) was amplified from HepG2 cells and inserted into the XhoI and BamHI sites of the shuttle vector pLVX-puro (P0247, Miaoling). Additionally, KEAP1 (NM_203500.2) and CSF1 (NM_000757.6) were inserted into the XhoI and BamHI sites of the blasticidin-resistance shuttle vector PHY-032 (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). The luciferase expression vector was inserted into the blasticidin-resistance shuttle vector PHY-032 (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). The pLKO.1-puro lentiviral vector (P0258, Miaoling) was used to insert the double-stranded short hairpin RNA (shRNA) against ADH4, while the pLKO.1-Hygro lentiviral vector (P0702) was used for the shRNA against CSF1. The sequences of the shRNAs are listed as follows: sh-NC forward sense: 5’ CCGG - CAA CAA GAT GAA GAG CAC CAA - CTCGAG - TTG GTG CTC TTC ATC TTG TTG - TTTTT 3’; sh-ADH4 forward sense: 5’ CCGG - GCT TCC AGA ATC ATA GGT ATT - CTCGAG - AAT ACC TAT GAT TCT GGA AGC - TTTTT 3’; sh-CSF1 forward sense: 5’ CCGG - TCT CCT GGT ACA AGA CAT AAT - CTCGAG - ATT ATG TCT TGT ACC AGG AGA - TTTTT 3’. The authenticity of the abovementioned constructs was confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR)

In brief, total RNA was extracted from clinical specimens and cell lines using TRIzol (15596018, Invitrogen) and was then reverse transcribed to cDNA by GoScript (A5001, Promega). Finally, TB Green Premix Ex Taq (RR420, Takara) was used to perform qRT‒PCR analysis to calculate relative gene expression levels (as a ratio of the expression of the target transcript to that of β-actin). The primers are listed in Table S1.

Western blotting analysis

In brief, protein lysates of liver samples or cells were extracted with cell lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime), and equivalent amounts of protein were separated by SDS/PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk diluted in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with the indicated antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the membranes were washed with TBST and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected using Ultrasensitive ECL western blotting HRP Substrate (17047, Zenbio). The primary antibodies are listed as follows: anti-ADH4 (16474, Proteintech), anti-NRF2 (16396, Proteintech), anti-Lamin B1 (12987, Proteintech), anti-NQO1 (11451, Proteintech), anti-HO-1 (10701, Proteintech), anti-CSF1 (ET1609-1, Huabio), anti-Flag-Tag (20543, Proteintech), anti-GAPDH (HRP-6004, Proteintech), and anti-β-actin (HRP-66009, Proteintech).

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin and were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and rinsed in distilled water. The sections were incubated with antibodies against ADH4 (1:50, 16474, Proteintech) or Ki67 (1:2000, HA721115, Huabio) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies and visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB).

Immunoprecipitation

ADH4-overexpressing and control HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells and ADH4-knockdown and control HepG2 cells were infected with Flag-tagged KEAP1 lentiviruses for 48 h. Cells were collected with cell lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were then incubated with an anti-Flag antibody (20543, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the immunoprecipitates were incubated for 6 h with protein A/G magnetic beads (HY-K0202, MCE), washed with lysis buffer three times and eluted from the beads by boiling in 1x loading buffer for 10 min. The eluted immunoprecipitates were then subjected to western blotting analysis.

Flow cytometry

THP1 cells were seeded in a six-well plate (1 × 106 cells/well) and treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; HY-18739, MCE) for 24 h to induce differentiation into M0 macrophages. Then, these THP1 cells were cocultured with equal volumes of supernatants from ADH4-overexpressing and control HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells or ADH4-knockdown and control HepG2 cells for 48 h to induce polarization into M2-type macrophages. Finally, the cells were collected and stained with anti-human CD11b-APC (E-AB-F108, Elabscience) and anti-human CD206-FITC (E-AB-F1161C, Elabscience) antibodies. Flow cytometry was performed on a CytoFLEX instrument, and the data were analyzed using CytExpert software.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

A CCK8 assay kit (C0042, Beyotime), 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay kit (C0071S, Beyotime), and colony formation assay were used to measure cell proliferation rates. For CCK8 assays, cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and cultured for 3 days, and the plate was scanned every 24 h. For EdU incorporation assays, cells were plated into six-well culture plates (1 × 105 cells/well). Twenty-four hours after plating, EdU labeling and fluorescence measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For colony formation assays, cells were seeded in six-well plates (1 × 103 cells/well) and incubated for 2 weeks in triplicate. Colonies were stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime), photographed and counted in triplicate wells.

NADPH, ROS and malondialdehyde (MDA) measurements

Intracellular NADP+/NADPH ratios were measured using an NADP/NADPH Assay Kit (S0179, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Signal intensities were determined by optical density (OD) measurements (Infinite 200Pro, Tecan, Swiss) at 450 nm. For ROS assays, cells were incubated with 10 µmol/L DCFH-DA (S0033, Beyotime) at 37 °C for 30 min and then washed with FBS-free culture medium. Intracellular ROS levels were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Green fluorescence intensity was quantified by using ImageJ software. Intracellular MDA levels were measured using an MDA Assay Kit (S0131S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Signal intensities were determined by measurement of the OD at 532 nm.

In vivo imaging

For nude mice, 3 × 106 HepG2.2.15 cells (with/without ADH4 overexpression) or HepG2 cells (with/without ADH4 knockdown) expressing a luciferase reporter were orthotopically injected into the left liver lobe. For adh4+/+/adh4−/− C57BL/6 mice, 3 × 106 Hepa1-6 cells expressing a luciferase reporter were injected into the left liver lobe. Bioluminescence signals of luciferase were detected after intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg D-luciferin potassium (MB1834, Meilunbio) on day 14 (C57BL/6 mice) and day 30 (nude mice) with a NightOWL LB983 system (Berthold Technologies, Germany).

Quantitative profiling of oxylipins

Quantitation of oxylipin contents was performed with MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/) based on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC‒MS/MS platform. The quantified metabolites included downstream metabolites derived from arachidonic acid (AA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), linoleic acid (LA), alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), and dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA). Specifically, samples stored in a −80 °C refrigerator were thawed on ice. Then, the samples were placed in liquid nitrogen for 5 min and on dry ice for 5 min and were then thawed on ice and vortexed for 2 min. This freeze‒thaw cycle was repeated three times. Next, the samples were precipitated at low temperature (−20 °C) for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C). All supernatants were collected, and the oxylipins in the supernatants were extracted using Poly-Sery MAX SPE columns (ANPEL). Prior to analysis, the eluents were dried under vacuum, redissolved in 100 µL of methanol/water (1:1, v/v) and sent for UPLC–MS/MS analysis. Significantly differentially abundant metabolites between groups were identified with the cutoff criteria |Log2FC|≥1 and variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1. VIP values were extracted from the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) data, which were generated using the R package MetaboAnalystR. To avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed.

mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted from ADH4-overexpressing or control HepG2.2.15 cells, and RNA quality was evaluated with an Agilent 2100/4200 bioanalyzer. Qualified RNA was used to construct the common transcriptome library. Next, the qualified library was submitted for mRNA-seq on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Berry Genomics Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The edgeR package of R software was used to identify the differentially expressed mRNAs, with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change)|>1 set as the cutoff criteria.

Bioinformatic analysis

ADH4 mRNA expression data of hepatocellular carcinoma patients were acquired from five GEO datasets (GSE22058, GSE66041, GSE76427, GSE94660 and GSE121248). Both the survival and mRNA expression data of hepatocellular carcinoma patients were obtained from the GEPIA2 website (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index). Clinicopathological and RNA expression data for 117 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma associated with HBV were acquired from the OncoDB website (https://oncodb.org/). The website includes merged RNA-seq data and relevant clinical information for individuals in the TCGA database, who have contracted particular viruses. The Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotations, which cover molecular function (MF), biological process (BP) and cellular component (CC) terms, of the ADH families were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID; version 6.8; http://david.ncifcrf.gov/). The KOBAS version 3.0 system (available at http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) was utilised to evaluate the pathway enrichment of the KEGG, accessible at www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html, with a p-value of less than 0.01. A Kaplan-Meier analysis followed by the log-rank test was conducted to determine survival with a p-value of less than 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM values. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.3.0, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical differences were determined using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA. Correlation analysis between ADH4 expression and other clinicopathological features, were assessed using the Chi square test. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Throughout the text, figures, and figure legends, the following terminology is used to denote statistical significance: *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; and ns, nonsignificant.

Results

ADH4 is downregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma and predicts poor prognosis

We initially analysed the levels of ADH4 mRNA expression in five sets of independent data containing hepatocellular carcinoma patients (GSE22058, GSE66041, GSE76427, GSE94660 and GSE121248), including 95, 60, 52, 21 and 37 pairs of tumour and normal liver tissue, respectively, obtained from the GEO database. The results showed a significant reduction in the expression of ADH4 mRNA in hepatocellular carcinoma tumour tissues when compared to normal liver tissues (Fig. 1A). Additionally, decreased ADH4 expression was linked to poor overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) times (Fig. 1B, C). Furthermore, a correlation analysis of ADH4 expression and clinicopathological features revealed that lower ADH4 expression is associated with a higher likelihood of HBV infection and advanced American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage (see Table 1). Next, ADH4 mRNA expression demonstrated significant downregulation in tumour tissue (n = 45, p < 0.0001, Fig. 1D). Then, ADH4 protein expression levels were assessed in 10 paired samples of hepatocellular carcinoma and normal tissue through western blotting (Fig. 1E) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining (Fig. 1F). The protein expression levels of ADH4 were significantly lower in tumour tissues when compared to the corresponding normal tissues. These results indicate a strong correlation between the downregulation of ADH4 and hepatocellular carcinoma.

ADH4 suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation in vitro

To explore the function of ADH4 in hepatocellular carcinoma, we conducted an initial analysis of the endogenous protein levels of ADH4 in select hepatoma cells. The results of Western blotting indicated that ADH4 was downregulated in the majority of hepatoma cells, which supports our previous research. Notably, HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells displayed significantly lower levels of ADH4 expression compared to HepG2 and Hep3B cells, as shown in Fig. 2A. To assess the impact of ADH4 on cellular proliferation, we created a lentiviral plasmid containing human ADH4 (LV-ADH4) and utilised an empty vector (LV-NC) as a reference. Verification of ADH4 overexpression in HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells was obtained through Western blotting (Fig. 2B). Additionally, ADH4-knockdown in HepG2 cells was accomplished through introduction of an shRNA that targets human ADH4 (sh-ADH4), employing the corresponding empty vector as a comparison. The western blotting analyses confirmed the knockdown efficiency (Fig. 2B). The significant suppression of cell proliferation in HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 was exhibited with overexpression of ADH4, as demonstrated by CCK8 (Fig. 2C, D), EdU incorporation (Fig. 2F, G) and colony formation assays (Fig. 2I, J). Conversely, knockdown of ADH4 promoted HepG2 cell proliferation (Fig. 2E, H) and colony formation (Fig. 2K). The results combined suggest that ADH4 suppresses the proliferation of hepatoma cells in vitro.

ADH4 inhibits tumour growth in vivo

To further verify the in vivo tumour suppressing responsibilities of ADH4, we executed orthotopic liver implantation experiments in BALB/c nude mice. Mirrored by the previous in vitro observations, HepG2.2.15 cells with ADH4 overexpression noticeably diminished the tumour growth rate compared to the control cohort (Fig. 3A, B). This was authenticated by IHC staining of the proliferation marker Ki67 (Fig. 3G). By contrast, liver tumours derived from HepG2 cells that had ADH4-knockdown showed increased cell proliferation, demonstrated by a higher luciferase signal (Fig. 3C), larger size (Fig. 3D) and more abundant Ki67 staining (Fig. 3H). Additionally, we injected Hepa1-6 mouse hepatoma cells that expressed a luciferase reporter into the left liver lobes of C57BL/6 mice with wild-type (Adh4+/+) or Adh4-KO (Adh4−/−), using an orthotopic method. The liver tumours that emerged in Adh4-KO C57BL/6 mice exhibited a higher ability to proliferate than those in their wild-type counterparts (as shown in Fig. 3E, F, I). Consequently, these in vivo functional studies provide added proof that ADH4 is a tumour suppressor.

Downregulation of ADH4 increases lipid peroxidation and the ROS level

Given that ADH4 plays a role in the metabolism of fatty acids (refer to Fig. S3), which was analysed by the KEGG research tool, we performed a quantitative profiling of oxylipins to evaluate the correlation between ADH4 and lipid peroxidation in HBV-associated HepG2.2.15 hepatoma cells (that are known for their stable expression of HBV DNA [16]) with the lowest level of ADH4 expression. The findings revealed that overexpression of ADH4 resulted in an overall reduction of oxylipin levels in HepG2.2.15 cells (refer to Fig. 4A). Moreover, the level of the most abundant oxylipin, 5,6-EET, showed a significant decrease, which was further validated in HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells overexpressing ADH4 (see Fig. 4B and C). The elevation in the 5,6-EET level in ADH4-knockdown HepG2 cells confirmed the finding (see Fig. 4D). Since ADH4 has been found to impact fatty acid oxidation in a manner that relies on the coenzyme nicotinamide adenine nucleotide (NAD+), which constitutes a crucial role in the initiation of hepatoma by being transformed into NADP + and NADPH and affecting cellular antioxidant capacity and ROS generation, it is reasonable to assume that ADH4 could impact the intracellular NADP+/NADPH ratio and ROS level. Consequently, we commenced by determining the intracellular NADP+/NADPH ratio and ROS level. As anticipated, the overexpression of ADH4 resulted in a decline of the intracellular NADP+/NADPH ratio and a decrease in the intracellular ROS level across HepG2.2.15 (Fig. 4E, H) and Huh7 (Fig. 4F, I). Conversely, loss of ADH4 exhibited the opposite effect on the NADP+/NADPH ratio and ROS production within HepG2 cells (Fig. 4G, J). Given that MDA functions as a stable metabolite of oxylipins, we gauged the cellular MDA level. In line with the preceding results, there was a drop in the MDA concentration in HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells that overexpressed ADH4 (Fig. 4K, L), whereas there was an increase in MDA concentration in ADH4-deficient HepG2 cells (Fig. 4M). This, in turn, triggers an escalation in ROS production. These observations led us to postulate that the downregulation of ADH4 prompts lipid peroxidation, chiefly, the synthesis of the AA metabolite 5,6-EET.

ADH4 deficiency activates the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway

The transcription factor KEAP1 exhibits high sensitivity towards ROS, and the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway has a crucial role in cellular antioxidant capacity. Briefly speaking, the interaction between NRF2 and KEAP1 is affected by ROS, causing the translocation of NRF2 to the nucleus, where it activates the transcription of certain antioxidant genes such as HO-1 and NQO1. To further verify the link between ADH4 and the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway, we initially assessed the levels of representative downstream target genes of NRF2. The mRNA levels of NQO1, HO-1, GCLM, SOD1 and Trx exhibited a decline in HepG2.2.15 (Fig. 5A) and Huh7 (Fig. 5B) cells that overexpressed ADH4 whereas they increased in ADH4-knockdown HepG2 cells (Fig. 5C). The levels of HO-1 and NQO1 proteins were notably reduced in HepG2.2.15 cells overexpressing ADH4 (Fig. 5D) and Huh7 cells (Fig. 5E) but increased in ADH4-knockdown HepG2 cells (Fig. 5F). Additionally, the overexpression of ADH4 led to a decrease in NRF2 nuclear translocation in HepG2.2.15 (Fig. 5G) and Huh7 (Fig. 5H) cells, while the knockdown of ADH4 had the inverse effect on the distribution of NRF2 (Fig. 5I). Immunoprecipitation followed by western blotting analysis was performed, revealing that HepG2.2.15 (Fig. 5J) and Huh7 (Fig. 5K) cells exhibited increased KEAP1 and NRF2 interaction when ADH4 was overexpressed, while HepG2 cells (Fig. 5L) exhibited decreased NRF2 interaction when ADH4 was knocked down. As a result, we hypothesized that the deficiency of ADH4 results in the activation of the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway, increasing ROS accumulation and upregulating gene expression downstream.

ADH4 expression negatively correlates with CSF1 expression

To fully comprehend the underlying ADH4 mechanism in HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma, we conducted mRNA-seq analysis on both overexpressed ADH4 and control HepG2.2.15 cells. This resulted in identifying 782 dysregulated genes, with the majority related to the cytokine‒cytokine receptor interaction pathway (refer to Fig. S4). Among these, CSF1, which displayed the highest expression level in control HepG2.21.5 cells, showed a significant decrease in expression within ADH4-overexpressing HepG2.21.5 cells (refer to Fig. 6A). The mRNA expression, protein expression, and secreted protein levels of CSF1 were measured in HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells overexpressing ADH4. Our analysis found that overexpression of ADH4 significantly suppressed the expression of CSF1 (Fig. 6B, C, E, F, G). Conversely, the inhibition of ADH4 in HepG2 cells resulted in an increase in the levels of CSF1 (Fig. 6D, E, H). Furthermore, an investigation of thirty pairs of HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma tissues and normal tissues verified that the expression of CSF1 mRNA was increased in tumourous tissues (n = 30, p = 0.0011, Fig. 6I). In these thirty HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma tissues, we also observed a negative correlation between CSF1 and ADH4 expression (r=−0.63, p < 0.001, Fig. 6J). Furthermore, a negative correlation between ADH4 and CSF1 expression was observed in a larger cohort of hepatocellular carcinoma patients from the TCGA database (analyzed by OncoDB; https://oncodb.org/; Fig. S5A, B). The protein expression levels of CSF1 were found to be greater in tumourous tissues when compared to their corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 6K). Additionally, an increased expression of CSF1 was found to be associated with higher tumour stage and shorter OS time in HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma patients from the TCGA database (Fig. 6L, M). Collectively, these findings suggest a correlation between reduced ADH4 expression and an elevated CSF1 expression in HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma cells and tumour tissues from patients.

ADH4 suppresses CSF1-mediated M2 polarization of macrophages

The cytokine CSF1 (also referred to as macrophage colony-stimulating factor) is secreted by tumour cells and is crucial in polarising macrophages. It also sustains the protumourigenic functions of tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) [17.18]. To verify the role of ADH4 in regulating TAM polarization, we used PMA to induce differentiation of THP1 cells into macrophages. We then cocultured them with supernatants of different types of hepatoma cells to assess the potential of their polarization into M2-type macrophages. The expression of M2 macrophage markers, namely CD206, CD163, and IL10, decreased upon co-cultivation with the supernatants from ADH4-overexpressing HepG2.2.15 (as shown in Fig. 7A) and Huh7 cells (as shown in Fig. 7B). Conversely, hindering ADH4 expression led to a rise in the expression of these markers (as demonstrated in Fig. 7C). Furthermore, we observed a positive correlation between NRF2 and CSF1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma patients from the TCGA database (analyzed by OncoDB; https://oncodb.org/; Fig. S5C, D). Next, we explored whether ADH4-mediated CSF1 inhibition relies on the NRF2 pathway or not, by treating HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells with ADH4-overexpression either exclusively or together with the NRF2 activator TAT. Over-expression of ADH4 reduced the intracellular and secreted protein levels of CSF1 in HepG2.2.15 (Fig. 7D, G) and Huh7 (Fig. 7E, H) cells, and this impact was counteracted by TAT treatment. In contrast, the enhancement of intracellular and secreted protein levels of CSF1 induced by ADH4 inhibition was reversed by brusatol treatment in HepG2 (Fig. 7F, I). Additionally, flow cytometric evaluation of cells labelled with antibodies that are unique to human CD11b (a marker for macrophages) and CD206 (a marker for M2-type macrophages) corroborated that overexpression of ADH4 decreased the ability of HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells to initiate M2 polarization, which was further counteracted by NRF2 activation (Fig. 7J, K). Contrarily, the potential of HepG2 cells to induce M2 polarization increased upon ADH4 downregulation and this effect was reversed upon inhibition of NRF2 (see Fig. 7L). All in all, this evidence confirms that ADH4 suppresses M2 macrophage mediated by CSF1.

ADH4 inhibits CSF1-mediated tumour progression is NRF2-dependent

To investigate whether ADH4 inhibits the development of hepatocellular carcinoma through the NRF2/CSF1 axis, we conducted in vitro and in vivo experiments to assess cell proliferation and tumour growth. We specifically examined the effects of overexpressing CSF1 in LV-ADH4 with Brusatol treatment and knocking down CSF1 in sh-ADH4 with TAT treatment in several hepatoma cells. The inhibition of cell proliferation in LV-ADH4 Huh7 and HepG2.2.15 was rescued by the overexpression of CSF1, which was then reversed by treatment with the NRF2 inhibitor Brusatol. This was demonstrated by CCK8 (Fig. 8A, Fig. S6A), colony formation assays (Fig. 8B, Fig. S6C), and EdU incorporation (Fig. 8C, Fig. S6E). The knockdown of CSF1 rescued the promotion of cell proliferation in sh-ADH4 HepG2. The effect was reversed by the treatment of the NRF2 activator TAT, as demonstrated by CCK8 (Fig. S6B), colony formation assays (Fig. S6D), and EdU incorporation (Fig. S6F). To further verify the tumour suppressing responsibilities of ADH4 via the NRF2/CSF1 axis in vivo, a Huh7 cell suspension was injected subcutaneously into BALB/c nude mice. Consistent with previous in vitro observations, the overexpression of CSF1 rescued the significant suppression of tumour growth in LV-ADH4 Huh7. This effect was then reversed by treatment with the NRF2 inhibitor Brusatol, as demonstrated by tumour size (Fig. 8D), tumour volume (Fig. 8E), and tumour weight (Fig. 8F). The proliferation marker Ki67 was used for authentication through IHC staining (Fig. 8G). The results indicate that ADH4 inhibits the proliferation of hepatocellular carcinoma cells and the growth of tumours through the NRF2/CSF1 axis.

Discussion

Chronic HBV infection continues to pose a significant health challenge with roughly 350 million individuals affected globally and at a heightened risk of developing hepatocellular carcinoma, cirrhosis, and hepatitis [17]. Typically, hepatocellular carcinoma emerges against a backdrop of long-standing liver inflammation [18].Although HBV is not cytopathic, hepatocellular carcinoma incidence increases with viral load and duration of infection [19], indicating an accumulated risk of chronic inflammation-induced tumourigenesis. Moreover, the accumulation and expression of HBV antigens in host cells may trigger a cascade of metabolic alterations, including abnormal lipid metabolism, ultimately disrupting the cellular metabolic homeostasis [20] as the liver plays a crucial role in fatty acid metabolism. However, the mechanisms behind the correlation between FAM and inflammatory pathogenesis in hepatocellular carcinoma associated with HBV, and respective therapeutic approaches, are yet to be significantly explored.

In this study, we analysed ADH4 mRNA expression in five separate datasets of hepatocellular carcinoma patients from the GEO database, as well as measuring the levels of ADH4 mRNA and protein expression in our gathered hepatocellular carcinoma samples. Our results showed that ADH4 expression was notably decreased in hepatocellular carcinoma tumour tissues when compared to normal liver tissues, and was found to be a predictor of a negative prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma patients. Nonetheless, there is currently a lack of comprehensive understanding regarding the underlying mechanisms of ADH4.

ADH4 catalyses the NAD-dependent oxidation of all-trans-retinol or 9-cis-retinol [21]. Additionally, it oxidises long-chain omega-hydroxy fatty acids such as 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE) [22]. This, in turn, increases NADPH oxidase-derived ROS production [23], resulting in potential mediating of ischemia/reperfusion injury. ADH4 plays a crucial role in fatty acid oxidation metabolism, dependent on the coenzyme NAD+. It also has the ability to regulate NADPH and ROS generation [24]. To evaluate the correlation between ADH4 and lipid peroxidation, we conducted quantitative profiling of oxylipins. The results indicated that overexpression of ADH4 led to an overall decrease in oxylipin levels in the HBV-associated hepatoma cell line HepG2.2.15, with the level of the most abundant oxylipin, 5,6-EET, strikingly decreased. The findings demonstrate that overexpression of ADH4 results in a significant reduction in overall oxylipin levels in the HBV-associated hepatoma cell line HepG2.2.15, with the prominent oxylipin 5,6-EET level strikingly decreased. AA metabolism involves three enzymatic factors and pathways: cyclooxygenases (COXs), lipoxygenases (LOXs) and the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) system. These produce prostaglandins (PGs), leukotrienes (LTs), hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs) and four regioisomeric epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs; 5,6-EET, 8,9-EET, 11,12-EET, and 14,15-EET) [25]. Endothelial cells secrete EETs, which play a critical role in cellular proliferation, migration, and inflammation [26]. Inhibition of EET production is a promising therapeutic approach to protect against cancer development [27].

We investigated the intracellular NADP+/NADPH ratio and ROS levels in hepatoma cells. Our findings revealed that ADH4 inhibition raised the NADP+/NADPH ratio and ROS production. As expected, downregulating ADH4 caused an increase in NADP+/NADPH ratio and ROS levels. It is well-established that the transcription factor KEAP1 is highly susceptible to ROS, and the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway is critical for cellular antioxidant capacity. To further establish the connection between ADH4 and the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway, we measured the levels of typical NRF2 downstream target genes, and observed the movement of NRF2 into the nucleus, along with the interaction between KEAP1 and NRF2. As a consequence, ADH4 represses the expression rates of conventional NRF2 downstream target genes (HO-1 and NQO1) and the movement of NRF2 into the nucleus, and may activate the deterioration of NRF2 by strengthening the interaction between KEAP1 and NRF2.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the mechanism of ADH4 in hepatocellular carcinoma associated with HBV, we conducted an mRNA-seq analysis of ADH4-overexpressing and control HepG2.2.15 cells. We identified a total of 782 dysregulated genes which were predominantly linked to the cytokine‒cytokine receptor interaction pathway. Within this pathway, the expression level of CSF1 was observed to be notably decreased in ADH4-overexpressing HepG2.21.5 cells compared to control HepG2.2.15 cells, where it exhibited the highest expression level. This discovery suggests that ADH4 participates in the tumour immune microenvironment. The cytokine CSF1, or macrophage colony-stimulating factor, secreted by tumour cells is crucial in macrophage polarization, supporting the protumourigenic functions of TAMs [28, 29]. To investigate the role of ADH4 in controlling TAM polarization, we induced differentiation of THP1 cells into macrophages using PMA. Next, we co-cultured them with supernatants from different types of hepatoma cells to evaluate their ability to polarize into M2-type macrophages. Treatment with the NRF2 activator TAT led to an increase in intracellular and secreted protein levels of CSF1 in HepG2.2.15 and Huh7 cells overexpressing ADH4. Conversely, the effect was opposite in ADH4-knockdown HepG2 cells when treated with the NRF2 antagonist brusatol.

Limitations

The findings presented in this paper were derived from adequate samples, meticulous processes and suitable methodology. However, it must be acknowledged that this study is not without its limitations. First, it is now known that many chemical inhibitors of single metabolism enzymes were found to be ineffective and with several side effects when tested in clinical trials [30].Thus, more effective approaches can be found in a multi-target metabolic strategy. Second, validation in a larger patient population is needed. Third, the exact molecular mechanism between ADH4 and the control of oxidative stress is not known. In subsequent studies, we will conduct a series of experiments to probe this question in depth. Fourth, Huh7 and HepG2 cells are not necessarily a perfect recapitulation of primary HCC biology, especially for HBV-associated HCC. More cell models, including primary cell lines established from HBV-associated HCC patient samples, should be introduced in subsequent experiments. Finally, although the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway is implicated, additional experiments such as chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) or KEAP1 knockout experiments can lend more verification to the impact of ADH4 on NRF2 degradation.

Conclusions

Taken collectively, Our findings suggest that ADH4 may act as a critical node in metabolic regulation, contributing to the coordinated control of oxidative stress, cell survival, and tumour progression by raising levels of lipid peroxidation, the NADP+/NADPH ratio, and ROS levels, thereby causing activation of the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway, upregulation of CSF1 and M2-type TAM polarization (Fig. 8H).
