Antiplasmodial activity of pentyloxyamide-based histone deacetylase inhibitors against Plasmodium falciparum parasites
Authors: Wisam A. Dawood, Gillian M. Fisher, Josefa Kremeyer, Fabian Fischer, Jessica L. Home, Christopher D. Goodman, Kwong Sum Lam, Alexander G. Maier, Thomas Kurz, Katherine T. Andrews
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites and remains a significant health concern for almost half the world's population. There are estimated to be > 240 million malaria cases and approximately 600,000 malaria-related deaths annually, mainly due to infection with P. falciparum parasites. Parasite drug resistance is impacting malaria prevention and control efforts, and as part of the malaria eradication agenda, new drugs with novel mechanisms of action are needed. Histone/lysine deacetylase (HDAC) enzymes play essential roles in Plasmodium biology and are potential targets for the development of new antiplasmodial agents. In this study, a panel of 24 HDAC inhibitors with hydroxamic acid zinc binding group, a pentyloxyamide connecting unit linker region and substituted 4-phenyl and 4(pyridinyl)thiazole cap groups were investigated for in vitro activity against asexual intraerythrocytic stage P. falciparum parasites, the life cycle stage responsible for the clinical symptoms of malaria. The most potent compound (4o) had a P. falciparum IC50 of 20 nM and >250-fold greater selectivity for P. falciparum versus human cells. Compound 4o was also active against exoerythrocytic stage parasites (IC50 24 nM), which are a target for malaria prevention. In contrast, 4o lacked potent activity against late-stage gametocytes (IC50 > 2 μM), which are a target for malaria transmission-blocking drugs. Compound 4o and analogues caused in situ hyperacetylation of P. falciparum histone H4, indicating deacetylase inhibition. Furthermore, 4o was found to stabilise PfHDAC1 in P. falciparum protein lysates using solvent-induced protein stability Western blot assays with anti-PfHDAC1 antibody. Together, these data provide new structure-activity relationship and mechanistic insights on pentyloxyamide-based HDAC inhibitors as potential therapeutic starting points for malaria.

Introduction

Malaria is a parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium parasites and in 2023 there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 malaria-associated deaths, with highest morbidity and mortality occurring in sub-Saharan Africa due to infections with P. falciparum parasites (World Health Organization, 2024a). There are currently two vaccines recommended for use in children in high transmission areas (RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M (World Health Organization, 2024b)); however, neither provides full protection from malaria (Abdulla et al., 2013; Datoo et al., 2024). Drugs remain critical for malaria prevention and treatment; however, parasite resistance is impacting the effectiveness of all current medicines. This includes reduced efficacy of the gold standard artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), with instances of treatment failure reported in southeast Asia (Noedl et al., 2008; Ashley et al., 2014; Fairhurst and Dondorp, 2016; Kagoro et al., 2022) and Sub-Saharan Africa (Uwimana et al., 2020; Tumwebaze et al., 2022; van Loon et al., 2022; Hamilton et al., 2023; Rosenthal et al., 2024). Thus, elimination of malaria requires a comprehensive strategy that involves the development of additional tools, including new drugs with novel modes of action to current options, to address the challenge of parasite drug resistance (Siqueira-Neto et al., 2023; World Health Organization, 2024a).

Histone/lysine deacetylases (HDACs) are enzymes that work together with histone/lysine acetyltransferases (HATs) to reversibly modify the acetylation state of lysine residues on both histones (Fioravanti et al., 2020; Zhou and Cole, 2025) and non-histone proteins (Glozak et al., 2005; Narita et al., 2019). HDACs and HATs play an important role in essential processes in mammalian cells and other organisms (Allis and Jenuwein, 2016; Fioravanti et al., 2020; Maran et al., 2021) and alterations in the normal functions of these enzymes can contribute to neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer's disease (Park et al., 2022), autoimmune diseases (Mu et al., 2024), inflammation (Jasim et al., 2024), and cancer (Li et al., 2020). Six HDAC inhibitors are approved for clinical use by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) against cancers or other human diseases: vorinostat, belinostat, panobinostat and givinostat (which all contain a hydroxamic acid zinc binding group), the cyclic peptide romidepsin, and the carboxylic acid valproic acid (Bondarev et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2023; Mullard, 2024).

HDACs play a critical role in transcriptional control, developmental progression, and other essential pathways in Plasmodium parasites (Coleman et al., 2014; Fioravanti et al., 2020; Maran et al., 2021; Kanyal et al., 2024), making these enzymes prospective therapeutic targets for malaria. The P. falciparum genome contains five annotated HDACs, a HDAC pseudogene, and a HDAC-like protein (Kanyal et al., 2018). Three of the annotated PfHDACs (PfHDAC1, PfHDAC2/PfHDA1 and PfHDAC3/PfHDA2) are homologues of class I/II human HDACs and are reported to be essential to asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasites (Chaal et al., 2010; Coleman et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2018; Kanyal et al., 2024), the life cycle stage responsible for the clinical symptoms of malaria. PfSir2A and PfSir2B are homologues of human class III sirtuin family HDACs and, while not essential to asexual intraerythrocytic stage P. falciparum and therefore not suitable as classical drug targets, may play a role in virulence (Freitas-Junior et al., 2005; Aurrecoechea et al., 2009; Tonkin et al., 2009). PfHDAC1, PfHDAC2/PfHDA1, and PfHDAC3/PfHDA2 are expressed in asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasites, gametocytes and exoerythrocytic liver stage parasites (Joshi et al., 1999; Coleman et al., 2014; Zanghì et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018; Lindner et al., 2019). Compared to human HDACs, PfHDAC1 shares greatest homology to class I HDACs (∼40–57 % amino acid identity to human HDAC1, 2, 3 and 8) while PfHDAC2/PfHDA1 and PfHDAC3/PfHDA2 have only ∼16–21 % amino acid identity to all class I, II and IV human HDACs (Supplemental Table S1).

The structure of HDAC inhibitors generally includes a zinc-binding group (ZBG) that targets the catalytic zinc site in HDAC enzymes, a linker (that may be joined to a connecting unit), and a cap group that blocks access to the enzyme's catalytic domain tunnel (Fig. 1) (Jung et al., 1997). HDAC inhibitors with different ZBG moieties have been identified with in vitro activity against P. falciparum parasites, including cyclic tetrapeptides (Darkin-Rattray et al., 1996), benzamides (e.g., entinostat (Andrews et al., 2012b)), cyclic depsipeptides (e.g., romidepsin (Andrews et al., 2012b)), carboxylic acid derivatives (e.g., valproic acid (Andrews et al., 2008)) and hydroxamic acid-based compounds (e.g., vorinostat (Engel et al., 2015; Chua et al., 2017)). Hydroxamic acid-based compounds have been the most extensively studied against malaria parasites, with variations in the linker, connecting unit and cap group conferring differing levels of potency and parasite-specific selectivity compared to human cells. Examples of hydroxamate type HDAC inhibitors with low nanomolar activity against P. falciparum include derivates of quisinostat (e.g., JX211108 and JX35 with Pf3D7 IC50 3.6 nM and 1.3 nM, respectively (Huang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022)) and peptoid-based HDAC inhibitors (e.g., 1u and 2h with Pf3D7 IC50 4.0 nM and 5.2 nM, respectively (Diedrich et al., 2018; Mackwitz et al., 2019)).Fig. 1Structures of pentyloxyamide-based HDAC inhibitors with indole and substituted 4-phenylthiazole and 4-(pyridinyl)thiazole cap groups. One of the challenges in progressing HDAC inhibitors to pre-clinical testing for malaria is the need for compounds with a combination of both high in vitro potency (e.g., low nanomolar to picomolar 50 % inhibitory concentrations (IC50)) and low toxicity for normal human cells versus P. falciparum parasites (i.e., high Selectivity Index (SI) - human cell IC50/P. falciparum IC50), together with suitable pharmacokinetic profiles (Andrews et al., 2012a, 2012b; Kanyal et al., 2018). In previous work, we explored structure-activity relationships (SARs) of HDAC inhibitors with an alkoxyamide connecting unit linker region and different cap groups (Hansen et al., 2014a, 2014b; Alves Avelar et al., 2017; Dawood et al., 2024). We identified two HDAC inhibitors with indole cap groups with P. falciparum IC50 < 0.1 μM and SI > 200 (1 and 2; Fig. 1A) and showed that both compounds cause hyperacetylation of P. falciparum histone H4, indicative of HDAC inhibition (Dawood et al., 2024). To investigate whether further modifications to the cap group could yield more potent and selective compounds, in this study we investigated a panel of 24 hydroxamic acids with substituted 4-phenylthiazole and 4-(pyridinyl)thiazole cap groups and a pentyloxyamide connecting unit linker region, including compounds previously tested in leukemia models (Fischer et al., 2024) and new compounds. Compounds were tested for their asexual intraerythrocytic-stage antiplasmodial activity, selectivity for P. falciparum versus human cells, and for selected compounds, their ability to induce hyperacetylation of P. falciparum histone H4 as a marker of HDAC inhibition. The most potent and selective compound was further examined for the ability to stabilise PfHDAC1 in a solvent-induced protein stability assay and for activity against P. berghei exoerythrocytic liver stage parasites and sexual stage P. falciparum gametocytes.

Material and methods

Synthesis of pentyloxyamide-based HDAC inhibitors 4n-x

Compounds 4a-4m were synthesized as previously published (Fischer et al., 2024). The novel compounds 4n-x were synthesized starting with the saponification of 4-phenylthiazole and 4-(pyridinyl)thiazole carboxylic esters (with the R group shown in Table 1). The HBTU-mediated coupling reactions to construct the pentyloxyamide moiety were carried out using the in situ-generated sodium salts of the respective carboxylic acids (as the free acids of 1a−x were prone to decarboxylation) and a hydroxylamine linker (2) containing the O-trityl-protected hydroxamic acid zinc-binding group. Subsequent deprotection of the O-trityl-protected hydroxamates with TFA and triethylsilane yielded the hydroxamic acids 4n−x (Scheme 1). Compound source and storage

Chloroquine (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) and vorinostat (Selleck Chem, USA) were used as controls. All HDAC inhibitors and control compounds were stored at −20 °C as 20 mM stocks in DMSO, except for chloroquine which was stored at 20 mM in phosphate buffered saline (PBS).

In vitro P. falciparum proliferation assays

Asexual intraerythrocytic stage P. falciparum 3D7 parasites were cultured in vitro in O+ human erythrocytes and RPMI 1640 media (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated human serum, 50 μg/mL hypoxanthine (Sigma–Aldrich, USA), and 5 μg/mL gentamycin (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C in a controlled 5 % CO2, 5 % O2, and N2 gas environment, modified from the method first described by Trager and Jensen (1976). Blood and sera were sourced from the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood under a research agreement. Compound activity was tested using [3H]-hypoxanthine uptake assays in 96 well plates, as previously described (Andrews et al., 2008), using synchronized ring-stage P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes at 0.25 % parasitemia and 2.5 % hematocrit. A final concentration of 0.5 % DMSO vehicle (non-toxic) was included in all wells. Following incubation for 48 h at 37 °C with serial dilutions of test compounds or controls, [3H]-hypoxanthine (0.25 μCi/well, PerkinElmer, USA) was added, and cells incubated for an additional 24 h before freezing at −80 °C to stop the assay. Following thawing, nucleic acids were collected onto Glass Microfibre paper mats (Filtech, Australia) using a Microbeta Filtermat-96 Cell Harvester (PerkinElmer, USA). Mats were air dried before sealing into plastic bags with BetaPlate Scint (PerkinElmer, USA) for analysis on a 1450 MicroBeta scintillation counter (PerkinElmer, USA). Three independent experiments were performed, with all samples tested in triplicate wells. Percentage proliferation inhibition was determined by comparing average corrected counts per minute (CCPM) for triplicate wells containing compounds, minus uninfected erythrocyte background, versus DMSO vehicle control wells. IC50 values (concentration required to inhibit 50 % of parasite proliferation) were determined by log-linear interpolation (Huber and Koella, 1993).

Human cell toxicity assays

Toxicity against human cells was assessed using neonatal foreskin fibroblasts (NFFs) in a sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay, as described previously (Skehan et al., 1990). NFFs were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies, USA) and 10 % fetal calf serum (Gibco, USA), seeded in 96-well plates (5000 cells/well; 100 μL/well) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5 % CO2, followed by exposure to serial dilutions of test and control compounds for 72 h. Chloroquine served as a positive control. Cells were then washed with prewarmed PBS, fixed in methylated spirits for 15 min, and stained with 0.4 % SRB (Sigma Aldrich, USA) in 1 % acetic acid for 1 h at RT. Excess SRB was removed by washing three times with 1 % acetic acid (100 μL/well), then 10 mM Tris Base added (100 μL/well) and plates incubated for 1 h at RT. Absorbance was read at 540 nm using a Synergy 2 plate reader (BioTek, USA). Three independent biological assays (each performed in triplicate wells) were carried out. Growth inhibition was calculated with DMSO-treated cells as the 100 % growth control, and IC50 values determined using log-linear interpolation (Huber and Koella, 1993). Selectivity indices (SI) were calculated as NFF IC50/Pf3D7 IC50.

Protein hyperacetylation assays

Protein hyperacetylation assays were conducted by incubating synchronous trophozoite stage P. falciparum 3D7 parasitized erythrocytes (3–5 % parasitemia, 5 % hematocrit) with 5x IC50 concentrations of compounds for 3 h. The HDAC inhibitor vorinostat was used as a positive control and the antimalarial drug chloroquine as a negative control. Samples treated with DMSO vehicle (0.08 %) taken at 0 h and 3 h, served as negative controls. Protein lysates were prepared by lysing infected erythrocytes for 5 min using 0.1 % saponin in PBS on ice, centrifuging at 4 °C for 20 min at 4000 rpm using a Megafuge 8R (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and washing the parasite pellet 3–4 times with ice-cold PBS. The parasite pellet was resuspended in 1x SDS loading dye, denatured at 94 °C for 3 min and the sample passed through a Tuberculin needle 3–4 times to shear nucleic acid. Samples were separated via 15 % SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore, USA) and total protein detected by staining with REVERT™ Total Protein Stain (Li-Cor Biosciences, USA). Membranes were blocked for 1 h with Intercept® (PBS) Blocking Buffer (Li-Cor Biosciences, USA) and Western blot performed using anti-(tetra)acetyl histone H4 antibody (1:2000 dilution; cat# 06–866, Millipore, USA) as primary antibody and IRDye® 680RD goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution; Li-Cor Biosciences, USA). Membranes were imaged using a Li-Cor Odyssey FC imager (Li-Cor Biosciences, USA) and densitometry analyses conducted using Image Studio Lite v5.2. PVDF membranes were then stripped at room temperature for 7 min in 1M sodium hydroxide, washed in PBS-Tween (0.1 %) and re-imaged to confirm removal of antibodies. Membranes were re-probed with anti-PfRAP2 monoclonal antibody (6C1; 1:2000 dilution) (Chavchich et al., 2010) and IRDye® 680RD donkey anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution; Li-Cor Biosciences, USA) and imaged at 680 nm, as described above. Three independent assays were completed (Supplemental Fig. S1 and S2 and S3) and statistical analysis was performed using a Student's t-test comparing the ∼11 kDa histone H4 bands, as well as the combined histone bands (histone H4 plus two bands corresponding to the H2B/H2Bv (∼13–14 kDa) and H2A.Z (∼16 kDa)), each normalized to the 3 h DMSO control. Both H4 and total histones were used as HDAC inhibitors could potentially alter the acetylation state of different histones, including those to which this antibody cross-reacts.

Solvent stability profiling via Western blot

Solvent stability profiling was conducted using lysates from synchronous trophozoite-stage parasitized erythrocytes at 5 % parasitemia and 5 % hematocrit. Protein lysates were saponin lysed as described in Section 2.5 and resuspended in 10 pellet volumes of lysis buffer (0.4 % NP40 (IGEPAL CA-630, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1x PBS with 1x cOmplete™, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich)). Samples were subjected to three freeze/thaw cycles (3 min each) before being sheared with a 27½ G needle and centrifuged at 16,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C to collect solubilized protein. For solvent challenge, solubilized protein lysates were divided into aliquots and subjected to treatment at RT for 10 min with 1,000 × IC50 concentrations of 4o (20 μM; Table 1), vorinostat as a positive HDAC inhibitor control (120 μM) or the antimalarial drug chloroquine as a negative control (10 μM). A concentration of 1,000 × IC50 was selected based on previously published data for this type of assay (Dans et al., 2024). DMSO vehicle (final 0.5 %) served as a no-compound control. Each sample was then split into two; one aliquot was treated with a mixture of acetone/ethanol/acetic acid (50:50:0.1 vol/vol/vol; AEA) to give a final concentration of 10 % solvent and the second aliquot with an equal volume of 1x PBS as a control. A solvent concentration of 10 % was selected based on dose-response solvent stability assays conducted using vorinostat versus DMSO vehicle only (Supplemental Fig. S4). Samples were incubated for 20 min at 37 °C with mixing at 800 rpm using a Thermomixer (Eppendorf), then centrifuged at 16,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed from all samples, mixed with 6x SDS loading dye (final concentration of 5:1 sample to dye) and denatured at 94 °C for 3 min. Samples were separated via 12 % SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore, USA) and total protein detected by staining with REVERT™ Total Protein Stain (Li-Cor Biosciences, USA). Membranes were blocked for 1 h with Intercept® (PBS) Blocking Buffer (Li-Cor Biosciences, USA) and Western blot performed using anti-PfHDAC1 as primary antibody (Engel et al., 2019) (1:5000 dilution; purified from rabbit serum using Pierce™ Protein A IgG Purification Kit, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and IRDye® 680RD goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution; Li-Cor Biosciences, USA). Membranes were imaged using a Li-Cor Odyssey FC imager (Li-Cor Biosciences, USA) and densitometry analyses conducted using Image Studio Lite v5.2. Analysis was performed by normalizing the density of proteins in the 10 % solvent-treated samples to the DMSO no-solvent control. Four independent experiments (Supplemental Fig. S5) were performed and the mean normalized density (±SD) in the 10 % solvent treated versus the 10 % solvent DMSO samples calculated. Statistical analysis was performed using a Student's t-test (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01).

Exoerythrocytic liver stage assays

In vitro exoerythrocytic liver stage efficacy of 4o was established by bioluminescence imaging, adapted as previously described (Annoura et al., 2013). P. berghei ANKA GFP-Luccon (RMgm-29) (Janse et al., 2006) sporozoites were dissected from the salivary glands of infected Anopheles stephensi mosquitoes. A total of 20,000 sporozoites were allowed to invade 1x104 HC-04 cells grown in IMDM (Gibco) supplemented with 5 % heated inactivated FBS, 1 % v/v 1x Glutamax (Gibco) and 1 % v/v 100x Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco) for 2 h. Following medium change, compounds were added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C and 6 % CO2 for 50 h before being washed with 1x PBS and stored at −80 °C for at least 30 min. Quantification of liver cell infection was performed using Luciferase Assay System Kit (Promega) and relative luminescence units were obtained on microplate reader (CLARIOstar PLUS, BMG Labtech). Compound 4o was tested in three independent experiments, in at least triplicate wells. The antimalarial drug atovaquone (0.016 μM; Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a control. The percentage inhibition of growth was compared to that of matched vehicle (0.001 % DMSO)-treated parasites and IC50 was determined by variable slope (four parameters) using GraphPad Prism®.

In vitro gametocyte inhibition assays

Asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasites from a gametocyte-producing 3D7 clone were induced to form gametocytes, as previously described (Fraser et al., 2024). These parasites have been cycled through the mosquito and human host and then expanded and cryopreserved to select suitable gametocyte production. Parasites were cultured in vitro in O+ human erythrocytes and RPMI 1640-HEPES with Glutamax (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 2.5 % vol/vol heat-inactivated human serum, 0.375 % (w/v) AlbuMAX™ II (Gibco, USA), 480 μM hypoxanthine (Sigma–Aldrich, USA), 20 μg/mL gentamicin (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) and 10 mM D-glucose (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C in a controlled 94 % N2, 5 % CO2 and 1 % O2 gas environment. Human blood cells and serum were provided by the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood. To induce gametocytes, synchronized trophozoite-stage parasites at 2 % parasitemia and 4 % hematocrit were incubated overnight and “stressed” by replacing only 1/4 of the culture medium. Two culture volumes of complete culture media were replenished the next day, with the hematocrit maintained at 4 %. On the subsequent day (designated as day 0) of gametocytogenesis, sorbitol synchronization was first performed to remove asynchronous trophozoites. The remaining trophozoite-stage parasites and mature gametocytes were removed by magnet purification (MACS® D column on SuperMACS™ II Separator). Sorbitol treatment was performed the next day to further remove non-committed trophozoite-stage parasites. The culture medium was changed daily from day 1 onwards and supplemented with 50 mM N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (Biosynth, Switzerland) to inhibit asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasite proliferation. Two additional sorbitol treatments were performed on day 7 and 8. Stage IV-V gametocytes were magnetically enriched on day 8 and eluted in culture media with 50 mM N-acetyl-D-glucosamine. Day 8 gametocytes were selected, as previously published (Ridgway et al., 2020). Active mitochondrial metabolism in all gametocyte stages has previously been shown (MacRae et al., 2013; Ramesh et al., 2023). The purity of eluted gametocyte was assessed by examination under a light microscope. Gametocyte viability was evaluated as previously described (Ridgway et al., 2020) with modifications. Compound was serially diluted across 96 well plates in culture media supplemented with 50 mM N-acetyl-D-glucosamine. 0.1 % (v/v) DMSO and 100 μM primaquine (Cayman Chemical, USA) were used as vehicle and positive control, respectively. Stage IV-V magnet-enriched gametocytes were added to each well, with a final volume of 200 μL per well. Following incubation for 72 h at 37 °C in a controlled 94 % N2, 5 % CO2 and 1 % O2 gas environment, gametocytes were stained with 500 nM MitoTracker™ Deep Red FM (Invitrogen, USA) and 5 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, USA) in PBS with 10 mM D-glucose for 30 min at 37 °C in the mentioned gas environment. Stained cells were then washed twice in PBS with 10 mM D-glucose and analyzed on a LSR II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). APC-Cy7 channel (644-nm excitation/665-nm emission) and Pacific Blue channel (405-nm excitation/461-nm emission) were used to detect MitoTracker™ Deep Red FM and Hoechst 33342 fluorescence, respectively. Live gametocyte populations were gated and quantified with FlowJo 10 on positive MitoTracker™ Deep Red FM and Hoechst 33342 fluorescence after the exclusion of debris, doublets and uninfected red blood cells. Gametocyte viability was expressed as a percentage of positive events in vehicle control (100 % viability) normalized to the positive control (background fluorescence). Data was graphed as a four-parameter [inhibitor] versus response curve in GraphPad Prism®.

Results and discussion

Antiplasmodial structure activity relationship (SAR) analysis

A series of 24 hydroxamic acid-based HDAC inhibitors containing a pentyloxyamide connecting unit linker region and substituted 4-phenylthiazole and 4-(pyridinyl)thiazole cap groups (Fig. 1B) were tested for in vitro activity against asexual intraerythrocytic stage P. falciparum parasites. The most potent compound within this series was 4o (Pf3D7 IC50 0.02 μM (±0.001 μM)) with a 4-(4-hydroxyphenyl)thiazole cap group (Table 1). Compound 4o also demonstrated the greatest selectivity towards Plasmodium versus human cells (SI 257; Table 1). This profile is ∼3-4-fold improvement in potency compared to previously identified pentyloxyamide-based compounds 1 (Pf3D7 IC50 0.07 μM; P ≤ 0.001) and 2 (Pf3D7 IC50 0.09 μM; P ≤ 0.001) with a similar selectivity for P. falciparum versus human cells (SI 207 and 219, respectively) (Fig. 1A (Dawood et al., 2024)). Compound 4d with a 4-(4-methoxyphenyl)thiazole and 4m with a 4-(4-(pyrrolidine-1yl)phenyl)thiazole cap group also had IC50 values < 0.1 μM (Pf3D7 IC50 0.05 μM (±0.002 μM) and 0.08 μM (±0.01 μM), respectively), however both were >25-fold less selective for P. falciparum cells (4d SI 9; 4m SI 8) compared to 4o (Table 1) or the indole analogues (Fig. 1) (Dawood et al., 2024). While less potent with respect to antiplasmodial activity, the 4-(4-trifluoromethyl)phenyl substituted derivative 4k (Pf3D7 IC50 0.20 μM (±0.02 μM)), the pyridine-4-yl analogue 4u (Pf3D7 IC50 0.34 μM (±0.09 μM)) and the pyridine-3-yl analogue 4t (Pf3D7 IC50 0.61 μM (±0.19 μM)) exhibited better selectivity (SI 52–109) than 4d and 4m (Table 1). Compounds 4s, 4v and 4w were the only compounds with Pf3D7 IC50 > 1 μM (Table 1). Compounds 4v (Pf3D7 IC50 2.52 μM (±0.34 μM)) and 4w (Pf3D7 IC50 2.55 μM (±0.29 μM)) with ortho- and meta-substituted nitrophenyl groups attached to position 4 of the thiazole ring respectively, were >10-fold less potent than 4x with a para-substituted nitrophenyl substituents at the thiazole ring (Pf3D7 IC50 0.24 μM (±0.04 μM)), however all had similarly poor selectivity indices (SI 6, 5 and 15, respectively). A similar trend was observed for compounds 4s (Pf3D7 IC50 1.41 μM (±0.21 μM)), 4t (Pf3D7 IC50 0.61 μM (±0.19 μM)), and 4u (Pf3D7 IC50 0.34 μM (±0.09 μM)), which feature pyridine-containing cap groups. The trend of para-substitution leading to greater antiplasmodial potency than meta-substitution, with the lowest potency observed for ortho-substitution, was consistent across all HDAC inhibitors examined in this study. A common structural feature of the most active compounds (4d, 4m, 4o) is the presence of electron-donating substituents (hydroxy, methoxy, pyrrolidine) at position 4 of the phenyl moiety in the cap group. In contrast, strong electron-withdrawing groups, such as nitro or trifluoromethyl, on the phenyl moiety significantly reduce antiplasmodial activity, particularly with meta and ortho substitutions. However, due to the lack of PfHDAC crystal structures, determining why HDAC inhibitors with electron donating para-substituted phenylthiazole cap groups exhibit enhanced potency is not possible. With respect to the linker-connecting unit, previous SAR analyses have shown that HDAC inhibitors with a linker length of five or six atoms exhibited the highest potency, whereas seven-carbon linkers resulted in significantly reduced activity regardless of modifications to the capping group and ZBG (Patil et al., 2010; Hansen et al., 2014a). All the pentyloxyamides examined in this study contain a five-carbon linker because the oxygen atom belongs to the connecting unit, which serves as a carboxamide bioisostere.

When we conducted binding interaction studies of 4o using the predicted model of PfHDAC1 generated using AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., 2021) (Supplemental Fig. S6), we found that the hydroxamate group coordinates to the catalytic zinc ion in the active site (zinc ion coordinated by Asp174, His176 and Asp262) and forms predicted hydrogen bonds with the side chain of His138 and Tyr301 (acting as a proton donor), and the backbone of Gly147. The linker region of the compound, positioned within the active site tunnel, was predicted to have hydrophobic interactions with Phe148, Phe203, and Leu269 (Supplemental Fig. S6). As described in previous studies (Chua et al., 2017, 2021), PfHDAC1 possesses a longer loop preceding Asp97 (comprising residues Ala95 and Thr96) which is absent in human HDAC1 and HDAC2. Notably, the amide group of the connecting unit of 4o was predicted to form hydrogen bonds with Asp97, while the hydroxy moiety of the cap group was predicted to engage in hydrogen bonding (however relatively weak due to the distance between the interactions) with the backbone of Ala95 (Supplemental Fig. S6). These binding interactions are consistent with previous studies for other hydroxamate type HDAC inhibitors (Andrews et al., 2008; Sumanadasa et al., 2012; Elbadawi et al., 2015; Chua et al., 2017, 2021; Huang et al., 2020) and provide insights into the predicted binding interactions of the 4o pharmacophore within PfHDAC1. However, the limitations of in silico modelling versus binding in solved crystal structures (Scardino et al., 2023; Terwilliger et al., 2024) must be kept in mind.

Overall, our data suggest that future work could focus on further improvement of the most active and selective compound 4o (Pf3D7 IC50 0.02 μM, SI 257) by introducing additional substituents (e.g., halogen substituents) on the 4-hydroxyphenyl moiety and by using phenol bioisosteres.

P. falciparum histone H4 hyperacetylation assays

The effect of eight compounds, each with Pf3D7 IC50 < 0.1 μM and SI > 10 (Table 1), on P. falciparum histone H4 lysine acetylation was investigated using in situ hyperacetylation assays. Trophozoite-stage infected erythrocytes were exposed to 5x PfIC50 concentrations of compounds for 3 h followed by preparation of protein lysates and Western blot using an anti-(tetra)acetyl-H4 antibody (Fig. 2; Supplemental Fig. S1) and, following stripping of the membranes, re-probing with a monoclonal antibody that recognizes rhoptry associated protein 2 (PfRAP2 (Chavchich et al., 2010)) (Supplemental Fig. S2 and S3). All eight compounds caused a ∼2.5–4.0-fold increase in the density of an ∼11 kDa band corresponding histone H4 compared to DMSO vehicle control samples when normalized to total protein (Fig. 2) or PfRAP2 (Supplemental Fig. S2 and S3). As expected (Sumanadasa et al., 2012; Chua et al., 2021), the HDAC inhibitor control vorinostat caused a ∼3-4-fold increase in the signal of this protein species (Fig. 2; Supplemental Fig. S3). As previous studies have shown that the anti-(tetra)acetyl-H4 antibody cross-reacts to H2B/H2Bv (∼13–14 kDa) and H2A.Z (∼16 kDa) proteins in P. falciparum (Miao et al., 2006; Dow et al., 2008; Chua et al., 2017; Dawood et al., 2024), the density of these bands were also examined, with data demonstrating a ∼2.5–4.0-fold increase in total acetylation signal compared to controls for vorinostat and the eight pentyloxyamide-based HDAC inhibitors (Fig. 2; Supplemental Fig. S3). PfHDAC1 solvent stability profiling

The absence of highly purified recombinant PfHDAC1-3 proteins (Ontoria et al., 2016) is a limitation in determining PfHDAC-isoform selectivity. To address this for PfHDAC1, where an antibody is available and has been confirmed to pull-down PfHDAC1 in proteomics analysis (Engel et al., 2019), a solvent stability profiling assay was developed as a method to test whether PfHDAC1 is stabilized in the presence of 4o, the most potent and selective of the compounds examined in this study. This strategy has previously been employed for P. falciparum to detect the stabilization of the lipid transfer protein PfSTART1 when treated with the aryl acetamide compound W-991 (10 μM) (Dans et al., 2024). To establish solvent stability conditions for PfHDAC1, assays were first performed comparing the density of the PfHDAC1 protein in Western blots of samples treated with DMSO vehicle and the control HDAC inhibitor vorinostat at 5 %, 10 % and 15 % acetone/ethanol/acetic acid (50:50:0.1 vol/vol/vol, AEA) solvent. Data demonstrated that at 10 % and 15 % AEA solvent, the DMSO control signal was reduced by >80 % compared to the 5 % AEA samples, indicating PfHDAC1 destabilization. However, at 10 % AEA solvent, vorinostat was found to stabilise the PfHDAC1 signal (Supplemental Fig. S4). When 4o was assessed in this assay at 0 % and 10 % solvent, a ∼3-fold-change in mean relative density was observed for the ∼51 kDa band corresponding to PfHDAC1 compared to DMSO vehicle or chloroquine lanes (Fig. 3). As expected (Supplemental Fig. S5), vorinostat gave a similar result to 4o. No stabilization effect was observed for chloroquine. Plasmodium exoerythrocytic liver and gametocyte stage activity of 4o

The compound with the most potent and selective asexual intraerythrocytic stage activity, 4o (Table 1), was selected for further investigation against P. berghei exoerythrocytic stage parasites (PbEEF) and P. falciparum stage IV-V gametocytes (PfLSG IV-V). Conclusions

In this study, a series of 24 hydroxamic acid-based HDAC inhibitors with a pentyloxyamide connecting unit linker region and substituted 4-phenylthiazole or 4-(pyridinyl)thiazole cap groups were investigated for their antiplasmodial activity and parasite-specific selectivity compared to human cells. Compounds 4d, 4m and 4o (Pf3D7 IC50 0.05 μM, 0.08 μM, and 0.02 μM, respectively; Table 1) with 4-(4-hydroxyphenyl)thiazole, 4-(4-methoxyphenyl)thiazole and 4-(4-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)phenyl)thiazole cap groups, respectively, were the most potent compounds. Compound 4o was also the most selective (SI 257), making it the only compound to fulfill “early-lead” criteria for antiplasmodial potency (IC50 < 0.1 μM) and selectivity (SI > 100), as defined by the Medicines for Malaria Venture in their Target Candidate Profile 1 (TCP-1) criteria for new treatment drug leads (Burrows et al., 2017). While recombinant PfHDAC2 and PfHDAC3 are not currently available, and PfHDAC1 material has low purity (Ontoria et al., 2016), which means that enzymatic inhibition assays cannot be performed, 4o and selected analogues were found to cause hyperacetylation of P. falciparum histone H4, indicating an effect on HDAC function (Fig. 2). Furthermore, 4o was shown to stabilise PfHDAC1 in P. falciparum protein lysates using a solvent-induced protein stability Western blot assay using anti-PfHDAC1 (Fig. 3), the first time that this approach has been used to investigate HDAC inhibitor action against a P. falciparum HDAC. These data indicate that 4o binds either directly to PfHDAC1 to stabilise this protein in the presence of solvent, or that there is an indirect stabilization effect due to 4o binding to PfHDAC1-associated protein/s.

In addition to the development of new antimalarial treatment drugs which target asexual intraerythrocytic stage Plasmodium (TCP-1), new drugs are also needed that target different developmental forms. This includes for chemoprevention, by targeting exoerythrocytic (liver) forms before blood stage infection occurs, and to block transmission of gametocytes to the mosquito vector to prevent onward transmission to new hosts following sexual reproduction. These types of drugs fall under the Medicines for Malaria Venture TCP-4 “Molecules with activity against hepatic schizonts” and TCP-5 “Molecules that block transmission (targeting parasite gametocytes)”, respectively (https://www.mmv.org/ (Burrows et al., 2017; Medicines for Malaria Venture, 2018)). As some hydroxamic acid-based HDAC inhibitors have been shown to have activity against exoerythrocytic stage parasites and/or gametocytes (Huang et al., 2020; Vanheer et al., 2020; Chua et al., 2021), compound 4o was also tested against these life cycle stages. Compound 4o has a similar potency against P. berghei exoerythrocytic liver stage parasites (PbEEF IC50 0.024 μM; Fig. 4A) as against asexual intraerythrocytic stage parasites but lacked potent activity against stage IV-V gametocytes (PfLSG IV-V IC50 > 2 μM; Fig. 4B). While these data suggest that 4o does not impact late stage gametocytes, future studies may be of interest to test effects on male gametocyte exflagellation (Delves et al., 2016)) and, if warranted, mosquito feeding and infection models (Collins et al., 2018). While the level of potency and selectivity of 4o does not warrant in vivo investigation in a murine malaria model, together, the data presented in this study provide new SAR and mechanistic insights on pentyloxyamide-based HDAC inhibitors with dual asexual intraerythrocytic and exoerythrocytic stage activity to inform further investigation of HDAC inhibitors for malaria.
