Black Phosphorus Molybdenum Disulfide Midwave Infrared Photodiodes with Broadband Absorption-Increasing Metasurfaces
Authors: Max R. Lien, Nan Wang, Silvia Guadagnini, Jiangbin Wu, Alexander Soibel, Sarath D. Gunapala, Han Wang, Michelle L. Povinelli
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Black phosphorus (BP) has been established as a promising material for room temperature midwave infrared (MWIR) photodetectors. However, many of its attractive optoelectronic properties are often observable only at smaller film thicknesses, which inhibits photodetector absorption and performance. In this work, we show that metasurface gratings increase the absorption of BP-MoS2 heterojunction photodiodes over a broad range of wavelengths in the MWIR. We designed, fabricated, and characterized metasurface gratings that increase absorption at selected wavelengths or broad spectral ranges. We evaluated the broadband metasurfaces by measuring the room temperature responsivity and specific detectivity of BP-MoS2 photodiodes at multiple MWIR wavelengths. Our results show that broadband metasurface gratings are a scalable approach for boosting the performance of BP photodiodes over large spectral ranges.

Recently, black phosphorus (BP) has been identified as a promising material for midwave infrared (MWIR, 3–5 μm) photodetection.1 Due to their high detectivity at room temperature (D* values exceeding 1010 cm Hz1/2 W–1) and ease of integration with many substrates,2−8 MWIR BP photodetectors have significant potential to benefit both civilian and defense applications. BP is an anisotropic 2D layered material that consists of planar sheets of phosphorus atoms held together by van der Waals forces.9,10 It exhibits a direct bandgap of 4.1 μm or 300 meV in its bulk form and a high carrier mobility of ∼1000 cm2 V–1 s–1.10,11 Additionally, its bandgap is statically tunable by arsenic alloying and absorber thickness and dynamically tunable with electric displacement fields.12−17 However, these attractive materials properties are often observable only at smaller film thicknesses, which reduces photodetector absorption and performance.18,19

To address this challenge, a variety of methods have been successfully tested with BP photodetectors to enable high performance devices. Device architectures that enable photogating and gate voltage tuning effects can dramatically increase the photoresponse of BP.6,14 Furthermore, integrated nanostructures such as dielectric waveguides, plasmonic apertures and gratings, and Fabry–Pérot cavities have experimentally shown to increase performance in BP devices.20−28 These nanostructures are often designed to boost photodetection around specific wavelengths, such as telecom bands, but many applications require a high photoresponse across large ranges of wavelengths.

We integrated BP–MoS2 photodiodes with metal–insulator–metal (MIM) metasurface gratings that have absorption-increasing capabilities that are expandable to large wavelength ranges. Previously, we showed that an MIM grating enhanced the performance of a BP photoconductor.25 But the MIM increased responsivity only near a select wavelength, and the BP photoconductor’s architecture resulted in moderate performance. Incorporating a transition metal dichalcogenide MoS2 layer with BP results in a more robust heterostructure photodetection mechanism. The BP–MoS2 interface, in which BP is a p-type MWIR absorber and MoS2 is an n-type hole barrier, creates a pn heterojunction that is capable of room temperature photodetection in the MWIR.7,29−35 The MoS2 also serves as a transparent optical window and a passivation layer that protects the BP from oxidation.36,37 In this work, we first show that integrating an MIM with a BP–MoS2 photodiode increases the absorption in the MWIR around a selected wavelength. Then, we adapt the MIM’s structure to support multiple resonances that expand the range of wavelengths in which absorption is increased. Finally, we evaluate broadband MIMs by measuring BP–MoS2 photodiode performance at multiple testing wavelengths in the MWIR. To our knowledge, we present the first demonstration of a BP–MoS2 photodiode with an integrated nanostructure that boosts absorption across a wide spectral range.

Our study begins with the fabrication of BP–MoS2 photodiodes. A schematic of a BP–MoS2 photodiode is shown in Figure 1a. The photodiode consists of a 50 nm thick Au back reflector, a BP layer with thickness TBP, a MoS2 layer with thickness TMo, and 50 nm thick Ti/Au contacts. The Au back-reflector and Ti/Au contacts support a vertical bias voltage VDS. Figure 1b is a scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of a BP–MoS2 photodiode. The photodiode was fabricated onto a silicon substrate with 300 nm of thermal oxide, which was cleaned with piranha solution and O2 ashing. The Au back-reflector was deposited with e-beam evaporation (CHA Industries) before transferring the BP and MoS2 onto the substrate. The heterostructure layers were created by mechanically exfoliating BP and MoS2 samples from bulk crystals (procured from HQ Graphene) and transferring them onto the substrate with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamps. We performed the exfoliation and transfer processes in an argon-purged glovebox to minimize oxidation and exposure to water vapor.37 For each device in this study, we selected BP samples with thicknesses around 80 nm and MoS2 samples with thicknesses of around 11 nm. The thicknesses of the samples were estimated with optical images due to their thickness-dependent color and later confirmed with atomic force microscopy (Bruker).38 Once the BP–MoS2 interface was transferred onto the substrate, we identified the orthogonal axes of BP and applied the Ti/Au contacts with electron beam lithography (Raith) and electron-beam evaporation (CHA Industries).

We determined the direction of BP’s orthogonal axes with a Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Bruker) and a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw). BP is an anisotropic material with high and low absorption axes that are respectively labeled armchair (AC) and zigzag (ZZ) (the nomenclature AC and ZZ are based on its orthorhombic crystal structure).10Figure 1c shows the absorption spectra of the device in Figure 1b along its AC and ZZ axes. The device has layer thicknesses TBP = 83.1 and TMo = 11.7 nm, and the two absorption spectra in Figure 1c were measured with FTIR and linearly polarized light along the AC or ZZ axes. The angle of linear polarization θ is shown in Figure 1b, and absorption is estimated with A = 1 – R – T (T = 0 because no light transmits through the Au back-reflector). From these spectra, it is apparent that absorption is higher along the AC axis than along the ZZ axis. In addition to the FTIR measurements, we include the Raman spectra of the device as the inset of Figure 1c. The Raman spectra were measured with 532 nm excitation linearly polarized along the AC or ZZ axis. We found that for our samples the Ag2 peak is maximized along the AC axis, as indicated by Figure 1c.38

We integrated MIM metasurface gratings with BP–MoS2 photodiodes. Figure 1d shows the device structure of a BP–MoS2 photodiode with an integrated MIM grating that is perpendicular to the AC axis. The MIM supports a lateral resonant mode by enclosing the BP and MoS2 layers between a metallic back-reflector and periodic grating with period A and width W.39,40 When incident light impinges on the MIM, it excites the mode which thereby increases its round-trip absorption.41 We fabricated MIMs onto photodiodes by patterning gratings with e-beam lithography (Raith) and then successively depositing 5 nm of Ti and 50 nm of Au with e-beam evaporation (CHA Industries). Figure 1e is an SEM image of the same device in Figure 1b after integration of the MIM. The inset of Figure 1e shows clearly distinguishable grating lines. Figure 1f shows the measured absorption spectra (FTIR) of the MIM-integrated BP–MoS2 photodiode at linear polarization angles of θ = 0° and θ = 90°. After the addition of the MIM (A = 450 nm and W = 235 nm), there is a clear absorption peak that appears when θ = 0° (light is perpendicular to the grating). Absorption is minimized when θ = 90° because the MIM acts as a reflector when linearly polarized light is parallel to the grating.25,42 In addition to the measured spectra, the simulated absorption spectrum of the MIM-integrated photodiode is shown in Figure 1f. The simulated spectrum is in good agreement with the measured spectrum, and it was calculated with finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations. The optical constants of BP and MoS2 were borrowed from refs (11 and 43), respectively. The inset of Figure 1f is the simulated intensity profile of |H|2 at λ = 3 μm, in which the field is strongly confined to the BP–MoS2 layers. Next, we adapted the MIM grating structure to broaden its absorption spectrum.

The absorption of a MIM structure can be expanded by adding additional resonators within the grating’s unit cell. Figure 2a shows an MIM structure with two distinct grating widths within one unit cell. The unit cell’s period is then increased to 2A while containing two gratings with distinct widths of W1 and W2. Similarly, Figure 2b is an MIM with three gratings W1, W2, and W3 inside one unit cell with period 3A. The additional gratings W2 and W3 support additional resonances that can be used to broaden an MIM’s absorption spectrum.44−48Figure 2c shows the measured (FTIR) and simulated (FDTD) absorption spectra of the double-resonator system in Figure 2a. Two resonant peaks are observable, which arise from the inclusion of the two distinct grating widths, W1 and W2. The device’s structural parameters are TBP = 75.4, TMo = 11.7, A = 450, W1 = 200, and W2 = 300 nm. The spectra were obtained at θ = 0° in order to maximize absorption. And to accommodate for the larger unit cell periods of the double- and triple-resonator MIMs, we obtained each measured absorption spectrum in this study with an FTIR spot size of 50 × 50 μm2. The spot size was controlled with a square knife-edge aperture in our instrument, and the side length of the square spot was much larger than each MIM’s unit cell period. Moreover, Figure 2d shows the measured and simulated absorption spectra for the triple-resonator system in Figure 2b with structural parameters being TBP = 83.2, TMo = 12.4, A = 600, W1 = 230, W2 = 320 nm, and W3 = 410 nm. Because of the addition of the third grating W3, a third resonant peak is clearly visible. Compared to the single-resonator system in Figure 1, the multiresonator MIMs in Figure 2 increase absorption across a greater spectral range.

The spectral position of the single, double, and triple MIM resonant peaks can be controlled with geometry. Figures 3a and 3b respectively show the measured (FTIR) and simulated (FDTD) absorption spectra of three single-resonator MIM devices. Each device in Figures 3a and 3b has a fixed period A = 450 nm and varying grating width W1. As W1 increases, the resonant mode shifts to higher wavelengths. A list of the structural parameters for each device used in Figure 3 can be found in Table 1. Figures 3c and 3d show the measured and simulated absorption spectra of the three double-resonator MIM devices. The spectral location of the two resonant peaks can be controlled by independently varying the W1 and W2. The lower-wavelength peak is controlled by varying W1, and the higher-wavelength peak is controlled by varying W2. Similar to the case for the single-resonator system, an increase in either W1 or W2 for a fixed period A will result in the corresponding resonance shifting to higher wavelengths. Figures 3e and 3f show the measured and simulated absorption spectra of two triple-resonator MIM samples. By simultaneous increase of W1, W2, and W3, the absorption spectrum, which exhibits three distinct peaks, shifts to higher wavelengths while retaining the relative spectral distance between each resonant peak.

To evaluate our broadband MIM structures, we compared the room temperature photodetection performance of BP–MoS2 photodiodes before and after integrating single-resonator and triple-resonator gratings. We denote the single-resonator photodiode as Device 1 and the triple-resonator photodiode as Device 2, which have the structural parameters of MIM 1 and MIM 7 in Table 1, respectively. Figure 4 compares each device’s absorption spectrum, dark current, and photocurrent at two testing laser wavelengths: λ1 = 3.39 μm and λ2 = 3.88 μm. Each photodiode had an active area of approximately 50 × 50 μm2 and was measured at room temperature. Absorption spectra were obtained with an FTIR spectrometer, and the dark currents were obtained with a source measure unit (Keithley). To measure photocurrent, the outputs of both lasers (Thorlabs) were modulated with an optical chopper (Thorlabs) at 200 Hz, and the generated photocurrents were extracted with a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research Systems). Both lasers were set to emit at 1.2 W cm–2, which resulted in approximately 30 μW of power impinging on each photodiode at both λ1 and λ2. The polarization of the lasers’ output was manipulated with a polarizer and half-wave plate (Thorlabs).

Device 1 is a single-resonator MIM BP–MoS2 photodiode. Figure 4a shows the absorption spectra of its single resonant peak, which has a center wavelength near λ = 3 μm, and vertical lines that represent λ1 and λ2. The dark current in Device 1 is shown in Figure 4b. Figure 4c shows the measured photoresponse of Device 1 at λ1 = 3.39 μm before and after integrating the single-resonator MIM grating. IOn is the current when the device is illuminated with approximately 30 μW of linearly polarized light, and IOff is the current under no illumination (dark current). The linearly polarized incident radiation is aligned to the AC axis of the device to maximize photocurrent. After the integration of the MIM structure, the generated photocurrent increased by 6.7 times at 3.39 μm and a reverse bias of VDS = −0.5 V. Photocurrent generation under reverse bias was significantly greater than photocurrent generation under forward bias, which is consistent with refs (7, 27, and 36). This behavior is likely due to the relative thicknesses of the BP p-type absorber and MoS2 n-type hole barrier, which affect the energy band and carrier dynamics. The inset of Figure 4c tracks the polarization-resolved responsivity R at a bias of VDS = −0.5 V as a function of the linear polarization angle θ (see Figure 1b). R is maximized at 737 mA W–1 when θ = 0° and minimized when θ = 90°. We define R as the extracted photocurrent divided by incident power. Figure 4d shows IOn and IOff at λ2 = 3.88 μm. Similar to Figure 4c, IOn was measured with linearly polarized incident light at θ = 0° before and after the inclusion of the MIM grating. After the inclusion of the MIM, the photoresponse increased by 3.9 times at 3.88 μm and a bias of VDS = −0.5 V. The inset of Figure 4d is the polarization-resolved responsivity at a bias of VDS = −0.5 V as a function of θ, which exhibits a maximum responsivity of 191 mA W–1 and the same anisotropic behavior as the inset of Figure 4c. The increase in photocurrent and responsivity is greater at λ1 than at λ2, which we attribute to the absorption peak in Figure 4a being closer to wavelength than λ1 than λ2.

Device 2 is a triple-resonator MIM BP–MoS2 photodiode. Its absorption spectrum is shown in Figure 4e, and its dark current is shown in Figure 4f. Figure 4g shows IOff and IOn at λ1 = 3.39 μm measured before and after the integration of the triple-resonator MIM, and the photocurrent increased by 7.5 times at a bias of VDS = −0.5 V. The inset of Figure 4g, which is the polarization-resolved responsivity at a bias of VDS = −0.5 V, has a maximum responsivity of 818 mA W–1. Likewise, at λ2 = 3.88 μm, Figure 4h shows IOff and IOn measured with and without the integrated MIM, and the inset of Figure 4h is the polarization-resolved responsivity at a bias of VDS = −0.5 V. At λ2, the MIM increased photocurrent by 12.8 times and maximized responsivity at 614 mA W–1. In contrast to Device 1, the triple-resonator MIM of Device 2 increased photocurrent by more than 7 times at both λ1 and λ2, which is due to the broad absorption spectra that arises from the multiresonator MIM system. Table 2 summarizes the performance enhancement from the integrated MIM by listing R and specific detectivity D* for both devices and testing wavelengths. D* is calculated with D* = RAdect1/2 (2eIOff)−1/2, in which R is the responsivity, Adect is the active area, e is the elementary charge constant, and IOff is the dark current. At VDS = −0.5 V, IOff = 2.33 μA for Device 1 and IOff = 1.19 μA for Device 2, which corresponds to room temperature dark current densities of 9.3 × 10–2 and 4.8 × 10–2 A cm–2 for Device 1 and 2, respectively. In future applications, dark current could be suppressed with gate-tuning mechanisms that decrease the conductance of the BP channel.12

In conclusion, we demonstrated a broadband and scalable approach to increasing the performance of BP–MoS2 photodiodes. We fabricated BP–MoS2 photodiodes and showed that single-resonator MIM structures increase the absorption around a selected wavelength. Then, we characterized double- and triple-resonator MIM structures with broadband absorption spectra that can be controlled by tuning their geometry. The broadband absorption spectra resulted in enhanced room temperature performance at multiple wavelengths. The process of integrating MIM structures in one lithographic and deposition step is scalable to large arrays of BP–MoS2 photodiodes that could be fabricated by large area growth methods.49−53 Each pixel in such an array could have different integrated MIMs that are tailored to specific wavelengths or wavelength ranges. Additionally, MIM metasurfaces and gold gratings can be applied to other 2D and conventional MWIR sensing materials, such as graphene and HgCdTe, to improve overall performance.55,56 Overall, our results show that integrated MIM structures enhance BP–MoS2 photodiodes over a broad range of wavelengths.
