MAX3 deficiency recruits protective Pseudomonas via modulating the SL-ABA-flavonoid axis to suppress soil-borne pathogen infection
Authors: Mimi Tian, Yiran Zheng, Wenhui Cao, Xinrun Yang, Tianjie Yang, Xiaofang Wang, Yangchun Xu, Alexandre Jousset, Jing-Jiang Zhou, Qirong Shen, Saskia C. M. Van Wees, Weiqiang Li, Zhong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Strigolactones (SLs) are carotenoid-derived hormones that regulate plant development and abiotic stress responses, but their role in regulating plant–microbe interactions remains unclear. Here, we show that Arabidopsis thaliana loss-of-function mutants of two SL biosynthetic genes, MORE AXILLARY GROWTH 3 (MAX3) and MAX4, exhibit opposite responses to soil-borne pathogen Ralstonia solanacearum, with max3 mutants displaying enhanced resistance, whereas max4 mutants are hypersusceptible. Exogenous SL analog rac-GR24 restores resistance in max4 mutants supporting a role for canonical SL-dependent immunity, while max3 mutants mediated resistance is SL-independent. Multi-omics analyses suggest that MAX3 deficiency is associated with enhanced abscisic acid (ABA) and flavonoid pathways under natural conditions, coinciding with the enrichment of beneficial Pseudomonas in rhizosphere. Both in vitro and in planta validations suggest that the ABA-flavonoid axis cooperatively enhances Pseudomonas-mediated niche competition and antibiotic biosynthesis, thereby potentially contributing to pathogen suppression. Our findings support a model in which MAX3 is associated with the modulation of rhizosphere-mediated defense, linking hormone signaling, secondary metabolism, and microbiome assembly in the context of soil-borne disease resistance.

Introduction

Strigolactones (SLs) are key plant hormones regulating shoot branching1–3 and root architecture4–6. Beyond development, SLs mediate interactions with rhizosphere organisms, including parasitic weeds7–9, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and rhizobia10,11, contributing to holobiont balance throughout plant growth12–14. SLs also improve plant tolerance to abiotic stresses such as salinity, drought and nutrient-deficiency15–19. SL biosynthesis originates from all-trans-β-carotene (C40) in plastids. The isomerase DWARF27 (D27) initiates the pathway, followed by CAROTENOID CLEAVAGE DIOXYGENASES 7 and CCD8, encoded by MORE AXILLARY GROWTH 3 (MAX3) and MAX4 in Arabidopsis thaliana, respectively. Subsequent cytosolic modifications by MAX1 and LATERAL BRANCHING OXIDOREDUCTASE (LBO) generate bioactive SLs that are secreted into the rhizosphere20–22. SL perception is mediated by the receptor D14 and the F-box protein MAX223. These core components underpin canonical SL-dependent developmental responses.

In addition to abiotic stress responses, increasing evidence indicates that SLs are involved in plant disease resistance. Both exogenous SL application and mutations in SL biosynthetic or signaling genes can alter pathogen susceptibility. For example, application of the synthetic SL analog rac-GR24 reduces the susceptibility to Verticillium dahliae in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), whereas knockdown of CCD7 and CCD8 decrease the resistance24. In Arabidopsis, max3, max4, max2 and d14 mutants show significantly increased susceptibility to Pseudomonas syringae DC300025,26 and Rhodococcus fascians27. Similarly, silencing SL biosynthesis genes in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) compromises resistance to the root-knot nematode (RKN) Meloidogyne incognita28, and SL-deficient rice (Oryza sativa) mutants such as d17 and d14 are more susceptible to the blast fungal pathogen Magnaporthe oryzae29. Notably, SLs can also promote susceptibility. For instance, SL signaling suppresses jasmonate accumulation in rice, facilitating infection by Meloidogyne graminicola30, while d10 and d14 mutants show enhanced resistance to pathogen Pyricularia oryzae31. These contrasting observations highlight the context-dependent roles of SLs in plant defense. However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.

SLs and abscisic acid (ABA) share carotenoid-derived biosynthetic origin20,32–35 (Supplementary Fig. 1). ABA is produced from β-carotene-based xanthophylls through enzymatic steps involving ZEAXANTHIN EPOXIDASE (ZEP), NEOXANTHIN SYNTHASE (NSY), 9-CIS-EPOXYCAROTENOID DIOXYGENASES (NCEDs), and downstream cytosolic enzymes XANTHOXIN DEHYDROGENASE (ABA2) and ABSCISIC ALDEHYDE OXIDASE 3 (AAO3)36. Both SL and ABA pathways are dynamically regulated under environmental stresses. For instance, drought promotes ABA accumulation at the expense of SL levels37, whereas SL application can reduce ABA under heat stress in Arabidopsis38. Importantly, SL-ABA interactions are highly context-dependent. SL-deficient mutants display contrasting ABA responses depending on species and conditions, including increased ABA level in rice under drought39 and decreased ABA in tomato under temperature stress40. In addition, cold stress also suppresses the expression of key ABA-responsive genes, including MYB DOMAIN PROTEIN 2 (OsMYB2) and RESPONSIVE TO ABSCISIC ACID (RAB16C) in the SL-deficient d27 rice mutant36. These findings suggest that the integration of SL and ABA pathways varies with environmental and physiological context, but their interaction under biotic stress remains largely unexplored.

Upon pathogen attack, plants activate innate immune responses to restrict pathogen growth. Pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) is initiated by recognition of conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) by cell-surface receptors, whereas effector-triggered immunity (ETI) is activated by intracellular recognition of pathogen effectors, often leading to stronger responses including localized cell death41. Together, PTI and ETI form the classical immune layers that form the foundation of plant immunity in Arabidopsis. Beyond these canonical immune layers, plants secrete diverse secondary metabolites into the rhizosphere, including coumarins, camalexin, benzoxazinoids, and flavonoids, which can directly inhibit pathogens or modulate rhizosphere microbial composition to favor beneficial taxa42–52. Phytohormones play key roles in regulating these metabolites and shaping the root-associated microbiome53–56. For example, the RIPENING-INHIBITOR (RIN) transcription factor promoted exudation of 3-hydroxyflavone and riboflavin, enhancing the recruitment of disease-suppressive microbes and conferring resistance against the Ralstonia solanacearum57. These studies highlight the importance of hormone-mediated metabolic regulation in microbiome-associated plant immunity.

Here, we investigated the role of SLs in Arabidopsis resistance to the soil-borne pathogen Ralstonia solanacearum, a major causal agent of bacterial wilt58–60. We hypothesized that SL deficiency may alter plant-pathogen interactions through changes in hormone signaling, secondary metabolism, and rhizosphere microbial composition. To test this, we analyzed MAX3- and MAX4-deficient mutants using integrated metabolomic, transcriptomic, and microbiome profiling under pathogen challenge, enabling a comprehensive framework to dissect SL-mediated mechanisms in root-associated defense.

Results

Contrasting resistance to bacterial wilt in max3 and max4 mutants

To investigate the role of SLs in soil-borne bacterial wilt disease, we used two Arabidopsis thaliana mutants affected in SL biosynthesis genes, MAX3 (max3-11) and MAX4 (max4-8), alongside Columbia-0 wild-type (Col-0) plants (Fig. 1a, b), to conduct infection assays (sterile 1/2 MS plate system, hereafter referred to as “Sterile” in figures; natural soil system, hereafter referred to as “Natural” in figures) with Ralstonia solanacearum (Fig. 1c). Time-course disease assays showed rapid disease progression between 4 and 8 days post inoculation (dpi), followed by a plateau phase from 8 to 12 dpi (Supplementary Fig. 2). Under both sterile 1/2 MS plate (12 replicates per genotype) and natural soil systems (60 replicates per genotype), clear genotype-dependent differences in disease severity were observed at 8 dpi (Supplementary Fig. 3a and Fig. 1d). In the sterile 1/2 MS plate system, max3-11 plants exhibited a significant reduction in disease severity compared to Col-0 (−38.5%, p < 0.05), whereas max4-8 plants showed a notable increase in susceptibility (+22.9%, p < 0.05). In the natural soil system, these differences were more pronounced. The max3-11 plants displayed a ~40.0% reduction in disease severity relative to Col-0 (p < 0.05), while max4-8 plants showed a substantial increase in susceptibility (+55.0%, p < 0.05). Additional alleles (max3-9, max4-1) also supported these contrasting phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c).

To assess whether exogenous SL application modulates disease responses, we treated plants with the synthetic SL analog rac-GR24 under identical systems (Fig. 1e). Using Col-0 as the baseline, we evaluated disease responses of SL-deficient mutants following rac-GR24 treatment. In the sterile 1/2 MS plate and natural soil systems, max3-11 plants consistently exhibited reduced disease severity relative to Col-0 regardless of rac-GR24 application (Sterile: −40.8%, Natural: −68.4%, p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 4a and Fig. 1f). In contrast, rac-GR24 treatment markedly reduced disease severity in max4-8 plants under both systems, restoring resistance to levels comparable to max3-11 (Sterile: −40.8%, Natural: −68.4%, p < 0.05). Similar trends were observed across additional alleles (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). To dissect genotype-specific responses to exogenous rac-GR24, we compared disease severity under −SL and +SL conditions for each genotype using previously collected disease assay data in both sterile 1/2 MS plate and natural soil systems. In sterile 1/2 MS plate systems, rac-GR24 treatment significantly reduced disease severity across all genotypes (Col-0: −37.8%, max3-11: −40.1%, max4-8: −70.0%; p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 5a). However, under natural soil systems, a significant reduction was observed only in max4-8 (−75.8%, p < 0.05), whereas Col-0 and max3-11 did not show statistically significant responses (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Together, these results suggest that exogenous SL can enhance disease resistance in an SL-deficient context, as exemplified by the restoration of resistance in max4-8 plants. In contrast, the reduced disease susceptibility observed in max3-11 plants is largely insensitive to SL supplementation, suggesting that MAX3-associated resistance is largely independent of canonical SL signaling and may be influenced by environmental context, including the presence of rhizosphere microbiota.

MAX3 deficiency is associated with ABA- and flavonoid-related metabolic shifts

To further explore the defense strategy associated with MAX3 deficiency, we conducted root transcriptome sequencing from the same natural soil experiment described above (Fig. 1c). At 8 dpi, a stage when disease symptoms had stabilized, n = 6 representative biological replicates per genotype (Col-0, max3-11, and max4-8) were harvested for RNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. 6, Gene expression levels were quantified as read counts see Supplementary Data 1). Principal component analysis (PCA) of root transcriptome data indicated distinct genotype-specific transcriptional patterns, with clear separation along PC1 (21.59%) and PC2 (16.74%) among Col-0, max3-11 and max4-8 plants (Supplementary Fig. 7a). To obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each mutant, gene expression levels were normalized as FPKM values (see Supplementary Data 2). Compared to Col-0, a total of 5056 and 4135 DEGs were identified in max3-11 and max4-8, respectively (false discovery rate - FDR < 0.05; |log2FC | ≥ 1, Supplementary Fig. 7b). KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs showed divergent transcriptional reprogramming in both mutants relative to Col-0. Notably, the ‘Plant-pathogen interaction’ pathway (ko04626), which includes key components of PTI and ETI, was not significantly enriched in either mutant (Supplementary Fig. 7c, p ≥ 0.05). Moreover, most immunity-associated genes annotated in this pathway were broadly downregulated in max3-11 and max4-8 compared to Col-0 (Supplementary Fig. 7d). These findings suggest that canonical PTI/ETI-associated immune signaling is attenuated in the SL-deficient mutants, and that the enhanced resistance observed in max3-11 plants is likely independent of classical PTI/ETI pathways.

Given that SL and ABA share a common biosynthetic origin from all-trans-β-carotene (Fig. 2a), and that max3-11 plants exhibited enhanced disease resistance in the absence of immune pathway activation, we hypothesized a metabolic reconfiguration toward the ABA branch under natural soil system. To test this, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling of rhizosphere soils paired with the transcriptomic analyses under Ralstonia infection conditions (n = 6 biological replicates per genotype). The PCA plot displayed genotype-distinct metabolic patterns (Supplementary Fig. 8a, PC1: 18.6%, PC2: 17.74%, Supplementary Data 3). Notably, the max3-11 rhizosphere accumulated abscisic aldehyde, a key intermediate in ABA biosynthesis, at significantly higher levels than other genotypes, while no reduction in carlactone levels was detected in these rhizosphere samples (Fig. 2b), suggesting an association with increased ABA biosynthetic output. The transcript levels of ABA biosynthetic genes (AtABA2, AtAAO3) showed no significant changes in the RNA-seq dataset (Supplementary Fig. 8b), while RT-qPCR showed elevated expression in max3-11 roots (Fig. 2c). Consistently, max3-11 exhibited significantly higher rhizosphere ABA levels (~1.2-fold increase relative to Col-0, p < 0.05), whereas levels in max4-8 remained comparable to Col-0 (Fig. 2d). To examine whether alternative metabolic pathways are preferentially associated with max3-11 under pathogen infection in the natural soil system, we performed an integrative analysis of root transcriptomic and rhizosphere metabolomic datasets, representing distinct but biologically connected compartments. From the previously identified DEGs under Ralstonia infection (Supplementary Fig. 7b), a Venn diagram defined 929 genes uniquely upregulated in max3-11 roots (Supplementary Fig. 8c). All 929 genes were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the STRING database, which suggested significant enrichment in KEGG pathways related to ‘Flavone and flavonol biosynthesis’ and ‘Flavonoid biosynthesis’ (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 2e). Rhizosphere metabolomic profiling identified 79 and 124 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs, representing metabolites with significantly altered abundance) in max3-11 and max4-8, respectively (VIP > 1; p < 0.05; |log2FC | ≥ 1, Supplementary Fig. 8d), with 36 DAMs uniquely enriched in max3-11 (Supplementary Fig. 8e, representing diverse metabolite classes). Among these, the ABA precursor abscisic aldehyde as well as two flavonoids, myricetin (a flavonol) and cyanidin 3-(2G-glucosylrutinoside) (an anthocyanidin glycoside), were significantly enriched in the max3-11 rhizosphere (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Consistent with the enrichment of flavonoid-related pathways, transcript levels of flavonoid biosynthetic genes, including CYTOCHROME P450 73A5 (CYP73A5, AT2G30490), CYP98A3 (AT2G40890), FLAVANONE 3-HYDROXYLASE (F3H, AT3G51240), CYP75B1 (AT5G07990), LEUCOANTHOCYANIDIN DIOXYGENASE (LDOX, AT4G22880), UDP-GLUCOSYL TRANSFERASE 78D1 (UGT78D1, AT1G30530) and UGT78D2 (AT5G17050), were significantly elevated in max3-11 roots compared to Col-0 and max4-8 (Fig. 2f). In addition, max3-11 rhizosphere exhibited a marked reduction in R. solanacearum density (~0.9-fold decrease relative to Col-0, p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 9b). The relative abundance of flavonoids, the majority of max3-11-specific DAMs, and ABA levels were significantly negatively correlated with pathogen density (R > 0.60, p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 9c–e), indicating a potential association with pathogen suppression. Together, these findings suggest that MAX3-deficient plants, despite lacking canonical immune activation, exhibit a coordinated metabolic reprogramming characterized by rhizosphere accumulation of ABA and flavonoids, which is associated with enhanced resistance against bacterial wilt.

MAX3 deficiency-associated metabolic reprogramming potentially requires key rhizosphere microbiota

Given the distinctive transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming observed in max3-11 plants under R. solanacearum infection in the natural soil system (n = 6 biological replicates per genotype), we first evaluated whether these responses were retained in the absence of microbiota. Under sterile 1/2 MS plate system following R. solanacearum infection, max3-11 roots displayed a markedly altered transcriptome profile relative to both Col-0 and max4-8 (PC1: 23.34%, PC2: 12.34%, Supplementary Fig. 10a). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the normalized expression tables (Supplementary Data 4). Gene was first compared between each mutant and Col-0, followed by Venn diagram analysis to identify genotype-specific responses. Based on this, we identified 1945 genes specifically upregulated in max3-11 but not in max4-8 (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). Functional enrichment analysis of these genes using STRING database showed significant enrichment in the ‘Plant-pathogen interaction’ pathway, particularly in components associated with basal innate immunity (Supplementary Fig. 10d), indicating activation of a microbe-independent innate immune program, consistent with a compensatory immune response in the absence of rhizosphere microbes. In contrast, untargeted metabolomic profiling (n = 6 biological replicates per genotype, Supplementary Data 5) indicated that levels of the terminal ABA metabolite abscisic aldehyde as well as the two flavonoids myricetin and cyanidin 3-(2G-glucosylrutinoside), remained unchanged across genotypes under sterile 1/2 MS plate systems (p ≥ 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 10e). These results suggest that microbiota contribute to the ABA-flavonoids-mediated defense strategy in max3-11, and that in their absence, the plant may compensate by activating a microbe-independent innate immune program.

Disease severity was consistently lower in the natural soil system, suggesting that rhizosphere microbiota is associated with modulation of max3-11 resistance (Fig. 1d). To dissect the microbial component of this phenotype, we performed metagenomic sequencing of rhizosphere soil samples from pathogen-infected plants grown in natural soil systems (n = 6 biological replicates per genotype, Supplementary Data 6 and Supplementary Data 7). Compared to Col-0 and max4-8, max3-11 plants harbored a significantly higher bacterial α-diversity, as indicated by increased Shannon and Simpson indices (p < 0.05, Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 11a), along with elevated Evenness but comparable species number (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) showed a clear genotype-driven separation in microbiota compositions (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.44, p = 0.001, Fig. 3b), with both SL-deficient mutants displaying distinct community structures relative to Col-0. Given this divergence from the wild-type baseline, we next compared the two mutants directly to identify taxa potentially associated with their contrasting disease phenotypes. Several bacterial genera, most prominently Pseudomonas, Massilia, Chryseolinea, Ohtaekwangia and Ramlibacter, were significantly enriched in the max3-11 rhizosphere relative to max4-8 (Fig. 3c). Notably, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas increased by approximately 2.6-fold in max3-11 relative to max4-8 (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon test). When referenced to the Col-0 baseline, these differences were less pronounced, with most genera showing comparable abundance levels, indicating that the observed shifts primarily reflect divergence between mutants rather than large deviations from the baseline community. Given the coordinated accumulation of ABA and flavonoids in the max3-11 rhizosphere under natural soil systems, we next assessed their potential contributions to microbiota assembly and compositional shifts. Correlation analyses suggested that both myricetin and cyanidin 3-(2G-glucosylrutinoside) were significantly positively associated with Shannon and Simpson indices, while ABA correlated positively with Shannon index alone (R > 0.60, p < 0.05, Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 12a). These flavonoids were positively correlated with the relative abundance of dominant bacterial taxa including Pseudomonas, Chryseolinea, Ohtaekwangia and Ramlibacter, whereas ABA showed a significant positive association with the relative abundance of Pseudomonas and Massilia (R > 0.60, p < 0.05, Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 12b). To evaluate the functional relevance of these microbial shifts, differential analysis of KEGG pathway abundance based on metagenomic annotations displayed a significant upregulation of the enediyne antibiotics biosynthesis pathway in max3-11 rhizosphere compared to max4-8 (adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC | ≥ 1, Supplementary Fig. 13a). Furthermore, enriched bacterial taxa, including Pseudomonas, Massilia, Chryseolinea, Ohtaekwangia and Ramlibacter, showed strong positive correlations with the predicted antibiotic biosynthetic capacity (R > 0.68, p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 13b). Together, these results indicate that MAX3 deficiency is accompanied by a root-derived metabolic shift that may contribute to the assembly of microbial communities enriched in potentially beneficial microbes, particularly antibiotic-producing taxa, to the rhizosphere. This microbiota restructuring likely contributes to enhanced disease suppression in max3-11 plants via a mechanism independent of canonical immune activation.

Synergistic enhancement of disease resistance by ABA-flavonoids via rhizosphere microbial recruitment

To further evaluate the functional contribution of these metabolites, we performed exogenous application assays under natural soil system. Among the flavonoids enriched in the rhizosphere metabolome, myricetin was selected as a representative compound based on its relatively high abundance (Supplementary Fig. 9a). We assessed whether exogenous ABA and myricetin could restore resistance in max4-8 plants, which lack the metabolic reprogramming observed in max3-11 (Fig. 4a). Disease severity was quantified using a normalized disease index (0–1 scale) as described above. To facilitate comparison of treatment effects across genotypes, disease responses in mutant lines were interpreted relative to the Col-0 control within each treatment condition. As previously observed, max4-8 plants showed heightened susceptibility to R. solanacearum (mock treatment), while max3-11 plants exhibited significant resistance (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05, Fig. 4b). Both ABA and myricetin treatments, whether individually or combined, maintained resistance in max3-11 plants. However, only combined treatment of ABA and myricetin restored resistance in max4-8, reducing disease severity by 40.0% (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05), which showed the similar levels to max3-11 (Fig. 4b). To further understand how ABA-flavonoids influence rhizosphere microbial composition in max3 plants, we focused on the genus Pseudomonas, which was consistently enriched in the max3-11 rhizosphere (Fig. 3c). As one of the most prominently enriched genera identified in our dataset, Pseudomonas was selected as a representative group for downstream mechanistic assays. Twelve representative Pseudomonas strains (P. baetica, P. brassicacearum, P. chlororaphis, P. donghuensis, P. fluorescens, P. koreensis, P. mohnii, P. monteilii, P. moorei, P. protegens, P. putida, P. thivervalensis) were selected from our laboratory collection based on species-level matches to the metagenomic taxonomic annotations (Supplementary Fig. 14a and Supplementary Table 2). These isolates were originally obtained from diverse rhizosphere environments and curated in our lab strain database. In vitro co-culture assays between individual Pseudomonas strain and Ralstonia showed that six Pseudomonas strains significantly inhibited R. solanacearum growth, two strains promoted its proliferation, and the remaining isolates showed no significant effect (Supplementary Fig. 14b). To simulate microbial community dynamics and evaluate the collective contribution of beneficial Pseudomonas strains to pathogen suppression, we constructed a synthetic Pseudomonas consortium (SynCom-A, consisting of eight isolates) by randomly selecting eight strains from ten candidates that did not significantly enhance R. solanacearum growth under in vitro co-culture assays (Supplementary Fig. 15a). We first tested whether ABA-flavonoids affect the SynCom growth within an in vitro SynCom assay. Compared to mock control (cultured without exogenous ABA and myricetin), SynCom cultures supplemented with ABA and myricetin showed a 14.9% increase in bacterial biomass (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 15b). We next assessed the effect of ABA and myricetin supplementation in an in vitro co-culture assay of SynCom and R. solanacearum. While the combination of ABA and myricetin did not significantly reduce R. solanacearum biomass, SynCom reduced pathogen biomass by 3.7% (p < 0.05, Fig. 4c). A comparable level was observed when SynCom was combined with ABA and myricetin (3.8% reduction, Fig. 4c), with no significant difference between the two treatments. In vitro planta, max4-8 plants infected with R. solanacearum showed severe biomass reduction, indicative of high susceptibility. SynCom alone is associated with limited protection, but combined supplementation with ABA, myricetin, and SynCom significantly mitigated disease severity and improved plant biomass (Supplementary Fig. 15c and Fig. 4d, Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05). Consistent results were obtained using an independent Pseudomonas SynCom (SynCom-B, consisting of ten isolates), supporting the reproducibility of these observations (Supplementary Fig. 16a–e). Together, these data suggest that ABA and flavonoids synergistically support the establishment and activity of beneficial Pseudomonas consortia during pathogen pressure, potentially contributing to microbiota-mediated plant protection.

To explore the molecular basis of the interaction between ABA, flavonoids, the Pseudomonas consortium, and R. solanacearum, we performed transcriptomic analyses of in vitro co-cultures with SynCom-A (consisting of eight isolates) and R. solanacearum, with or without ABA-myricetin supplementation. PCoA of R. solanacearum profiles showed clear transcriptional shifts between treatments (Supplementary Fig. 17a and Supplementary Data 8), with distinct clustering of R. solanacearum alone (Rs) versus co-cultures with Pseudomonas (PSE) or combined with ABA-myricetin (CPSE) (PCoA1, 82.35% explained variance). The CPSE versus Rs group showed significant divergence from the PSE versus Rs group, indicating that ABA and myricetin supplementation modulated Pseudomonas-associated transcriptional dynamics (PERMANOVA, adonis, R2 = 0.89, p = 0.001). Notably, 171 DEGs were downregulated with SynCom alone, and 122 DEGs were further downregulated with ABA-myricetin treatment (normalized gene expression see Supplementary Data 9, Supplementary Fig. 17b, c, FDR < 0.05; |log2FC | ≥ 1). KEGG analysis showed downregulation of genes involved in steroid degradation, ribosome pathways, and the Caulobacter cell cycle (p < 0.05, Fig. 4e), suggesting that ABA-flavonoids suppress R. solanacearum growth by modulating its metabolic and cell cycle pathways. We also analyzed the transcriptional response of the Pseudomonas consortium after ABA-myricetin treatment. The addition of ABA and myricetin significantly altered the transcriptional profile of SynCom, compared to untreated controls (Supplementary Data 10 and Supplementary Fig. 17d, adonis, R2 = 0.41, p = 0.014, PCoA1, 75.33% explained variance). Differential gene expression analysis displayed the upregulation of 1,837 DEGs in SynCom during co-cultured with R. solanacearum upon ABA-myricetin supplementation (Supplementary Data 11 and Supplementary Fig. 17e, FDR < 0.05; |log2FC | ≥ 1), with genes enriched in pathways related to flagellar assembly, biofilm formation, antibiotic biosynthesis, and NOD-like receptor signaling (p < 0.05, Fig. 4f). These findings suggest that ABA and flavonoids enhance the transcriptional responses of both the Pseudomonas consortium and R. solanacearum, promoting beneficial microbial colonization and antimicrobial activity while suppressing R. solanacearum proliferation.

Discussion

This study focused on MAX3 and MAX4 as representative perturbations of the SL biosynthetic pathway. These two genes encode the carotenoid cleavage dioxygenases CCD7 and CCD8, respectively, catalyzing consecutive steps and occupying key nodes in carotenoid metabolism61,62. Despite their sequential roles, our results suggest a striking functional divergence in their contributions to plant immunity. Only plants lacking functional MAX3 enzyme exhibited enhanced resistance to R. solanacearum, accompanied by distinct rhizosphere metabolic and microbial profiles, whereas plants lacking functional MAX4 enzyme remain susceptible (Fig. 1d, f). Partial rescue of susceptibility in plants lacking MAX4 enzyme by exogenous application of the synthetic SL analog rac-GR24, with no further enhancement in plants lacking MAX3 enzyme, supports that enhanced resistance in the absence of functional MAX3 enzyme is largely independent of canonical SL signaling outputs. In wild-type plants, MAX3 activity is associated with carotenoid-derived metabolic processes, consistent with its enzymatic role in SL biosynthesis from carotenoid precursors63,64. Carotenoid-derived pathways generate multiple stress-related metabolites, including ABA and apocarotenoids65,66, suggesting that the MAX3 enzyme may contribute to maintaining metabolic homeostasis under non-stress conditions. Loss of MAX3 enzyme function shifts the rhizosphere toward a defense-associated metabolic state, reflected by coordinated changes in metabolite profiles and microbial community assembly, consistent with prior observations linking carotenoid metabolism to stress-related metabolites and plant-microbiome interactions1–3.

SL- and ABA-related pathways share carotenoid precursors and respond dynamically to environmental cues37,40. While previous studies report minimal ABA changes in SL mutants under non-stress conditions63, lack of functional MAX3 enzyme is associated with increased ABA-related metabolites under pathogen challenge, indicating context-dependent regulation. Given that SL and ABA biosynthesis intersect at 9-cis-β-carotene, disruption of upstream enzymatic steps may alter carotenoid-derived metabolite patterns67. The absence of detectable changes in rhizosphere carlactone likely reflects limitations associated with detecting this highly labile metabolite in soil extracts and therefore should not be interpreted as evidence of unchanged endogenous SL biosynthesis64,68,69. Variation among species further highlights context-dependent SL-ABA responses: SL-deficient mutants in Arabidopsis thaliana and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) show reduced ABA under abiotic stress, whereas rice (Oryza sativa) d10 and d17 mutants display elevated ABA levels and improved drought resilience36,39. Such variation may arise from differences in upstream enzymes (e.g., D27) or downstream modifications (e.g., MAX1 oxidases) whose integration with ABA biosynthesis remains underexplored62,70. Our results are consistent with a model in which MAX3 activity correlates with distinct carotenoid-derived metabolic states rather than directly regulating metabolic flux, which requires dedicated flux analyses for confirmation. Transcriptomic and metabolomic data were derived from root and rhizosphere compartments, respectively, and thus reflect coordinated but not necessarily causal relationships. Although loss of MAX3 enzyme function was linked to increased ABA-related metabolites in the rhizosphere, transcriptional changes in ABA biosynthetic genes were relatively modest, suggesting that altered ABA-associated responses may involve additional regulatory layers beyond strong transcriptional activation. ELISA-based ABA measurements in rhizosphere soil should also be interpreted cautiously because of potential matrix interference. Exogenous ABA partially restored resistance in plants lacking MAX4 enzyme (Fig. 4b), supporting a functional contribution of ABA-associated processes to disease resistance17,71. Potential microbial contributions to rhizosphere ABA accumulation also cannot be excluded.

MAX3 activity appears associated with a relatively immune-quiescence state under favorable conditions, whereas its loss correlates with co-enrichment of ABA- and flavonoid-related metabolites and preferential association with specific microbial taxa, particularly Pseudomonas, in microbe-containing environments (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Figs. 7c, d, 9c–e, 10a–e). Flavonoids influence microbial colonization52,72, and ABA has also been implicated in shaping rhizosphere assembly73–75. Their co-induction suggests coordinated regulation, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be resolved76–78. Reduced flavonoid accumulation associated with loss of MAX4 enzymatic function coincides with altered microbiome composition. While MAX1 has been reported to positively regulate flavonoid biosynthesis79–81, the extent to which this contributes to the metabolic phenotype related to impaired MAX4 function remains unresolved. Notably, comparable reductions in flavonoid accumulation are not observed following disruption of MAX3 function, despite both MAX3 and MAX4 acting within the SL biosynthetic pathway, suggesting a potential functional divergence between these enzymes.

Notably, alternative mechanisms may also contribute to the enhanced resistance observed in plants lacking MAX3 enzyme. Under sterile 1/2 MS plate conditions, roots of plants lacking MAX3 enzyme (max3-11) show enrichment of genes associated with the “plant-pathogen interaction” pathway, indicating activation of a microbe-independent innate immune program. Given the well-established growth–defense trade-off during pathogen challenge82, growth-associated phenotypes may not be readily separable from disease-related responses. Together, these observations suggest that ABA-flavonoid-associated metabolic reprogramming likely operates alongside additional immune or physiological adjustments.

Consistent with metabolite shifts, rhizospheres of plant lacking functional MAX3 enzyme are associated with higher relative abundance of specific microbial taxa, notably Pseudomonas, known for motility, niche colonization, and antibiotic production83,84. As the most enriched genus, it served as a representative system for metabolite-mediated microbial functions (Fig. 3c). In vitro assays showed that Pseudomonas consortia can suppress R. solanacearum growth but alone is insufficient to confer disease resistance in plants lacking MAX4 enzyme (Fig. 4c, d), indicating that microbial presence alone is insufficient to fully recapitulate the protective phenotype conferred by loss of MAX3 enzyme function. Supplementation with ABA-flavonoid metabolites restores disease-suppressive capacity, suggesting host-derived metabolites potentiate microbial functions under pathogen challenge52,85. Consistently, ABA-flavonoid treatment does not directly inhibit pathogen biomass but enhances microbial traits such as motility and secondary metabolism, constraining pathogen proliferation via microbe-mediated interactions (Fig. 4d–f). These results suggest that the observed effects of Pseudomonas in SynComs primarily reflect functional responsiveness to host metabolic cues rather than ecological dominance in natural soils. While the SynCom captures intra-genus diversity, it does not replicate the full complexity of natural rhizosphere communities, and disease suppression likely arises from emergent community interactions rather than single-strain effects86–88 (Supplementary Fig. 14b).

Collectively, loss of MAX3 enzyme function shifts the rhizosphere toward a defense-associated metabolic and microbiome configuration associated with enhanced resistance to R. solanacearum (Fig. 5). Our results support a model in which coordinated metabolic and microbiome-associated processes involving components related to both SL and ABA pathways contribute to rhizosphere-associated disease resistance, rather than direct SL-ABA crosstalk. Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana mutants max3-11, max3-9, max4-8 and max4-1, previously described as T-DNA insertion alleles in the MAX3 (At2g44990) and MAX4 (At4g32810) genes63,89, were kindly provided by Professor Weiqiang Li (Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). All mutants are loss-of-function alleles and are in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) genetic background, which was used as the wild-type control throughout this study. For experiments, seeds were surface sterilized in 70% ethanol for 1 min, followed by treatment with 5% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for 5 min, and rinsed five times with sterile distilled water. Sterilized seeds were stratified at 4 °C in the dark for 2 days and subsequently germinated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) agar medium supplemented with 1.0% (w/v) sucrose and 0.8% (w/v) agar, adjusted to pH 5.7. Plants were grown under long-day conditions (16-h light, 8-h dark cycle) at 22 °C, with a light intensity of 80−90 μE m−2 s−1 and 60% relative humidity.

Pathogen infection and disease assessment

R. solanacearum strain QL-Rs1115 (GenBank accession GU390462)60, a highly virulent phylotype I isolate, was used as the model plant pathogen strain for all infection assays in this study. Before each inoculation, bacteria were revived from glycerol stocks on nutrient agar (NA) medium (glucose 10.0 g l−1, peptone 5.0 g l−1, yeast extract 0.5 g l−1, beef extract 3.0 g l−1, agar 15.0 g l−1) and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. A single colony was transferred to NA liquid medium and cultured overnight at 30 °C with shaking at 170 r.p.m. until the OD600 reached 0.8−1.0. Cells were then collected by centrifugation (5000 × g, 6 min), washed twice with sterile distilled water, and resuspended to the desired concentration (approximately 1 × 107 colony forming units ml−1 (c.f.u. ml−1), OD600 = 0.55) for inoculation. Rhizosphere infection assays were conducted using two distinct systems: a sterile 1/2 MS plate system (referred to as “Sterile” in figures) and a natural soil system (referred to as “Natural” in figures). In the sterile 1/2 MS plate system, seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS agar plates. In the natural soil system, plants were grown in pots (5 cm × 5 cm) filled with a 1:2 (v/v) mixture of sterilized perlite and rice field soil collected from the Baima Experimental Station of Nanjing Agricultural University (31° 36′ 50″ N, 119° 10′ 50″ E). A total of 14-day-seedlings were transferred into both systems. Inoculation with R. solanacearum was performed either 3 or 7 days post-transplantation, by applying 20 µl of bacterial suspension (1 × 107 c.f.u. ml−1) per plant directly to the rhizosphere in the sterile 1/2 MS plate system, or 5 ml per plant in the natural soil system. Control plants were mock-inoculated with an equal volume of sterile water. In the sterile 1/2 MS plate system, each treatment included 12 biological replicates, with one plant considered as one biological replicate. In the natural soil system, each treatment included 60 biological replicates. Plants were maintained under disease assay chamber (16 h light/8 h dark, constant light at 500 μE m−2 s−1, 25 °C) and monitored daily for disease symptoms. Disease severity was assessed at 8 days post inoculation (dpi), a time point at which disease symptoms were stable (Supplementary Fig. 2). This time point was determined based on time-course disease progression assays (4–12 dpi) performed under the same experimental conditions as described above. Disease symptoms were scored on a 0–4 scale based on the percentage of wilted leaves (0 = no symptoms; 1 = 1–25%; 2 = 26–50%; 3 = 51–75%; 4 = 76–100%), following established protocols for R. solanacearum pathogenicity phenotyping58,90. For quantitative analysis, scores were converted into a normalized disease index (0–1 scale; 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1), representing increasing levels of disease severity.

Exogenous SL treatment assay

The synthetic SL analog rac-GR24 was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China; Assay >98%, CAS No. 76974-79-3) to assess the effect of exogenous SL on bacterial wilt resistance. We note that rac-GR24 is a racemic mixture containing GR245DS and GR24ent−5DS, which can differentially activate SL and Karrikin signaling pathways via D14 and KAI2 receptors, respectively. While widely used as a standard tool compound to probe SL-related biological responses, rac-GR24 does not allow strict discrimination between SL- and Karrikin-mediated effects.

rac-GR24 powder was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol to make a 10 mM stock solution, which was stored at −20 °C in the dark until use. Before rhizosphere treatment, the stock solution was diluted with sterile distilled water to prepare working concentrations of 1 μM. In the sterile 1/2 MS plate system, square Petri dishes (13 cm × 13 cm) were lined with sterile filter paper strips (1 cm × 10 cm) fully soaked in 1 μM rac-GR24 solution. The paper was saturated until evenly moist but without excess dripping. Sterile water-soaked filter paper served as the mock control. A total of 14-day-seedlings (Col-0, max3-11, max4-8) were carefully transferred onto the filter paper and incubated under long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark, 22 °C). After three days of rac-GR24 treatment, plants were inoculated with R. solanacearum and grown in the disease assay chamber. Disease severity was evaluated at 8 dpi based on visible wilting symptoms. In the natural soil system, 14-day-seedlings were transplanted into specific pots. Four days after transplantation, 5 ml of 1 μM rac-GR24 working solution was applied to each plant by soil drench. Control plants received 5 ml of sterile water. Pathogen inoculation was performed 3 days after rac-GR24 application, and disease progression was assessed at 8 dpi by scoring leaf wilting symptoms across genotypes in the disease assay chamber. Each system was set up with at least 12 biological replicates per treatment.

Validation of SL mutant alleles

Arabidopsis max3-11 and max4-8 mutants were used for the initial set of pathogen infection assays. Upon identifying genotype-specific resistance patterns, independent allelic mutants (max3-9 and max4-1) were incorporated in follow-up experiments to confirm phenotypic consistency. These validation assays were performed under the same experimental conditions used for the primary infection assays, including the sterile 1/2 MS plate system, the natural soil system, pathogen inoculation procedure, and disease assessment described in the corresponding sections. Each treatments designed 10 biological replicates at least, and one plant per replicate.

Root sampling and RNA-seq under natural soil system

Root sampling and RNA-seq library preparation: Roots were carefully harvested at 8 dpi with R. solanacearum under natural soil conditions. For each biological replicate (n = 6), roots from individual plants were gently washed with sterile water to remove adhering soil, lightly blotted on sterile absorbent paper to remove excess water, placed into pre-chilled 2 ml tubes, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further processing. Frozen root tissues were ground to fine powder under liquid nitrogen using mortar and pestle. Total RNAs were isolated using TRIzol kit (Invitrogen) and genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (TaKara). RNA quality and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser (Agilent Technologies), and RNA concentration was quantified by Nanodrop ND-2000 (Thermo Fished Scientific). Samples with OD260/280 between 1.8 and 2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 6.5, 28S:18S ratio ≥1.0, and total RNA quantity >10 μg were used for library construction. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) from 1 μg of total RNA. Briefly, poly (A) + mRNA was enriched with oligo (dT) beads and fragmented enzymatically. cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-base addition and ligation of the Illumina-indexed adapters were performed according to Illumina’s protocol. Libraries were size-selected (~200−300 bp) on 2% low-range ultra agarose gels and amplified by PCR with 15 cycles using Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB). Final libraries were quantified using a TBS380 fluorometer and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 150 bp reads) by Shanghai BIOZERON Co., Ltd.

RNA-seq data processing and differential expression analysis: Raw sequencing reads were quality-trimmed and filtered using Trimmomatic (v0.35) with parameters SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 and MINLEN:75. Clean reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10) using HISAT2 (default parameters, orientation mode). Alignment quality was assessed with Qualimap (v2.2.1). Gene-level read counts were obtained using HTSeq (v0.11.1). Gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis between genotypes was performed using the edgeR package (v3.38.4), with thresholds of |log2 fold change | > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p value < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis: Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment of all differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were initially performed using GOATOOLS and KOBAS, respectively, following standard RNA-seq analysis pipelines. From these analyses, selected KEGG pathways relevant to “plant-pathogen interaction” were reported in the main text based on statistical significance. To further examine genotype-specific transcriptional responses, particularly for uniquely upregulated gene sets (e.g., the 929 genes in max3-11), additional KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the STRING database with Arabidopsis thaliana as background. For these analyses, all genes within each defined subset were included without additional filtering beyond the DEG criteria (FDR < 0.05).

Root sampling and RNA-seq under sterile 1/2 MS plate system

Root samples under sterile 1/2 MS plate system were collected and processed following the same procedures as described above. Briefly, seedlings (Col-0, max3-11 and max4-8) were grown on 1/2 MS agar plates and harvested at 8 dpi after R. solanacearum inoculation. Root tissues were collected with 6 biological replicates per genotype, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subjected to RNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing, and downstream analysis as described for natural soil samples.

Rhizosphere soil sampling and untargeted LC-MS analysis under natural soil system

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from the same Arabidopsis plants used for root RNA-seq analysis, grown under natural soil system and harvested at 8 dpi with R. solanacearum. For each biological replicate (n = 6), rhizosphere soil was sampled by gently shaking off loosely attached bulk soil, and the tightly adhering soil at the root surface was collected into 2 ml tube and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until extraction. For untargeted metabolite profiling, 500 mg of frozen rhizosphere soil was thawed on ice and mixed with 1000 μl of 70% methanol containing internal standards. Samples were vortexed for 3 min, sonicated in an ice-water bath for 10 min, and incubated at −20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min, 300 μl of supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged again under the same conditions for 3 min. The final supernatant was used for LC-MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B), with a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min. The gradient program was follows: 95:5 (A:B, v/v) at 0 min, 10:90 at 11.0 min, held until 12.0 min, re-equilibrated to 95:5 at 12.1 min, and maintained until 14.0 min. The injection volume was 2 μl. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes following instrument-standardized acquisition protocols. Raw data were processed using in-house software to align retention times and extract peak intensities. Metabolite data were log2-transformed and mean-centered prior to analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) was first performed as an unsupervised method to assess data quality, overall variation, and sample clustering without prior class assignment. Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified based on VIP > 1, |log2 fold change | ≥ 1 and p value < 0.05. Supervised multivariate analysis was conducted using orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) implemented in the MetaboAnalystR package in R. Model robustness was evaluated using 200-permutation tests to avoid overfitting. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores derived from the OPLS-DA model were used to rank discriminative metabolites. Metabolite annotation was performed using the KEGG Compound database, followed by pathway mapping to KEGG Pathway entries. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using a hypergeometric test, and significantly enriched pathways were defined by Bonferroni-corrected p-value < 0.05.

Quantification of ABA biosynthetic genes expression by real-time PCR under natural soil system

Root samples used for qPCR validation of ABA biosynthetic genes were collected at 8 dpi from Arabidopsis plants grown under the same conditions and experimental batch as those used for RNA-seq analysis, but from different individual plants. Each biological replicate (n = 6) consisted of roots from one plant. Total RNA was isolated from the root samples using AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan)Co., Ltd., China), and reverse transcription was performed with Evo M-MLV Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., China) to synthesis cDNA. Expression analysis of the ABA biosynthesis genes (AtABA2, AtAAO3) was performed by quantitative real-time PCR using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) with Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The expression levels of AtABA2 and AtAAO3 were normalized to the internal reference gene Arabidopsis β-actin with the following primer sequences:

Forward: 5′- ATTCA GATGC CCAGA AGTCT TGT −3′

Reverse: 5′- GAAAC ATTTT CTGTG AACGA TTCC −3′

Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method91. The gene-specific primers for target genes are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Rhizosphere ABA measurement under natural soil system

Rhizosphere soil ABA levels were quantified from the same individual plants used for untargeted metabolomic profiling at 8 dpi. For each biological replicate (n = 6), approximately 1 g of rhizosphere soil was collected, suspended in 9 ml of PBS (PH 7.2–7.4, 0.01 mol l−1), vortexed thoroughly, and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and analyzed using plant ABA ELISA kit (purchased from Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay employed a double-antibody one-step sandwich ELISA format, in which ABA-specific monoclonal antibodies were pre-coated onto microtiter wells. A horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were added along with sample extracts, and color development was achieved using TMB (3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine) substrate. HRP catalyzed the oxidation of TMB to produce a blue-colored product, which was converted to yellow upon addition of stop solution. Absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, USA), and ABA concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated using serial dilutions of ABA standards provided with the kit.

Root exudate sampling and untargeted LC-MS analysis under sterile 1/2 MS plate system

To characterize root-derived metabolites in the absence of soil and microbiota, root exudates were collected from Arabidopsis plants grown in the sterile 1/2 MS plate system and harvested at 8 dpi with R. solanacearum infection following established protocols43,92. For each biological replicate (n = 6), seedlings were carefully removed from the agar medium, and residual agar adhering to the roots was gently removed by rinsing with sterile distilled water to minimize medium carryover. Intact root systems were then transferred into 5 ml sterile distilled water and incubated for 30 min under sterile conditions to allow the release of root exudates. The exudate solution was collected, passed through a 0.22-μm membrane filter to remove particulates, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until metabolite extraction. Untargeted LC-MS acquisition and downstream data processing were performed as described for the rhizosphere metabolomics analysis above.

Quantitation of R. solanacearum density in rhizosphere soil under natural soil system

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from the same Arabidopsis plants used for root RNA-seq analysis, grown under natural soil system and harvested at 8 dpi with R. solanacearum. For each biological replicate (n = 6), 0.5 g of rhizosphere soil was collected and total DNA was extracted from using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR targeting the fliC gene, encoding the flagellin protein of R. solanacearum, was performed to estimate bacterial abundance in the rhizosphere93. Specific primers used for fliC quantification were:

Forward: 5′- GAA CGC CAA CGG TGC GAA CT −3′

Reverse: 5′- GGC GGC CTT CAG GGA GGT C −3′

qPCR reactions were carried out in 20 μl volumes containing 10 μl of SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Japan), 0.4 μl of both forward and reverse primers (10 μM each), and 2 μl of template DNA. The qPCR analyses were carried out with an Applied Biosystems 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A post-PCR melting curve confirmed amplification specificity. Absolute quantification was achieved using a 10-fold serial dilution standard curve derived from a plasmid carrying the fliC fragment. Quantified gene copy numbers were normalized per gram of soil and expressed as fliC copies/g rhizosphere soil94.

Soil metagenome sequencing and microbial community analysis under natural soil system

DNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing: Rhizosphere soil samples (n = 6) were collected from the same individual plants used for root RNA-seq and metabolome profiling at 8 dpi, ensuring cross-omics comparability within each genotype. Total microbial DNA was extracted from rhizosphere soil samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality was assessed via Nanodrop spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis. Metagenomic shotgun sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced at Shanghai Biozeron Biological Technology Co. Ltd. Briefly, for each sample, 1 μg of genomic DNA was sheared by Covaris S220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Woburn, MAUSA) and sequencing libraries were prepared with a fragment length of approximately 450 bp. All samples were sequenced in the Illumina HiSeq X instrument with pair-end 150 bp (PE150) mode. Raw sequence reads underwent quality trimming using Trimmomatic (v0.39) to remove adapter contaminants and low quality reads95. Reads through quality control were then mapping against human genome (hg19) by BWA mem algorithm (parameters: -M-k 32-t 16, http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/bwa.shtml). The reads removing host-genome (Arabidopsis thaliana) contaminations and low-quality data were called as clean reads and used for the further analysis.

Reads-based phylogenetic annotation: Taxonomy of clean reads for each sample was determined by Kraken296 using the customized kraken database. The customized kraken database included all bacteria, archaea, fungi, virus, protozoa and algae genome sequences in NCBI RefSeq database (release number: 90). All reads were classified to seven phylogenetic levels (domain, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species) or unclassified. The abundances of taxonomy were estimated by Bracken which can produce accurate species- and genus-level abundance even in multiple near-identical species. Relative abundance of certain level in the article is a total of abundance of species belonging to certain level.

Metagenomic de novo assembly, gene prediction and annotation: Clean sequence reads were generated a set of contigs of each sample using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with “--min-contig-len 500” parameters97. The open reading frames (ORFs) of assembled contigs were predicted using Prodigal (v2.6.3)98, and all ORFs were generated to a set of unique genes after clustering using CD-HIT (parameters: -n 9-c 0.95-G 0-M 0-d 0-aS 0.9-r 1)99. The longest sequence of each cluster was considered as the representative sequence of each gene in the unique-gene set. Gene abundances was estimated using Salmon software100 based on read mapping to non-abundant gene set, following previously described methods101. This approach accounts for both uniquely mapped and multi-mapped reads during abundance estimation. The longest sequence of each cluster was selected as the representative sequence for the non-abundant gene set. Functional annotation was performed by searching against the KEGG databases using BLASTX to assign KEGG Orthology (KO) terms, followed by pathway mapping using the KEGG Pathway Database. Predicted genes sequences were translated into amino acid sequences and aligned against the Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes (CAZy) database102 and eggNOG database103 using DIAMOND (v0.9.14)104.

Data filtering and preparation for downstream analyses: Protein-coding genes were annotated using the NCBI NR (Non-redundant protein database) via DIAMOND. Resulting annotations were used to construct a taxonomic abundance table for downstream visualization and statistical analysis. To ensure robust data representation, taxa with zero counts in more than two-thirds (66%) of all samples were excluded. In addition, taxa with total read counts less than 20 across all samples were removed to reduce noise from low-abundance features. The resulting filtered abundance table, referred to as the cleaned NR dataset, was used for all subsequent analyses, including diversity estimation, differential abundance testing, and multivariate visualization. Unless otherwise specified, all microbial analyses presented in this study were based on the bacteria domain extracted from the cleaned NR dataset. Microbial community composition was analyzed based on relative abundance profiles at the genus level. Differential abundance between genotypes was assessed using two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, focusing on the top abundant taxa. Multivariate analyses, including principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, adonis function in vegan R package), were conducted to evaluate differences in community structure across genotypes and treatments. Unless otherwise specified, microbial community composition was assessed based on relative abundance estimates derived from shotgun metagenomic sequencing, which reflect the proportional representation of taxa within each sample rather than absolute microbial load.

Exogenous ABA and flavonoids treatment in soil-grown plants disease assays

To assess the role of ABA and flavonoids in plant defense under the natural soil conditions, 14-days-old Arabidopsis seedlings (Col-0, max3-11 and max4-8) with uniform growth status were transplanted into the specific the natural soil system described above. 7 days post-transplantation, plants received one of the following rhizosphere treatments: Treatment 1, 5 ml of 500 nM (+)-Abscisic acid (ABA, dissolved in 0.1% ethanol, purchased from Solarbio, China) per plant, applied directly to the rhizosphere soil. Treatment 2, 5 ml 0.1 mM Myricetin (flavonoids, dissolved in 0.1% ethanol, Sigma-Aldrich) per plant. Treatment 3, 5 ml of mixture of 500 nM (+)-Abscisic acid and 0.1 mM Myricetin per plant. Control, 5 ml sterile water (Mock treatment) per plant. All chemical solutions were freshly prepared with sterile distilled water. Each treatment included 8 biological replicates per genotype, with one plant considered as one biological replicate. Two days after treatment application, each plant was inoculated with R. solanacearum strain QL-Rs1115 by drenching 5 ml of bacterial suspension (OD600 = 0.55, ~ 1 × 107 c.f.u. ml−1) into the rhizosphere. Following inoculation, plants were maintained in the disease assay chamber. Disease progression was assessed at 8 dpi by scoring leaf wilting symptoms across genotypes. Disease severity was quantified using a normalized disease index (0–1 scale) based on wilting symptoms. For comparative purposes, disease responses in mutant lines were evaluated relative to the Col-0 control within each treatment condition.

KEGG functional annotation and differential pathway analysis of the rhizosphere microbiome under natural soil system

Based on the KEGG Orthology (KO) annotations derived from metagenomic datasets under natural soil system, we used the DESeq2 package (v1.38.0) in R (4.2.2) to explore the functional shifts in the rhizosphere microbiome across genotypes under R. solanacearum infection. The raw KO count matrix was normalized internally using the median-of-ratios method. Significantly enriched or depleted KO terms were identified using Wald test statistics, and adjusted p values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. KO terms with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and an absolute |log2 fold change | ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially abundant.

Selection and phylogenetic analysis of Pseudomonas strains

To generate a representative collection of Pseudomonas strains functional validation, we performed a targeted selection based on metagenomic profiling results. Taxonomic analysis of the rhizosphere metagenome indicated Pseudomonas as the most highly enriched bacterial genus in the disease-resistant genotype. To functionally assess this dominant taxon, we randomly selected twelve Pseudomonas isolates from our laboratory strain collection, prioritizing those with species-level names matching the metagenomic taxonomic annotations. All strains were previously isolated form diverse rhizosphere environments and stored in the lab database105. Identity of each strain was confirmed by 16S rRNA gene amplification using universal primers (27 F/1492 R), and species assignments were performed using BLASTn searches against the NCBI 16S rRNA database (≥ 98% identity threshold). A total of 12 Pseudomonas strains were included in subsequent experiments (PSE1: P. baetica, PSE2: P. brassicacearum, PSE3: P. chlororaphis, PSE4: P. donghuensis, PSE5: P. fluorescens, PSE6: P. Koreensis, PSE7: P. mohnii, PSE8: P. monteilii, PSE9: P. moorei, PSE10: P. protegens, PSE11: P. putida, PSE12: P. thivervalensis), covering a taxonomic breadth representative of the Pseudomonas genus detected in the metagenome. To assess phylogenetic relationships among these isolates, 16S rRNA sequences were aligned using ClustalW and a maximum likelihood (ML) tree was constructed in MEGA X (v10.2.6) with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree is shown in Supplementary Fig. 14b, and detailed strain information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

In vitro co-culture assay between Pseudomonas isolates and R. solanacearum

Exogenous ABA and flavonoids co-treatment in synthetic Pseudomonas communities

To assess the impact of exogenous ABA and flavonoids on the growth of Pseudomonas synthetic communities (SynComs), in vitro SynCom assays with or without metabolites supplementation were performed using two randomly assembled SynComs: SynCom-A (consisting of eight isolates, P. baetica, P. brassicacearum, P. chlororaphis, P. koreensis, P. moorei, P. protegens, P. putida, P. thivervalensis) and SynCom-B (consisting of ten isolates, P. baetica, P. brassicacearum, P. chlororaphis, P. fluorescens, P. koreensis, P. mohnii, P. moorei, P. protegens, P. putida, P. thivervalensis). These isolates were pre-screened based on in vitro co-culture assays and exhibited no significant effect on R. solanacearum growth. Each Pseudomonas isolate was grown overnight in LB medium (4 ml) at 30 °C with shaking at 170 r.p.m. in 6-well plates. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 5 min, washed twice, and resuspended in sterile water to an optical density (OD600) of 0.5. Equal volumes of individual isolate were mixed to construct the SynCom, resulting in a final OD600 of 0.5. For chemical treatment, 20 μl of the SynCom suspension was inoculated into 160 μl of 1/10 strength NB in 96-well plates, followed by the addition of 20 μl of ABA and myricetin mixture (final concentrations: 500 nM (+)-Abscisic acid and 0.1 mM Myricetin). For the control group, an equal volume (20 μl) of sterile water was added instead. All cultures were incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 170 r.p.m. for 48 h. Bacterial growth was quantified by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using the microplate reader. Each treatment was performed with 8 biological replicates.

In vitro planta validation of SynCom-chemical interactions in disease suppression:

RNA-seq analysis of bacterial co-cultures

Bacterial pellets were harvested after 48 h of in vitro co-culture of R. solanacearum and Pseudomonas SynCom (SynCom-A) consisting of eight isolates: P. baetica, P. brassicacearum, P. chlororaphis, P. koreensis, P. moorei, P. protegens, P. putida and P. thivervalensis. The culture was grown in 1/10 NB medium at 30 °C with shaking at 170 r.p.m. For each biological replicate (n = 5), total RNA was extracted from an independent co-culture using TRIzol® reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen), and genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (TaKara). Then RNA quality was determined using 2100 Bioanalyser (Agilent) and quantified using the ND-2000 (Nanodrop Technologies). High-quality RNA sample (OD260/280 = 1.8 ~ 2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RIN ≥ 6.5, 23S:16S ≥ 1.0, > 10 μg) is used to construct sequencing library. RNA-seq strand-specific libraries were prepared following TruSeq RNA sample preparation Kit from Illumina (San Diego, CA), using 5 μg of total RNA. Shortly, rRNA removal by RiboZero rRNAremoval kit (Epicenter), fragmented using fragmentation buffer. cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-base addition and ligation of the Illumina-indexed adapters were performed according to Illumina’s protocol. Libraries were then size selected for cDNA target fragments of 200–300 bp on 2% Low Range Ultra Agarose followed by PCR amplified using Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB) for 15 PCR cycles. After quantified by TBS380, Paired-end libraries were sequenced by Illumina Nova Seq 6000 sequencing (150 bp*2, Shanghai BIOZERON Co., Ltd). The raw paired end reads were trimmed and quality controlled by Trimmomatic with parameters (SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:75). Then clean reads were separately aligned to reference genome with orientation mode using Rockhopper (http://cs.wellesley.edu/~btjaden/Rockhopper/) software. Rockhopper was a comprehensive and user-friendly system for computational analysis of bacterial RNA-seq data. As input, Rockhopper takes RNA sequencing reads generated by high-throughput sequencing technology. This software was used to calculate gene expression levels with default parameters. Differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package with thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC | ≥ 1. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was conducted using GO and KEGG databases in R (v4.2.1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R software (v4.2.1) unless otherwise specified. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc least significant difference (LSD) tests was used to assess differences among genotypes across multiple groups. For pairwise comparisons, two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied. Exact n values for biological replicates are provided in the corresponding figure legends. Box plots represent the median (center line), first and third quartiles (box bounds), and whiskers extending to 1.5 times the interquartile range. Bar plots show the mean ± standard error of the mean (s. e. m.), unless otherwise stated. For dot-and-line plots, data points represent group means and error bars indicate standard deviation (SD), reflecting data dispersion. Levels of significance were indicated as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****), unless otherwise stated. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess pairwise relationships between variables. Mantel tests based on Pearson distance matrices were used to evaluate correlations among multiple datasets (e.g., transcriptome-metabolome-microbiome integration). All schematic diagrams, including experimental design and summary figures, were created with Biorender.com.
