Long‐term plasma monitoring of THC and CBD in pediatric drug‐resistant epilepsy: Implications for cannabidiol therapy with Epidyolex®
Authors: Alessia Cafaro, Antonella Riva, Federica Pigliasco, Sebastiano Barco, Valentina Manca, Maria Stella Vari, Maria Margherita Mancardi, Lino Nobili, Simona Pichini, Francesco Paolo Busardò, Pasquale Striano, Giuliana Cangemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated long‐term plasma concentrations of cannabidiol (CBD) and Δ9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) in children receiving highly purified cannabidiol (hpCBD; Epidyolex®) therapy for drug‐resistant epilepsy. A retrospective analysis was performed on 38 patients with a median age of 11 years (IQR, 5) treated in a single center from 2019 to 2024. Incremental doses of Epidyolex® (mean dose: 13.6 mg/kg/day) were administered, and plasma levels of CBD and THC were measured using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). THC remained consistently undetectable (<0.2 μg/L) at all time points, confirming the negligible systemic exposure. CBD levels increased dose‐dependently, reaching a median concentration of 89.8 μg/L (IQR, 112.2) at final follow‐up, demonstrating a significant positive correlation (p < 0.0001) between dosage and plasma concentration. Epidyolex® treatment effectively maintains therapeutic CBD levels without detectable THC, supporting the safety and suitability of this therapy. INTRODUCTION

The most studied phytocannabinoids in Cannabis sativa are cannabidiol (CBD) and Δ9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9‐THC). 1 These cannabinoids act on CB1 and CB2 receptors, metabotropic G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) that primarily signal through inhibitory G proteins (Gi and Go). 2 , 3 , 4 Δ9‐THC is the main responsible for cannabi's psychoactive effects, while CBD has a minimal euphoric effect due to its low CB1 affinity. 5 CBD exhibits a broad spectrum of therapeutic properties, including antiseizure, antipsychotic, anxiolytic, antiemetic, neuroprotective, anticancer, and sleep‐promoting effects. 5 Its antiseizure activity, for instance, appears to involve multiple targets 6 : CBD acts as a functional antagonist at GPR55 receptors, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 desensitizes transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1 (TRPV1) channels, 6 , 11 and inhibits the equilibrative nucleoside transporter 1 (ENT1), 6 thereby enhancing extracellular adenosine levels.

Highly purified CBD (hpCBD; Epidyolex®) is approved as add‐on therapy for drug‐resistant epilepsy in Dravet Syndrome (DS), Lennox–Gastaut Syndrome (LGS), and Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC). However, the use is going beyond the labeled indications, showing promising results also in other drug‐resistant epilepsies. 12 Epidyolex®, with a Δ9‐THC content of no more than 0.15%, has no recognized potential for abuse or risk of dependence. 13 The legal status of CBD, particularly when derived from cannabis extracts, in Italy has remained unstable, with frequent regulatory changes since 2020 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 and no final decision yet reached. These ongoing regulatory shifts have created significant challenges for both patients and the pharmaceutical industry. Frequent changes to prescription and dispensing requirements have made treatment access unpredictable and complicated the work of healthcare providers. Simultaneously, companies must continuously update packaging, labeling, and regulatory documentation, leading to increased costs and logistical difficulties. This persistent instability highlights the urgent need for a clearer, more consistent regulatory framework to ensure continuity in patient care and business operations.

Herein, we describe the C trough levels of THC and CBD in a cohort of children with drug‐resistant epilepsy receiving Epidyolex® as adjunctive therapy, aiming to assess whether long‐term treatment leads to systemic THC exposure or accumulation.

METHODS

A retrospective single‐center cohort study was conducted on pediatric patients treated at a third‐level pediatric hospital in Genoa, Italy. The study adhered to the ethical standards set by the Institutional and National Research Committees and complied with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2013. As the study was purely observational and based on retrospectively collected, fully anonymized data, ethical committee approval was not required. The data were retrieved from an anonymized laboratory database. Moreover, informed consent for the anonymous use of clinical data for research purposes is routinely obtained at the time of patient admission or diagnostic procedures. Patients were included in the study if they were receiving Epidyolex® as adjunctive therapy for drug‐resistant epilepsy. In accordance with routine clinical practice, plasma predose (C trough) levels of both CBD and THC were measured using a previously described validated microsampling method based on liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). 18 All concentrations measured during the period from 2019 to 2024 were included in the analysis. Epidyolex® dosage was initiated at approximately 2.5 mg/kg/day and increased by increments of 2.5–5 mg/kg/day every 1–2 weeks, according to patient‐specific seizure control, tolerability, and therapeutic drug monitoring outcomes, reaching typical therapeutic doses between 10 and 20 mg/kg/day.

Descriptive statistics were computed for the entire cohort. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while nonnormally distributed variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationship between CBD plasma concentrations and CBD dosage. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with all p‐values derived from two‐tailed tests. All the statistical analyses were carried out with Medcalc (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).

RESULTS

Description of the cohort

The study included 38 children (22 boys) with drug‐resistant epilepsy and a median age of 11 years (IQR, 5). The cohort included patients diagnosed with DS (8 patients, 21.05%), TSC (4 patients, 10.53%), LGS (1 patient, 2.63%), Rett syndrome (2 patients, 5.26%), postherpetic drug‐resistant epilepsy (1 patient, 2.63%), PCDH19‐related epilepsy (1 patient, 2.63%), Kleefstra syndrome (1 patient, 2.63%), alternating hemiplegia (1 patient, 2.63%), epilepsy with myoclonic‐atonic seizures (EMAS) (1 patient, 2.63%), developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) (17 patients, 44.74%), and CDKL5 deficiency disorder (1 patient, 2.63%). The mean number of follow‐up visits per patient was 2.7 ± 3.4, with a median follow‐up duration of 17 months (IQR, 23). All patients received escalating doses of Epidyolex®, with a mean dose of 13.6 mg/kg/day.

CBD and THC plasma levels

A total of 103 samples were analyzed. CBD plasma concentrations‐to‐dose ratios (C/D ratios) at each follow‐up visit for each patient are shown in Figure 1. At the final follow‐up, the median plasma concentration of CBD was 89.8 μg/L (IQR, 112.2), while THC levels remained consistently undetectable (below 0.2 μg/L) at all time points, despite the incremental doses of Epidyolex® administered. An example of chromatograms obtained from a patient sample is shown in Figure 2A, where CBD, CBD‐d3, and THC‐d3 are clearly visible and properly integrated, while no peak for THC is observed. A significant positive correlation between CBD plasma concentrations and dosage (mg/kg) was observed (Figure 2B) (p < 0.0001), whereas THC was undetectable in plasma at all dosage levels.

DISCUSSION

We describe the assessment of the plasma levels of CBD and THC in children treated with hpCBD (Epidyolex®) for drug‐resistant epilepsy. Previous reports from the Italian Expanded Access Program demonstrated improved seizure control and a favorable safety profile of cannabidiol therapy in highly refractory epilepsy patients, regardless of concomitant clobazam use. 19 The present findings are consistent with the expected effects of hpCBD. We showed that CBD concentrations increased with incremental doses of Epidyolex®, while THC remained undetectable across all time points, irrespective of the dose administered. These results are consistent with existing clinical evidence indicating the absence of THC‐related effects, further confirming the absence of accumulation at therapeutic dosages. Minimally invasive approaches such as venipuncture to assess plasma concentrations have shown to be good predictors of accumulation also in other tissues (e.g., adipose tissue, skeletal muscle and liver). 20 The lack of detectable THC is significant from a clinical perspective, as it supports the therapeutic use of Epidyolex® without the associated risks of THC. The median plasma concentration of CBD at the final follow‐up was 89.8 μg/L (IQR, 112.2), showing a significant positive correlation with the administered dosage (p < 0.0001). This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that CBD plasma concentrations are dose‐dependent, 18 , 21 , 22 , 23 underscoring the importance of individualized dosing to achieve therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential side effects. A previous study 23 proposed a CBD plasma reference range of 50–150 ng/mL. However, we observed greater variability among our patients, with several values surpassing the proposed upper threshold. This finding suggests the need for an individualized approach when interpreting CBD plasma levels, as factors such as metabolism, body weight, and drug–drug interactions could contribute to this variability.

The observed safety profile and absence of THC accumulation validate Epidyolex® as a reliable therapeutic option, underscoring the importance of routine therapeutic drug monitoring. Regular monitoring protocols are essential not only for ensuring patient safety but also for guiding clinical decision‐making regarding dose adjustments. Given the evolving regulatory landscape surrounding cannabinoid therapies, establishing clear, standardized monitoring guidelines will be indispensable for consistent clinical practice and optimal patient management.

CONCLUSION

This study describes plasma concentrations of CBD and the absence of detectable THC in pediatric patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy receiving hpCBD (Epidyolex®) as adjunctive therapy. The consistent lack of THC detection across all measured time points and dose levels suggests that systemic exposure to THC remains negligible, even with prolonged administration.
