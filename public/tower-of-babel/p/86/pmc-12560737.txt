Case Study of an Unusual Intracranial Mesenchymal Neoplasm
Authors: Camille Carlisle, Alexandra Lesko, Vassil Kaimaktchiev, Vivek Deshmukh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE:A 51-year-old woman with multiple sclerosis (MS) presented for MS management and follow-up, including repeat MRIs. On imaging, a slowly growing enhancing lesion was noted in the left posterior insula. Histopathology reported this tumor as a low-grade mesenchymal neoplasm, not elsewhere classified. The case reported here will add to the library of central nervous system tumors, which may help identify other previously unclassifiable tumors.CLINICAL PRESENTATION:The lesion was inconsistent with MS plaques, and MRI and computed tomography angiography showed no signs of aneurysm. After evaluation, we recommended surgery to remove the lesion. Surgical excision posed a challenge as the dominant hemisphere posterior insula can be difficult to access. We used a trans-sylvian approach, sparing vasculature within the sylvian fissure with clean excisional margins. The trans-sylvian approach enabled minimal manipulation of brain tissue surrounding the lesion, including the receptive speech center. The patient recovered without complications. Postsurgical follow-up revealed no new neurological symptoms or deficits and no sign of tumor recurrence.CONCLUSION:The trans-sylvian approach we used to excise the tumor resulted in a favorable outcome for the patient. This case supports that the trans-sylvian approach, while technically more demanding, is feasible and potentially beneficial.

Standard of care for multiple sclerosis (MS) includes repeat central nervous system (CNS) MRIs to track disease progression and treatment efficacy.1 Repeat MRIs may detect other pathologies in addition to the underlying MS, such as brain tumors. There is no clear association between MS and tumor growth. However, published literature includes hypotheses that patients with MS may be at increased risk of CNS cancer. For example, remyelination of MS plaques may promote tumor growth, there may be a common genetic factor between MS and tumor growth, and the possible role of an underlying viral agent (such as John Cunningham virus) that promotes neoplastic growth.2

Our patient presented with numbness to her feet. On imaging, a slowly growing enhancing lesion was noted in the left posterior insula. The lesion was inconsistent with MS plaques, and MRI and computed tomography angiography showed no signs of aneurysm. After evaluation, we recommended surgery to remove the lesion.

The location of the tumor in the dominant hemisphere posterior insula posed a challenge. This is a difficult region to access safely without incurring neurological symptoms or deficits. Surgical strategies to access this region have been described and include the transcortical and trans-sylvian approaches.3,4

Histopathology reported the tumor as a low-grade mesenchymal neoplasm, not elsewhere classified. The case reported here will add to the library of CNS tumors, which may help identify other previously unclassifiable tumors. Furthermore, the trans-sylvian approach we used resulted in a favorable outcome for the patient. This case supports that the trans-sylvian approach, while technically more demanding, is feasible and potentially beneficial.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

The patient in this case was a 51-year-old White, Hispanic, right-handed woman with a history of relapsing remitting MS and hypertension. The patient was diagnosed with MS at the age of 39 years.

Ethics and Informed Consent

The patient's consent was obtained for this case report through our institution's Human Research Protection Program. This case study was approved by the Providence Saint Joseph Institutional Review Board.

Medical History

The patient first presented with numbness to the balls of her feet in 2009 and was diagnosed with MS. She had initial success with intravascular steroid infusion. Then, she went through several different rounds of monoclonal antibody and other immunosuppressive treatments including interferon beta-1, fingolimod, dimethyl fumarate, teriflunomide, and ocrelizumab. She was placed on teriflunomide as her MS symptoms were largely benign. Additional medications the patient used included metoprolol and losartan for hypertension management. Her MS had been stable on repeat brain MRIs.

Primary Concerns and Symptoms

The patient reported occasional numbness to her hands and feet that would resolve within 1 to 2 days. However, she had no recurrence of an MS episode since 2009. In 2016, a routine brain MRI to monitor MS progression found a new enhancing lesion in the left posterior sylvian fissure. While the patient reported headaches, she denied any other new symptoms that could be associated with the lesion. The slowly growing lesion did not resemble a typical MS plaque and was monitored for 4 years before intervention.

Timeline

A timeline of care is shown in Figure 1. In 2016, a brain MRI revealed a new enhancing lesion in the posterior insula with a differential diagnosis of a 3-mm vascular lesion or neoplasm. However, a 2016 magnetic resonance angiography showed no signs of aneurysm. Subsequent MRIs described differentials of thrombosed aneurysm vs atypical cavernoma, leptomeningeal or superficial cortical mass. In late 2021, neurosurgical evaluation determined that the lesion had grown to 7 mm. Cerebral catheter angiography revealed no aneurysm or vascular malformation. We offered the patient the choice of continued observation or microsurgical excision. Given the growth of the lesion, the patient's young age, and otherwise good health, we advised microsurgical excision. Continued enlargement of the lesion would also increase risk of surgical excision. In 2022, the mass was removed. The patient had an uncomplicated postoperative course. Follow-up MRIs and neurology visits showed no recurrence of symptoms. In the autumn of 2023 during a follow-up examination, the patient had impaired vibration sensation to her bilateral big toes. This symptom was attributed to her MS rather than the excised tumor.

Therapeutic Interventions

Trans-sylvian and transcortical approaches are two methods to access the posterior insula. In our case, the trans-sylvian approach was used to minimize risk to the Wernicke area. On preoperative imaging, we identified a direct route to the lesion through a sulcus at the posterior sylvian fissure. A posterior temporal craniotomy was fashioned over the region of the lesion. Using MRI-based neuronavigation and the operating microscope, the arachnoid overlying the posterior sylvian fissure was opened. Distal middle cerebral artery branches were preserved, and the use of electrocautery was minimized to mitigate risk to the vessels. The arachnoid bands were opened in the sulcus to reach the exophytic posterior insular tumor. Using microsurgical dissection techniques, the tumor was resected by establishing a clear boundary from the surrounding insula. The lesion was removed en bloc with a gross total resection (Video 1).

Diagnostic Assessment

After extensive diagnostic workup including histological examination with immunohistochemistry and sequencing, the pathology material was sent to the University of California San Francisco and finally to the National Institutes of Health National Cancer Institute for DNA methylation-based profiling. The final diagnosis was a “morphologically low-grade mesenchymal neoplasm, not elsewhere classified.” Despite the most recent expansion of the 2021 World Health Organization Guidelines,5 this tumor could not be classified within 1 category.

The tumor had some morphological features of perineurioma, schwannoma, and gliofibroma. However, it did not meet criteria for these classifications in full (Figure 2 immunohistochemistry Images). Sequencing was negative for nucleotide insertions, deletions, amplifications, rearrangements, or other variants. There was no loss of heterozygosity and no chromosomal copy number changes. The tumor was microsatellite stable, and the tumor mutational burden was low (3.4 mutations/mutational burden). Vestigial-like family fusions recently described in an intraparenchymal central nervous system schwannoma6 were not part of the panel at the time the study was conducted.

There was an in-frame insertion variant of the fumarate hydratase tumor suppressor gene. This variant was of unknown significance.7

The tumor did not meet any criteria for methylation classes. The National Institutes of Health did not detect any fusion abnormalities. Intracranial mesenchymal tumors with FET-CREB (FUS, EWSR1, TAF-15 and cAMP-responsive element-binding) fusion8 and another with EWSR1:CREM (Ewing sarcoma breakpoint region 1 and cAMP-responsive elemend modulators) fusion9 have recently been documented. However, our tumor could not be classified as either of those tumors. Since this tumor does not fit any known diagnostic entity in the current 2021 classification of CNS tumors, a descriptive diagnosis was rendered.

Follow-up and Outcomes

After surgery, the patient showed no new neurological symptoms or deficits. In fact, by our assessments, the patient is neurologically normal. The patient reported mild urinary incontinence and was referred to urology for management. Follow-up MRIs have shown no evidence of recurrent tumor. We determined that this low-grade mesenchymal neoplasm was otherwise benign and would not affect the management of the patient's MS.

DISCUSSION

Primary mesenchymal tumors in the brain, while rare, have been described in previous literature.10 However, the tumor type found in this patient has not been classified elsewhere. The case reported here will add to the library of CNS tumors, which may help identify other, previously unclassifiable tumors. In this case, the trans-sylvian approach used to excise the tumor resulted in a favorable outcome for the patient.

CONCLUSION

This case supports that the trans-sylvian approach, while technically difficult, is possible and beneficial. The trans-sylvian approach was chosen because it enabled minimal manipulation of the brain tissue surrounding the tumor, which included areas associated with important functions, including speech.
