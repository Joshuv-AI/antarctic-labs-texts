Psychotropic prescription trends in jails from 2013 to 2023: findings from the REACH database
Authors: Amber H. Simpler, Adam P. Natoli, William Jett, Yash A. Patade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In light of escalating concerns about the increasing number of individuals in United States’ jails with mental health conditions, the current investigation sought to examine population trends in psychotropic prescription patterns in 34 jails over an 11-year epoch. Leveraging data from a largescale, multisite database derived from 1,251,837 jail detainees’ electronic health records (i.e., the Repository of Electronic Archives in Correctional Healthcare, or REACH, database), General Estimating Equations (GEE) models were used to estimate population-averaged probabilities of prescriptions for any psychotropic agent and specific agent classes (e.g., antianxiety, antidepressant, antipsychotic, anticonvulsant). While GEE analysis revealed year-to-year variability, overall significant increases (i.e., > 100%) in prescription probability were observed for all agent classes from 2013 to 2023 except lithium, which declined significantly over time. Notably, the prescription probability for antipsychotic agents increased 249% during the study epoch. These findings add further evidence of the increasing mental health needs of jail populations. To better understand the increase in psychotropic prescriptions among jail detainees, additional inquiries should explore the clinical justification, therapeutic value, and impact of treatment compliance.

Introduction

While it is broadly recognized incarcerated individuals experience mental health problems more often than the general population1–6, there is wide variability and fluctuation in reported rates of mental health concerns among individuals detained in jail. Despite lack of consensus to explain these trends, researchers generally agree rates of mental health problems – especially severe mental illness (SMI) – among individuals in jail custody (IJC) have continually increased7–10.

Teplin’s research from the 1980s and 1990s estimated rates of SMI in a jail population in Cook County, Illinois to be 6.4% among male inmates7. In 1999, the Bureau of Justice Statistics noted 10.5% of IJC self-reported a “mental or emotional condition”8. In 2006, the bureau reported approximately 14% of IJC disclosed use of at least one medication for mental health symptoms (hereafter, psychotropic agents) in the year prior to their arrest, while 64% of IJC exhibited signs or symptoms of severe psychopathology9. In 2009, Steadman and colleagues provided an overall rate of 14.5% of individuals booked into four different county jails at two different points in time, though specific county rates ranged from 7.7% to 20.8%5. Employing a cross-sectional design to examine rates of prescribed pharmacological treatment among IJC nationwide, Simpler and colleagues10 observed an increase in overall prescription rates of psychotropic agents from 25.7% in 2019 to 32.5% in 2023.

Although these reported rates suggest a continual increase of mental health issues among jail populations, examination of dissimilar samples across time and inconsistencies in the operationalization and measurement of mental health conditions contribute to confusion about the purported increase in SMI among IJC. Moreover, the extent to which these rates reflect unique individuals versus repeated cycling of the same individuals with mental health conditions is not clear. Arguably, this distinction is relevant in light of research suggesting increased odds of positive mental health screenings with each subsequent booking11 and high rates of re-arrest for individuals diagnosed with SMI compared to non-SMI counterparts12,13.

Despite urgent requests to track and monitor the healthcare needs of incarcerated individuals4,14–16, knowledge of ICJ’s mental health needs remains incomplete. Jails carry a heavy, disproportionate disease burden and serve as the primary—often only—source of healthcare for many individuals17. Thus, it is imperative to understand the extent and severity of health needs among IJC to inform resource allocation and respond to demands for improved correctional healthcare.

In an effort to further elucidate jail population mental health trends, the present study leveraged a largescale, multisite database derived from electronic health records (EHR) of individuals discharged from 34 jails across the United States between 2013 and 2023. Detailed elsewhere, the Registry of Electronic Archives in Correctional Healthcare (REACH) database was established, in part, to better understand the evolving landscape and historical trends of correctional healthcare, as well as inform predictive models to facilitate growing healthcare service demands18. Primary REACH database elements include medications (or agents) prescribed to individuals while in custody, as well as demographic variables, discharge histories, and various clinical indicators (e.g., urine drug screen results, emergency department utilization).

Researchers have leveraged prescription databases to confidently predict healthcare utilization and costs19,20, estimate presence and number of health conditions21, and describe disease prevalence and severity22,23. Compared to administrative databases, these clinical databases are viewed as highly accurate, consistent, and complete given the regulations surrounding prescription medication records24. Moreover, real-world clinical data offers insights about actual heterogenous patient populations that may otherwise go undetected with necessarily rigid randomized clinical trials or other strict research designs25.

Focusing exclusively on mental health conditions, the current investigation aimed to identify population trends in psychotropic prescription patterns in jails over an 11-year epoch. Specifically, we examined longitudinal trends of prescriptions for any psychotropic agent as a global estimate of changes in mental health needs over time and specific classes of psychotropic agents to characterize changes in the types of symptoms/conditions for which IJC were prescribed treatment.

Method

Data source

Detailed fully in18, the REACH database was established through a data sharing agreement between a for-profit national correctional healthcare company (NaphCare, Inc.) and a non-profit research organization (NaphCare Charitable Foundation, Inc.). Deidentified health information was extracted from the healthcare company’s proprietary EHR system for all individuals who discharged from 34 jails across the United States between January 01, 2013 and December 31, 2023. Included jails held contractual agreements with NaphCare, Inc. for the provision of comprehensive healthcare services, and only data generated during contractual periods were provided.

Ethics declaration

This study was approved by a research ethics committee as IRB-exempt. Specifically, Salus IRB determined projects making use of the archival, retrospective data contained in the REACH Database are not considered human subjects research warranting IRB approval (Case ID# 23120-01). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study. All individuals who contributed to these data provided general informed consent to receive jail medical services, which included notification about release of deidentified data for health research purposes. This study was conducted in accordance with all relevant guidelines and regulations.

Current study sample

Initial data extraction yielded information pertaining to 2,734,096 jail discharges. Data were organized by discharges instead of unique individuals or initial bookings to capture repeated arrest/booking/discharge instances and clinical data from the entire detention period. Preprocessing strategies removed data for four entire sites due to systematic omission of primary variables (e.g., race). Discharges occurring outside the contractual period and instances whereby individuals who were less than 18 years old at time of discharge were also removed from the dataset. Review of box plots and frequency counts revealed extreme values likely indicative of initial data entry errors. A one-sided trimming procedure was used to remove discharges with the 0.01% longest lengths of stays (LOSs). All prior and subsequent discharges for those individuals were also removed to avoid misrepresentation of rebooking/discharge trends. Finally, anonymized participant IDs were created to isolate unique individuals and identify repeat discharge instances, which were labeled to reflect temporal order overall and within a given year. The final sample included 2,527,808 discharges for 1,251,837 unique individuals.

Relevant database elements

Demography. Age at the time of discharge was captured for each instance. Race and ethnicity and assigned sex were based on data listed in the health record (i.e., not self-reported information). Because race/ethnicity and sex data are initially entered into each facility’s jail management system (JMS) at the time of booking and transmitted via electronic interface to the EHR, these datapoints cannot be altered by healthcare personnel to ensure consistency between the systems. Within this context, race and ethnicity categories aligned with American Medical Association26 and American Psychological Association27 publication guidelines: Asian, Black or African American, Indigenous, Latine/o/a/x, MENA (Middle Eastern North African), Multiracial, South Asian, White, and Unknown (i.e., not documented in the health record). Assigned sex categories included Female, Male, and Unknown (i.e., not documented in the health record).

Prescribed Psychotropic Medications. The Master Drug Database (MDDB) is MediSpan’s proprietary coding and ontological system of pharmaceutical agents28 and includes Generic Product Identifiers (GPIs). GPIs rely on unique 14-digit strings to categorize agents by therapeutic class, agent class/subclass, and product details (e.g., base agent name, dose form, and dose amount). The current study focused exclusively on agents targeting psychiatric conditions, the majority of which are catalogued in the Central Nervous System (CNS) therapeutic class and target conditions impacting brain and spinal cord functions. Medispan’s CNS therapeutic class includes the following agent classes: Antianxiety, Antidepressant, Antipsychotic, Hypnotics, Stimulants, and Miscellaneous Psychotherapeutics. Although lithium is considered an antipsychotic agent under Medispan’s classification, it was treated as a separate agent class in the current study given its primary use to treat mood instability29.

Anticonvulsant agents—from the Neuromuscular therapeutic class—were also included in select instances when there was indication of off-label prescribing for psychiatric symptoms, such as affective instability. Specifically, prescription of an anticonvulsant was recognized as psychiatric treatment when there was no indication the individual experienced a seizure disorder or underwent detoxification/withdrawal protocols during the detention period30,31. Data analyses

Descriptive statistics (e.g., frequency distribution, central tendency) were calculated to characterize population demography (e.g., sex, race and ethnicity, age) and detail rates of psychotropic medication prescriptions generally and with regard to specific agent classes. General Estimating Equation (GEE) modeling—a repeated measures methodology—was used to estimate the population-averaged probability of psychotropic prescription over the years using a binary logistic regression approach with Firth-type penalizations to provide bias-corrected finite estimates of coefficients appropriate for use with unbalanced samples and low base rate events32. Within-person dependency, due to bookings being nested within individuals, was accounted for using an exchangeable correlation structure. Predicted absolute probabilities for each year, based on the logistic regression model with penalizations, were also calculated. GEE methods are preferred to examine longitudinal, clustered, and other correlated (especially binary) data due to their ability to handle unmeasured dependence between outcomes33. GEE generated coefficient estimates depict changes in population mean while making no assumptions about the underlying data distribution, though sufficiently large samples, such as that used in the current study, are required34. Although Firth’s penalization was used to appropriately handle the low base rates of prescriptions, the exceptionally low base rates for hypnotic, stimulant, and miscellaneous psychotherapeutic prescriptions (≤ 0.1%) prohibited their statistical examination. Therefore, population averaged probabilities were only estimated for antianxiety, antidepressant, antipsychotic, anticonvulsant, and lithium agent classes. GEE analysis was performed in R using the geepack35 and logistf36 packages.

Results

Sample demography

Overall, the sample (N = 1,251,837 unique individuals) was primarily comprised of males (72.0%) who were, on average, 35.9 years old (SD = 12.4) at the time of their most recent discharge. Females (28.0%) were, on average, 34.4 years old (SD = 11.5) at the time of their most recent discharge. Approximately 55.3% of the sample were identified in the health record as White and 33.5% as Black or African American. No race data were documented for 6.7% of the sample (classified as “Unknown Race”). Individuals identified as Asian, Indigenous, Latine/o/a/x, MENA, Multiracial, or South Asian comprised the remaining 4.5% of the sample. Table 2 contains additional details about sample demography.

Psychotropic agent prescription rates

Approximately 17.4% of IJC who discharged in 2023 were prescribed at least one psychotropic agent during their respective detention period, compared to 8.6% of IJC who discharged in 2013. Detailed below, increased prescription rates were observed across four agent classes (i.e., antianxiety, antidepressant, anticonvulsant, antipsychotic). Decreased rates were observed for agents within the hypnotic and lithium classes, while no statistically significant changes were observed in rates of stimulant or miscellaneous psychotherapeutic classes.

In 2013, approximately 3.1% of the sample were prescribed agents from two or more CNS agent classes. This rate increased approximately 1% annually until 2019. Between 2019 and 2023, rates of multiple CNS agent prescriptions were between 7.1% and 7.3%, with a slight decrease observed in 2022 (i.e., 6.7%).

After isolating cases where any CNS agent was prescribed, discharges were dichotomized based on the presence (n = 115,208) or absence (n = 253,049) of antipsychotic agent prescription. Medications from two or more CNS agent classes were prescribed in 69.4% of discharges where an antipsychotic prescription was present, but only 28.5% of discharges where antipsychotic prescription was absent.

Population trends in psychotropic agent prescriptions

GEE analysis revealed significant changes in psychotropic prescription probabilities over the 11-year epoch (2013–2023), with the odds of being prescribed one or more psychotropic medications in 2023 being 2.24 times higher than in 2013 (95% CI [2.19, 2.30]). This represents a relative increase of approximately 103% in prescription probability, from 8.6% in 2013 to 17.4% in 2023. Although an overall upward trend in psychotropic prescription probability was seen between 2013 and 2023, sequential comparisons between consecutive years revealed variation in the year-to-year increases. Except for 2021, which demonstrated a small, significant decrease in psychotropic prescriptions compared to 2020 (OR = 0.95; 95% CI [0.94, 0.97]), all year-to-year increases were significant. The largest year-to-year increase occurred from 2014 to 2015, with an odds ratio of 1.23 (95% CI [1.21, 1.26]), reflecting the odds of receiving a psychotropic prescription in 2015 were 23% higher than in 2014. Population trends of psychotropic prescriptions for specific agent classes

Antianxiety. GEE analysis revealed significant changes in antianxiety prescription probabilities, demonstrating an overall upward trend over the 11-year epoch. Specifically, the odds of being prescribed one or more antianxiety agents in 2023 was found to be 2.12 times higher than in 2013 (95% CI [2.04, 2.20]). This represents a relative increase of approximately 104% in prescription probability, from 3.68% in 2013 to 7.49% in 2023. No significant change in year-to-year prescription probability was observed between 2020 and 2021. Significant decreases were observed between 2013 and 2014, 2017 and 2018, and 2021 and 2022, with the greatest decrease between 2013 and 2014 (OR = 0.93; 95% CI [0.89, 0.97]). Conversely, significant increases were seen between all other years, with the largest year-to-year increase occurring between 2014 and 2015, with an odds ratio of 1.50 (95% CI [1.45, 1.55]), reflecting the odds of receiving an antianxiety prescription in 2015 being 50% higher than in 2014.

Antidepressant. Significant changes in antidepressant prescription probabilities between 2013 and 2023 were also observed, revealing the odds of being prescribed one or more antidepressant agent in 2023 were 2.08 times higher than in 2013 (95% CI [2.00, 2.15]). This represents a relative increase of approximately 99% in prescription probability, from 4.16% in 2013 to 8.27% in 2023. Except for a significant decrease in antidepressant prescription odds between 2021 and 2022 (OR = 0.93; 95% CI [0.91, 0.95]), all other year-to-year comparisons revealed significant increases, with the largest increase occurring between 2016 and 2017 (OR = 1.17; 95% CI [1.15, 1.20]).

Antipsychotic. The odds of being prescribed one or more antipsychotic agent in 2023 were found to be 3.66 times higher than in 2013 (95% CI [3.48, 3.85]). This reflects a relative increase of approximately 249% in antipsychotic prescription probabilities over the 11-year epoch, with rates increasing from 1.84% in 2013 to 6.42% in 2023. This overall upward trend in antipsychotic prescription probability was seen between 2013 and 2023, except for a non-significant change between 2021 and 2022 (OR = 0.98; 95% CI [0.96, 1.00]). All other sequential comparisons demonstrated significant increases in the population-averaged probability of antipsychotic prescriptions each year, with the largest year-to-year increase occurring between 2015 and 2016 (OR = 1.28; 95% CI [1.23, 1.32]).

Anticonvulsant. GEE analysis revealed significant changes in psychotropic prescription probabilities of anticonvulsant agents over the studied 11-year epoch, with the odds of being prescribed one or more anticonvulsant agent for psychiatric symptoms in 2023 being 1.84 times higher than in 2013 (95% CI [1.76, 1.93]). Specifically, prescription probability increased from 2.29% in 2013 to 4.14% in 2023, representing a relative increase of approximately 81%. Despite the overall upward trend in psychotropic prescription probabilities of anticonvulsant agents between 2013 and 2023, sequential comparisons between consecutive years revealed notable variations in the year-to-year changes. Significant decreases in the odds of being prescribed one or more anticonvulsant agents for psychiatric symptoms occurred between 2015 and 2016, 2018 and 2019, 2019 and 2020, and between 2021 and 2022, with the greatest decrease occurring between 2015 and 2016 (OR = 0.84; 95% CI [0.81, 0.87]). However, significant increases were observed between 2013 and 2014, 2016 and 2017, 2017 and 2018, and between 2022 and 2023, with the largest year-to-year increase occurring between 2013 and 2014 (OR = 1.83; 95% CI [1.74, 1.92]).

Lithium. GEE analysis revealed significant changes in lithium prescription probabilities since 2013; the odds of being prescribed lithium in 2023 were approximately 38% of that observed in 2013 (OR = 0.38; 95% CI [0.34, 0.42]). This represents a relative decrease of 62.4% in lithium prescription probability, from 0.73% in 2013 to 0.27% in 2023. Except for a significant increase in lithium prescription probability between 2016 and 2017 (OR = 1.17; 95% CI [1.08, 1.28]) and non-significant changes between 2015 and 2016, 2020 and 2021, and between 2022 and 2023, sequential comparisons between consecutive years revealed significant decreases in lithium prescription probability across all other years. The largest year-to-year decrease occurred between 2013 and 2014 (OR = 0.64; 95% CI [0.57, 0.71]).

Discussion

Despite some year-to-year variability, population trend analyses of psychotropic agent prescription rates among a nationwide sample of jail detainees between 2013 and 2023 revealed substantial increases overall and for all specific agent classes (i.e., antianxiety, antidepressant, antipsychotic, and anticonvulsant) except lithium, which significantly declined across the 11-year study epoch. Although the exceptionally infrequent prescription of hypnotic, stimulant, and miscellaneous psychotherapeutic agent classes precluded trend analysis, this is consistent with recommended—though debatable—prescribing practices in correctional settings given the increased potential for misuse and diversion of these agents in secure settings37–39.

Based on information obtained through the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) data tool40, CNS prescription trends among the US general population showed an inverse trend between 2013 and 2022 (i.e., the most recent year of available data), with more individuals being prescribed a CNS agent in 2013 (27.1%) than in 2022 (23.4%). However, comparisons should made with caution, as the MEPS dataset may be incomplete and underrepresent prescription rates for select agent classes (e.g., the antipsychotic agent haloperidol was not listed in the MEPS dataset). Moreover, the dataset aggregates prescriptions across agent classes, and more detailed information is required to determine the extent to which prescription trends for specific agent classes observed in the current sample parallel those in the general population. Nevertheless, the overall decline in CNS agent prescriptions among the general population does not mirror the overall increase in prescription rates of these pharmaceuticals in the current sample and warrants additional investigation.

While the odds of being prescribed psychotropic agents increased more than 100% overall and for most specific agent classes in the current sample, the most extreme shift (i.e., 249% increase in prescription probability) was observed with antipsychotic agents. Generally, antipsychotic agents are primarily prescribed to treat symptoms associated with schizophrenia spectrum disorders and other variants of psychosis. On the surface, these findings support assertions of increased prevalence estimates of SMI among jail detainees, which some attribute to deinstitutionalization efforts and subsequent transinstitutionalization—that is, the shift of individuals from one type of institutional setting (e.g., inpatient state psychiatric hospital) to another (e.g., jail)41. Although the early work of Steadman and colleagues42 concluded there was no appreciable relationship between the massive reduction in hospital census and increase in prison population in the 1960/1970s, they cautioned local jails were more likely than prisons to endure the burden of deinstitutionalization.

Decades later, researchers continue to question the cause of increased SMI rates among individuals in correctional settings, though all agree rates continued to increase year over year41,43. While evidence of an association between incarceration and psychosis is mounting44, it is possible the increased observance of SMI treatment among jail detainees parallels a concurrent increase in treatment of psychotic disorders among community populations. However, widescale longitudinal investigations of mental illness prevalence estimates are notably lacking information about psychotic disorders45–47. Nevertheless, researchers’ consistent observations of low lifetime prevalence rates of psychotic disorders among community populations48–50 does not confirm a universal increase in psychosis prevalence to account for the increase in antipsychotic prescriptions observed in the current study.

Alternatively, factors such as improved access to jail mental health services, increased willingness to accept mental health treatment, and/or overuse of antipsychotic medications may additionally explain the dramatic increase in use of antipsychotic agents in jails. Future research should examine changes in jail mental health workforce and staffing, treatment adherence while in custody, mental illness stigmatization and perceptions of mental health treatment, and targeted symptomatology to further illuminate influences on increased prescribing of antipsychotic—and other psychotropic—agents.

As noted, lithium was the only agent where prescription rates decreased over the study epoch. While this agent boasts indisputable utility treating symptoms of bipolar disorder, these findings are consistent with overall declining use of lithium due to challenges and risks of maintenance treatment (e.g., routine blood panels to assess lithium levels, heightened risk of toxicity and related disruption to thyroid, renal, and parathyroid functions)29,51. However, recent findings suggest off-label benefit of lithium in low dosages to assist with irritability, anger, and addiction52. Such behaviors are commonly observed in jail settings9, and despite challenges of lithium therapy in jails, the agent may be a viable option for managing behavioral and emotional dysregulation.

Along these lines, stimulant agents may also facilitate behavioral regulation and impulse control among individuals who experience symptoms of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). Despite the wide variability in ADHD prevalence estimates among adult correctional populations (i.e., 9.1–45%)53, researchers agree rates of ADHD are higher in jails than rates in the general population (i.e., 2.7%)54. However, consistent with observations in the current study, stimulant agents are infrequently prescribed among adult jail populations due to the high potential for abuse and diversion related to increased prevalence of substance use disorders among IJC, jail economies (e.g., bartering), and victimization potential of IJC who are prescribed stimulant agents55. Nevertheless, withholding treatment may represent a disparity in care between correctional and community populations, and further research is needed to ensure equitable mental health treatment across settings.

While current findings lend strong support for increasing prescription population trends of psychotropic agents in jails, certain limitations must be considered. Generally, the REACH database does not reflect a purely random sampling of jails across the county. Instead, jails included in this study have contractual relationships with the same correctional healthcare company. Although prescribers exercise professional autonomy in their clinical decision-making, it is possible the organization’s policies and procedures indirectly limit the generalizability of findings (e.g., continuation of community medications, strategies for managing acute symptoms of psychosis). Moreover, the extent to which facility-level factors (e.g., geographic location, jail size) moderate these findings should be explored.

In addition, while sample demography was generally consistent with broader US data regarding IJC in terms of age and race, there was a notable difference in sex representation compared to national averages. Specifically, females accounted for 28% of the current sample, which is substantially higher than 10% rates of females in jail custody cited by the US Department of Justice (DOJ)56–58 and may limit generalizability of findings. However, the DOJ reports often rely on snapshot census information and offer an overview of IJC on a specific date, and ancillary studies highlight an overall increase in justice-involved females over the past decade59. Sex demography of the current sample better aligns with information obtained through the US Federal Bureau of Investigation’s (FBI) Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) program, which indicates females accounted for 26.8% of arrests between January 2013 and December 202360. While further research is required to actualize jail demography, this disparity highlights the value of the REACH dataset and others like it to improve the accuracy of IJC statistics.

The current study only highlights agents prescribed and does not precisely reflect mental health trends, as prescription patterns are a complex interplay of clinical presentation, systemic policy, and judgment/preference of providers. In addition, this study does not evaluate the clinical accuracy or efficacy of prescribed treatments, nor individuals’ compliance with prescribed treatment regimens. Moreover, this study only broadly considered interclass polypharmacy and does not capture intraclass polypharmacy prescription trends. Because some CNS agents are increasingly prescribed for off-label purposes (e.g., prescribing antipsychotic agents for aggression or impulsivity, antidepressant agents for sleep) and/or may be overprescribed for certain symptoms (e.g., psychosis), the increased prescription rates of CNS agents alone may not solely indicate an increase in SMI among jail populations61–63. Future investigations focusing on clinical justification, symptom abatement, and the moderating effects of prescription adherence are warranted to better understand the notable increase in psychotropic prescriptions among IJC.

Conclusions

The REACH database was established to better understand the evolving landscape of healthcare needs among IJC. The current investigation chronicles increased use of psychotropic agents in jails over an 11-year epoch. Despite a deliberately conservative approach, including use of statistical penalizations that reduce estimates to safeguard against Type I errors, drastic increases in the use of major psychotropic agent classes were evident and affirm the increased need for—or, alternatively, overuse of—psychotropic agents in jails. These findings advocate taking important next steps, including leveraging the REACH database to link prescribed treatments to targeted symptomatology and examining treatment efficacy (e.g., based on treatment adherence and symptom amelioration). This progressive probing is necessary to shed light on the nuanced needs of an evolving inmate-patient population and improve the quality of behavioral healthcare services in jails.
