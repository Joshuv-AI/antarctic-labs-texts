A step-by-step guide for systematic reviews and meta-analyses in spine surgery-study execution: a narrative review
Authors: Paul G. Mastrokostas, Leonidas E. Mastrokostas, Ahmed K. Emara, Mena Salman, Ian J. Wellington, Brian T. Ford, John K. Houten, Ahmed Saleh, Jad Bou Monsef, Afshin E. Razi, Mitchell K. Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveSystematic reviews and meta-analyses are crucial in spine surgery, offering a robust approach to integrating evidence from multiple studies and guiding clinical decision-making. These reviews resolve inconsistencies across studies and increase statistical power, making them indispensable for assessing the effectiveness and safety of surgical interventions. This review serves as a comprehensive guide on how to: (I) design; (II) implement; and (III) publish a systematic review in spine surgery.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review by searching key databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science, to identify studies that demonstrate the best practices in conducting systematic reviews in spine surgery. Studies were selected for their methodological rigor and adherence to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. The included systematic reviews were evaluated based on how they applied standardized quality assessment tools, such as the Cochrane risk of bias (RoB) tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria or Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for non-randomized studies. A narrative synthesis was performed to summarize the findings and highlight best practices.Key Content and FindingsSystematic reviews stand at the pinnacle of the evidence-based hierarchy in spine surgery, integrating findings from various primary investigations. This study explores techniques for evaluating data quality using tools such as the Cochrane RoB, the MINORS criteria, and the NOS. We detail methods for interpreting and analyzing data, and we outline the process of transforming the findings into a publishable manuscript, with reference to a previously published example. Adhering to the PRISMA guidelines is advocated as a standard across all scientific literature, inclusive of spine surgery. Presenting data through pooled analyses with Forest Plots, along with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals, is a customary practice.ConclusionsIn the manuscript preparation phase, it is vital to address and debate the intrinsic limitations of systematic reviews, such as their selection criteria and the overall quality, which may be constrained by the caliber of the included studies (e.g., publication bias, heterogeneity, search/selection bias).

Introduction

Systematic reviews are crucial in the progression of spine surgery literature, offering an exhaustive union of current evidence. Within the changing state of spine surgery, where clinical methods are constantly being refined, systematic reviews emerge as essential instruments for clinicians, researchers, and decision-makers (1). They provide a unified summary of varied studies, empowering healthcare professionals to base their clinical decisions on the highest quality evidence available. In spine surgery, systematic reviews are instrumental in promoting evidence-based practice by aggregating results from numerous studies, thereby unveiling trends, patterns, and differences in surgical treatment options (2-4). They act as bridges, connecting discrete studies and affording a comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness, safety, and comparative benefits of spine surgery interventions (5). These reviews play a significant role in enhancing patient care, advancing surgical techniques, and developing evidence-based protocols (6-9). At their core, systematic reviews constitute the peak of the evidence-based hierarchy, signifying the most authoritative form of evidence by compiling findings from multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (10).

While the foundational methodology of systematic reviews is broadly applicable across medical fields, spine surgery presents several specialty-specific challenges that warrant a tailored approach. These include considerable heterogeneity in surgical techniques (e.g., anterior vs. posterior approaches), variability in implant design, inconsistent outcome reporting (e.g., fusion rates, pain scores, neurological recovery), and a frequent reliance on non-randomized or retrospective data due to practical and ethical constraints in trial design. These factors can complicate study selection, risk-of-bias assessment, and data synthesis. As such, the spine surgery literature benefits from a methodological framework that both adheres to general evidence-based principles and accounts for these domain-specific complexities.

This review aims to act as a comprehensive manual on the process of designing, conducting, and publishing a systematic review in the field of spine surgery. In this study, we outline the steps to: (I) evaluate data quality using the Cochrane risk of bias (RoB), Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria, or the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS); (II) present results following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines; and (III) transform the gathered evidence into a publishable manuscript, including an example from a study previously published. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-49/rc).

Methods

Search strategy

This narrative review aimed to identify and evaluate studies that exemplify best practices in conducting systematic reviews in the field of spine surgery. The initial search strategy, as outlined in part I, involved querying several key databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search focused on identifying high-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses published in peer-reviewed journals from January 1, 2000, to April 13, 2024 (Table 1). Search terms were carefully selected to capture relevant studies that demonstrated methodological rigor in conducting systematic reviews in spine surgery.

While search strategies were tailored to the indexing structure of each database, the approach used in PubMed/MEDLINE illustrates our general methodology. We combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms encompassing surgical and outcome-related concepts relevant to spine surgery. For example, a representative search string included: (“spine surgery” OR “spinal surgery”) AND (“systematic review” OR “meta-analysis”) AND (“surgical outcomes” OR “surgical techniques” OR “postoperative care” OR “patient outcomes”). Filters were applied to limit results to human studies, English language, and publications between January 1, 2000, and April 13, 2024. Searches were refined iteratively to optimize specificity and sensitivity.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible studies. While this process can be facilitated using tools such as Covidence, Rayyan, or EndNote to manage citations and track decisions, screening for this review was conducted manually. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. Studies were selected based on inclusion criteria focused on methodological rigor, transparency, and adherence to established guidelines such as PRISMA. The final selection aimed to identify systematic reviews that exemplified best practices in review methodology.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed systematically to gather detailed information from each included study. The extraction process was standardized using a predefined form to ensure consistency across reviewers. Data were organized and stored using Microsoft Excel, allowing for efficient management and retrieval during the synthesis phase. Multiple reviewers independently extracted the data, and any discrepancies were resolved through collaborative review.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment methods employed within each included systematic review were examined to evaluate their adherence to established standards. Specifically, we analyzed whether and how the authors of each review applied appropriate tools—such as the Cochrane RoB tool for RCTs, the MINORS criteria, or the NOS—to assess the quality of the primary studies included in their analyses. Our focus was on the appropriateness, consistency, and transparency of these methods as reported by the review authors. Two reviewers independently evaluated this information, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

We did not apply the Cochrane RoB tool directly to the systematic reviews included in this narrative review. Instead, we examined how these tools were implemented by the authors of each review to assess the primary studies included in their analyses.

Data synthesis

Although this review includes a structured search and study selection process, it is classified as a narrative review rather than a scoping review. The purpose was not to comprehensively map all existing literature, but to provide a focused, critical synthesis of studies that exemplify best practices in conducting systematic reviews in spine surgery. This approach allowed for detailed commentary on methodological standards rather than an exhaustive survey of the field. The findings from each study were summarized, with particular emphasis on the strengths and weaknesses of their methodological approaches. This synthesis highlighted key trends, areas where current practices align with best practices, and opportunities for further refinement in conducting systematic reviews in spine surgery. The results were structured around key themes such as data extraction, quality assessment, and reporting standards, and presented in accordance with PRISMA guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness.

Manuscript preparation

The findings from the narrative synthesis were compiled into a manuscript that emphasizes the importance of methodological rigor in systematic reviews. The manuscript discusses the implications of these findings for future research in spine surgery, particularly in the context of improving the quality and reliability of systematic reviews. Ethical considerations were addressed by confirming that no new patient data were collected and that ethical approval was not required for this review.

Discussion

Data quality

Researchers are advised to register their review in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) once they ascertain that the necessary data for synthesis are accessible. It is crucial to note that if a review on a similar topic is already underway, as indicated in the PROSPERO database, researchers should refrain from conducting an overlapping study. Key elements of the review protocol are preserved in PROSPERO as a lasting record, with the goal of offering a comprehensive index of systematic reviews to prevent redundancy and minimize reporting bias.

Upon compiling data from individual studies for inclusion in a review listed in PROSPERO, it’s imperative for authors conducting any systematic review and/or meta-analysis to evaluate the quality of these studies, given the broad spectrum of available methodologies (10). The integrity of a systematic review or meta-analysis is fundamentally dependent on the quality of the included studies. For this purpose, we advocate for the use of the Cochrane RoB tool for appraising randomized studies, and the MINORS criteria and/or the NOS for assessing non-randomized studies (11-14).

Cochrane RoB—quality assessment for RCTs

The Cochrane RoB tool is instrumental in critically assessing the methodological rigor of RCTs featured in systematic reviews (15-17). This tool is adept at examining core domains pertinent to spine surgery studies, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, incomplete outcome data, and selective outcome reporting (18). With the continual advancement in spine surgery through new implant technologies, surgical methods, and post-surgical care practices, the Cochrane RoB tool offers a consistent and transparent framework for evaluating the potential bias in various studies (2,3,19-21). Utilizing this tool allows researchers in spine surgery to thoroughly investigate the methodological quality of the RCTs they include, ensuring the evidence gathered is both strong and dependable. Such careful assessment is crucial for informing evidence-based clinical decisions in the rapidly evolving and vitally significant area of spine surgery (Figure 1) (22).

MINORS criteria—quality assessment for non-randomized studies

In spine surgery research, where non-randomized studies are a common approach for evaluating new surgical techniques and interventions, the use of the MINORS criteria is exceedingly beneficial (23-25). Given the variety in study designs within spine surgery research, including cohort studies and case series, the MINORS criteria furnish a structured method for assessing the methodological integrity of these studies (13). Comprising 12 key aspects, such as having a clearly defined aim, inclusion of patients in a consecutive manner, objective endpoint assessment, and appropriate statistical analysis, the MINORS criteria are tailored to gauge the methodological quality of non-randomized studies in systematic reviews (13). This ensures a thorough and rigorous evaluation and reporting process. Focusing on areas like research objectives, patient selection processes, and statistical methods, the MINORS criteria facilitate a critical examination of the quality of studies investigating new surgical techniques, innovative devices, or novel rehabilitation methods in spine surgery. By applying the MINORS criteria, systematic reviews can offer a detailed and standardized evaluation of the methodological robustness of non-randomized studies, enhancing the quality of evidence synthesis in the field of spine surgery (Table 2).

NOS—quality assessment for non-randomized studies

Within the field of spine surgery, an area deeply invested in exploring and validating new treatment approaches, the NOS plays a vital role. This tool is indispensable for the critical evaluation of the quality of non-randomized studies, which are often pivotal in systematic reviews (26-30). These reviews typically investigate the effectiveness of emerging surgical techniques, novel spinal implants, or innovative rehabilitation protocols. The NOS, focusing on criteria such as selection processes, comparability, and outcome determination, offers a bespoke framework for rigorously analyzing the methodological integrity of these studies (31). As the field of spine surgery continues to advance with new therapeutic options, employing the NOS facilitates a comprehensive assessment of evidence quality and bias risk. By integrating the NOS into their systematic reviews, spine surgery researchers can ensure a stringent scrutiny of non-randomized studies, thus fortifying the evidence base surrounding new advancements in spine treatment (Table 3).

In the context of spine surgery research, comparing the MINORS criteria with the NOS reveals both as valuable tools for appraising the methodological quality of non-randomized studies, albeit with distinct emphases and applications. The MINORS criteria, through its 12-item checklist, offers an extensive evaluation encompassing aspects like the clarity of reporting, adequacy of sample size calculation, and statistical analysis detail. Conversely, the NOS, with its star-based evaluation system, primarily targets the selection of study participants, comparability, and the verification of outcomes. This makes it a streamlined option, particularly well-suited for case-control and cohort studies within spine surgery research. The choice between MINORS and NOS is dictated by the research goals and the level of detail desired in assessing the quality of non-randomized studies in the context of spine surgery.

Level of evidence

It’s essential to understand that the strength of evidence provided by a systematic review and meta-analysis is capped by the quality of its weakest included study. For instance, if a systematic review comprises both level I and II evidence, it will be classified as level II evidence.

Checklist for Presenting Spine Surgery Systematic Review—PRISMA guidelines (32)

In the field of spine surgery research, the adoption of PRISMA guidelines is highly recommended to ensure methodological precision and clear reporting of systematic reviews (10,33). The literature on spine surgery covers a wide range of surgical techniques, implant designs, and therapeutic strategies. PRISMA guidelines offer a detailed roadmap for researchers undertaking systematic reviews in this area, directing them through the essential steps of the review process. By following PRISMA, researchers focused on spine surgery are able to define exact inclusion criteria, craft thorough search strategies, and rigorously evaluate and compile evidence from pertinent studies. Additionally, these guidelines underscore the significance of transparent result reporting, thereby clarifying the evidence’s quality and assisting healthcare professionals, surgeons, and policymakers in making well-informed choices regarding spine surgery practices.

The significance of adhering to PRISMA guidelines in systematic reviews of spine surgery extends to promoting the progression of evidence-based practice within this specific sector. Compliance with PRISMA ensures that systematic reviews offer a dependable and exhaustive aggregation of the existing evidence, permit a critical examination of bias risk in the included studies, standardize result presentation, and improve the replicability of the review methodology. Through fostering uniformity and openness, PRISMA guidelines enhance the caliber of systematic reviews in spine surgery, promoting the distribution of solid evidence that aids in clinical decision-making and propels the specialty towards more efficacious and patient-focused treatments.

Data analysis/presentation

In the findings section of a spine surgery systematic review, adopting a methodical and orderly approach like the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) framework proves invaluable for systematically detailing evidence. Start with a succinct overview of the included studies’ characteristics, outlining the investigated population, the examined surgical interventions, any pertinent comparison groups, and the primary outcomes measured. Organize the presentation of each PICO component in a sequence that mirrors the research questions or aims. Employ tables or figures to succinctly convey essential data, thereby enhancing the narrative’s clarity. After showcasing the results from individual studies, proceed to the evidence synthesis. Use the PICO framework as a guide to methodically amalgamate findings, comparing outcomes across studies to spotlight trends or disparities. Finalize the synthesis with a comprehensive interpretation of the aggregated evidence within each PICO segment, discussing the evidence quality and its ramifications for clinical practice or future research inquiries. This orderly and systematic strategy ensures the systematic review’s results and synthesis portions are delivered in an accessible and organized fashion, aiding readers in comprehending the compiled evidence comprehensively.

When incorporating a meta-analysis with a systematic review in spine surgery, presenting the data demands extra focus on statistical amalgamation. Similar to a systematic review, begin by detailing the characteristics of the studies included, such as essential demographics, interventions, and study designs. Sequentially outline the primary outcomes of interest, offering effect estimates along with their confidence intervals (CIs) for each study. Utilize graphical tools like Forest Plots to visually summarize individual study findings and the collective effect size, which can be crafted using statistical software like Review Manager (RevMan) or R statistical software (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Forest plots effectively consolidate study comparisons, showcasing odds ratios (OR) and 95% CIs. It’s crucial to clearly explain the statistical approaches used, whether fixed or random-effects models, and to tackle heterogeneity by presenting I2 statistics. Cochran’s Q test, in this context, is utilized to evaluate the heterogeneity across studies, with the I2 statistic quantifying the total variation due to heterogeneity. A significant Cochran’s Q test outcome indicates heterogeneity among the groups or treatments, warranting further investigation into the sources of variability. Following the statistical data presentation, provide a narrative synthesis that interprets the pooled effect sizes, discusses their clinical significance, and acknowledges any limitations. Ensure the findings are sequentially structured, guiding readers through the meta-analysis from individual study outcomes to the collective evidence synthesis.

Strengths/limitations

This review provides a comprehensive and practical guide for conducting systematic reviews in spine surgery, offering detailed insights into the evaluation of study quality, data synthesis, and the manuscript preparation process. One of the key strengths of this work is its focus on best practices, including the use of established tools like the Cochrane RoB, MINORS criteria, and the NOS, which are crucial for ensuring the methodological rigor and reliability of systematic reviews. In addition, the emphasis on adhering to PRISMA guidelines underscores the importance of transparency and consistency in reporting. Nevertheless, there are limitations to this review. As this study primarily focuses on narrative synthesis rather than quantitative meta-analysis, the findings are more descriptive and may lack the statistical robustness that comes with pooled data. Furthermore, the selection of studies exemplifying best practices, while rigorous, is inherently subject to selection bias, as it relies on the availability of high-quality studies in the existing literature. Lastly, the applicability of the recommendations may vary depending on the specific context and goals of the systematic review being conducted, potentially limiting the generalizability of some of the guidance provided.

Conclusions

This document acts as the second installment in a guide on planning, conducting, and disseminating a systematic review within the realm of spine surgery. In this section, our emphasis lies on evaluating data quality utilizing tools such as the Cochrane RoB, MINORS criteria, or the NOS, tailored to the review’s focus on either RCTs or cohort studies. We advocate for the adoption of the PRISMA guidelines, aligning with the conventions of scientific publication, including those specific to spine surgery. We suggest a detailed analysis and presentation of variables, employing an organized and progressive methodology like the PICO framework. For systematic reviews that are complemented by meta-analyses, we advise on illustrating pooled analyses through Forest Plots, along with their corresponding OR or 95% CIs. It’s crucial to acknowledge the inherent constraints of systematic reviews in the manuscript, which predominantly depend on the nature and caliber of the included individual studies. Ultimately, our goal is for these documents to act as foundational resources for those undertaking the process of conducting a systematic review in spine surgery.
