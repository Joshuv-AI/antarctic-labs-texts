Prosthetic restoration material selection using an integrated Fermatean fuzzy MCDM method
Authors: Ece Akkaya, Melike Cari, Tolga Kudret Karaca, Bahar Yalcin Kavus, Ertugrul Ayyildiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelecting an appropriate dental crown material requires the simultaneous consideration of clinical performance, aesthetics, cost, and patient-related expectations. This study aimed to support dentists in selecting the most suitable dental prosthetic restoration material under uncertainty. An integrated Fermatean fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) framework was employed by combining the CRITIC (Criteria Importance Through Intercriteria Correlation) method for weighting and the WASPAS (Weighted Aggregated Sum Product Assessment) method for ranking alternatives. Six restorative material alternatives were evaluated using eleven sub-criteria grouped under three main dimensions: mechanical, visual, and eco-nomic. The criteria were identified through literature review and expert opinion. The findings indicated that clinical longevity was the most important criterion in the evaluation process. Among the six alternatives, the resin nano-ceramic CAD/CAM material achieved the highest overall performance, followed by the lithium disilicate CAD/CAM and zirconia-based ceramic materials. Across the tested λ values and ±20% changes in the three highest-weighted criteria, the ranking order remained unchanged, indicating that the results were stable within the examined sensitivity scenarios, confirming the robustness of the proposed framework. The proposed framework provides a transparent decision-support tool for restorative dentistry and demonstrates the applicability of Fermatean fuzzy MCDM methods to dental material selection.Keywords: dental prosthetic restoration material selection, clinical decision support, health informatics, patient-centered care, Fermatean fuzzy sets

1. Introduction

Dental crowns are artificial prosthetic restorations that mimic natural teeth and are designed to treat tooth loss, severe decay, or substantial loss of dental material. A dental crown can be used to rebuild a broken tooth or to support a large filling in a tooth that has extensive decay. It is also used to restore the tooth’s lost shape or color, to cover a dental implant placed in an area with a missing tooth, or to anchor a dental bridge using adjacent teeth for support. A continuing objective in restorative and prosthetic dentistry is to select materials that can reproduce the optical characteristics of natural teeth while providing sufficient mechanical resistance for intraoral function. 1 Therefore, the selection of crown material is a clinically important decision that directly affects restoration longevity, functional performance, aesthetics, and patient satisfaction.

Prosthetic crown materials have evolved from conventional metal-ceramic systems to contemporary all-ceramic and CAD/CAM materials. This transition has been driven by the need to balance mechanical reliability, biocompatibility, optical performance, and increasing patient expectations. Depending on their composition, microstructure, and clinical indication, contemporary ceramic systems can provide suitable performance for both anterior and posterior restorative applications.1,2 Despite significant material advancements, selecting the optimal prosthetic crown material remains challenging because dentists must evaluate multiple material properties and patient-related expectations simultaneously. This complexity creates a need for a structured decision-support approach that can integrate clinical, mechanical, aesthetic, and economic criteria under uncertainty.

Previous studies have shown that multi-criteria decision making (MCDM) based decision-support approaches can provide systematic, transparent, and reproducible frameworks for dental and biomedical material selection. Deniz and Orhan 3 developed an MCDM-based assessment model for engine-driven nickel-titanium instruments and emphasized that structured decision models can support dental practitioners in evaluating complex material alternatives. Similarly, Yadav et al. 4 applied a hybrid Entropy-VIKOR method to rank dental restorative composite materials using multiple performance-defining attributes, while Yadav et al. 5 further demonstrated the usefulness of MCDM techniques for optimizing resin-based dental composites through physical, mechanical, and clinical performance parameters. In another relevant study, Savran and Karpat 6 combined finite element analysis with MCDM methods to compare dental implant materials according to biomechanical and economic criteria. In parallel, clinical studies have also highlighted the importance of CAD/CAM-based restorative materials. Vervack et al. 7 reported promising clinical outcomes for resin composite CAD/CAM restorations, including Lava Ultimate CAD/CAM, whereas Aswal et al. 8 reviewed the clinical performance, survival, and complication profiles of CAD/CAM lithium disilicate and zirconia crowns. These studies collectively indicate that dental material selection increasingly requires evidence-based and multi-criteria decision frameworks.

To address this gap, this study proposes an integrated Fermatean fuzzy MCDM framework combining Fermatean Fuzzy The CRiteria Importance Through Intercriteria Correlation (FF-CRITIC) and Fermatean Fuzzy Weighted Aggregated Sum Product Assessment (FF-WASPAS). FF-CRITIC is used to determine criterion weights from the aggregated expert evaluations, while FF-WASPAS is used to rank dental prosthetic restoration alternatives under uncertainty. Unlike previous studies, the proposed framework focuses specifically on dental prosthetic restoration material selection and incorporates expert judgments across mechanical, visual, and economic dimensions. The use of fuzzy sets was motivated by the qualitative and imprecise nature of criteria such as color harmony, dental aesthetics, marginal fit, and clinical longevity, which cannot be consistently expressed through exact numerical values. Fermatean fuzzy sets enable linguistic expert judgments to be represented through membership, non-membership, and hesitation degrees, thereby preserving the uncertainty and indecision embedded in the assessments.9–11

Although several studies have utilized MCDM approaches for material selection, no prior research has integrated the FF-CRITIC and FF-WASPAS methods for the systematic selection of dental prosthetic materials. This research addresses the following gaps in the literature:

2. Literature review

MCDM methods have undergone rapid evolution, providing structured frameworks for solving complex problems. In a typical MCDM process, the problem is defined, alternatives and criteria are identified, and the importance (weights) of each criterion is determined. These weights reflect the relative significance of each criterion in the decision context.

The main challenges in MCDM, such as the uncertainty and subjectivity of the information, have led to the adoption of fuzzy logic and related methodologies to better model the ambiguity and uncertainty in real-world problems. Fuzzy set theory enables the mathematical modeling of imprecise, vague, or incomplete information. However, classical fuzzy set theory faced certain limitations in capturing real-life complexities, prompting researchers to develop a variety of fuzzy set extensions to better address uncertainty. Over the past several decades, a variety of fuzzy set extensions have been proposed to better represent the uncertainty and complexity. Among these sets, Fermatean Fuzzy Sets (FFSs), introduced by Senapati and Yager 12 as a special case of q-level orthopair fuzzy sets, allow even greater flexibility in representing uncertainty. This enhanced modeling capability has made FFSs especially attractive for complex MCDM problems.

Recently, researchers have begun to integrate FFSs into MCDM techniques such as FF-CRITIC and FF-WASPAS, to process subjective expert assessments through structured mathematical procedures.13–16 To capture the current state of the art, a literature review focusing on the use of FF-CRITIC and FF-WASPAS was conducted. 3.2. Proposed hybrid decision making methodology

The FF-CRITIC and FF-WASPAS methods are combined to address the weighting and ranking of alternatives in the dental prosthetic material selection problem. In decision making problems, criteria often have varying degrees of importance, which are reflected by their weights. The CRITIC, introduced by Diakoulaki et al., 37 is widely used for determining criteria weights in fuzzy decision-making environments, as it accounts for both the variability and inter-criteria correlations. Mishra et al. extended CRITIC into the Fermatean Fuzzy environment (FF-CRITIC). 24 The key steps involve constructing a Fermatean Fuzzy decision matrix, determining criteria and alternatives, aggregating expert opinions, and then calculating criteria weights based on standard deviation and correlation. Although CRITIC employs statistical measures such as standard deviation and correlation, the input data in this study originate from expert judgments. Therefore, the resulting criterion weights are interpreted as expert-based rather than fully objective. 38

The WASPAS method, developed by Zavadskas et al., 39 is a hybrid MCDM approach that combines the Weighted Sum Model and Weighted Product Model to improve reliability. Keshavarz-Ghorabaee et al. proposed the Fermatean Fuzzy extension (FF-WASPAS), which further increases robustness when handling uncertainty. 40 This integrated approach improves accuracy and reliability in ranking alternatives.19,41 The combination of these methods provides a systematic and reliable framework to support dentists in selecting the optimal prosthetic restoration material.

4. Real case application

Artificial prosthetic restorations, which have been developed using various techniques until today, are selected among the artificial prosthetic restorations and included among the alternatives and are frequently used today. In this study, the alternatives to be used for prosthetic restoration material selection in dentistry are are shown in Figure 2.

Images of the alternatives.

The six alternatives were determined to represent different stages of development and major material families used in fixed prosthodontics rather than only the most recently introduced commercial products. This comparative framework includes a conventional metal-supported ceramic crown, early all-ceramic systems, lithium disilicate and zirconia-based ceramics, and a resin nano-ceramic CAD/CAM material. Such a structure enables the evaluation of restorative materials with different microstructures, mechanical properties, and clinical indications.48,49

IPS Empress II and In-Ceram Alumina were retained not because they are the newest materials available, but because they are well-documented reference systems that represent important milestones in the evolution of all-ceramic restorations. In-Ceram Alumina is a glass-infiltrated alumina ceramic system that demonstrated favorable long-term clinical performance and contributed significantly to the development of high-strength all-ceramic restorations. 50 IPS Empress II represents an early lithium disilicate-based glass-ceramic system and served as the basis for subsequent generations of lithium disilicate restorations, which are currently among the most widely used esthetic restorative materials.48,51 Therefore, retaining these materials provides a broader comparative perspective and allows the proposed decision-support framework to distinguish between conventional restorations, legacy all-ceramic systems, and contemporary CAD/CAM-based alternatives.

Furthermore, zirconia-based ceramics and lithium disilicate systems have become dominant restorative materials because of their favorable combination of mechanical strength, esthetics, and clinical reliability. 52 Resin nano-ceramic CAD/CAM materials have also emerged as alternatives that combine ceramic-like optical properties with improved machinability and simplified intraoral adjustment procedures. 53 Consequently, the selected alternatives collectively represent the major categories of restorative materials that have shaped contemporary prosthodontic practice.

Although contemporary materials such as high-translucency multilayer zirconia, VITA Enamic, and Cerasmart are highly relevant in current restorative dentistry, they were not added as separate alternatives in this study because the expert evaluations, criteria weighting, and decision matrix were constructed based on the predefined six-material framework. Incorporating additional materials at the revision stage would require a new expert assessment process and would alter the original data structure. Therefore, these materials are acknowledged as important candidates for future research.

Metal Supported Ceramic Crown (A1): These restorations, which have been used for many years and still constitute 80% of the veneers made in Turkiye, are usually made of a mixture of Nickel and Cobalt metals, and the superstructure is made of porcelain that tries to imitate the color of the tooth.

IPS Empress II (A2): IPS Empress II is an earlier lithium disilicate-based glass-ceramic system developed for all-ceramic restorations. It is included in this study as a clinically documented reference material representing earlier generations of metal-free ceramic restorative systems.

In-Ceram Alumina (A3): In-Ceram Alumina is a glass-infiltrated alumina ceramic system. It is retained in the comparative framework because it represents an established all-ceramic material category and provides a useful benchmark for comparing older alumina-based ceramics with more contemporary CAD/CAM-based materials.

IPS E-Max CAD/CAM (A4): These are prosthetic all-ceramic restorations produced by controlled crystallization on a glass matrix with heat and pressure, reinforced with leucite crystals and computer-aided design and fabrication.

Zirconia Block (A5): Prosthetic all-ceramic restorations consisting of zirconium dioxide (ZrO2) crystals and computer-aided design and fabrication.

Lava Ultimate CAD/CAM (A6): These are zirconia blocks made of organic materials such as silica powder and zirconium silicate, reinforced with 80% nanoceramic filler particles and stabilized with yttrium. These are computer-aided designed and fabricated prosthetic full ceramic restorations.

The criterion set is broadly consistent with Grachev et al., 54 who evaluated dental materials using mechanical, tribological, technological, biological, and economic factors. The present criteria similarly address mechanical performance, wear resistance, cost, and clinical efficiency. However, because this study focuses on fixed dental crowns, crown-specific factors such as marginal fit, thermal conductivity, color harmony, light transmittance, and dental aesthetics were also included.

The mechanical criteria encompass fracture resistance, marginal fit, corrosion, thermal and electrical conductivity, and resistance to abrasion. These aspects evaluate each material’s ability to withstand chewing forces, ensure a proper fit to the tooth structure, resist chemical degradation, and maintain stability under thermal and mechanical stresses. Visual criteria include color harmony, light transmittance, and dental aesthetics, focusing on the extent to which each restoration matches natural tooth color and translucency, as well as its overall aesthetic integration within the patient’s smile. Economic criteria are represented by pricing, clinical longevity, and the number of treatment sessions required. These consider the cost-effectiveness of the material, its expected lifespan in clinical conditions, and the convenience for patients in terms of the duration and number of clinical appointments. In this study, these criteria, identified through literature review and expert opinion, were used to systematically evaluate and select the most suitable dental prosthetic restoration material from a professional perspective, taking into account both clinical and patient-centered outcomes.

4.1 Implementation of the proposed methodology

This study employed an expert-based evaluation process to support the selection of dental prosthetic restoration materials from both clinical and laboratory-oriented perspectives. Seven experts were consulted in the study. The panel consisted of six dentistry professionals and one dental prosthetics technician, all of whom had substantial professional experience in dental treatment, prosthetic restoration, or dental prosthesis production. The experts had at least 14 years of professional experience, providing experience-based clinical and laboratory perspectives. However, the panel size and composition were not intended to establish an objective or universally generalizable decision pattern; rather, they supported a case-specific expert assessment of the predefined alternatives. The inclusion of experts with different professional roles was intended to capture the multidimensional nature of dental prosthetic material selection, which involves not only clinical outcomes but also material handling, fabrication feasibility, marginal adaptation, CAD/CAM compatibility, finishing, and laboratory-related constraints. Thus, the proposed framework should be used as a decision-support tool that helps structure clinical judgment rather than as a rigid prescription.

4.2 Sensitivity analysis

When integrating the WSM and WPM values calculated during the evaluation of FF-WASPAS, the value of λ was set as 0.5. The parameter λ is the WASPAS balancing coefficient that determines the relative contribution of the WSM and the WPM to the final preference score. A value of λ=1 represents full reliance on WSM, λ=0 represents full reliance on WPM, and λ=0.5 assigns equal importance to both components. 5. Discussion

This study applied the integrated FF-CRITIC and FF-WASPAS methods to the selection of dental prosthetic restoration materials, addressing the complex multi-criteria decision faced by dentists in clinical practice. Notably, this is one of the first applications of FFSs in this context, contributing a novel decision-support framework to literature. The proposed approach systematically processes subjective expert evaluations and derives criterion weights from the variation and correlation structure of the aggregated assessments, providing a systematic structure for expert-driven material selection under uncertainty. The practical motivation for using FFSs lies in the linguistic and imprecise nature of the expert assessments. 55 Assigning exact numerical values to criteria such as dental aesthetics, marginal fit, and clinical longevity would imply a level of precision that expert judgments may not support. FFSs represent each linguistic assessment through membership and non-membership degrees while retaining the associated hesitation, thereby preventing uncertainty from being reduced immediately to a single crisp value. However, because this study did not directly compare Fermatean fuzzy sets with crisp or alternative fuzzy representations, this advantage should be interpreted as representational rather than as evidence of empirical superiority. Unlike single-criterion material comparisons, the proposed model reflects the fact that prosthetic material selection is not determined only by mechanical strength, but also by longevity, marginal adaptation, aesthetics, treatment burden, and cost-related considerations.

The results indicated that clinical longevity was the most significant criterion, consistent with the priority of maximizing restoration durability and patient health outcomes. This result is consistent with clinical survival studies showing that long-term performance is one of the main indicators of restorative success. For example, Aziz and El-Mowafy 56 reported 6-year cumulative survival rates of 96.0% for lithium disilicate glass-ceramic CAD/CAM crowns and 90.8% for metal-ceramic crowns. Similarly, Sailer et al. 57 reported an estimated 5-year survival rate of 94.7% for metal-ceramic single crowns, while all-ceramic systems such as lithium disilicate, alumina, and zirconia showed comparable survival patterns. Larsson and Wennerberg 58 also reported high 5-year survival rates for zirconia-based crowns. These findings support the high weight assigned to clinical longevity in the present study.

Aesthetic outcomes were ranked as the second most important criterion, highlighting the growing emphasis on patient satisfaction and natural appearance in modern dentistry. Since aesthetic perception is subjective and influenced by socio-cultural factors, its high weight underscores the need for restorations that closely mimic natural teeth. 59 This finding is also supported by clinical evidence indicating that CAD/CAM lithium disilicate crowns can provide higher patient-rated color, chewing ability, and overall satisfaction compared with metal-ceramic crowns. 56 In the present study, the high importance of dental aesthetics and light-related criteria reflects the clinical reality that anterior and visible-zone restorations require not only adequate strength but also shade stability, translucency, and natural optical integration.

The third most important criterion, marginal fit, is essential for minimizing microleakage, preventing recurrent caries, and ensuring the long-term integrity of restorations. 60 Marginal fit also has practical implications for cementation quality and restoration survival. Poor marginal adaptation may increase the risk of microleakage, cement dissolution, recurrent caries, marginal discoloration, and eventual restoration replacement. Therefore, the relatively high weight of marginal fit is clinically meaningful and consistent with the role of CAD/CAM accuracy in improving restoration adaptation.

A counter-intuitive finding is the relatively low weight assigned to fracture resistance. This should not be interpreted as meaning that fracture resistance is clinically unimportant. Rather, it reflects the logic of the CRITIC method, in which a criterion receives a higher weight when it shows greater contrast among alternatives and stronger conflict with other criteria. In this study, most evaluated materials received relatively high and close expert scores for fracture resistance, suggesting that the alternatives were generally perceived as mechanically acceptable. Therefore, fracture resistance had limited discriminatory power compared with clinical longevity, dental aesthetics, and marginal fit. Clinically, this means that once a material satisfies an acceptable mechanical threshold, dentists may place greater emphasis on factors that more clearly differentiate treatment success, such as long-term serviceability, marginal adaptation, aesthetics, and treatment efficiency.

Among the alternatives, Lava Ultimate CAD/CAM emerged as the top-ranked material. This result should be interpreted as an overall multi-criteria performance outcome rather than a universal clinical recommendation for all crown indications. Lava Ultimate CAD/CAM and other resin nano-ceramic CAD/CAM materials may offer advantages related to machinability, repairability, chairside workflow, and patient comfort. Vervack et al. 7 reported a survival rate of 91.4% and a success rate of 87.2% for resin composite CAD/CAM restorations over a mean follow-up of 28 months, while also noting that marginal changes may occur over time. However, clinicians should still consider restoration type, tooth location, occlusal load, adhesive protocol, and manufacturer indications before selecting this material.

IPS E-Max CAD/CAM and Zirconia Block also performed strongly and consistently appeared among the top alternatives. This result is clinically plausible because lithium disilicate CAD/CAM materials are widely used due to their favorable balance between aesthetics and mechanical performance, while zirconia-based restorations are preferred when higher strength and posterior load-bearing capacity are required. Clinical evidence also supports the favorable performance of lithium disilicate CAD/CAM crowns, with reported survival rates ranging from 83.5% to 100% in clinical studies and a 6-year cumulative survival rate of 96.0% in one retrospective clinical comparison. 56 Zirconia-based crowns have also shown high clinical survival in systematic reviews, although technical complications such as chipping or retention-related issues may still occur depending on crown design and fabrication approach.57,58

From a practical clinical perspective, the proposed model can support dentists by clarifying which material may be more appropriate under different clinical priorities. When treatment efficiency, aesthetics, and balanced overall performance are emphasized, CAD/CAM-based materials such as Lava Ultimate CAD/CAM and IPS E-Max CAD/CAM may become more attractive. When strength and posterior load-bearing reliability are dominant concerns, zirconia-based restorations may be preferred. When cost sensitivity or conventional laboratory familiarity is important, metal-supported ceramic crowns may still remain a feasible alternative despite their lower overall ranking. Therefore, the model should be viewed as a decision-support tool that complements, rather than replaces, clinical judgment.

6. Conclusions

This study developed an integrated Fermatean fuzzy decision-support framework for dental prosthetic restoration material selection. The findings highlighted clinical longevity, aesthetics, and marginal fit as the most important factors, and demonstrated that Lava Ultimate CAD/CAM was the top-ranked material, followed by IPS E-Max CAD/CAM and Zirconia Block. The sensitivity analysis supported the stability of the ranking results across different λ values. The main contribution of this study is the adaptation of an advanced fuzzy MCDM framework to a clinically relevant dental material selection problem. By combining objective criterion weighting with expert-based alternative evaluation, the proposed model offers a transparent structure for supporting evidence-based and patient-centered restorative decisions.

However, some limitations should be noted. The number of experts consulted in the evaluation process was limited, and all experts were selected from a single country. This may restrict the generalizability of the results to broader clinical populations or different healthcare settings. In addition, only six prosthetic materials were compared, and other relevant alternatives might be considered in future studies. Another limitation is that the expert panel was not internationalized and did not include formal inter-rater reliability statistics. Although the panel included experienced professionals from different dental practice roles, future studies may validate the proposed framework with a larger and internationally diverse expert group and apply agreement measures such as Kendall’s coefficient of concordance or intraclass correlation after appropriate transformation of linguistic evaluations.

The findings should be interpreted within the boundaries of the case considered. The ranking is dependent on the seven-member expert panel, the professional and regional contexts represented by the experts, and the six predefined material alternatives. Different regions, candidate materials, market conditions, or expert compositions may produce different results. Moreover, mechanically and physically oriented criteria were evaluated qualitatively rather than through laboratory measurements. Future studies should include materials scientists and integrate expert judgments with quantitative mechanical, optical, biological, and cost data. The present study focused on six predefined. However, newer materials such as high-translucency multilayer zirconia, VITA Enamic, Cerasmart, and other hybrid CAD/CAM blocks were not included in the current decision matrix. Future studies may expand the alternative set by incorporating these contemporary materials and collecting new expert evaluations accordingly. For future research, expanding the diversity and number of experts, as well as including a wider range of dental materials and clinical scenarios, could further validate and strengthen the proposed decision-support framework. Incorporating real-world clinical outcome data would also help assess the practical effectiveness of the model in guiding restorative material choices.
