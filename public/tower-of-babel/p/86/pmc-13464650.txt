MicroRNA-563 as a potential biomarker in triple-negative breast cancer: impeding tumor progression by targeting ITGAV and epithelial-mesenchymal transition
Authors: Xiaobo Li, Huiling Qin, Jing Zhai, Xingzhen Hu, Lan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by its aggressive nature and limited treatment options, which contribute to a poor prognosis. However, the potential role of microRNA-563 (miR-563) in this malignancy remains unclear. This study investigates the tumor-suppressive role of miR-563 and its underlying mechanisms, proposing its potential as a novel biomarker in TNBC.MethodsThe expression of miR-563 was analyzed in clinical samples of TNBC in comparison to healthy controls and non-TNBC cases. Functional assays were conducted using TNBC cell lines. Bioinformatics prediction and luciferase reporter assays identified integrin subunit alpha-V (ITGAV) as a direct target, which was further validated through rescue experiments. Additionally, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers were assessed to elucidate the mechanistic pathway.ResultsMiR-563 was significantly downregulated in TNBC patients, demonstrating an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.895 for diagnosis. This downregulation correlated with advanced disease stage, lymph node metastasis, and poorer progression-free survival. Overexpression of miR-563 resulted in the suppression of TNBC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. ITGAV was identified as a direct target of miR-563, and rescue experiments indicated a partial reversal of the tumor-suppressive effects of miR-563. Furthermore, miR-563 inhibited EMT, as evidenced by the upregulation of E-cadherin and downregulation of Vimentin.ConclusionsThese findings identify miR-563 as a crucial tumor suppressor and a potential diagnostic predictor in TNBC, operating in part through the ITGAV-mediated pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04392-6.Keywords: MicroRNA-563, Triple-negative breast cancer, Biomarker, EMT

Background

Among gynecological malignancies, breast cancer exhibits particularly high incidence rates, creating substantial burdens on women’s physical and psychosocial health [1, 2]. The triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) subgroup is characterized by considerable molecular diversity and the most clinically aggressive traits [3]. Its molecular profile indicates undetectable levels of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epithelial growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [4]. This aggressive nature is reflected in a poor prognosis and a strong capacity for invasion and metastasis. Notably, there are significant differences in clinical features, pathological characteristics, treatments responses, and prognoses when TNBC is compared to other breast cancer subtypes [5]. TNBC predominantly affects younger female patients (age < 40) and is associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including reduced recurrence-free and long-term survival rates [6]. The high risk of distant metastasis, elevated rates of central nervous system metastases, early recurrence, and poor prognosis highlight the necessity of exploring new molecular biomarkers and developing effective treatment strategies for TNBC [7].

As small non-coding RNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenously expressed single-stranded molecules approximately 21–25 nucleotides long [8]. MiRNA-mRNA interactions at the 3’ untranslated region (3’-UTR) predominantly downregulate gene expression by interfering with the translational machinery or recruiting decay complexes [9]. Emerging evidence highlights miRNAs as pivotal regulators of fundamental biological processes, including cellular development, oncogenic transformation, and metastatic progression [10–12]. A substantial body of evidence indicates that aberrantly expressed miRNAs function as crucial regulators of tumorigenesis and development, a notion that has been validated across a diverse array of tumors, including TNBC [13]. The dysregulated expression of miRNAs has emerged as a potential predictor, offering novel opportunities for the diagnosis, therapy, and prognosis of tumors [14]. The published literature indicates that miRNA-563 (miR-563) plays a significant role in the pathophysiological processes of tumorigenesis, such as in lung cancer [15]. However, the clinical significance of miR-563 and its molecular mechanisms have not yet been explored in TNBC.

The epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) has been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in tumor metastasis and local or distant invasion. In TNBC, a highly aggressive subtype characterized by the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression, EMT serves as a critical driver of disease progression [16]. As TNBC cells transition from an epithelial to a mesenchymal state, they acquire pro-metastatic properties through altered marker expression, which enhances their ability to infiltrate secondary sites, such as the lungs and central nervous system.

This investigation quantified the expression profiles of miR-563 in breast cancer patients, including those with TNBC and assessed its diagnostic performance. Additionally, we explored the effects of miR-563 on cellular functions in TNBC cells and predicted its target genes through bioinformatics analysis.

Methods

Participants and sample collection

This study was supported by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo Yinzhou No.2 Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection.

A total of 250 participants were enrolled, including 60 healthy volunteers (age- and gender-matched controls), 70 non-TNBC patients, and 120 TNBC patients, all diagnosed and treated at Ningbo Yinzhou No.2 Hospital. Inclusion criteria of non-TNBC and TNBC groups were as follows: (1) no history of malignancy or chronic inflammatory diseases; (2) in non-TNBC group, histologically confirmed breast cancer with positive expression of ER, PR, or HER2; (3) For TNBC patients, histologically confirmed TNBC with negative ER, PR, and HER2. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with other malignancies, autoimmune diseases, or severe systemic infections; (2) individuals receiving immunosuppressive therapy within the past 3 months.

Clinical and pathological data were retrospectively collected from the medical records of all enrolled patients. The following variables were recorded: age, menopause, tumor size, tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage, lymph node metastasis (LNM), molecular subtypes (basal-like, mesenchymal, luminal), treatment regiments (chemotherapy alone, immunotherapy, targeted therapy), and metastatic sites (none, lung/liver, brain, bone/lymph node).

Peripheral blood samples (5mL) were collected from each participant under fasting conditions, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the obtained serum was stored at -80℃ until further analysis.

Cell culture and transfection

All cell lines utilized in this study, including the non-tumorigenic MCF10A control and four carcinoma lines (BT-474, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, BT-549), were commercially sourced from Procell (China). Culture media formulations were line-specific: DMEM/F12 for MCF10A, RPMI-1640 for three cancer lines, and DMEM for BT-549, all containing 10% FBS and antibiotic/antimycotic solution. Cells were propagated under standard incubator conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

Cells were plated in 6-well plates until reaching 60–70% density prior to transfection. Using Lipofectamine® 3000 (Thermo Fisher), we introduced: (i) miR-563 mimic/inhibitor, (ii) matched negative controls (NC-mimic/inhibitor), and (iii) integrin subunit alpha-V (ITGAV) overexpression construct (pcDNA3.1-ITGAV), following manufacturer guidelines. Post-transfection (48 h), cells were collected for functional analyses or molecular extraction to assess transfection efficacy and biological consequences.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) detection

Total RNA was isolated from both clinical serum specimens and cultured cell lines (MCF10A, BT-474, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, BT-549) using TRIzol® reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) per manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was verified spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop Technologies) with A260/A280 ratios maintained at 1.8-2.0. Reverse transcription was performed with 1 µg RNA input using PrimeScript™ RT reagents (Takara). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR® Green Master Mix (Life Technologies) on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies). GAPDH or U6 snRNA served as internal controls for mRNA and miRNA normalization, respectively. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each reaction was performed at least in triplicate.

Cell proliferation assay

Proliferative capacity was determined using a colorimetric Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) system (Dojindo, Shanghai, China). Post-transfection cells (MDA-MB-231, BT-549) were cultured in 96-well plates, with CCK-8 reagent (10 µL/well) added at 24 h intervals over 4 days. Following 2 h incubation, absorbance readings (450 nm) were acquired with background subtraction (media blanks). Triplicate measurements were used to calculate proliferation rates based on time-dependent OD changes.

Cell migration and invasion assay

To evaluate migratory capacity, transfected MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells were serum-deprived for 12 h before being plated in serum-free medium into Transwell upper chambers. The lower compartment contained complete medium with 10% FBS to establish a chemotactic gradient. Following 24 h incubation, non-migratory cells on the upper membrane surface were mechanically removed, while transmigrated cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde), stained (0.1% crystal violet), and quantified microscopically.

For the invasion assay, Transwell inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel (diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium) and allowed to solidify for 4 h at 37 °C. Cells were seeded in the upper chamber, and the subsequent steps were performed as described for the migration assay. The number of migrated or invaded cells was quantified by counting three random fields per well using ImageJ software. Experiments were repeated three times independently.

Luciferase reporter assay

To confirm miR-563’s direct regulation of ITGAV, we engineered pmirGLO dual-luciferase constructs containing either the wild-type ITGAV 3’UTR (ITGAV-WT) or a mutated binding site variant (ITGAV-MUT). Following co-transfection of MDA-MB-231/BT-549 cells in 24-well plates with reporter plasmids and miR-563 modulators (mimic/inhibitor + controls), dual-luciferase activity was quantified after 48 h using a commercial detection system. Normalized firefly/Renilla luminescence ratios from triplicate experiments demonstrated miR-563’s sequence-specific targeting. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Bioinformatics analysis

To predict potential target genes of miR-563, we utilized three online databases: miRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/), miRPathDB (https://mpd.bioinf.uni-sb.de/), and TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/). The predicted targets from each database were cross-referenced to identify high-confidence overlapping genes. For Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses, the overlapping target genes were submitted to the online database (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/login/). Functional enrichment analysis was carried out to evaluate miR-563 target genes, including Gene Ontology (GO) categorization (biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components) and KEGG pathway mapping to determine significantly modulated signaling cascades.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured by BCA assay. Equal protein (30 µg) was separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies against E-cadherin (1:1000), Vimentin (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:5000). After HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation, bands were visualized by ECL and quantified using ImageJ.

Statistics

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Categorical data comparisons were conducted using χ² tests, while continuous variables were analyzed with Student’s t-test. For comparisons across multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was utilized, followed by accompanied by Tukey’s post-hoc test after verifying the homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Alternatively, Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn’s correction were applied when necessary. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. For survival analysis, Kaplan-Meier curves were employed along with log-rank tests. To identify independent prognostic factors, univariate Cox regression was conducted for all clinicopathological variables. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model using a enter selection procedure. The results are represented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The proportional hazards assumption for the multivariate model was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. A two-tailed P-level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Relative abundances of miR-563 in TNBC patients

Analysis of the GSE45498 dataset revealed a significant reduction of miR-563 in TNBC patients compared to healthy controls, as illustrated by the volcano plot (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, serum miR-563 expression was markedly lower in TNBC patients than in both healthy controls and non-TNBC patients (P < 0.001). The ROC curve analysis indicated a high diagnostic potential, with an AUC of 0.895 (95% CI: 0.842–0.948), sensitivity of 82.50%, and specificity of 85.00%, suggesting that miR-563 may serve as a potential biomarker for distinguishing TNBC from healthy individuals (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, ROC analysis corroborated its discriminative capability between TNBC and non-TNBC, yielding an AUC of 0.770 (95% CI: 0.702–0.839), sensitivity of 73.33%, and specificity of 74.29% (Fig. 1D).

Association of miR-563 and clinicopathological factors of TNBC

To explore the association between miR-563 expression and clinicopathological factors in TNBC, 120 TNBC patients were stratified into low (n = 62) and high (n = 58) miR-563 expression groups based on the median expression value (0.65). Patients with miR-563 expression ≤ 0.65 were classified as the low expression group (n = 62), and those with expression > 0.65 were classified as the low expression group (n = 58). Our analysis of clinicopathological features revealed significant associations between miR-563 expression and key prognostic factors (Table 1). Patients with high miR-563 expression exhibited a lower incidence of LNM (81.0% vs. 51.6% in low-expression group, P = 0.001) and were more likely to present with early-stage (I-II) disease (17.2% vs. 38.7% in low-expression group, P = 0.009). Nevertheless, no distinct correlations were found between miR-563 expression and age (P = 0.339), menopausal status (P = 0.906), or tumor size (P = 0.073), though a trend toward smaller tumors (≤ 2 cm) in the high-expression group was noted. Additionally, regarding the subgroup analysis by molecular subtypes, treatment regimens, and metastatic sites, a significant association was observed between miR-563 expression and metastatic sites (P = 0.005). Patients with brain metastases showed the highest proportion of low miR-563 expression (7/8, 87.5%), followed by those with lung/liver metastasis (16/23, 69.6%). However, miR-563 expression was significantly associated with molecular subtypes (P = 0.344) and treatment regimens (P = 0.165).

Prognostic value of miR-563 expression in TNBC

Through Kaplan-Meier survival estimation, patients expressing high miR-563 expression (n = 58) demonstrated significantly better progression-free survival compared to those with low expression group (n = 62) (log-rank test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Kaplan-Meier method analysis for progression-free survival of TNBC patients. Patients expressing high miR-563 levels yielded a better progression-free survival than those expressing low miR-563 levels (log-rank test P < 0.001)

Cox regression analysis further affirmed the independent prognostic significance of miR-563 in TNBC (Table 2). In univariate analysis, low miR-563 expression was associated with poorer progression-free survival (HR = 0.325, 95%CI = 0.191–0.551, P < 0.001), as well as advanced TNM stage (HR = 5.220, 95%CI = 3.121–8.730, P < 0.001), LNM (HR = 5.333, 95%CI = 3.168–8.978, P < 0.001), and brain metastasis (HR = 5.518, 95%CI = 2.460-12.376, P < 0.001). In multivariate analysis, after adjusting for tumor size, TNM stage, LNM, and metastatic sites, miR-563 remained an independent protective index (HR = 0.374, 95%CI: 0.209–0.668, P = 0.001). Brain metastasis remained an independent risk factor (HR = 2.530, 95%CI = 1.076–5.949, P = 0.033), while lung/liver metastasis and bone/LNM were not significant after adjustment (all P > 0.05).

Influences of miR-563 on cellular functions of TNBC

To evaluate the impact of miR-563 on the cellular functions of TNBC, miR-563 mimic was transfected into MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cell lines. The relative expression of miR-563 was assessed across various breast cancer cell lines. Notably, miR-563 expression was significantly lower in TNBC cell lines against MCF10A cell line, with the most pronounced decrease observed in MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells, which were selected for the subsequent experiments to maximize the dynamic range for miR-563 overexpression studies (P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). Additionally, in clinical data, no significant difference of miR-563 was found among three molecular subtypes of breast cancer patients, including basal like, mesenchymal, and luminal types (P > 0.05, Figure S1). The introduction of the miR-563 mimic significantly increased miR-563 expression in both cell lines compared to the NC-mimic control (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B). In TNBC cells, cell proliferation was significantly inhibited in the miR-563 mimic group compared to the NC-mimic group at 72 and 96 h post-transfection (P < 0.001, Fig. 3C-D). Overexpression of miR-563 significantly decreased the cell migration of MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells compared to the NC-mimic control (P < 0.001, Fig. 3E). The results from the cell invasion assay indicated that the number of invaded cells was distinctly lower in the miR-563 mimic group against the NC-mimic control in TNBC cells (P < 0.001, Fig. 3F), suggesting that miR-563 inhibits cell invasion.

The effects of miR-563 overexpression (mimic) and inhibition (inhibitor) on the luciferase activity of ITGAV-WT and ITGAV-MUT in breast cancer cells were assessed. The results revealed that miR-563 significantly reduced the luciferase activity of ITGAV-WT, whereas it had no significant effect on ITGAV-MUT (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, serum ITGAV expression was significantly elevated in TNBC patients against healthy controls and non-TNBC patients (P < 0.001, Fig. 4C). An increased level of ITGAV expression was also observed in TNBC cell lines relative to human normal breast cell line MCF10A (P < 0.001, Fig. 4D). Notably, a negative correlation was identified between serum miR-563 expression and ITGAV mRNA expression (r=-0.6952, P < 0.001, Fig. 4E).

Bioinformatics analysis of miR-563 predicted targets

Bioinformatics analysis of miR-563 predicted targets was obtained in the intersection of three databases via Venn diagram (Fig. 5A). In biological processes, miR-563 targets were notably involved in ion transport regulation (e.g., sodium ion transmembrane transport), small GTPase-mediated signaling, and fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signaling pathways (Fig. 5B). For cellular components, miR-563 targets localized to critical structures such as the phagophore assembly site (autophagy-related), integrin complexes, and the extracellular matrix (ECM) (Fig. 5C). Molecular function analysis identified targets with ion channel inhibitor activity (e.g., chloride channels) and protein kinase activity (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway enrichment underscored associations with ECM-receptor interaction, neurotrophin signaling, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, pathways frequently dysregulated in cancer progression (Fig. 5E).

MiR-563 impacts cellular activities via targeting ITGAV in TNBC cells

To further investigate the molecular mechanism of miR-563 in TNBC cells, a recovery experiment was conducted. Transfection with the miR-563 mimic resulted in a significant reduction of ITGAV mRNA expression in TNBC cells compared to the blank group (P < 0.001). Co-treatment with the miR-563 mimic and ITGAV restored ITGAV mRNA expression that was suppressed by miR-563 (P < 0.001, Fig. 6A). Overexpression of miR-563 overexpression inhibited cell proliferation compared to the blank group. However, this effect was diminished when ITGAV was co-expressed with the miR-563 mimic, indicating that ITGAV restoration partially rescues the inhibitory effect of miR-563 on cell proliferation (P < 0.001, Fig. 6B-C). The significant decrease in cell migration and invasion induced by the miR-563 mimic was attenuated upon co-expression of ITGAV in both TNBC cell lines, suggesting that ITGAV restoration partially mitigates the suppressive effect of miR-563 on cell migration (P < 0.001, Fig. 6D-E). Regarding EMT-related biomarkers, E-cadherin mRNA expression was significantly elevated following transfection with the miR-563 mimic. This increase was reversed with co-transfection of ITGAV in TNBC cell lines (P < 0.001, Fig. 6F). Conversely, overexpression of miR-563 led to a decrease in Vimentin mRNA expression in TNBC cells (P < 0.001), an effect that was moderately counteracted by ITGAV co-expression (Fig. 6G).

To further validate the effect of miR-563 on EMT at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 6H-I, miR-563 mimic transfection significantly upregulated E-cadherin protein expression and downregulated Vimentin protein expression in both MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells compared to the blank control (P < 0.001). Consistent with the mRNA results, these protein-level changes were partially reversed upon co-transfection with ITGAV overexpression construction (P < 0.01).

Discussion

The present research demonstrates that miR-563 is significantly downregulated in TNBC and exhibits strong diagnostic and prognostic value, correlating with LNM and advanced stage. Although a trend toward smaller tumor size was observed in the high miR-563 expression group, it did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the sample size. These findings align with previous studies in other cancers, where miR-563 functions as a tumor suppressor, such as in lung cancer through LIN28B regulation [17, 18]. This is the first comprehensive study to explore miR-563’s role in TNBC, providing novel mechanistic insights.

Functional assays indicated that miR-563 overexpression inhibited TNBC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. ITGAV was validated as a direct miR-563 target. ITGAV, a subunit of integrin receptors, promotes cell adhesion, migration, and cancer progression [19]. Elevated ITGAV expression has been reported in gastrointestinal malignancies [20], gastric cancer [21], head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [22], and breast cancer [23], where it drives tumorigenesis. Consistently, we observed a negative correlation between miR-563 and ITGAV, with rescue experiments confirming that miR-563 exerts tumor-suppressive effects partially through targeting ITGAV. This aligns with integrins’ known role in promoting EMT and metastasis in TNBC [24]. ITGAV is known to activate downstream signaling pathways such as FAK/PI3K/AKT [25], which may mediate its oncogenic effects [26]; future studies should explore whether miR-563 influences these pathways.

MiR-563 modulates EMT markers, such as E-cadherin and Vimentin, indicating reversal of the mesenchymal phenotype and reduced invasiveness. This mechanism is pertinent to TNBC pathogenesis, where EMT drives metastasis and therapy resistance. Western blot validation at protein level strengthens this conclusion. Partial reversal upon ITGAV co-expression suggests involvement of additional targets and pathways. Integrin-mediated signaling is associated with other oncogenic pathways like TGF-β and Wnt/β-catenin, which are critical for EMT progression [27], suggesting miR-563 may exert multifaceted effects through multiple nodes in these networks.

Beyond its cell-autonomous effects, miR-563 may influence the tumor microenvironment (TME) through ITGAV-mediated mechanisms. ITGAV, as part of integrin heterodimers, plays a critical role in extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion, migration, and signaling [28]. By downregulating ITGAV, miR-563 may therefore exert pleiotropic effects on multiple TME compartments, including ECM remodeling, angiogenesis, and immune modulation. Future studies should investigate these possibilities in co-culture systems or in vivo models.

When compared to other well-studied miRNAs in TNBC, such as the miR-200 family and miR-34a [29, 30], miR-563 appears to function through distinct mechanisms involving integrin signaling. This diversity highlights the potential for combinatorial miRNA-based therapies targeting multiple oncogenic pathways.

Bioinformatics analysis supported these findings, revealing enrichment of miR-563 targets in pathways critical for cancer progression, including ECM-receptor interaction and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. The ECM-receptor interaction pathway regulates cell adhesion, migration, and survival in TNBC [31]. By targeting ITGAV and other components, miR-563 may disrupt the tumor microenvironment’s metastatic support. Ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis enrichment suggests that miR-563 may influence protein stability, destabilizing oncoproteins or stabilizing tumor suppressors. These findings highlight miR-563’s potential as a master regulator targeting multiple hallmarks of cancer. The presence of other potential targets, such as FGFR family and ubiquitin-related genes, suggests that miR-563 may exert multi-target synergistic effects, warranting further investigation.

The clinical translation of miRNA-based therapies faces several challenges, including efficient delivery, stability in circulation, and potential off-target effects. Nevertheless, ongoing clinical trials using miRNA mimics provides a proof-of-concept that such approaches may eventually become feasible for cancers like TNBC.

Although our study establishes ITGAV as a direct and functional target of miR-563, it is important to note that miRNAs often regulate multiple genes within complex networks, the bioinformatics analysis revealed several other potential targets involved in pathways such as ECM-receptor interaction and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. Thus, while ITGAV appears to be a major mediator, the overall tumor-suppressive effects of miR-563 may involve coordinated regulation of multiple downstream effectors. Future studies should also explore the upstream regulatory mechanisms of miR-563 downregulation in TNBC, such as promoter methylation or ceRNA networks, to provide a complete picture of its dysregulation.

The limitations of this study should be stated in our study. Firstly, the study primarily relied on in vitro cell line models, which lack the intricate stromal interactions and cellular diversity characteristic of human TNBC tumors. Moreover, the two TNBC cell used (MDA-MB-231 and BT-549) both represent the mesenchymal subtype, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other TNBC subtypes. The absence of in vivo validation is also a significant limitation, as our current experiments cannot recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) involving immune cells, fibroblasts, and extracellular matrix components. Future research should incorporate additional TNBC subtype cell lines (e.g., HCC1806 for basal-like 1, HCC1937 for basal-like 2) and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models or genetically engineered mouse models to better mimic human disease progression and validate these findings in vivo. Secondly, while the study identified miR-563 as a potential therapeutic target, the clinical applicability of miR-563 mimics or ITGAV inhibitors remains unexplored. Preclinical studies evaluating delivery methods, pharmacokinetics, and combination therapies with standard TNBC treatments are needed to assess translational potential. Thirdly, it should be noted that the EMT marker analysis was performed at the mRNA level and protein level. However, cellular localization of EMT markers and functional EMT phenotype assays were not performed. These experiments would provide additional evidence for the phenotypic transition induced by miR-563. Future studies should incorporate immunofluorescence, as well as functional adhesion assays. Such comprehensive validation would further strengthen the conclusion that miR-563 inhibits EMT in TNBC cells. Finally, the single-center design limit the generalizability and precision of our clinical conclusions. Moreover, the sample sizes for certain subgroups remain limited, which affect that statistical power of these subgroup findings. Multicenter studies with larger, diverse cohorts are needed to validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of miR-563 across different populations and clinical settings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study establishes miR-563 as a tumor suppressor of TNBC cells that directly targets ITGAV and modulates EMT at both mRNA and protein levels, which are based on the mesenchymal subtype of TNBC. Its downregulation is associated with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor prognosis, supporting its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. These findings contribute to the growing understanding of miRNA-based mechanisms in TNBC and highlight miR-563 as a candidate for further therapeutic exploration. However, validation in diverse subtypes and in vivo models, and comprehensive EMT functional assays is essential before clinical translation.
