UGA4 and YBR062C Regulate Oxidative Stress Tolerance Under Heavy Metal Toxicity
Authors: Mustafa Al‐gafari, Houman Moteshareie, Thomas D D Kazmirchuk, Jiashu Wang, Sarah Takallou, Parvin Khosravifar, Mohsen Hooshyar, Bahram Samanfar, Ashkan Golshani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHeavy metals impose a major cellular stress by promoting protein dysfunction and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae , the glutathione S‐transferase Ure2p is a key determinant of resistance to metal‐induced oxidative stress and can be synthesized through a stress‐responsive, cap‐independent internal ribosome entry site (IRES) in the URE2 5′ UTR. Much has been learned about the cellular responses to heavy metal exposure; however, the mechanistic details of how response genes are regulated in response to stress require further investigation. Here, we identify two previously unrecognized contributors to heavy metal tolerance, UGA4, a 4‐aminobutyric acid GABA permease, and YBR062C, a poorly characterized gene previously linked to filamentous growth. Deletion of UGA4 or YBR062C caused notable sensitivity when cells were exposed to sub‐inhibitory concentrations of cadmium (Cd), arsenite (As(III)), and nickel (Ni), with colony‐forming units reduced by approximately 50%–80% relative to the wild‐type strain. Genetic analysis positioned both genes in the URE2 pathway. Double mutants ure2Δ uga4Δ and ure2Δ ybr062cΔ were not more sensitive than the single mutant ure2Δ. URE2 overexpression restored metal resistance in uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ backgrounds, whereas UGA4 or YBR062C overexpression did not rescue ure2Δ. Mechanistically, UGA4 and YBR062C acted post‐transcriptionally. URE2 mRNA abundance was unchanged, but Ure2p protein levels were reduced, particularly under Cd stress. Polysome profiling revealed decreased ribosomal association of URE2 mRNA in both mutants, and a URE2‐IRES–dependent β‐galactosidase reporter showed approximately 80% lower activity without affecting cap‐dependent translation or altering reporter mRNA levels. Together, these data demonstrate that UGA4 and YBR062C promote heavy metal tolerance by enabling IRES‐mediated translation of URE2 mRNA.Keywords: heavy metals, IRES, oxidative stress, translation, URE2, yeast

Introduction

Heavy metals and metalloids such as cadmium (Cd), arsenic (As(III)), and nickel (Ni) have long been recognized as toxic to biological organisms, even at trace concentrations [1, 2, 3]. While certain metals (e.g., iron, cobalt, zinc) are essential micronutrients, the unchecked accumulation of non‐essential heavy metals poses serious risks to environmental and human health [4]. Industrial activities, including mining, manufacturing, and improper waste disposal, have greatly increased the prevalence of heavy metal contamination, concentrating these toxic elements in many ecosystems [5, 6]. The rise in environmental heavy metal levels is a growing concern for ecological stability and public health worldwide [7, 8, 9]. Living organisms have evolved defense mechanisms to withstand heavy metal stress. Microorganisms, in particular, have long been exposed to naturally occurring fluctuations in heavy metal availability and have adapted specialized mitigation strategies [10]. These include metal‐ion efflux pumps [11], metal‐sequestering proteins such as metallothioneins [12] and antioxidant enzymes that counteract reactive oxygen species (ROS) [10, 13]. Baker's yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a model eukaryote that employs such defenses and has been extensively used to study cellular responses to heavy metal‐induced oxidative stress [14, 15, 16, 17].

Heavy metals inflict damage on cells through multiple molecular pathways. Metals like Cd and Ni can bind to protein sulfhydryl groups, causing protein misfolding and loss of function [17]. They can also displace essential cations (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+, Zn2+) from the active sites of enzymes, disrupting critical biological processes [18]. These disturbances often impair the cell's antioxidant systems, leading to accumulation of ROS [14]. Excess ROS in turn damages nucleic acids, lipids and proteins, exacerbating oxidative stress and eventually triggering cell death if not remedied [17, 19]. To mitigate heavy metal toxicity, cells often activate detoxification pathways such as glutathione‐mediated detoxification [17, 20, 21]. Glutathione, a cysteine‐rich tripeptide, plays a central role by directly scavenging ROS [22] and serves as a cofactor for glutathione S‐transferases (GSTs) that conjugate glutathione to toxic substrates. In S. cerevisiae , the URE2 gene encodes a human GST homolog that helps neutralize oxidative stress through peroxidase activity of its glutathione moiety [23]. Yeast strains lacking URE2 exhibit heightened sensitivity to heavy metals [23]. Notably, Ure2p is translated by both the canonical cap‐dependent mechanisms and an alternative cap‐independent mechanism involving an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) in its 5′ untranslated region (UTR) [24, 25]. Under normal conditions, most eukaryotic mRNAs require recognition of the 5′ cap by translation initiation factors to recruit ribosomes [26]. However, during stress conditions such as oxidative or heavy metal stress, global cap‐dependent translation is often attenuated [27]. Cells can selectively bypass this translational blockade via IRES elements, which can recruit ribosomes to structured 5′ UTRs without the need for a 5′ cap [27, 28, 29]. The efficiency of IRES‐mediated translation can depend on the activity of IRES Trans‐Acting Factors (ITAFs). These are proteins that facilitate ribosome‐IRES mRNA binding [30]. IRES‐mediated translation is exploited by many viruses (e.g., poliovirus, HCV) to synthesize proteins when host cap‐dependent translation is inhibited [31, 32]. The non‐canonical mode of translation in response to stress has been a subject of numerous investigations and a growing number of factors are being discovered that regulate this process [33, 34, 35]. Despite these advances, the mechanisms regulating URE2 translation during heavy metal stress remain poorly understood. Further studies are needed to clarify how URE2 translation is controlled under stress conditions. In this study, we identify two novel factors that influence the heavy metal stress response in S. cerevisiae. Using genetic approaches, we discovered two genes, UGA4 and YBR062C, whose deletions confer sensitivity to Cd, As(III) and Ni. Although prior studies have identified multiple heavy metal‐sensitive mutants and several translational regulators of URE2, neither UGA4 nor YBR062C has previously been linked to heavy metal detoxification or cap‐independent translation control. UGA4 encodes a 4‐aminobutyric acid GABA permease involved in GABA import [36], whereas YBR062C encodes a protein of unknown function with no previously reported link to stress adaptation, though it may play a role in filamentous growth. We show that deleting either UGA4 or YBR062C leads to marked heavy metal sensitivity. Further experiments demonstrate that these genes promote expression of Ure2p, post‐transcriptionally. Our findings indicate that UGA4 and YBR062C help maintain cellular resistance to heavy metals by facilitating the IRES‐mediated translation of URE2 mRNA, thereby linking these factors to the broader oxidative stress response.

Materials and Methods

Strains, Plasmids and Deletion Library

In this study we used S. cerevisiae , mating type a (MATa) strain BY4741 with: orfΔ::KanMAX4 his3Δ1 leu2Δ0 met15Δ0 ura3Δ0 modifications and mating type α (MATα) strain BY7092 with: can1Δ::STE2pr‐Sp_his5 lyp1Δ his3Δ1 leu21Δ0 ura3Δ0 met15Δ0 modifications. The MATa yeast knockout library [37] was used for genetic screenings and mated with PCR transformed MATα strains as the query for interaction analysis. Overexpression strains and their respective extracted plasmids were obtained from the yeast overexpression plasmid library [38] and confirmed by sequencing. Yeast GFP clone collection was used to obtain Ure2p‐GFP fusion strain for subsequent modifications to be used for immunoblotting. Double gene mutations were conducted using homologous recombination via PCR with templates containing nourseothricin sulfate (clonNAT) resistance gene, amplified from pAG25 plasmid [39]. Primer sequences used for amplification and verification of deletion cassettes are listed in Table S1. Gene deletions were confirmed via PCR using primers flanking the target loci, demonstrating successful deletion of UGA4 or YBR062C open reading frames. Transformations in yeast for plasmids or homologous recombination knockouts were performed using the lithium acetate process, with subsequent colony PCR validation [40]. To investigate Ure2p‐IRES and cap‐dependent translation, p281 and p281‐4 plasmids were used [41], each carrying the β‐galactosidase open reading frame regulated by GAL1/10 galactose promoter as previously described [42]. URE2‐IRES region is cloned upstream of the β‐galactosidase reporter in p281‐4 plasmid (p281‐4‐URE2). All plasmids carry an ampicillin resistance gene for selection in DH5α E. coli and URA3 gene for selection in yeast.

Yeast culture media consisted of YP (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone) or SC (synthetic complete) composed of 0.67% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids and 0.2% dropout mixture with select amino acids. Yeast media was supplemented with either 2% glucose or 2% galactose as a carbon source and grown at 30°C. Standard lysogeny broth at 37°C was used for E. coli culture, and 2% agar was used for solidifying when required for all media. Plasmid extraction of yeast was done using Yeast Plasmid Mini Kit (Omega Bio‐tek), and E. coli plasmid extraction was done using GeneJET Plasmid Miniprep kit (Thermofisher) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Metal‐Sensitivity Analysis

Sub‐inhibitory concentrations of CdCl₂ were determined based on observed growth characteristics, ensuring induction of stress without unintended growth inhibition for each assay. Heavy metal sensitivity was measured using a colony counting assay. Yeast strains were grown in liquid YPD or SC without uracil overnight. The cultures are normalized to OD600 = 0.1 and 100 μL of diluted, 10−4 culture was used for quantitative analysis on solid media as done previously [43]. Cells are cultured on solid at 30°C for 48 h with or without the following: CdCl2 (Cd (0.1 mM)), Na3AsO3 (As(III) (1 mM)), NiCl2 (Ni (8 mM)), 6% ethanol and 6% ethanol + CdCl2 (Cd (0.04 mM)). YP + 2% glucose (YPD) was used for strains without plasmids and YP + 2% galactose (YPGal) was used for strains with overexpression constructs containing GAL1/10 galactose promoter. Colony‐forming units (CFUs) are counted after the 48‐h incubation period and compared to control media. A concentration of 0.1 mM CdCl₂ was used for mutant strain sensitivity analysis, as previously described [44]. A low sub‐inhibitory concentration (0.04 mM CdCl₂) was used for ethanol‐treated cells, as higher concentrations were lethal when combined with ethanol. For expression analyses, 0.06 mM CdCl₂ was used to induce mild stress without compromising cell growth. Genetic interaction analyses were performed using 0.08 mM CdCl₂ to maximize growth effects without reaching the concentration used for mutant sensitivity assays (0.1 mM). A high sub‐inhibitory concentration of 0.13 mM CdCl₂ was used for genetic compensation (phenotypic suppression) analyses to assess the ability of overexpression to overcome sensitivity.

Quantitative β‐Galactosidase Assay

Influence of gene deletion strains on IRES‐mediated translation of Ure2p was quantified using LacZ reporter constructs. The p281‐4‐URE2 plasmid contained four highly structured hairpins upstream to the URE2‐IRES region mRNA. The four constructs inhibit cap‐dependent translation, allowing for cap‐independent translation to be measured. The background plasmid p281 carries only the β‐galactosidase reporter as a control to cap‐dependent translation [45, 46]. The assay was carried out using O‐Nitrophenyl‐β‐D‐galactopyranoside, as done previously [44].

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total mRNA of yeast cells is extracted with Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen Inc., Germantown, MD, USA), followed by reverse transcription into complementary DNA (cDNA), using iScript Select cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) according to manufacturer instructions. Transcribed cDNA is used in qRT‐PCR using Bio‐Rad iQ SYBR Green PCR mix and CFX connect real‐time system software, according to the manufacturer's protocol. In the experiment, Pgk1p is used as an internal control.

The qRT‐PCR primers used are as follows:

Forward URE2: ATGATGAATAACAACGGCAA; Reverse URE2: TCATTCACCACGCAATGCCT; Forward PGK1: ATGTCTTTATCTTCAAAGTT; Reverse PGK1: TTATTTCTTTTCGGATAAGA.

Immunoblotting Analysis

Protein quantification of Ure2p‐GFP fusion protein is quantified via western blot analysis. Samples were cultured overnight in liquid YPD and treatment samples were supplemented with CdCl2 (Cd 0.06 mM) for 2 h. Extractions were performed using detergent free methodology as previously described [47, 48]. Bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermofisher Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) was used to determine total protein concentration as per manufacturer's procedure. Total protein extract at 50 μg was loaded onto 10% sds‐page gel and RESOLVED using Mini‐PROTEAN Tetra cell electrophoresis apparatus system (Bio‐Rad). Protein transfer was conducted using nitrocellulose 0.45 μm paper (Bio‐Rad) via Trans‐Blot Semi‐Dry Transfer (Bio‐Rad). Antibody incubation was done using anti‐GFP (Santa Cruz) and anti‐Pgk1 (Abcam Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Blots were visualized with chemiluminescent substrates (Bio‐Rad), using Vilber Lourmat; gel doc Fusion FX5‐XT (Vilber). Densitometry was performed using Fusion FX software (Vilber) with each density normalized to the PGK1 control. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate or more, with three different total protein isolates.

Polyribosome Profiling

RNA extraction from yeast cells was performed following previous methodology [49]. Cell culture was grown overnight in YPD liquid medium at 30°C. Cycloheximide (100 μg/mL) was supplemented to samples for 15 min prior to extraction. Yeast culture is harvested at OD600 0.6–0.8 then chilled in dry ice. Cells are lysed via mechanical disruption with 425–600 μm acid‐washed glass beads (Sigma). Following mechanical lysis and clarification, an aliquot of the polysome lysate was used for RNA extraction (RNeasy Mini Kit) and RNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Only lysates displaying intact yeast rRNA bands with minimal degradation were used [50]. Total RNA at 50 μg is loaded onto a 10%–50% sucrose gradient, formed by the Biocomp gradient maker (Biocompinstruments), with centrifugation done at 40 000 rpm for 2 h at 4°C (Beckman Optima LE‐80 K Ultracentrifuge) for separation of particles. Following centrifugation, samples are analyzed via Biocomp Gradient Station (Biocompinstruments) equipped with a flow cell connected to a spectrophotometer (Bio‐Rad Econo UV monitor) to read absorbance at 254 nm. Untranslated mRNAs were found in the top fractions, separated from polysome‐associated mRNAs (bottom fractions) as described previously [44]. Fractions at ~650 μL each were collected and adjusted to 1% SDS for URE2 mRNA analysis via qRT‐PCR as described previously [50]. Each fraction had Luciferase RNA (0.1 μg/mL) added as internal control. RNA was precipitated using Glycoblue and acidic phenol (pH 4), as described previously [51]. Purified RNA is analyzed with qRT‐PCR to quantify URE2 mRNA levels. Normalized and relative values of each fraction were calculated using Sehgal's formula [52].

Genetic Interaction Analysis

Genetic interaction analysis was conducted using Synthetic Genetic Array (SGA), through large screening via double deletion mutants and subsequent colony size analysis. UGA4 and YBR062C were each substituted with clonNAT markers in MATα strain and crossed with two MATa haploid deletion arrays. The first array consisted of 384 deletion mutants involved in translation processes, while the second array consisted of 384 random deletions for control [53]. Following selection rounds using various markers in both MATα and MATa strains, haploid progeny with double deletions were selected for further analysis as done previously [54]. Colony size was quantified using SGAtools to generate normalized fitness scores. For ease of visualization, these scores were subsequently converted into color coded gradients representing relative fitness differences across the genetic interaction networks [55, 56, 57]. Conditional SGA analysis is performed using sub‐inhibitory concentrations of Cd (0.08 mM) and cycloheximide (60 ng/mL). Phenotypic suppression array (PSA) is performed following similar procedures described earlier but with overexpression plasmids of either UGA4 or YBR062C in the arrays used. PSA measured phenotypic changes that arose from the overexpression by using higher sub‐inhibitory concentrations of Cd (0.13 mM) and cycloheximide (100 ng/mL). For all arrays, interactions with ≥ 25% colony size change with two of three biological replicates reflecting this are classified as candidate genetic interactions, consistent with standard methodologies [37, 55]. Identified hits are categorized based on biological processes, gene ontology, and molecular function. Gene ontology enrichment was conducted using the Genemania database (http://genemania.org, accessed on June 2024).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism. Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data meeting parametric assumptions were analyzed by ANOVA appropriate to the design; two‐way ANOVA for strain/genotype × treatment/condition experiments and one‐way ANOVA when only genotype varied, followed by planned comparisons of each genotype/strain to WT within the same treatment/condition using Dunnett's multiple comparisons test (two‐tailed). Results are reported as mean ± SD. In figures, a single asterisk (*) denotes Dunnett‐adjusted p‐value < 0.05; full statistical details are provided in each figure legend. For network figures (Figures 6, 7), functional enrichment was performed in GeneMANIA and reported as p‐values. For large‐scale genetic interaction analyses, predefined colony size thresholds and reproducibility across independent biological replicates were used to define high‐confidence interactions, consistent with standard practices [55, 56, 57].

Results

UGA4 And YBR062C Deletions Increase Yeast Sensitivity to Heavy Metals

Investigating the phenotype of mutant strains has been extensively used to study novel gene functions [58, 59]. In the current study we identified UGA4 and YBR062C as candidate genes whose loss impairs cell growth under heavy metal stress. Colony count assays revealed that uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ single gene deletion mutants exhibit reduced survival (50%–80% fewer colonies) compared to WT when exposed to mild concentrations of Cd, As(III), or Ni (Figure 1A). Raw CFU counts are provided in Table S2. To ensure these sensitivities were specifically due to the deleted genes and not related to an unintended secondary change within the yeast genome, we reintroduced UGA4 or YBR062C on plasmids into their respective deletion strains. Overexpression of each gene rescued the observed metal‐sensitive phenotype of the corresponding mutant (Figure 1B), indicating that the observed hypersensitivity was not caused by secondary mutations and further suggesting that deletions of UGA4 and YBR062C influence cellular heavy metal tolerance. Notably, such a role in heavy metal response has not been previously reported for either gene.

URE2 is a key gene in yeast responses to heavy metal tolerance. Deletion of URE2 is known to confer similar metal hypersensitivity as observed for uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ [44, 60]. We next tested the potential genetic relationship between URE2 and the two candidate genes. We constructed double mutants lacking URE2 and either UGA4 or YBR062C. In both cases, the double deletions (ure2Δ/uga4Δ and ure2Δ/ybr062cΔ) showed heavy metal sensitivity that was similar to the single ure2Δ mutant (Figure 1C). In other words, combining ure2Δ with UGA4 or YBR062C deletion did not exacerbate the phenotype beyond the effect of ure2Δ alone. Overexpression of UGA4 or YBR062C in a ure2Δ strain failed to improve metal tolerance (Figure 1D). Conversely, overexpressing URE2 in the uga4Δ or ybr062cΔ background restored resistance to Cd, As(III), and Ni (Figure 1E). Together, these epistasis experiments suggest that UGA4 and YBR062C may function in the same pathway as URE2. The fact that high expression levels for URE2 can compensate for the absence of UGA4 or YBR062C, whereas the reverse is not true, suggests that URE2 acts downstream of UGA4 and YBR062C in the cellular response to heavy metal stress.

Relative Ure2p Protein Levels Are Influenced by UGA4 and YBR062C Deletion

Next, we investigated whether UGA4 and YBR062C influence the expression of Ure2p at the transcript or protein level. Quantitative RT‐PCR revealed no significant difference in URE2 mRNA abundance between the mutants and WT cells (Figure 2A), indicating that transcription of URE2 seems not to be affected by the loss of either gene. In contrast, western blot analysis of Ure2p‐GFP fusion protein showed reduced Ure2p protein levels in both uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ strains relative to WT (Figure 2B). Under normal growth conditions, total Ure2p levels were approximately 30% lower in uga4Δ and 20% lower in ybr062cΔ compared to WT. This reduction became more pronounced under heavy metal stress caused by a mild concentration of Cd, 0.06 mM. Under the stress conditions, uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ mutants exhibited ~80% and ~70% decreases in Ure2p levels, respectively, compared to WT (Figure 2C). Throughout these experiments, the content of the control protein 3‐phosphoglycerate kinase (Pgk1p) remained at relatively constant levels. These results demonstrate that UGA4 and YBR062C appear to be required for maintaining Ure2p protein levels, especially during heavy metal stress, despite having no effect on URE2 transcription. Together, the observed mRNA and protein content data indicate that UGA4 and YBR062C seem to positively regulate Ure2p expression at the level of translation. The requirement for these factors becomes especially notable when cells are challenged with heavy metal stress, consistent with a role in promoting cap‐independent, stress‐responsive translation of Ure2p.

Polysome Profiling Analysis of URE2 mRNA

To further investigate whether the reduced Ure2p levels in the uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ mutants stem from impairment of URE2 mRNA translation, we conducted polysome profiling. The overall ribosome distribution in both mutants was comparable to WT cells, with no significant changes in the abundance or position of free 40S, 60S, and 80S subunits or polysome peaks (Figure 3A). This indicates that global translation seems to remain largely intact in the absence of UGA4 or YBR062C and suggests that the observed decrease in Ure2p is unlikely to result from a general shutdown of protein synthesis. To assess whether URE2 mRNA itself is being translated less efficiently, we quantified its distribution across sucrose gradient fractions using qRT‐PCR. In WT cells, a substantial proportion of URE2 transcripts localized to heavy polysome fractions, consistent with active translation by multiple ribosomes (Figure 3B). In contrast, both uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ mutants showed a marked shift of URE2 mRNA toward lighter fractions, including monosomes and sub‐polysomal regions. This redistribution suggests that URE2 mRNA is less efficiently loaded onto ribosomes in the mutant strains.

Ethanol Exacerbates cd Sensitivity in the UGA4 and YBR062C Knockout Strains

One known enhancer of IRES‐mediated translation in yeast is ethanol. It promotes alternative translation initiation by inhibiting initiation factor eIF2A [61, 62]. Therefore, under ethanol exposure, cells exhibit an increased reliance on IRES‐mediated translation, suggesting that ethanol treatment may serve as an indirect measure of IRES activity. Consequently, we next examined the effect of combining a mild ethanol treatment with Cd exposure. Neither 6% ethanol alone nor 0.04 mM Cd alone caused notable growth defects in WT or single mutants; however, the combination of ethanol and Cd dramatically reduced the growth of uga4Δ, ybr062cΔ, ure2Δ, and double deletion strains (Figure 4 and Table S2). It was observed that in Cd (0.04 mM) + 6% ethanol conditions, uga4Δ and ure2Δ had approximately 90% reduction, followed by approximately 50% reduction for ybr062CΔ single deletion mutant, while eif2aΔ had no significant changes compared to WT. The greater reduction observed in uga4Δ compared with ybr062cΔ suggests a quantitative difference in their contributions to stress tolerance under conditions where cap‐dependent translation is further impaired. Specifically, this indicates that UGA4 seems to play a more prominent role than YBR062C in supporting cell viability when cap‐dependent translation is inhibited by ethanol under the current experimental condition. This synergistic hypersensitivity to combined stress is in agreement with UGA4 and YBR062C becoming more relevant for cell viability when cap‐dependent translation is further impaired by ethanol. It should be noted that the reliance on IRES‐mediated translation in the presence of ethanol should not be considered as a direct measurement of IRES‐mediated translation.

To directly test the involvement of an IRES‐specific translational factor, we constructed double mutants lacking EIF2A along with UGA4, YBR062C, or URE2. We found all ure2Δ double mutants as well as eif2aΔ/uga4Δ to exhibit approximately 90% count reduction in Cd plus ethanol exposure, while eif2aΔ/ybr062cΔ double mutants exhibited approximately 60% (Figure 4). Additionally, we found that with Cd (0.04 mM) exposure alone, eif2aΔ/uga4Δ and eif2aΔ/ure2Δ had a reduction of approximately 90%, while eif2aΔ/ybr062cΔ had an approximately 50% reduction. This is consistent with a central role for URE2 in the observed sensitivities. These findings support a model in which UGA4 and YBR062C function alongside other IRES regulators (like eIF2A) to enable cap‐independent translation during heavy metal stress. When this alternative translation pathway is compromised either by ethanol or by removal of eIF2A, the absence of URE2, UGA4, or YBR062C becomes acutely detrimental.

Deletion of UGA4 or YBR062C Impairs the Expression of URE2 ‐IRES Driven Reporter Gene

To further assess the involvement of UGA4 and YBR062C in cap‐independent translation of URE2, we employed a reporter assay that utilizes IRES‐mediated translation. In this system, the URE2 5′ UTR drives expression of β‐galactosidase mRNAs (p281‐4‐URE2 plasmid). A control plasmid (p281) that carries a β‐galactosidase gene lacking IRES elements and reports cap‐dependent translation was also utilized. Deletion of UGA4 or YBR062C led to a notable reduction in IRES‐driven reporter activity. In strains carrying the URE2–IRES reporter, β‐galactosidase levels in uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ were only 20% of the WT level (an 80% decrease; Figure 5A). By contrast, in strains carrying the cap‐dependent control reporter, β‐galactosidase activity was similar in mutants and WT (Figure 5A). The loss of reporter expression under IRES‐dependent conditions implies that UGA4 and YBR062C are required for efficient internal initiation on the URE2 mRNA. Importantly, quantitative RT‐PCR confirmed that β‐galactosidase mRNA levels were similar in WT and mutant cells (Figure 5B). Thus, the reduced enzyme output in uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ seems to reflect impaired translation of the reporter mRNA rather than decreased transcript abundance. These results are in strong agreement with the notion that UGA4 and YBR062C specifically enhance IRES‐mediated translation of URE2, without detectably affecting conventional cap‐dependent translation.

Genetic Interaction Analysis Further Links the Activity of UGA4 and YBR062C to the Regulation of Translation Under cd and Cycloheximide Stress Conditions

To place UGA4 and YBR062C in the broader context of cellular physiology, we mapped their genetic interactions with genes involved in translation. Using SGA analysis, we screened for negative genetic interactions (nGIs), where the combined deletion of our query gene and another gene causes a more severe growth defect than expected from the individual deletions. Under standard conditions, UGA4 showed nGIs mainly with genes implicated in translation‐related processes (e.g., an interaction with RPL37B, a ribosomal 60S subunit protein [63]), whereas YBR062C displayed relatively fewer interactions but did contain many involved in translation processes with similar interactions such as RPL37B and NPL3 found in UGA4. However, upon exposure to translation‐inhibitory stress, more novel genetic interactions emerged for both genes. In the presence of sub‐inhibitory Cd (0.08 mM) or the translation elongation inhibitor cycloheximide (60 ng/mL), uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ shared several nGIs with factors involved in mRNA translation (Figure 6). Under Cd stress, both mutants showed strong negative interactions with NPL3 and HCR1. NPL3 encodes an RNA‐binding protein involved in mRNA export and elongation [64, 65, 66], and HCR1 is a component of eIF3 that modulates translation initiation under stress conditions [67, 68]. Under cycloheximide treatment [69], uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ both exhibited nGIs with TIF1 and TIF2 genes encoding the two paralogous subunits of the eIF4A helicase complex. The appearance of these condition‐specific interactions indicates that UGA4 and YBR062C seem to become important for cell fitness when conventional translation initiation is compromised. In other words, the deletion of UGA4 or YBR062C exacerbates growth defects when cells are already challenged in their translational capacity, consistent with a role for these proteins in alternative translation pathways during stress. It should be noted that the nGIs reported in this study do not necessarily capture the biological relevance of individual genetic interactions; additional studies will be required to assess their significance.

Phenotypic Suppression Analysis Links the Activity of UGA4 and YBR062C to Regulation of Translation Under Stress Conditions

Finally, we asked whether increasing the dosage of UGA4 or YBR062C could compensate for the loss of other genes involved in translation. Using a phenotypic suppression array (PSA) approach, we overexpressed UGA4 or YBR062C in a set of ~768 deletion strains (enriched for translation‐related genes) and screened for improved growth under stress [70, 71]. Notably, overexpression of UGA4 or YBR062C reversed the hypersensitive phenotypes of numerous mutants on media containing Cd (0.13 mM) or cycloheximide (100 ng/mL) (Figure 7A,B). The majority of strains rescued by UGA4 or YBR062C overproduction were deficient in factors that regulate translation. For example, elevated UGA4 or YBR062C expression restored growth to mutants lacking GIS2, ITT1, or DOM34 among others. GIS2 encodes an RNA‐binding protein that promotes IRES‐dependent translation [72] and is reported to physically interact with Ybr062cp [73]. ITT1 is a translation termination factor whose deletion was previously linked to defects in URE2 IRES‐mediated translation [74]. DOM34 is a ribosome rescue factor [75] reported to function as an ITAF [76] during certain stress conditions. The notion that overexpressing UGA4 or YBR062C can compensate for the absence of these diverse ITAFs further supports the role of UGA4 and YBR062C in promoting cap‐independent translation. In summary, the PSA results underscore that UGA4 and YBR062C can functionally substitute for multiple translation‐associated factors under conditions that impair normal translation initiation.

Discussion

In this study, we identified UGA4 and YBR062C as previously unrecognized contributors to the cellular response against heavy metal stress. Loss of either gene caused hypersensitivity in S. cerevisiae under heavy metal stress, a phenotype we linked to the impaired production of antioxidant Ure2p. Rather than a transcriptional decrease, the defect seems to be translational: URE2 mRNA levels remained unchanged while Ure2p protein drops markedly under metal stress exposure. This is to our knowledge the first evidence that UGA4, a membrane transporter, and YBR062C, a protein of unknown function, act as translational regulators to maintain heavy metal tolerance. Our data place UGA4 and YBR062C in the context of an emerging network of IRES‐mediated regulation of gene expression in yeast. Prior studies had implicated specialized translation factors in URE2 expression. For example, deletion of ITT1 or RPS1A also compromises Ure2p production and confers metal sensitivity [44]. UGA4 and YBR062C may also be part of this group of factors that ensure URE2, and perhaps certain other stress‐responsive genes, bypass global translation inhibition. Under heavy metal stress, cells often downregulate cap‐dependent translation, yet URE2 is still required to neutralize toxins [23, 60]. We found in WT cells that a large fraction of URE2 transcripts are associated with polysomes, whereas in uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ mutants, URE2 mRNA is concentrated toward non‐polysomal fractions, consistent with a failure of ribosome loading and a decrease in translation of URE2 mRNA. Furthermore, a reporter bearing URE2 5′ UTR showed an 80% drop in translation for mutant strains, while the cap‐dependent reporter was unaffected. Together, these results argue that Uga4p and Ybr062cp specifically enable IRES‐dependent initiation on URE2 mRNA, thereby contributing to maintaining Ure2p synthesis when canonical translation is impaired. Although we did not directly quantify oxidative stress markers such as ROS accumulation or glutathione redox status, URE2 has been extensively characterized as a critical mediator of oxidative stress tolerance in yeast. As URE2 overexpression rescues growth defects in uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ strains, it supports a functional link between URE2 translation and observed heavy metal‐sensitivity phenotypes. Future work may explore ROS accumulation and glutathione redox status in these mutant strains. These observations reinforce that in addition to other regulatory steps, heavy metal detoxification in yeast also hinges on post‐transcriptional regulations. A direction for future work will be to determine whether UGA4 and YBR062C act directly on the translational machinery or indirectly through broader effects on other cellular factors. Although our data support a role in cap‐independent translation, it remains unclear whether these proteins directly influence translation or modulate it indirectly via metabolic or signaling pathways.

Although our work points to an IRES‐mediated translational defect for uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ, the exact function of these genes in translation requires further investigation. Uga4p is a GABA transporter localized to the plasma membrane [77]. One possible interpretation is that its loss perturbs intracellular metabolite levels that indirectly modulate translation initiation. For instance, altered amino acid flux could influence TOR signaling or Gcn2p kinase activity, both known to affect IRES‐mediated translation [78]. It is also possible that our observations stem from a completely independent activity of Uga4p. Ybr062cp, in contrast, is uncharacterized but has been suggested in interaction screens to bind translational regulators such as Gis2p, a known IRES trans‐acting factor [73]. We speculate that Ybr062cp may act as an accessory ITAF. While definitive evidence will require further biochemical validation, our data provide the genetic framework for these hypotheses.

Our findings also highlight the layered regulation underlying heavy metal tolerance in yeast. Previous studies emphasized the transcriptional induction of certain detoxification genes such as CUP1 (copper) or HMT1 (cadmium) [79, 80]. By contrast, URE2 regulation appears to also operate post‐transcriptionally. This may reflect the rapid kinetics required, upon acute heavy metal exposure. Transcriptional induction may be too slow, whereas translational regulation allows cells to quickly prioritize the synthesis of pre‐existing detoxification‐related mRNAs. The inability of uga4Δ and ybr062cΔ cells to achieve this prioritization may explain their hypersensitivity phenotype. The idea that IRES‐mediated translation plays a key role in metal detoxification may also carry relevance beyond yeast. In mammalian cells, IRES elements regulate translation of stress‐responsive genes such as XIAP and c‐MYC [30]. Furthermore, oxidative stress and heavy metal exposure are known to activate similar translational stress pathways in higher eukaryotes [33]. Thus, the principle uncovered here, that selective translation initiation can maintain detoxification capacity under inhibitory conditions, may represent a conserved eukaryotic strategy. Future work could test whether mammalian orthologs of UGA4, or YBR062C analogues, also contribute to IRES‐mediated regulation under toxic metal stress. Although we establish the link between UGA4 and YBR062C loss and reduced IRES‐mediated URE2 translation, several limitations remain. First, we cannot yet distinguish whether these genes act directly on the URE2 mRNA or indirectly by modulating cellular physiology or other factors like ribosomal proteins and translation factors. Second, while URE2 is the clearest effector, additional targets may exist; other stress protective transcripts may also depend on Uga4p and Ybr062cp. High‐throughput ribosome profiling under metal stress could reveal the full spectrum of mRNAs requiring their activity. Finally, the molecular mechanism, whether metabolic signaling, ITAF activity, or RNA‐binding, warrants dedicated biochemical study. In summary, this work defines UGA4 and YBR062C as novel players in the heavy metal stress response, functioning at the translational level to preserve Ure2p. It expands our understanding of the cellular stress network, revealing possible crossover between translation and metabolism‐related processes in maintaining homeostasis under stress.
