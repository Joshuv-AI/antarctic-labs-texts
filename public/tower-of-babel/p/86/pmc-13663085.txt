Design and immunoinformatics characterization of a novel multi-epitope vaccine rPRRSV-35N targeting NADC30-like and NADC34-like porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus variants
Authors: Dawei Xu, Hefei Wang, Miao Li, Ying Zhang, Qiyuan Qin, Shenyang Gao, Anqi Cheng, Yushan Chen, Bing Li, Yuming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPorcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS), caused by the genetically diverse porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), remains a major economic burden to the global swine industry. Current commercial vaccines, including modified live virus (MLV) and inactivated vaccines, have shown limited effectiveness against newly emerging PRRSV variants, particularly NADC30-like and NADC34-like strains. There is therefore a need for improved vaccine strategies targeting these circulating strains. In this study, we used immunoinformatics approaches to design a multi-epitope vaccine candidate, rPRRSV-35N, against PRRSV NADC30-like and NADC34-like strains, based on antigenic profiling and prediction of T- and B-cell epitopes.ResultsThis multi-epitope vaccine candidate integrates six cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) epitopes, three helper T lymphocyte (HTL) epitopes, and seven linear B-cell epitopes (LBE). The epitopes were linked sequentially by flexible linkers. Porcine β-defensin-2 (PBD-2) and PADRE epitopes were incorporated to potentially improve immunogenicity. In silico prediction showed an antigenicity score of 0.6597 and a solubility probability of 0.530. It showed no toxicity or allergenicity, with a molecular weight of 36.64 kDa and a theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of 9.81. Molecular docking and normal mode analysis indicated stable interactions with TLR2 and TLR4 receptors. Immunoinformatics simulations further revealed that the candidate has the potential to induce both humoral and cellular immune responses. The construct was designed for in silico cloning into pET28a(+), and the plasmid was commercially synthesized. Following transformation of the synthetic plasmid into E. coli BL21(DE3), protein expression was verified by Western blot analysis.ConclusionsThis study constructed the multi-epitope vaccine candidate rPRRSV-35N targeting NADC30-like and NADC34-like PRRSV strains. Its expressibility was preliminarily verified via molecular cloning and protein expression assays. Further in vivo studies are required to assess its protective efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05726-y.

Background

The pig industry is a key pillar of global agricultural development, food security and socio-economic growth. However, pig production is constantly challenged by a variety of pathogens, which undermine pig production and hinder the industry’s sustainable development [1]. Among these, Porcine Reproductive and Respiratory Syndrome Virus (PRRSV) is a particularly harmful pathogen. It induces reproductive dysfunction in sows, as well as acute respiratory symptoms and growth retardation in piglets and growing pigs [2]. First identified in the United States in 1987, the virus spread rapidly to Europe. It was introduced into China in 1996 and has since become endemic globally, causing persistent economic losses to pig farming [3].

PRRSV is a non-segmented, enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the order Nidovirales, family Arteriviridae and genus Arterivirus [4]. Its genome is approximately 15 kb long and contains at least 10 open reading frames (ORFs) encoding various structural and non-structural proteins [5, 6]. Among these, GP3 is an essential structural protein of PRRSV that mediates viral entry by binding to the CD163 receptor. Its antigenic epitopes are highly conserved across strains (sequence conservation > 85%) and can induce cross-reactive neutralizing antibodies [7]. GP5, the major envelope glycoprotein, carries immunodominant epitopes in its extracellular domain. However, frequent mutations in its hypervariable region (HVR) reduce the protective potency of traditional vaccines [8]. Furthermore, the nucleocapsid (N) protein regulates host innate immunity via the NF-κB signaling pathway. It possesses abundant antigenic epitopes and elicits persistent non-neutralizing antibodies during viral infection [9].

Based on genomic features, PRRSV is divided into two genotypes: Type 1 (European genotype), represented by the Lelystad strain, and Type 2 (North American genotype), represented by VR‑2332. The two genotypes share only 55%-70% nucleotide sequence identity. Compared with Type 1, Type 2 exhibits higher genetic diversity and virulence [10]. Phylogenetic analysis of the ORF5 gene shows that Type 2 PRRSV comprises nine distinct lineages. NADC30‑like and NADC34‑like strains undergo rapid antigenic drift driven by frequent genomic recombination, with a recombination rate above 40% [11]. These two groups have become the predominant circulating variants in recent years. Their high transmissibility and frequent recombination pose a serious threat to the swine industry. For example, the NADC30‑like strain HENZXC‑4, isolated in China in 2024, has amino acid insertions in GP5 and a 131‑residue discontinuous deletion in NSP2. Such genetic changes are predicted to reduce the neutralizing activity of commercial vaccines [12]. Additionally, the NADC34‑like strain HuN2021 possesses a NADC30‑like genomic backbone. Recombination with a highly pathogenic PRRSV isolate further enhances its ability to evade host immunity [13].

For PRRSV prevention and control, modified live vaccines (MLVs) and inactivated vaccines have been widely developed and applied. However, MLVs have inherent safety concerns and cannot provide full protection against emerging PRRSV variants. Inactivated vaccines fail to induce strong PRRSV-specific antibodies and effective cell-mediated immune (CMI) responses against wild-type virus challenge, resulting in poor viral clearance in vivo [14]. Accordingly, conventional vaccines show obvious limitations in tackling PRRSV’s high genetic variability and strong immune evasion.

Antigenic epitopes are core components that trigger cellular and humoral immune responses and play a key role in the prevention of swine viral diseases. Accordingly, multi-epitope vaccines constructed with immunodominant peptides are a highly effective strategy for controlling viral infections in pigs [15]. Although peptide vaccines generally show weak immunogenicity, they have unique advantages against highly variable viruses such as PRRSV and can overcome the drawbacks of traditional vaccines. By incorporating multiple conserved B- and T-cell epitopes, multi-epitope vaccines induce cross-neutralizing antibodies and broad-spectrum T-cell immunity across different strains, greatly reducing the risk of immune escape caused by viral mutations. Moreover, adding adjuvant sequences and cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) improves antigen presentation efficiency. This approach addresses the low immunogenicity of simple peptide vaccines and elicits long-lasting, multivalent immune protection [16, 17]. Multi-epitope vaccine design has shown great application potential against various viruses. For example, immunoinformatics has been used to develop a multi-epitope vaccine targeting SARS-CoV-2 variants, which induced strong antigen-specific humoral and cellular immunity in mice [18]. Additionally, researchers constructed three multi-epitope antigens (MEASs) against Streptococcus suis by integrating 16 conserved epitopes screened from 8 conserved proteins, combined with a dendritic cell-targeting peptide to improve antigen presentation. MEASs exhibited favorable cross-reactivity with 29 serotype-specific antisera and induced robust humoral and cellular immune responses in mice, providing substantial protection against virulent strains of different serotypes [19]. Collectively, these studies provide critical theoretical and experimental support for the rational design of multi-epitope vaccines. However, current vaccine platforms are inadequate for controlling emerging NADC30-like and NADC34-like PRRSV strains. Their high genetic variability and sophisticated immune evasion mechanisms prevent conventional vaccines from providing durable, broad-spectrum protection. Accordingly, developing tailored vaccine candidates against these circulating variants is of significant practical and industrial importance.

This study aimed to design a multi-epitope vaccine candidate targeting prevalent NADC30-like and NADC34-like PRRSV strains using multiple immunoinformatics approaches, with the goal of overcoming limitations of conventional vaccines against these emerging variants. The design strategy used herein provides a theoretical basis for the development of vaccines against emerging PRRSV strains and may support further vaccine design for other viral pathogens.

Methods

Retrieval of PRRSV sequences

The complete genome sequences of NADC30-like (ON254651.1) and NADC34-like (PQ373814.1) strains were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and saved in FASTA format. Eight structural proteins, including GP2a, GP3, GP4, GP5, GP5a, M, E and N, were selected as candidate antigens. Referring to previously reported multi-epitope vaccine designs against group B coxsackieviruses (CVB), we set the protein length threshold at 100 amino acids [20]. Short proteins often provide insufficient structural information, which reduces the accuracy and reliability of epitope prediction. Furthermore, GP5a and E are mainly involved in viral assembly and release, with limited immunodominance and low potential to elicit protective immune responses. Therefore, these two proteins were excluded from further analysis. Antigenicity prediction was subsequently performed for the remaining candidate antigens.

Prediction of protein antigenicity and selection of target proteins

The antigenicity of the identified proteins was predicted using VaxiJen v2.0 (http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) [20]. This study employed the server’s default threshold of 0.4 for the universal model. The threshold of 0.4 was selected based on thresholds commonly used in previous studies for predicting viral antigens [21, 22]. Proteins with an antigenicity score of ≥ 0.4 were deemed to possess potential antigenicity. In addition to antigenicity, we prioritized proteins with documented roles in eliciting protective immune responses against PRRSV, as these are key targets for vaccine development. Based on the combined criteria of antigenicity score (≥ 0.4) and functional relevance to protective immunity, GP3, GP5, and N proteins were selected for further epitope prediction and vaccine design.

Prediction of cytotoxic T lymphocyte epitopes

Using the IEDB Conservancy Analysis server (http://tools.iedb.org/conservancy), the conservancy of candidate epitopes was evaluated. For cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) epitope prediction, three representative swine leukocyte antigen class I (SLA I) molecules were used. Epitope length was set to 9 amino acids, and the IEDB Recommended method was applied for prediction [23]. According to conventional criteria, IC50 values of 50 nM, 500 nM, and 5000 nM correspond to high, intermediate, and low peptide–MHC binding affinity, respectively [24]. Epitopes with IC50 < 500 nM were selected to retain candidates with moderate-to-high binding capacity, a widely used cutoff in swine viral epitope screening. Potential epitopes were subsequently validated using CTLpred (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/ctlpred) and VaxiJen v2.0 [25].

Prediction of helper T cell epitopes

Helper T-lymphocyte (HTL) epitopes were predicted using the NetMHCII-2.3 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetMHCII-2.3) and the NetMHCIIpan-4.0 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetMHCIIpan-4.0) [26, 27]. Given the limited number of well-validated prediction models for swine leukocyte antigen (SLA) class II, and studies demonstrating high structural and binding motif conservation between HLA and SLA molecules, nine common human HLA-DR alleles were used for analysis. Peptide length was set to 15 amino acids. Consensus predictions from the two tools with IC50 < 50 nM and low percentile rank were retained. A strict high-affinity threshold was used because efficient HTL activation requires strong MHC II binding, and IC50 < 50 nM is a widely accepted standard for high-affinity MHC II ligands [28]. To further enrich for immunologically functional epitopes, the candidate epitopes were submitted to the IFN-γ Epitope Server (http://crdd.osdd.net/raghava/ifnepitope/index.php) for assessment and refined by additional filtering using VaxiJen v2.0 [29]. Epitopes with positive IFN-γ response and favorable antigenicity were finally selected for multi-epitope vaccine design.

Linear B-cell epitope prediction

Linear B-cell epitopes were predicted in silico using the ABCpred server (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/abcpred/ABC_submission.html), an artificial neural network-based platform validated for B-cell epitope identification [30]. The window length was set to 16 amino acids, with a threshold of 0.51. To minimize false positives and select high-confidence candidates, only epitopes with a score ≥ 0.85 were retained for further screening [31]. These candidates were subsequently evaluated using VaxiJen v2.0. The final linear B-cell epitopes were defined as those satisfying both an ABCpred score ≥ 0.85 and the predefined antigenicity criterion.

Construction of multi-epitope vaccine candidate

The final multi-epitope vaccine candidate was generated by tandem assembly of conserved immunodominant epitopes screened through immunoinformatic approaches, along with immunoadjuvants and flexible peptide linkers. Cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL), helper T lymphocyte (HTL), and linear B-cell epitopes were linked by AAY, GPGPG and KK linkers, respectively. Porcine β-defensin-2 (PBD-2) and pan-HLA-DR-binding epitope (PADRE) were incorporated as an immunoadjuvant and a T-cell agonist, respectively, and fused to the N-terminus of the polypeptide via the rigid EAAAK spacer. Furthermore, a TAT cell-penetrating peptide was appended to the C-terminus of the vaccine candidate to improve cellular uptake.

Evaluation of vaccine candidate antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and solubility

The antigenicity of the multi-epitope vaccine candidate was predicted using VaxiJen v2.0. To evaluate safety, allergenicity was assessed using the AllerTOP v.2.0 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/) and AllergenFP 1.0 (https://ddg-pharmfac.net/AllergenFP/) servers [32, 33]. Toxicity prediction was performed using ToxinPred2 (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxinpred2/batch.html) [34]. Solubility was evaluated using SolPro (http://scratch.proteomics.ics.uci.edu) and the Protein-Sol server (http://protein-sol.manchester.ac.uk) [35, 36].

Evaluation of the physicochemical properties of vaccines candidate

The physicochemical properties of the vaccine construct were analyzed using the ProtParam server (https://web.expasy.org/protparam.html) [37]. Calculated parameters included molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), amino acid composition, atomic composition, extinction coefficient, half-life, instability index (II), aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY). The instability index was used to predict protein stability in vitro. The Instability Index of < 40 indicates a stable protein, whilst a value of > 40 indicates an unstable protein.

Secondary structure prediction

The PSIPRED workbench server (http://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/) was used to predict the secondary structure of the multi-epitope vaccine candidate [38]. The full amino acid sequence of the vaccine candidate was submitted for analysis.

Prediction of the tertiary structure

The tertiary structure of the multi-epitope vaccine candidate was modeled using the trRosetta server (https://yanglab.qd.sdu.edu.cn/trRosetta/). Model quality was assessed using ProSA-web (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php), where the Z-score reflects the overall quality of the predicted structure. Ramachandran plot analysis was performed using the SWISS-MODEL workspace (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/assess) [39].

Prediction of discontinuous B-cell epitopes

Discontinuous B-cell epitopes in the vaccine candidate were predicted using the ElliPro server (http://tools.iedb.org/ellipro/). Predictions were performed with default parameters (minimum score: 0.5, maximum distance: 6) [40].

Molecular docking

Molecular docking between the vaccine candidate and toll-like receptors (TLRs) was performed using the ClusPro 2.0 server (https://cluspro.org/tut_dock.php) [41]. The receptors used were TLR2 (PDB ID: 6NIG) and TLR4 (PDB ID: 4G8A), with the multi-epitope vaccine candidate (rPRRSV-35N) serving as the ligand. Docking poses were visualized using PyMOL to examine the interacting interface residues. Hydrogen bond interactions and binding affinity were analyzed using PDBePISA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/) and PRODIGY (https://rascar.science.uu.nl/prodigy/), respectively [42, 43].

Normal mode analysis

To evaluate the structural stability and flexibility of the vaccine candidate–receptor complex, conformational dynamics was analyzed using the iMODS server (http://imods.chaconlab.org/) based on elastic network model (ENM) and normal mode analysis (NMA) [44].

Immune simulation

To evaluate the immune response induced by the multi-epitope vaccine candidate in silico, immune simulation was performed using the C-ImmSim server (http://150.146.2.1/C-IMMSIM/index.php) [45]. The simulation length was set to 1050 steps, with injections at time steps 1, 63, and 126 (each step corresponding to 8 h). All other parameters were used at their default values [23].

Optimization of codons and in silico cloning

To achieve efficient heterologous expression of the recombinant vaccine candidate protein, the nucleotide sequence of the multi-epitope vaccine candidate was codon-optimized for Escherichia coli K12 using the JCat tool (https://www.jcat.de/) [46]. XhoI and NcoI restriction sites were introduced at the N-terminus and C-terminus of the optimized sequence, respectively. The vaccine candidate was designed for in silico cloning into the pET28a(+) vector using SnapGene software [47].

Protein expression and detection

The recombinant plasmid encoding the vaccine candidate was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. The plasmid was directly transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) for protein expression. Protein induction was carried out at 37 °C for 8 h using 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). After incubation, bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended and lysed by ultrasonication on ice. The target recombinant protein was detected by Western blot with an anti-His tag primary antibody.

Antigenic analysis of PRRSV and selection of vaccine candidates

The complete genomic sequences of PRRSV were retrieved from the NCBI database, which revealed that the viral genome encodes eight structural proteins. Reference sequences for these proteins were collected and stored in FASTA format for subsequent analyses. Among these, the GP5a and E proteins were excluded from epitope prediction due to their insufficient sequence lengths (< 100 amino acids), which are inadequate for reliable epitope identification. Selection of candidate proteins capable of eliciting protective immune responses represents a critical initial step in the rational design of epitope-based vaccines. In the present study, the sequences of the remaining six eligible structural proteins were subjected to antigenicity profiling using the VaxiJen v2.0 server, with antigenic scores serving as the primary criterion (Table 1). Proteins with a score ≥ 0.4 were designated as antigenic. Collectively, based on both their antigenicity scores (≥ 0.4) and functional significance during viral infection, the GP3, GP5, and N proteins were selected as the core target antigens for multi-epitope vaccine candidate construction.

Prediction of cytotoxic T lymphocyte epitopes

Using the IEDB server, we predicted CTL epitopes for the selected target proteins. Based on our strict selection criteria (percentile rank < 1% and IC50 < 500 nM), eight high-affinity CTL epitopes were initially identified. These preliminary epitopes were further validated using the CTLpred server, and antigenicity was assessed via VaxiJen v2.0 to prioritize candidates with superior predicted immunogenicity. This multi-step filtering eliminated epitopes that failed secondary validation or showed low antigenicity, leaving six CTL epitopes that met all our stringent requirements. Prediction of helper T cell epitopes

For HTL epitopes, we used both NetMHCII-2.3 and NetMHCIIpan 4.0 servers to identify sequences capable of activating helper T cells, promoting cytokine secretion, and enhancing immune responses. We prioritized epitopes that were consistently predicted by both tools with IC50 < 50 nM, yielding 62 candidates. To further improve epitope quality, all sequences were submitted to the IFN-γ Epitope Server and VaxiJen v2.0. Only epitopes that simultaneously passed both IFN-γ induction and antigenicity filters were retained, resulting in three HTL epitopes meeting all requirements. These epitopes were chosen for their exceptional predicted ability to drive helper T-cell responses, a critical factor for supporting both humoral and cellular immunity (Table 3).

Evaluation of the physicochemical properties of vaccine candidate

ProtParam prediction results indicate that the final vaccine candidate comprises 335 amino acids with an estimated molecular weight of 36.64 kDa. The theoretical pI value is 9.81. It contains 23 negatively charged residues (Asp + Glu) and 54 positively charged residues (Arg + Lys). The vaccine candidate structure comprises 5129 atoms with the chemical formula C₁₆₃₆H₂₅₄₇N₄₆₉O₄₆₃S₁₄. The instability index of the vaccine candidate is 36.73, which is lower than the threshold value of 40, indicating good in vitro stability. The in vitro half-life in mammalian reticulocytes is estimated at 1.1 h. In vivo, the half-life in E. coli exceeds 10 h. The designed vaccine candidate exhibits an aliphatic index of 60.39, indicating its thermal stability. The GRAVY value is -0.521, suggesting the vaccine candidate possesses hydrophilicity. Additionally, physicochemical parameters for HTL, CTL, and B-cell epitopes were separately evaluated (Table 6).

Secondary structure prediction

The secondary structure of the vaccine candidate was predicted using the PSIPRED online server. Results show that rPRRSV-35N consists of 31.64% α-helices, 19.4% extended β-strands and 48.96% random coils (Fig. 2B).

Prediction of tertiary structure and quality assessment

The trRosetta server was used to predict the 3D structure of the multi-epitope vaccine candidate, followed by structural analysis and quality evaluation. Five structural models were generated in total. All models were assessed via ProSA-web, and the Z-score was adopted to evaluate model quality, with a lower Z-score representing a higher-quality model. The Z-scores of Models 1 to 5 were − 5.04, − 3.01, − 4.77, − 5.42 and − 4.05, respectively. Model 4 was selected as the final 3D structure of rPRRSV-35N (Fig. 2C) owing to its lowest Z-score (Fig. 2D), which demonstrated the best structural quality. The Ramachandran plot from the SWISS-MODEL workspace showed that 98.5% of residues resided in the favored regions, with no residues in Ramachandran outlier regions or rotamer outliers (Fig. 2E). These results confirm that the constructed model has reliable stereochemical quality.

Molecular docking analysis

To evaluate the binding affinity between rPRRSV-35N and TLR2/TLR4, molecular docking was performed using the ClusPro Server. Thirty binding complexes were generated for each docking combination of rPRRSV-35N with TLR2 and TLR4. The top-ranked conformation of the rPRRSV-35N-TLR2 complex is shown in Fig. 4A, with a center-of-mass energy of − 1026.6 and a minimum energy of − 1111. Its interfacial residues were analyzed via PyMOL and presented in Figs. 4B-E. Likewise, the optimal rPRRSV-35N-TLR4 complex is displayed in Fig. 4F, with a center-of-mass energy of − 864.8 and a minimum energy of − 1146.5. The corresponding interfacial residues are shown in Figs. 4G-K. PDBePISA analysis revealed that rPRRSV-35N forms 10 hydrogen bonds and 6 salt bridges with TLR2, and 18 hydrogen bonds and 10 salt bridges with TLR4. Negative ΔG values indicate that both complexes are thermodynamically favorable. The dissociation constant (Kd) is inversely correlated with binding affinity. Normal mode analysis

To evaluate the structural stability of complexes formed between rPRRSV-35N and TLR2 or TLR4, we used the iMODS server to perform normal mode analysis and explore the kinetic characteristics of these complexes. The main-chain deformability graph (Figs. 5A and 6A) shows atomic deformation by mapping atomic displacement across all modes. Higher peaks indicate flexible regions, whereas lower peaks represent rigid regions. The B-factor graph reflects relative atomic displacement at equilibrium. Regions with high peaks possess strong dynamic flexibility and may be involved in molecular binding or conformational changes, while low peaks correspond to rigid domains. Collectively, these results indicate that both docked complexes contain flexible and rigid regions, and maintain structural equilibrium via the balance of flexibility and rigidity (Figs. 5B and 6B). Eigenvalue analysis showed that the rPRRSV-35N-TLR2 complex had an eigenvalue of 1.593692 × 10− 6 (Fig. 5C), and the rPRRSV-35N-TLR4 complex had an eigenvalue of 1.3977277 × 10− 6 (Fig. 6C). In the principal mode, a smaller eigenvalue corresponds to a larger motion amplitude and higher overall structural flexibility. Such properties are conducive to antigen presentation and molecular binding, demonstrating favorable dynamic adaptability of both complexes. The variance graph presents the contribution of each normal mode to overall motion. The purple area indicates individual variance of a single mode, and the green area represents cumulative variance; modes with higher variance play a greater role in structural dynamics (Figs. 5D and 6D). The covariance matrix (Figs. 5E and 6E) takes residue number as horizontal and vertical axes. Red indicates positive correlation, white indicates no correlation, and blue indicates negative correlation, which reflects cooperative or independent motions of residues. In the elastic network model (Figs. 5F and 6F), darker colors stand for higher atomic flexibility, and lighter colors represent stronger rigidity. A large number of light-colored regions were observed in both complexes, confirming abundant rigid domains and satisfactory structural stability.

Immune simulation

As an intracellular pathogen, PRRSV primarily relies on vaccine-induced cellular and humoral immunity for clearance. To evaluate the immunogenicity of rPRRSV-35N, immune simulation was performed using the C-ImmSim server. The results demonstrated that three immunization doses of the vaccine candidate elicited robust immune activation, with antibody titers showing three distinct elevation peaks (Fig. 7A). During the primary immune phase, substantial production of IgM and IgG antibodies, including IgG1 and IgG2 subclasses, was observed. Continuous increases in total IgM, IgG, IgG1, and IgG2 levels were detected after the second and third immunizations, indicating progressively enhanced antibody responses. The elevated antibody production was accompanied by increased populations of B and T lymphocytes (Fig. 7B). In addition to B-cell activation, three successive increases in T helper (Th) cell counts were triggered after the three vaccinations (Fig. 7C), accompanied by increased active Th cell proportions (Fig. 7D). Similarly, active cytotoxic T lymphocyte (Tc cell) numbers accumulated gradually after each immunization (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, the key immune mediators interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) were persistently upregulated after repeated vaccinations (Fig. 7F), collectively verifying the strong immunogenic potential of the rPRRSV-35N vaccine candidate.

Discussion

Since first identified in the United States in 1987, Porcine Reproductive and Respiratory Syndrome Virus (PRRSV) has spread globally and caused substantial economic losses to the swine industry worldwide [48]. The virus impairs reproductive performance and growth, increases mortality, and triggers immunosuppression that facilitates secondary bacterial or viral infections, with annual economic losses reaching approximately $664 million in the United States alone. Conventional modified-live and inactivated vaccines fail to provide adequate protection against the currently prevalent NADC30-like and NADC34-like strains [49]. Additionally, traditional vaccine development is time-consuming, costly and strain-dependent, making it difficult to cope with the high variability and rapid transmission of PRRSV. As a promising alternative, immunoinformatics enables rapid design of multi-epitope vaccines merely based on pathogen sequences. Such vaccines consist of dominant viral epitopes without redundant components, effectively avoiding viral shedding and vaccine-related adverse reactions [50]. Accordingly, this study aimed to design a multi-epitope vaccine candidate targeting emerging PRRSV strains using immunoinformatics approaches.

Appropriate antigen selection is critical for multi-epitope vaccine construction. We screened eight PRRSV structural proteins using VaxiJen v2.0 with an antigenicity threshold of 0.4, and excluded proteins shorter than 100 amino acids or with sub-threshold antigenicity. GP3, GP5 and N protein were finally selected as target antigens for epitope prediction due to their essential biological functions and excellent immunogenicity throughout the PRRSV life cycle. As documented previously [51]. GP5 acts as the major envelope protein carrying abundant neutralizing epitopes to induce humoral immunity. N protein is the predominant viral antigen that elicits long-lasting specific immune responses. GP3 participates in viral invasion and contains rich T-cell epitopes to activate cellular immunity. This combination of antigens lays a rational foundation for developing vaccines against highly variable PRRSV field strains.

A multi-epitope vaccine composed of CTL, HTL, and linear B-cell epitopes can induce broad immune protection. T cells, as crucial components and effector cells of the immune system, play an indispensable role in controlling and inducing immune responses [52]. T cell epitopes consist of peptide fragments from protein antigens presented by MHC molecules on antigen-presenting cells. These epitopes stimulate effector T cell activation, the formation of immunological memory T cells, and the secretion of cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [53]. To address the high polymorphism of swine leukocyte antigens (SLA), three representative and widely prevalent SLA class I alleles, namely SLA-1*04:01, SLA-2*04:01, and SLA-3*04:01, were selected for T-cell epitope prediction in this study. Recent population genetic studies have confirmed that these three alleles constitute the dominant haplotype Hp-4.0 (Lr-4.0), which is highly prevalent in major commercial breeds, including Yorkshire and Landrace pigs—the dominant breeds in Chinese swine farms [54, 55]. This haplotype therefore reflects the primary immune characteristics of mainstream commercial pig populations. Although genetic diversity exists within the pig population, this dominant haplotype is sufficiently representative to support the evaluation of PRRSV vaccine candidates for commercial pigs.

HTL epitopes play a crucial role in both humoral and cellular immunity by activating helper T cells (Th cells) [56]. Cytokines secreted by HTL cells, such as IL-2 and IFN-γ, promote B cell proliferation and differentiation, enhance antibody production, and simultaneously support the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [57]. In predicting HTL epitopes, we selected nine HLA alleles for analysis. Based on binding affinity and IFN-γ stimulation capacity, we identified three antigenic HTL epitopes. B-cell epitopes are antigenic determinants derived from antigen surfaces, recognized and bound by B-cell receptors (BCRs) to induce humoral immune responses. We identified seven linear B-cell epitopes from the antigen protein using classical bioinformatics tools.

Multi-epitope vaccines are constructed by splicing CTL, HTL, and linear B-cell epitopes with AAY, KK, and GPGPG linkers, respectively. These linkers are critical components of the vaccine, ensuring each epitope can independently trigger an immune response and preventing the formation of new epitopes that could interfere with the immune response to the original epitopes. The AAY linker used for CTL epitopes avoids the formation of fusion epitopes, enhances the natural presentation of CTL epitopes, and thereby increases their immunogenicity [58]. GPGPG and KK are used as spacers for HTL and linear B-cell epitopes, respectively, minimizing epitope fusion while enhancing proteasome processing and vaccine immunogenicity [59]. Multivalent vaccines composed solely of T-cell and B-cell epitopes typically exhibit poor immunogenicity; adjuvant inclusion enhances the vaccine’s specific immune response, growth, stability, and persistence.To enhance vaccine immunogenicity, we employed the EAAAK linker to conjugate the adjuvant porcine β-defensin 2 (PBD-2) and pan-HLA-DR-binding epitope (PADRE) to the N-terminus. PBD-2 is a crucial antimicrobial peptide with a broad antibacterial spectrum that plays a significant role in innate immunity; its use as an adjuvant enhances vaccine-induced immune responses [60]. PADRE, a universal adjuvant agonist, facilitates activation of antigen-specific CD4+ T cells [61]. The EAAAK sequence, acting as a rigid helix-forming linker, minimizes interactions between the adjuvant and other regions, optimizing the physicochemical properties of the vaccine. This approach enhances both immunogenicity and protective efficacy [62]. Additionally, we conjugated the TAT cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) to the C-terminus of rPRRSV-35N via a KK linker to facilitate the intracellular delivery of the vaccine candidate. The C-terminal CPP was rationally designed to enhance the uptake of rPRRSV-35N by porcine antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Previous studies have shown that CPP-conjugated peptide vaccines significantly promote antigen endocytosis by dendritic cells and macrophages, thereby improving antigen presentation and subsequent T-cell activation [63]. Consistent with studies on PRRSV-targeted delivery, the CPP sequence is capable of efficiently delivering exogenous antigens into porcine immune cells without significant cytotoxicity [64]. The final synthetic vaccine candidate consists of 335 amino acids, with a secondary structure dominated by random coils. This flexible conformation facilitates antibody binding and recognition.

Next, we analyzed the physicochemical properties of the vaccine candidate. The molecular weight of rPRRSV-35N is 36.64 kDa, with a theoretical pI of 9.81, indicating the fundamental characteristics of the vaccine candidate construct. The instability index, GRAVY score, and fat fraction suggest that the vaccine candidate protein is stable, hydrophobic, and thermally stable. Antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity assessments confirm the vaccine candidate is highly antigenic, non-allergenic, and non-toxic.

The 3D structure provides spatial coordinate information for all atoms in the protein molecule, laying the foundation for subsequent studies on ligand interactions, protein function, and dynamic simulations. In this study, the 3D structure of the PRRSV vaccine candidate was generated using SWISS-MODEL, followed by model quality assessment and validation. The Ramachandran plot indicates that 98.5% of amino acids reside within the Ramachandran favorable region, with 0.00% of residues falling into the Ramachandran outlier region or Rotamer outlier region. This signifies the high quality of the refined model. Furthermore, potential errors in the optimized model structure were queried via the ProSAweb server. The results yielded a Z-score of -5.42, confirming the overall structural reliability and high quality of the model.

TLRs are pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on innate immune cells and non-immune cells that recognize conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from various pathogens, thereby activating innate immune responses [65]. In this study, molecular docking and normal mode analysis demonstrated that the rPRRSV-35N vaccine candidate possessed strong binding affinity for TLR2 and TLR4 and formed structurally stable vaccine–TLR complexes, indicating its favorable potential to activate innate immune responses. Notably, these binding predictions were generated based on human TLR2 and TLR4 crystal structures. Although porcine TLR structures are theoretically more biologically appropriate, no high-resolution experimentally verified porcine TLR2/TLR4 crystal structures are currently accessible, and the structural accuracy of existing AlphaFold porcine TLR models remains unvalidated. In contrast, human and porcine TLR ligand-binding domains share high structural conservation, and the utilization of human TLR templates for cross-species docking has been widely accepted in swine virus vaccine research, supporting the rationality of the present methodological design [28]. Nevertheless, we explicitly acknowledge that the use of human-derived receptor structures may not fully recapitulate authentic porcine receptor–ligand interactions, which may impose minor restrictions on the species-specific accuracy of the current docking results.

Building on this, the present study utilised the C-ImmSim server to perform computer-based immunological simulations, providing a preliminary qualitative assessment of the immunogenic potential of multi-epitope vaccine candidates. However, when interpreting these results, the inherent limitations of this method must be fully taken into account. The default parameters of C-ImmSim are primarily derived from immunological data in humans and mice, and may not fully replicate the species-specific characteristics of the porcine immune system. Although core mechanisms such as antigen presentation, T/B cell activation and cytokine signalling are highly conserved across mammals, key differences remain. These include swine-specific antibody subclasses (such as IgG1 and IgG2 subtypes with distinct effector functions), the unique diversity of swine leukocyte antigen (SLA) alleles, and the characteristic immunosuppressive phenotype induced by PRRSV infection in pigs. These factors have not been explicitly incorporated into the simulation framework and may affect the quantitative accuracy of the predicted responses. Furthermore, the simulation does not account for the complex in vivo context of PRRSV infection, including viral persistence, antigenic variation, and the virus’s ability to modulate the host immune response. Consequently, the observed cytokine profiles and antibody kinetics should be regarded as potential trends rather than definitive predictions of protective responses against PRRSV infection. Despite these limitations, the C-ImmSim platform remains a valuable tool for high-throughput immunogenicity screening in veterinary vaccine development, a fact that has been extensively demonstrated in previous studies on PRRSV and other porcine pathogens [66–68]. The results of this study lay the foundation for the further optimisation of the vaccine candidate and subsequent validation in in vivo porcine models, the latter being crucial for confirming the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of this multi-epitope vaccine candidate.

Prior studies have developed multi-epitope vaccines for PRRSV. A reported candidate THs-CTLs-NEs was constructed using conserved epitopes from seven viral strains, with a VaxiJen antigenicity score of 0.4084 and an E. coli solubility value of 0.293, lower than the conventional threshold of 0.45 [28]. In comparison, we performed systematic epitope screening targeting GP3, GP5 and N protein, the three major antigens involved in the PRRSV life cycle. This strategy enabled our vaccine candidate to achieve better performance in key indices including antigenicity and solubility. Furthermore, normal mode analysis was incorporated into our workflow, which further strengthened the structural stability and overall reliability of the final construct.

This study also has several limitations. We initially screened HTL epitopes derived from NADC34-like strains to broaden the vaccine’s strain coverage, yet these candidates did not pass subsequent antigenicity validation and were therefore excluded from the final construct to ensure reliable immunogenicity of each incorporated epitope. Given that the selected epitopes share higher sequence homology with NADC30-like strains, the vaccine is likely to confer relatively weaker protection against NADC34-like variants. Furthermore, unlike most studies relying solely on computational prediction, we supplemented prokaryotic expression and Western blot assays and confirmed stable in vitro expression of rPRRSV-35N, which adds preliminary experimental evidence to our design. Even so, computational analysis combined with basic protein expression verification cannot recapitulate the complex immune microenvironment in pigs or reflect the actual protective outcomes of vaccination. Accordingly, the true immunogenicity, safety and cross-protective capacity of rPRRSV-35N remain to be further validated. Our follow-up work will include protein purification, in vitro functional assays and systematic in vivo immunization and virus challenge experiments using pig models, to fully evaluate the immune-activating capacity and protective potential of this candidate. Collectively, this study combines systematic immunoinformatics design with preliminary in vitro data, laying a solid theoretical and experimental foundation for developing novel vaccines against currently circulating PRRSV variant strains.

Conclusion

This study has rationally designed a multi-epitope candidate vaccine, rPRRSV-35N, targeting the currently prevalent NADC30-like and NADC34-like strains of PRRSV. Bioinformatics analysis predicts that this vaccine candidate possesses favourable antigenic and biosafety characteristics. Furthermore, the in vitro expression of rPRRSV-35N has been successfully achieved, validating the structural feasibility of the designed vaccine candidate. However, further in vitro immunological evaluations and in vivo animal validation are required to characterise its actual immunogenicity.
