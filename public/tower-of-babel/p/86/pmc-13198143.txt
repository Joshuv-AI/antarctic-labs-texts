Use of Latex Pulmonary Artery Catheters in Patients With a History of Cutaneous Latex Hypersensitivity: A Single-Center Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Brady L Houtz, Kellen J Round, Paul J Slota, Megan K Ford, Michael L Scharf, David L Fischman, Mahek Shah, Yevgeniy Brailovsky, Nicholas J Ruggiero, Alec Vishnevsky, Jordan Price, Michael P Savage
Published: 2026

Right heart catheterization (RHC) is one of the most common invasive diagnostic cardiac procedures. Balloon-tipped catheters used to perform RHC are typically made from latex. Rare cases have been reported of anaphylactic shock due to latex allergy from balloon-tipped pulmonary artery catheters or other endovascular latex devices.1 Accordingly, latex-containing balloon-tipped catheters are generally avoided in patients with prior anaphylaxis to latex. However, most hypersensitivity reactions to latex manifest as nonanaphylactic contact dermatitis.2,3 There is a paucity of data on the safety of using latex catheters in patients with only cutaneous latex hypersensitivity. Given the large number of RHCs performed and the frequency of latex allergy, this issue has important implications for a large subset of patients. The goal of this study was to assess the safety of using latex-containing catheters for RHC in patients with a prior history of cutaneous latex hypersensitivity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to address this question.

A retrospective review of 128 consecutive patients with a history of cutaneous latex hypersensitivity who underwent RHC was performed. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Thomas Jefferson University. Outcomes of patients undergoing RHC with latex-containing catheters (n = 100) were compared to outcomes of patients undergoing RHC with latex-free catheters (n = 28). Latex catheters were 5F and 7F Swan-Ganz catheters from Edwards Lifesciences (REF 110F5 and REF 111F7, respectively). Latex-free catheters were 7F latex-free Swan-Ganz catheters from Edwards Lifesciences (REF C146F7). Patients with a history of known anaphylaxis to latex were excluded (n = 5). Patients with reported latex allergy but no description as to the specific reaction, cutaneous or anaphylactic, were also excluded (n = 2). The primary outcome measure was the occurrence of periprocedural hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylaxis, angioedema, bronchospasm, allergic rhinitis, urticaria, and hives).

The study population included 100 women and 28 men, aged 65 ± 14 years. All patients had a history of prior cutaneous hypersensitivity (rash, urticaria) to latex. Patients in the latex-containing and latex-free groups were comparable in terms of demographics, cardiac risk factors, and indication for RHC (Table 1).

A total of 233 RHC procedures were performed: 150 procedures in the latex-containing group and 83 in the latex-free group. Intravascular catheter dwell times in the latex-containing and latex-free groups were 8.6 ± 5.0 and 9.7 ± 6.1 minutes, respectively (P = .31). Use of latex-free catheters was restrictive with respect to selection of venous sheath size and access site. Venous sheath size was significantly smaller in the latex-containing group; 5F sheaths were used in 53 of 100 (53%) in the latex-containing group compared with 0 of 28 in the latex-free group. In contrast, sheaths ≥7F were used in 47 of 100 (47%) in the latex-containing group compared with 28 of 28 (100%) in the latex-free group (P < .001). Antecubital venous access was used in 43 of 100 (43%) patients in the latex-containing group compared with 0 of 28 patients in the latex-free group (P < .001).

There were no periprocedural hypersensitivity reactions observed in either the latex-containing or latex-free groups for all 233 procedures. Review of electronic medical record charts revealed no cases of delayed hypersensitivity reactions after hospital discharge.

Hypersensitivity to latex has been problematic because of its widespread use in medical gloves and other commercial products.3 The prevalence of latex allergy is estimated to be 4.3% in the general population and 9.7% among health care workers.4 Given the large number of RHCs performed, the issue of whether latex-containing balloon-tipped catheters can be safely used in these patients has important clinical ramifications. Pinnock et al1 reviewed 53 perioperative cases of anaphylaxis in patients with indwelling central venous and pulmonary artery catheters containing chlorhexidine, sulfadiazine, or latex. Two patients with prior latex allergy developed anaphylaxis, one possibly immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated and one non-IgE-mediated. In contrast, our study evaluated only patients with a history of nonanaphylactic cutaneous latex hypersensitivity who underwent diagnostic RHC during which the mean intravascular exposure to latex material was <9 minutes. These specific design factors likely contributed to the lack of any hypersensitivity reactions in 150 procedures.

Although complications during RHC are infrequent, the most common complications are related to vascular access site injuries including carotid artery injury and arterial venous fistula formation.5 Accordingly, flexibility in options for venous access is important to minimize vascular complications. Use of antecubital veins is associated with fewer vascular complications, reduced fluoroscopy times, and earlier ambulation.5 Latex-containing catheters are available in smaller sizes that allow for greater access using antecubital veins. Latex-containing catheters also offer substantial cost savings over latex-free catheters ($89 vs $445 at our institution).

Contact dermatitis, which can be due to either irritant or allergic responses, is the most common clinical reaction associated with latex and its additives.2,3 Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed hypersensitivity (type IV) allergic reaction due to T-cell sensitization in response to chemical additives incorporated during manufacturing. In contrast, anaphylactic reactions to latex represent a type I hypersensitivity mediated by IgE. The absence of allergic reactions to latex-containing catheters in our study can likely be attributed to the different mechanisms of immune reaction responsible for cutaneous and anaphylactic reactions.

A limitation of this study is its retrospective design. Choice of the specific type of catheter was left to operator preference, which could have introduced an element of selection bias. It is also important to emphasize that this study pertains exclusively to RHC procedures in which catheters were removed immediately after the procedure. Therefore, these results cannot be extrapolated to patients with prolonged indwelling pulmonary artery catheters in intensive care unit settings.

In conclusion, in patients with a prior history of cutaneous hypersensitivity to latex, use of latex balloon-tipped right heart catheters was not associated with any periprocedural hypersensitivity reactions. It should be re-emphasized that anaphylaxis to latex was an exclusion criteria, and these were “in-and-out” procedures; therefore, these results cannot be extrapolated to patients with a history of anaphylaxis or to patients with prolonged indwelling latex catheters. Additional confirmatory studies are warranted to further define the relationship between cutaneous hypersensitivity and use of latex-containing right heart catheters.
