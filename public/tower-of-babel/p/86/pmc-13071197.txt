Cardio‐Kidney‐Metabolic Therapy Use Among Adults With Type 1 Diabetes and Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: M Luiza Caramori, Enrico Repetto, Christopher Perkins, Ambarish Pandey, Peter Rossing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsKidney and cardiovascular diseases are highly prevalent among patients with Type 1 diabetes. To date, no randomised clinical trial has reported on the impact of novel cardio‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) therapies (GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i) on kidney function in this population. We investigated a large US database to determine whether CKM therapies were used, beneficial and safe among patients with Type 1 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD).Materials and MethodsAdults with Type 1 diabetes in Optum's de‐identified Market Clarity database, consisting of claims and electronic medical records data, between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023, were included. Patients who had their first CKM therapy prescription during the study period were propensity score‐matched to those not on CKM therapy. The 253 patients with sufficient data to assess changes in urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) were included in the analyses. Rates of hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis were also assessed.ResultsCKM therapy was used by more than 10% of the patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD. At baseline, average age was 52 years, HbA1c 8.1%, eGFR 77 mL/min/1.73 m2. UACR median was 60.8 mg/g. Time to 30% or more UACR reduction was shorter among patients on CKM therapy versus controls [HR 0.76 (CI 0.61–0.95), p = 0.0158]. Rates of hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis were not increased among patients on CKM therapy.ConclusionsCKM therapy was frequently used in patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD, associated with UACR reduction and safe. These clinically relevant data warrant confirmation in randomised clinical trials.Keywords: database research, diabetic nephropathy, GLP‐1 analogue, real‐world evidence, SGLT2 inhibitor, type 1 diabetes

Introduction

Cardio‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) therapies, namely sodium glucose co‐transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) and a non‐steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (nsMRA), have been approved for treatment of patients with Type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD). These classes of therapies were shown to confer kidney and heart benefits in patients with Type 2 diabetes [1, 2, 3] and reshaped treatment goals and guidelines, now with greater focus on kidney and heart protection [4, 5, 6]. However, it is still unknown whether these agents confer similar benefits to patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD.

Data on the kidney protection effect of these agents in the Type 1 diabetes population are scarce. The EMPA‐KIDNEY trial included a small number of participants with Type 1 diabetes and showed that empagliflozin improved kidney health independently of disease aetiology [7]. A post hoc analysis of the DEPICT randomised clinical trial also indicated that treatment with dapagliflozin resulted in a reduction in urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) in a small number of individuals with Type 1 diabetes [8]. Similar results were observed in the post hoc analysis of the Tandem1 and Tandem2 trials, where there was a trend to UACR reduction among patients with Type 1 diabetes randomised to sotagliflozin compared to those randomised to placebo [9]. Similarly, a randomised clinical trial on the safety and efficacy of finerenone (a nsMRA) in patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD [10] was recently reported and indicated that finerenone use was associated with a reduction in albuminuria when compared to placebo [11]. In a recent retrospective study, tirzepatide, a dual GLP‐1 and glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist, appeared to preserve kidney function among overweight or obese individuals with Type 1 diabetes and mostly normal kidney function [12]. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) remained stable among tirzepatide users while it declined significantly in controls [12]. Moreover, previous real‐world studies on the beneficial impact of CKM therapy in patients with Type 1 diabetes included a very small number of participants with CKD, not allowing for sensible conclusions in this at high‐risk population [13, 14]. Although ongoing studies are underway, these agents have not yet been tested in dedicated randomised clinical trials nor approved for cardio‐ or kidney‐protection in patients with Type 1 diabetes. Regardless, a significant proportion of patients with Type 1 diabetes have received these therapies off label [15]. Herein, we conducted a real‐world data analysis of a large database to determine the frequency of use of CKM therapy among individuals with Type 1 diabetes and CKD and assess kidney outcomes and key safety parameters.

Materials and Methods

Optum's de‐identified Market Clarity database was used for these analyses. Data consisted of claims and electronic medical records (EMR) data between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023. The compilation of claims and medical records was loaded onto an Amazon Redshift server and analysed using SAS 9.4 M8 (SAS Institute Inc., 2023). As the data contain both EMR and Insurance Payment data, we referred to a specific EMR record as an entry and an Insurance Payment as a claim. Type 1 diabetes diagnosis was ascertained by a modified Klompas algorithm [16] in which individuals were identified as having Type 1 diabetes if they had a greater proportion of E10.X than E11.X ICD diagnosis codes before the index date. Applied this way, this algorithm has a positive predictive value for Type 1 diabetes between 94% and 96.4% [16, 17]. The index date was the first date a person with Type 1 diabetes received any CKM therapy (GLP‐1RA, SGLT2i, or nsMRA) among those who did not have a paid prescription for a CKM therapy in the previous 180 days. Thus, participants needed to be continuously enrolled for 180 days prior to their index date with both prescription and medical benefits. Moreover, to be included in the intervention group, patients with Type 1 diabetes must have a paid CKM therapy fill (refill) in the 179 days following their index date. A multi‐factorial approach was used to define CKD, which included at least one of the following, prior to the index date: (1) any CKD ICD‐10 diagnosis code (Table S1); (2) two UACR values ≥ 30 mg/g at least 90 days apart; (3) two eGFR values < 60 mL/min/1.73m2 at least 90 days apart, thus meeting Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria for CKD [5]. Patients' disposition on the KDIGO prognosis of CKD by GFR (G1–G5) and albuminuria (A1–A3) categories is provided on Figure S1. Patients who received a kidney transplant or had evidence of ESRD or dialysis (based on ICD‐10 codes, Table S1) prior to their index date were excluded from these analyses. All participants were required to have UACR and eGFR value available within 720 days before their index date. The closest value prior to their index date was used as baseline value. Additionally, all study participants were required to have at least one UACR value 30 or more days after the index date. eGFR was calculated using the CKD‐EPI 2021 race‐agnostic creatinine equation [18].

The control group met the same criteria as the exposure group, except that it had no evidence of CKM therapy prescription during the study period. Due to the low prevalence of nsMRA use in this population during the study period, all follow‐up analyses centred on GLP‐1RA and/or SGLT2i use. Once the exposure and control cohorts were identified, a control group was odds‐weighted for each one of the four possible CKM therapy groups (GLP‐1RA and/or SGLT2i, GLP‐1RA only, SGLT2i only, and GLP‐1RA + SGLT2i).

Propensity score variables included: age, biological sex, race, ethnicity, baseline UACR and eGFR, ACEi/angiotensin receptor blocker or statin use during the 180 days prior to the index date as well as the most recent HbA1c, BMI, diastolic and systolic blood pressure values and claim‐based Charlson Comorbidity Index [19] prior to the index date. The control group was odds ratio weighted to estimate the average treatment effect of the treated (ATT). Balanced information between groups can be found in Table S2.

To account for HbA1c, BMI and blood pressure missing data, SAS's Proc MI imputation procedure (SAS 9.4) was employed to estimate missing values for both exposure and control groups. A sensitivity analysis was performed for n = 5, 10 and 20 imputations, and calculations indicated only minor differences in the time to the first event (data not shown). Therefore, data are reported with 10 imputations.

The efficacy analysis was calculated as time from index date to the first 30% reduction in UACR, assessed at least 30 days after CKM therapy initiation. Hazard ratios (95% CI) were calculated between the intervention and control groups using SAS Proc PHREG combined with Proc MIANALYZE (SAS 9.4 M8; SAS Institute Inc., 2023) using the time to first event approach. The safety signal analyses were performed using the same cohorts and statistical approaches and estimated for the time from the index date to the first hypoglycaemic, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), or pancreatitis event, respectively. All events were assessed from ICD‐10 codes (Table S1).

Results

Over 13 million unique individuals with diabetes were identified among > 79 million individuals in the Optum Market Clarity database between 2016 and 2023. Of these, 337 013 were adults with Type 1 diabetes (Figure S2). The prevalence of CKD in this adult cohort of patients with Type 1 diabetes was 26.3%. Among patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD, 11.2% had a new CKM therapy prescription during the study period.

Considering the availability of medical and prescription benefit information as well as baseline and follow‐up UACR data, 257 people met criteria for the UACR analyses. Of these, four patients were receiving GLP‐1RA or SGLT2i therapy in combination with finerenone and were excluded from subsequent analyses. The final analysis, therefore, included 253 adults with Type 1 diabetes and CKD stages 1–4 who were initiated on GLP‐1RA and/or SGLT2i during the study period and had continuous prescription (Figure S2). Except for a lower Charlson comorbidity index and higher prevalence of congestive heart failure, demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between patients with Type 1 diabetes with CKD stages 1–4 who were started on CKM therapy during the study period and had UACR data available at follow‐up (and thus included in these analyses) and those who did not have UACR data available at follow‐up (n = 202; Table S3).

Patient Characteristics and CKM Therapy Use

Of the 253 patients included in the final analysis, 116 (45.8%) received GLP‐1RA only; 76 (30.0%) SGLT2i only, and 61 (24.1%) GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i (Figure S2). The 1670 patients meeting the same criteria as the study group but not on CKM therapy were weighted by odds to compose the control group (see Section 2 and Table S2).

Patients on CKM therapy were 52 years old on average, about half of them was female, and the majority was Caucasian (78%) (Table 1). HbA1c was 8.1% ± 1.8%, BMI 34.1 ± 7.1 kg/m2, median UACR 60.8 mg/g (95% CI 45.8–83.9), eGFR 77.3 ± 26.7 mL/min/1.73 m2, systolic and diastolic blood pressure 131.5 ± 15.7 and 77.9 ± 9.5 mmHg, respectively. Most patients were receiving renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors (73%) and statins (70%). Charlson comorbidity index was 6.3, with most patients carrying a diagnosis of hypertension (82.6%). A previous history of myocardial infarction (almost 10%), congestive heart failure (almost 14%) and stroke (almost 12%) were present in a significant proportion of patients (Table 1). Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients on the pre‐defined study subgroups: GLP‐1RA only, SGLT2i only, and combined CKM therapy (GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i) are provided in Tables S4–S6. Patients on combined CKM therapy had numerically higher UACR values at baseline as compared to patients on a single CKM agent.

UACR Outcome Analysis

The time to 30% or more UACR reduction (Figure 1) was shorter among patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD stages 1–4 who received CKM therapy compared to those not on these agents [HR 0.76 (CI 0.61–0.95), p = 0.0158]. Results were consistent among those on GLP‐1RA only [HR 0.71 (CI 0.52–0.97); p = 0.0324] or SGLT2i only [HR 0.71 (0.51–0.98); p = 0.0385]. Time to 30% or more UACR reduction in patients who were on both therapies (GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i) was not statistically different from the control group [HR 0.82 (0.56–1.20); p = 0.3197], although there was a trend favouring therapy, consistent with what was observed among patients on monotherapy.

The beneficial impact of CKM therapy was similar among patients with (UACR ≥ 30 mg/g) and without (UACR < 30 mg/g) albuminuria at baseline (p‐interaction = 0.6231; Figure 2). Results were similar for patients receiving GLP‐1RA only and those receiving both agents (GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i). There was an interaction between treatment response and baseline UACR (p < 0.0396) among patients on SGLT2i only, where time to ≥ 30% UACR reduction was only reduced among patients with albuminuria (UACR ≥ 30 mg/g) at baseline. There also was no interaction between age, BMI or HbA1c and treatment effect (Figure S3).

In addition, a greater proportion of individuals receiving CKM therapy achieved 30% (55.3% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.0235), 40% (47% vs. 37.8%, p = 0.0323), or 50% (39.9% vs. 31.1%, p = 0.0513) UACR reduction from baseline compared to the control group (Figure 3).

Safety Analyses

Safety analysis included all 253 patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD stages 1–4 who received GLP‐1RA and/or SGLT2i (Table 2). The rate of adverse events of interest, hypoglycaemia and DKA, was not increased among patients on CKM therapy. There was a trend toward a lower proportion of patients experiencing hypoglycaemia among those on CKM therapy (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.56–1.00). There were 137 patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD stages 1–4 who were treated with SGLT2i, either alone (n = 76) or in combination with GLP‐1RA (n = 61). SGLT2i use was not associated with an increased risk of DKA events compared to patients not on CKM therapy (p = 0.4441). Results were similar among patients on GLP‐1RA only, where GLP‐1RA use was not associated with an increased rate of DKA events compared to patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD not on CKM therapy (p = 0.3081). The number of patients with pancreatitis events was too low to allow for meaningful analysis (data not shown).

Discussion

The results of these analyses indicate that CKM therapy is safe and effective in reducing UACR in patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD. The analysis of this large dataset also confirms CKD remains highly prevalent among patients with Type 1 diabetes. Within the last years, SGLT2i, GLP‐1RA and nsMRA have been proven to reduce kidney and cardiovascular disease progression among people with Type 2 diabetes and CKD [20, 21, 22]. However, no agents in these classes have yet been approved for this purpose in patients with Type 1 diabetes. Despite that, our analysis indicates that a significant proportion of patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD are treated with these agents off label, as also observed in a recent publication [15].

It is well established that CKM agents modify both cardio and kidney outcomes in various patient populations, including those with Type 2 diabetes, CKD from other causes, high cardiovascular risk and obesity. Many progression factors are likely common among people with CKD and independent of disease aetiology. Moreover, the albuminuria‐lowering effect observed in patients with Type 2 diabetes on CKM therapy could be explained by the reduction in intra‐glomerular pressure, among others, and it is possible that similar mechanistic effects are induced in patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD. Herein, we assessed whether use of CKM therapy was safe and effective in delaying CKD progression among patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD.

UACR was selected as the efficacy endpoint for these analyses. UACR measurements can help to inform kidney and cardiovascular health and drive tailored disease management. A UACR decline of 30% or more has been associated with better kidney outcomes both in observational and in cohort studies [23, 24, 25], and proposed as a therapy target by the National Kidney Foundation [26] and the American Diabetes Association [4], among others. In our study, we observed that patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD who received CKM therapy were not only more likely to achieve clinically meaningful UACR reductions, but also achieved this favourable endpoint faster compared to those not on CKM therapy. These results are comparable to data from patients with Type 2 diabetes and CKD. In the DAPA‐CKD trial, dapagliflozin reduced UACR by 29% during the study period [27]. Similarly, in the EMPA‐KIDNEY trial [7], the relative difference in average UACR between empagliflozin and placebo was 28% among patients with Type 2 diabetes. Moreover, in a post hoc kidney analysis of the DEPICT trial, which included patients with Type 1 diabetes, the placebo‐adjusted UACR change from baseline to Week 52 was 31% for dapagliflozin 10 mg [8]. There are no published data from prospective, randomised trials on the impact of GLP‐1RA on UACR in the Type 1 diabetes population. However, the FLOW trial, which studied the effect of semaglutide in people with Type 2 diabetes, showed a significant reduction in UACR (a key secondary endpoint) compared to placebo [2]. Adding support to this premise, patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD randomised to a nsMRA in the recently completed FINE‐ONE study showed a 25% reduction in UACR compared to placebo [11]. Although these results are not directly comparable, this is similar to the 31% UACR reduction observed among patients with Type 2 diabetes with CKD randomised to finerenone in the FIDELIO‐DKD trial [3].

As important as the analysis of potential kidney benefits is that of safety signals of special interest, hypoglycaemia, DKA and pancreatitis. We observed a potentially protective effect on hypoglycaemia events among CKM therapy users, which deserves further investigation. It's well documented that when insulin dosage is significantly decreased, as well as in special circumstances (acute illness, dehydration, etc.), SGLT2i can enhance ketogenesis to the extent that the risk of DKA is increased [28]. This is particularly important among patients with Type 1 diabetes, who have insulin insufficiency as the main hallmark of their condition. Of relevance, a prospective SGLT2i study reported a similar rate of adverse events between patients with Type 1 diabetes receiving and those not receiving SGLT2i once a ketone testing strategy was implemented [29]. There was only one case of mild DKA in the dapagliflozin treated study participants [29]. In addition, a recent prospective study assessing the effect of GLP‐1RA (semaglutide) on glycaemic control among patients with Type 1 diabetes did not report any DKA events, despite reductions in insulin dose [30]. In the current report, time to first DKA was not different among patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD receiving any CKM therapy, or SGLT2i specifically, as compared to patients not on these agents. However, the total number of patients at risk, the relatively short follow‐up and the small number of DKA and pancreatitis events do not allow for meaningful conclusions regarding these agents' safety in patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD. However, our previous report on the cardiovascular outcomes of CKM therapy in a larger population of adults with Type 1 diabetes (not limited to those with CKD) also suggests these agents are both efficacious and safe in patients with Type 1 diabetes [31].

This report has multiple strengths, including access to a very large data set from which a well‐balanced control cohort was derived, its novelty, and the clinical relevance of these observations. Furthermore, only patients who were not on CKM therapy before study entry, who had continued use of CKM therapy, and had both baseline (before CKM therapy initiation) and follow‐up UACR data were included. Additionally, although we employed a well‐validated method to input missing data, no UACR or eGFR data were imputed. We are also cognizant that a high rate of drug discontinuation has been reported among patients with CKD on CKM therapy, perhaps especially among those on GLP‐1RA therapy [32]. Although our study did not directly assess this important aspect of therapy, enrolment in this study required a prescription renewal, and that, in combination with other study entry criteria, although significantly reduced the sample size, made these observations more robust.

As with any real‐world analysis, the limitations of our study stem from the use of EMR and claims data when compared to the standard data collection from prospective studies in a Type 1 diabetes population with CKD. Although we used a well validated algorithm [16, 17] to identify patients with Type 1 diabetes, it is possible that a small proportion of patients (less than 5%) who indeed don't have Type 1 diabetes were included in these analyses. However conceivable, it is most unlikely that this would significantly impact our observations. In addition, despite our efforts to devise a balanced control group by odds‐weighting to estimate the average treatment effect of the treatment treated (ATT), we cannot rule out residual confounding due to indication and channelling bias. Thus, causal inference is limited by the non‐randomised, retrospective study design. We also suggest caution when interpreting subgroup analyses as these had limited statistical power and may have thus missed potentially statistically significant differences.

In summary, our study indicates that, similarly to what has been primarily documented among patients with Type 2 diabetes, CKM therapy, namely GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i, not only confer kidney protection but are also safe in patients with Type 1 diabetes and CKD. Importantly, a GLP‐1RA mechanistic study (REMODEL‐T1D; NCT05822609) [33] and a randomised clinical trial assessing the impact of SGLT2i (SUGARNSALT; NCT06217302) [34] in the kidney, as well as a multifactorial intervention (STENO 1) including CKM therapies assessing cardiovascular disease outcomes in patients with Type 1 diabetes [35] are underway. These timely studies will add to the evidence provided by this real‐world data analysis.
