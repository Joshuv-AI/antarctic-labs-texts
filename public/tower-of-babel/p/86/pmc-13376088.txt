PSMB1 screened by CRISPR-CAS9 promoted breast cancer progression via PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling
Authors: Lu-Wen Chen, Jing Ning, Yong-Qiang Ye, Hua-Bin Ye, Hui Zeng, Li-Juan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer is a threat to women’s health worldwide. Recent studies have focused on the role of differentially expressed genes in tumors. PSMB1 has been reported to play an important role in tumors, however, its role in breast cancer has not been well defined.MethodsThe CRISPR-CAS9 data was analyzed and intersected with Cox univariate analyzation to screen differentially expressed genes in breast cancer. The prognosis of PSMB1 was predicted by Kaplan-Meier plotter, and the related major pathways were analyzed through GO analysis and GSEA. PSMB1-related gene mutations and Tumor microenvironment characteristics were analyzed. The expression of PSMB1 in breast cancer tissues and cells were determined by RT-qPCR and IHC. MTT, colony formation assay, EdU, Transwell, wound healing assay and cell cycle assay were performed to observe its function in vitro. The western blot was conducted to detect its regulation on EMT markers and key proteins in PI3K pathway.ResultsIn this study, PSMB1 was screened by CRISPR-Cas9 and identified to be upregulated in breast cancer samples. By further analysis, PSMB1 was found to be involved in tumor-related biological process and correlated with worse prognosis in breast cancer patients. In vitro experiments verified that downregulation of PSMB1 could inhibit the malignant phenotype of breast cancer cells. The western blot showed that si-PSMB1 significantly decreased the expression of key proteins and their phosphorylation form in PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling.Conclusionsi-PSMB1 inhibited breast cancer cell proliferation, invasion and migration probably by targeting PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, which brings innovative mechanisms for breast cancer regulation and emerging ideas for therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05272-x.Keywords: PSMB1, Breast cancer, CRISPR-Cas9, PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling

Introduction

Breast cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality among women worldwide [1]. With the year-on-year increase in the incidence of breast cancer, many female patients have suffered greatly [2]. While the precise etiology of breast cancer is still not fully understood, several risk factors have been identified, including age, family history, genetic mutations, hormonal influences, and lifestyle choices [3, 4]. Ongoing research efforts are essential to develop more effective treatments for all stages of breast cancer.

PSMB1, also known as proteasome subunit beta type-1, is a crucial component of the proteasome complex [5]. The proteasome is responsible for degrading unneeded or damaged proteins within the cell, playing a vital role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [6]. PSMB1 specifically contributes to the catalytic activity of the proteasome, allowing for efficient protein degradation [7]. Research on PSMB1 has revealed its involvement in numerous biological processes, including cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and immune response [8]. Dysregulation of PSMB1 has been implicated in various diseases such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune conditions. Several previous studies have reported the role of PSMB1 in tumor progression. Yang et al. demonstrated that PSMB1 directly binds to the onco-protein RAB34 and facilitates its proteasome-dependent degradation, potentially resulting in the suppression of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway and inhibition of colorectal cancer progression [9]. While in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), PSMB1 was identified as a potential target for bortezomib to inhibit the proteasome degradation of cell cycle check points and block cell proliferation [10]. In breast cancer, PSMB1 was predicted to serve as one of the biomarkers to prognosis and immunotherapy response, however, further verifications were lacked [11].

In our current study, we extensively mended the CRISPR database, and undergone the cox univariate analysis of breast cancer, therefore identified PSMB1 as an up-regulated gene in breast cancer regulation. Understanding the precise mechanisms by which PSMB1 functions may provide valuable insights into breast cancer progression and potentially lead to novel therapeutic strategies (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Analyses based on bioinformatics

The CRISPR-CAS9 data were obtained from the DepMap database (https://depmap.org/portal/). Genes were classified as proliferation-dependent if their CERES scores were below − 1 in at least 75% of the tested breast cancer cell lines. Univariate Cox proportional-hazards regression was performed to identify genes significantly associated with overall survival (p < 0.05). Additionally, the bulk RNA datasets were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, and the project name is TCGA-BRCA. The TCGA-BRCA dataset was downloaded by TCGAbiolinks package. Multi-dimensional validation was conducted through: (1) UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) interrogation of PSMB1-clinical characteristic associations, and (2) Kaplan-Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) survival analysis with log-rank testing. The clusterProfiler package (v4.6.2) implemented systematic enrichment analyses including: (i) Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, (ii) Gene Ontology profiling, (iii) KEGG pathway impact analysis, and (iv) Disease Ontology mapping using the DOSE algorithm. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between PSMB1 expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cell abundances (CIBERSORTx deconvolution) and immune checkpoint modulators. Maftools (v2.14.0) was employed to compare heterogeneity among distinct subgroups of patients. The Connectivity Map (https://clue.io/) was interrogated to identify potential compounds showing inverse association with PSMB1-high transcriptional profiles.

Samples, cells and plasmids

30 paired breast normal tissues, collected from a distance of 5 cm from the tumor boundary, along with breast cancer tissues, were obtained from Ganzhou Cancer Hospital. The protocols for the collection of human tissue in this study received approval from the Human Medical Ethics Committee of Ganzhou Cancer Hospital and were conducted in accordance with all pertinent ethical regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the research.

Human breast cancer cell lines (MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, CAL-51, SUM159 and SK-BR-3), as well as normal breast epithelial cell line (MCF-10 A) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Rockville, USA). The cells were cultured with corresponding mediums that contain 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum, Gibco, US) and 1% PS (penicillin/streptomycin, Solarbio, China).

The siRNAs of PSMB1 were purchased from Ribobio (China), the specific interference sequence is as follows: si-PSMB1-1 GGATCTGCATAGTGACCAA, si-PSMB1-2 GGCGCTTCTTTCCATACTA, si-PSMB1-3 GGAGGAAACTGTTTCCTTA. The transfection was conducted utilizing Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo, US) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

RT-qPCR

The total RNA was isolated from breast tissues and cells utilizing Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and detected by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). The Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgene, China) was utilized for qPCR according to the protocols recommended by manufactures. The PSMB1 and GAPDH primers were purchased from Azenta Life Science (Suzhou, China). The specific sequences were listed as follows:

PSMB1 forward: 5’-CCTCTACAGCCATGTATTCGGC-3’, reverse: 5’-CGTTGAAAACGTAGGGCGAAAAT-3’.

GAPDH forward: 5’- CTGGGCTACACTGAGCACC-3’, reverse: 5’- AAGTGGTCGTTGAGGGCAATG3’.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The breast cancer tissues and normal tissues were stored in formalin and encased in paraffin. A rotary microtome was used to cut the thick consecutive Sect. (4 μm). After removing paraffin, hydrating, retrieving antigen, and blocking endogenous peroxidase, a slide was then incubated with a specific primary antibody at 4℃ overnight, and subsequently treated with a HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Diaminobenzidine was added for color enhancement. The specimens were dyed with hematoxylin and observed with an Olympus inverted microscope imaging system. Two pathologists evaluated the expressions of PSMB1 without knowing which samples they belonged to. Scoring was performed based on the intensity of staining observed in positive tumor cells.

Western blot

The protein was extracted from breast cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) contained 1% PMSF (Solarbio, China). The proteins were separated by an SDS-PAGE gel following the instructions of epizyme (Shanghai, China) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). The separated proteins were treated with primary and secondary antibodies, and visualized by ECL reagent (Millipore, USA). The details of antibodies involved are as follows: PSMB1 (proteintech, 11749-1-AP), GAPDH (proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, 3195), N-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, 13116), β-catenin (proteintech, 66379-1-Ig), Vimentin (Cell Signaling Technology, 5741), snail (Cell Signaling Technology, 3879), AKT (Abclonal, A17909), p-AKT (Cell Signaling Technology, 4060), PI3K (Abclonal, A22730), p-PI3K (Cell Signaling Technology, 17366), mTOR (proteintech, 66888-1-Ig), p-mTOR (proteintech, 67778-1-Ig), cyclin-D1 (proteintech, 60186-1-Ig). The normalized data are presented as relative protein expression levels. Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, and the results are displayed as bar graphs in Figure S2.

MTT

Concerning the MTT assay, 3*103 cells were seeded into each well of 96-well plates. Throughout the same daily timeframe, cells were treated with MTT solution (Solarbio, China) from the start of day 0 to day 4. After incubating for 4 h, the cells were spun down and then exposed to 150 µl of a dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution purchased from Bocheng Chem (China). Following that, the microplate reader from Bio-Rad (US) was utilized to measure the absorbance at 570 nm.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates with 800 cells per well and incubated for approximately 2 weeks for colony formation assay. After the cell colonies reached the proper size, the cells were rinsed with PBS and then preserved using 4% polyformaldehyde from Solarbio in China. Crystal violet was utilized for the purpose of staining.

EdU assay

The EdU test was carried out following the manufacturer’s instructions using the EdU kit from RiboBio (China). In short, 4*104 cells were plated in 24-well plates and treated with EdU solution A. Following a 2-hour incubation, the cells were rinsed, fixed, and stained with apollo dye solution. The nuclear was dyed with Hochest 33,342 solution. A fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany) was utilized for image capturing.

Transwell assay

The Matrigel-coated Transwell from BD Biosciences, USA, was employed to measure breast cancer cells’ invasiveness. In summary, 40,000 cells were placed in the upper chambers with serum-free medium, and the lower chambers were filled with medium containing 20% FBS. Following an appropriate period of incubation, the migrating cells were fixed and stained using a three-step kit from Thermo Scientific in the USA. Next, the pictures were examined and tallied using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) at a 100× magnification.

Wound healing assay

In relation to the wound healing assay, the cells were grown in 6-well plates after transfection for 48 h until they were fully spread out. A new 10 µl pipette tip was utilized to make one scratch in each well. Afterwards, each cell was placed in a medium without FBS to avoid FBS affecting migration. The marks were captured with a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) at 0 h and 24 h. Distances were assessed and examined in reference to 0 h.

Cell cycle

The cells were disrupted, washed with PBS, and then stored in a 95% ethanol solution for analysis of cell cycle distribution. The cells were placed in a freezer at -20℃ for the entire night before being washed and moved to flow cytometry tubes. The cells were stained with a PI/RNaseA solution for 15 min at room temperature, followed by detection using a flow cytometer.

Statistical analysis

Prism v.8.0 (CA) performed the statistical analysis. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple group comparisons. All data was presented as average ± standard deviation. The statistical significance level was determined to be p < 0.05.

Results

PSMB1 was upregulated in breast cancer samples screened by CRISPR-Cas9

The CRISPR-CAS9 data was downloaded from the depmap database, which studied the effects of proliferation on more than 1,000 tumor cell lines by knocking out each gene individually across the genome. Among them, there are 25 breast cancer cell lines. This data is a matrix showing the cere score of each gene corresponding to different cell lines, and the lower the score, the greater the effect on the proliferation of the corresponding tumor cell line, that is, the more it promotes the proliferation of tumor cells. Finally, 697 genes were screened, and these genes were then co-analyzed with the Cox univariate analyzation, resulting in 78 intersected genes (Fig. 2A). PSMB1 was selected as our research object through ranking analysis of HR (Hazard Ratio) and statistical differences (Fig. 2B). Then, the expression profiles of PSMB1 were downloaded from TCGA-BRCA dataset, which results showed that PSMB1 was widely upregulated in breast cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2C). Specifically, with the development of breast cancer stage, the expression of PSMB1 also increased (Fig. 2D), the expression of PSMB1 in different breast cancer subtypes was also exhibited in Figure S1A.

PSMB1 was correlated with prognosis and major pathways in breast cancer

To further analyze the correlation between PSMB1 and the prognosis of breast cancer patients, the Kaplan-Meier plotter website (https://kmplot.com/analysis) was utilized, which results exhibited that higher expression of PSMB1 was associated with worse prognosis for OS (Overall Survival) (Fig. 3A), RFS (Recurrence Free Survival) (Fig. 3B), DMFS (Distant Metastasis-free Survival) (Fig. 3C) and PPS (Post-Progression Survival) (Fig. 3D). In addition, we have predicted the correlation between PSMB1 expression levels and overall survival (OS) across different molecular subtypes of breast cancer (Figure S1B). Then, we performed GO (Gene Ontology) analysis of PSMB1, including BP (Biological Process) (Fig. 3E), CC (Cellular Component) (Fig. 3F) and MF (Molecular Function) (Fig. 3G). The results indicated that PSMB1 was closely associated with tumor-related biological processes, such as cell cycle, mitotic nuclear division, especially breast carcinoma. The GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) was performed and revealed several pathways in tumor that PSMB1 involved in, including E2F Targets (Fig. 3H), G2M Checkpoint (Fig. 3I), Myc Targets (Fig. 3J), mTORC1 Signaling (Fig. 3K), PI3K AKT mTOR Signaling (Fig. 3L) and Oxidative Phosphorylation (Fig. 3M). These results indicated that PSMB1 might play an essential role in tumor.

PSMB1-related gene mutated pattern in breast cancer

Gene mutation is considered as one of the characteristics of tumor initiation. Thus, we identified the gene mutation in PSMB1 differential expressed tissues. Among all breast cancer tissues, there came 481 samples with PSMB1 high expression (Fig. 4A) and 466 samples with PSMB1 low expression (Fig. 4B). The analysis showed that the expression of PSMB1 was involved in the mutation of major genes in tumor progression, such as TP53, PIK3CA, TTN and other genes. Then we analyzed the focal somatic single nucleotide variant (SNV) and copy-number alteration (CNA) frequencies that are remarkably different between patients in PSMB1 high or low expressed samples, which indicated that genes, such as GOLGA4, FBXW7, MECOM and TP53 were mutated more in PSMB1-high groups. While GEMIN5, SEMA3A and CDH1 showed more mutations in PSMB1-low groups (Fig. 4C). Finally, the landscape of co-mutations of PSMB1-high group (Fig. 4D) and PSMB1-low group (Fig. 4E). These results showed that PSMB1 was involved in specific gene mutations in cancer progression.

Tumor microenvironment characteristics and potential therapy based on PSMB1

The immune microenvironment plays an important role in the background of tumor progression. Herein, we analyzed the correlation between PSMB1 and the immune microenvironment. As for immune cells, PSMB1 was negatively associated with plasmacytoid dendritic cells, mast cells, activated CD4 T cells and so on (Fig. 5A). As for immune checkpoints, PSMB1 was found to be negatively correlated with CD160, SDORA2A and BTNL2 (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that PSMB1 might be involved in immune microenvironment regulation. Besides, the molecular pathways identified in high PSMB1 group were analyzed for potential drugs, including mTOR inhibitor, CDK inhibitor and PI3K inhibitor (Fig. 5C). These inhibitors had been confirmed to be effective for multiple cancers, which might provide emerging drugs for breast cancer therapy.

PSMB1 was upregulated in breast cancer cells and tissues

To verify the expression pattern of PSMB1 in breast cancer, 30 paired breast cancer tissues and normal tissues were collected. The result of RT-qPCR analysis showed that compared with normal tissues, the mRNA expression level of PSMB1 was higher in breast cancer tissues (Fig. 6A). Be consistent with this, the result of immunohistochemistry also indicated that the protein expression of PSMB1 was significantly upregulated in breast cancer tissues (Fig. 6B). Then, we selected normal breast epithelial cells MCF-10 A and 5 breast cancer cell lines, including MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, CAL-51, SUM159 and SK-BR-3. The RT-qPCR showed that compared to MCF-10 A, PSMB1 was upregulated in all breast cancer cell lines, especially in MCF-7 and SK-BR-3 cells (Fig. 6C). This phenomenon was also verified by western blot (Fig. 6D). For further explore the function of PSMB1, the small interference RNA of PSMB1 was transfected into MCF-7 cells, which exhibited higher initial expression of PSMB1. The knock down efficiency was detected by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6E) and western blot (Fig. 6F). According to these results, si-PSMB1-1 and si-PSMB1-2 demonstrated high knock down efficiency and were selected for subsequent experiments.

Downregulation of PSMB1 inhibited cell proliferation, invasion and migration by PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling

Cell functional experiments were performed in vitro to identify the roles of PSMB1 in breast cancer cells. The MTT assay showed that downregulated PSMB1 resulted in a decrease of proliferation rate (Fig. 7A). The result of colony formation assay showed that the size and number of colonies were less in si-PSMB1 groups than control group (Fig. 7B). Consistent with this, as for the EdU assay, the proportion of EdU positive cells was also lower in si-PSMB1 groups (Fig. 7C). These findings indicated that the downregulation of PSMB1 inhibited the proliferation of breast cancer cells. Additionally, the result of transwell assay showed that si-PSMB1-1 and si-PSMB1-2 significantly decreased the number of invasive cells (Fig. 7D). While the wound healing assay indicated that the downregulation of PSMB1 inhibited the migration of breast cancer cells (Fig. 7E). Then, the flow cytometry was conducted to detect the cell cycle distribution of control groups and PSMB1 downregulated groups, which results showed that si-PSMB1 increased the proportion of cells in the G1 phase but reduced the proportion of cells in the S phase (Fig. 7F). The results of western blot showed that the downregulation of PSMB1 increased the expression of E-cadherin (epithelial marker) and β-catenin, while decreased the expression of N-cadherin, Vimentin and Snail (Fig. 7G), indicating its important roles in regulating EMT progress. In the previous analysis, we predicted that PSMB1 was involved in PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling. Herein, the results of western blot verified that si-PSMB1 downregulated the protein expression of PI3K, AKT, mTOR, as well as their phosphorylated proteins and target protein cyclin D1 (Fig. 7H). These findings indicated that the inhibition of si-PSMB1 on breast cancer cell malignant phenotype might be completed by regulating PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling.

Conclusion

This study screened PSMB1 that was upregulated in breast cancer, and further analysis revealed its participation in multiple tumor-related processes and pathways. Additionally, functional experiments verified its role in vitro and indicated its regulation on EMT process and PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling. These findings were meaningful to identify potential targets for breast cancer therapy.

Discussion

The CRISPR-Cas9 system has brought about a revolutionary change in gene editing, both at the level of single genes and in multiplexed loss-of-function screens, thereby facilitating the precise genome-scale identification of genes that are essential for the proliferation and survival of cancer cells [12–14]. In our study, we analyzed CRISPR-Cas9 related differentially expressed genes and intersected with Cox univariate analysis. PSMB1 was selected by HR and statistical differences, which showed a significant upregulation in breast cancer, especially high-grade breast cancer. An interesting observation in our study was that when investigating the relationship between PSMB1 expression and patient prognosis, we performed predictive survival analyses across breast cancer patients. The overall results indicated that the correlation between PSMB1 expression and prognosis appeared inconsistent across different molecular subtypes. However, upon comprehensive evaluation, no statistically significant associations were observed in other breast cancer subtypes, except in Luminal A breast cancer. This finding partly explains why we selected MCF-7 cells as the primary model for subsequent mechanistic investigations. This discrepancy may be attributed to tumor heterogeneity, though further evidence is still required. We aim to address this in future research. These analyses suggested that PSMB1 might be a potential oncogene, which also laid a foundation for our following research.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes Pathway (KEGG) enrichment analysis were commonly used tools for analyzing gene function and related signaling pathways, which indicated the critical roles of PSMB1 in tumor-related biological processes. Besides, PSMB1 was also found to be involved in gene mutation and immune microenvironment regulation, and the potential sensitive drugs were predicted at the same time. These findings showed the essential performance of PSMB1 in breast cancer development. Although, the function of PSMB1 in multiple tumors have been reported before, including renal cell carcinoma [15], myeloma [16], bladder carcinoma [17], lung adenocarcinoma [18] and hepatocellular carcinoma [19]. However, the studies of PSMB1 in breast cancer remain scanty. Herein, we identified PSMB1 to be upregulated in breast cancer, and verified its role of proliferation, invasion and migration in vitro, which filled the gap in this research direction and provided convincing evidence of PSMB1 as an oncogene in tumor progression.

The dysregulation of cell cycle and EMT process is considered as significant links for cancer initiation and progression [20–22]. The promotion of tumor cell cycle leads to uncontrolled proliferation and expansion [23, 24]. In our study, the cell cytometry was performed to detect the distribution of different phases in PSMB1- deleted breast cancer cells. The S phase, the phase of replication, is well-known exhibiting positive correlation with cell proliferation [25], was remarkably inhibited by PSMB1 downregulation, indicating proliferation inhibition role of PSMB1 in breast cancer. While the EMT process is important for embryogenesis and organ development, but in tumor progression, it allows benign tumors to develop into metastatic tumors, which can invade other tissues [26, 27]. EMT is characterized by the absence of epithelial cell markers, such as cytokeratins and E-cadherin, and upregulation of mesenchymal cell markers, such as N-cadherin, vimentin and snail [28]. Our study showed that downregulation of PSMB1 increased the protein expression levels of E-cadherin and β-catenin, but decreased the protein expression of N-cadherin, vimentin and snail, indicating the regulation of PSMB1 on EMT process. With the development of more and more anti-tumor drugs targeting the regulation of cell cycle or EMT process, PSMB1 is expected to be a potential target and provide meaningful strategies for breast cancer treatment.

The PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway is one of the pathways that have been extensively studied in tumor progression, which is reported to be a highly conserved signal transduction network in promoting cell survival, cell growth, and cell cycle progression. Polo-like kinase 4 was reported to mediate EMT process in neuroblastoma via PI3K/Akt signaling [29]. Besides, OCIAD2(Ovarian carcinoma immunoreactive antigen-like protein 2) was found to promote pancreatic cancer progression through the AKT signaling pathway [30]. In addition, Tian et al. also reported that MIR497HG could mediate tamoxifen resistance by regulating PI3K-AKT signaling in breast cancer [31]. Therefore, many drugs targeting PI3K-AKT-mTOR have also been developed for tumor therapy [32–34]. In our research, GO analysis and GSEA were performed to predict PSMB1 related biological processes and pathways, which resulted in PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling enrichment. In vitro experiments, western blot was conducted to verify the regulation of PSMB1 on PI3K-AKT-mTOR related proteins. These findings indicated that PSMB1 might serve as an oncogene by regulating PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling. However, our study has great limitations, the relevant mechanisms still need to be further explored. But we are pleased to provide some new perspectives and ideas for future research.
