Domain validation of the CRASH prognostic model for predicting 14-day mortality among patients with traumatic brain injury with intracranial hemorrhage in a Thai emergency department
Authors: Welawat Tienpratarn, Phichayut Phinyo, Chaiyaporn Yuksen, Sirote Wongwaisayawan, Jiraporn Khorana, Jayanton Patumanond
Published: 2025

Background

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major health concern, contributing significantly to both mortality and long-term disability [1, 2]. Accurate prognostic tools are essential for guiding clinical decisions. Among these tools, the Corticosteroid Randomization After Significant Head Injury (CRASH) prediction model is widely acknowledged for its effectiveness in predicting 14-day mortality and long-term outcomes in TBI patients [1].

The CRASH prediction tool consists of two models designed to estimate outcomes in TBI patients. The Basic model uses clinical factors, while the CT Model enhances this by including computed tomography (CT) findings, such as intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), a common complication after injury. In the CT model, ICH included petechial hemorrhage, basal cistern, subarachnoid bleed, and non-evacuated hematoma, and mass effect in the CT model included obliteration of the third ventricle or basal cistern and midline shift, which are independent factors associated with 14-day mortality [1]. While the CRASH model has demonstrated strong discriminatory performance across various settings, its calibration and accuracy often vary due to differences in patient populations and healthcare environments [3–11].

Previous external validation studies of the CRASH model have primarily focused on severe TBI cohorts, where accurate prognostication is particularly critical. Han et al. validated the CRASH model specifically in severe TBI cases, highlighting its clinical relevance for high-risk groups [5]. In contrast, Dullaert et al. expanded the scope by including patients with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15, emphasizing the importance of assessing the model across the full spectrum of TBI severity [9]. This broader use of GCS helps mitigate potential biases, such as overestimating mortality outcomes. However, previous external validation studies have not specifically focused on patients with ICH despite its significance in unique pathophysiological mechanisms, a distinct risk of secondary brain injury [12], and influencing the prognosis with a higher mortality rate [1, 2]. This, more specifically, minimizes selection bias related to the inclusion of C-negative results, ensuring a more precise evaluation of prognostic factors relevant to this ICH subgroup.

This study aimed to conduct a domain validation of the CRASH prediction model in a cohort of TBI patients with ICH presenting to a Thai emergency department (ED), assessing whether the model maintains its discriminative performance and calibration in a population distinct from the original development cohort. The findings are also intended to support the refinement of predictions and to inform clinical decision-making in resource-limited settings.

Methods

This study was a retrospective observational cohort study conducted to validate the CRASH prediction model for TBI patients. Data were collected from TBI patients who visited the ED at Ramathibodi Hospital, a Level 2 trauma center in Bangkok, Thailand, between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022. Eligible participants included adult trauma patients aged 15 years or older with positive findings of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) on CT scans, including epidural hemorrhage, subdural hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraparenchymal hemorrhage, and intraventricular hemorrhage. Patients were excluded if they lacked CT scan results or had incomplete outcome data, including mortality records, within 14 days.

The CRASH prediction model

The CRASH prediction model [1] was developed using data from the CRASH trial [13], which included 10,008 adult TBI patients with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 14 or less who sustained injuries within 8 hours of enrollment. The model predicts 14-day mortality and unfavorable outcomes at 6 months. It was designed for use in two distinct settings: high-income countries and low- and middle-income countries.

The model is available in two forms: the Basic model and the CT model. The Basic model incorporates four key predictors: age, GCS score, pupillary reaction, and the presence of major extracranial injuries. The CT model expands on the Basic model by including findings from the initial post-injury CT scan. The CT criteria considered are petechial hemorrhages, obliteration of the third ventricle or basal cisterns, subarachnoid hemorrhage, midline shift, and non-evacuated hematoma.

Data collection

The data required to calculate the CRASH model and the outcome data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records and official imaging reports provided by emergency radiologists.

The CRASH model was calculated using a freely accessible online tool, the CRASH Risk Calculator (URL: https://crash2.lshtm.ac.uk/Risk%20calculator/index.html) [1].

Data collection, including patient characteristics, CT findings, and outcomes, was performed by one observer. CRASH score calculations were performed independently by another observer who used only predictor variables and was blinded to the outcome data to minimize bias.

Predictors in the CRASH model

Predictors for the CRASH model included variables from both the Basic and CT models:

Basic model: Predictors included age (16 years and older), GCS score (3–14), pupillary reaction (both reactive, one reactive, or non-reactive), and the presence of major extracranial injuries at initial ED visit. Major extracranial injuries were defined as any injuries requiring hospital admission.

Basic model: Predictors included age (16 years and older), GCS score (3–14), pupillary reaction (both reactive, one reactive, or non-reactive), and the presence of major extracranial injuries at initial ED visit. Major extracranial injuries were defined as any injuries requiring hospital admission.

CT model: Additional predictors from CT findings included petechial hemorrhages, obliteration of the third ventricle or basal cisterns, subarachnoid hemorrhage, midline shift, and non-evacuated hematomas at initial ED visit.

CT model: Additional predictors from CT findings included petechial hemorrhages, obliteration of the third ventricle or basal cisterns, subarachnoid hemorrhage, midline shift, and non-evacuated hematomas at initial ED visit.

For the CRASH model calculation, we selected Thailand as the country and used the prediction time corresponding to the initial ED visit. In cases where the initial GCS score was 15, we recoded it to 14. This adjustment was necessary because the original CRASH model does not allow input of a GCS score of 15. This pragmatic adjustment allowed consistent application of the model without excluding mild TBI patients. We followed the method used by Dullaert et al. [9], where GCS 15 was considered as 14 for model input purposes. The probability of mortality, calculated using the CRASH head injury prognosis calculator, was displayed as a percentage along with the 95% confidence interval. A screenshot of the CRASH calculator website is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Outcome

The primary outcome was 14-day mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring within 14 days of the initial injury. Mortality data were verified through multiple sources, including medical records from the ED visit, ward or intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, and follow-up records for discharged patients.

Sample size estimation

Based on the CRASH development study and our pilot data from October to December 2022, the sample size was estimated to ensure sufficient reliability for external validation [14]. For calibration, a mortality proportion of 0.11 from our pilot data and a confidence interval width of 0.1 required approximately 151 patients. For discrimination, using a C-statistic of 0.84 from the development dataset with the same confidence interval width, the required sample size was 207 patients. To ensure both calibration and discrimination metrics were sufficiently reliable, the larger sample size of 207 patients was chosen as the minimum requirement.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) with a two-tailed significance level of P < 0.05. A complete case analysis was used without imputation. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Patient characteristics between validation and development datasets with unequal sample size were compared using standardized difference (STD) values, with values >0.1 indicating significant differences [15].

The predictive performance of the CRASH prediction model was evaluated through discrimination, calibration, and predictive accuracy. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AuROC) curve, where an AuROC of 0.5 indicates a non-discriminatory model and an AuROC ≥ 0.8 reflects adequate discriminative ability. Calibration was evaluated using calibration plots and statistical metrics to assess the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. Logistic recalibration methods were applied to adjust the intercept and slope [16]. Calibration plots illustrated deviations from ideal agreement by comparing predicted versus observed outcomes. Calibration-in-the-large (CITL) was assessed by fitting a logistic regression model with model predictions as an offset variable. The intercept, ideally 0, indicates systematic overestimation or underestimation, with negative values suggesting better-than-predicted outcomes. The calibration slope, ideally 1, reflects the strength of the predictors compared to the original development dataset [15]. Predictive accuracy was assessed by applying a cutoff value from previous external validation research [9] to split patient scores into two groups. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated using a 2 × 2 table.

Results

From 2020 to 2022, a total of 2,203 adult trauma patients aged 15 years or older underwent CT brain or whole-body trauma CT scans in the ED. Of these, 1,968 patients without intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) on CT and 3 patients with ICH who were referred to another hospital, making follow-up on 14-day mortality impossible, were excluded. The remaining 232 patients who met the study criteria were included in the CRASH prediction model validation dataset. The study flow is illustrated in Supplementary Figure 2, and a comparison of eligibility criteria between the validation and development datasets is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Patient characteristics of validation and development datasets

The patient characteristics of the validation dataset from Ramathibodi Hospital (N = 232) and the development dataset from the original CRASH model study conducted in a low- and middle-income country setting (N = 7,526) are summarized in Table 1. The validation dataset exhibits significant demographic and clinical differences compared to the development dataset, particularly in age distribution and CT findings. Patients in the validation dataset were predominantly older, with 75% aged 55 or above, compared to only 14.5% in the development cohort (STD = 1.940). Conversely, younger patients under 20 years were underrepresented in the validation dataset (2.2%) compared to 12.5% in the development dataset (STD = 1.560).

Clinical characteristics, including CT findings and 14-day mortality outcomes, also differed between the datasets. Non-evacuated hematomas were significantly more common in the validation dataset (62.1% vs. 27.3%; STD = 0.746), while petechial hemorrhages were less frequent (15.1% vs. 28.7%; STD = 0.334). Moreover, the 14-day mortality rate was lower in the validation dataset (9.1%) compared to the development dataset (20.7%; STD = 0.332). These results are presented descriptively to show differences in case-mix between cohorts.

Performance of the basic model

The Basic model demonstrated outstanding discriminative performance in the validation dataset, with an AuROC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.84–1.00), effectively distinguishing between survivors and non-survivors within 14 days. However, overestimation of mortality in high-risk groups was observed, as indicated by a CITL of -0.916 (95% CI: -1.420, -0.413) and a calibration slope of 1.69 (95% CI: 1.14–2.24). Calibration plots highlighted discrepancies between predicted and observed outcomes, with detailed metrics and plots provided in Table 2 and Fig. 1A. At a threshold score of 31.5, the Basic model achieved high sensitivity (81.0%) and specificity (93.8%), with a positive predictive value (PPV) of 56.7% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 98.0% (Table 3). Recalibration improved predictive accuracy, particularly for high-risk groups, by reducing overestimation and better aligning predictions with observed outcomes. These improvements are illustrated in Fig. 2A and B, and the final recalibrated equations are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Performance of CT model

The CT model also demonstrated outstanding discriminative performance in the validation dataset, with an AuROC of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.86–0.99), slightly higher than the Basic model with no statistical difference (P-value=0.679). Overestimation of mortality in high-risk groups was indicated by a CITL of -1.257 (95% CI: -1.751, -0.763) and a calibration slope of 1.86 (95% CI: 1.25–2.47), necessitating recalibration. Calibration plots showed discrepancies between predicted and observed outcomes, with detailed metrics and plots provided in Table 2 and Fig. 2B. At a threshold score of 31.5, the CT model achieved a sensitivity of 85.7%, specificity of 90.5%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 47.4%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 98.5%, indicating high sensitivity and specificity compared to the Basic model (Table 3). Recalibration reduced overestimation and improved alignment with observed outcomes, particularly in high-risk groups. These changes are shown in Fig. 2C and D, and the recalibrated equations for the CT model are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

To facilitate clinical application, we developed a web-based calculator to compute recalibrated probabilities using the original CRASH Basic and CT models (https://bit.ly/CRASHRecal).

Discussion

This study externally validated the CRASH prediction model for 14-day mortality in TBI patients with ICH within a Thai emergency department setting—a population that differs significantly from the original development cohort, as reflected in distinct patient characteristics and outcome prevalence. Our findings confirm the strong discriminative performance of both the Basic and CT models. These results directly address the research question, supporting the model’s generalizability to a distinct clinical subgroup and affirming its potential utility in identifying high-risk patients in resource-limited settings. However, calibration metrics indicated a systematic overestimation of mortality, suggesting that recalibration of both the intercept and slope is necessary before the model can be implemented in local clinical practice.

Our eligibility criteria included patients regardless of timing, reflecting real-world delays in care, unlike the CRASH study [1], which included only those within 8 hours of injury. While CRASH included patients with GCS scores ≤ 14, our study extended the eligible criteria to GCS scores of 15 and focused only on those with positive ICH, whereas CRASH included patients with or without CT findings. By focusing on Thailand, our study aligns with CRASH’s low- and middle-income group, emphasizing applicability in resource-limited settings. Compared to findings from other external validations, such as those conducted in Hong Kong, Iran, and Belgium [3, 7, 9], this study further confirms the robust discrimination of the CRASH model across diverse healthcare settings.

However, calibration metrics in this study indicated a significant overestimation of mortality risk, as evidenced by a negative calibration-in-the-large. These results suggest that the models systematically predict higher risks of 14-day mortality than observed, particularly in higher-risk groups. Such overestimation has been previously reported in validation studies from Spain and Austria​ [6, 11]. The calibration slope for the Basic model further highlights disproportionate predictions across the risk spectrum. This finding reflects demographic disparities, with an older patient population (75% aged ≥ 55 years) and a predominance of mild TBI cases (78% with GCS 13–15), which contrasts with the original CRASH dataset’s more balanced GCS. Additionally, the inclusion of a higher prevalence of specific CT findings, such as subarachnoid hemorrhage (48.7%) and non-evacuated hematomas (62.1%), compared to the original dataset, underscores the clinical differences in this subgroup. The recalibration process effectively reduced this overestimation, aligning predicted outcomes with observed mortality rates. These adjustments highlight the necessity of recalibrating prediction models tailored to specific population characteristics of TBI with ICH, ensuring the model’s relevance in Thailand and other countries with similar healthcare settings.

Despite these calibration challenges, both models exhibited high sensitivity and specificity at a score threshold of 31.5. This threshold, initially proposed by Dullaert et al., was based on their external validation of the CRASH model in an ED setting that included GCS scores of 15. It demonstrated effective discrimination and practical utility in identifying high-risk patients [9]. Incorporating this threshold in the present study ensures comparability with previous research and supports its clinical applicability across different populations. Even in TBI patients with ICH, the performance of the basic model and CT model demonstrated no significant differences. Therefore, in resource-limited settings in Thailand, where CT scans may not be readily available in some hospitals, the basic model could serve as an alternative to the CT model for predicting 14-day mortality. This point aligns with Hashemi et al. [7], who suggested the Basic model as a reliable alternative to the CT Model in settings with limited access to CT scans.

The strengths of this study include its focus on a well-defined cohort and the successful recalibration of the CRASH models for local use. However, this study has several limitations. Our cohort differed from the original CRASH population, with older patients, the inclusion of GCS 15, and varying CT patterns, reducing comparability. The inclusion and pragmatic recoding of GCS 15 represent a methodological compromise that may introduce misclassification, though its impact is likely limited given the predominantly mild TBI. Mortality differences are presented descriptively only, as external validation aims to test generalisability rather than repeatability. Additional limitations include potential system-level factors, the single-centre design, modest sample size, and the inability to assess calibration across GCS strata, all of which may affect generalisability and risk overfitting.

Future research should explore dynamic models incorporating real-time clinical data or artificial intelligence (AI) into the CRASH model, which could enhance its predictive power [17] and assess the recalibrated model’s performance across broader, multi-center cohorts to confirm its stability.

Conclusions

The original CRASH prediction model demonstrated strong discriminative performance in predicting 14-day mortality among TBI patients with ICH; however, miscalibration was observed. Recalibration of the intercept and slope helped to correct this miscalibration, enhancing predictive accuracy in the local context and supporting its potential applicability in Thai emergency departments. Nonetheless, further studies are required to confirm the stability and generalisability of the recalibrated models.
