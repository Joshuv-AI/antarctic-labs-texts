Fair-efficient allocation mechanism with meta-types resources in cloud computing
Authors: Fengyue Zhang, Chunlei Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fair and efficient resource allocation is a fundamental goal of cloud computing systems. However, diverse user requirements and heterogeneous resource types make it difficult to balance utilization efficiency and user-perceived fairness. To address this challenge, we propose a meta-type-based resource allocation mechanism, GAF-MT, which is based on the principle of asset fairness. GAF-MT introduces meta-types to model structured resource groupings and supports user-specific requirements while reducing fragmentation. We design a scheduling algorithm to find feasible solutions and implement GAF-MT based on GUROBI. Extensive experiments in small-scale and large-scale user environments show that GAF-MT not only ensures fairness, but also significantly improves resource utilization and maintains high performance even under high user loads.

Introduction

Cloud computing is a mode of providing services to users by integrating various resources together through a network1–4. The advantage of cloud computing is that it can provide resources of variable scale according to the actual needs of users, and cloud computing systems are distinguished by unprecedented heterogeneity of servers and workloads5–7, which brings convenience to users and also puts forward higher requirements for resource allocation techniques, making resource allocation a hot and difficult issue in cloud computing research.

Resource allocation is a key component in any shared computer system. At any given time, there are many users running their tasks on the cluster, and the tasks submitted by users often have heterogeneous demands on resources8–11. In addition to the heterogeneity of user demands, resource allocation under the concepts of fairness and efficiency is also a fundamental issue in the design of cloud computing systems. Under-allocation of resources can reduce the QoE of users, and over-allocation can adversely affect other users in a shared cloud computing environment12–16.

Currently in multi-resource allocation, DRF proposed by Ghodsi et al. is one of the most commonly used fair allocation strategies, which is achieved by maximizing the minimum global dominant share among all users17. Later, considering the heterogeneity of servers, Wang et al. described DRFH, a dominant resource fair allocation strategy in heterogeneous servers. In addition to fair allocation of multiple resources, there have also been many works that focus extensively on the trade-off between fairness and efficiency. Tang et al. proposed a new fair-efficient scheduler, QKnober, for cloud computing systems, which, unlike previous schedulers, allows users to flexibly balance fairness and efficiency18–20. Steven Yin et al. proposed the DRF-MT allocation mechanism in 2021, which proposed different types of GPUs/CPUs under different nodes to accept user-specific demands, but it does not consider the trade-off between fairness and efficiency21. We consider another multi-resource fairness strategy, asset fairness (AF). AF achieves fairness by equalizing the aggregation results of various types of resources allocated to each user.

This paper proposes a new resource allocation mechanism, GAF-MT (Generalized Asset Fairness with Meta-types), to address fairness and efficiency issues in multi-resource cloud computing environments. The mechanism considers scenarios with multiple meta-type resources and multiple users, each of whom undertakes multiple computing tasks and has heterogeneous resource requirements. To capture user-specific preferences and achieve efficient modeling, we introduce a price attribute for each meta-type and transform the resource allocation problem into a linear programming problem subject to both capacity and asset fairness constraints.

Related work

We review the literature work closely related to this paper from the following two aspects:

Multi-User Multi-Resource Fair Allocation: max-min fairness was the original allocation method used for resource allocation, and later on, max-min fairness was generalized to include the concept of weights, taking into account the fact that users have different priorities among themselves17–20. Currently in multi-resource allocation, DRF built by Ghodsi et al. is one of the most commonly used fair allocation strategies, which is based on the principle of maximizing the smallest dominant share among all users17. After this, there have been many extensions and generalizations of DRF. Wan et al.22 proposed hierarchical scheduling model combined with improved firefly genetic algorithm to optimize multi-composite task allocation for cloud manufacturing. Zhou et al.23 proposed a joint resource allocation method for short packet computation and transmission in URLLRAN. Zhou et al.24 proposed a resilient dynamic resource allocation model based on an artificial fish swarm algorithm to achieve better emergency response at lower cost. Li et al.25 proposed TapFinger scheduler to optimize task placement and resource allocation through multi-intelligence reinforcement learning. Later, Wang et al.17 proposed DRFH, a strategy for fair allocation of dominant resources in heterogeneous servers, which jointly considers resource allocation across all servers and defines a user’s dominant resource based on the sum of all the resources. Lin et al.26 proposed a single-layer and multi-layer dominated max-min fair allocation scheme. Kurokawa et al.27 studied the problem of fair distribution of indivisible items and proposed a max-min share guarantee. Tang et al.28 proposed FairCache policy to ensure fairness and resource efficiency for long-term multi-users in on-demand caching systems. Li et al.29 proposed a Cooperative Differential Learning based Particle Swarm Optimization algorithm. Long et al.30 proposed a particle swarm optimization algorithm based on collaborative difference learning to improve convergence accuracy. Li et al.31 generalized DRF in the problem of fair allocation of multiple resources with a limited number of tasks and proposed a dictionary-ordered maximum-minimum-normalized sharing (LMMNS) fairness allocation mechanism. In order to solve the problem of users with specific needs, Steven Yin et al.21 proposed the concept of “meta-types” to refine resources hierarchically to satisfy users’ specific needs. In summary, all the above works are centered around 100% fairness, while this paper integrates the consideration between fairness and efficiency, which can ensure asset fairness and maximize resource utilization at the same time.

Fairness-Efficiency Tradeoff Allocation: there is a tradeoff between fairness and efficiency in multi-resource allocation, and there have also been many research works that focus extensively on the tradeoff between fairness and efficiency8,32–35. Panayiotou et al.36 proposed a multi-objective optimization function to achieve the fairest possible bandwidth allocation. Tang et al.37 proposed a new strategy called ElasticSEM for semi-external memory by proposing a new strategy called ElasticSEM, which allows users to flexibly adjust and balance fairness and efficiency. Furthermore, Tang et al.23 provided a new fair-efficient scheduler, QKnober, for cloud computing systems, which allows users to flexibly balance fairness and efficiency. Zhang et al.38 described a GAF allocation strategy and designed a scheduling algorithm to find a feasible solution. Ma et al.39 studied the problem of sequential resource allocation for balancing fairness and efficiency in nonprofit operations and proposed an optimal allocation strategy based on two thresholds. Meskar et al.40 proposed a task-sharing fairness mechanism TSF-ER based on the Kalai-Smorodinsky negotiation solution, which achieves a fair allocation of multiple types of resources in a MEC environment. However, the above strategies do not satisfy the special needs presented by users. For this reason, this paper proposes a new resource allocation mechanism, GAF-MT, where resources in the system are organized in meta-types, and each meta-type contains one or more subtype resources, allowing users to specify the use of a subset of each meta-type to accomplish a task.

The GAF-MT mechanism allows to maximize resource utilization while guaranteeing user asset fairness, solves the problem of distributing multiple types of resources to users with heterogeneous needs in a fair and efficient manner, and can meet user-specific needs as an extension of asset fairness.

Generated asset fairness with meta-types mechanism

In this section, we first describe the problem of equitable allocation of multiple resources with meta-types in cloud computing systems. We then formalize some properties that are important in the distribution mechanism of a cloud computing environment.

Basic settings

We divide resources into meta-types (such as CPU, memory, etc.), and each meta-type (such as CPU) contains different sub-types of resources (such as Intel CPUs, AMD CPUs, SAMSUNG CPUs, etc.). This hierarchical classification reflects the heterogeneous nature of cloud computing environments, where users may require specific brands, performance levels, or compatibility characteristics of a resource. By organizing resources into meta-types and sub-types, we enable the allocation system to better accommodate user-specific requirements while maintaining flexibility. This structure not only avoids resource mismatches and waste, but also supports fine-grained substitution between acceptable sub-types. As a result, it improves overall resource utilization and allows the fairness mechanism (based on asset fairness) to more accurately reflect user consumption, as users are only charged for resources they can actually use. This normalized form reflects the user’s relative demand intensity on each meta-type resource. We define the dominant meta-type of user i as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_{i}^{*}$$\end{document}, in other words, in the entire system, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_{i}^{*}$$\end{document}is the meta-type that requires the most share per task performed by user i. It effectively identifies the most critical resource for user i, and may vary across users. Then it can be obtained from (2):3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_{i}^{*} \in \mathop {\arg \hbox{max} }\limits_{{l \in [1,L]}} \{ \frac{{{D_{il}}}}{{\sum\limits_{{r \in {R_l}}} {{C_r}} }}\} \in \mathop {\arg \hbox{max} }\limits_{{l \in [1,L]}} {d_{il}}.$$\end{document}

Then \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d_{il_{i}^{*}}}$$\end{document} represents the share of dominant meta-types required by user i to complete one unit of the task, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i})$$\end{document} represents the number of tasks that user i can complete under the given allocation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}$$\end{document}. We use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s_i}$$\end{document} to represent the share of the dominant meta-type allocated to user i under the allocation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}$$\end{document}, which is mathematically expressed as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s_i}={N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i}) \times {d_{il_{i}^{*}}}.$$\end{document}

This expression links resource allocation to effective task completion, highlighting how the dominant meta-type constrains user throughput.

Resource allocation

The allocation vector for user i is represented by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}={({A_{i1}},\ldots ,{A_{im}})^T}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${A_{ir}}(i \in U,r \in [1,m])$$\end{document} represents the share of resource r allocated to user i. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varvec{A}}=({{\varvec{A}}_1},\ldots ,{{\varvec{A}}_n})$$\end{document} is the overall allocation of all users in the system.

Given the allocation matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varvec{A}}=({{\varvec{A}}_1},\ldots ,{{\varvec{A}}_n})$$\end{document} for all users, if for any resource in the system, the allocation of the resource by all users does not exceed the total amount of the system, that is, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum\nolimits_{{i \in U,r \in g_{l}^{i}}} {{A_{ir}}} \leqslant {c_r}{\text{ }}\forall l \in [1,L],r \in {\mathbb{R}}$$\end{document}, then we call the allocation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varvec{A}}$$\end{document} feasible allocation. In the case based on the assignment vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}$$\end{document}, we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i})$$\end{document} to represent the maximum number of tasks (possibly scores) that user i can perform. We have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i}) \times {d_{il}} \leqslant \sum\nolimits_{{r \in g_{l}^{i}}} {{A_{ir}}} {\text{ }}l \in [1,L]$$\end{document}, and in fact, the number of tasks that user i can perform can be expressed as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i})=\mathop {\hbox{min} }\limits_{{g_{l}^{i} \in {G_i}}} \left\{ {\frac{{\sum\nolimits_{{r \in g_{l}^{i}}} {{A_{ir}}} }}{{{d_{il}}}}} \right\}.$$\end{document}

This reflects a classic bottleneck structure: a user’s throughput is determined by the meta-type with the least proportional allocation. Intuitively, users prefer resource allocation that performs more tasks.

A reasonable allocation of resources to the user should not exceed the amount of resources actually used in the server, we call this allocation is non-waste allocation:

We use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}$$\end{document} to represent the allocation of user i, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i})$$\end{document} to represent the number of tasks that user i can perform, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{d}}_i}$$\end{document} to represent the demand vector of user i. Allocating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}$$\end{document} is called a non-wasteful allocation if and only if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i}={N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i}) \times {{\varvec{d}}_i}$$\end{document}. Equation (6) ensures non-wasteful allocation: the allocated resources should match the exact amount needed for completing the tasks. If any unused portion exists, it can be revoked without affecting user performance.

By grouping heterogeneous sub-type resources into meta-types, the allocation system enables fine-grained substitution within each meta-type: sub-types that are unusable for some users can still be allocated to others who accept them. This mechanism mitigates resource fragmentation because partially stranded sub-types are reallocated instead of remaining idle. As a consequence, residual fragments are systematically reduced, and overall capacity utilization is improved compared with schemes that treat each sub-type independently. The revised theoretical framework therefore provides a rigorous justification that fragmentation reduction is not only intuitive but also directly supported by the allocation equations.

System model

In this section, we model a multi-resource allocation problem with meta-types in cloud computing and then propose a scheduling policy to apply to this setting.

Alternative resources

We now propose a resource allocation mechanism based on generalized asset fairness to deal with the case of meta-typed resources, in which each meta-typed resource l has its own price \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p_l}$$\end{document}, and call this mechanism GAF-MT (Generated Asset Fairness with Meta-Types). In order to ensure that users can allocate the resources they need as much as possible, the specific allocation for each user is determined after the allocation is completed.

GAF-MT mechanism

The DRF described in the previous section is an allocation model that achieves 100% fairness by guaranteeing that the weighted dominant resource shares are the same among users. This means that each user’s dominant share is proportionally scaled by their weight, ensuring fairness across heterogeneous demands. It can be used with the following formula.7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{{s_i}}}{{{\omega _i}}}=\frac{{{s_j}}}{{{\omega _j}}}.$$\end{document}

By modifying the existing model for cloud computing resource allocation environments where type l has price \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p_l}$$\end{document} per unit dollar, we proposed a resource allocation strategy based on generated asset fairness.

The basic idea is as follows: instead of pursuing equal dominant resource shares among users as in DRF, we use an alternative fairness strategy from the literature called asset fairness AF, which achieves fairness by equalizing the aggregation results of various types of resources allocated to each user. In GAF-MT, asset fairness means that every user spends an equal total value across all allocated resources, considering both price and share. This is captured by:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum\nolimits_{{l=1}}^{L} {{p_l}{d_{1l}}{A_{1l}}} =\sum\nolimits_{{l=1}}^{L} {{p_l}{d_{2l}}{A_{2l}}} = \cdots =\sum\nolimits_{{l=1}}^{L} {{p_l}{d_{nl}}{A_{nl}}} .$$\end{document}

Equation (8) ensures that all users pay the same total cost, regardless of their resource preferences. This asset fairness constraint implies that, under GAF-MT, users are treated equally in terms of economic expenditure.

Different from AF, GAF-MT maximizes the resource utilization, namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox{max} \eta$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta ={\hbox{min} _{{{\varvec{R}}_l}}}\sum\nolimits_{{r \in {{\varvec{R}}_l},l \in [L]}} {\sum\nolimits_{{i=1}}^{n} {{d_{ir}}{N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i})} }$$\end{document}, under the premise of ensuring the fairness of user assets. According to GAF’s allocation strategy, the meta-type-based resource allocation is modeled as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} \hbox{max} \,\,\,\quad \quad \eta \hfill \\ s.t.\quad \quad \quad \sum\nolimits_{{i=1}}^{n} {{A_{ir}}} \leqslant {c_r},\forall r \in {\mathbb{R}} \hfill \\ \quad \quad \quad \quad \sum\nolimits_{{l=1}}^{L} {{p_l}{d_{1l}}{N_1}({{\varvec{A}}_1})} =\sum\nolimits_{{l=1}}^{L} {{p_l}{d_{2l}}{N_2}({{\varvec{A}}_2})} =\cdots =\sum\nolimits_{{l=1}}^{L} {{p_l}{d_{nl}}{N_n}({{\varvec{A}}_n})} \hfill \\ \quad \quad \quad \quad \sum\nolimits_{{i=1}}^{n} {{d_{il}}{N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i})} \geqslant \eta ,\forall l \in [1,L] \hfill \\ \quad \quad \quad \quad \sum\nolimits_{{l=1}}^{L} {{p_l}>0} \hfill \\ \quad \quad \quad \quad {A_{ir}} \geqslant 0,\forall i \in U,r \in {\mathbb{R}} \hfill \\ \quad \quad \quad \quad {p_l} \geqslant 0,\forall l \in [1,L] \hfill \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

Equation (9) formulates the overall resource allocation problem as a linear program. The goal is to maximize the minimum utilization across all meta-type resources, while satisfying constraints on capacity, fairness, and non-negativity. The purpose of allocation is to maximize the minimum meta-type resource utilization in the system under the constraint of resource capacity and asset equity. The objective function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} represents the lowest utilization rate of meta-type resources. The first constraint is to ensure that the amount of resources allocated does not exceed its capacity. The second constraint is to ensure the equity of assets among users, that is, to ensure that the total cost of each user is equal. The third constraint is to ensure maximum resource utilization, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} has a maximum value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hbox{min} _{{{\varvec{R}}_l}}}\sum\nolimits_{{r \in {{\varvec{R}}_l},l \in [L]}} {\sum\nolimits_{{i=1}}^{n} {{d_{ir}}{N_i}({{\varvec{A}}_i})} }$$\end{document}. The fourth constraint guarantees that at least one meta-typed resource has a non-zero price, that is, there is a meta-typed resource l that makes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p_l}>0$$\end{document}. The fifth constraint means that the allocation of all resources by all users is non-negative. The last constraint means that the price of all meta-type resources is non-negative. With the increase of distribution, the objective function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} of linear programming (9) is non-decreasing, that is, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{1}^{*} \leqslant \eta _{2}^{*} \leqslant \cdots$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{t}^{*}$$\end{document} is the optimal solution at round t.

While prior mechanisms such as DRF and DRF-MT aim to equalize dominant shares across users, they do not explicitly enforce price-sensitive fairness in hierarchically structured (meta-type) resource settings. The novelty of GAF-MT is to extend asset fairness into the meta-type context by embedding asset-equality constraints (price × allocated share) into the allocation model, allowing fine-grained substitution among subtypes while treating heterogeneous user requirements equitably.

The fairness guarantee in GAF-MT follows directly from the asset-fairness equality (Eq. (8)), which enforces that each user’s total expenditure (sum over meta-types of price × allocated share) is identical. The efficiency gain is established by the objective in Eq. (9), which maximizes the minimum meta-type utilization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} across the system. Because formulation (9) is a linear program over a convex feasible region defined by capacity and asset-equality constraints, a global optimum \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{t}^{*}$$\end{document} exists and is found efficiently by GUROBI. At \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _{t}^{*}$$\end{document} any further increase of the minimum meta-type utilization would violate capacity or the asset-equality constraints; therefore the obtained solution simultaneously satisfies the enforced fairness condition and attains maximal worst-case utilization. In short, fairness and efficiency are enforced by the model formulation itself (Eqs. (8) and (9)).

Scheduling algorithm

The pseudocode of GAF-MT assignment is given in Algorithm 1. At the beginning of the GAF-MT allocation algorithm, the first check is whether the allocated resource share exceeds the total amount of resources in the system. If the share of resources allocated does not exceed the total amount of resources, then the minimum resource utilization in the system is maximized while ensuring that the capacity and asset equity constraints are held. After the allocation is complete, the allocation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{A}}_i},{\text{ }}i \in U$$\end{document} for each user can be obtained. According to the allocation vector for each user, the number of tasks that can be executed by each user and the utilization of resources of each meta-type can be obtained.

In order to ensure that users are allocated as many resources as possible to meet their needs when the algorithm is executed, the specific allocation of each user can only be determined after the allocation is complete. If the current resource type or resource capacity cannot increase the number of tasks that can be executed by the user, the number of tasks that can be executed by the user is determined, although the specific allocation of the user is uncertain. The scheduling algorithm operationalizes the mathematical model by iteratively solving the linear program defined in Eq. (9). This ensures that the theoretical fairness and efficiency guarantees are preserved during practical allocation.

Results and analysis

In this section, we implement the GAF-MT method using GUROBI41, and compare its performance with three baseline methods: DRF17, AF38, and DRF-MT21—an extension of DRF that supports meta-type-aware allocation by incorporating dominant resource shares over user-specific meta-type requirements.

Experimental setup

In the experiment, we fixed a meta-type structure and randomly generated user requirements, group structure, demand vector, and the price of each meta-type resource. The GAF-MT allocation model described in Eq. (9) was implemented using the Python interface of the GUROBI optimizer (GUROBI Python interface). The model was constructed by invoking the Model.addVar(), Model.addConstr(), and Model.setObjective() functions to define variables, constraints, and the objective function, respectively. The default LP solver provided by GUROBI was used unless otherwise specified. For the number of choices for each user, we conducted 5 experiments, taking the average as the experimental result. All algorithmic assignments for AF, DRF, DRF-MT, and GAF-MT are rounded down. For each user, the size between 0 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| {{{\varvec{R}}_l}} \right|$$\end{document} is first uniformly sampled to generate the group structure size, and a subset of that size is randomly selected from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{R}}_l}$$\end{document} as the requirements group. The number of meta-type demands is sampled uniformly from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[1,10]$$\end{document}, the number of users ranges from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=5$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=1000$$\end{document}, the total amount of each resource is sampled uniformly from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[n \times 2,n \times 4]$$\end{document}, and the price of each meta-type resource is sampled uniformly from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[1,10]$$\end{document}. To evaluate both efficiency and fairness, we measured meta-type resource utilization and compared fairness metrics including variance, max/min ratio, and Gini coefficient, calculated over user-level asset spending under different mechanisms. We categorize experiments into small-scale (0–100 users) and large-scale (> 100 users) cases. This classification is based on experimental settings used in similar work such as Yin et al.21, and aims to facilitate performance comparison across different user scales. To manage computational complexity for large-scale experiments, we adopted several optimization strategies: (1) using sparse vector representations for user demand and resource allocation data to reduce memory overhead, and (2) generating input data in batches to prevent excessive memory usage during model construction.

Conclusions and future work

In this work, the issue of how to enhance meta-type resource usage in a cloud computing system using meta-types while maintaining user asset fairness is examined. We propose a novel fairness-aware mechanism, GAF-MT, based on the asset fairness principle and meta-type stratification. Different from traditional scheduling methods such as DRF and AF, GAF-MT considers both cost sensitivity and resource stratification in allocation decisions. We implement GAF-MT using GUROBI and conduct extensive simulations in small-scale and large-scale user environments. Comparative experiments with AF, DRF, and DRF-MT show that GAF-MT consistently achieves higher resource utilization across different meta-types. Moreover, fairness evaluations based on variance, maximum/minimum ratio, and Gini coefficient confirm that GAF-MT is able to provide the most balanced asset allocation among users. In addition, we evaluate GAF-MT in scenarios with severe imbalance in meta-type resource capacity, further demonstrating its robustness in heterogeneous cloud infrastructures. However, this paper does not consider the distribution of real-time cloud computing environment, that is, users can join the system at any time. An important future direction is to extend the GAF-MT mechanism to real-time cloud computing environments, where users may dynamically join or leave the system. To this end, future research will investigate how to design responsive resource adjustment strategies, maintain fairness under dynamic workloads, and ensure scalability in realistic deployment scenarios.
