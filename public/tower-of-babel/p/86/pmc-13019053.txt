Vascular β-amyloid potentially colocalises with phosphorylated tau in cerebral amyloid angiopathy
Authors: Hsin-Hsi Tsai, Bo-Ching Lee, Chia-Ju Liu, Pu-Tien Chiang, Ya-Chin Tsai, Li-Kai Tsai, Syu-Jyun Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTau pathology is observed in cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and is related to cognitive impairment and neurodegeneration. However, the relationship between tau pathology and amyloid accumulation in the vasculature is unknown. We aimed to assess if regional associations exist between vascular amyloid and tau protein in sporadic CAA.MethodWe assessed cerebral amyloid and hyperphosphorylated tau in patients with probable CAA or Alzheimer’s disease (AD) using 11C-Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) and 18F-T807 positron emission tomography (PET). PET data for each region of interest were extracted using an automated anatomical labelling atlas. We generated correlation matrices to investigate the regional correlations between PiB and T807 uptake, which were further adjusted for multiple comparisons. We evaluated if the severity of regional cortical superficial siderosis (cSS) mediates these associations.Results67 patients with CAA (38 with intracerebral haemorrhage and 29 with cognitive impairment) and 21 patients with AD were included. Significant correlations between amyloid and tau PET uptake were observed in regions of interest for the temporal, parietal and occipital lobes in CAA (adjusted p<0.05); these correlations were not observed in AD. In CAA, the severity of regional cSS correlated positively with T807 uptake in the temporal (β=0.108, 95% CI 0.030 to 0.186, p=0.007) and occipital lobes (β=0.088, 95% CI 0.008 to 0.168, p=0.032). Regional cSS showed no mediating effect on the association between PiB and T807 in the occipital and temporal lobes.ConclusionsIn CAA, tau pathology exhibits significant regional in situ correlations with amyloid deposition in the posterior brain, which suggests a distinct pathophysiology to AD.Keywords: Stroke, Brain

Introduction

Sporadic cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a disorder that involves progressive deposition of β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques in the leptomeningeal and cortical vessels in the ageing brain. In addition to spontaneous lobar intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), cognitive impairment is considered to be one of the major clinical manifestations of CAA.1 The elevated risk of dementia observed in patients with CAA is mainly attributed to a neuronopathic process related to vascular pathology.2 3 In addition to vascular Aβ plaques, phosphorylated tau pathology has been observed in CAA in recent reports and is related to the symptoms of cognitive impairment and neurodegeneration.4 5 The aggregation of misfolded phosphorylated tau in neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) is one of the characteristic pathological changes associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Therefore, the presence of tau pathology in CAA is typically speculated to represent an overlap with AD, as the accumulation of Aβ plaques in the brain occurs in both of these diseases.6

The relationship between Aβ and tau in AD has been investigated extensively. Deposition of extracellular parenchymal plaques initially occurs throughout the neocortex in patients with AD, particularly in the medial prefrontal and medial parietal regions.7 Tau aggregates are commonly observed at a later stage of AD and probably develop first in the entorhinal cortex and medial temporal region.8 9 Although there are some interactions between the Aβ and tau pathways, Aβ and tau can also act independently of each other.9

In contrast to AD, the relationship between amyloid accumulation in the vasculature and the tau pathology observed in CAA has rarely been investigated. Perivascular tau aggregates have been observed close to depositions of vascular amyloid in pathological examinations,10 11 which provides a hypothetical basis that the tau pathology observed in sporadic CAA may co-occur with deposition of amyloid. Additionally, cortical superficial siderosis (cSS)—a consequence of chronic leptomeningeal bleeding from amyloid-laden vessels—has been associated with tau pathology in CAA,5 12 13 which suggests that cSS could be a mediating factor that contributes to the tau deposition in CAA.

To confirm our hypothesis that amyloid and tau pathology commonly co-occur in patients with sporadic CAA, we investigated the regional associations between amyloid and tau uptake on positron emission tomography (PET) using a correlation matrix. We aimed to identify whether significant correlations exist between amyloid and tau in specific regions of the brain in patients with CAA and to evaluate whether regional cSS mediates the association between amyloid and tau.

Methods

Study subjects

We enrolled patients with CAA from September 2018 onwards. Patients clinically diagnosed with vascular cognitive impairment (defined as a clinical dementia rating scale≥0.5) or a history of symptomatic spontaneous ICH who met the criteria for probable CAA based on the modified Boston criteria 1.5 (up to May 2022) or 2.0 criteria (from June 2022 onwards)1 14 were enrolled from either memory clinics or stroke clinics.

Additionally, as the comparison group, we recruited patients with probable AD who fulfilled the diagnostic criteria of the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association.15 Patients with probable AD were only included in the analyses if they exhibited positive amyloid and tau on PET using relevant biomarkers.

All eligible patients who agreed to participate in our study underwent neuroimaging studies that included MRI, amyloid PET and tau PET. At enrolment, the cognitive status of each patient was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination and Cognitive Dementia Rating Scale.

MRI of the brain acquisition and analysis

MRIs of the brain were acquired using a 3-Tesla scanner (Siemens Verio, TIM or mMR, Siemens Medical Solutions, Malvern, Pennsylvania, USA). The imaging protocol included T1-weighted magnetisation-prepared rapid gradient-echo imaging (MPRAGE, flip angle 9°, repetition time/echo time=1460/2.39 ms, field of view=25.6 cm, slice thickness=1 mm), T2-weighted imaging (repetition time/echo time=3530/83 ms, field of view=23 cm, slice thickness=5 mm), FLAIR (Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery) (repetition time/echo time=10 000/89 ms, field of view=23 cm, slice thickness=5 mm), susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI, flip angle 15°, repetition time/echo time=28/20 ms, matrix number=221×320, field of view=23 cm, slice thickness=2 mm), diffusion-weighted imaging and apparent diffusion coefficient maps, as previously described.16

MRI markers were evaluated based on the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging criteria 2.0.17 Briefly, the number of cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) and the presence of cSS were evaluated using axial SWI sequences, according to current standards. A number of CMBs in the lobar, deep regions, and cerebellum were counted. Lacunes were evaluated in the supratentorial region and defined as ‘round or ovoid, subcortical, fluid-filled cavities, from 3 to 15 mm in diameter’. The severity of cSS in the individual lobes (frontal, temporal, parietal and occipital) was graded using a three-point scale: 0: no cSS; 1: focal (<3 gyri); 2: diffuse (≥3 gyri).

The volume of white matter hyperintense (WMH) lesions was calculated based on FLAIR imaging of the ICH-free hemisphere and multiplied by two. Perivascular spaces (PVSs) were evaluated on T2-weighted imaging and defined as sharply delineated structures measuring <3 mm following the course of perforating or medullary vessels.18 The severity of enlarged PVSs at the levels of the basal ganglia and centrum semiovale (CSO) on the side of the brain with more severe involvement was assessed using a five-point scale (grade 0, no enlarged PVS; grade 1, 1–10 enlarged PVSs; grade 2, 11–20 enlarged PVSs; grade 3, 21–40 enlarged PVSs; grade 4, >40 enlarged PVSs).19

PET acquisition and analysis

Radiotracers were prepared at the Cyclotron and Radiopharmaceutical Laboratory of National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH). PET images were obtained using a Discovery 710 scanner (GE Healthcare, USA) following the administration of 370 MBq of either 11C-Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) or 18F-T807, with data acquisition in list mode over 70 min. Images were reconstructed on a 192×192 matrix using 3D mode and reconstructed using ordered set expectation maximisation with 18 subsets and 3 iterations.16 Static images from the final 30 min of the scan period were used for the subsequent imaging analysis. Attenuation correction was performed using a non-enhanced CT scan.

For the enrolled patients with probable AD, we determined amyloid and tau PET biomarkers using the following approaches: amyloid scan positivity was determined by two nuclear medicine specialists (authors C-J L and Y-C T) using a previously proposed visual approach20; all discordant results were resolved by discussion. For the tau PET scans, a meta-temporal region of interest (ROI) that includes the entorhinal cortex, amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus, fusiform gyrus and inferior and middle temporal gyri was created as an AD signature.21 22 Based on results for previous cohorts using the mean and two SD of T807 uptake in a meta-temporal ROI from amyloid-negative and cognitively unimpaired participants,23 a standardised uptake value ratio (SUVR) of 1.26 (reference: cerebellar cortex) was applied as the cut-off to determine tau PET positivity.

The amyloid PET, tau PET and MPRAGE images were processed using MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA), incorporating the Statistical Parametric Mapping programme SPM12 (Functional Imaging Laboratory, Institute of Neurology, University College London, London, UK). We created personalised atlases for each patient using the parcellated automated anatomical labelling (AAL) atlas for Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration through Exponential Lie Algebra.24 These atlases generate anatomical labels, which we used to extract the normalised PiB and T807 PET uptake values of the ROIs. Mean normalised amyloid and tau PET uptake values were extracted for the grey matter using the pons or cerebellar cortex as a reference region. For amyloid PET, we used the pons as the reference region because CAA can involve the cerebellar cortex (thus using the cerebellar cortex would result in elevated cerebellar PiB uptake values).25 26 PET data were extracted from the contralateral hemisphere to the prior haematoma for the survivors of lobar ICH or from the left hemisphere for the patients with cognitive dysfunction. Four deep nuclei ROIs (caudate, putamen, pallidum and thalamus) were excluded from the analyses to minimise potential bias due to off-target binding of T807.27

A correlation matrix for PiB and T807 uptake was generated to investigate the regional correlations. The details and a flowchart of the imaging processing and the methods used to generate the correlation matrix are provided in figure 1 and in online supplemental method.

Flowchart of image processing and generation of the correlation matrix for Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) and T807 images. We coregistered the positron emission tomography (PET) images to 3D-T1 images, normalised and smoothed the intensity of the registered PiB and T807 images and wrapped the automated anatomical labelling (AAL) atlas into individual brain spaces. The mean uptake values were calculated in each region of interest (ROI) within the grey matter (GM). The correlation and p value matrices were generated by performing rank partial correlation analysis between PiB and T807 uptake in each ROI using the parcellated AAL atlas.

Statistical analyses

Discrete variables are presented as counts (%) and continuous variables as mean (± SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate, based on their distribution. The clinical and imaging characteristics of the patients with CAA and AD were compared using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables.

We performed correlation analysis for PiB and T807 uptake in each ROI using the parcellated AAL atlas. We extracted the mean normalised amyloid and tau PET uptake values within 41 ROIs, as defined by the parcellated AAL atlas, which is described in detail in the online supplemental method. Subsequently, adjacency matrices were estimated by calculating the PiB-to-T807 rank partial correlation across subjects, controlling for age, sex and ApoE4 status in each ROI. From this analysis, we also obtained p value matrices and defined significant correlations as p values less than 0.05 after correction for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Specifically, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control for multiple comparisons across the regional correlation analyses of the PiB and T807 uptake values. The raw p values from the correlation matrix were ranked in ascending order, and adjusted thresholds were determined to ensure that the expected proportion of false positives remained below the predefined FDR level (q=0.05).

The associations between neuroimaging SVD (small vessel disease) markers and whole cortex T807 uptake were examined using a multivariate linear regression model with adjustment for age, sex, ApoE4 status and CAA-ICH/CAA-CI. Similarly, we examined the associations between cSS and T807 PET uptake and between cSS and PiB uptake in the temporal and occipital lobes using multivariate linear regression models. We adjusted for the covariates age and sex in the linear regression analysis. Mediation analysis was then conducted to characterise the extent of T807 uptake that could be explained by the severity of cSS or CSO-PVS; specifically, these analyses were used to assess and explain the mechanistic pathways that underlie the observed relationship between an independent variable (amyloid uptake) and outcome variable (tau uptake) via a mediator (cSS or CSO-PVS). Establishment of a mediation effect requires three prerequisites to be met: (1) a significant association between the independent variable and the outcome variable (the path ‘c’), (2) a significant association between the independent variable and the mediator (the path ‘a’) and (3) a significant association between the mediator and the outcome variable when controlling for the effect of the independent variable (the path ‘b’).28 The proportion of mediation was then calculated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect ((c−c′)/c). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, V.27 (Chicago, Illinois, USA).

Results

We included 67 patients with probable CAA (mean age, 74.3±6.9 years; 44.8% men) and 21 patients with AD (mean age, 74.3±6.9 years; 33.3% men) in this study. The demographics and neuroimaging characteristics of the patients with CAA and AD are shown in table 1. In the group with CAA, 38 (56.7%) patients presented with symptomatic ICH and 29 (43.3%) patients presented with cognitive impairment. 65 (97.0%) patients had lobar CMB(s) and 24 (35.8%) patients had cSS. The median whole cortex PiB SUVR (using the pons as a reference) was 0.97 (IQR 0.74–1.15) and the median whole cortex T807 SUVR (using the cerebellar cortex as a reference) was 1.10 (IQR 1.02–1.18) for the patients with CAA. Patients with AD had non-significant higher whole cortex PiB values (1.10 (0.89–1.18), p=0.095) and significantly higher whole cortex T807 SUVR values (1.29 (1.17–1.61), p<0.001), than the group with CAA.

Relationships between cSS, amyloid and tau burden in CAA

Next, we investigated the associations between neuroimaging SVD markers (lobar CMB count, total cSS score, WMH volume and CSO-PVS grade) and tau accumulation. The total cSS score (β=0.061, 95% CI 0.024 to 0.097, p=0.001) and grade of CSO-PVS (β=0.044, 95% CI 0.004 to 0.085, p=0.032) were significantly associated with the whole cortex T807 SUVR. However, after controlling for age, sex, ApoE4 status and CAA-ICH/CAA-cognitive impairment, only the association between the total cSS score and T807 SUVR remained significant (β=0.052, 95% CI 0.013 to 0.093, p=0.010; table 2).

As cSS has also been proposed to be associated with tau accumulation in CAA,5 13 29 we examined the association between the regional severity of cSS and T807 uptake. We assessed the severity of cSS in the temporal and occipital lobes in each patient with CAA. Regional PiB uptake correlated with the regional severity of cSS in the temporal (β=0.104, 95% CI 0.008 to 0.200, p=0.035) and occipital lobes (β=0.127, 95% CI 0.008 to 0.247, p=0.038). In addition, the regional severity of cSS correlated positively with T807 uptake in both the temporal (β=0.108, 95% CI 0.030 to 0.186, p=0.007) and occipital lobes (β=0.088, 95% CI 0.008 to 0.168, p=0.032) in the patients with CAA.

We next investigated whether the relationships between regional amyloid and tau uptake in CAA are mediated by cSS (figure 4). Mediation analyses indicated the associations between regional PiB and T807 uptake were not mediated by regional cSS in the occipital (figure 4A) or temporal lobes (figure 4B). Similar analyses suggested CSO-PVS did not have a mediating effect on the associations between regional PiB and T807 uptake in CAA (figure 4C,D).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the regional correlations between amyloid and tau load in patients with CAA using dual PiB and T807 PET scans and an ROI-based approach. We observed significant correlations between PiB and T807 uptake in the posterior brain (temporal, parietal and occipital lobes), which suggests that vascular Aβ potentially has a local effect on the development of phosphorylated tau in CAA. Interestingly, this association was not observed in patients with AD, which implies that distinct pathways lead to tau pathology in CAA and AD. In addition to amyloid uptake, we confirmed previous observations that cSS is associated with local tau deposits in CAA. However, our analysis indicates the regional association between amyloid and tau uptake in CAA is not mediated by cSS, but appears to be directly contributed by amyloid. Overall, this study supports our hypothesis that vascular Aβ may have direct toxic effects and lead to local deposition of hyperphosphorylated tau in CAA.

CAA involves cerebrovascular deposition of Aβ, which is also the primary component of the senile plaques observed in patients with AD. The shared pathology of Aβ in both CAA and AD results in frequent co-occurrence of these age-related disorders.6 Tau pathology is traditionally not a prominent feature of CAA, and the presence of tau in patients with CAA is usually considered representing overlapping AD. This assumption was supported by Schoemaker et al, who found that concomitant tau pathology in patients with CAA who presented with cognitive impairment was more related to the amnestic phenotype, which is more suggestive of the clinical features of AD.4 We recently investigated tau deposition in patients with sporadic CAA who presented with spontaneous lobar ICH. We observed higher tau burdens in the brain regions related to Braak staging, which suggests the accumulation of tau in sporadic CAA could follow a similar pattern as the patterns of tau deposition in AD.5 However, it is important to note that these previous reports only provided speculative evidence that the tau pathology in CAA is a result of coexisting AD, and the direct contribution of vascular Aβ to the development of tau pathology in CAA remained unknown. In addition, studies investigating the differences in the topographical pattern of tau deposition between CAA and AD were also lacking.

Only a small number of histological reports have explored the relationship between vascular Aβ and perivascular tau deposits in cases of pure CAA. One case report of two patients with dementia and severe CAA, but no parenchymal Aβ pathology, detected significant perivascular tau in pathological examinations and suggested that the tau deposits may be pathogenically related to vascular amyloid pathology.10 Similarly, a neuropathological study of cases of AD with severe CAA showed that perivascular amyloid deposits were accompanied by perivascular tau neuritic pathology.11 Additionally, the perivascular tau aggregates were associated with activated astrocytes,30 and the end-feet of astrocytes play a crucial role in maintaining the blood–brain barrier. Thus, this finding suggested that the vascular damage caused by amyloid accumulation in CAA may induce an astrocytic response that triggers tau aggregation. Collectively, this evidence provides a hypothetical basis that tau pathology may colocalise with amyloid deposition in CAA, which is in line with our main findings of significant regional correlations between PiB and T807 uptake in CAA. Interestingly, this correlation was most pronounced in the posterior brain, which is in line with previous observations of the primarily posterior involvement in CAA.31 32

However, similar colocalisation of amyloid and tau was not observed in the patients with AD in our study, which suggests that the tau pathologies in sporadic CAA and AD may be caused by distinct pathogenic mechanisms. Selective regional vulnerability to Aβ and tau pathologies is observed in AD; Aβ initially accumulates in the medial frontal and medial parietal cortices,33 whereas tau is first deposited in the medial temporal lobe and then affects other brain regions.34 A recent analysis of histological brain samples from patients with AD further complicated this matter, as tau-positive vessels were occasionally found to co-occur with CAA and were associated with local areas of increased tau NFTs.35 The processes that explain how CAA contributes to neurodegeneration as a copathology in AD remain to be clarified. However, obtaining a better understanding of tau pathology in sporadic CAA may provide an ideal model to develop potential therapeutics to target the vascular pathology in AD. We have to acknowledge that, in the current study, we were unable to confirm whether the regional association between amyloid and tau is attributed to perivascular neuritic dystrophy around CAA-laden vessels or to local increases in regional NFTs. Future neuropathological studies are required to more precisely delineate this relationship in sporadic CAA.

In this study, the severity of CSO-PVS on MRI correlated with the tau burden, which implies perivascular drainage dysfunction may potentially play a role in the deposition of tau in CAA. The observation of tau deposits around cerebral vessels in CAA also provides potential evidence of a vascular clearance pathway for tau pathology. The glymphatic system contributes to and exacerbates tau accumulation in mouse models of AD.36 37 Moreover, a neuroimaging study in humans revealed a correlation between enlargement of PVSs in the CSO and higher levels of CSF p-tau and t-tau in individuals with positive amyloid pathology.38 This suggests perivascular drainage may contribute to tau pathophysiology, and these effects may be more pronounced in brain regions affected by severe CAA.

Previous studies observed a potential relationship between tau pathology and cSS, which is considered to be a specific marker of underlying CAA.5 12 13 29 39 40 Our earlier study of tau pathology in sporadic CAA supported this observation, in that the tau burden was associated with the severity of cSS, independent of the whole cortex cerebral amyloid load.5 Notably, some case reports of patients with CAA who underwent tau PET scans indicated higher tau PET uptake in the cortical regions affected by cSS.13 29 As cSS may represent chronic bleeding due to advanced CAA of the leptomeningeal vessels and could trigger secondary ischaemic injury in the affected areas,41 the associations between amyloid and tau may potentially be mediated by cSS. However, this hypothesis was not supported by our mediation analysis, which indicated that cSS had no effect on the regional correlations between PiB and T807 uptake in patients with CAA. We postulate that vascular amyloid deposition and chronic leptomeningeal bleeding both independently contribute to the local tau pathology in CAA. Overall, these findings on the role of vascular Aβ and its related toxic effects could represent a potential intervention target to decelerate the rate of neurodegeneration in CAA.

Our study has several limitations. First, amyloid and tau deposition were measured using PET scans instead of direct histopathological sampling. While this imaging analysis provides evidence of regional correlations, the exact pathogenic mechanisms that lead to the correlations between vascular Aβ and colocalised tau remain undetermined. Second, as an important limitation of amyloid PET, the current tracers (including PiB) bind to both vascular and parenchymal Aβ42; thus, we cannot elucidate how concomitant parenchymal Aβ contributes to the local tau deposition in CAA using our data. Third, T807 is a first-generation tau tracer with significant binding affinity for monoamine oxidase B43 and also shows off-target binding to haematoma.5 However, previous studies suggested T807 has high binding affinity for paired helical filament tau within NFTs, and T807 is the most widely used tau tracer to date.44 45 At last, the sample size was relatively small, which potentially limits the robustness and generalisability of our conclusions. However, this study should be viewed as a proof-of-concept, and to our knowledge, our cohort represents the largest CAA cohort for which amyloid and tau PET data is available to date; thus, our findings offer novel insight into the interactions between vascular amyloid and tau in sporadic CAA.

In conclusion, we observed significant in situ regional correlations between amyloid and tau deposition in the posterior brain in sporadic CAA, which implies that vascular Aβ and tau aggregates occur in close vicinity and are regionally closely related to each other. Our results indicate that the tau pathologies observed in sporadic CAA and AD may be caused by distinct pathogenic mechanisms. More research is required to delineate the impact of the perivascular tau tangles in CAA, which could potentially represent a novel therapeutic target to prevent neurodegeneration in CAA.
