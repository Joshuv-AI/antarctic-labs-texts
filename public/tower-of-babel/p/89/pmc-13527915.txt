EIT-assessed ventilation–perfusion responses to prone positioning and inhaled nitric oxide in mechanically ventilated COVID-19-related ARDS: a prospective sequential study
Authors: Yuxian Wang, Yuyang Dai, Xin Zheng, Ling Zhu, Yaxiaerjiang Muhetaer, Zhanqi Zhao, Kai Liu, Ming Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProne positioning (PP) and inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) are widely utilized rescue therapies for severe hypoxemia during invasive mechanical ventilation. However, their combined effects on regional ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) matching remain incompletely characterized. This study utilized single-plane saline-contrast electrical impedance tomography (EIT) to achieve real-time bedside visualization and evaluate the relative spatial mechanisms of these interventions in patients with COVID-19-related ARDS.MethodsWe conducted a prospective sequential physiological study in mechanically ventilated patients with moderate-to-severe COVID-19-related acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Relative regional ventilation, perfusion, and V/Q matching distributions were monitored across four predefined, fixed sequential conditions: supine position ventilation (SPV), SPV with iNO (20 ppm for 1 h; SPV + iNO), prone position ventilation after stabilization (PPV), and PPV with an additional 1 h of iNO (PPV + iNO). Primary analyses focused on prespecified within-patient contrasts, supplemented by exploratory difference-in-differences (DiD) analyses to evaluate position-dependent vascular responses.ResultsTwenty-eight patients completed all study phases. Hemodynamics and conventional respiratory mechanics remained remarkably stable across all four conditions. Systemic oxygenation improved progressively; both SPV + iNO and PPV significantly increased PaO2 and PaO2/FiO2 compared to SPV alone, while the addition of iNO during PPV (PPV + iNO) yielded further oxygenation improvements and reduced FiO2 requirements. Within the EIT-assessed thoracic slice, SPV + iNO produced the most widespread V/Q improvements, significantly reducing the global non-perfused fraction (−7.28%), wasted ventilation (−8.14%), wasted perfusion (−5.89%), and the global inhomogeneity of perfusion (GIper). Transitioning to PPV similarly reduced the global non-perfused fraction (−8.76%) and GIper, while ventral-to-dorsal ventilation distribution remained stable. Notably, while PPV + iNO improved systemic oxygenation, it did not produce additional significant reductions in global EIT V/Q metrics, and locally increased the dorsal low V/Q compartment (+ 4.01%). DiD analyses revealed that while body position did not significantly modify the acute systemic oxygenation response to iNO, it significantly altered EIT-derived V/Q responses, indicating an attenuated capacity for iNO to improve regional V/Q matching in the imaged slice when administered in the prone position.ConclusionsBoth prone positioning and iNO improved oxygenation in moderate-to-severe COVID-19-related ARDS. Within the EIT-assessed thoracic slice, these improvements were accompanied by better regional V/Q matching, which was mainly perfusion-related rather than ventilation-related and suggests limited recruitability of the imaged region; the effect of iNO on regional V/Q matching was attenuated in the prone position. As EIT samples a single thoracic cross-section, these are localized findings and should not be generalized to the whole lung.Trial registration ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05715762. Introduction

Severe hypoxemia remains a major challenge in patients with acute respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. Prone positioning (PP) is a well-established intervention that improves oxygenation and survival in patients with moderate-to-severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [1]. The beneficial effects of prone positioning are generally attributed to improved ventilation distribution, reduced lung stress and strain, and the macro-redistribution of pulmonary perfusion [2–5]. Conversely, inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) acts as a selective pulmonary vasodilator, diverting blood flow toward well-ventilated lung regions to improve ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) matching [6–11]. Although both strategies are clinically viable, their pathophysiological interplay remains poorly characterized. Specifically, it remains unclear whether gravity-dependent postural rearrangements and pharmacologic microvascular vasodilation operate synergistically or exhibit overlapping physiological limits [4, 6–11].

Previous clinical investigations have focused predominantly on systemic oxygenation metrics. While arterial blood gas analysis tracks global gas exchange efficiency, it functions as a "black-box" metric that obscures underlying regional mechanisms. Global data alone cannot distinguish whether oxygenation gains are driven by dorsal alveolar recruitment, the reduction of true shunt fractions, or improved matching in transitional zones. Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) addresses this limitation by providing non-invasive, real-time bedside visualization of regional V/Q coupling [12–21]. Recent methodological advancements, such as the saline-bolus contrast technique, enable highly granular quantification of localized pulmonary perfusion profiles [12–17].

Evaluating these regional responses is particularly critical in COVID-19-related ARDS. Unlike classic ARDS, the vascular pathobiology of COVID-19 is dominated by severe endothelial injury, disrupted hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, and widespread microthrombosis. This frequently results in profound V/Q mismatching that is highly disproportionate to the loss of lung volume [22–27]. Therefore, assessing the regional impact of iNO as a targeted vascular therapy—define how its efficacy is modulated by the structural lung rearrangements induced by PP—is of paramount physiological interest.

Accordingly, we conducted a prospective sequential physiological study using saline-contrast EIT to evaluate regional V/Q characteristics in mechanically ventilated patients with COVID-19-related ARDS across four predefined sequential conditions: supine position ventilation (SPV), SPV with iNO (SPV + iNO), prone position ventilation (PPV), and PPV with iNO (PPV + iNO). This fixed sequential framework was specifically selected to mimic the clinical escalatory transition from standard mechanical ventilation to advanced rescue therapies, allowing us to determine if body position acts as a modifier of the acute pulmonary vascular response to nitric oxide [28, 29].

Physiological and imaging measurements were consistently obtained at the end of each stable phase. The study was conducted in the intensive care unit (ICU) of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (No. B2019-230(2)R). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients’ legally authorized representatives. The study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05715762). The originally registered sample size (n = 20) reflected a feasibility-based estimate at the time of study registration. No formal a priori power calculation was performed, consistent with the exploratory physiological nature of the study. The final analyzed sample included all patients who successfully completed the full sequential protocol.

Study population and safety monitoring

Eligible patients were adults with moderate-to-severe ARDS according to the Berlin criteria who were enrolled during their first prone positioning session. Exclusion criteria included pre-admission hypoxia lasting more than 7 days, severe hemodynamic instability, pregnancy, and standard contraindications to EIT. Given the baseline severity of hypoxemia, stringent safety monitoring and clear termination criteria were strictly enforced during the 20-s end-inspiratory breath-hold maneuvers required for EIT saline-contrast perfusion tracking. Criteria for immediate protocol interruption were predefined as: a drop in oxygen saturation (SaO2 < 85%) lasting more than 5 s, an acute fluctuation in mean arterial pressure (MAP) exceeding ± 20% of the baseline value, or the development of new-onset cardiac arrhythmias.

Experimental protocol and ventilator management

Patients underwent the interventions in a rigid, non-randomized, fixed sequential order to characterize the incremental physiologic responses under two distinct gravitational baselines. Under deep sedation and continuous neuromuscular blockade, patients were managed with lung-protective mechanical ventilation. Tidal volume was strictly targeted at ≤ 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, and the respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain arterial pH within the clinically acceptable range. Inspired oxygen fraction (FiO2) was adjusted clinically according to standard care protocols to maintain target oxygen saturation.

Importantly, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) was titrated based on a compliance-based assessment after standard recruitment at baseline and was maintained completely constant across all four sequential study conditions. This strict setting was maintained to isolate the independent and combined vascular/positional effects of the interventions without confounding airway pressure variables. The iNO dose was fixed at 20 ppm across both the supine and prone exposure phases, selected based on established clinical trial benchmarks and prior adult ARDS physiological investigations [30–34]. The portable Nitric Oxide Generator and Delivery System (INOwill N200, Novlead Biotech, China) was connected to the ventilator circuit for iNO delivery. Following the completion of the SPV + iNO phase, iNO was discontinued, allowing for a 1-h washout period concurrent with the physical repositioning and the initial phase of the 2–3 h stabilization period in the prone position before PPV recordings were initiated.

Electrical impedance tomography measurements

A 16-electrode EIT belt was placed around the thorax at the fourth or fifth intercostal space and connected to a PulmoVista 500 monitor (Dräger Medical GmbH, Lübeck, Germany). To minimize position-related measurement variability, the belt was kept at the identical anatomical level throughout the protocol. EIT signals were acquired at a frame rate of 20 Hz. Regional pulmonary perfusion was assessed during a 20-s end-inspiratory breath-hold via a rapid bolus injection of 10 mL of 5% NaCl through a central venous catheter within 2 s, generating a first-pass impedance dilution curve [12–18].

EIT data were analyzed using customized software in MATLAB R2023a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). It is critical to emphasize that EIT does not measure absolute volumetric air flow or local organ blood flow in milliliters; instead, it tracks relative regional changes in thoracic bioimpedance over time (ΔZ). Consequently, all derived indices of ventilation (V), perfusion (Q), and the resulting V/Q represent relative spatial distributions and slice-based surrogates across a single cross-sectional thoracic plane, rather than direct measurements of absolute whole-lung volumetric exchange units [12–18].

At the pixel level, regional ventilation (V) was defined as the tidal impedance change between expiration and inspiration (ΔZV). Regional perfusion (Q) was estimated from the normalized steepest slope of the impedance signal during saline-bolus passage (ΔZP). Functional lung zones were defined using a pixel-level threshold of ≥ 10% of the maximum regional standard deviation of impedance changes. Ventral and dorsal regions of interest (ROIs) were defined according to anatomical orientation consistently across study conditions. Global inhomogeneity (GI) indices were calculated for ventilation, perfusion, and V/Q matching as GIven, GIper, and GIvq, respectively [12, 18, 20, 21, 28, 29].

Parallel quantification of ventilation–perfusion mismatching

To provide a rigorous assessment of pulmonary gas exchange efficiency, we evaluated regional ventilation–perfusion mismatching using two parallel, complementary analytical approaches:

Discrete categorical approach

The local relative V/Q was computed directly as the ratio of the relative percentage of ventilation to the relative percentage of perfusion within each pixel, without calibration by global cardiac output or absolute minute ventilation. Pixels within the functional lung matrix were classified into five mutually exclusive discrete compartments based on predefined relative V/Q ranges: non-ventilated (V/Q ≤ 0.1), low V/Q (0.1–0.8), normal V/Q (0.8–1.25), high V/Q (1.25–10), and non-perfused (V/Q ≥ 10) [13, 15, 28, 29].

Threshold-free continuous approach

To completely avoid potential edge-effects inherent to arbitrary categorical thresholds, we calculated two global, continuous distribution indices—wasted ventilation and wasted perfusion—across the entire unsegmented EIT matrix using the established Pavlovsky logarithmic weighting method. These global indices integrate information across multiple regions on a continuum and are mathematically independent of (orthogonal to) the single discrete compartments:

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for the primary repeated-measures comparisons, while descriptive medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) are provided for clinical context. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

Because the four study conditions were applied in a fixed sequence rather than as a randomized crossover design, the primary analysis treated the protocol as four sequential repeated-measures conditions and focused on prespecified within-patient contrasts, analyzed chronologically: (1) SPV + iNO versus SPV, to estimate the acute effect of iNO in the supine position; (2) PPV versus SPV, to estimate prone-associated change without concurrent iNO; (3) PPV + iNO versus PPV, to estimate the acute effect of iNO during prone ventilation; and (4) PPV + iNO versus SPV + iNO, to estimate prone-associated change in the presence of iNO.

For each outcome, paired mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Multiple comparisons across outcome domains were rigorously controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure. To preserve statistical power while strictly controlling Type I error inflation, the correction was applied independently within designated conceptual "families" of physiological outcomes: (1) the hemodynamic family (comprising heart rate, MAP, and norepinephrine dosage); (2) the global EIT ventilation/perfusion metrics family, and (3) the regional EIT distribution family (applied separately to the data matrix presented in Table S2). Adjusted values are reported throughout as q-values, with statistical significance established at q < 0.05.

In addition, exploratory difference-in-differences analyses, defined as (PPV + iNO−PPV)- (SPV + iNO−SPV), were performed to assess whether body position significantly modified the acute vascular response to iNO. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Screening, enrollment, and baseline cohort characteristics

Between December 2022 and March 2023, a total of 33 patients with moderate-to-severe COVID-19-related ARDS were screened, of whom 30 eligible patients were enrolled in this sequential physiological study (Fig. 2, flowchart). During the study, 2 patients were withdrawn from the protocol immediately following the transition to PPV due to the development of new-onset, transient cardiac arrhythmias. No other severe or persistent adverse events, prolonged desaturations, or profound hemodynamic instability occurred during the completed EIT maneuvers. Hemodynamic variables, including heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and norepinephrine dose, remained stable across the four sequential conditions, with no significant FDR-adjusted differences in any prespecified contrast (Table S1).

Effects of supine iNO initiation (SPV + iNO vs. SPV)

During supine ventilation, iNO initiation produced the most widespread improvements in V/Q matching (Table 3, Fig. 3B–D). Compared with SPV, SPV + iNO significantly reduced the global non-perfused fraction (mean difference,−7.28%; q = 0.0001), wasted ventilation (−8.14%; q < 0.001), wasted perfusion (−5.89%; q = 0.001), GIper (−0.078; q = 0.001), and GIvq (−0.097; q = 0.001). Regionally, these improvements were distributed across both ventral and dorsal lung regions, with significant reductions in ventral and dorsal non-perfused fractions, ventral and dorsal wasted ventilation, and ventral and dorsal wasted perfusion (all q < 0.05; Table S2).

Effects of adding iNO during prone ventilation (PPV + iNO vs. PPV)

In contrast, adding iNO during prone ventilation significantly improved systemic oxygenation but did not produce additional significant reductions in global EIT-derived indices, including the non-perfused fraction, wasted ventilation, wasted perfusion, GIper, or GIvq (Table 3). Regionally, the only significant change after adding iNO during PPV was an increase in the dorsal low V/Q compartment (+ 4.01%; q = 0.034; Table S2).

Effects of prone positioning (PPV vs. SPV)

Transition from SPV to PPV was also associated with significant physiological improvements. Globally, PPV reduced the non-perfused fraction (−8.76%; q < 0.001), wasted ventilation (−7.96%; q = 0.017), and GIper (−0.103; q < 0.001) compared with SPV (Table 3). At the regional level, these changes were accompanied by significant reductions in both ventral and dorsal non-perfused fractions (q = 0.007 and q = 0.002, respectively), as well as a reduction in ventral wasted ventilation (q = 0.042; Table S2).

Comparison of combined therapy vs. supine iNO (PPV + iNO vs. SPV + iNO)

When the two iNO-active conditions were compared, PPV + iNO was associated with a higher global low V/Q fraction (+ 7.14%; q = 0.020), higher global wasted perfusion (+ 3.04%; q = 0.032), and lower global normal V/Q fraction (−4.22%; q = 0.024) than SPV + iNO (Table 3). Regionally, these differences were mainly reflected by a higher dorsal low V/Q fraction (+ 4.47%; q = 0.019) and higher ventral wasted perfusion (+ 2.50%; q = 0.031) during PPV + iNO (Table S2).

Exploratory difference-in-differences assessment of position-dependent iNO responses

Exploratory difference-in-differences analyses showed that body position did not significantly modify the acute systemic oxygenation response to iNO, as indicated by non-significant DiD estimates for PaO₂ and the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio (Table 2). In contrast, body position significantly modified several EIT-derived V/Q responses to iNO (Table 3). Significant DiD signals were observed for the global non-perfused fraction (+ 5.72%; q = 0.014), wasted ventilation (+ 5.89%; q = 0.035), wasted perfusion (+ 5.32%; q = 0.038), GIper (+ 0.067; q = 0.022), and normal V/Q fraction (−5.43%; q = 0.020), as well as for the dorsal non-perfused fraction (+ 3.08%; q = 0.009; Table S2).

These findings indicate that, although iNO improved systemic oxygenation in both body positions, its incremental effects on EIT-derived V/Q matching were more pronounced during supine ventilation and attenuated during prone ventilation.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this prospective sequential physiological study of mechanically ventilated patients with COVID-19-related ARDS, both PP and iNO were associated with significant short-term improvements in systemic oxygenation. Parallel EIT assessments suggested that these clinical benefits were accompanied by favorable, localized changes in relative regional V/Q indices within the EIT-assessed thoracic slice, including reductions in non-perfused regions and lower GIper. Taken together, these findings indicate that the primary physiological signal of these rescue interventions is mediated by a rebalancing of regional pulmonary perfusion distributions and an optimization of microvascular V/Q coupling, rather than by major structural modifications in bulk airway respiratory-system mechanics [28, 29]. However, given the sequential non-randomized design, these effects should be interpreted as time-evolving physiological responses occurring under different intervention states, rather than strictly isolated effects attributable to each intervention.

Another important physiological observation within this protocol is that the clearest measurable EIT-derived improvements occurred immediately after the initiation of supine iNO and following the acute transition from the supine to the prone position. By contrast, once patients were established in the prone baseline state, the additional short-term regional modifications induced by adding iNO were smaller in magnitude and less consistent after strict multiplicity correction, suggesting a postural limit to iNO-induced microvascular recruitment.

Pathophysiological interpretation of EIT dynamics and COVID-19 phenotypes

An interesting finding of this study is that the ventral-to-dorsal ventilation distribution remained relatively stable during the transition from supine to prone positioning, which differs from the typical response observed in early or recruitable ARDS [28, 36]. This suggests a reduced proportion of recruitable lung units in patients with severe, prolonged COVID-19-related ARDS. In such cases, structural heterogeneity and dependent consolidation may limit gravity-dependent alveolar recruitment under a fixed PEEP strategy. Accordingly, the absence of a pronounced ventilation redistribution should not be interpreted as a lack of physiological response to prone positioning, but rather as an indicator of limited mechanically recruitable lung tissue.

Importantly, the therapeutic effects of prone positioning in this cohort appear to be driven predominantly by modulation of pulmonary perfusion rather than ventilation. By reshaping gravitational gradients within the pulmonary circulation and reducing dorsal- ventral perfusion mismatch, prone positioning effectively decreased non-perfused regions and reduced GIper. This vascular-dominant mechanism improved V/Q matching, providing a coherent explanation for the dissociation between stable ventilation distribution and markedly improved perfusion profiles.

Postural modulations and iNO sequence interactions

A key focus of this investigation was to explore whether body position acts as a significant modifier of the acute pulmonary vascular response to iNO. Interestingly, our exploratory DiD analyses revealed a dissociation between systemic oxygenation and regional EIT-derived V/Q responses. While the acute systemic oxygenation benefit of iNO was largely preserved regardless of posture, the regional V/Q improvements captured by EIT were significantly attenuated when iNO was administered in the prone position compared to the supine position.

From a physiological standpoint, prone positioning and iNO target V/Q mismatch through distinct yet topographically overlapping pathways. Prone positioning promotes a more homogeneous distribution of transpulmonary pressure and exploits the large-scale gravitational redistribution of pulmonary blood flow. Conversely, iNO acts as a highly selective pulmonary vasodilator, preferentially diverting microvascular blood flow toward well-ventilated alveoli [10, 11]. In our cohort, the EIT responses aligned with these complementary mechanisms: the initial prone transition fundamentally alleviated macro-perfusion mismatches and maximized overall structural perfusion homogeneity. Consequently, once prone positioning had structurally rearranged macro-perfusion distributions, the physiological window for further acute microvascular optimization by iNO was narrowed—a phenomenon suggesting a "ceiling effect" in microvascular recruitment.

This ceiling effect may also explain the apparent paradox observed during the combined PPV + iNO phase, where systemic PaO2 improved despite a lack of global EIT V/Q improvement and a localized increase in dorsal low V/Q compartments. In the prone position, administering a potent vasodilator like iNO may locally override hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction in structurally diseased but now non-dependent dorsal units, diverting blood flow to partially ventilated areas and thereby increasing the local low V/Q fraction on EIT.

Although the four study conditions may superficially resemble a within-subject 2 × 2 design, the fixed sequential protocol precludes a formal crossover factorial interpretation. Later experimental phases may have been inherently influenced by elapsed time, prior drug exposures, residual washout profiles, and cumulative recruitment during ongoing prone stabilization. Therefore, the sequential changes observed in this study should be interpreted as part of an evolving physiological trajectory rather than strictly independent effects of discrete interventions.

Clinical implications and hemodynamic tolerance

Systemic hemodynamic variables remained remarkably stable throughout the experimental sequence, confirming the short-term physiological tolerability of iNO at a fixed dose of 20 ppm during both supine and prone ventilation in this selected cohort. This observation is consistent with prior physiological reports indicating that inhaled nitric oxide exerts a highly localized pulmonary vascular action, improving oxygenation without inducing systemic hypotension or altering exogenous vasopressor requirements [24, 25].

However, the clinical implications of these physiological findings must be interpreted with modest expectations. While an improvement in the PaO2/FiO2 and selected EIT-derived indices reflects a favorable short-term physiological response, it does not necessarily translate into improved patient-centered outcomes or long-term survival benefits at the bedside. In clinical ARDS management, overall outcomes are determined not only by gas exchange efficiency, but also by the evolution of underlying parenchymal injury, systemic organ cross-talk, and the cumulative risk of ventilator-induced lung injury. This distinction is particularly relevant for iNO, which frequently demonstrates rapid improvements in arterial oxygenation without consistently improving survival in large randomized controlled trials [6, 7, 9–11]. In clinical practice, EIT-guided iNO may therefore be considered a selective, short-term physiological adjunct in patients with severe refractory hypoxemia despite lung-protective ventilation and prone positioning, particularly when bedside imaging suggests ongoing perfusion heterogeneity or severe ventilated but non-perfused dead-space uncoupling [17, 21].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employed a fixed sequential, non-randomized design due to clinical feasibility and safety considerations. As a result, time-dependent physiological drift, carryover effects, and cumulative recruitment during prolonged prone positioning cannot be fully separated from intervention-specific effects. This limits strict causal attribution of observed changes to individual interventions. Second, although a washout period was implemented following discontinuation of iNO prior to prone positioning, no intermediate EIT measurements were obtained at the end of this washout phase. Therefore, we cannot confirm whether pulmonary vascular tone or regional V/Q distribution had fully returned to baseline prior to the PPV phase. While the very short biological half-life of iNO makes persistent direct pharmacological effects unlikely, residual downstream microvascular or V/Q-related effects cannot be excluded. Third, the physiological effects of prone positioning may be time-dependent and non-linear, involving gradual alveolar recruitment and progressive redistribution of perfusion. Consequently, improvements observed during prolonged prone ventilation may partly reflect cumulative recruitment effects rather than the immediate effects of iNO alone. Fourth, the use of a fixed PEEP across all phases may have constrained optimal mechanical recruitment in prone positioning, as lung mechanics and optimal distending pressures vary with posture. Fifth, EIT assesses only a single two-dimensional thoracic slice at the level of the electrode belt and therefore provides localized rather than whole-lung information; cranio-caudal heterogeneity, including that of the dependent dorsocaudal regions frequently involved in ARDS, is not captured, and all indices reported here, including those termed global, refer to this slice. Additionally, ROI-based and threshold-dependent definitions of V/Q compartments may not reflect absolute regional flow distributions, and regions with moderately low or high V/Q that also contribute to hypoxemia are only partly detected by EIT; this may contribute to the dissociation between systemic oxygenation and EIT-derived indices observed during PPV + iNO. Sixth, given that the physiological effects of inhaled nitric oxide are closely related to pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular afterload, the absence of standardized right ventricular functional data limits our ability to further characterize inter-individual variability in hemodynamic response or to identify potential right ventricular-dependent responder phenotypes. Finally, the cohort represents a highly selected population of severe COVID-19-related ARDS patients who were able to complete a complex sequential protocol. This may limit generalizability to less severe ARDS phenotypes or non-COVID populations.

Conclusions

In mechanically ventilated patients with moderate-to-severe COVID-19-related ARDS, both prone positioning and iNO improved short-term systemic oxygenation. Within the EIT-assessed thoracic slice, these gains were accompanied by better regional V/Q matching, driven mainly by fewer non-perfused regions and a more homogeneous perfusion distribution rather than by redistribution of ventilation, a pattern consistent with limited mechanical recruitability of the imaged region. The regional V/Q improvement with iNO appeared position-dependent, being most evident during supine ventilation and attenuated in the prone position, which may indicate a postural ceiling on microvascular recruitment. Since EIT samples a single thoracic cross-section, these regional observations are localized findings and should not be taken to represent whole-lung characteristics. Given the exploratory, fixed-sequential design of this study, future randomized controlled trials are warranted to isolate these intervention-specific effects, validate these physiological phenotypes, and assess their impact on long-term clinical outcomes.
