EEG entropy modulation as a biomarker of emotion regulation and resilience
Authors: W Tanner Creel, Richard E Hartman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA fundamental challenge in clinical neuropsychology is the objective assessment of emotion regulation and resilience. Current reliance on self-report is hampered by bias, creating a critical need for brain-based biomarkers that can capture the dynamics of emotional functioning. This review advances EEG entropy modulation as a theoretically appealing and emerging candidate. Converging research indicates that neural complexity, quantified by entropy measures, reflects the flexible information processing that underpins adaptive self-regulation. We synthesize evidence demonstrating diminished neural complexity in emotional dysregulation and anxiety, while interventions like mindfulness may work by restoring it. Moving beyond static, resting-state analysis, we argue that the modulation of entropy during cognitive-emotional tasks provides a far more powerful and ecologically valid marker of regulatory capacity. This dynamic approach, enhanced by emerging methods for capturing EEG in real-world settings, captures the brain’s flexible response to challenges. Future research will benefit from exploring task-based entropy modulation in key regulatory hubs like the prefrontal cortex. Integrating these data with machine and deep learning approaches is a critical frontier, poised to identify distinct 'entropy profiles' of dysregulation and predict therapeutic response with an objectivity current methods lack. This interdisciplinary agenda seeks to bridge the gap between subjective experience and objective physiology, transforming how we assess and improve mental health outcomes.Keywords: Entropy, EEG, Emotion regulation, Biomarkers, Resilience

Introduction: towards the development of biomarkers

A fundamental challenge in clinical neuropsychology is accurately assessing emotion regulation and resilience. Despite their centrality to mental illness, objective brain-based measures remain underdeveloped. These processes are not static traits but dynamic, adaptive functions that unfold over time in response to environmental and internal demands. Current gold-standard assessment tools rely heavily on subjective self-report methods, which are prone to bias and threaten validity (Lira et al., 2022). This gap highlights a critical need to advance our understanding of the neurobiological underpinnings of these processes and to develop novel biosignatures that can capture the dynamics of emotional functioning with greater objectivity.

The brain’s ability to adaptively process information underpins emotion regulation and resilience. Neural entropy, a measure of brain signal complexity, captures how flexibly neural systems respond to changing internal and external demands (Gu et al., 2024), making it a theoretically compelling candidate for an objective biomarker (Hager et al., 2017, Keshmiri, 2020). Beyond its theoretical appeal, electroencephalography (EEG) entropy offers distinct advantages that position it as a pragmatically attractive and feasible solution for translational biomarker research. Modern high-density scalp systems can be obtained for a fraction of the cost required for functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) or magnetoencephalography (MEG), and recent innovations in wireless, dry-electrode headsets have driven costs even lower while eliminating the need for shielded rooms or specialized technical staff (Casson, 2019).

Since the hardware is lightweight and increasingly motion-tolerant, entropy modulation can now be tracked in ecologically valid settings including virtual-reality (VR) and augmented-reality (AR) paradigms and field environments (Krugliak and Clarke, 2022). Additionally, EEG’s millisecond temporal resolution matches the rapid timescale on which emotion-regulation processes unfold, allowing precise quantification of entropy modulation that slower imaging modalities cannot resolve. Taken together, these considerations underscore why EEG-based entropy modulation is well positioned for development as a clinically viable biomarker.

While much EEG research has concentrated on linear and functional connectivity features to understand various emotions and psychopathologies, there has been comparatively less focus on the complex, non-linear neural dynamics of emotion regulation and resilience themselves. This is a critical oversight, as emotion regulation and resilience are dynamic processes that are poorly captured by linear models. Non-linear methods like multiscale entropy (MSE), and particularly the modulation of MSE during cognitive-emotional tasks, may therefore offer novel insights where other methods fall short (Gu et al. 2024). A focus on such methods promises to advance not only our fundamental understanding but also practical applications that can inform the development of new mental health diagnostics and interventions. This review synthesizes the current knowledge on neural entropy, with a particular focus on EEG, and makes the case for its continued development as a critical biomarker for emotion regulation and resilience. We will first establish the theoretical and methodological basis of a popular entropy measure, multiscale entropy (MSE), then link neural complexity to the cognitive flexibility that underpins emotion regulation and resilience. Literature search strategy

This article is a narrative review intended to synthesize emerging conceptual and empirical evidence in the field. Literature was identified primarily through PubMed searches (2022–2025) using terms such as EEG entropy, multiscale entropy, neural complexity, emotion regulation, and resilience. Additional references were identified through citation tracking and ongoing engagement with the field. Inclusion was limited to peer-reviewed, English-language articles examining entropy measures in relation to neural, cognitive, emotional, or behavioral processes, while studies applying entropy exclusively to unrelated biomedical contexts were excluded.

Entropy and neural complexity

A growing body of research has identified the entropy of electroencephalographic (EEG) data as a valuable indicator of brain health and function (Keshmiri, 2020, Garrett et al., 2013). Originating in thermodynamics, entropy has been adapted to describe biological systems, where it broadly refers to the degree of disorder, randomness, or unpredictability in a system (Costa et al., 2005). In neuroscience, higher entropy is thought to reflect more flexible and adaptable information processing and has been proposed as a fundamental property of conscious experience. This neural complexity plays a vital role in the brain's capacity for adaptation, while a loss of complexity is associated with aging and disease (Keshmiri, 2020). Consequently, entropy has emerged as a powerful tool for quantifying neural complexity and information processing across multiple fields of neuroscientific research.

Multiscale entropy (MSE)

Traditional entropy algorithms, such as Sample Entropy (SampEn), proved to have limitations when applied to physiological signals. SampEn measures the probability that matching sequences of data points remain similar as their length increases, but single-scale measures like it fail to account for the multiple time scales inherent in healthy biological systems. To address this, Costa et al. (2002) introduced multiscale entropy (MSE), a method that quantifies complexity across a range of time scales. The MSE algorithm involves two steps: first, a "coarse-graining" procedure (progressively averaging data points to create time series at longer scales) generates a set of time series representing system dynamics at different scales; second, sample entropy is calculated for each of these time series (Humeau-Heurtier, 2015).

The utility of this multiscale approach is well-established. In a classic demonstration, single-scale entropy measures of heart rate time series data were indistinguishable between healthy individuals and those with congestive heart failure. However, MSE analysis revealed significantly higher complexity across multiple time scales in the healthy group (Costa & Healey, 2003). This highlights how measures of irregularity best capture true biological complexity when applied across multiple scales. This principle holds true for brain signals as well, where MSE consistently indicates that aging and disease are associated with a loss of complexity, resolving contradictions found in certain studies using single-scale measures (Costa et al., 2005). The distinct MSE curve profiles generated under different conditions suggest diagnostic value, reflecting how physiological systems are regulated by interacting mechanisms across multiple temporal scales.

Methodological considerations

Despite its utility, MSE has methodological challenges (Humeau-Heurtier, 2015). Thus, the translation of MSE from a theoretical concept to a reliable clinical biomarker necessitates addressing several key methodological considerations to ensure rigor and replicability. For instance, using a fixed pattern-matching threshold (r) can introduce a scale-dependent bias; a proposed solution is to use scale-variant bounds that keep the threshold proportional across all scales (Kosciessa et al., 2020). Moreover, the choice of parameters such as pattern length (m), tolerance (r), and scale factors varies widely across studies, complicating direct comparisons. Standardizing these parameters, or at minimum, reporting them transparently with justification, is critical for enabling cross-study reproducibility. For example, a pattern length of m = 2 and tolerance r = 0.2 times the standard deviation of the signal are most commonly used (Wesley et al., 2025), but deviations from these conventions should be clearly rationalized to facilitate integration with existing literature. Furthermore, variants like Fuzzy Entropy (FuzzEn), which uses fuzzy logic (gradual membership functions rather than rigid binary cutoffs), have been developed to reduce the impact of arbitrary parameters. The application of multiscale analysis to these refined methods, such as Multiscale FuzzEn, retains the benefit of assessing complexity across multiple temporal resolutions (Cataldo et al., 2024).

For practical application, open-source toolkits like EntropyHub provide researchers with a comprehensive suite of functions to estimate various forms of entropy, helping to navigate the growing "entropy universe" (Flood and Grimm, 2021). This 'entropy universe' is populated by a heterogeneous array of measures, each with unique parameters and underlying assumptions. While this diversity offers tailored tools for specific research questions, it also presents a significant challenge for clinical translation, where standardization is key. Future research must prioritize systematic comparisons of these methods to clarify which measures and parameter settings are most reliable and valid for assessing emotion regulation and resilience. Though many approaches exist, the demonstrated utility of MSE, or a variation thereof, in capturing the dynamics of biological systems makes it a particularly strong candidate for continued refinement and focus in the pursuit of a clinical biomarker.

Methodological rigor also extends to data preprocessing. Recent work comparing common EEG analysis pipelines suggests that many automated correction steps can fail to improve or even decrease performance, leading to the recommendation that EEG data may be "better left minimally perturbed" to retain its integrity (Delorme, 2023). Other authors have also provided guidelines for establishing an adequate number of data points for conducting reliable MSE analyses (Richman and Moorman, 2000). Adhering to rigorous standards and being aware of such pitfalls is crucial for generating accurate insights into neural dynamics given the reproducibility and standardization issues that plague EEG research.

Neurophysiological basis of MSE

The MSE profile is more than a mathematical abstraction, it serves as a neurophysiological fingerprint that reflects distinct modes of information processing. In brain signal analysis, a consensus has emerged in the literature whereby MSE is thought to capture both local and global information processing (Kaur et al., 2021, Courtiol et al., 2016). Complexity at fine time scales (e.g., scales 1–4) is thought to primarily reflect the dynamics of local, high-frequency neuronal activity (e.g., beta, gamma) within circumscribed neural populations and, to a lesser extent, slower frequencies (Jaworska et al., 2018). In contrast, complexity at coarse time scales (e.g., scales >10) is associated with the integration of information across large-scale neural networks, a process mediated by lower-frequency oscillations such as delta, theta and alpha (Niknazar et al., 2022).

Wang et al. (2018) offered converging cross-modal evidence that coarse-scale MSE tracks large-scale network integration. Across three complementary datasets, including neural simulations, simultaneous local field potential (LFP)-fMRI in rats, and Human Connectome Project (HCP) resting-state fMRI, they found that higher MSE at slow, coarse time-scales correlated positively with stronger FC, whereas the association weakened or reversed at faster, fine scales. Because the LFP–fMRI data demonstrated this link in real time, the authors argued that coarse-scale MSE reflects the same integrative neural dynamics that give rise to distributed BOLD networks. However, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to human scalp EEG recordings, as LFP measurements in animals capture invasive, high-resolution signals from specific neural populations, whereas scalp EEG reflects broader, non-invasive signals attenuated by the skull and scalp. Although these findings provide cross-modal evidence linking greater temporal complexity in regional electrical activity to enhanced global BOLD network integration, further research is needed to confirm this relationship in human EEG studies.

McDonough and Nashiro (2014) analyzed HCP rs-fMRI and found a complimentary pattern whereby lower MSE at fine scales correlated with stronger local connectivity and higher MSE at coarse scales correlated with greater large-scale integration, most prominently in the default-mode network (DMN). Imperatori et al. (2019) later showed that individuals with elevated trait anxiety exhibit both reduced DMN EEG connectivity. Together, these studies suggest that across LFP, fMRI and EEG, diminished coarse-scale complexity, alongside weakened network integration, marks a less flexible neural system and may index vulnerability to anxiety. These findings further support the role of MSE as a window into large-scale integrative brain dynamics associated with emotional disorders.

Complimenting scale-wise entropy measures, recent advances in EEG microstate analysis provide additional insights into temporal dynamics of brain activity, which may be particularly relevant to disorders involving emotional dysregulation. Microstates represent brief, quasi-stable patterns of electrical activity that are thought to represent the ‘atoms of thought’, or the brain’s basic building blocks of information processing (Bréchet and Michel, 2022). These rapid shifts in neural activity provide a granular window into the disorganization often seen in psychiatric conditions (Michel and Koenig, 2018). For instance, Raeisi et al. (2025) investigated EEG microstate biomarkers for schizophrenia, a disorder characterized by aberrant neural complexity and impaired emotional processing, and reported classification accuracies of 98.31 % with a deep neural network and 99.94 % with a random forest classifier when distinguishing patients with schizophrenia from healthy controls.

Building on this, Lashaki et al. (2025) applied EEG microstate analysis to identify potential biomarkers in trigeminal neuralgia, a neuropathic pain disorder associated with emotion regulation challenges. The authors achieved 91.9 % classification accuracy with a support vector machine (SVM) using extract EEG microstate features. Similarly, Raeisi et al. (2025) examined EEG microstates in tinnitus, often comorbid with anxiety a depression, reporting 100 % classification accuracy versus healthy controls using a hybrid deep learning and machine learning pipeline. The findings indicated abnormal beta and gamma band EEG microstate dynamics as reliable biomarkers in tinnitus patients. Finally, Rahmati et al. (2025) combined EEG microstate and entropy features in major depressive disorder, achieving 99.60 % classification accuracy with deep learning techniques using entropy features and 96.96 % accuracy with microstate features. Together, these findings highlight how microstate alterations capture pathological neural dynamics, potentially extending entropy findings by quantifying temporal instability. Integrating microstate analysis with entropy could enhance biomarker development for emotion regulation, especially in transdiagnostic contexts where rigidity or chaos in brain states underlies resilience deficits.

Optimal complexity

The concept of optimal complexity posits that healthy brain function exists in a balanced state—neither too predictable (low complexity) nor too random (high complexity) (Bornas and de la Torre-Luque, 2016). This balance is considered essential for adaptive cognitive and emotional processes. Challenging the traditional view of health as simple stability, chaos theory asserts that even seemingly random systems can be governed by deterministic rules (Bornas and de la Torre-Luque, 2016). Optimal complexity, therefore, reflects a system that maximizes adaptive benefits by operating in a critical zone between order and disorder, a concept reinforced by the Entropic Brain Hypothesis (Carhart-Harris, 2018). Entropy measures thus provide a quantitative index of the brain's adaptive functioning, capable of bridging the divide between physiological processes and subjective experience.

A growing body of research demonstrates that healthy brain function occurs when neural complexity exists within a “critical zone” and falls off when the system drifts towards lower levels of entropy (rigidity) and higher levels of entropy (randomness). Criticality confers functional advantages by maximizing a system’s information processing capacity and efficiency, striking an optimal balance between adaptability and stability (Carhart-Harris, 2018, Shew and Plenz, 2013). Across the lifespan, MSE follows an inverted-U trajectory whereby complexity rises from childhood to early adulthood, plateaus in mid-life, and then declines with ageing, a pattern that mirrors the developmental arc of cognitive flexibility and executive function (Cacciotti et al., 2024). Further, aberrant entropy levels have been demonstrated in pathological conditions. Reduced complexity has been observed in autism spectrum disorder (Liu et al., 2017, Catarino et al., 2011), ADHD (Angulo-Ruiz et al., 2023, Gu et al., 2022), and Alzheimer’s disease (Akbar et al., 2025). In contrast, elevated complexity has been observed in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (Hernández et al., 2023). These divergent findings suggest that psychopathology may manifest at both extremes of the complexity spectrum, with low entropy reflecting rigid neural dynamics and high entropy indicating chaotic, under-constrained processing. For example, reduced complexity in autism or ADHD may stem from impaired network integration, limiting adaptive responses, whereas elevated complexity in schizophrenia or severe GAD could arise from disrupted inhibitory mechanisms, leading to excessive neural noise. These distinct “entropy profiles” highlight the need for context-specific considerations when interpreting complexity biomarkers. While resting-state complexity has demonstrated group-level patterns across diagnoses, there is less consensus regarding what constitutes optimal neural complexity during task engagement, with findings varying by context, cognitive demands, and individual differences.

Neural complexity, cognitive flexibility, and emotion regulation

Cognitive flexibility, the ability to adapt one's thinking in response to new or unexpected events, is a fundamental component of healthy psychological functioning (Gabrys et al., 2018). MSE measures are emerging as a key indicator of this adaptive capacity, which is central to effective emotion regulation.

Mechanisms linking neural complexity and cognitive flexibility

Neural complexity can be understood as representing the brain's capacity to explore a wide range of neural states (Liu et al., 2024). Access to a broader repertoire of brain network configurations is thought to facilitate more appropriate and flexible responses to environmental demands. Supporting this, studies have shown that higher levels of modified MSE are associated with greater cognitive flexibility, predicting more liberal shifts in decision-making biases (Kloosterman et al., 2019). This suggests that increased complexity reflects greater neural flexibility, allowing for the consideration of more diverse perspectives. This link is further substantiated by findings that both heart rate variability (HRV) and the variability of EEG functional connectivity are directly correlated with cognitive flexibility (Alba et al., 2019). The relationship between HRV and cognitive flexibility appears to be mediated by the variability of the brain's functional connectivity dynamics, providing strong support for complexity as a direct index of cognitive flexibility.

Mechanistically, neural entropy may foster cognitive flexibility by enabling the brain to operate in a state of criticality, where information processing is optimized by a balance of integration and segregation, allowing for efficient transitions between neural states mediated by frontoparietal networks (Uddin, 2021). Specifically, entropy may modulate PFC-limbic interactions by reducing top-down suppression of rigid emotional prior beliefs, thereby promoting adaptive reconfiguration of emotional responses, a concept encapsulated in the Relaxed Beliefs Under pSychedelics (REBUS) model (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019). Similar entropy increases and PFC-limbic decoupling have been observed in mindfulness practices, proposed to support flexible emotion regulation (Vivot et al., 2020, Taren et al., 2015). Interestingly, research into psychedelic states supports this idea by demonstrating relationships between increases in entropy and enhanced cognitive flexibility, including the relaxation and revision of negative self-beliefs (Zeifman et al., 2025). Further, Singleton et al. (2022) used neural states derived from fMRI to show that psychedelics alter brain dynamics, including serotonin 2a (5-HT2a) receptor agonism and entropy, consistent with REBUS. This evidence supports the broader Entropic Brain Hypothesis, which posits that higher entropy generates a richer repertoire of neural states, enabling the revision of rigid belief patterns and ultimately increasing resilience as observed in treatment-resistant depression (Daws et al., 2022).

The brain's adaptive capacity, indexed by neural complexity, also appears to support other higher-order cognitive functions. For instance, creativity, which relies on flexible thinking, is positively correlated with regional brain entropy in areas crucial for cognitive control, such as the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) (Shi et al., 2020). Similarly, intelligence has been positively associated with brain entropy, with the strongest relationships found in the prefrontal cortex, inferior temporal lobes, and cerebellum (Saxe et al., 2018). This suggests that access to a more variable set of neural states enables adaptive, intelligent behavior by affording greater cognitive flexibility.

The role of cognitive flexibility in emotion regulation

Cognitive control and flexibility are inextricably linked to emotion regulation; disruptions in these functions are a common feature of mood and anxiety disorders (Gabrys et al., 2018). Individuals with psychological disorders often exhibit inflexible emotion regulation, using strategies like cognitive reappraisal less frequently than their healthy counterparts (Zhang et al., 2022). Conversely, a high sense of self-efficacy in emotion regulation is associated with more frequent use of cognitive reappraisal, indicating greater cognitive flexibility (Schroder et al., 2015). As such, cognitive flexibility is widely accepted as a core mechanism of effective emotion regulation (Zhang et al., 2022).

The close relationship between cognitive flexibility and emotion regulation points to overlapping neural mechanisms, with a critical role for the prefrontal cortex (PFC). A systematic review by Morawetz and Basten (2024) provides compelling evidence, concluding that successful emotion regulation, whether measured as a transient state, a stable trait, or by physiological markers like skin conductance and HRV, is consistently associated with increased activity in the lateral PFC. Pinpointing the PFC as the central hub for these overlapping processes is therefore crucial, as it provides a clear and neurobiologically-justified anatomical target for biomarker research on emotion regulation and resilience using region-specific EEG analysis. Although the previous evidence robustly links neural complexity, cognitive flexibility, and emotion regulation, its predominantly correlational nature limits causal inferences. It remains unclear whether neural complexity drives flexibility and regulation or vice versa. Future research should employ experimental designs to establish causal pathways and inform interventions for emotion regulation and resilience (Danks, Davis, 2023).

Neural complexity in emotion dysregulation and the potential for intervention

When the adaptive processes of cognitive flexibility are impaired, individuals may struggle to regulate their emotional responses. This emotional dysregulation is a transdiagnostic feature prevalent in many forms of psychopathology, including mood and anxiety disorders. A well-supported framework for understanding the neurobiology of these conditions is the “complexity-loss” hypothesis, which posits that a reduction in neural complexity underlies impaired adaptability (de la Torre-Luque et al., 2016, Lipsitz and Goldberger, 1992). However, it is important to note that this relationship is not always linear, as some findings suggest a more nuanced association between symptom severity and neural complexity (Li et al., 2019). The following will explore how altered neural dynamics manifest specifically in anxiety disorders and also discuss how interventions aimed at promoting self-regulation may function by restoring neural complexity.

Altered neural complexity in anxiety

Anxiety disorders, characterized by persistent apprehension, have been linked to altered neural dynamics (Li et al., 2019, Imperatori et al., 2019). A meta-analysis spanning cardiac, EEG, and hormonal measures found that anxious individuals consistently show lower physiological complexity compared to healthy controls (de la Torre-Luque et al., 2016). This reduction in complexity is believed to impair flexible adaptation to environmental demands and may explain the cognitive rigidity often seen in anxiety disorders. Additional research on generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) has reinforced this, finding significantly decreased neural complexity in key cognitive control regions, including the frontoparietal network and the dlPFC (Li et al., 2019). Research into trait anxiety has also revealed alterations in network dynamics, with higher trait anxiety being negatively correlated with DMN functional connectivity during rest (Imperatori et al., 2019). This finding, combined with evidence linking coarse-scale complexity to DMN connectivity, suggests that both could serve as important neurobiological markers of anxiety.

As noted, the relationship is not always linear. One study found that among GAD patients, those with more severe symptoms paradoxically exhibited higher complexity, suggesting a more nuanced relationship between complexity and brain health than simple complexity loss (Li et al., 2019). This implies that pathology exists at both extremes; a rigid, low-complexity state and a noisy, chaotic, high-complexity state, with health residing in an optimal, intermediate range (Bornas and de la Torre-Luque, 2016). Notably, this non-linearity makes biomarker development more challenging as low complexity may reflect impaired integration and high complexity, excessive neural noise. Yet, this nuance also underscores the potential to capture transdiagnostic profiles of dysregulation. To enhance biomarker validity, future research must clarify these divergent entropy profiles by integrating task-based EEG measures, longitudinal designs, and advanced computational models to identify optimal complexity thresholds (Gu et al., 2024).

Mindfulness interventions and neural complexity

Interventions designed to enhance emotional self-regulation may function by restoring or enhancing neural complexity. Somatic-mindfulness interventions like the Community Resiliency Model (CRM) focus on training awareness of bodily sensations and physiological signals (e.g., interoceptive cues, breath, muscle tension) as anchors for regulation. These techniques are designed to promote wellness and emotional stabilization by activating the interoceptive network via the insula, a key hub for emotion regulation (Grabbe et al., 2023, Miller-Karas, 2015). While the direct impact of CRM on complexity is not yet well understood, research on related mindfulness practices offers a potential mechanism.

For example, higher dispositional mindfulness has been associated with increased EEG complexity, suggesting that such practices may bolster the flexibility of neural networks (Aguerre et al., 2023). Further, Vivot et al. (2020) found that vipassana (open monitoring) meditation practices increased neural entropy, particularly in the alpha and low gamma frequency bands. These finding suggests that mindfulness practice may enhance the flexibility and adaptability of neural networks, hinting at a potential mechanism through which CRM may contribute to emotion regulation. However, the literature is not entirely consistent, with some studies reporting both increases and decreases in complexity during meditation (Deolindo et al., 2020). These discrepancies likely stem from variations in meditation type (e.g. focused attention vs open monitoring), task demands, and participant expertise. Future research would benefit from standardized task-based EEG protocols and machine learning methods to identify practice-specific entropy profiles. Such approaches can clarify whether different practices restore optimal complexity, informing precise interventions for emotion regulation and resilience.

Entropy modulation as a dynamic marker of emotion regulation

While much of the research has focused on static levels of entropy, a paradigm shift towards examining the dynamics of entropy modulation between different states (e.g., from stress to recovery), offers a far more powerful and ecologically valid lens for assessing emotion regulation. This framework allows for the assessment of how flexibly the brain adapts to changing contexts and is crucial for understanding the mechanisms of emotion regulation. Real-time, dynamic assessment is becoming increasingly feasible through the integration of wearable EEG with immersive VR and AR, enabling measurement of neural activity during ecologically valid tasks (Tadayyoni et al., 2024, Tauscher et al., 2019). Insights from cardiac complexity

The Neurovisceral Integration Model (NVM) provides a strong conceptual bridge from cardiac to neural complexity, highlighting the heart-brain connection in adaptive self-regulation (Thayer & Lane, 2000). The NVM posits that lower complexity in the interplay between the central and autonomic nervous systems is associated with less adaptability and more emotional dysregulation (Fiskum et al., 2019). Research has validated this, showing that higher cardiac entropy (SampEn) predicts fewer emotional problems in children and is a better predictor of emotional dysregulation than traditional linear HRV measures (Fiskum et al., 2017).

In a follow-up study on reactive cardiac entropy, Fiskum et al. (2019) showed that dynamic changes in entropy are a highly sensitive marker of emotion regulation. The researchers found that adaptive emotion regulation is associated with an increase in cardiac entropy following a stressor, such as when listening to relaxing music after a stress induction. This modulation reflects the heart's adaptive response to stressors, a principle that the NVM suggests may be related to neural processes as well.

From cardiac to neural entropy modulation

The NVM establishes a foundation for extending cardiac to neural entropy modulation and is complemented by the Entropic Brain Hypothesis, positing that higher neural entropy enables flexible state transitions, supporting adaptive regulation (Carhart-Harris, 2018). Criticality principles further suggest that optimal complexity balances integration and segregation, facilitating dynamic responses (Bornas & de la Torre-Luque, 2016). Together, these frameworks justify applying cardiac modulation insights to the brain.

Abnormal neural entropy modulation has been observed in psychopathologies like schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, as well as in aging, suggesting it is a robust marker of impaired neural adaptability (Molina et al., 2018, Molina et al., 2020, Xiang et al., 2019, Alù et al., 2021). Supporting this view, a recent study by Gu et al. (2024) explored EEG MSE during an emotion conflict resolution task and found that psychiatric patients exhibited lower MSE, particularly at coarse scales in the left frontal region, compared to healthy controls. These findings extend the insights from cardiac research to the brain, suggesting that individuals with impaired emotion regulation exhibit diminished task-based modulation of neural complexity. The convergence of evidence from cardiac physiology, the entropic brain hypothesis, and criticality principles, alongside initial EEG findings, makes a compelling case for exploring the modulation of neural entropy as a neurobiological marker of emotion regulation and resilience..

Future directions and conclusions

This review synthesizes emerging evidence suggesting that EEG entropy modulation may serve as a promising candidate biomarker for emotion regulation and resilience. MSE offers a theoretically grounded method for quantifying neural complexity, a property fundamentally linked to the cognitive flexibility that underpins effective emotion regulation, with the PFC serving as a critical neural hub. Alterations in neural complexity are evident in emotion dysregulation and related disorders with entropy modulation, reflecting dynamic changes between cognitive-emotional states, emerging as a particularly powerful and nuanced framework for investigation. To advance the field, we propose a prioritized roadmap of research directions, focusing on those with the greatest potential to bridge gaps in understanding and transform clinical practice. These directions are ordered by their immediacy, feasibility, and anticipated impact, with specific methodological recommendations to guide future work.

Task-based entropy modulation

There is a clear need to investigate task-based entropy modulation as a direct proxy for emotion regulation and resilience. Task-based neural complexity has been shown to outperform both self-report and resting-state measures in predicting therapeutic outcomes, maintaining its predictive accuracy over time (Månsson et al., 2022). Examining how neural complexity changes in the PFC during targeted regulatory tasks or interventions could provide a sensitive, region-specific marker of adaptive neural function. For example, consider a task-based EEG protocol assessing emotion regulation in patients who undergo a standardized stressor, such as the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST), which has been used extensively in research to investigate the effects of stress on mental health disorders (Birkett, 2011), followed by one minute in which individuals engage in their preferred emotion regulation strategy. EEG entropy from the PFC would be recorded during both phases, capturing the modulation of entropy during the recovery phase relative to the stress-induced phase. This protocol could even be adopted towards custom VR-EEG paradigms that provide condition-specific stressors that simulate real-world emotional challenges (Kim et al., 2024). Such designs could clarify how entropy modulation tracks within-person resilience in real-time.

Interventions that enhance neural complexity

A range of interventions targeting emotion regulation, from somatic-mindfulness practices to emerging psychedelic-assisted therapies, hold promise for enhancing neural complexity in regulatory networks, offering novel pathways to restore adaptive brain function. Traditional mindfulness practices have shown preliminary evidence of increasing neural complexity, while recent studies suggest psychedelics, such as psilocybin, may exhibit its therapeutic effects by increasing entropy, thereby promoting neuroplasticity that can lead to more flexible thinking and emotional breakthroughs (Smausz et al., 2022). Conducting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing somatic-mindfulness interventions to active controls in populations with emotion dysregulation (e.g. anxiety, PTSD) could uncover the neural mechanisms driving the therapeutic effects of such interventions, testing whether they work by restoring or enhancing the complexity of brain activity in key regulatory networks.

AI-assisted precision neuropsychology

Finally, integrating advanced computational methods and artificial intelligence (AI) is a critical frontier that pushes a needed interdisciplinary agenda in clinical neuropsychology (Lundervold, 2025). The multidimensional nature of MSE data is well-suited for machine learning algorithms, which have already shown promise in classifying mental states based on EEG features (Fang et al., 2024, Shen et al., 2022). An emerging trend is the successful use of SVM and convolutional neural networks (CNN) for this purpose (Ancillon et al., 2022). Future work should leverage these approaches to move towards a framework of precision neuropsychology. For instance, machine learning models could extract entropy-based features from EEG signals to identify distinct 'entropy profiles' of emotional dysregulation, predict which patients are most likely to respond to specific interventions, and dynamically track treatment response with a level of objectivity current methods lack. In practice, entropy profiles might be derived by classifying multiscale entropy patterns across key neural hubs to differentiate resilient from dysregulated emotional responses. Predictive models could then be trained on baseline signatures to forecast which individuals are most likely to benefit from a given intervention. Finally, deep learning models could be applied to longitudinal entropy measures, providing feedback on whether brain dynamics are normalizing as treatment progresses.

These approaches highlight important directions forward, but they also bring challenges for clinical translation. Entropy metrics are sensitive to preprocessing choices and EEG system variability, making standardization across equipment and analytic pipelines essential for reproducibility. Clinicians will also require specialized training to interpret entropy metrics alongside more traditional neuropsychological constructs, while practical considerations such as cost-effectiveness and scalability must be addressed before widespread adoption is feasible. Progress in these areas will be crucial to move entropy modulation from a promising research tool to a clinically actionable biomarker. With such advances, continued innovation at the intersection of neuroscience, psychology, and AI holds the potential to deliver more sophisticated and precise tools, bridging the gap between subjective experience and objective physiology and transforming how we assess and enhance emotional well-being and resilience.
