Evaluation of Drug–Drug Interaction Potential Between the Oral Antibiotic Zoliflodacin and the CYP3A4 Inhibitor Itraconazole: A Phase 1 Study in Healthy Participants
Authors: Alison Luckey, Kajal B. Larson, Noha Rayad, Markus Heep, Sophie Delhomme, Gabrielle Kornmann, John P. Mueller, John P. O'Donnell, Seamus O'Brien, Rainard Fuhr, Jean‐Yves Gillon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTZoliflodacin is a first‐in‐class oral spiropyrimidinetrione antibiotic being developed for patients with uncomplicated gonorrhea, including those infected with multidrug‐resistant strains. Because zoliflodacin is metabolized by cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), concomitant administration with a CYP3A inhibitor has the potential to increase zoliflodacin plasma exposure. The aim of this phase 1 drug–drug interaction (DDI) study was to assess the effect of the strong CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole on the pharmacokinetics (PK) and safety of a 3 g single oral dose of zoliflodacin. This was an open label, 2‐period, 2‐treatment, fixed sequence crossover DDI study. Eighteen eligible adult participants were dosed and completed the study. When zoliflodacin was co‐administered with itraconazole at steady state, as determined by itraconazole trough plasma concentrations, zoliflodacin exposure increased as measured by peak concentration (Cmax) by 1.03‐fold and by area under the plasma‐concentration time curve (AUC) from time 0 to last measurable plasma concentration (AUC0‐last) and from time 0 extrapolated to infinity (AUC0‐∞) by 1.39‐ and 1.38‐fold, respectively. Eight participants (44.4%) had a total of 15 mild treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), with three considered related to treatment. There were no deaths or serious TEAEs and no participant withdrew. No clinically significant abnormal laboratory values, vital signs, or electrocardiogram findings were reported. In conclusion, the Cmax of zoliflodacin was essentially unchanged (1.03‐fold higher) when co‐administered with a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, while AUC exposures increased by less than 1.5‐fold, which, combined with an acceptable safety profile, indicate a low DDI potential between zoliflodacin and CYP3A4 inhibitors.

Introduction

Gonorrhea is the second most common notifiable sexually transmitted infection (STI) in the United States (US) with 601,319 cases reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 2023 [1]. However, due to factors such as asymptomatic infections these rates are likely to be underreported; and about 1.6 million new infections were estimated in the US in 2018 [2, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated a worldwide incidence of 19 per 1000 women and 23 per 1000 men in 2020 [4]. STIs such as gonorrhea can increase the risk of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) acquisition [5], and at‐risk people living with HIV have a particularly high prevalence of gonorrhea [6].

The standard of care treatment for uncomplicated gonorrhea is a single dose intramuscular injection of ceftriaxone with lidocaine [2], which must be administered in the clinic. Zoliflodacin is a first‐in‐class oral spiropyrimidinetrione antibiotic being developed for individuals with uncomplicated gonorrhea, including those infected with multidrug‐resistant (MDR) strains [7]. A single oral dose treatment potentially improves patient access by reducing the need for skilled healthcare resources and provides ease of administration and improved tolerability. By addressing the limitations of the current available therapy, zoliflodacin could lower barriers for patients seeking treatment for themselves and their sexual partners, and for those with an aversion to needles, or a β‐lactam allergy.

Over time, clinical isolates of Neisseria gonorrhoeae , the pathogen that causes gonorrhea, have become resistant to most classes of antibiotics recommended for treatment [8, 9]. In 2018, in the US, around half of gonorrhea strains demonstrated resistance or elevated minimum inhibitory concentrations to antibiotics [3]. Some isolates have also been shown to be MDR organisms [10, 11]. The worldwide public health threat of increasing prevalence of antibiotic‐resistant N. gonorrhoeae , including strains resistant to cephalosporins, necessitates development of new classes of effective antibiotics as an urgent priority [12]. This increase in MDR strains is a major concern because, if not treated successfully, gonorrhea can have serious sequelae in women and men, including pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy, infertility, and disseminated infections which can result in cardiovascular and neurological problems [13]. Accordingly, new treatment options that are effective against N. gonorrhoeae are urgently needed [14].

Importantly, zoliflodacin has a novel mechanism of action and has demonstrated in vitro activity against MDR gonococcal isolates [15, 16]. The safety, tolerability, efficacy, pharmacokinetic (PK), and food effect potential of zoliflodacin have been evaluated and established in previous clinical studies [17, 18, 19]. Recent results from a large phase 3 trial also demonstrated non‐inferiority of a single, oral 3 g dose of zoliflodacin compared to the combination of standard‐of‐care intramuscular injection of ceftriaxone 500 mg and administration of oral azithromycin 1 g. Treatment with zoliflodacin was generally well tolerated with no serious adverse events or deaths [20, 21].

Zoliflodacin is extensively metabolized via cytochrome P450 (CYP) and non‐CYP‐mediated mechanisms in the liver and reductive metabolism in the gut in non‐clinical studies [22]. In vitro, the CYP‐mediated metabolism is predominately via the CYP3A4/5 enzyme (~70%) with lesser contributions from CYP1A2, 2C9, 2C8, and 2C19 (unpublished data). Physiologically based PK (PBPK) modeling predicted that the median fraction metabolized by CYP3A4 is 0.31 (unpublished data). These data suggest that concomitant administration of a CYP3A inhibitor with zoliflodacin in humans may lead to potential drug–drug interactions (DDIs) with an increase in zoliflodacin plasma exposure [18]. This may be relevant for those living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) who are co‐infected with gonorrhea and may be taking, as anti‐retrovirals, protease inhibitors or certain non‐nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors that are CYP3A inhibitors [23]. Consistent with the M12 Drug Interaction Studies guidance [24], whereby a clinical study is needed when an enzyme is responsible for at least 25% of the total elimination of the drug, a clinical DDI study was conducted to evaluate the effect of the co‐administration of itraconazole, a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, on the PK and safety profile of zoliflodacin in healthy participants and to support future prescribing information.

Methods

Study Design

This study was a phase 1, single‐dose, open‐label study in healthy participants designed with 2 parallel cohorts. Cohort 1 was designed as a bioequivalence study to compare 2 manufactured products of zoliflodacin granules for oral suspension under fasting and fed conditions [25]. Cohort 2 (presented herein) was a 2‐period, 2‐treatment, fixed‐sequence crossover DDI study enrolling 18 healthy adult participants. The aim of this cohort was to evaluate the effect of multiple doses of itraconazole on the PK of a single 3 g oral dose of zoliflodacin. The study was registered in EudraCT (number: 2020–000379‐19) and was conducted at Parexel International, Early Phase Clinical Unit, Berlin, Germany.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonization Guideline for Good Clinical Practice. The study protocol and written informed consent were approved (approval number: 22/131—IV E 10) by the Berlin State Office for Health and Social Affairs, Office of the Berlin State Ethics Committee.

Study Objectives

The main objective of the DDI study was to evaluate the effect of steady state exposures of itraconazole on the PK of a single 3 g oral dose of zoliflodacin under fasted conditions. The primary PK endpoints included changes in zoliflodacin exposure parameters area under the curve (AUC) from time 0 extrapolated to infinity (AUC0‐∞) and peak concentration (Cmax). The geometric mean ratio (GMR) with associated 90% confidence intervals (CIs) of the observed PK zoliflodacin exposure measures with and without itraconazole were calculated. The trough concentrations (Ctrough) of itraconazole on Days 4–11 were also assessed.

The safety and tolerability of zoliflodacin with and without itraconazole was also evaluated.

Participants

Healthy males and females not of childbearing potential, aged 18–55 years of age, who provided written informed consent to participate in the study were included. Healthy status was determined by meeting all the inclusion and none of the exclusion criteria based on assessment of medical/surgical history, vital signs, physical examination, standard 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG), and laboratory investigations. Body mass index (BMI) was between 18.0 and 30.0 kg/m2 and weight was at least 50 kg and not more than 95 kg inclusive, at screening. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Table S1.

Interventions

This study (cohort 2) was conducted in two distinct periods with a fixed sequence as shown in Figure 1, the first beginning on Day 1 when participants received a single 3 g oral dose of zoliflodacin, administered under fasting conditions. After a washout of 72 h after dosing of zoliflodacin, on Day 4, participants received a 400 mg loading dose of itraconazole followed thereafter by 200 mg of itraconazole once daily. From Day 4 to Day 8, itraconazole was administered immediately after a full meal. On Day 9, both itraconazole and zoliflodacin were administered under fasting conditions at −1 h and 0 h, respectively. Itraconazole was then administered with food 24 h (Day 10) and 48 h (Day 11) after administration of zoliflodacin.

Zoliflodacin drug–drug interaction study schedule. On Day 1 a single 3 g oral dose of zoliflodacin was administered under fasting conditions. Itraconazole was then administered with food 24 h (Day 10) and 48 h (Day 11) after administration of zoliflodacin.

Trough concentrations of itraconazole from Day 4 to Day 11 were determined to verify that steady state was reached. A 3 g single oral dose of zoliflodacin was selected to better understand potential DDIs at a clinically relevant dose level [20].

Evaluations

Venous blood samples, 4 mL each, were collected into labeled polyethylene terephthalate tubes containing dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (K2EDTA) and were gently inverted several times. Within 45 min of collection, blood samples were centrifuged at approximately 2700 g at room temperature for 10 min. Thereafter, the supernatant of each sample was divided into 2 aliquots (of at least 0.5 mL each), transferred to labeled polypropylene tubes and frozen within 90 min of collection. Plasma samples were stored at approximately −80°C in a temperature monitored freezer.

Determination of zoliflodacin and itraconazole concentrations in plasma samples was completed by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry using a method validated by KCAS Bio LLC (KS, US), according to internationally accepted standards. The lower limits of quantification of the zoliflodacin and itraconazole assays were 10.00 ng/mL.

On Day 1, blood samples were collected at pre‐dose and at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 9.0, 12.0, 24.0, and 48.0 h post‐dose. Blood samples for analysis of itraconazole concentrations were collected pre‐dose on Days 4 through 8. On Day 9, a pre‐dose PK sample was collected before administration of itraconazole (1 h before administration of zoliflodacin). Further blood samples were collected post‐dose relative to zoliflodacin dosing at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0, 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, 9.0, 12.0, 24.0 (Day 10), 48.0 (Day 11), and 72.0 h (Day 12).

Adverse events (AEs)/serious adverse events, vital signs (heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure), physical examination, tympanic temperature, 12‐lead ECG, and laboratory investigations (hematology, coagulation, clinical chemistry, urinalysis, and pregnancy tests) were performed throughout the study. Prior and concomitant medications were monitored.

After the first zoliflodacin administration, AEs were considered as treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs). AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, version 26.0.

Statistical Analysis

PK parameters were calculated from the plasma concentration profiles using noncompartmental analysis methods. AUC and half‐life (t1/2) were calculated using the log‐linear trapezoidal rule, using Phoenix WinNonlin software Version 8.3 (Certara, Princeton, NJ).

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). To assess the effect of co‐administration of itraconazole on the PK of zoliflodacin in plasma, natural log‐transformed primary plasma PK parameters (AUC from the first observed to last measurable plasma concentration [AUC0‐last], AUC0‐∞, and Cmax) were analyzed using a linear mixed model analysis of variance with treatment (zoliflodacin + itraconazole [Test] vs. zoliflodacin alone [Reference]) as a fixed effect and participant as a random effect. The estimated least square (LS) means difference (Test—Reference) and its 90% CI on the log‐scale was constructed.

For AUC0‐last, AUC0‐∞ and Cmax, an estimate of the adjusted GMR (Test/Reference) was calculated by exponentiation of the difference between estimated LS means. Similarly, the 90% CI of the estimated means difference was back transformed to obtain the results on the original scale (i.e., to calculate the 90% CI of the GMR).

Descriptive summaries were used for all safety data (AEs, vital signs, physical examination, ECGs, and safety laboratory assessments).

In the presence of steady‐state exposures of itraconazole, peak plasma concentration of zoliflodacin was reached with a median time to maximum observed plasma concentration (Tmax) of 3.3 h (range of 2.0 to 5.0 h). This was generally comparable to a median Tmax of 2.5 h (range of 2.0 to 3.5 h) in the absence of itraconazole.

The zoliflodacin elimination t1/2 was longer and apparent clearance (CL/F) was decreased in the presence of itraconazole, when compared with zoliflodacin in the absence of itraconazole, which would be anticipated based on inhibition of metabolic pathways.

As shown in Table 3, itraconazole at steady state increased the zoliflodacin exposure by approximately 1.03‐, 1.39‐, and 1.38‐fold for Cmax, AUC0‐last, and AUC0‐∞, respectively. These data indicate that in the presence of itraconazole steady state concentrations, zoliflodacin Cmax is essentially unchanged while the AUC0‐last and AUC0‐∞ of zoliflodacin are increased by less than 1.5‐fold.

Safety and Tolerability Analysis

A single 3 g oral dose of zoliflodacin was generally well tolerated during the study. All reported TEAEs were of mild severity. The most frequently reported TEAE was headache. Overall, eight participants (44.4%) had 15 TEAEs. Four participants (22.2%) in the zoliflodacin alone treatment period had four TEAEs while eight participants (44.4%) in the zoliflodacin+itraconazole treatment period experienced 11 TEAEs. Three of the TEAEs (two participants with headache during zoliflodacin+itraconazole treatment and one participant with headache during zoliflodacin treatment) were considered study treatment‐related by the investigators. No deaths or serious TEAEs were reported. No participants withdrew from the study. A summary table and a full listing of TEAEs are provided in Tables S2 and S3.

No clinically meaningful changes were observed for any hematology, coagulation, clinical chemistry, or urinalysis parameters. During the study, minor fluctuations from baseline in vital signs values and post‐dose ECG parameters were observed but the changes were not considered clinically significant.

Discussion

The incidence of gonorrhea, the second most reported notifiable bacterial STI in the US, remains high [1, 26]. Between 2009 and 2020, an increase of 111% was documented [27, 28]. The ability of N. gonorrhoeae to rapidly develop antibiotic resistance leads to clinical failure [2, 27], and thus highlights the need for new treatment options for gonorrhea. Zoliflodacin is a spiropyrimidinetrione antibacterial that is being developed as a single oral dose for the treatment of uncomplicated gonorrhea, including infections caused by MDR strains of N. gonorrhoeae . Previous clinical studies in healthy participants and patients with gonorrhea have supported the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of oral doses of zoliflodacin from 2 to 4 g [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 29], including a 3 g dose in a phase 3 clinical study [21].

Nonclinical studies have shown that zoliflodacin is extensively metabolized via CYP‐ and non‐CYP‐mediated mechanisms in the liver and reductive metabolism in the gut [22]. In vitro CYP phenotyping studies suggested that the metabolism of zoliflodacin was primarily via CYP3A4/5 (approximately 68.4% contribution, unpublished data), indicating that zoliflodacin could be an object of CYP3A4‐mediated DDIs. Therefore, a clinical study was performed to characterize the risk of DDI, using a strong index inhibitor of CYP3A4, i.e., itraconazole, according to US Food and Drug Administration guidance [24]. Investigation of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion after single dose administration in healthy participants showed that the major elimination pathway was via metabolism and elimination by the fecal route [18]. That study found 79.6% of the dose was recovered in feces with metabolism contributing 98% of that value [18]. In addition to oxidation by cytochrome P450 in the rodent studies [22], the study in humans suggested reduction and hydrolysis, as well as the involvement of N‐ and O‐dealkylation reactions.

The results from this study are of particular relevance to treatment of gonorrhea in individuals living with HIV who are treated with antiretroviral therapy that exhibits CYP3A4 inhibition. While treatment of HIV infection has advanced beyond many traditional agents as first line therapy [30], protease inhibitors, ritonavir and cobicistat, for example, which are CYP3A inhibitors, are still administered in some situations including as “pharmacokinetic boosters” [31].

In this study, a loading dose of itraconazole (400 mg) was administered on Day 4, followed by 200 mg once daily from Days 5 to 11. This allows faster steady state attainment, compared to more traditional studies with itraconazole that used more standardized dosing. The systemic exposure and t1/2 of zoliflodacin were increased when zoliflodacin was administered with itraconazole, compared to zoliflodacin administration alone, consistent with the inhibition of zoliflodacin metabolism. The statistical analysis showed an increase in systemic exposure of less than 1.5‐fold with a Cmax that was essentially unchanged. Typically, a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor is expected to increase the AUC of a sensitive CYP3A4 clinical index substrate by approximately 5‐fold; therefore, the data in this trial suggests that the metabolism of zoliflodacin involves additional CYP‐ and/or non‐CYP‐mediated pathways. CYP‐mediated metabolism of zoliflodacin and the impact of other CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers will therefore be further evaluated using a PBPK model.

In addition to being a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, itraconazole is also a P‐gp inhibitor. While in vitro studies have shown that zoliflodacin is a substrate of P‐gp and BCRP transporters, the results of this clinical trial do not seem to indicate P‐gp mediated DDI.

Exposures observed in this study were well within those observed in other trials with zoliflodacin [19]. Although the systemic exposure and t1/2 of zoliflodacin were increased in this study when co‐administered with itraconazole, the proposed single dose regimen for the treatment of gonorrhea limits concerns over accumulation.

The number of participants experiencing AEs was low, and the intensity mild for all TEAEs. The types of AEs raised no new safety concerns and were similar to the most common TEAEs reported in previous clinical trials with zoliflodacin in healthy participants and in patients [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. The safety analysis suggested a single 3 g oral dose of zoliflodacin was generally well tolerated during the study. Given the magnitude of the increased exposure of zoliflodacin when co‐administered with itraconazole, the overall safety profile, and its proposed use as a single oral dose, the potential for DDIs with CYP3A4 inhibitors can be considered to be low.

There remains a possibility that CYP3A4 induction may lead to decreased exposures of zoliflodacin, with potential impact for microbiological efficacy, although this prediction has not been clinically validated. The co‐administration of zoliflodacin with strong or moderate CYP3A4 inducers is therefore contraindicated.
