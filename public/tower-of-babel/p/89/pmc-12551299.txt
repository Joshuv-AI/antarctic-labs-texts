Discovery of ultrasound-derived fat fraction as a non-invasive tool for MASLD diagnosis
Authors: Huiru Jin, Mengfan Jiao, Chengxiao Yu, Tingting Ren, Qingling Chen, Zixing Dai, Erfu Xie, Longfeng Jiang, Yuwen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of ultrasound-derived fat fraction (UDFF) for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), by directly comparing it with the non-invasive gold standard MRI-PDFF and the established ultrasound method CAP.MethodsThe diagnostic criteria for MASLD were used, we included 103 individuals with 53 MASLD patients and 49 healthy controls. All participants underwent liver MRI for MRI-PDFF quantification; ultrasound elastography for UDFF measurement; and vibration-controlled transient elastography for CAP assessment. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the performance of UDFF and CAP for predicting MRI-PDFF ≥ 5%, the imaging threshold defining hepatic steatosis in MASLD diagnosis. Finally, we analyzed the correlation between UDFF and serological indicators and the consistency between UDFF and MRI-PDFF.ResultsAcross all 103 participants, the median UDFF value was 6.0%, median CAP was 246 dB/m, and median MRI-PDFF was 5.44%. Overall, UDFF was positively correlated with MRI-PDFF (R = 0.876; P < 0.001), and CAP was positively correlated with MRI-PDFF (R = 0.792, P < 0.001). For diagnosing 5% and above MRI-PDFF, UDFF was noted to have a mean AUC of 0.981. Additionally, CAP was noted to have an AUC of 0.932 in diagnosing MASLD. In addition, the Bland–Altman difference plots showed overall UDFF and MRI-PDFF, with a mean deviation of -0.2%. A linear regression model between the difference and the mean suggested proportional error.ConclusionsUltrasound testing for UDFF provides a simple and noninvasive clinical tool to quantify the extent of hepatic steatosis. The diagnostic efficacy of UDFF is superior to CAP. Notably, while UDFF shows strong agreement with MRI-PDFF overall, Bland–Altman analysis revealed a proportional bias, with UDFF tending to underestimate MRI-PDFF at higher fat content levels. Despite this limitation, UDFF demonstrates potential as a noninvasive alternative to MRI-PDFF for MASLD diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03229-4.

Background

Hepatic steatosis is distinguished by the atypical and excessive fat buildup within hepatocytes [1]. Steatotic liver disease (SLD) encompasses conditions characterized by abnormal lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. Etiologies include: (i) Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), (ii) Alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), (iii) MASLD with increased alcohol intake (MetALD), (iv) Specific etiologies (e.g., monogenic disorders, drug-induced SLD), and (v) Cryptogenic SLD (where no identifiable cause is present). Notably, MASLD stands out as the most prevalent chronic liver ailment globally, impacting an estimated one billion individuals, which accounts for over a quarter of the global adult population [2, 3].

Liver biopsy remains the histological gold standard for MASLD diagnosis; however, its invasive nature, procedural risks, and susceptibility to sampling variability have relegated its use primarily to research protocols or cases with diagnostic uncertainty. In contemporary clinical practice, magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF), an FDA-approved non-invasive modality, has supplanted biopsy as the reference standard for hepatic steatosis quantification due to its high sensitivity [4], reproducibility, and capacity for whole-liver assessment. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS)-PDFF and MRI-PDFF are distinct techniques for quantifying hepatic fat. While MRS-PDFF samples a small liver region, MRI-PDFF provides whole-liver coverage using chemical shift-encoded imaging, making it the clinically accepted non-invasive gold standard for assessing hepatic steatosis heterogeneity.

However, multiple limitations have hindered the widespread clinical use of magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) and MRI-PDFF. The clinical application of MRI-PDFF is still limited by: high cost, equipment availability, contraindications for patients with metal implants, and longer standardized acquisition times compared to ultrasound technology (requiring breath-holding cooperation) [5]. Controlled Attenuation Parameters (CAP), a novel parameter derived from vibration-controlled transient elastography, is a commercialized liver fat quantification technique that has been integrated into the transient elastography system (FibroScan®) since 2010 [6]. CAP quantifies fatty degeneration by measuring the degree of ultrasound attenuation in liver tissue (in dB/m), while vibration controlled transient elastography (VCTE) assesses liver stiffness by evaluating shear wave velocity. Although both utilize the same device platform, they achieve independent measurements based on different physical principles. Ultrasound fat fraction (UDFF) is an independent technique for quantifying liver fat content by analyzing the acoustic attenuation coefficient (AC), backscatter coefficient (BSC), and sound velocity (SV) in radiofrequency signals [7]. In this study, we used an integrated platform (Siemens Acuson Sequoia) to simultaneously acquire UDFF and shear wave elastography (SWE) data [8]. However, based on the predefined study objectives, subsequent analyses focused on the correlation between UDFF and MRI-PDFF/CAP for fat quantification. Shear wave data will be used for future fibrosis studies.

Therefore, they can simultaneously measure liver tissue stiffness and hepatic fat content and produce UDFF values expressed as a percentage, similar to MRI-PDFF. This study aims to assess the potential of UDFF as an alternative indicator for fat quantification (rather than independent diagnostic efficacy) by comparing its correlation and consistency with the current non-invasive gold standard MRI-PDFF. Additionally, we systematically analyze the association between UDFF and serum metabolic markers to explore its clinical value as a comprehensive diagnostic tool.

Material and methods

Research subjects

54 patients with MASLD were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Jiangsu Provincial People's Hospital) from April 2023 to May 2023. Additionally, 49 healthy individuals who engaged in physical examination at the same hospital during this period were included in this study. The study delineated the following exclusion criteria: (1) < 18 years old or > 60 years old; (2) pregnant women; (3) those who were undergoing systemic chemotherapy or biologic therapy, or post-TACE; (4) the presence of tumors in the lobular segments of the liver to be sampled; (5) those who refused to sign the informed consent form; (6) those who were suffering from alcoholic liver disease, viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, drug-induced liver disease, and peritoneal effusion; (7) the patient has a history of chronic alcohol consumption, usually > 5 years, with a converted ethanol volume of ≥ 40 g/dL for men and ≥ 20 g/dL for women, or a history of heavy alcohol consumption within 2 weeks, with a converted ethanol volume of > 80 g/dL; (8) those who did not cooperate with ultrasound UDFF, Fibroscan, and MRI-PDFF examinations. The study protocol underwent a thorough review and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the aforementioned hospital. Prior to their inclusion, patients or their families were provided with comprehensive information about the treatment protocol and willingly signed the informed consent form.

MRI diagnosis

Diagnostic magnetic resonance imaging is performed by our radiologists who have more than a decade of experience. All participants underwent liver imaging using a Siemens (Siemens Healthineers) 3 T Magnetom Skyra MRI scanner equipped with a standard torso phased array coil. Whole liver fat quantification using a multi-echo gradient echo sequence (3D scan).

Image post-processing was performed using Syngo MR D13D platform software (Siemens Healthineers, Germany) to generate whole liver PDFF parametric maps by chemical shift-encoded multi-echo signal decomposition. MRI-PDFF calculations were based on pixel-level analysis of eight anatomical segments of the liver. Within each liver segment, a circular ROI (approximately 1.5 cm in diameter) was manually placed, avoiding blood vessels, bile ducts, and artifactual areas. Specifically, one ROI was placed in each of the right anterior hepatic lobe (segments 5 and 8), right posterior hepatic lobe (segments 6 and 7), left lateral hepatic lobe (segments 2 and 3), left medial hepatic lobe (segment 4), and caudate lobe (segment 1) for each participant (eight ROIs total). The mean value of the fat fraction of all ROIs was used as the final MRI-PDFF value. MASLD was defined as MRI-PDFF of 5% or higher [9]. Accordingly, participants were categorized into a healthy control group (< 5%) and a MASLD group (≥ 5%).

Ultrasonic diagnosis

Patients underwent UDFF and CAP in a fasted state. All UDFF and CAP exams are operated by two certified sonographers with more than 5 years of experience in liver ultrasound. The operators received specialized training in UDFF techniques from Siemens Healthineers. The results were independently analyzed by two senior clinicians with more than 10 years of experience in diagnostic liver imaging. The two analysts evaluated the patient's clinical information in a blinded manner, and the final result was taken as the consensus value of both to reduce subjective bias.

First, a two-dimensional liver ultrasound scan was performed with a conventional 5C1 probe. Subsequently, liver parenchymal echo intensity, homogeneity, and posterior liver echogenicity were recorded to produce qualitative diagnostic grading results (normal, mild, moderate, and severe). Immediately, images of the left and the right lobe, as well as liver and kidney sections, were stored. The focal hepatic lesions were also recorded, and information such as lesion size, internal echogenicity, borders, morphology, location, adjacency to blood vessels, and blood flow signals were documented. Afterward, the Deep Abdominal eXtra (DAX) probe was switched, and virtual touch quantification (VTQ, Siemens Healthineers) was initiated. The region of interest (ROI) sampling frame was positioned perpendicular to the liver surface. A 1.5 cm depth-labeled cursor was positioned on the liver capsule, selecting the right intercostal space for scanning. Segment 8 of the right lobe of the liver is a standard measurement site for elastic imaging because it has stable liver parenchyma thickness, avoids interference from large blood vessels, and has high operational repeatability. Subsequently, the labels of six measurements were recorded with Site 1-Site 6. All six measurements were concentrated in segment 8 of the right anterior lobe of the liver. Software of the machine recorded the distance from the whole liver capsule to the center of the ROI (4.0 cm–7.0 cm). Additionally, the software provided the mean, standard deviation (SD), median, interquartile range (IQR), and IQR/median of SWV and listed all results on the report page. Following the UDFF test, the Fibroscan test was promptly conducted utilizing the same testing path, featuring ten CAP measurements. Only examinations with at least ten valid measurements, a success rate of ≥ 60%, and an interquartile range to median ratio (IQR/M) of CAP values of < 0.3 were considered reliable and included in the final analysis [10]. Finally, the machine software automatically calculated the average of these ten measurements.

All imaging examinations strictly adhered to the three-blind principle: (1) Ultrasound operators could not access MRI-PDFF results when measuring UDFF/CAP (system isolation: ultrasound and MRI used independent workstations); (2) MRI analysts were shielded from ultrasound reports when generating PDFF parameter maps (PACS system settings restricted access permissions); (3) Statisticians de-identify examination result codes prior to data analysis. The interval between ultrasound and MRI examinations is > 24 h (median 3 days) to ensure results are not influenced by each other.

Serological testing

All participants had venous blood samples collected early in the morning in a fasting state (no more than 3 days before or after the time of testing with CAP, UDFF, and MRI-PDFF). All test specimens were immediately sent to our laboratory department, where the analysis was completed by a laboratory physician with many years of experience on the same day. All testing follows the manufacturer's operating manual and standardized laboratory procedures. Specific blood specimens include: lipid profile, glucose metabolism index, liver function index, fibrosis index, etc.

Data acquisition

General data (age, gender, etc.) and clinical examination data (serologic test results, UDFF values via ultrasound elastography, CAP values via vibration-controlled transient elastography, and MRI-PDFF values via MRI) were collected from all patients.

Statistical analysis

Medians and quartiles were used to represent continuous data. At the same time, numbers and percentages were employed for categorical data. Continuous data between groups was compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. In contrast, the Fisher exact test was utilized to compare categorical data between groups. To examine univariate correlations between UDFF measurement and other variables like MRI-PDFF and BMI, Spearman's rank correlation was utilized. In addition, multivariate linear regression models were constructed using a stepwise method to evaluate the predictors of UDFF. Variables with a significance level of P < 0.05 were included in the model, while those with P > 0.10 were excluded. Bland–Altman difference plots were generated to evaluate the mean bias and 95% consistency between UDFF and MRI-PDFF measurements. The Bland–Altman study could not be performed because of the different measurement units between CAP and PDFF. Scatterplot analysis was used to assess the consistency of CAP and UDFF with PDFF. ROC curves were plotted to evaluate the performance (AUC, sensitivity, and specificity) of UDFF and Fibroscan in diagnosing hepatic steatosis. The optimal threshold for the diagnosis of hepatic steatosis by UDFF was determined using the Youden index. Statistical significance of AUC differences was evaluated using DeLong's test (primary cohort), with P < 0.05 considered significant. Kappa consistency test was used to test the consistency between the two tests. In addition, P < 0.05 suggested significance for all inference tests. Statistical tests were carried out employing MedCalc (version 20.013) and SPSS 22.0 statistical software.

Results

General information

A total of 103 participants (54 were diagnosed with MASLD and 49 were healthy controls) were enrolled in this study. The values of BMI, CAP, TG, TC, LDL, FBG, ALT, AST, APRI and APOB were higher among patients with MASLD than among healthy controls (P < 0.05). The proportions of Male, UDFF and HbA1c were higher in patients with MASLD than in healthy controls (P < 0.05). By contrast, lower HDL was observed in patients with MASLD (P < 0.05). According to the available literature, the UDFF grading thresholds for fatty liver were referenced as follows, UDFF result less than 5% was used as the diagnostic criterion for the absence of fatty liver, 5%−10% was considered as mild fatty liver, 10%−25% was considered as moderate, and greater than 25% was considered as severe fatty liver [11–13]. The CAP results were categorized as absence of fatty liver (0–238 dB/m), mild fatty liver (S1:239–259 dB/m), moderate fatty liver (S2:260–292 dB/m) and severe fatty liver (S3: > 292 dB/m) [11, 12, 14]. Correlation analysis of UDFF and CAP with MRI-PDFF and their efficacy in diagnosing MASLD

UDFF demonstrated a significant positive correlation with MRI-PDFF (R = 0.876, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). The AUC for mean UDFF in predicting MRI-PDFF ≥ 5% was 0.981 (Fig. 2), with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.961–1.000, a sensitivity of 90.7%, a specificity of 95.9%, and a cut-off value of 6.17% (Table 2). In addition, CAP presented an association with MRI-PDFF (R = 0.792, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). The AUC for CAP in diagnosing MASLD was 0.932 (Fig. 2), with a 95% CI of 0.883–0.981, a sensitivity of 85.2%, a specificity was 92%, and a cut-off value was 250.50 dB/m (Table 2). Consistency of UDFF mean with MRI-PDFF

A total of 103 volunteers were diagnosed by MRI-PDFF, UDFF and CAP, and a total of 54 cases were diagnosed with MASLD by MRI-PDFF, and a total of 49 cases were diagnosed without fatty liver disease. A total of 56 cases were diagnosed with MASLD by CAP, 48 cases were diagnosed with MASLD by both MRI-PDFF and CAP, and 46 cases were diagnosed with no fatty liver by both MRI-PDFF and CAP, and there were 14 (13.6%) cases with conflicting test results, and the consistency test of the two methods yielded a Kappa value of 0.727 (P < 0.001). The consistency was moderate. The sensitivity of CAP for the diagnosis of MASLD was calculated to be 88.9%, the specificity was 83.7%, the Yoden index was 72.6%, and the concordance between vibration controlled transient elastography and MRI-PDFF for the diagnosis of MASLD was 77.4%, as shown in Additional Table 4.

A total of 57 cases were diagnosed with MASLD by UDFF, 51 cases were diagnosed with MASLD by both MRI-PDFF and UDFF, and 43 cases were diagnosed as having no fatty liver by both MRI-PDFF and UDFF. Consistency test between the two methods of MRI-PDFF and UDFF yielded a Kappa value of 0.842 (P < 0.001), which was good agreement. The sensitivity of UDFF for diagnosing MASLD was calculated to be 94.4%, the specificity was 87.8%, the Yoden index was 82.8%, and the concordance between UDFF and MRI-PDFF for the diagnosis of MASLD was 85%, as shown in Additional Table 5. Among the results obtained in this study, the sensitivity, specificity, Yoden index, compliance and concordance of UDFF for the diagnosis of MASLD were higher than that of CAP.

The Bland–Altman plot, which compared the UDFF mean and MRI-PDFF measurements, indicated a mean deviation of 0.2% (95% LOA: −9.1% to 8.6%). The plot demonstrated that, on average, UDFF measurements were slightly higher than MRI-PDFF measurements (Fig. 3). Moreover, linear regression analysis of difference values and means indicated proportional errors (coefficient, − 0.35; P < 0.001). Univariate and multivariate analysis of UDFF averages

In a univariate analysis, UDFF values correlated significantly with key clinical parameters: red blood cell, white blood cell, and neutrophil counts; hemoglobin; prothrombin activity; triglycerides and cholesterol; aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, alkaline phosphatase; serum creatinine; blood glucose; low-density lipoprotein; glycated hemoglobin A1c; and fasting insulin levels. It was negatively correlated with prothrombin time, partially activated prothrombin time, international standard value, high-density lipoprotein content, and apolipoprotein A1, with statistical differences (Additional Table 6).

Discussion

Clinically, MASLD is typically asymptomatic in early to moderate stages, with most symptoms arising from advanced fibrosis or comorbidities. Hepatic discomfort, when reported, often correlates with concurrent metabolic disorders rather than steatosis severity per se [15, 16]. Early detection of hepatic steatosis is crucial as it allows for the possibility of controlling or reversing the process before irreversible damage takes place. While liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing and grading hepatic steatosis, it is not practical for routine screening or frequent monitoring due to the risk of sampling errors. In addition, its invasive nature may lead to potential complications, including pain, hepatocellular necrosis, peritonitis, abdominal hemorrhage, and hemophilia [17–20].

In this study, UDFF exhibited superior concordance with MRI-PDFF (AUC = 0.981 for MRI-PDFF ≥ 5%) compared to CAP (AUC = 0.932), aligning with preclinical validations by Labyed et al. (AUC = 0.97) [7]. Pairwise comparison by DeLong's test revealed a statistically significant difference in AUC between UDFF and CAP (ΔAUC = 0.0489, 95% CI: 0.00507–0.0928, z = 2.187, P = 0.0288). UDFF demonstrated high accuracy for predicting MRI-PDFF ≥ 5%, with an AUC of 0.981 at the optimal cutoff of 6.17%. Bland–Altman analysis revealed minimal mean bias between UDFF and MRI-PDFF (−0.2%), though proportional errors suggested UDFF underestimates fat content at higher steatosis levels—a finding discordant with prior reports [21, 22]. This discrepancy may stem from MRI’s superior sensitivity to trace fat (detection threshold: 0.85% vs. UDFF’s 2%).

In this study, we found a tendency for UDFF to overestimate normal to mild steatosis (MRI-PDFF < 10%), which may be related to the low sensitivity of ultrasound signals to trace fat. When liver fat content approaches the threshold of 5%, UDFF is susceptible to interference from tissue heterogeneity or background noise, leading to a false-positive diagnosis.

The analysis revealed a positive correlation between the two variables, with the regression equation y = 2.12 × 102 + 4.83 × and a coefficient of determination R2 = 0.535. This indicates that CAP explains approximately 53.5% of the variance in PDFF (%). The scatter plots visually illustrate the consistency between UDFF and CAP with PDFF (%). UDFF showed a stronger correlation with PDFF compared to CAP, as reflected by the higher R2 value. These findings suggest that UDFF may be a more reliable indicator of PDFF than CAP in this context.

Our data revealed that patients with discordant UDFF-positive/MRI-negative results (n = 9) exhibited significantly elevated ALT (30.44 vs 19.60 IU/L, P = 0.029), GGT (44.03 vs 17.78 IU/L, P = 0.011) and TG (1.62 vs 0.95 mmol/L, P = 0.048) compared to healthy controls (Table 4), suggesting UDFF may detect subclinical metabolic dysfunction prior to MRI-detectable fat accumulation.

UDFF values correlated significantly with metabolic derangements, including elevated BMI, triglycerides, LDL, HbA1c, and liver enzymes (ALT, AST, GGT), while inversely associating with HDL. The significant correlations between UDFF values and key metabolic indicators (e.g., HbA1c, TG, ALT) validate its utility as a biomarker for metabolic syndrome, thereby highlighting lifestyle interventions as a core strategy in MASLD management [23, 24].

While previous studies have mostly assessed the concordance of CAP or UDFF with MRI-PDFF alone, the present study compares the diagnostic performance of the two in the same cohort for the first time and found that the AUROC of UDFF (0.981) was significantly higher than that of CAP (0.932), and the difference was especially significant in mild steatosis (MRI-PDFF 5%–10%) (sensitivity 90.7% vs. 85.2%) [25–27].

The tendency of UDFF to underestimate MRI-PDFF at high fat content (proportional error coefficient − 0.35) was revealed for the first time by Bland–Altman analysis, a phenomenon contradictory to Dillman et al. and probably stemming from the high sensitivity of MRI to trace fat (detection threshold 0.85% vs. 2% for UDFF) or due to ultrasound physical limitations, such as acoustic attenuation saturation effects and increased tissue heterogeneity [28].

In this study, we observed a significant tendency to underestimate both UDFF and CAP when MRI-PDFF exceeded 35%. This phenomenon may be related to the physical properties of the ultrasound technique: as fat content increases, the linear relationship between the acoustic attenuation coefficient (AC) and the backscattering coefficient (BSC) breaks down, leading to signal saturation. In addition, severe steatosis is often accompanied by hepatocyte ballooning and fibrosis, and the heterogeneity of the tissue structure may interfere with the resolution of the ultrasound signal. Of course, this phenomenon may also be related to disease spectrum bias.

The key point is that in this study, UDFF tends to overestimate normal to mild fatty degeneration and underestimate cases with fatty degeneration severity of PDFF > 35%. This phenomenon may lead to false-negative results for advanced MASLD and false-positive diagnoses for early metabolic dysfunction, so caution is required when interpreting extreme values.

The multidimensional correlations between UDFF and metabolic/liver injury markers offer clinically actionable insights (rather than causal claims):

(1) The positive UDFF-TG/LDL correlation suggests concurrent manifestation of hepatic steatosis and lipid dysregulation, warranting combined screening in high-risk cohorts;

(2) The positive UDFF-ALT/AST correlation signals statistical association with liver injury risk, though mechanistic causality requires histopathological validation;

(3) The negative UDFF-HDL correlation helps profile high-risk phenotypes with impaired protective mechanisms, pending prognostic verification in longitudinal studies.

These patterns, while biologically plausible, are interpreted as associative evidence for MASLD stratification—not causal pathways—as per STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

This study employs triple quality control standards to ensure data quality: In UDFF measurements, the coefficient of variation (CV) of six measurements must be < 10% (if exceeded, the measurement is repeated); CAP data with a signal-to-noise ratio < 30 dB or a success rate < 60% is automatically excluded; the UDFF radiofrequency signal stability curve is displayed in real time (if the fluctuation exceeds 5%, the measurement is interrupted); to ensure consistency between operators, two physicians perform independent measurements, and if the difference exceeds 15%, a third arbitrator is consulted.

UDFF demonstrates significant health economic advantages, with equipment costs only 3–8% of those of MRI-PDFF, single test costs approximately 60% lower than MRI-PDFF, and maintenance costs two orders of magnitude lower than MRI.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration: Firstly, although we enrolled 103 participants, the subgroup of MASLD patients (n = 53) may restrict the statistical power for analyzing steatosis severity stratification. Larger cohorts are needed to validate cutoff values across the full clinical spectrum of hepatic fat content (5%–30%). Secondly, all participants were recruited from a tertiary hospital in China, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to diverse ethnic populations or primary care settings. Thirdly, key populations were excluded (e.g., pediatric/geriatric patients, pregnant women, and individuals with advanced fibrosis), potentially underestimating UDFF performance in complex comorbidities. Fourthly, the cross-sectional design precludes evaluation of UDFF's utility for monitoring steatosis progression or treatment response over time. Lastly, MRI-PDFF and UDFF measurements were performed on different days (within 3 days), though fasting conditions were maintained to minimize intra-individual variability.

In the present study, the limits of agreement between UDFF and MRI-PDFF were found to range from − 9.1% to 8.6% (mean deviation − 0.2%) by Bland–Altman analysis, suggesting that there are some differences between the two methods for absolute quantification of fat content. Although this range is slightly above the clinically acceptable threshold suggested in the literature for PDFF comparisons based on different MRI sequences (usually deviation < 7%), the difference may stem from the inherent differences in technical principles between ultrasound and MRI: MRI is based on the fat–water separation technique of chemical shifts, whereas UDFF indirectly estimates the fat content by means of ultrasound acoustic parameters, which may be more sensitive to tissue heterogeneity.

Notably, despite the wide limits of concordance, UDFF still performed well in categorical diagnosis (AUC = 0.981), indicating its high sensitivity and specificity in differentiating between the presence or absence of significant hepatic steatosis (MRI-PDFF ≥ 5%). The main value of UDFF in clinical practice may lie in screening and dynamic monitoring rather than absolute quantification, especially for groups with limited resources or who need to avoid MRI. UDFF replaces traditional ultrasound/CAP as a secondary screening tool for FIB-4 intermediate-risk patients, simultaneously quantifying fatty degeneration (UDFF%) and fibrosis risk (Auto pSWE) in a single examination, thereby reducing the need for secondary referrals. Future studies may further reduce inter-method variability by optimizing the calibration of ultrasound parameters, standardizing the measurement process and expanding the sample size.

Conclusion

This study verified the high consistency of UDFF and CAP with MRI-PDFF of MRI in measuring hepatic steatosis. However, UDFF presented strong consistency with MRI-PDFF, exhibiting good efficiency and diagnostic efficacy. What is more, UDFF was significantly correlated with serological metabolic indicators. Further large-sample, multi-center trials are warranted to validate UDFF as a robust, accessible alternative to MRI-PDFF for MASLD screening and monitoring. While MRI-PDFF remains the non-invasive reference standard for precise fat quantification, UDFF offers significant advantages in cost-effectiveness, portability, and point-of-care applicability, positioning it as a complementary tool for routine clinical practice—particularly in resource-limited settings or serial assessments.
