Mepiquat chloride enhances cold tolerance in cotton via regulating root growth and antioxidant enzymes
Authors: Irshad Ahmad, Tianyao Meng, Xuebing Yan, Zhu Guanglong, Muhammad Usama Younas, Guisheng Zhou, Yanqing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cold stress severely affects cotton growth and yield worldwide. Mepiquat chloride (MC), a widely used plant growth regulator in cotton, has not been extensively studied under cold stress conditions. This study evaluated the effects of three MC levels (0 mM, 50 mM, and 100 mM) applied at 21 days after sowing (DAS) on the morpho-physiological and biochemical responses of two cotton varieties (Sikang 1 and Sikang 3) under normal temperature and cold stress during the seedling stage. The present study revealed that the MC application reduced leaf length, leaf width, shoot length, and plant height but increased total plant fresh weight and root growth attributes. Furthermore, the MC application enhanced chlorophyll pigments, protein content, superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) while reducing malondialdehyde (MDA). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses (representative images, n = 3 biological replicates) revealed that MC-treated plants exhibited more intact cell membranes, better organized chloroplasts, and preserved thylakoid structures under cold stress compared with untreated plants. The improvement in morpho-physiological activity was largely attributed to the increment in root growth characteristics. Overall, MC at 50 and 100 mM significantly strengthened root architecture, chlorophyll content, cellular integrity, and antioxidant defense, resulting in improved cold tolerance in cotton seedlings. Among the tested concentrations, 100 mM MC provided the most substantial protective effects, particularly in Sikang 1. The corresponding findings highlight MC as a promising regulator for alleviating cold stress in cotton, but its molecular and hormonal mechanisms require further investigation. Introduction

Cotton is one of the world’s most important commercial crops [1, 2]. Cotton plant has an indeterminate growth habit that contributes to the industrial and economic growth of different countries [3, 4]. China is the world’s largest producer and consumer of cotton, with an average yield of 2014.9 kg ha− 1 [5]. The growth and yield of cotton are adversely affected by cold stress when the average daily temperature is less than 14 °C, and as a result, lint yield and fiber quality are decreased [6].

The effect of cold stress on growth and yield relies on the growth stage of the crops. The cotton plants are most likely exposed to cold stress during the first few weeks after sowing [7]. Different research has demonstrated that cold stress has a season-long effect on cotton fruiting, fiber quality and yield [8]. For example, cold stress adversely affects the growth and yield of cotton in different parts of the world; in China, the province of Xinjiang is frequently affected by cold stress for almost more than half of the cotton growing season [9]. In Xinjiang, the current investigations and long-term production practices in cotton during the seedling stage showed that cold stress significantly reduces the growth and fiber yield of cotton during this period [10, 11].

It has been demonstrated that cold stress reduced the growth and yield of various crops, including cotton, rice, cucumber, maize and tomatoes, by reducing their morpho-physiological and biochemical activity [12–16]. For example, Bhattacharya [17] showed that during cold stress, the reduction in net photosynthesis activity often leads to a decrease in plant growth and yield. To cope with the adverse effects of cold stress, plants need to maintain the stability of the cell membrane and structure of a protein with biological activity [18]. Plants deal with the adverse effect of cold stress via the higher accumulation of osmolytes such as soluble protein, sugars and proline and improving antioxidant activity in plants [19]. It has been demonstrated that plants adaptive to cold stress may store more sugar (amylose, maltose, starch, and D-glucose) in their cell tissue [20, 21].

Research about cold tolerance in plant regulation has recently been an exciting research area for increasing plant growth and yield. Exogenous hormone has been recently found to be one of the most efficient signaling regulators in plant growth and yield [22]. It has demonstrated that MC treatment restricts some growth attributes such as node number, length of internodes, canopy, leaf length and width, and plant height due to suppression of gibberellic acid (GA) production in cotton [4]. MC decreases GA levels in plants and, as a result, reduces cell enlargement and excessive vegetative growth [23]. The reduction in GA disrupts cellular movements due to a reduction in cell wall relaxation and increases cell wall stiffness and plasticity [4]. MC regulates the source-sink relationship by reducing the GA synthesis in plants [24]. Different research showed that MC treatment improves early buds and bolls and reduces boll age, which can lead to hastened cotton maturity [25, 26]. MC treatment has shown a balance between vegetative and reproductive growth, which improves cotton growth, maturity, fiber quality, and lint yield. However, the growth and yield of cotton rely on the application time and dose of MC [23, 27].

A number of studies have investigated whether MC can mitigate the adverse effects of various abiotic stresses in cotton and other crops. For example, treatment with MC at a dose of 60–75 g L⁻¹ has been reported to increase soluble protein, sugar, and proline content in cotton, reduce malondialdehyde levels, enhance membrane stability, and improve resistance to oxidative damage by increasing antioxidant enzyme activities such as CAT, SOD, and POD [28]. Similarly, MC at 50–80 g ha⁻¹ has been shown to enhance chlorophyll pigments and reduce excessive vegetative growth, thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency under drought stress conditions in soybean plants [29]. This improvement was associated with elevated production of secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, tannins, lignin, phenols, and related compounds (Zhou et al., 2019). In another study, MC at 100–125 µg L⁻¹ promoted pepper growth under cold stress by increasing abscisic acid (ABA) accumulation and enhancing osmoregulatory compounds such as proline, sugars, and proteins [17, 30]. Despite these advances, the role of MC in improving cotton seedlings remains insufficiently understood, especially regarding its machanistic basis. It is therfore critically important to investigate cold stress mechanism and to develop effective strategies to enhance early-stage cold tolerance in cotton. Hence, the present study investigated the efficacy of MC in regulating morpho-physiological activities, root growth attributes to improve cold stress resilience in cotton at earlier growth stages.

In the present study, the MC concentrations were selected based on preliminary trials conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with cold stress, considering the specific cotton growth stage. These preliminary tests evaluated a range of concentrations to identify doses that effectively modulated growth without causing toxicity, which differed from those reported in previous studies. These differences in experimental settings and dose optimization ensure that our findings provide novel insights into the morphological, physiological, and biochemical responses of cotton to MC under cold stress.

Therefore, the main objective of this work: (1) evaluate the effects of MC concentrations on morpho-physiological and biochemical responses of cotton seedlings under cold stess, (2) assess the influence of MC on root grwoth characteristics contributing to enhance cold tolerance, (3) elucidate the cellular structural mechanisms involved in MC-mediated alleviation of cold stress in cotton.

Materials and methods

The experiment was conducted at Joint International Research Laboratory of Agriculture and Agri-Product Safety of the Ministry of Education of China (32°30/N, 119°43/E), Yangzhou University, Jiangsu Province, China (20 March 2023). A randomized complete block design with three replications was used to investigate the effects of three factors such as MC, temperature and cultivars. Two cotton varieties, Sikang 1 (G1) and Sikang 3 (G2), three replications and three levels of MC (0 control, 50 mM, and 100 mM) were investigated in 72 pots during the seedling experiment under cold 12 °C and normal temperature 25 °C. The cotton varieties such as Sikang 1 and Sikang 3 provided by the Siyang Academy of Agricultural Science. The length and width of each pot size were 16 × 13 cm. The soil in each pot was 1000 g in weight. Twelve seeds were sown in each pot. The seeds were sown on 20 March 2023. To prevent fungal infection, healthy seeds uniform in size, shape and color were manually selected from both cultivars and surface-sterilized by soaking in a 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) solution for ten minutes. Seeds were then rinsed 5 times with distilled water and air-dried at 25 °C for 24 h. MC (99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, China) was used in this study. Stock solutions of three concentrations (0, 50, and 100 mM) were prepared by dissolving the required amount of MC in distilled water. The different levels of MC were foliar sprayed by a knapsack electric sprayer on the 21 st DAS. The spraying was applied once daily for three consecutive days until the leaves begin to drip ensuring uniform coverage. After the final application, the plants were kept under cold and normal temperature for seven days (Fig. 1). At the same time, the control pots were sprayed with tap water. The data were collected on 28 and 45 days after sowing.

Observation and measurements

Morpho-physiological parameters were assessed at 28 and 45 DAS during the seedling stage. Five plants were randomly selected each time on 28 and 45 DAS and the determination was made on root projected area (cm2), root length (cm), root diameter (mm), root surface area (cm2), root volume (cm3) and root tips, plant height (cm), leaf length (cm) and width (cm), shoot length (cm), and total plant fresh weight (g). The antioxidant and physiological parameters were measured, including the activities of SOD, CAT, POD, APX, and MDA, along with chlorophyll contents (a + b and carotenoids) and soluble protein.

Agronomic parameters

Root parameters were analyzed using an image scanner (12000 XL, Seko Epson Co. Ltd, Japan) and WinRHIZO software. Plant height was determined from the bottom to the top using a measuring rod. Data on leaf length and width and shoot length were measured using a measuring rod according to Tung et al. [4] with minor modifications. For the total plant fresh weight, all plants were weighed via electronic scales.

Physiological parameters

At 28 and 45 DAS, the upper expanded leaves from five cotton plants in each pot were sampled and stored in an ultra-low temperature refrigerator at −80 °C for physiological assessment.

SOD (µg g⁻¹ FW h⁻¹) activity was measured according to the method of Ahmad et al. [31]. The CAT (µg− 1 FW. min− 1) activity was measured by following the method of Wang et al. [32] with minor modifications. The POD (µg− 1 FW. min− 1) activity was determined by following the method of Ahmad et al. [31] with minor modifications. The ascorbate APX (µg− 1 FW. min− 1) was determined by following the method of according to the method of Sheri et al. [33, 34]. The content of MDA (µg− 1 mg− 1 FW.) was measure according to the method Meloni et al. [35] with minor modifications. The chlorophyll contents (a, b and carotenoids) were measured according to Luo and Tang [36] with minor modifications. The activity of the soluble protein (mg g⁻¹ FW) was measured by following the method according of He et al. [37].

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance was investigated using MSTAT-C in a randomized complete block design, including three levels of MC, two cultivars and two temperature conditions. A three-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the main effects and interactions among MC, cultivar, and temperature. Data were log-transformed when assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance were violated. Treatment means were compared using the least significant difference test (LSD) at P < 0.05 probability level. Graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 software.

Results

Root growth attributes

MC treatments, G1, and G2 cultivars, and the interaction between MC and G1 and G2 significantly affected the root surface area at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 1). As compared with G2, G1 increased the root surface area by 25.2%, 14.2%, and 6.5% and reduced by 14.0% at 28 and 45 DAS, under 12 °C and 25 °C (P < 0.05). The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC increased the root surface area by 61.5%, 53.6%, 69.2%, and 62.5% under 25 °C, and increased by 45.87%, 17.5%, 109.2%, and 51.0% DAS under 12 °C, at 28 and 45 DAS, as compared with control. The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC increased the root surface area of G1 and G2 at 28 and 45 DAS, under 12 °C and 25 °C. The root diameter was significantly affected by MC treatments, G1, and G2, and the interaction between MC and cultivars at 28 and 45 DAS, under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 1). As compared with G2, G1 increased the root diameter by 8.2%, 1.0%, and 7.6% and reduced by 15.0% at 28 and 45 DAS, under 25 °C and 12 °C (P < 0.05). The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC increased the root diameter by 12.1%, 95.7%, 20.9%, and 79.7% under 25 °C and increased by 75.4%, 38.5%, 51.4, and 103.0% under 12 °C at 28 and 45 DAS, as compared with control. MC treatments showed an increasing trend in root length under 12 °C, indicating its potential role in alleviating cold-induced root surface growth inhibition (Fig. 2b).

The root volume of G1 and G2 was significantly affected by MC treatments and the interaction between MC and cultivars at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 1). At 28 and 45 DAS, the 50 and 100 mM levels of MC increased the root volume by 20.0%, 50.0%, 40.0%, 99.0% 33.3%, 50.0%, 100.0%, and 70.7% under 12 °C and 25 °C, as compared with control (P < 0.05). As compared with 25 °C, the MC increased the root volume at both developmental growth stages under 12 °C, however, the 100 mM level performed best among all treatments (Fig. 2c).

The root tips of G1 and G2 were significantly affected MC treatments, cultivars, and the interaction between MC and cultivars at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 1). As compared with G2, G1 reduced the root tips by 3.5%, 15.2%, and 6.3% and increased them by 6.0% at 28 and 45 DAS under both conditions (P < 0.05). At 28 and 45 DAS, the root tip was increased by 41.6%, 72.1%, 24.8%, and 40.2% with a 50 mM level of MC and increased by 74.8%, 12.5%, 42.3%, and 84.3% with a 100 mM level of MC under 12 °C and 25 °C, as compared with control. However, as compared with 25 °C, MC treatments showed a slight decreased in the root tips of both cultivars under 12 °C (Fig. 2d).

Roots length was significantly affected by MC treatments, cultivars, and the interaction between MC and cultivars at 28 and 45 days after sowing (DAS) under cold stress (12 °C) and normal temperature (25 °C). The root length of Sikang 1 (G1) was reduced by 18.3%, 20.9%, and 1.7% and increased by 6.9%, as compared with Sikang 3 (G2) at 28 and 45 DAS under 25 °C and 12 °C (P < 0.05). The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC gradually increased the root length by 20.8–45.4% under 25 °C and increased by 28.9–47.3% under 12 °C at 28 and 45 DAS, as compared with control (Table 1). However, the 100 mM MC treatment reduced the root length by 1.5% at 28 DAS under 25 °C, as compared to the control. Under 12 °C, MC reduced root length compared with 25 °C; however, it increased root length relative to the control, indicating a partial alleviation of cold stress (Fig. 2e).

The MC treatments, cultivars G1 and G2, and the interaction between MC and cultivars were significantly affected the root projected area at 28 and 45 DAS, under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 1). As compared with G2, G1 increased the root projected area by 11.7% and reduced by 12.5% and 2.7% at 28 and 45 DAS, under 12 °C and 25 °C. At 25 °C, the 50 and 100 mM levels of MC increased the root projected area by 25.8%, 94.9%, and 35.2% at 28 and 45 DAS, as compared to the control, respectively. Likewise, as compared with control, the 50 and 100 mM levels of MC increased the root projected area by 60.5%, 45.2%, 143.6%, and 81.8% at 28 and 45 DAS, under 12 °C. In contrast, the 50 mM level of MC reduced the root projected area by 35.8% at 28 DAS, under 25 °C, as compared to the control. At 28 DAS, under 12 °C, MC treatments showed an increasing trend in root length, as compared to the control and normal conditions, indicating its role in mitigating cold stress effects (Fig. 2f).

As compared with G2, G1 showed a higher leaf length by 48.5% and lowered by 39.1%, 9.9% and 14.3% under 12 °C and 25 °C. The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC decreased the leaf length by 10.2% 20.4%, 20.0%, and 37.1% at 28 DAS and decreased by 9.6%, 14.4%, 18.3%, and 31.1% at 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C, as compared with control. The leaf width was significantly affected by MC treatments and cultivars at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 2). G1 showed a higher leaf width by 16.4% and lowered by 40.0%, 50.2%, and 16.4% at 28 DAS and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C. The 50 mM and 100 mM levels of MC decreased the leaf width by 8.9%, 28.9%, 27.7%, 38.3%, 17.6%, 23.6%, 26.1%, and 33.6% at 28 and 45 DAS, under 12 °C and 25 °C, as compared with control. At 12 °C and 25 °C, the MC treatments decreased the leaf width of G1 and G2 cultivars, but a higher reduction was observed in terms of G1 cultivar under 12 °C (Fig. 3b).

MC treatments, G1 and G2 cultivars, and the interaction between MC and cultivars significantly affected the shoot length at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 2). G1 increased the shoot length by 2.2%, 1.5%, and 22.1% while reduced by 10.4% at 28 DAS and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C. The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC decreased the shoot length by 7.5%, 6.1%, 16.9%, 16.7%, 17.3%, 19.1%, 22.0%, and 40.9% at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C, as compared with control. As compared with control, the MC treatments decreased the shoot length of both cultivars under 12 °C and 25 °C, however, the 100 mM level of MC showed a higher decreased under 12 °C (Fig. 3c).

The plant height of G1 and G2 were significantly affected by MC treatments, and the interaction between MC and cultivars at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 2). G1 showed a higher plant height by 12.6%, 4.1%, and 4.8% and lowered by 6.4% at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C. The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC decreased the plant height by 13.0%, 26.0%, 9.6%, 24.5%, 14.4%, 31.1%, 10.9%, and 17.3% under 12 °C and 25 °C, as compared with control. As the concentrations of MC were increased, a significant reduction was observed in plant height under 12 °C and 25 °C (Fig. 3d).

The total plant fresh weight of G1 and G2 were significantly affected by MC treatments, cultivars, and the interaction between MC and cultivars at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 2). As compared with G2, G1 increased the total plant fresh weight by 50.0% and decreased by 10.4% and 7.4% at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C, respectively. The 50 and 100 mM levels of MC increased the total plant fresh by 28.8%, 33.8%, 93.4%, and 75.0% at 28 DAS and increased by 40.9%, 69.3%, 72.7%, and 72.5% at 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C, as compared with control. The 100 mM level showed a higher increased under 25 °C in terms of G1 cultivar among all treatments (Fig. 3e).

MDA and soluble protein content

MC levels, G1, and G2 cultivars, and the interaction between MC and cultivars significantly affect the MDA content at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 3). As compared with G2, G1 increased the MDA content by 31.3% and 13.9% and decreased by 15.9% and 8.8% at 28 and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C. The 50 mM and 100 mM levels of MC significantly reduced the MDA content by 36.2%, 50.9%, 35.1%, and 50.8% under 25 °C and reduced by 51.6%, 70.3%, 60.0%, and 78.3% under 12 °C at 28 and 45 DAS, as compared with control. The reduction in MDA content due to MC represents the alleviation of the negative effects of cold stress on plants (Fig. 5e).

The soluble protein content was significantly affected by MC treatments, G1, and G2 cultivars and the interaction between MC and cultivars at 28 DAS and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C (Table 3). As compared with G2, G1 significantly increased the soluble protein content by 8.5%, 4.9%, 10.7%, and 77.5% at 28 DAS and 45 DAS under 12 °C and 25 °C. As compared with control, the 50 mM and 100 mM levels of MC increased the protein contents by 77.0%, 108.2%, 68.7%, and 79.1% under 25 °C and increased by 85.9%, 96.4%, 87.9%, and 98.3% under 12 °C at both developmental growth periods. At 28 and 45 DAS, the soluble protein contents were improved by MC treatments under 12 °C and 25 °C, however, the 100 mM showed a prominent performance under 25 °C (Fig. 5f).

Leaf anatomical structure

By observing the surface porosity of Sikang 1 cultivar via SEM, we found that it was regular in shape, the guard cells around the stomata were round, the stomata were slightly expanded, and the stomata tended to arch outward at room temperature. After MC application, the shape of the epidermal cells was slightly deformed and slightly wrinkled, the guard cells around the stomata were more rounded and the stomata slightly expanded. At low temperature, the leaf epidermal cells of Sikang 1 were irregular in shape and severely wrinkled, the guard cells around stomata were also severely wrinkled and stomata were closed. After MC application, the epidermal cells were less wrinkled and the guard cells around the stomata were partially rounded.

By observing the ultrastructure of Sikang 1 in TEM, our results showed that the leaf mesophyll cell structure of Sikang 1 was intact, the plasma membrane structure was clear, the contents were abundant and the chloroplast structure was full at room temperature. After MC application, the structure of Sikang 1 was relatively clear and complete, the chloroplasts became smaller and the starch granules increased. At low temperature, the chloroplasts in leaf mesophyll cells of Sikang 1 expanded, the outer plasma membrane was got wrinkled, the inner fiber structure was loose and disorganized, and the plasmolysis was observed. Under MC application, chloroplasts became narrow, long and small, but the chloroplast structure was basically intact and the internal fiber structure was compact.

Through SEM observation of the stomata on the lower surface of the leaves of cotton Sikang 3, it was found that the shape of the epidermal cells was regular, the guard cells around the stomata were round, and the stomata were slightly dilated at room temperature. After MC application, the shape of epidermal cells was slightly deformed and slightly wrinkled, the guard cells around the stomata were rounded and the stomata tended to arch outward. At low temperature, the shape of epidermal cells of Sikang 3 was irregular and wrinkled, and the guard cells around stomata were wrinkled and the stomata were closed. After MC application, the stomatal opening was slightly increased.

By observing the ultrastructure of Sikang 3 in TEM, it was found that the leaf mesophyll cell structure of Sikang 3 was complete and the inner fiber structure was slightly loose at room temperature. Chloroplasts of Sikang 3 became smaller after MC treatment. At low temperature, the chloroplasts in the leaf mesophyll cells Sikang 3 became narrow and long, the internal fiber structure was loose and disorganized, and plasmolysis was observed. However, under MC application, chloroplasts became narrow, long and small, but the chloroplast structure was basically intact and the internal fiber structure was compact (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Cold stress severely inhibits the plant growth and yield in different crops. An optimal rate of MC application is essential for achieving higher cotton growth and yield. MC has been widely investigated as a growth retardant in cotton [22]. However, the different concentrations in cotton under cold stress at the seedling stage are still unclear. The phenotypic studies of the current research showed that MC application significantly inhibited cotton seedling growth but increased its physiological activities under normal temperature and cold stress at different growth stages (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

In the current experiment, the foliar application of MC treatments (50 and 100 mM) significantly reduced the plant height, shoot length, leaf length, and width under cold stress and normal temperature at 28 and 45 days after sowing (DAS), as compared to the control. However, the 50 and 100 mM MC treatments increased the total plant fresh weight and root growth attributes of Sikang 1 and Sikang 3 cultivars at 28 and 45 DAS under both conditions. Tung et al. [38] demonstrated a similar reduction in plant height, leaf length and internode length for cotton. Our results are consistent with Luo and Tang [36, 39], showing MC treatment reduced leaf length, leaf width, and plant height. In the present study, the reduction in plant height and leaf size following MC application can be attributed to MC-induced structural alterations at the cellular level. The SEM analysis revealed deformation and wrinkling of the epidermal cells, along with modified guard cell morphology, suggesting a decline in cell expansion and proliferation caused by MC. In addition, TEM analysis indicated that MC treatment led to chloroplast shrinkage and increased compactness of the internal fibrous structures within mesophyll cells, indicating suppressed metabolic and structural activity. These MC-induced cellular changes collectively contributed to the inhibition of cell elongation and division, ultimately restricting shoot and leaf growth under 12 °C and 25 °C (Fig. 6).

The root system of a crop plant plays an indispensable role in plant growth and yield by providing above-ground mechanical support and regulating nutrient uptake and water management. In the present study, the Sikang 3 variety has shown the best response and increased the root length, root projected area, and root tips under various MC applications under cold stress and normal temperature, as compared with the control (Table 1). The enhancement in root parameters in the present study in MC-treated plants is closely associated with its protective effects on leaf and vascular structures, as revealed by microscopy. Under both normal and cold conditions, MC maintained intact and regularly shaped stomata, likely improving gas exchange and photosynthetic performance, thereby increasing carbohydrate allocation to roots. Wu et al. [40] similarly reported that MC promoted lateral root growth in cotton via protein upregulation, consistent with our findings. MC applications preserved vascular bundle and chloroplast ultrastructure, ensuring efficient water and nutrient uptake and sustained energy production. These corresponding results are supported by the study of Dos santos et al. [41], who showed that MC treatments enhanced root growth parameters in Eucalyptus by improving its stress tolerance. Hence, MC-treated plant’s reduced structural damage and oxidative stress further supported root elongation, branching, and overall root system development, even under cold stress.

MC application significantly influenced cotton physiology by enhancing chlorophyll pigment accumulation. In the present study, MC treatments increased chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid contents at 28 and 45 DAS under normal temperature and cold stress, as compared with control. The 50 and 100 mM MC treatments consistently resulted in higher chlorophyll pigments, with 100 mM producing the greatest increase under both temperature regimes. Foliar spraying of MC led to a gradual rise in chlorophyll pigments, particularly at 45 DAS. This improvement in chlorophyll content in the present study may be attributed to multiple physiological effects of MC: (i) suppression of excessive stem elongation, which reduces leaf shading and enhances light capture efficiency; (ii) stimulation of antioxidant defense systems, which protects chloroplast membranes and pigments from oxidative degradation; and (iii) regulation of enzymes associated with chlorophyll metabolism [4]. Furthermore, MC-induced compact leaf morphology with darker green coloration, suggests increased pigment density per unit leaf area, thereby enhancing photosynthetic efficiency [38]. Higher chlorophyll contents directly supports greater photosynthetic carbon fixation, increasing the production of photoassimilates available for growth, stress recovery, and yield formation [42]. Sikang 1 showed higher pigments retention than Sikang 3, with notable increases in chlorophyll pigments under both MC levels, indicating stronger MC regulation of chlorophyll biosynthesis and photo-protection under normal and cold stress conditions.

Antioxidant enzymes are a key component of plant defense, enabling rapid scavenging of ROS and enhancing tolerance to abiotic stress [43]. In this study, MC application under normal temperature and cold stress markedly increased SOD, CAT, POD, and APX activities in cotton leaves at 28 and 45 DAS, as compared with control (Table 3). Enhanced activity of these enzymes helps maintain ROS homeostasis by balancing production and scavenging, thereby protecting cellular structures and sustaining metabolic function. Both 50 and 100 mM MC treatments increased the SOD activity, with 100 mM producing the highest levels, particularly at 28 DAS under cold stress, indicating a strong induction of enzymatic defense pathways. Similar trends were observed for CAT and POD, where 100 mM MC outperformed 50 mM, especially at 45 DAS under both temperature regimes. The strong antioxidant activity at 100 mM was consistent with the SEM and TEM observations, where MC-treated plants maintained more intact and regularly shaped stomata, as well as preserved chloroplast and vascular bundle ultrastructure, as compared to the control. These structural improvements suggest that enhanced enzymatic ROS scavenging directly protected cell organelles from oxidative injury, delaying damage to photosynthetic activity and promoting growth even under cold stress.

MDA, a widely used marker of membrane lipid peroxidation, showed a significant reduction with both MC concentrations under normal and cold stress, with the greatest decline at 100 mM at 45 DAS (Fig. 6). Lower MDA levels indicate reduced oxidative injury and improved membrane stability, which corresponded with TEM images showing well-organized thylakoid membranes and fewer signs of chloroplast degradation in MC-treated plants. This finding agrees with Song et al. [44], who reported that MC reduced MDA content in soybeans. One possible explanation is that MC stimulates flavonoid biosynthesis, as flavonoids possess potent antioxidant properties, directly quench ROS, and slow leaf senescence [30]. By maintaining chloroplast integrity and delaying senescence, flavonoids contribute to sustained photosynthesis and stress resilience. Furthermore, reduced MDA is often correlated with higher antioxidant enzyme activity, suggesting a synergistic effect between enzymatic and non-enzymatic defense systems [45].

Genotypic differences were evident, with Sikang 1 consistently showing higher antioxidant enzyme activities and greater reductions in MDA than Sikang 3 at both MC levels and sampling times. SEM and TEM results also revealed that Sikang 1 maintained better stomatal morphology and chloroplast ultrastructure than Sikang 3 under both normal and cold stress, supporting its superior physiological resilience. This stronger performance may be due to a combination of MC responsiveness and inherent genetic traits that favor ROS detoxification and stress adaptation. Such genotype-specific responses highlight the importance of integrating growth regulators like MC into breeding and management strategies aimed at improving cold tolerance in cotton.

Conclusion

In this study, foliar application of MC at 50 and 100 mM significantly improved cotton morpho-physiological activity and alleviated cold stress during the seedling stage. MC application slightly reduced shoot growth and plant height but were beneficial to total plant fresh weight, root growth parameters, chlorophyll contents, and ROS scavenging systems due to an increase in stem weight. Sikang 1 variety performed better in root and shoot traits and antioxidant enzymes, whereas Sikang 3 exhibited higher root length, and leaf size under normal temperature and cold stress. Overall, 100 mM MC was most effective for Sikang 1, suggesting that optimized MC application and suitable variety selection can enhance cotton seedling resilience, however, further field trials are necessary to confirm their agronomic benefits, safety, and regulatory compliance.
