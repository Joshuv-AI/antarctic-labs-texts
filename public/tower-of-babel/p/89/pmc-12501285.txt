Germline JAK2 R564Q variants presenting as hereditary thrombocytosis: case report
Authors: Stephanie Franco, Kinga Krawiec, Piotr Strzalka, Lucy A. Godley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are classically attributed to somatic variants in JAK2, CALR, and MPL, with epidemiologic studies demonstrating an increased risk for MPNs within first-degree relatives. Germline variants associated with MPN predisposition include rare variants of low population frequency/high penetrance alleles, often in JAK2, resulting in non-clonal MPN-like disorders, including hereditary thrombocytosis (HT). The first activating germline JAK2 variant encoding a residue other than V617F identified in association with a hereditary MPN-like syndrome was c.1691 G > A, p.Arg564Gln (R564Q), a gain-of-function missense variant. Here, we present the cases of three unrelated individuals with HT and the germline JAK2 R564Q variant, two of whom were tested in 2023, and received clinical reports classifying the variant as a variant of uncertain significance, and the third, tested more recently from a different laboratory, which classified the variant as likely pathogenic (LP). We propose that germline testing should be offered at minimum to MPN patients presenting at a young age, those with apparent familial clustering, and those with triple negative/idiopathic disease. These three cases provide additional segregation data that should sway consensus regarding the pathogenicity of this variant toward LP and resolve the conflicting classifications for this variant in ClinVar.

Introduction

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are clonal hematopoietic stem cell disorders involving excessive proliferation of terminally differentiated myeloid cells of one or more lineage [1]. The classical Philadelphia chromosome (BCR::ABL1)-negative MPNs are polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PM), which are associated with increased risk of thrombosis, systemic symptoms related to increased cytokine production such as aquagenic pruritus, risk of progression to myelofibrosis (in the case of PV and ET), and potential for leukemic transformation [2]. Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is defined by the BCR::ABL1 fusion protein resulting from t(9;22)(q34;q11), which is required for diagnosis according to 5th edition WHO classifications [1]. Other MPNs include chronic neutrophilia leukemia (CNL), chronic eosinophilic leukemia (CEL), juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML), and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)/MPN overlap neoplasms [1]. Here, we focus on the classical BCR::ABL1-negative MPNs.

The gene encoding Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) is located on 9p24 and contains 28 exons [3]. JAK2 is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase associated with a receptor intracellular domain [4]. The protein consists of four domains: an N-terminal FERM domain, a Src homology-2 (SH2)-like domain, a JAK homology 2 (JH2) pseudokinase domain, and a C-terminal JH1 active tyrosine kinase domain [3, 5, 6]. JH2 negatively regulates kinase activity and receptor-induced catalytic activity [3]. JAK2 activation leads to receptor phosphorylation and downstream phosphorylation of STAT transcription factors that then translocate to the nucleus to induce gene transcription [4]. JAK/STAT signaling cascades drive cellular proliferation and inhibit apoptosis, resulting in megakaryocyte and erythrocyte production [4, 7, 8].

Somatic JAK2 variants are well-known drivers of BCR::ABL1-negative MPNs due to overactivation of the JAK-STAT pathway. The most common pathogenic variant, JAK2 V617F, is identified in 50–60% of cases of ET and PM and 95% of cases of PV [5, 8]. JAK2 V617F disrupts the JH2 pseudokinase domain, resulting in uninhibited JH1 kinase activity [8, 9]. Thus, JAK2 V617F-associated MPNs tend to present more aggressively [9]. The common JAK2 V617F allele is encoded within exon 14, whereas the majority of other clinically significant somatic JAK2 variants occur in exon 12 and are responsible for the remaining ~5% of PV cases, and other alleles are distributed along the entire gene [8]. Other somatic gene variants associated with the development of MPNs occur with the CALR and MPL genes, and are identified in 30 and 5% of cases of ET and PM, respectively [2, 5]. Notably, up to 10–15% of patients with ET do not have somatic variants in JAK2, CALR, or MPL, and are thus classified as “triple-negative” or idiopathic [2].

More comprehensive and widely available next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based molecular profiling has facilitated the identification of additional gene mutations involved in the pathogenesis of MPNs. Up to one third of patients diagnosed with MPNs have at least one additional somatic mutation associated with MPN development, including in genes such as epigenetic regulators (e.g., TET2, DNMT3A, IDH1/2), chromatin modification (e.g., ASXL1, EZH2), spliceosome genes (e.g., U2AF1, SF3B1, SRSF2) and DNA damage response genes (e.g., TP53) [9].

Epidemiologic studies demonstrate familial predisposition to classical MPNs, with first-degree relatives of MPN patients having a 5.7- and 7.4-fold increased risk of PV and ET, respectively [2, 10]. Germline variants associated with MPN predisposition can be divided broadly into: (1) high population frequency/low penetrance alleles that may result in mildly increased risk of both sporadic and familial cases of MPNs; (2) higher penetrance alleles leading to familial clustering of MPNs; and (3) extremely rare cases of low population frequency/high penetrance alleles resulting in non-clonal MPN-like disorders, hereditary thrombocytosis (HT) and hereditary erythrocytosis (HE) [2, 11, 12]. Unlike classical MPNs resulting from somatic variants in JAK2, CALR, or MPL, HT and HE are rare, non-neoplastic disorders resulting from germline variants in genes such as JAK2 that present with autosomal dominant inheritance patterns and apparent familial clustering [2, 12, 13].

HT is characterized by megakaryocyte hyperplasia driven by overactivation of the TPO-MPL pathway [11]. In contrast to ET, HT results from germline variants involving THPO, MPL, and JAK2, the genes coding for human thrombopoietin, its receptor, and an intracellular signaling protein, respectively [11, 14].

The first activating germline JAK2 variant encoding a residue other than V617F identified in association with a hereditary MPN-like syndrome was c.1691 G > A (within exon 13; numbering according to NM_004972.4), which encodes p.Arg564Gln (R564Q), a gain-of-function missense variant that substitutes glutamine in place of arginine at amino acid 564 [5, 7].

Etheridge et al. first described germline JAK2 R564Q variants presenting as HT in three family members with previously unexplained thrombocytosis [5]. Through functional studies, the authors found that although JAK2 R564Q is located on the same pseudokinase domain as V617F and results in similarly increased levels of tyrosine phosphorylation of JAK2 and downstream signaling STAT proteins, JAK2 R564Q variants are associated with milder promotion of platelet production and greater response to JAK inhibitors compared to V617F [5, 7, [8]. They proposed that this is likely a result of counterregulatory measures from which JAK2 V617F-expressing cells can escape, while JAK2 R564Q-expressing cells cannot [5]. Specifically, JAK2 V617F disrupts the JH2 pseudokinase domain which regulates the catalytic activity of the JH1 tyrosine kinase domain, resulting in constitutive activation [5]. In addition to having more mild effects on cellular proliferation, they found that cells harboring JAK2 R564Q variants were more sensitive to the effects of the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib compared to those with JAK2 V617F variants, with clinically significant responses at 1000-fold lower concentrations [5].

Similarly, Stewart et al. recently described a second family with the germline JAK2 R564Q variant presenting with familial MPNs over three generations [8]. The authors also described nine additional cases of individuals with germline JAK2 R564L variants, who were phenotypically more diverse than those with R564Q variants and presented with various MPN-like syndromes (3 cases of HT; 3 cases of HE) and other hematopoietic malignancies (acute myeloid leukemia (AML); chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL); monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) [8]. Among the cases of HE, two had concurrent JAK2 V617F variants [8].

Case 1

The first proband is a 41-year-old woman with a long-standing history of unexplained thrombocytosis and intermittent leukocytosis. Over a 14-year period, her platelet count fluctuated between 311 × 109/L and 750 × 109/L. Other pertinent past medical history included recurrent early pregnancy loss, low protein S activity, psoriatic arthritis, and iron deficiency anemia. She had no history of known thrombotic events. The patient’s thrombocytosis was initially believed to be secondary to iron deficiency anemia and/or autoimmune disease. She was placed on oral iron supplementation, but no anti-thrombotics. A bone marrow biopsy demonstrated a variably cellular bone marrow ( ~ 50%) with trilineage hematopoiesis, no dysplasia, and mildly increased numbers of megakaryocytes, without any pathologic diagnosis rendered. NGS analysis of the bone marrow sample was notable for a JAK2 R564Q variant at a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 51%, highly suggestive of a germline variant. Variants in CALR and MPL were not identified. The patient was referred for genetic counseling and confirmatory testing. A skin biopsy was performed, and comprehensive germline cancer risk testing on DNA derived from cultured skin fibroblasts confirmed the JAK2 R564Q variant to be germline, with a classification as a variant of unknown significance (VUS). The patient’s one-year old daughter was also found to have thrombocytosis (700 × 109/L), but germline testing was declined by the patient. Additional family members declined cascade testing. The patient was treated with prophylactic low-dose aspirin during pregnancy but otherwise is being managed with observation alone.

Case 2

The second proband is a 45-year-old woman with a longstanding history of unexplained thrombocytosis since age 23. Over an 8-year period, her platelet count was documented between 351 × 109/L and 850 × 109/L. Other pertinent past medical history included recurrent early pregnancy loss and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) secondary to spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) of the distal left anterior descending artery, which was further complicated by pericarditis and left ventricular (LV) thrombus. She was managed with long-term antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy with aspirin and warfarin. Detailed family history was notable for unexplained thrombocytosis in her father and daughter; breast cancer in her mother, maternal grandmother, and sister of maternal grandmother; and early myocardial infarction in her mother and maternal grandfather. The patient underwent a bone marrow biopsy in 2017, which was notable for a hypercellular marrow with increased megakaryocytes, which was favored to be reactive at the time. NGS analysis of the bone marrow revealed a JAK2 R564Q variant with VAF of 51%, suspicious for a germline allele. Variants in CALR and MPL were not identified. The patient was eventually referred for genetic counseling, and testing via buccal swab confirmed the germline JAK2 R564Q variant. The laboratory classified the variant as a VUS. In addition to continuation of previously prescribed antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy, the patient is managed with observation of her peripheral blood cell counts every 3–6 months.

Case 3

The third proband is a 48-year-old female with a longstanding history of unexplained thrombocytosis over several decades. Over a 2-year period, her platelet count ranged from 609 × 109/L to 692 × 109/L. Other pertinent medical history included hyperlipidemia and pre-diabetes. Detailed family history was notable for colon cancer in her mother, multiple myeloma in her maternal aunt, and leukemia of unknown type in her maternal cousin. There was no personal or family history of thrombotic events. NGS analysis of her peripheral blood revealed a JAK2 R564Q variant with VAF 51%, suspicious for a germline allele. The patient declined having a bone marrow biopsy. The patient underwent comprehensive cancer risk testing using DNA derived from cultured skin fibroblasts from a different clinical laboratory compared to the other two cases described above. The only germline cancer risk allele identified was the JAK2 R564Q variant, which was classified as likely pathogenic (LP). She is being followed conservatively, without anti-thrombotics.

Discussion

MPNs are classically attributed to somatic variants in JAK2, CALR, and MPL. More comprehensive and accessible NGS testing has allowed for greater appreciation of the genomic landscape of MPNs, including identification of rare cases of low population frequency/high penetrance germline variants in genes such as JAK2, resulting in familial MPN-like syndromes, HT and ET.

JAK2 R564Q is the first missense variant identified other than V617F to be associated with hereditary MPN-like syndromes, specifically HT, in previous reports by Etheridge et al. and more recently Stewart et al. [5, 8]. Here, we present the cases of three unrelated individuals with HT and the germline JAK2 R564Q variant. Two of these individuals were tested in 2023, and received clinical reports in which this variant was classified as a VUS, whereas the third individual, tested more recently and from a distinct laboratory, received a clinical report designating the variant as LP. As expected, ClinVar lists this variant with conflicting classifications, with one entry from August 2024, as VUS, and another entry from one month later from a different laboratory as LP. Conflicting classifications within ClinVar present challenges for other clinical laboratories, clinicians who may be aware of the conflicting classifications and uncertain of how to frame alternative classifications for patients and their families, and patients, who if informed of the conflict, may feel less confident in medical opinion. These three cases provide additional segregation data that should sway consensus regarding the pathogenicity of this variant toward LP.

Although rare, these cases offer some insight into the significance of certain germline JAK2 variants and potential key clinical distinctions from somatic variants resulting in classical MPNs. Perhaps the most notable distinction is that familial MPN-like syndromes are non-clonal and thus have significantly lower risk of leukemic transformation [7, 11]. Additionally, the association with thrombotic events appears to be rare in those with HT and HE [7]. These key distinctions suggest that classical MPNs and hereditary MPN-like syndromes are phenotypically quite distinct and should not be treated as a single entity. Further studies are needed to form consensus expert opinions on the screening and management of those with MPN-like syndromes.

Cannova et al. recently called for universal germline testing in all individuals with MPNs, regardless of whether somatic driver mutations have been identified [15]. While greater access to germline testing is ideal, it may be technically challenging at this time. We propose that germline testing should at minimum be offered to patients presenting at a young age, those with apparent familial clustering, and those with triple negative/idiopathic disease. Additionally, those found to have gene variants with abnormal functional testing and VAFs near (especially consistently) 50% should be prioritized for germline counseling and testing. We advocate for broad, comprehensive cancer risk testing whenever possible to avoid the ascertainment bias inherent to the family history.

Regarding patient management, there are currently no guidelines for the treatment of those with activating germline JAK2 variants. Although germline JAK2 variants encoding missense mutations involving residues other then V617F tend to have milder impacts on cellular proliferation due to inability to escape counterregulatory measures and lower risk of thrombotic complications, it may be reasonable to adopt a similar approach as classical MPNs. Although complications appear to be relatively infrequent, these patients should be followed closely and monitored for development of thrombotic complications and leukemic transformation. Additional studies are needed to better characterize the risk of leukemic transformation in individuals with non-clonal MPN-like syndromes and create guidelines for their management. Additionally, it is worth noting that hereditary MPN-like syndromes tend to present at a younger age than classical MPNs, which typically occur between the sixth and seventh decade of life. Therefore, patients with hereditary MPN-like syndromes may be at increased risk for accumulation of additional somatic variants, particularly in the setting of increased proliferation, which could result in increased risk of clonal MPNs or leukemic transformation over the lifespan.

Finally, clinical laboratories identifying germline DNA variants are required to provide a functional classification on their reports. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology have outlined general DNA variant curation rules [16] that are specified in a gene-specific manner by Variant Curation Expert Panels (VCEPs) organized by ClinGen. However, no VCEPs have developed JAK2-specific curation rules. In their absence, clinical laboratories must rely on the published literature to count segregation data, both segregations of the DNA variant with disease within a family as well as segregations of disease in independent probands [17–19]. Thus, we hope that these three cases will allow clinical laboratories to curate the JAK2 R564Q allele as LP based on our and prior cases [5, 8].
