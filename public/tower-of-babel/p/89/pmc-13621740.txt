Coxsackievirus A6 infection exacerbates kidney injury in mice through the p38-MAPK pathway
Authors: Tiantian Sun, Wenjie Jiang, Guangcai Duan, Dong Li, Wangquan Ji, Shuaiyin Chen, Haiyan Yang, Fang Wang, Shouhang Chen, Yuefei Jin, Peiyu Zhu, Mingjie Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) poses a risk of serious multi-organ complications in children. While renal dysfunction has been observed in some cases, detailed investigations on kidney injury and molecular mechanisms remain limited. Coxsackievirus A6 (CVA6) has emerged as a major pathogen responsible for HFMD. To reveal the underlying pathways of CVA6-associated renal injury, we enrolled 90 children diagnosed with CVA6 infection and 45 healthy children. The levels of uric acid (UA), creatinine (CREA) and UREA in the serum were measured. We found that serum levels of UA, CREA, and UREA were significantly elevated in CVA6-infected children compared to healthy children. Subsequently, a previously established mouse model of CVA6 infection was used to assess urinary retention, kidney inflammation, and histopathological changes. Consistent with the clinical findings, infected mice exhibited increased CREA, UREA, and UA levels, urinary retention, and renal inflammation. Electron microscopy revealed glomerular atrophy and basement membrane thickening in CVA6-infected mice, accompanied by reduced expression of VEGF, Occludin, and VE-cadherin. Furthermore, complement deposition was also observed in glomeruli and renal tubules. Finally, CVA6 infection significantly increased phospho-p38 levels in mouse kidneys. To investigate the role of the p38-MAPK signaling pathway in CVA6-induced renal injury, we treated the CVA6-infected mice with PD169316, a specific inhibitor of p38/MAPK signaling pathway, and observed that the kidney injury in the treated group was alleviated. Overall, our findings highlight a critical role for the p38 phosphorylation in CVA6-induced renal injury, and provide new insights into the management of severe HFMD-associated kidney complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03236-9.

Introduction

Hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) is an acute, highly contagious viral illness primarily affecting young children, caused by various serotypes of human Enteroviruses (EVs) [1–3]. The main pathogens responsible for HFMD include EV-A71 and Coxsackievirus (CV)A6 [4]. With the extensive rollout of EV-A71 vaccination across Asia, the epidemiological pattern of HFMD has evolved significantly, marked by a notable increase in cases attributed to CVA6, which has emerged as a leading cause of HFMD outbreaks in many regions [5, 6]. CVA6 infections manifest as mild symptoms, including herpangina and a distinctive rash, or may even be asymptomatic [7]. However, some children develop severe complications such as encephalitis, acute flaccid paralysis, even fatal pulmonary edema and septicemia [3]. At present, the number of children infected with CVA6 continues to rise annually, but the mechanisms of systemic damage (especially kidney injury) caused by severe CVA6 infection remains unclear [8, 9].

EVs typically enter the body through the digestive tract and subsequently invade the intestine, and evade the immune system and spreads to various organs via the lymphatic and circulatory systems, ultimately leading to widespread organ damage. The organ damage induced by virus infection primarily manifests in the cardiac and neurological systems [10, 11]. Notably, renal involvement has been reported in a number of studies as part of the systemic manifestations associated with EVs infections. The autopsy report of an infant infected with CVA10 revealed the presence of virus in multiple organ tissues, including the kidneys [12]. In 2011, a pediatric hospital in China reported acute kidney injury (AKI) in patients caused by EV-A71 infection [13]. Children co-infected with EV-A71 and CVA16 were also diagnosed with AKI, presenting clinical manifestations such as hypertension, decreased urine output, mild proteinuria, and impaired kidney function [9, 14]. Our previous research established a severe animal model by intraperitoneal injection of CVA6 and detected viral antigens in the kidneys of CVA6-infected mice, along with renal edema [11]. However, the specific mechanism by which CVA6 causes kidney injury is unclear. Therefore, we collected plasma from children with CVA6-induced HFMD and utilized the previously constructed mouse model of CVA6 infection to explore the effects of CVA6 on the kidneys. Our study contributes to a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of HFMD-associated kidney injury, improve comprehension of the disease, and provide potential intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Our study enrolled pediatric patients with HFMD who were hospitalized in the Henan Children’s Hospital from January to December 2018. Diagnosis and classification of cases were established in accordance with the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hand, Foot and Mouth Disease (2010 Edition). Based on the established diagnostic criteria and patient data, the complications of 217 severe children infected with CVA6 were analyzed. 45 healthy children (HC) recruited from Henan Children’s Hospital in 2018 were matched with 45 children with mild CVA6 infection and 45 children with severe CVA6 infection respectively by age and sex.

Cell culture and CVA6 strains

African green monkey kidney cell (Vero cell) and human rhabdomyosarcoma cell (RD cell) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd, Waltham, US) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco Co., New York, NY, US) and incubated at 37 °C at 5% CO2. The CVA6 strain (GenBank: OM179765) was isolated from stool samples from a case of HFMD. The virus titer was determined to be 105 TCID50/mL, by 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) following the method of Reed and Muench.

Animal infection experiments

The specific-pathogen-free (SPF) ICR mice used in our study was provided by the Medical Experimental Animal Center of Henan Province of Zhengzhou University. All mice were raised at an SPF laboratory environment at the College of Public Health of Zhengzhou University for a 12 h light/dark cycle. We introduced a mouse-infection model based on intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) inoculating 10-day-old ICR mice with a lethal dose of CVA6 strain (104 TCID50/mouse) [11]. Kidney was either soaked with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded with paraffin for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunofluorescence (IF), and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Kidneys were collected and used to extract total RNA and total protein. The weight and clinical scores of mice were recorded 0–15 days after infection (dpi). The grade of clinical disease was scored as follows: 0, healthy; 1, lethargy and inactivity; 2, ataxic; 3, losing weight; 4, hind limb paralysis; 5, moribund or death. Mice with a clinical score of less than two points within 3–4 days of infection with CVA6 were defined as mild. Mice with there-five points of symptoms within 5–10 days were defined as severe. The control mice were healthy during the experiments. To investigate therapeutic intervention, PD169316 (10 mg/kg; Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 3 h post-infection (hpi) and subsequently at 1, 2, and 3 dpi. Serum biochemical analyses were performed by Servicebio Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Finally, mice were euthanized via inhalation of isoflurane. Specifically, each animal was placed in an induction chamber prefilled with 4–5% isoflurane. After loss of consciousness was confirmed (by the absence of muscle tone and cessation of rhythmic breathing), the animal was maintained under deep anesthesia via continuous exposure to isoflurane until death. Death was confirmed by the absence of spontaneous respiration, followed by verification of cardiac arrest through thoracic palpation. All tissue collection procedures were performed post-mortem.

Biochemical analysis

After the mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, the thoracic cavity was opened, and blood was collected via cardiac puncture from the apical region of the heart. The collected blood was allowed to clot at room temperature, and the upper serum layer was subsequently separated by centrifugation. The levels of creatinine (CREA), urea (UREA), and uric acid (UA) in serum were measured by Biochemical kit (URIT-500B) or on hospital testing platforms.

Transmission electron microscopy

After deep anesthesia with isoflurane in mice, and images were quickly collected, and then the kidneys were collected. Renal cortical tissue (1 mm³) was isolated and placed in a fixation solution for electron microscopy. The samples were fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature for 2 h, dehydrated through a series of alcohol gradients, and then embedded overnight in an embedding medium. The embedded tissue was subsequently placed in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h. Afterward, 80 nm ultrathin sections were prepared, positioned, and stained, and images were captured using a transmission electron microscope for further analysis. The electron microscopy service was provided by Servicebio Biotech Co., Ltd.

Histopathological analyses

Kidney was collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Subsequently, the paraffin-embedded kidneys were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm and stained with H&E staining. The score is indicated by a number ranging from 0 (normal) to 5 (severe inflammation). Complement 3 (C3) and C5a were determined by IHC staining. p-p38 and CD45 were detected by IF staining.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from kidney using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme Biotechnology, Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China), and RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The transcription levels of relevant genes were normalized to those of the β-actin gene and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences for the CVA6 VP1 gene-forward: 5’-CAAGCTGCAGAAACGGGAG-3’ and reverse: 5’-GCTCCACACTCGCCTCATT-3’-were synthesized by BGI (Beijing, China). The viral load was calculated according to our previous study [11].

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from kidney by using Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and mixed with an equal amount of 2×sodium dodecyl sulfate loading buffer. The proteins were first separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were washed three times, incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h. Finally, the membranes were developed using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence kit (Absin Bioscience, Inc., Shanghai, China).

Antibodies

The following antibodies were used in this study: anti-ZO-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 13663), anti-Occludin (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 91131), anti-VE-Cadherin(Affinity Biosciences, Ltd, Cat#: AF6265), anti-VEGF (R&D System, Inc., Cat#: MAB293); anti-p-ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 4370), anti-ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 4695), anti-p-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185) (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 9255), anti-JNK (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 4672), anti-p-p38 (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 9216), anti-p38 (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Cat#: 8690); anti-CD45 (Servicebio Biotech Co., Ltd, Cat#: GB113886-100), anti-mouse IgG and anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech Biotech Co., Ltd, Cat#: SA00001-1/2). MAPKAPK2 (Absin Biotech Co., Ltd, Cat#: abs178630), p-MAPKAPK2 (Absin Biotech Co., Ltd, Cat#: abs173005).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis and Graphs were made using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad 8.3 Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Adobe Illustrator CS6 software (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA) was used for figures composition. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. The Mantel-Cox log rank test was used to evaluate differences in survival between groups, and a survival curve was plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method. The Mann-Whitney test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences in relative protein expression or transcription levels. A P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Renal parameters in children with CVA6 infection

A total of 90 children infected with CVA6, admitted to Henan Children’s Hospital, were enrolled in our study. The baseline details and clinical assessments of the patients upon admission are presented in Table 1. Among the children infected with CVA6, severe cases ultimately developed complications including sepsis, myocardial injury, liver injury, and encephalitis. Notably, the incidence of sepsis was the highest, accounting for 60.53% of severe cases (Fig. 1A). Given that sepsis is a primary precipitating factor for AKI and exacerbates patient prognosis, and the onset of AKI significantly aggravates the condition of septic patients, we further measured the plasma levels of UREA, CREA, and UA in the patients upon admission. As shown in Fig. 1B-D, compared to the HCs, the infected children exhibited significantly lower plasma UREA levels (P < 0.001), while UA and CREA levels were significantly elevated (P < 0.05). These results suggest that the occurrence of severe complications, particularly sepsis, in children with HFMD may be associated with alterations in renal function parameters.

CVA6 infection caused kidney injury in mice

Using the established CVA6-infected mice model, we observed that both mildly and severely infected mice exhibited urinary retention compared with the control group (Fig. 2A). Histological examination revealed collapsed glomeruli, accompanied by dilatation of capillary loops, widened collecting duct spaces, and prominent inflammatory infiltration in the kidneys of CVA6-infected mice (Fig. 2B and C). In contrast, renal tissues from control mice showed no significant histopathological alterations. We further detected the presence of CVA6 viral antigen in renal tissue, and the results confirmed direct renal infection by CVA6 (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, serum levels of UREA, CREA, and UA were measured to assess renal function (Fig. 2E-G). The CVA6-infected group showed significantly elevated levels of CREA, UREA, and UA relative to the control mice (P < 0.05). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CVA6 directly targets the kidney, leading to pronounced renal pathology and dysfunction.

Glomerular permeability changes and complement deposition in CVA6-infected mice

To further elucidate the impact of CVA6 infection, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was adopted. CVA6-infected mice exhibited significant glomerular atrophy, extensive fusion of foot processes, reduction or absence of fenestrations in endothelial cells, accompanied by a marked increase in glomerular basement membrane (GBM) thickness through transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the structure of the glomerular capillary loops, podocytes, endothelial cells, and basement membrane in the control mice appeared normal. Additionally, quantitative analysis of basement membrane width revealed a significant increase in the infected group. Since the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier is vital for renal function, we assessed the expression of tight junction proteins. As shown in Fig. 3B, the expression of the ZO-1, Occludin, VE-Cadherin, and VEGF was significantly reduced in the kidney of infected mice compared to the control mice (Fig. 3B and C). Moreover, the severity of injury was more pronounced in the severe group than in the mild group. Given that glomerular permeability is associated with complement deposition, we detected the deposition of C3 and C5a in glomeruli and renal tubules by immunohistochemistry. As shown in Fig. 3D, CVA6 infection induced complement deposition, particularly evident as C3 accumulation within renal tubules of severely infected mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CVA6 disrupts the glomerular filtration barrier through structural damage and molecular dysregulation, thereby impairing renal function.

Inhibition of the p38/MAPK signaling pathway alleviated kidney injury

Given the pivotal role of complement in mediating chemotaxis and amplifying local inflammatory responses, we assessed the activation of MAPK signaling pathways. As shown in Fig. 4A, CVA6 infection robustly activated the MAPK cascade in mouse kidneys, as evidenced by elevated phosphorylation levels of ERK, JNK, and p38. This marked enhancement of p38 phosphorylation was quantitatively confirmed by Image J in both mildly and severely affected animals compared to the control group (Fig. 4B-D). IF staining further revealed enhanced p-p38 signals localized within infiltrating inflammatory cells and resident renal cells (Fig. 4E). Moreover, the phosphorylation of MAPKAPK2, a downstream effector of p38, was also markedly upregulated (Fig. 4F). To elucidate the role of p38 hyperphosphorylation in CVA6 infection, we administered the p38/MAPK inhibitor to infected mice. As illustrated in Fig. 5A-C, mice treated with PD169316 exhibited slight improvements in clinical symptom scores and body weight from 0 dpi to 15 dpi, compared to the untreated infected group. Although PD169316 did not significantly reduce the mortality outcome caused by systemic injury induced by CVA6 infection, inhibition of p38 phosphorylation markedly alleviated severe urinary retention (Fig. 5D), and reduced pathological damage in both the renal medulla and cortex (Fig. 5E and F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CVA6 infection exacerbates renal inflammation by activating the p38/MAPK signaling pathway, and that pharmacological inhibition of p38 phosphorylation effectively mitigates virus-induced AKI.

Discussion

Our study confirms that CVA6 infection induces AKI in children with severe HFMD, primarily characterized by complement-mediated basement membrane destruction and subsequent hyperactivation of the p38/MAPK inflammatory signaling pathway. Children constitute the highest-risk population for sepsis-associated AKI. Among children with severe HFMD caused by CVA6 infection, 60% develop complications of sepsis. Sepsis, a common complication of HFMD, is also the most the leading cause of AKI in critically ill children [15]. Rapid increases in serum CREA levels and decreases in urine volume are key indicators for assess AKI [16]. Our study found significant increases in serum CREA and UA in both CVA6-infected children, suggesting that patients may progress to AKI. We employed the lethal CVA6 infection mouse model established by Li et al. [11], utilizing i.p. inoculation of 10-day-old ICR mice with the CVA6 strain (104 TCID50/mouse); further investigations revealed that infection with CVA6 resulted in marked urinary retention and significant pathological changes in the kidney. Notably, the occurrence of urinary retention and abnormal renal pathology are important manifestations of the decline in renal function caused by viral infection [17]. Meanwhile, we detected abnormally elevated serum CREA, UREA and UA levels in the infected mice, confirming the abnormal renal function caused by CVA6 infection. A patient with acute renal failure secondary to CVB3 infection experienced a rapid decrease in UREA within 48 h after admission, which was consistent with the results observed in our study [18]. Furthermore, aberrant fluctuations in serum CREA and UA levels have similarly been utilized to identify renal impairment in patients with severe SARS-CoV-2 infection [19]. Li et al. reported significantly increased serum CREA levels and decreased urine output in critically ill children infected with EV-A71 [9, 13]. In summary, kidney dysfunction is a significant contributor to AKI and renal failure, which present major challenge for the treatment and prognosis of children with severe HFMD [20].

Using our established CVA6 infection model, we confirmed that CVA6 directly targets the kidneys, as evidenced by viral replication in renal tissues [11]. This direct nephrotropism is consistent with the detection of EV-A71 VP1 antigen in glomerular tissues [21], and mirrors the pathogenesis of other enteroviruses, such as CVA10, which induces acute renal inflammation in rhesus macaques [22]. Viral infection leads to glomerular dysfunction mainly by damaging the endothelial structure and disrupting the glomerular filtration function [23]. Endothelial cells and basement membranes, as the core for glomerulus to perform filtration functions, are crucial for excreting metabolic wastes and regulating the stability of the internal environment. Mice infected with CVA6 showed obvious glomerular collapse and severe GBM destruction under electron microscopy. In extremely rare cases of AKI caused by Hantavirus infection, the kidneys show a significant increase in inflammatory cells and renal tubule dilation [24, 25]. The main reason might be that the virus invades the kidneys, triggering an immune response. Subsequently, the inflammatory environment triggers and amplifies the activation of the complement system, further intensifying local inflammation and ultimately leading to the collapse of the GBM and the dilation of renal tubules [26]. As our assessment results of complement deposition in the glomerulus and renal tubules show, C3 and C5a deposition was significant in the infected group of mice, especially in the renal tubules. SARS-CoV-2 infection also activated the complement pathway in the kidneys of patients, with the highest deposition of C3 in renal tubules, similar to our results [27].

Subsequently, we explored the downstream inflammatory signaling pathways of complement activation. Activation of the JNK and p38/MAPK signaling pathways is crucial for the secretion of inflammatory cytokines in nephritis [28]. Therefore, we investigated downstream effectors of this inflammatory cascade and found that the p38/MAPK signaling pathway was the most prominently activated in CVA6-infected kidneys, surpassing JNK and ERK activation. p38/MAPK signaling pathway is a key signaling pathway for the progression of AKI, regulating the progression of inflammation and apoptosis [29–31]. Moreover, CVA6 infection significantly enhanced MAPKAPK2 phosphorylation, demonstrating that p38/MAPK hyperactivation mediates virus-induced renal injury. The p38 phosphorylation inhibitor, PD169316, plays a significant role in protecting against renal tubular injury [32, 33]. Accordingly, we employed PD169316 to target the p38/MAPK signaling, which robustly protected against CVA6-induced renal damage. This therapeutic efficacy aligns with recent reports demonstrating that PD169316 reduces viral load and cytokine storms in EV-A71-infected mice [34]. At present, the arsenal of drugs specifically targeting inflammatory signaling pathways for preventing or treating AKI remains limited. the arsenal of drugs specifically targeting inflammatory signaling pathways for preventing or treating AKI remains limited. In the future, we will systematically evaluate the efficacy of various anti-enterovirus agents to determine their feasibility in mitigating renal injury induced by CVA6 infection [35].

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate that CVA6 infection induces glomerular basement membrane remodeling and renal impairment via complement deposition and activation of the p38/MAPK signaling pathway. In addition, targeting the p38/MAPK signaling pathway represents a potential therapeutic option for managing CVA6-induced renal injury.
