Non-structural carbohydrates in root and stem show contrasting relationships with tree growth and drought resilience in a semiarid forest
Authors: Yang Qi, Hongyan Liu, Jingyu Dai, Wenqi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-structural carbohydrates (NSCs) are central to plant carbon metabolism, acting as critical buffers that regulate tree growth and survival under environmental stress. In semiarid regions, increasing hydroclimatic variability poses significant challenges to forest sustainability. However, whether different NSC components (soluble sugars vs. starch) across distinct organs (aboveground vs. belowground) exhibit consistent relationships with tree growth and post-drought resilience remains poorly understood. We quantified soluble sugar and starch concentrations in leaves, branches, stems, and roots of four coexisting tree species in the Saihanwula Nature Reserve during the growing season. Principal component analysis, hierarchical partitioning, and linear mixed-effects models were combined with tree-ring-derived growth and drought resilience indices to evaluate the relationships between organ-specific NSC composition, radial growth, and drought resilience.ResultsWe observed clear interspecific differences in NSC composition and drought resilience traits among the four coexisting tree species. Organ-specific NSC composition was more strongly associated with radial growth than with drought resilience. Within these relationships, organ-specific NSCs were explained as distinct functional specialization. Fine-root starch associated positively with radial growth and growth stability. Conversely, starch accumulation in stems was negatively associated with structural growth, strongly corroborating a growth–storage trade-off. In particular, readily metabolizable branch soluble sugars positively associated with post-drought growth recovery and hydraulic repairment following extreme water deficit.ConclusionsOur results suggested that NSC composition differed substantially among organs and species in this semiarid forest ecosystem. Soluble sugars and starch exhibited contrasting relationships with tree growth and drought resilience across aboveground and belowground organs. These findings highlight the importance of considering organ-specific NSC components separately when evaluating tree growth responses and drought resilience under increasing climate variability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09556-w.Keywords: Carbon metabolism, Drought resilience, Non-structural carbohydrates (NSC), Organ-specific partitioning, Semi-arid forests, Radial growth

Introduction

Increasing interannual precipitation variability has been widely documented as a consequence of climate change, with its impacts most pronounced in arid and semiarid regions [1, 2]. As temperate forest ecosystems are critical components of terrestrial ecosystems, their resilience—the ability to resist disturbances and maintain or restore structure and function—is of vital importance in the context of global environmental change [3, 4]. Tree resilience is commonly expressed through growth resistance and post-disturbance recovery, which determine the capacity of trees to withstand extreme climatic events such as drought and to resume growth in subsequent years [5]. Here, growth resistance refers to the ability to maintain radial growth during drought, whereas growth recovery denotes the capacity to restore growth post-drought. In water-limited ecosystems, recurrent drought events strongly constrain tree growth and survival, making tree growth performance and drought resistance and recovery critical components of forest resilience.

Carbon allocation to non-structural carbohydrates (NSCs) plays a central role in regulating tree growth and drought responses. NSCs include soluble sugars and starch, representing the readily available mobile fraction and the storage carbon pool of plants, respectively. NSCs are closely linked with multiple physiological processes, including respiration, osmoregulation, hydraulic maintenance (e.g. reducing vulnerability to xylem embolism), and stress tolerance [6]. Functionally, soluble sugars are directly involved in metabolic activity and osmotic regulation to prevent cavitation, whereas starch primarily functions as a longer-term carbon reserve that can be remobilized when carbon demand exceeds supply [6, 7]. Because soluble sugars and starch differ substantially in their physiological functions, they may also differ in their relationships with tree growth and drought resilience. However, whether different NSC components are consistently associated with variation in tree growth resistance and post-drought recovery remains unclear.

Patterns of NSC allocation among different organs further reflect species-specific drought-related trait strategies. Under drought stress, trees tend to accumulate starch in stems [8], facilitating its rapid mobilization when needed. In contrast, several studies report declines in root NSC concentrations during drought, suggesting belowground carbon depletion or altered allocation priorities under prolonged stress [9, 10]. Soluble sugars in canopy tissues are often associated with rapid metabolic adjustment during drought recovery, whereas starch concentrations in woody tissues may reflect longer-term carbon retention [11, 12]. More recent studies have recognized NSCs as key components of plant drought adaptation strategies, and different functional groups exhibit different NSC adaptation strategies under drought stress [13]. Previous studies have shown that evergreen species exhibit smaller seasonal fluctuations in NSCs than deciduous species [14, 15], and that coniferous and broadleaf species differ substantially in NSC dynamics and allocation strategies [16], reflecting contrasting physiological responses to environmental stress. Despite increasing interest in NSC dynamics across organs and species groups, relatively few studies have examined how multi-organ NSC concentration patterns are associated with interspecific variation in tree growth resistance and recovery under drought conditions.

The forest-steppe ecotone in semiarid regions represents a marginal area of forest distribution characterized by high interannual rainfall variability [17]. In these regions, increasing precipitation variability is expected to further amplify the effects of drought, posing significant challenges to forest survival and growth [18, 19]. Consequently, forests in these areas experience a range of drought-induced stresses and are likely to be highly sensitive to drought events intensified by climate change [20–22]. Species growing in these environments therefore exhibit pronounced differences in growth performance and drought responses, which may be associated with contrasting NSC concentration patterns among organs and functional types.

This study was conducted in a natural forest within the Saihanwula Nature Reserve in Inner Mongolia, China, where coniferous and broad-leaved tree species coexist under semiarid climatic conditions. We quantified soluble sugar and starch concentrations in leaves, branches, stems, and roots during the growing season and examined their relationships with tree-ring-derived radial growth and drought resilience indices. We hypothesize that (1) soluble sugars and starch would show contrasting associations with tree growth and drought resilience, corresponding to differences between metabolically available carbon and longer-term carbon reserves; and (2) NSC concentrations in aboveground and belowground organs would differ in their relationships with drought resistance and recovery because these tissues perform distinct physiological functions during drought stress and post-drought recovery.

Materials and methods

Study site and hydro-climatic conditions

Saihanwula National Nature Reserve (43°59’–44°27’N, 118°18’–118°55’E) is located in the mid-temperate semi-humid and warm climate zone of China. The average annual precipitation is 374.9 mm, while the annual evaporation is 2050 mm. Precipitation is highly seasonal and unevenly distributed throughout the year. Approximately 70–80% of the annual precipitation occurs during the summer months (June to August), which largely coincides with the peak growing season of the plants. Despite the concentration of precipitation in summer, high evaporative demand during the growing season frequently results in soil water deficits and episodic drought stress. Annual precipitation showed no significant long-term increasing or decreasing trend during the study period (P > 0.05), and annual precipitation ranged from approximately 200 mm to 700 mm among years (Fig. 1).

The dashed line indicates the linear regression trend for annual precipitation. The years of extreme drought (2002, 2007, 2011) are highlighted by red open circles

The Palmer drought severity index (PDSI) was employed as an indicator of soil moisture availability for tree growth, as it integrates the cumulative effects of antecedent precipitation and the lagged response of vegetation to drought conditions [23]. Annual PDSI values from 1970 to 2019 were used to characterize long-term hydro-climatic variability in the study region. To ensure independence among drought events and improve the robustness of resilience estimates, we identified extreme drought years based on local PDSI minima within a seven-year moving window. These years corresponded to periods of severe water deficit (PDSI < − 3.5) followed by a progressive alleviation of drought intensity in subsequent years, rather than a full return to long-term mean conditions [4, 24]. Accordingly, 2002, 2007, and 2011, were selected as extreme drought years (Fig. 1). In 2019, annual precipitation was 328.1 mm, and mean annual PDSI was − 0.61, both falling within the central range of long-term hydro-climatic variability, indicating that sampling was conducted under non-extreme climatic conditions.

Plot survey and species selection

Field sampling was carried out within a 6-ha permanent forest plot representing a typical temperate deciduous broadleaved forest. In 2019, comprehensive forest inventories were conducted. We established three 10 m × 10 m representative subplots to record the dendrometry characteristics of all woody individuals with a diameter at breast height (DBH) > 1 cm. Recorded parameters included species identity, tree height, and DBH. Based on the importance value and dominance, four co-occurring tree species in the plot—Betula platyphylla, Populus davidiana, Quercus mongolica, and Larix gmelinii var. principis-rupprechtii (hereafter L. gmelinii)— as representative subjects to encompass distinct functional types (coniferous vs. broad-leaved) and wood anatomical structures (Table 1). Tree species in these forests differ in growth phenology and stem anatomy. Ring-porous species exhibit earlier cambial reactivation and more distinct seasonal growth patterns than diffuse-porous species, which may reflect differences in xylem formation strategies and seasonal carbon requirements [25]. These differences provide a physiological context for comparing organ-specific NSC allocation patterns among species with contrasting anatomical and functional characteristics.

All the studied tree species in this region are deciduous. Their seasonal leaf shedding is primarily an evolutionary adaptation to avoid frost damage during the severely cold winters, though it concurrently serves as a drought-avoidance strategy to prevent excessive water loss during the dry non-growing season.

To better characterize the diverse physiological strategies of the studied species, key hydraulic traits, including branch specific hydraulic conductivity (Kh) and stem water potential at a 50% loss of conductivity (P50), were concurrently measured in situ during the 2019 field campaign. Detailed protocols for the hydraulic trait measurements are described in previous work [26].

Tree-ring data and basal area increment

To compare stem growth rates across species, tree-ring cores were sampled in July 2019 using a 5 mm inner diameter increment borer. For each species, two cores were extracted from a minimum of 12 trees at the study site. Cores were taken at breast height (1.3 m) on opposite sides of the trunk, parallel to the contour. After air-drying, the cores were progressively polished with fine-grit sandpaper until tree-ring boundaries were clearly visible. All samples were cross-dated following standard dendrochronological techniques [27]. Tree-ring widths were measured with a LINTAB measuring system with an accuracy of 0.001 mm. The quality of cross-dating was verified using the COFECHA program [28], and cores with series intercorrelation below 0.6 were re-examined and re-dated when necessary to ensure measurement reliability. After this rigorous quality control process, a total of 56 cores were retained for subsequent analysis, representing 12 trees for Q. mongolica, 6 trees for L. gmelinii, and 5 trees each for B. platyphylla and P. davidiana. These retained samples showed acceptable cross-dating quality and were used for subsequent analyses. Tree growth variability and drought resistance and recovery

The coefficient of variation (CV) of BAI was selected to evaluate intra-specific growth variability. The CV is dimensionless and standardizes variation relative to the mean, facilitating more meaningful comparisons across species compared to variance or standard deviation [31].

Non-structural carbohydrate measurements

While taking the tree ring core sample, we collected plant tissues to measure NSC concentration for each tree in July 2019. Sampling was conducted during the middle of the growing season, when canopy development was complete and radial growth remained active, allowing organ-specific NSC composition to be evaluated during the principal period of carbon assimilation. To minimize the potential confounding effects of diurnal variability on leaf NSC, all tissue samplings were strictly conducted within a narrow time window between 9:00 and 12:00. Considering that the tree canopy position has no significant effect on the branch NSC concentration in previous studies [32], this study did not sample according to the canopy position. The first-level branches in the middle of the canopy were randomly selected to standardize sampling procedures and minimize potential microenvironmental variation among individuals. Branches in healthy growth were cut off with branch shears. Leaves on the branches were picked off, with the leaves without insect eggs collected for NSC measurement. Stem samples were collected using an increment borer, with four tree cores collected at the breast height of each sample tree. During sampling, the drilling position was slightly moved, and the sampling was in a Z-shape to avoid overlapping with the previous sampling position and decrease the experimental error. Two root samples from each tree were excavated using iron picks, shovels, and were cut from the root canopy at a depth of 5–30 cm. This depth range corresponds to the most active soil layer for fine-root resource acquisition in temperate forest ecosystems and was selected to ensure comparability among individuals. After washing the soil off the root surface, roots were separated into fine roots (< 2 mm) and thick roots (> 5 mm) based on their diameters. Roots with intermediate diameters between 2 and 5 mm were excluded from the analysis to ensure a clear functional separation between absorptive fine roots and transport/storage thick roots.

Immediately after field collection, all samples were rapidly stabilized to prevent post-harvest metabolic alteration. All collected samples were immediately treated in a microwave oven (600 W) for 3–5 min for enzyme deactivation and then dried to constant weight in a drying oven at 65 °C. The concentrations of starch and soluble sugar were determined by the anthracene copper-concentrated sulfuric acid method [33, 34] and expressed as a percentage of dry mass (% dry mass). The total non-structural carbohydrate (TNC) concentration for each organ was defined and calculated as the sum of its soluble sugar and starch concentrations.

Anthrone reagent was prepared by dissolving 1 g of purified anthrone in 1000 mL of dilute sulfuric acid. A standard curve was constructed using a 100 µg/mL glucose solution. Approximately 0.05 g of ground plant tissue (exact weight recorded) was repeatedly extracted with 80% ethanol. The supernatant was collected, mixed with anthrone reagent, and absorbance was measured at 620 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-1800 PC, Shanghai MAPADA Instruments). Sugar concentration was determined from the standard curve (or via linear regression) and expressed as a percentage of sample dry weight. For starch quantification, the residue after soluble sugar extraction was treated with perchloric acid and distilled water, and the extracted solution was similarly analyzed with anthrone reagent. Starch concentration was derived from the same standard curve based on measured absorbance.

Statistical analysis

Non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to assess interspecific differences in NSCs, tree growth, and drought resistance. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant differences, post hoc multiple comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjustment for P-value adjustment using the ‘rstatix’ package. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to examine the relationships between tree growth resistance and recovery, NSC concentration patterns, relative BAI (BAIr), and BAI variability (CV) across species.

Spearman correlation analysis was applied to quantify the relationships between tree drought resilience indices and NSC components. Preliminary analyses indicated that hydraulic traits (Kh and P50) showed limited explanatory power for growth and drought resilience compared with NSC-related variables, and were therefore not included in subsequent analyses. To further disentangle the relative importance of functional type (coded as a two-level factor: L. gmelinii vs broad-leaved species) and organ-specific NSC storage patterns in driving tree growth and drought responses, hierarchical partitioning (HP) based on redundancy analysis (RDA) was conducted using the ‘rdacca.hp’ package. This method quantified the independent contribution (percentage of explained variance) of each predictor. Subsequently, to evaluate the magnitude and direction of these effects, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were employed using the ‘lme4’ package. The top five physiological drivers identified by HP were Z-score standardized and included as predictors, with functional type accounted for as a random effect within the model structure. Standardized effect sizes (Beta coefficients) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to evaluate the magnitude and direction of these effects. Predictors were considered statistically significant if their 95% CIs did not overlap with zero.

All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using R 4.1.2 [35] and Origin 2020b.

Results

Species-specific variations in radial growth and drought resilience

This study compared radial growth and interannual growth variation among four coexisting tree species in a semiarid forest. Analyses of radial stem growth demonstrated remarkable differences in growth rates among the four species. Specifically, the BAIr of Q. mongolica (3.05 mm/year) was significantly lower than that of other species (P < 0.05, Fig. 2a). No significant interspecific differences were observed in terms of tree growth variation (BAI CV) or resistance to extreme drought events (P > 0.05, Fig. 2b, c). The recovery of B. platyphylla (2.18) and P. davidiana (1.72) were significantly higher than that of L. gmelinii (0.85) (P < 0.05, Fig. 2d).

Organ-specific patterns of NSC concentration and storage

The four tree species exhibited distinct organ-specific patterns in NSC concentrats (Fig. 3; Fig. S3). P. davidiana showed notably high soluble sugar concentrations across both aboveground and underground organs, whereas Q. mongolica displayed relatively low soluble sugar levels throughout all tissues (Fig. S3a). Soluble sugars were primarily concentrated in leaves of the three broad-leaved species, whereas L. gmelinii (6.59%) exhibited its highest soluble sugar concentration in branches (Fig. S3b). Specifically, B. platyphylla (10.71%) showed relatively high starch concentrations in leaves, whereas P. davidiana (9.84%) and Q. mongolica (14.40%) exhibited higher starch concentrations in thick roots. In contrast, L. gmelinii (17.91%) exhibited the highest starch concentration in fine roots (Fig. S3b).

Distinct differences in NSC concentrations were observed between broad-leaved species and the conifer L. gmelinii across aboveground and belowground organs (Fig. 3; Fig. S3). In canopy tissues, the soluble sugar concentration in leaves was significantly higher in P. davidiana (7.53%) than in Q. mongolica (5.03%) and L. gmelinii (4.33%) (P < 0.05; Fig. 3a). In contrast, leaf starch concentrations were significantly higher in L. gmelinii (15.24%) than in Q. mongolica (9.63%) and P. davidiana (5.55%) (P < 0.05; Fig. 3a).

For woody aboveground tissues, L. gmelinii exhibited significantly higher soluble sugar concentrations in branches (6.59%) and stems (3.40%) than Q. mongolica (3.06% and 1.83%) (P < 0.05; Fig. 3b, c). Branch starch concentrations were significantly higher in Q. mongolica (10.36%) than in B. platyphylla (7.51%) and P. davidiana (7.24%), whereas stem starch concentrations were significantly higher in L. gmelinii (12.79%) than in all three broad-leaved species (P < 0.05; Fig. 3b, c). Belowground tissues showed contrasting NSC concentration patterns among species. Fine-root soluble sugar concentrations were significantly higher in P. davidiana (6.29%) than in Q. mongolica (3.56%) and L. gmelinii (2.51%), starch concentrations were significantly higher in L. gmelinii (17.91%) than in Q. mongolica (6.76%) (P < 0.05; Fig. 3d). In contrast, no significant interspecific differences were detected in soluble sugar, starch, or TNC concentrations within thick roots (P > 0.05; Fig. 3e).

Patterns of total non-structural carbohydrates (TNC) concentrations generally mirrored those of starch concentrations. Specifically, L. gmelinii exhibited significantly higher TNC concentrations in leaves (19.57%) than P. davidiana (13.08%) and Q. mongolica (14.66%), and higher TNC in branches (16.76%) than B. platyphylla (11.21%) and P. davidiana (11.62%). Possible role of organ-specific NSCs on radial growth and drought resilience

Network correlation analysis revealed distinct coupling patterns between tree growth traits, drought resilience and organ-specific NSC concentrations (Fig. S4). Tree radial growth (BAIr) was significantly and positively correlated with fine root starch (r = 0.63, P < 0.05), while no significant associations were detected for growth stability (BAI CV) across any organ (P > 0.05; Fig. S4). Regarding drought resilience, tree resistance (Rt) was negatively associated with stem starch (r = -0.42, P < 0.05). In contrast, post-drought recovery (Rc) exhibited significant positive correlations with leaf (r = 0.43, P < 0.05) and fine root (r = 0.44, P < 0.05) soluble sugars, but was negatively coupled with leaf starch (r = -0.57, P < 0.05; Fig. S4).

Given the strong inter-correlations observed among NSC components (Fig. S4) and the need to distinguish response differences between species identity (as a binary factor: Larix gmelinii vs. broad-leaved species), hierarchical partitioning (HP) and linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were employed. HP revealed that organ-specific NSCs and tree type provided a high degree of explanation for tree growth indices (Total R2 > 0.65 for BAIr and BAI CV), whereas their explanatory power for drought resilience was relatively lower (Total R2 < 0.50 for Rt and Rc; Fig. 5).

Discussion

NSC composition was more strongly associated with tree growth than drought resilience

Hierarchical partitioning and mixed-effects models showed that organ-specific NSC concentrations explained a relatively high proportion of variation in radial growth and growth variability, whereas their explanatory power for drought resistance and recovery was comparatively lower. In particular, the total explanatory power of NSC variables and tree type exceeded 65% for BAIr and BAI CV, but remained below 50% for Rt and Rc. These results suggest that NSC composition was more closely associated with tree growth performance than with drought resilience in the semiarid forest ecosystem studied here. In contrast, post-drought recovery was more strongly associated with species identity, with Larix gmelinii showing distinct patterns compared to the broad-leaved species.

Among NSC variables, fine-root starch and branch soluble sugars showed positive associations with radial growth, whereas stem starch exerted negative effects on BAIr. Fine-roots starch also represented the strongest independent contributor to both radial growth and growth variability. The positive association between fine-roots starch and growth may reflect the role of belowground carbon reserves in sustaining root activity and resource uptake. Previous studies have shown that starch accumulation in fine roots can support root proliferation and post-stress carbon supply under drought conditions [36]. In semi-arid ecosystems, hydraulic limitation is a major constraint on cambial activity and carbon utilization [37]. Soluble sugars in branches have been linked to osmotic regulation, phloem transport, and hydraulic maintenance during drought [38–40], potentially supporting continued stem growth under declining water availability.

In contrast to soluble sugars, starch concentrations in woody tissues tended to be negatively associated with growth-related traits. Stem starch showed significant negative effects on BAIr in the mixed-effects models, while starch concentrations in branches and stems were positioned opposite to soluble sugars along PCA1. This pattern not only reflects the functional divergence between soluble sugars and starch, but also supports the growth–storage trade-off hypothesis, whereby greater investment in longer-term carbon storage constrains structural biomass production [6, 41, 42]. These patterns align with the fast–slow plant economics spectrum, in which slower-growing species invest more in NSCs to enhance tolerance to environmental stress [43, 44].

Compared with growth traits, drought resistance and recovery exhibited weaker relationships with NSC variables overall. Although canopy soluble sugars were associated with recovery, the total explanatory power of NSC variables for resilience indices remained relatively low. This finding suggested that drought resilience might be influenced not only by carbon reserves, but also by other factors such as hydraulic traits, stomatal regulation, and species-specific drought sensitivity.

Contrasting NSC patterns among organs and species may reflect different drought adaptation strategies

The four tree species exhibited clear differences in NSC composition and organ-specific distribution patterns. Differences among the studied species were observed in organ-specific NSC composition, with relatively higher soluble sugar concentrations in leaves and fine roots in broad-leaved species, and higher starch concentrations in Larix gmelinii, particularly in fine roots and stems. These differences were accompanied by contrasting growth and drought-resilience responses among species.

Such interspecific variation in NSC allocation may be partly associated with differences in anatomical and functional traits among species. Diffuse-porous species (Betula platyphylla and Populus davidiana) and the ring-porous species (Quercus mongolica) differed in xylem anatomical organization and seasonal wood formation patterns. Ring-porous species generally showed earlier cambial reactivation and concentrated earlywood vessel formation during the initial stage of the growing season, whereas diffuse-porous species typically exhibited a more gradual pattern of vessel production and carbon investment during wood formation [25, 45]. Because cambial reactivation and xylem differentiation required substantial carbon input, with stored carbohydrates being mobilized to support cell division and secondary cell wall formation [46]. Therefore, differences in seasonal carbon demand associated with wood formation might contribute to species-specific NSC allocation strategies. Previous studies have also shown that ring-porous species may exhibit greater seasonal variation in NSC dynamics than diffuse-porous species, reflecting differences in carbon demand and storage strategies [45, 47]. Meanwhile, Larix gmelinii, as a conifer with needle foliage and distinct wood anatomical characteristics, might exhibit different carbon allocation strategies from broad-leaved species, potentially involving different reliance on stored carbon reserves to buffer seasonal mismatches between carbon assimilation and growth demands [6, 48]. Therefore, the observed species-specific NSC patterns likely reflected integrated outcomes of species-specific anatomical and functional traits.

Aboveground and belowground organs exhibited distinct NSC composition patterns, reflecting their different physiological functions in carbon storage and drought response. Among the broad-leaved species examined here, the highest concentrations of soluble sugars were consistently found in leaves, this preferential accumulation in foliage serves as a vital ‘C-consuming’ strategy. Because leaves face direct atmospheric evaporative demand, they require consistently high levels of readily available sugars to stabilize cellular membranes and secure immediate stress tolerance [38, 49, 50]. Thus, maintaining high foliar sugar levels could prioritize immediate survival and rapid metabolic reactivation following drought, rather than long-term carbon retention.

Conversely, the belowground root systems exhibited a distinct ‘C-saving’ strategy characterized by pronounced starch accumulation. Because starch is immobile and metabolically inert, storing starch rather than soluble sugars minimizes carbon loss from maintenance respiration in fine roots [51]. This robust starch pool acted as a stable but flexible buffer; it could be rapidly degraded into soluble sugars to support root proliferation and soil resource foraging when water availability declines [52–54]. Such contrasting coordination of NSC components between leaves and root systems highlighted the divergent physiological roles of above- and belowground organs in coping with environmental stress. These distinct carbon management strategies and species-specific differences were clearly captured in our PCA results, emphasizing the intrinsic linkage between specific NSC concentration modes and tree functional types.

Implications for forest resilience under increasing drought variability

Our results provided evidence that organ-specific NSC composition was associated with interspecific variation in tree growth and drought response strategies. Notably, our models revealed that individual NSC components showed remarkably high explanatory power for both radial growth and growth variability, highlighting their role in contributing to baseline tree performance patterns. The contrasting associations of soluble sugars and starch across aboveground and belowground organs further indicated that NSC components might play distinct functional roles in tree carbon management. Integrating organ-specific NSC dynamics with detailed hydraulic traits across broader species and climatic gradients would further unravel the mechanisms by which carbon storage shapes forest resilience under increasing climate variability. We have to stress that, this study focused on trait–response associations rather than temporal or mechanistic causal relationships.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. A key limitation is the temporal mismatch between NSC measurements and drought response indices: NSC concentrations were measured during the 2019 growing season, whereas drought resistance and recovery indices were derived from previous drought events. Therefore, the current NSC measurements cannot be interpreted as the direct carbon status of trees during those historical drought periods. Instead, they represent contemporary organ-specific carbon allocation characteristics under active growth conditions, which may reflect persistent interspecific differences in carbon management strategies. Previous evidence suggests that interannual variation in NSC concentration is considerably smaller than the intra-annual variation driven by seasonal shifts between growing and dormant periods [55]. Thus, the observed relationships should be interpreted as associations between relatively stable functional traits and variation in drought responses, rather than direct causal effects of current NSC pools on past drought resistance or recovery.

Because NSC concentrations were measured at a single time point during the middle of the growing season, they represented the physiological status of trees during active growth rather than seasonal variation in NSC storage and allocation. Seasonal variation in NSC composition may influence the strength of the observed relationships and should be evaluated through repeated sampling across different phenological stages. In addition, although the four selected species represent dominant canopy components in these semiarid forests, the relatively limited number of species and sampled individuals may constrain the broader extrapolation of our findings across diverse forest ecosystems. Future studies integrating multi-year NSC monitoring, larger species datasets, and broader climatic gradients will be essential to further evaluate how organ-specific carbon allocation traits contribute to forest drought responses.

Conclusions

Our results suggested that organ-specific non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) composition was strongly associated with radial growth and growth variability across four coexisting dominant tree species in a semiarid forest ecotone, with relatively high explanatory power in hierarchical partitioning analyses. In contrast, drought resistance and recovery exhibited weaker relationships with NSC variables and were more strongly influenced by interspecific differences. Organ-specific NSCs exhibited distinct functional decoupling: belowground fine-root starch served as the primary driver for growth, while readily metabolizable branch sugars positively associated with post-drought hydraulic repairment. Furthermore, starch accumulation in woody tissues constrained structural growth, strongly corroborating a growth–storage trade-off. Ultimately, these highly coordinated, organ-specific NSC patterns may allow coexisting species to balance immediate stress tolerance with long-term structural growth.
