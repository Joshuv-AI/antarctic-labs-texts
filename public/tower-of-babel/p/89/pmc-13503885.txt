Quantifying outer membrane permeability to β-lactam antibiotics using outer membrane vesicles that protect antibiotic-susceptible bacteria
Authors: Alicja Razew, Quentin Herail, Isabel Ayala, Myriam Guissouma, Michel Arthur, Jean-Pierre Simorre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of innovative methods for quantitatively assessing drug efficacy is essential for advancing effective treatments against antibiotic-resistant bacteria. A key determinant of drug activity against resistant Gram-negative bacteria is the permeability of the bacterial cell envelope, particularly that of the outer membrane, which is the first line of defense against antimicrobials. In this study, we report a method to quantitatively evaluate outer membrane permeability to β-lactams (nitrocefin, mecillinam, cloxacillin) and to a β-lactamase inhibitor (avibactam) by monitoring their entry into outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) produced by Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. We also show that drug-susceptible bacteria are effectively shielded from β-lactams by OMVs emanating from resistant bacteria that contain a periplasmic β-lactamase captured upon their formation. Altogether, our work provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how antimicrobial molecules interact with bacterial OMVs and how this process contributes to the spread of antibiotic resistance.Subject terms: Drug delivery, Solution-state NMR, Small molecules, Enzymes, Permeation and transport

Introduction

In the era of the antibiotic crisis, understanding the molecular mechanisms by which antibiotics penetrate bacterial cells is crucial for developing new drugs effective against resistant bacteria. In Gram-negative bacteria, the outer membrane limits the access of many antibiotics to their target (Fig. 1a). This permeability barrier is one reason why relatively fewer antibiotics have been successfully developed against Gram-negative compared with Gram-positive bacteria. For example, antibiotics such as vancomycin and erythromycin are only active against Gram-positive bacteria, whose envelope lacks an outer membrane (Fig. 1b). The great challenges encountered in the development of drugs that penetrate the outer membrane account for the fact that all pathogens listed as critical priorities for antibiotic development by the World Health Organization (WHO) are Gram-negative bacteria, except for drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis. The former group of critical pathogens includes Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and enterobacteria resistant to carbapenems or producing extended-spectrum cephalosporinases.

The outer membrane is an asymmetric lipid bilayer composed of phospholipids in the inner leaflet and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in the outer leaflet (Fig. 1a). Embedded within this barrier is a diverse group of integral membrane proteins known as porins, which form aqueous channels that allow the passive diffusion of small hydrophilic molecules (Mw <600 Da) from the environment into the periplasm1,2. Bacteria regulate both the abundance and the types of porins to adapt to environmental conditions3. In low-osmolarity environments, typically characterized by low concentrations of nutrients and toxic compounds, bacteria tend to express porins with larger channels to maximize nutrient uptake. Conversely, high-osmolarity environments are often associated with higher concentrations of both nutrients and potentially harmful molecules. In this case, the adaptive response is the production of porins with narrower channels, which still permit sufficient nutrient influx while limiting the entry of toxic compounds.

Reduced outer membrane permeability, whether through mutations altering porin sequences or changes in porin expression levels, is a widespread mechanism of acquired resistance to β-lactam antibiotics in Gram-negative bacteria. These drugs act in the periplasm by inactivating essential enzymes, the penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which are located on the outer surface of the inner membrane and catalyze the last step of cell-wall peptidoglycan synthesis (Fig. 1a). This β-lactam resistance mechanism often operates in combination with the production of β-lactamases. The penetration of β-lactams may be further decreased by modifications of the outer membrane lipid composition4 and the upregulation of active efflux pumps5.

Promising strategies to overcome the permeability barrier of the outer membrane involve interfering with key envelope biogenesis machinery components. Examples include the β-barrel assembly machinery (BAM complex6), the LPS transport system (Lpt7), and the outer membrane-peptidoglycan connections established by the BAM8 and Lpp9 proteins. For the above reasons, quantifying the permeability barrier of the outer membrane is critical for drug development. Computational predictions based on molecular dynamics simulations have proven valuable for drug screening10, linking outer membrane permeability to specific chemical features of compounds11. Classical experimental approaches remain technically challenging, as they require precise measurement of drug concentrations in the surrounding medium and within cellular compartments, namely the periplasm and cytoplasm, which differ vastly in volume. Radioactive drugs are the basis of sensitive and specific assays, but radiolabeled compounds are rarely available due to the costs associated with their synthesis and handling. Use of fluorescent derivatives of drugs suffers from severe limitations since introduction of fluorophores alters the physicochemical properties of the antibiotics. Liposome swelling assays allow monitoring of unlabeled drug entry, while single-molecule investigations of drug transport through specific porins have been carried out by reconstituting porins into artificial bilayers and tracking current fluctuations during drug passage12. Yet, generating liposomes or artificial bilayers with the correct lipid asymmetry and oriented protein insertion remains technically demanding, particularly when a comprehensive evaluation of porin-mediated drug transport is required13,14.

Recently, in-cell nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) has been applied to the study of diverse cellular processes, including metabolite turnover, enzymatic catalysis, and protein modifications15. We previously demonstrated that in-cell NMR can also be used to follow the intracellular fate of β-lactams, including their inactivation by β-lactamases and their binding to targets16. However, this approach is limited by the tendency of cells to lyse in the buffer during prolonged NMR experiments and by the lack of biosafety containment for pathogenic bacteria in standard NMR setups.

To overcome these limitations, we developed an NMR-based approach using outer membrane vesicles (OMVs). OMVs can be safely separated from producing bacteria by filtration through a 0.20 µm membrane, a widely accepted sterilization method in biotechnology and medicine. These 20–250 nm vesicles are spontaneously released from the outer membrane without cell lysis17 and remain stable for up to two weeks at 4 °C18. OMVs consist of an outer membrane-derived lipid bilayer containing correctly oriented porins and periplasm-derived material in the lumen, potentially including β-lactamases (Fig. 1c). OMVs have been successfully employed for drug screening19, as outer membrane-mimicking vaccine components20, and for validating computational models of outer membrane permeability10. However, their direct use in assessing small-molecule permeability has not yet been explored.

Here, we present quantitative NMR evaluation of the permeability rates of β-lactam antibiotics across the outer membranes of Escherichia coli and P. aeruginosa. The described approach provides an efficient drug screening method for the identification of compounds that present suitable characteristics with respect to outer membrane permeability in Gram-negative bacteria, including multidrug resistance isolates, by alteration of cell-wall envelope components. Furthermore, we show that β-lactamase-containing OMVs may protect susceptible bacteria from β-lactams depending on their permeability properties.

Results

β-lactamase and peptidoglycan fragments are present in the OMVs lumen

Our first objective was to characterize the content of OMVs from β-lactamase-producing bacteria. To this end, OMVs were prepared from 15N-labeled cultures of a derivative of E. coli BW25113 ΔampC producing the β-lactamase KPC-2 and lacking the chromosomal ampC β-lactamase gene. This strain harbors the recombinant plasmid pTRC99kΩkpc-2, which carries the kpc-2 gene under the control of an inducible promoter, while the ampC deletion eliminates production of the species-specific β-lactamase of E. coli, namely the AmpC cephalosporinase.

To establish that KPC-2 was present in the lumen rather than in the surrounding medium, purified OMVs were treated with the broad-specificity protease Proteinase K21. To validate that this procedure does not negatively affect the integrity of the outer membrane, we first monitored purified OMVs by negative-stain microscopy, which showed that their membrane remained intact after the Proteinase K treatment (Figure S1).

1H-15N SOFAST 2D NMR experiments performed on OMVs from the KPC-2-producing strain revealed sharp and well-resolved amide resonances. Comparison with spectra of purified KPC-2 confirmed that the resonances in OMVs correspond to amino acid residues of the folded protein in solution (Fig. 2a). Notably, the background in OMVs NMR spectra was much lower than that observed with whole cells16, as expected, since signals from the cytoplasm and inner membrane are absent.

Additionally, intense 15N amide resonances were observed in the 122-131 ppm region (Fig. 2b and Figure S2). Comparison with annotated E. coli spectra16 identified peaks corresponding to known peptidoglycan recycling fragments, including fragments containing anhydro-MurNAc residues resulting from cleavage of glycan strands by lytic transglycosylases. These data indicate that OMVs contain periplasm-derived peptidoglycan fragments17.

Together, these results demonstrate that the lumen of OMVs originates from the periplasm of the producing bacteria and that the OMV content can be genetically manipulated, in the current study by expressing the plasmid gene encoding KPC-2.

Porin-based permeability of OMV membranes to small molecules

We then asked whether penetration of small molecules into the lumen of OMVs could be determined by NMR. To this end, we tested avibactam22, a β-lactamase inhibitor that effectively crosses the outer membrane of E. coli and inactivates KPC-2 by reversible covalent binding. Spectra were recorded for KPC-2-containing OMVs in the absence or presence of avibactam (75 µM; Fig. 2c and Figure S3a). Resonance shifts were observed for specific KPC-2 residues23 located within or near the catalytic cavity (Figure S3b, c), affecting regions similar to those monitored directly in the bacterial periplasm16. This demonstrates that avibactam crosses the outer membrane of OMVs and binds to KPC-2 in their lumen.

In E. coli, OmpC and OmpF are the major porins responsible for passive uptake of small hydrophilic molecules13. OMVs were prepared from the commercially available E. coli BL21(DE3) ∆ompABCF strain, which lacks ompA, lamB, ompC, and ompF, and from a derivative of that strain overexpressing the ompF gene. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, Figure S4) determined the OMV concentration24. It showed that the ΔompABCF background produced more OMVs per bacterium (Figure S5). This is because the lack of porins compromises the connection between the OM and the PG, resulting in hypervesiculation. Nonetheless, this did not alter OMV permeability to drugs, and this strain produces intact OMVs21,25. OMVs were incubated with nitrocefin for 30 min and 1H NMR was used to determine the number of molecules present out or in OMVs (“external fraction” or “internal fraction”, respectively, in Fig. 2d). The 1H NMR peak of the nitrocefin molecule at 8.8 ppm was integrated and normalized to the “no OMVs” control variant, which contained nitrocefin diluted in the buffer only. Nitrocefin stability was monitored over the extended time course and showed no degradation, indicating that the observed decrease in NMR peak intensity stems from drug entry into the OMVs (Figure S6). Because hypervesiculation may promote misfolding of overexpressed proteins, we verified whether OmpF is correctly structured by a heat modifiability assay22,26. It allows assessment of the folding state of β-barrel proteins, as determined by their migration pattern following denaturation by boiling (Figure S7). This approach revealed distinct migration patterns of the OmpF band, indicating that porin retains its β-barrel shape in the OMVs and is therefore properly folded.

Overexpression of the ompF gene in the ΔompABCF mutant led to a 4- to 8-fold increase in the internal amount of nitrocefin, in agreement with the known role of OmpF porin in the transport of small molecules across the outer membrane, including nitrocefin (Fig. 2e and Figure S8). Together, these findings show that OMVs provide an experimental model to explore the permeability properties of the outer membrane to small molecules.

Differential permeability of OMV membranes to β-lactam antibiotics

Our next objective was to develop an assay to quantitatively assess the permeability of OMVs. To this end, we developed an NMR-based method to compare the rates of β-lactam hydrolysis by KPC-2 within the lumen of intact OMVs or in lysates of the same OMVs that were obtained by disrupting their membrane with colistin. In the extract treated with colistin, OMVs were lysed, and there is consequently no permeability barrier; i.e., KPC-2 and β-lactam exist in solution, and the active site of the β-lactamase is freely accessible to the substrate by diffusion. The same amount of lysed OMVs was used for the nitrocefin and mecillinam kinetics. Thus, the difference between the two kinetics reflects differences in the catalytic efficacy of KPC-2 for the two substrates. Under the assay conditions, mecillinam is hydrolyzed faster than nitrocefin (Figure S9). The meaningful comparison in this analysis concerns the difference in the kinetics of hydrolysis of the same β-lactam (nitrocefin or mecillinam) by KPC-2 present in the same amount of OMVs with the treatment of OMVs by colistin (the permeability is destroyed) versus without colistin (the permeability barrier is present, Figure S10). Drug permeability across the outer membrane depends on several physicochemical properties, particularly polarity and molecular weight, as well as interactions with specific amino acid residues forming porin channels, which can confer selectivity27. To explore these factors, we compared the permeation of two β-lactams: mecillinam (molecular weight: 325.4 g/mol; polarity surface area 98.5 Å²) and nitrocefin (molecular weight: 516.5 g/mol; polarity surface area 232 Å²). Both compounds yield well-resolved 1H NMR spectra that identify the hydrolyzed (KPC-2 product) and non-hydrolyzed (KPC-2 substrate) drug (Fig. 3a). We used resonances at 8.88 and 8.80 ppm for nitrocefin and its hydrolysis product, respectively, and at 7.94 and 7.88 ppm for mecillinam and its hydrolyzed product. NMR-generated progression curves obtained with four enzyme concentrations indicated that purified KPC-2 hydrolyzes nitrocefin with a turnover of 3.5 ± 1.5 s⁻¹ (Fig. 3b and Figure S11). This estimate is consistent with the turnover estimated by the classical spectrophotometry assay (7.8 ± 0.9 s⁻¹). Although powerful, that method is limited to chromogenic β-lactams that can be assayed using visible light. Therefore, we used NMR as the method of choice, which is applicable to a broad range of drugs irrespective of their spectrophotometric properties.

To assess whether the outer membrane of OMVs restricts drug access to periplasmic KPC-2, we compared hydrolysis kinetics of mecillinam and nitrocefin by KPC-2 from intact and colistin-disrupted OMVs (Fig. 3c). This analysis confirmed that the OMV membrane acts as a permeability barrier for both drugs, as hydrolysis rates increased 75 and 148 times upon OMV disruption by colistin for nitrocefin and mecillinam, respectively (Table S1 and Fig. 3d). Kinetic fits of substrate depletion and product accumulation (Fig. 3d and S12) allowed calculation of initial velocities, from which we derived the permeability rate constant (Table S1), which describes how steadily a molecule passes through the outer membrane of the OMVs based on the disappearance of the KPC-2 substrate NMR peak. Kdiff has been normalized to the OMV surface area determined by NTA (Figure S4). OMV samples display a well-defined size distribution with a sharp peak around the mean value, indicating a largely homogeneous population. In all, this analysis revealed that mecillinam exhibited a 2–3-fold higher permeation rate than nitrocefin (Fig. 3e).

Porin loss alters OMV permeability to mecillinam and nitrocefin

Porins are protein channels with a well-established role in the passive transport of drugs across the outer membrane28. We next asked whether our NMR-based OMV membrane permeability assay could be used to quantitively determine the known contribution of porins to the penetration of β-lactams through the outer membrane. Proteinase K trims exposed regions of outer membrane proteins21, and by that potentially affecting outer membrane permeability properties. We performed a heat modifiability assay to assess the folding state of porins before and after Proteinase K treatment using OMVs containing overexpressed OmpF (Figure S14). We observed that OmpF retains its native, β-barrel fold upon Proteinase K treatment. Based on these findings, we concluded that Proteinase K treatment does not negatively impact outer membrane permeability properties related to porin functionality.

Hydrolysis rates of mecillinam and nitrocefin by KPC-2 in intact and colistin-disrupted OMVs were measured by NMR (Fig. 4a and Figure S15). Deletion of porin genes ompABCF reduced OMV permeability 4-fold for nitrocefin and 2-fold for mecillinam (Fig. 4b and Table S1) as compared to OMVs extracted from E. coli BW25113 ∆ampC. It should be noted that there are differences in the BW25113 and BL21(DE3) backgrounds, but known polymorphisms are not expected to affect outer membrane permeability29,30. Thus, β-lactam transport through the OMV membranes was more dependent on porins for nitrocefin than for mecillinam. The difference might be accounted for by the higher polarity surface area of nitrocefin (232 Å² versus 98.5 Å²) that is expected to hamper porin-independent crossing of the lipid bilayer.

P. aeruginosa OMVs are less permeable to nitrocefin than E. coli OMVs

Because OMVs mimic the envelope properties of their producer strain, they could potentially provide a safe alternative to in-cell NMR for pathogenic bacteria. To examine this possibility, we extracted OMVs from P. aeruginosa PA14 ΔldtPae1-3. This strain hyperproduces vesicles due to the deletion of the genes encoding the three L,D-transpeptidases responsible for the tethering of an outer membrane lipoprotein to peptidoglycan31. Resulting OMVs display similar sizes as reported for the PA14 strain32. This strain also produces the species-specific AmpC β-lactamase at wild-type levels.

To assess membrane permeability, P. aeruginosa OMVs were treated with Proteinase K to remove extravesicular AmpC, followed by extensive washing. NMR analysis revealed decreased resonances of intact nitrocefin and increased resonances of its hydrolysis product, confirming that nitrocefin reaches the OMV lumen where it is hydrolyzed by AmpC (Fig. 4c and Figure S16). Furthermore, treatment with cloxacillin, a β-lactam that inhibits AmpC33,34, blocked the catalytic reaction, demonstrating that the inhibitor also reached its target within the OMVs (Fig. 4d).

The outer membrane of P. aeruginosa is known to be less permeable to antibiotics than that of E. coli35–37. To test whether our NMR assay could evidence this difference, we compared nitrocefin hydrolysis in intact and colistin-treated OMVs from both species. Strikingly, P. aeruginosa OMVs displayed 800–1000-fold lower permeability than E. coli OMVs, in agreement with previously reported values (Fig. 4e and Table S1)36.

β-lactamase-containing OMVs shield bacteria from β-lactam exposure

β-lactamase-containing OMVs have been previously shown to protect bacteria susceptible to β-lactams38–40 (Fig. 5a). However, the mechanisms by which they contribute to the spread of antibiotic resistance are not well understood. Possible scenarios include the release of OMVs luminal contents, membrane fusion, or antibiotic uptake. To test the third scenario, our next objective was to determine whether the extent of this protective effect could be correlated to OMV membrane permeability. We determined the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of mecillinam against the β-lactam-susceptible E. coli BW25113 ∆ampC strain in the presence of fixed amounts of OMVs derived from KPC-2-producing E. coli strains differing by the presence or absence of the ompABCF porin genes (Fig. 5b and Figure S17). The MIC of mecillinam against BW25113 ∆ampC was 0.25 µg/ml in the absence of OMVs. In the presence of OMVs from the porin-producing strain, the MIC increased 128-fold to reach 32 µg/ml. OMVs from the porin-deficient ∆ompABCF strain resulted in a lower increase in the MIC of mecillinam (8-fold from 0.25 to 2 µg/ml). These results show that porin-mediated OMV permeability to mecillinam is crucial for the protection of the susceptible strain.

Discussion

Developing innovative approaches to quantitatively evaluate drug efficacy is critical for advancing therapies against antibiotic-resistant bacteria. A key determinant of drug effectiveness in resistant strains is the permeability of the bacterial outer membrane, the primary barrier protecting Gram-negative bacteria from antimicrobial agents. In this study, we present an OMV-based strategy to monitor interactions between β-lactams and the outer membrane in real time to quantify outer membrane permeability, providing an alternative to assays with intact cells. This approach could be suitable for other families of drugs by addressing suitable cytoplasmic drug detoxifying enzymes to the periplasm. This can be achieved by engineering translational fusions with an export signal such as that of the tween arginine system. Of note, drug detoxifying enzymes have been reported for most antibiotic families, such as erythromycin esterases for macrolides41,42, metalloenzymes for fosfomycin43, and nucleotidyltransferases, phosphotransferases, or acetyltransferases for aminoglycosides44. An advantage of monitoring the reaction kinetics rather than directly visualizing the enzymes is that it broadens the applicability of the method to reactions catalyzed by enzymes present at low concentrations, for which export from the periplasm into OMVs may be limited.

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study to use OMVs to quantify outer membrane permeability. Compared to whole-cell systems, OMVs offer distinct advantages: (i) they provide low background in 2D 1H-15N NMR, enabling direct monitoring of protein-small molecule interactions inside the lumen (Fig. 2b), independently from any labeling of the drugs or any intrinsic absorbance or fluorescence properties of the drugs; (ii) OMVs are more stable than live bacterial cells, ensuring reproducible measurements over extended time periods; (iii) OMVs pose no biosafety risk, as OMV preparations can be sterilized by filtration; (iv) OMVs are easy to genetically manipulate and study of particular features of the outer membrane impacting drug transport, for instance porin content or LPS composition. However, it should also be stressed that using OMVs to assess the permeability of the outer membrane of intact cells has several limitations. The possible alterations concern the lack of outer membrane-PG cross-links important for outer membrane stability in bacterial cell45, the absence of efflux pumps impacting the final concentration of the drug in the cell interior5, or the local concentration and global distribution of LPS or porins, which may deviate from the bacterial producer46.

Another limitation of the described system concerns OMV stability, as vesicles may undergo aggregation depending on production conditions and storage time18. The described system is therefore best suited for comparative analysis of different drugs using the same OMV stock, rather than extraction of the absolute permeability values. Taking into account all these advantages and limitations, OMVs serve as a simplified, yet physiologically relevant model for comparatively assessing permeability in drug screenings.

In all experiments, NMR served as the detection method. NMR offers several advantages for antimicrobial drug screening. It provides residue-level insights into cell envelope components (e.g., β-lactamase inhibition; Figs. 2b, 2c, and Figure S3) and enables direct, quantitative, label-free monitoring of substrates and products of enzymatic reactions (Figs. 3a, 3d and 4a, 4c). This allows precise measurement of turnover and accumulation dynamics occurring in complex native environments, such as the periplasm or the lumen of OMVs, with high sensitivity and good signal-to-noise ratios. Consequently, NMR offers a clear advantage over other methods commonly used to study intra-OMV cargo, such as mass spectrometry.

Permeability measurements performed with OMVs were well correlated with both outer membrane composition and drug physicochemical properties. We showed that nitrocefin transport correlates positively with OmpF expression levels. The same experimental setup could be extended to a more functionally relevant system, such as OMVs containing overexpressed low-permeability porin OmpC47, which confer reduced permeability of the outer membrane commonly observed in uropathogenic E. coli strains48. Apart from the effect of the outer membrane composition, we showed that nitrocefin permeated intrinsically less efficiently than mecillinam, consistent with its higher molecular weight and was more strongly affected by deletion of porin genes, consistent with its higher surface polarity (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, P. aeruginosa OMVs displayed an 800- to 1000-fold reduction in permeability compared to E. coli OMVs (Fig. 4e), consistent with previously reported values, which were attributed to differences in porin content and lipid composition of their outer membranes35–37.

OMVs produced by resistant bacteria are known to protect susceptible bacteria38–40, but the underlying mechanism is not fully understood. Several possibilities have been proposed: (i) release of active β-lactamase from the OMV lumen into the external environment, (ii) fusion of OMVs with the outer membrane of susceptible strains with direct delivery of the enzyme into their periplasm, or (iii) uptake of antibiotics into the OMV lumen followed by hydrolysis. Here we show that permeability is positively correlated with this protective effect, supporting the third possibility (Fig. 5b); however, with current data we could not exclude that other possible scenarios do not play a role.

In summary, we introduce an OMVs-based strategies for qualitative and quantitative assessment of drug permeation across the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. NMR-based characterization of OMVs proved powerful for tracking selective cargo export (peptidoglycan fragments, β-lactamase), underscoring the broader relevance of this approach. We demonstrate the utility of OMVs in screening β-lactams, a strategy that could be extended to monitor other kinetic reactions and, consequently, other antimicrobial classes.

Methods

This work complies with all relevant ethical regulations.

Strains, plasmids

Strains and plasmids used in this study were listed in the Supplementary Table S3.

KPC-2 purification

KPC-2 was produced and purified as described before16. Briefly, E. coli BL21(DE3) cells were cultured in LB or 15N-M9 medium. Expression of the gene encoding KPC-2 was induced at OD600 ~ 0.6 with 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Incubation was continued overnight at 20 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. For purification, cells from a 1 L-culture were resuspended in 20 mL of cold buffer A (50 mM Hepes pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 25 mM imidazole, 3% glycerol) with protease inhibitors (cOmplete™, Sigma-Aldrich), lysed by sonication, and centrifuged at 46,000 × g, at 4 °C for 40 min. The supernatant was applied to a 3 mL HisTrap gravity column equilibrated with buffer A, followed by washing with 20 mL of buffer A and elution of KPC-2 in six 1-mL fractions with buffer B (50 mM Hepes pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, 3% glycerol). Fractions were analyzed by the absorbance at 280 nm and by SDS-PAGE to identify fractions containing KPC-2. For size exclusion chromatography, a Superdex 75 (16/600) column was equilibrated with buffer C (50 mM Hepes pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 3% glycerol), and samples were injected using a 5 mL loop at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Fractions containing KPC-2 were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, concentrated using a 30 kDa MWCO Vivaspin tube, aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

OMVs production and purification

The OMVs protocol was adapted from previous publications18,49. Briefly, bacteria were cultured in 0.5 L of LB or minimal 15N -M9 medium supplemented with unlabeled glucose (4 g/L). Protein expression (KPC-2 or OmpF) was induced at OD600 ~ 0.6 with 0.1 mM IPTG and carried out overnight at 37 °C. For OMVs purification, cultures were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. To confirm the absence of viable bacteria, 100 μL of the filtrate was plated. The sample was further centrifuged at 46,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C, and the vesicle-containing pellet was resuspended in the remaining supernatant (~1 mL) or DPBSS buffer (Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline). Proteinase K (100 µg/mL) was added to the OMV sample to degrade KPC-2 present in the external environment as a result of OMV lysis, while not penetrating the membrane of intact OMVs21. The sample was subsequently incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. This suspension was subjected to at least three washing rounds using DPBSS buffer followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The folding state of OmpF in the OMVs has been determined using heat modifilibility assay22,26 in which we compared the migration pattern of the OmpF protein band before and after boiling (95 °C, 10 min) on the SDS-PAGE (15%). The final vesicle preparation was either stored at −20 °C or kept at 4 °C, where it remained stable for up to two weeks.

Direct permeability assay

OMVs from E. coli BL21(DE3) ∆ompABCF, or E. coli BL21(DE3) ∆ompABCF complemented by ompF were adjusted to the same concentration (6.8 × 1012 OMVs/ml), mixed with nitrocefin (200 μM), and topped up with DPBSS buffer to 150 μl. After incubation for 30 minutes, the antibiotic that entered the OMVs was separated from the soluble fraction by ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 10 min). Then, the supernatant was collected and used for 1H NMR (“Supernatant”, 100 μl per tube). OMVs containing antibiotics were resuspended in fresh buffer (150 μl), and 100 μl was used to prepare the NMR sample. The control sample (nitrocefin in DPBSS buffer) was prepared the same way. Each sample was supplemented with 3-(trimethylsilyl)-propane-1-sulfonate (DSS, 200 μM) as an internal concentration standard. The values of particular NMR peaks were expressed as compared to the DSS peak (around 0.01 ppm) and the nitrocefin reference tube to determine antibiotic concentration.

Indirect permeability assay using KPC-2

OMVs from E. coli BW25113 ∆ampC or E. coli BL21(DE3) ∆ompABCF were mixed with colistin (100 μM, disrupted OMVs) or DPBSS buffer (intact OMVs) and topped up with DPBSS-D2O buffer to 180 µl. To initiate the reaction, mecillinam or nitrocefin (200 µM) was added to the NMR tube and the experiment was recorded immediately after. To minimize dead time between initiation of the reaction and NMR acquisition, all spectrometer parameters (locking, tuning/matching, shimming, and 1H 90° pulse calibration) were determined in advance using the same OMVs sample in the absence of antibiotic. The reaction was initiated by addition of a concentrated antibiotic stock solution (<2 μL added to 180 μL sample) directly into the NMR tube already positioned in the spinner. Because the sample remained in place, no further tuning or pulse calibration was required; only a rapid automatic reshimming procedure was optionally performed. Under these conditions, data acquisition started within approximately 1 min after antibiotic addition. For reactions requiring shorter dead times, rapid-mixing NMR devices can be employed50. For each kinetic experiment, the first spectrum acquired immediately after antibiotic addition to intact OMVs was used as the internal reference and assigned to the nominal initial antibiotic concentration of 200 µM. Subsequent substrate and product concentrations were calculated from peak integrals relative to this initial value.

NMR experiments

All NMR experiments were recorded using a 600 MHz (Bruker, CryoProbe, Advance III console), 700 MHz, 850 MHz, or 950 MHz (Bruker, CryoProbe, Neo console) spectrometer equipped with helium-cooled cryogenic solution-state 1H, 2H, 13C, 15N-resonance probes. For direct permeability assay, the 8.88 ppm peak corresponding to intact nitrocefin and, in certain instances, its degradation derivative at 8.80 ppm [presence of AmpC β-lactamase in BL21(DE3)] were followed by 1Η NMR52 at 298 K. For indirect permeability assay, the 8.88 and 7.94 ppm peaks, corresponding to intact nitrocefin and mecillinam, respectively, and 8.80 and 7.88 ppm peaks, corresponding to hydrolysis products of nitrocefin and mecillinam, were followed by a series of 1H NMR experiments recorded over the time and stored as a 2D experiment (pseudo-2D) recorded at 298 K (32 scans, acquisition time ~68 s) in DPBSS buffer at pH 7.5 with 5% D₂O.

2D ¹H–¹⁵N SOFAST experiments53 were recorded to improve signal sensitivity while maintaining good resolution of NH signals. Spectra were collected over ca. 3 h (256 scans) using ¹⁵N-labeled OMVs containing KPC-2, resuspended in DPBSS buffer at pH 7.5 with 5% D₂O, before and after the addition of avibactam (75 µM) at 310 K. The chemical shifts were transferred to assign the KPC-2 NMR peaks (BMRB ID: 27617). The additional NMR dataset was acquired under the same buffering and temperature conditions to aid optimal transfer of NMR peak positions between the two spectra. Chemical shift perturbations for both experiments were calculated as the weighted average distance between the NMR peaks recorded for KPC-2 inhibitor-free and the avibactam-treated samples (Figure S3, Source Data File). The data were transformed and analyzed in TOPSPIN (4.2.0.); default weighting parameters from the CcpNmr software (3.2.0) were used54, and Python scripts (3.10.12) using nmrglue51 were used to extract the integral values.

Quantification of OMVs

Quantification of the OMVs was performed using Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NanoSight NS300, Malvern), which enables accurate determination of OMV concentration and size distribution down to ~50 nm55. Prior to the measurement, a blank measurement was performed using filtered (0.2 μm cut-off) DPBSS buffer. The same buffer was used for OMVs dilution to obtain between 40 and 100 particles per recorded frame (5000 to 50,000 times dilutions depending on the OMVs sample). Each experiment was recorded for one minute, and then the trajectory of the particles visualized by light scattering was analyzed by the NanoSight Software. Measurement was performed at 25 °C, and each sample was analyzed in three replicates.

Negative stain microscopy imaging

Negative Stain-Mica-Carbon Flotation Technique (MFT) was based on a published procedure56. Briefly, samples were absorbed to the clean side of a carbon film on mineral mica, stained, and transferred to a 400-mesh copper grid. The images were taken under low dose conditions (<10 e−/Å2) with defocus values between 1.2 and 2.5 μm on a Tecnai 12 LaB6 electron microscope at 120 kV accelerating voltage using CCD Camera Gatan Orius 1000. Phosphotungstic acid (PTA, 1%) diluted in distilled water was used as sample stain.

MIC assay in the presence of OMVs

The procedure followed a previously published protocol57. Briefly, the optical density of an overnight E. coli culture was measured and adjusted in sterile PBS to a predetermined OD₆₀₀ corresponding to approximately 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. The MIC assay was carried out in 96-well plates using the broth microdilution method. A volume of 150 µL of Mueller–Hinton Broth II (MHB II) was added to each well, except for the wells in the first column, which contained 300 µL of mecillinam diluted in MHB II at the highest concentration used in the assay. Two control rows contained antibiotic-free MHB II. Except for these, two-fold serial dilutions of mecillinam were performed across the plate, generating a concentration gradient ranging from 128 to 0.0625 µg/mL. Five µL of the bacterial inoculum was added to each well (excluding the negative control row). OMVs produced and purified from E. coli BW25113 ∆ampC or E. coli BL21(DE3) ∆ompABCF were filter-sterilized (0.45 µm membrane) and added to the wells. The concentration of the OMVs was adjusted based on nitrocefin hydrolysis using OMVs pre-treated with colistin to adjust for the same concentration of KPC-2 present in the OMVs (Figure S17). A control without added OMVs was included. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C under static conditions. The next day, they were placed in the FLUOstar® Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech), shaken for 30 s, and the OD₆₀₀ was measured. The experiment was performed in biological triplicate.

Statistics and reproducibility

NMR experiments were performed in duplicate as technical replicates to confirm the reproducibility of spectral observations. Duplicate measurements were averaged, and individual data points are shown in the corresponding figures. As NMR spectroscopy is a highly reproducible analytical technique with negligible instrumental variability, these technical replicates were used to verify spectral consistency rather than to assess biological variability. Accordingly, no statistical analyses were performed, and the NMR data are presented as representative, reproducible measurements supporting the conclusions on outer membrane permeability.
