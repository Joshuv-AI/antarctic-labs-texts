Epidemiology, presentation, and outcomes of sigmoid volvulus in malawi: an 11-Year retrospective review
Authors: Thokozani Masina, James Alinafe Junior N’gombe, Florence Msiska, Patrick Masangano, Lucy Yemely Kaomba, Wakisa Mulwafu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSigmoid volvulus (SV) is a significant gastrointestinal condition in sub-Saharan Africa, although its epidemiology and outcomes are poorly understood.ObjectivesTo determine the prevalence, demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, and outcomes of SV in Malawi.MethodsAn 11-year retrospective review of patients with SV at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital, Malawi. Patient records were analysed for demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, mode of management, and outcomes.ResultsWe identified 301 cases of SV, with a prevalence of 27.3 cases per year. Males comprised 91.4% of cases, with a mean age of 51. Emergency presentations accounted for 69.8% of cases. Resection and primary anastomosis were the most common surgical procedures. The mortality rate was 11.3%, with non-viability of bowel being a significant predictor of mortality.ConclusionsSV contributes significantly to the disease burden, particularly among middle-aged males seen in the surgical department. It presents acutely, and resection and primary anastomosis are the primary surgical approaches. Non-viability of bowel was identified as a significant predictor of mortality, emphasising the need for prompt surgical intervention.Trial registrationNot Applicable.

Introduction

Colonic volvulus is a pathological condition characterised by the rotation of a long and redundant colonic segment around an elongated mesentery, resulting in intestinal obstruction and ischaemic changes [1, 2]. Among cases of colonic volvulus, the most prevalent occurs in the sigmoid colon, commonly referred to as sigmoid volvulus (SV). This condition often accounts for about half of all cases of intestinal obstruction and exhibits a higher incidence in Eastern Europe, India, and Africa [1, 3–6].

Sigmoid volvulus poses a life-threatening situation and predominantly affects males, with an average age of 70 in developed countries [1, 7]. In low-middle income countries (LMIC), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), SV manifests at a much younger age, with an average of 53 and a range of 42–68, with South Africa representing the higher end of the range [4, 7–9].

The multifactorial aetiology of SV remains speculative, with suggested factors including a high-fibre diet, chronic constipation, previous abdominal surgery, neurological disorders, living at high altitudes, and megacolon [6, 10, 11]. Patients typically present with a combination of abdominal pain, constipation, and abdominal distension. Diagnosis relies on both clinical presentations and radiological findings, with characteristic radiological signs such as the coffee-bean sign—an inverted U-shape indicative of twisting and distending of the sigmoid colon and mesenteric axis, observed in at least 60% of cases [4, 7, 11].

Preliminary treatment measures involve resuscitation with intravenous fluids and correction of electrolyte imbalances. Decompression and detorsion through sigmoidoscopy are initial interventions, aiming to untwist the sigmoid colon and restore blood supply for the assessment of tissue damage [1]. Surgical intervention is necessary if the colon’s viability is compromised, if decompression proves unsuccessful and to prevent recurrence. Surgical options, contingent on the clinical situation and available resources, encompass resection and primary anastomosis (RPA), Hartmann’s procedure (HP), and mesosigmoidopexy [2, 11].

While some studies report short-term surgical outcomes like mortality and complications for SV in Malawi, long-term follow-up data on recurrence rates, quality of life, and functional outcomes is lacking. Surgical approaches vary, from detorsion with or without colostomy to resection and anastomosis, but no consensus exists on the most effective method in low-resource settings where intensive care and post-operative monitoring are limited. Although predictors like delayed presentation and bowel viability have been identified, more research is needed to determine modifiable risk factors that could inform preventive strategies or early interventions [12]. Despite Africa being part of the ‘volvulus belt’, there is limited data on the aetiology and outcomes of sigmoid volvulus (SV) in sub-Saharan Africa [6, 11]. Of what is known about outcomes, SV predominantly affects males [11] with a mortality range of 5.5–17.4% [1, 6, 8].

This study, therefore, aims to assess the clinical presentation and outcomes of sigmoid volvulus in a resource-limited setting. By analysing current surgical practices and outcomes, it seeks to establish baseline metrics for monitoring improvements over time. The study also aims to identify predictors of poor outcomes, which can guide targeted interventions such as community education and strengthening ambulance systems in Malawi.

Methods

We performed a retrospective cohort analysis of all patients admitted at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital (QECH) with a diagnosis of SV from January 1 st, 2010, to December 31 st 2021, aged 18 years and above. QECH has a 1200-bed capacity and is one of the two tertiary and public referral hospitals in the southern region of Malawi.

We searched the departmental database for all patients who had been admitted with the diagnosis of sigmoid volvulus. We identified the patient files using the file numbers registered in the system. All patient data and physical records that were identified were included in this study. The physical files were sourced from the Hospital Management Information System (HMIS) using file numbers.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to investigate associations between various demographic and clinical parameters and mortality in patients with sigmoid volvulus. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the characteristics of the study population, and inferential statistics were employed to assess the significance of associations. The primary outcome was mortality, and secondary outcomes were complications, bowel viability, heart rate and blood pressure categories, length of hospital stay, type of surgery, emergency/referral status, sex, and age groupings.

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and ranges, were calculated for continuous variables such as age, heart rate, white cell count, haemoglobin level, pre-operative stay, post-operative stay, and total hospital stay. Frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables such as sex, referral status, emergency status, blood pressure categories, bowel viability, type of surgery, recorded complications, and mortality.

Chi-square tests were performed to examine the association between categorical variables, including sex, referral status, emergency status, blood pressure categories, bowel viability, complications, and mortality. For continuous variables, independent sample t-tests were used when appropriate. The Pearson Chi-Square test was applied to explore the association between heart rate categories and mortality. Linear-by-linear association tests were used to assess the linear trend in heart rate categories.

Additionally, logistic regression analyses were conducted to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for predictor variables associated with mortality. Variables included in the logistic regression model were white cell count, haemoglobin level, blood pressure categories, heart rate categories, sex, referral status, age (categorised as < 50 vs. >=50 years), bowel viability (non-viable vs. viable), complications (yes vs. no), and emergency status (emergency vs. other).

Multivariate analysis was not performed due to the limited number of mortality events (n = 34) in our cohort, based on the “10 events per variable” rule [13]. This restricted the number of variables that could reliably be included in a multivariable logistic regression model. While this rule has been increasingly debated, it still serves as a pragmatic guideline for avoiding overfitting in small datasets [13–15]. More recent research has suggested modelling that considers factors like overall sample size, predictor strength, and variable correlations to try and overcome this [13, 15–17]. However, given our small event size and the exploratory intent of our study, we chose to conduct univariate analysis to ensure statistical validity and transparency.

A subgroup analysis was performed for patients with documented referrals to investigate mortality rates in referred and non-referred patients. The association between mortality and referral status was further examined using chi-square tests.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25, and a significance level of 0.05 was used for all tests.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Head of Department of Surgery, Management of QECH and QECH Research Committee (Ref.QE/Research/09/03/2023). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of anonymised data.

Patient characteristics

Clinical presentations revealed that 39.9% of patients (n = 120) arrived with prior referrals, while 60.1% (n = 181) sought medical attention independently. A majority of cases (69.8%, n = 210) were emergencies, underscoring the critical nature of sigmoid volvulus, with 30.2% (n = 91) scheduled for elective surgery.

Temporal aspects indicated varied preoperative stays (mean = 2 days, range: 0–27) and postoperative stays (mean = 7 days, range: 0–52). The overall hospital stay averaged 8 days (SD ± 6), ranging from 1 to 53 days.

Bowel was clinically assessed for viability intra-operatively by checking for the presence of peristalsis, the colour of the bowel and presence of pulsations in the vessels supplying the bowel and 22.9% had non-viable segments, 17.6% (n = 69) had viable segments, and documentation was missing for 59.5% (n = 179) of cases. Documented complications occurred in 11.0% (n = 33) of cases, while 89.0% (n = 268) had no recorded complications.

Surgical procedures varied, with 59.5% (n = 179) undergoing resection and primary anastomosis, 15.3% (n = 46) involving Hartmann’s procedure, and 3.0% (n = 9) undergoing Plication and Retroperitonization of the Sigmoid Colon (PROSC). Sigmoidoscopy was done in 16.6% (n = 50) of cases. Spontaneous untwisting occurred in 1.0% (n = 3), and 1.3% (n = 4) of patients died before surgical intervention.

Among the 122 patients with documented bowel viability, 43.4% (n = 53) had a viable bowel and 56.6% (n = 69) had a non-viable bowel. Resection and primary anastomosis was the most common surgical procedure for both groups. In the viable group, 69.8% (n = 37) underwent RPA, while 72.5% (n = 50) of the non-viable group also received RPA. Hartmann’s procedure was more common in the context of non-viable bowel and was used in 17.0% (n = 9) viable cases and 40.6% (n = 28) non-viable cases.

Outcome analysis revealed a mortality rate of 11.3%, while 88.7% (n = 267) of patients were discharged alive. Out of the 120 (39.9%) patients referred from other facilities, 17 died, resulting in a mortality rate of 14.2% for referred patients. The remaining 164 patients (54.5%), who came from within Blantyre, experienced 17 deaths (10.4%). No association was found between gender, referral status, age, blood pressure, haemoglobin level, white cell count, post-operative complications, and mortality. Patients with non-viable bowels were 11 times more likely to die than those with viable bowels (confidence interval: 1.375–87.133).

Discussion

This study found a substantial burden of sigmoid volvulus (SV) at QECH in Blantyre, Malawi, with approximately 27.3 cases per year over eleven years. A study by Chalya in 2015 reported a male-to-female ratio of 5.1:1 among 146 patients, with 83.6% males and 16.4% females [4, 6, 8, 23]. The causes for male volvulus prevalence in Africa, Asia, and Latin America remain unknown.

The mean age at presentation was 51 years, consistent with other developing countries such as Iran (51.8 years) and Kamuzu Central Hospital in Malawi (52.05 years) [7, 25]. Similarly, a study in Ethiopia by Awedew and colleagues reported a mean age of 58.5 [18]. In developing countries, the mean age at the time of sigmoid volvulus diagnosis tends to fall within the range of 40 to 60 years, diverging from the higher mean age of 60 to 70 years reported in developed countries. Our observed median age of 51 years is in line with the demographic characteristics reported in other African studies, such as Tanzania, Nigeria, and Uganda [8, 26–28]. The younger age at diagnosis in our population may be due to the overall youthfulness of our demographic profile relative to ageing populations in developed countries [6, 29]. Age did not significantly affect mortality, with similar risks observed for patients above and below 50 years.

In this study, nearly 40% of patients with sigmoid volvulus (SV) were referred, highlighting the role of referral pathways in SV care. However, the majority (60.1%) presented without referral, reflecting structural challenges in Malawi’s health system, where patients often access a tertiary facility like QECH as their first point of care—particularly notable as Blantyre lacks a district hospital. While diagnosis is possible at the secondary level, limited expertise and critical care capacity underscore the need for improved infrastructure [30]. Strengthening primary care through training for early detection and timely referral is essential [31].

Surgical timing is critical, as elective management after conservative treatment carries a high risk of recurrence [20, 21, 33, 34]. Resection and primary anastomosis was the most common procedure in both emergency and elective settings, though balancing these approaches requires individualized decision-making [31, 35]. Further research is needed to optimize surgical timing within Malawi’s resource-limited context.

A majority of SV cases (69.8%) presented as emergencies, similar to findings in Uganda and Tanzania [8, 28, 32] emphasizing the high acuity of SV. Despite this, emergency status did not significantly affect mortality, possibly due to the study’s smaller sample size [29]. This highlights the complex interplay of clinical and demographic factors in outcomes. The timing of surgery is crucial, as conservatively managed patients scheduled for elective surgery face a high risk of recurrence, aligning with previous findings [20, 21, 33, 34]. Resection and primary anastomosis emerged as the most common procedure in both acute and elective scenarios. Balancing emergency and elective interventions in SV management remains a complex challenge, necessitating individualised approaches for optimal outcomes [31, 35]. Further research is needed to refine timing strategies in SV management within the Malawian healthcare context.

The overall mortality rate was 11.3%, lower than the 17.1% reported in Tanzania [8], but higher than the 6.9% in another Malawian study [7], reflecting a declining trend aligned with global improvements in SV management, especially when compared to historical mortality rates exceeding 50% [33, 36]. Most patients (88.7%) were discharged alive, highlighting the impact of effective care, with early presentation, accurate diagnosis, and prompt treatment being key to favourable outcomes [8]. However, documentation of bowel viability was incomplete in 59.5% of cases, with only 22.9% showing non-viable bowel and 17.6% viable, mirroring documentation gaps in similar settings [6]. Nonetheless, findings from Ethiopia by Mulugeta (2019) reported comparable viability rates (74% viable, 26% non-viable) [37], suggesting consistency despite documentation challenges.

Additionally, a significant linear-by-linear trend (p = 0.007) was shown amongst the heart rate categories, indicating a proportional increase in mortality as heart rate increases from bradycardia to tachycardia. This suggests that high heart rate may indicate severity and systemic stress in sigmoid volvulus patients, emphasising the need for early detection and treatment in tachycardic individuals. Our study identified both bowel viability and heart rate as significant predictors of mortality, emphasising the crucial need for early recognition of tachycardia, prompt intervention, and comprehensive intraoperative documentation.

Complications following surgery for sigmoid volvulus are pivotal determinants of patient outcomes [31]. In our study, 11% of patients experienced documented complications during their hospital stay, highlighting the role of robust monitoring. However, the low prevalence of complications recorded raises concerns about underreporting, suggesting this may mask the true burden of complications. Our findings echo challenges identified in a recent study by Biccard et al. (2021), which assessed enhanced postoperative surveillance interventions across diverse African hospitals. Despite implementing comprehensive surveillance strategies for high-risk surgical patients, the trial did not show a reduction in 30-day in-hospital mortality [35]. This underscores the complexities of postoperative care in resource-limited settings, where factors such as limited expertise and critical care services may contribute to challenges in complication detection and management [38].

This study identified diverse surgical interventions, with resection and primary anastomosis (59.5%) and Hartmann’s procedure (15.3%) being prominent. Sigmoidoscopy, detorsion and flatus tube placement (16.6%) and retroperitonealisation of the sigmoid colon (3%) were alternative interventions. Ideally, patients are readmitted 2 weeks after sigmoidoscopy and detorsion for a definitive procedure. However, there is no record of this particular group of patients having returned for the definitive surgery. A subset (1.3%) did not survive to undergo surgery, emphasising the urgency of rapid intervention. Adding crucial mortality statistics to our findings, the mortality for RPA was 9.8% (n = 179), while for Hartmann’s procedure, it was 15% (n = 46). These figures provide essential insights into the comparative outcomes of the two surgical approaches, underscoring the need for further investigation into the factors influencing mortality in each subgroup. Our findings suggest that RPA, despite previous concerns about anastomotic dehiscence, can be a safe and effective option when carefully selected [7, 8, 39]. Restoration of intestinal continuity post-Hartmann’s procedure remained a reliable intervention, albeit with increased mortality. In Mali, two-stage surgery with an initial colostomy or simple detorsion showed promise, highlighting the importance of tailoring interventions to the patient’s condition [40].

Our data demonstrates how intraoperative findings of bowel viability influenced the choice between resection with primary anastomosis (RPA) and Hartmann’s procedure. Among patients with documented bowel viability, RPA was the most commonly performed procedure, reflecting a preference for restoring bowel continuity when feasible. However, this pattern also aligns with the higher number of non-viable cases in our cohort, consistent with established surgical practices where Hartmann’s procedure is typically used for non-viable bowel, unstable patients or managing severely compromised bowels [8, 9, 40]. These results highlight the role of intraoperative judgement in selecting the most appropriate intervention, particularly within resource-limited environments where surgical outcomes must be balanced against available expertise and postoperative care capacity [35, 39].

The multifactorial nature of mortality in sigmoid volvulus emphasises the need for an integrated clinical assessment [8, 31, 35, 39]. While certain variables independently contribute to prognostication, their combined consideration provides a more holistic understanding of patient risk [41]. These findings offer valuable insights for clinicians, guiding risk stratification and decision-making. Continuous monitoring, timely interventions, and a comprehensive assessment of bowel viability and complications are crucial in managing high-risk cases [25, 37]. Further research with larger datasets and exploration of additional clinical parameters is essential to refine prognostic models and enhance our understanding of sigmoid volvulus. This approach will contribute to improved patient outcomes in this complex medical condition. A systematic review of sigmoid volvulus in Africa needs to be done.

Limitations and strengths

This study provides insights into sigmoid volvulus (SV) in Blantyre, Malawi, contributing to understanding disease patterns, management practices, and outcomes in a region where SV is prevalent but underreported. It highlights management approaches, complications, and mortality rates, offering a baseline for future multicenter studies. However, the study has limitations: its retrospective nature introduces biases due to incomplete or inaccurate documentation, particularly regarding bowel viability and complications. The single-centre design limits generalizability, as SV characteristics and outcomes may vary across regions. Additionally, the 11-year timeframe may not reflect recent advancements in SV management. Despite these limitations, the study lays the groundwork for further research, emphasising the need for improved documentation, expanded collaborations, and prospective studies to better understand evolving trends in SV management in Africa.

Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrate that patients who had findings of non-viability of bowel have significantly higher odds of mortality, highlighting the need for prompt diagnosis, early intervention, improved perioperative care and early recognition of complications to enhance survival.

Recommendations

Future efforts should focus on optimising pre-hospital referral systems, enhancing emergency surgical capacity, and implementing structured post-operative monitoring to reduce mortality in this high-risk patient group.
