Non-coding RNA networks in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: Mechanisms and therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Ruizhe Ren, Siying Ren, Yihan Xu, Xiao Liang, Xiyang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide, characterized by complex molecular regulatory networks driving its pathogenesis. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), serve as critical regulators of gene expression and have been increasingly recognized for their pivotal roles in MASLD development. Rather than functioning in isolation, these ncRNAs form intricate regulatory networks that integrate and amplify disease signals across multiple cellular compartments and pathological stages. This review provides a comprehensive overview of how these ncRNA networks orchestrate MASLD progression, focusing on their roles in metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and fibrosis. We further evaluate the diagnostic potential of circulating ncRNAs as stable, non-invasive biomarkers for disease stratification and monitoring, and discuss emerging therapeutic strategies targeting ncRNAs, including antisense oligonucleotides, synthetic mimics, and advanced delivery platforms such as lipid nanoparticles and engineered exosomes. Despite significant progress, challenges related to delivery efficiency, tissue specificity, and safety remain barriers to clinical translation. By synthesizing current knowledge of ncRNA networks in MASLD and highlighting opportunities for therapeutic intervention, this review provides a roadmap for translating ncRNAs into clinical applications for this increasingly prevalent metabolic liver disease.

Introduction

As the metabolic nexus of the body, the liver coordinates a broad range of physiological processes ranging from nutrient metabolism to immune surveillance through its specialized cellular composition: hepatocytes maintaining metabolic homeostasis, Kupffer cells orchestrating innate immunity, and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) serving as vitamin A reservoirs within cytoplasmic lipid droplets.1 This functional versatility is complemented by a remarkable regenerative capacity, enabling prompt tissue recovery following injury.2 However, in the modern era, this inherent resilience is increasingly compromised by shifts in lifestyle and dietary patterns, which contribute to the growing global incidence of liver diseases.3 Among these, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as one of the most prevalent forms of chronic liver disease (CLD), affecting approximately 30%–40% of the global adult population and 7%–14% of children and adolescents.4,5

The concept of MASLD has undergone substantial refinement over the past decades. The nomenclature transition from NAFLD (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease) to MASLD reflects a paradigm shift in understanding this condition as a hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic dysregulation, independent of alcohol intake.6 MASLD is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis (involving ≥5% of hepatocytes) along with at least one feature of cardiometabolic dysfunction, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, or hypertension. Moreover, MASLD exhibits a bidirectional relationship with cardiovascular disease, linked by shared mechanisms such as chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, underscoring its multisystemic nature.7 Given its high prevalence and strong association with metabolic comorbidities, elucidating the molecular mechanisms that drive MASLD pathogenesis has become a critical research priority.

In this context, the discovery of functional non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) has reshaped our understanding of liver pathophysiological processes, uncovering their essential contributions to the pathogenesis of MASLD. Three principal classes of ncRNAs—microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs)—cooperatively regulate key aspects of hepatic pathology, including inflammation, fibrosis, and metabolic dysregulation, via intricate molecular networks. Among them, miRNAs are the most extensively studied; their high degree of evolutionary conservation across vertebrates highlights their fundamental role in liver development and metabolic zonation.8 LncRNAs, in contrast, exert regulatory influence through epigenetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms. Although they exhibit lower sequence conservation, their pronounced tissue specificity renders them attractive candidates for liver-directed therapeutics.9 Characterized by covalently closed loops, circRNAs dominate the hepatic RNA landscape owing to their exceptional stability, exhibiting a half-life exceeding 48 h compared to less than 10 h for linear RNAs.10 Collectively, the interconnected regulatory circuits, disease-stage-specific expression patterns, and detectability in biofluids position ncRNAs as promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets in MASLD, justifying a systematic investigation into their clinical applicability.

Despite considerable progress in delineating the roles of ncRNAs in MASLD, a persistent translational gap remains between mechanistic understanding and clinical implementation. This review summarizes the stage-specific functions of different ncRNA classes, including their dysregulation in steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, while critically assessing their diagnostic and therapeutic potential. Special emphasis is placed on overcoming delivery challenges, which currently constitute the primary bottleneck in translating ncRNA discoveries into viable treatments. Finally, we propose a translational roadmap that identifies targeted delivery systems as pivotal to realizing the clinical potential of ncRNA-based interventions for MASLD.

Pathogenesis and clinical aspects of MASLD

Mechanistic insights into MASLD pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of MASLD is complex and multifactorial, involving intricate interactions among metabolic, inflammatory, and fibrotic pathways that collectively drive disease initiation and progression.11 MASLD follows a well-characterized pathological continuum, advancing from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and ultimately to fibrosis, driven by a self-reinforcing cycle of metabolic dysregulation and cellular damage.12 The disease typically originates as isolated fat accumulation within hepatocytes, in the absence of significant inflammation or fibrosis. However, approximately one-third of MASLD patients develop MASH within 4–5 years, a stage marked by persistent lipid deposition accompanied by inflammation, hepatocyte injury, and cell death.13 MASH may then progress further to fibrosis and cirrhosis, characterized by progressive scarring and stiffening of the liver architecture, and can eventually lead to liver failure or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).14

At the core of MASLD pathogenesis lies a self-sustaining cycle of metabolic dysfunction rooted in hepatocellular lipid overload. This overload results from an imbalance among fatty acid uptake mediated by CD36 and FATP5, de novo lipogenesis controlled by sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c), and very low density lipoprotein (VLDL) export, initiating a cascade of lipotoxic injury.15 This manifests through mitochondrial β-oxidation overload generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that exceed antioxidant defenses, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress triggering unfolded protein responses, and insulin receptor substrate phosphorylation impairments that exacerbate peripheral lipolysis.16 The ensuing flux of free fatty acids not only perpetuates hepatic steatosis but also activates Kupffer cells to release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). These signals, together with lipid-stimulated HSCs, establish a pro-fibrogenic niche that accelerates the transition from simple steatosis to inflammatory MASH.17 Critically, this vicious cycle is driven by key cytokines (TGF-β, PDGF) and oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, forming a feed-forward loop that propels the disease from metabolic disturbance to end-stage fibrosis.18 This fibrogenic cascade is further amplified by extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, which generates a stiffened microenvironment that in turn promotes HSC activation, culminating in architectural distortion and cirrhosis.19

Superimposed upon these core metabolic and inflammatory disturbances, additional layers of regulation, including genetic predisposition, epigenetic modifications, and gut-liver axis dysregulation, further amplify disease progression. Risk alleles in genes such as patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 3 (PNPLA3), transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2), and membrane-bound O-acyltransferase domain containing 7 (MBOAT7), together with dynamic epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation patterns, foster a permissive metabolic environment.20 Concurrently, disruptions of the gut-liver axis contribute to hepatic inflammation, wherein microbiota-derived signals such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) compromise intestinal barrier integrity and trigger Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, while microbial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids influence hepatic lipid metabolism.21 Together, these multilayered interactions among host genetics, environmental triggers, and gut-derived metabolites account for the clinical heterogeneity of MASLD.

Within this already complex landscape, ncRNAs add an additional dimension of regulatory control, orchestrating disease progression through interconnected networks that operate across multiple pathological processes including lipid metabolism, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, mitochondrial homeostasis, and fibrogenesis, while also mediating critical crosstalk along the gut-liver axis.22,23 By functioning as both downstream effectors and upstream regulators within these pathways, ncRNAs form a sophisticated regulatory network that not only governs the intensity and duration of pathogenic insults but also contributes to the disease-stage-specific phenotypic transitions that characterize MASLD progression.

Diagnostic approaches and therapeutic interventions

Given the complex and multifactorial nature of MASLD pathogenesis, accurate diagnosis and effective therapeutic intervention remain central challenges in clinical management. The diagnosis of MASLD requires the concurrent presence of hepatic steatosis and at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia), highlighting the disease's fundamental association with metabolic syndrome. Definitive confirmation of hepatic steatosis relies on liver biopsy, defined histologically by macrovesicular fat in ≥5% of hepatocytes.24 Despite its diagnostic authority, the dependence on liver biopsy entails several limitations, including sampling variability, procedural risks, and cost considerations, which collectively restrict its utility for repeated monitoring.25 These constraints have motivated the development of non-invasive alternatives. Emerging biomarker panels (e.g., NIS4, which incorporates miR-34a and adipokine signals) and multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques (including proton density fat fraction (PDFF) and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE)) have demonstrated >85% concordance with biopsy for steatosis and fibrosis staging in prospective validation studies.26 Importantly, the early detection capability of these modalities may allow intervention at reversible disease stages, potentially preventing progression to decompensated cirrhosis and related mortality. This underscores the translational importance of non-invasive tools, even as liver biopsy remains valuable in clinical trials and mechanistic research.

Lifestyle interventions constitute the cornerstone of MASLD management, where a 7%–10% reduction in body weight through caloric restriction and aerobic exercise has been histologically proven to ameliorate hepatic steatosis and attenuate fibrosis.27 In morbidly obese individuals with MASLD, metabolic surgery has been shown to significantly improve both liver histology and metabolic parameters, providing a viable therapeutic option for this patient subgroup.28 In recent years, pharmacologic approaches have advanced considerably. In March 2024, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor-beta (THR-β) agonist, as the first pharmacologic therapy for the treatment of MASH with moderate to advanced liver fibrosis, marking a pivotal milestone in the field.29 Vitamin E (as an antioxidant) and pioglitazone (a PPAR-γ agonist) have demonstrated efficacy in reducing inflammation and fibrosis in patients with MASH.30 Furthermore, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (e.g., liraglutide) and sodium glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin and dapagliflozin), which target underlying metabolic dysregulation and have demonstrated beneficial effects on hepatic steatosis and comorbid type 2 diabetes, represent promising therapeutic avenues.31

Despite the progress made in non-invasive diagnostics and pharmacotherapy, significant gaps remain. Current diagnostic tools, while improving, still lack the sensitivity and specificity to reliably distinguish between simple steatosis and high-risk MASH without the use of invasive biopsy.32 Similarly, available pharmacologic options remain limited, with only one FDA-approved therapy and a paucity of agents that directly target fibrotic progression. In this context, ncRNAs, particularly those that are stably detectable in circulation, hold substantial promise. Their disease-stage-specific expression patterns, tissue origins, and presence in biofluids position them as ideal candidates for non-invasive liquid biopsy biomarkers. Moreover, the growing understanding of ncRNA regulatory networks in MASLD pathogenesis opens new avenues for mechanism-based therapeutics. As research continues to elucidate the complex roles of ncRNAs in this disease, their integration into diagnostic algorithms and therapeutic platforms represents a promising frontier for precision medicine in MASLD.

NcRNA networks in MASLD pathogenesis

Classification and function of ncRNAs

NcRNAs comprise a diverse array of RNA molecules that, despite lacking protein-coding potential, exert profound regulatory functions across virtually all biological processes. Based on their size, structure, and biogenesis, ncRNAs are broadly classified into several categories, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), and tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs). Together, these ncRNA species constitute an intricate regulatory network that governs gene expression at multiple levels.

MiRNAs are small non-coding RNAs of approximately 22 nucleotides that function as key post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression. More than 2600 mature miRNAs are now annotated in humans, and thousands more identified across species, underscoring their evolutionary conservation and wide-ranging functional significance.33 It is estimated that miRNAs regulate the expression of nearly 30% of the human genes, frequently through combinatorial mechanisms: individual miRNAs can target multiple mRNAs, and single genes are often influenced by multiple miRNAs.34,35 These small non-coding RNAs are commonly located within the introns of host genes and share promoters to enable coordinated expression, although some are encoded within exons or governed by independent regulatory elements.36 Functionally, miRNAs fine-tune gene expression through two principal mechanisms: mRNA degradation or translational repression, allowing for precise modulation of diverse biological processes.37 Recognized as critical regulators in development, metabolism, and disease pathogenesis, miRNAs integrate into complex regulatory networks to maintain cellular homeostasis.38,39

LncRNAs are defined as transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that lack protein-coding capacity. They display characteristic features such as tissue-specific expression patterns, generally low abundance, predominant nuclear localization, and limited evolutionary sequence conservation across species.9 Despite their divergent primary sequences, lncRNAs play essential roles in gene regulation and diverse cellular processes, indicating that their functional significance likely derives from conserved secondary structures or protein interaction networks, rather than specific linear sequence motifs.40 Mechanistically, lncRNAs function through a variety of regulatory paradigms. One well-established mechanism involves their role as modular scaffolds, where they recruit epigenetic modifiers to specific genomic loci to modulate histone modifications and DNA methylation, thereby facilitating gene activation or silencing.41 The lncRNA HOTAIR classically directs the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) to target genes, leading to transcriptional repression.42 LncRNAs can also bind directly to transcription factors to fine-tune transcriptional activity, or act as molecular sponges for RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), sequestering them and altering their regulatory functions.43 A subset of lncRNAs contains open reading frames (ORFs) and can be translated into functional micropeptides, further expanding their functional repertoire.44 Notably, the biological functions of lncRNAs are closely tied to subcellular localization: nuclear lncRNAs predominantly regulate chromatin architecture and transcription, whereas cytoplasmic lncRNAs more commonly influence mRNA stability and signal transduction pathways.45 This functional versatility establishes lncRNAs as dynamic and multifaceted regulators of cellular homeostasis.

CircRNAs are covalently closed RNA molecules generated through back-splicing of pre-mRNA. This unique circular architecture endows them with exceptional resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation and allows them to exhibit distinct tissue- and cell type-specific expression profiles, positioning them as promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets.46 Based on genomic origin and subcellular localization, circRNAs are categorized into three major subtypes: exonic circRNAs (ecircRNAs), which are predominantly cytoplasmic; intronic circRNAs (ciRNAs); and exon–intron circRNAs (EIciRNAs), both of which are retained in the nucleus and modulate parental gene expression through interactions with RNA polymerase II.47 Recent advances have established circRNAs as versatile regulators functioning through miRNA sequestration, protein scaffolding, transcriptional modulation, and even peptide encoding, thereby influencing cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.48

In addition to these well-characterized classes, emerging ncRNA species have recently garnered attention for their potential roles in metabolic regulation. snoRNAs are small ncRNAs of 60–300 nucleotides that primarily guide chemical modifications of ribosomal RNA.49 piRNAs, typically 24–31 nucleotides in length, function in transposon silencing within germline cells; however, emerging evidence implicates them in somatic tissues and metabolic processes.50 tsRNAs are derived from mature or precursor tRNAs and have been linked to disease progression.51 Although their roles in MASLD remain largely unexplored, preliminary studies suggest that they may contribute to hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity, representing an exciting frontier for future investigation.52, 53, 54

Rather than functioning in isolation, these diverse ncRNA classes engage in extensive crosstalk through the competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network.55 The ceRNA hypothesis posits that various RNA species—including mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and pseudogenes—communicate with one another by competing for shared miRNA binding sites. By acting as miRNA sponges, these ceRNAs modulate miRNA availability, thereby establishing a complex post-transcriptional regulatory network.56 This interplay adds an additional layer of complexity to gene expression control and has been increasingly recognized as a key mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of various diseases, including MASLD.57 Deciphering these ceRNA networks is therefore essential for understanding how ncRNAs collectively orchestrate disease progression and for identifying critical regulatory hubs that may serve as therapeutic targets.

Among the most extensively studied hepatic miRNAs, miR-122 stands out as a master regulator of lipid homeostasis. Comprising over 50% of the liver's miRNA content, miR-122 coordinately suppresses key lipogenic targets including fatty acid synthase (FASN), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl CoA reductase (HMGCR), and SREBP isoforms.59 Genetic ablation studies indicate its essential role in MASLD pathogenesis: miR-122-deficient mice develop severe hepatic steatosis due to enhanced triglyceride synthesis (via SREBP-1c activation) and impaired VLDL secretion. The spontaneous progression to steatohepatitis and HCC in these models, coupled with phenotypic rescue upon miR-122 restoration, establishes this miRNA as both a guardian of metabolic health and an inhibitor of hepatocarcinogenesis in CLD.60 Beyond its cell-autonomous regulatory functions, miR-122 is actively released into the circulation during MASLD progression. Serum levels closely correlate with histological disease severity, supporting its dual role as a pathogenic mediator and a clinically relevant biomarker.61

Another key player is the miR-33 family (miR-33a/b), which is genomically linked to SREBP transcriptional regulators and coordinates lipid and cholesterol homeostasis.62 Elevated miR-33 expression in MASLD promotes hepatic steatosis by concurrently suppressing cholesterol efflux transporters (ABCA1/ABCG1) and mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (via CPT1α/PGC1α inhibition), while also impairing energy sensing through downregulation of AMP-activated protein kinase alpha (AMPKα). This multi-targeted action establishes a self-reinforcing metabolic imbalance marked by lipid accumulation and insulin resistance.63 The conservation of miR-33's regulatory network across cardiometabolic diseases, from atherosclerosis to obesity, highlights its potential as a unique therapeutic target capable of addressing both hepatic and systemic manifestations of metabolic dysfunction.64

LncRNAs also contribute significantly to metabolic regulation, as exemplified by steroid receptor RNA activator (SRA). Initially identified as a steroid receptor coactivator, SRA modulates metabolism in both adipocytes and hepatocytes. In adipocytes, lncRNA SRA enhances PPARγ-mediated transcriptional activation to promote glucose uptake and differentiation, while simultaneously strengthening insulin signaling via dual mechanisms: suppression of p38/JNK phosphorylation and upregulation of insulin receptor expression.65 In the liver, SRA promotes steatotic progression by driving lipid accumulation and repressing adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) expression through forkhead box protein O1 (FoxO1)-mediated transcriptional interference, underscoring its role as a cross-tissue metabolic regulator.66 These findings establish SRA as a prototypical multifunctional lncRNA that integrates nuclear receptor signaling, insulin action, and lipid homeostasis in MASLD development.

In addition to linear ncRNAs, circRNAs have emerged as important regulators of lipid metabolism through both non-coding and coding mechanisms. For instance, circRNA RRM2 (circRRM2) is significantly downregulated in MASLD models and functions as a molecular sponge for miR-142-5p. By sequestering miR-142-5p, circRRM2 relieves the inhibition of its target neuregulin 1 (NRG1), thereby upregulating NRG1 expression. The consequent activation of NRG1 signaling suppresses key lipogenic genes (e.g., SREBP1 and FASN), ultimately attenuating triglyceride accumulation and steatosis in both cellular and mouse models of MASLD.67 This study shows a novel circRNA-miRNA-mRNA network in hepatic lipid metabolism, positioning modulation of this axis as a potential therapeutic strategy for MASLD.

Another intriguing circRNA is circ-SLC9A6, which encodes a functional peptide, SLC9A6-126aa, that acts as an epigenetic regulator. Under lipid overload conditions, SLC9A6-126aa translocates to the nucleus and directly binds to the promoter of the fatty acid transporter gene CD36. This interaction recruits the acetyltransferase MOF to catalyze histone H4 lysine 16 acetylation (H4K16ac) at the promoter, thereby epigenetically activating CD36 transcription. Upregulated CD36 subsequently triggers phosphorylation of p38 and ERK-MAPK signaling cascades, driving hepatic lipid dyshomeostasis characterized by increased lipid uptake and storage. The clinical relevance of this axis is underscored by the positive correlation between SLC9A6-126aa levels and both CD36 expression and activated MAPK signaling in livers of MASLD patients.68 Thus, this circRNA-derived peptide exacerbates MASLD progression through H4K16ac-mediated transcriptional reprogramming, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

NcRNA networks regulating oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ER stress in MASLD

Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction represent core pathological drivers in MASLD progression, characterized by excessive ROS generation resulting from impaired β-oxidation and defective electron transport chain (ETC) complexes, which collectively promote lipid peroxidation and hepatocyte injury.92 In parallel, ER stress has emerged as an additional cellular stress pathway that amplifies inflammation and metabolic disturbances. Within this complex stress landscape, ncRNAs function as key regulators at multiple levels (Fig. 1B).

Among the most extensively studied stress-related ncRNAs is miR-34a, which has emerged as a central regulator by concurrently modulating lipid metabolism and cellular stress responses.59 Its hepatic overexpression directly suppresses Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)-mediated deacetylation of lipogenic regulators and represses PPARα-driven fatty acid oxidation, resulting in impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and incomplete β-oxidation.93 Clinically, circulating miR-34a levels correlate strongly with histological disease severity, reflecting its pathophysiological contribution to hepatocyte apoptosis. Therapeutic inhibition of miR-34a consistently alleviates steatohepatitis and fibrosis in preclinical models, supporting its inclusion in the NIS4 diagnostic algorithm for identifying at-risk MASH patients.94, 95, 96 Together, these findings place miR-34a at the intersection of metabolic dysregulation and hepatic injury, underscoring its dual utility as a biomarker and therapeutic target in MASLD management.

Beyond miRNAs, circRNAs also play important roles in modulating stress responses. Notably, circ_0046366 and circ_0046367 function as endogenous modulators of MASLD by acting as molecular sponges for miR-34a. Through this competitive binding, they derepress PPARα expression, thereby activating its downstream transcriptional network of lipid metabolic genes and restoring hepatic metabolic homeostasis. Both circRNAs are significantly downregulated in steatotic liver tissue, and experimental restoration of their expression mitigates multiple MASLD pathological features, including oxidative stress, programmed cell death, and mitochondrial impairment.3

Another mitochondria-enriched circRNA, circRNA SCAR (steatohepatitis-associated circRNA ATP5B regulator), has been identified as a key regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis. Its expression progressively decreases from steatosis to cirrhosis in patient-derived HSCs. Mechanistically, circRNA SCAR binds directly to ATP5B, competitively inhibiting cyclophilin D (CypD)-mediated mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening and thereby reducing ETC leakage and mitochondrial ROS overproduction. This mitochondrial stabilization is transcriptionally regulated by the PGC-1α/CHOP axis, which becomes suppressed under lipid-induced ER stress conditions typical of MASH. Clinically, circRNA SCAR expression exhibits a strong inverse correlation with the severity of hepatic fibrosis and insulin resistance in patients with MASLD, supporting its role as both a sensitive progression biomarker and a promising candidate for mitochondrial-targeted therapies.97

LncRNAs also contribute to cellular stress responses, as exemplified by lncRNA-Gm9795. This lncRNA is significantly upregulated in both in vivo and in vitro models of MASH. Functional studies reveal that while it does not directly affect hepatic lipid accumulation, its overexpression potently promotes the expression of key inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. Mechanistically, Gm9795 exerts its pro-inflammatory effect by activating ER stress, as evidenced by its upregulation of critical ER stress markers inositol-requiring enzyme 1 alpha (IRE1α) and binding immunoglobulin protein (BIP). This ER stress activation subsequently triggers the nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-κB) and JNK signaling pathways. The causal link is demonstrated by the fact that the pro-inflammatory activation of NF-κB/JNK by Gm9795 can be rescued by the ER stress inhibitor tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), while ER stress agonists can reverse the effects of Gm9795 knockdown.98 These findings position Gm9795 as a key molecular link between ER stress and inflammation in MASH, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for intervening in disease progression.

Additionally, emerging evidence highlights the role of snoRNAs in metabolic stress regulation. The snoRNA SNORA73, encoded within the small nucleolar RNA hosting gene 3 (Snhg3) locus, is a critical regulator of cellular metabolism and the response to lipotoxic stress. In the context of MASLD, loss of SNORA73 function reprograms cellular metabolism, leading to enhanced mitochondrial oxidative function and increased glutathione (GSH) production, which protects against lipid-induced oxidative stress and cell death. In vivo, knockdown of SNORA73 in mouse models protects against hepatic steatosis and attenuates diet-induced inflammation and oxidative stress. Specifically, in a methionine- and choline-deficient (MCD) diet model, SNORA73 deficiency reduces hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammatory infiltrates, and the expression of pro-fibrotic markers.54 These findings establish SNORA73 as a key modulator of metabolic stress responses and hepatic inflammation, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target for steatohepatitis.

Taken together, these findings establish ncRNAs as master regulators of oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ER stress in MASLD, forming a multi-layered regulatory network that governs cellular stress responses. NcRNA networks modulating inflammation in MASLD

Inflammation serves as a critical driver of MASLD progression, primarily mediated through sustained Kupffer cell activation and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) via pattern recognition receptor signaling pathways, notably the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB cascade and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation.17 Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that ncRNAs act as key modulators of these inflammatory networks, fine-tuning the intensity and duration of immune responses through precise regulation of cytokine production and inflammasome activity in MASLD (Fig. 1C).

Among the most well-characterized inflammatory miRNAs is miR-155, whose pleiotropic functions in MASLD pathogenesis display remarkable cell type-specificity, exerting divergent but synergistically pathogenic effects across different hepatic cell populations. In hepatocytes, downregulation of miR-155 promotes LXRα/SREBP-1c-mediated lipogenic programming by directly targeting negative regulators, thereby exacerbating hepatic steatosis.103 In contrast, within the hepatic immune microenvironment, elevated miR-155 expression in Kupffer cells drives a potent pro-inflammatory response through several mechanisms: (i) enhancing TLR4/NF-κB signaling via suppression of the negative regulators, suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1) and inositol polyphosphate-5-phosphatase (SHIP1); (ii) promoting NLRP3 inflammasome activation; and (iii) modulating the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway.104,105 The central role of miR-155 in linking metabolic dysregulation with inflammatory cascades establishes it as both a key pathophysiological mediator and a promising therapeutic target in advanced MASLD.

In contrast to the pro-inflammatory role of miR-155, miR-223 functions as a potent anti-inflammatory regulator in MASLD pathogenesis, operating through a distinctive intercellular communication network. Whereas miR-155 amplifies inflammation via Kupffer cell activation, miR-223 is transported via neutrophil-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) and selectively internalized by hepatocytes through an LDLR/APOE-dependent uptake mechanism—a discovery that unveils a novel mode of cellular crosstalk in steatohepatitis.106 Within hepatocytes, miR-223 exerts multifaceted immunosuppressive effects by directly targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Clinical investigations reveal a strong inverse correlation between circulating miR-223 levels and histological disease severity. Furthermore, gain- and loss-of-function studies in preclinical models confirm its hepatoprotective role in attenuating both inflammatory infiltration and fibrotic progression.107

LncRNAs also play critical roles in inflammation regulation, as exemplified by metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1). This nuclear-enriched lncRNA serves as a critical upstream regulator of both metabolic and inflammatory cascades in MASLD. MALAT1 enhances the stability of SREBP-1c, thereby promoting hepatic steatosis, lipid accumulation, and insulin resistance.108 In contrast to the cytoplasmic miRNA-mediated mechanisms described earlier, MALAT1 coordinates inflammatory responses through nuclear-specific actions: it drives pro-inflammatory signaling by enhancing chemokine CXCL5 expression via NF-κB-dependent transcriptional activation, thereby facilitating neutrophil recruitment and Kupffer cell activation. Preclinical intervention study shows that MALAT1 silencing not only reduces steatosis but also significantly attenuates key features of metabolic inflammation and pro-fibrotic signaling, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target for the inflammatory axis of advanced MASLD.109

CircRNAs have also emerged as key players in inflammation, as demonstrated by circRNA SOD2 (circSOD2). Upregulated in the livers of both animal models and MASLD patients, circSOD2 functions as a ceRNA by sponging miR-532-3p, which is downregulated in the MASLD. This sequestration prevents miR-532-3p from binding to its target, thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) mRNA, leading to TXNIP upregulation and subsequent NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which triggers hepatocyte pyroptosis. Notably, circSOD2 knockdown attenuates this cascade, reducing the expression of pyroptosis effectors (GSDMD and cleaved caspase-1) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), thereby alleviating hepatic inflammation.110 Thus, the circSOD2/miR-532-3p/TXNIP axis represents a critical regulatory circuit that exacerbates MASLD progression by potentiating NLRP3 inflammasome-driven inflammatory responses and pyroptosis.

Collectively, these findings establish ncRNAs as master regulators of immune-metabolic crosstalk in MASLD, forming an intricate regulatory network that governs inflammatory disease progression and opens new avenues for targeted therapeutic intervention. NcRNA networks driving liver fibrogenesis in MASLD

Liver fibrogenesis marks a critical transition in MASLD pathogenesis, promoting irreversible liver damage through sustained activation of HSCs and ECM deposition. This process is orchestrated by key signaling pathways, including TGF-β, PDGF, and VEGF, that drive HSC activation and proliferation. The resulting architectural distortion and portal hypertension not only accelerate hepatic dysfunction but also elevate the risk of HCC, establishing fibrosis stage as the strongest predictor of MASLD-related mortality.120

Among the miRNAs implicated in fibrogenesis, miR-192 has emerged as a crucial regulator, exhibiting dual tissue-specific and circulating biomarker functions. Predominantly expressed in quiescent HSCs, miR-192 exerts potent anti-fibrotic effects by suppressing HSC activation, proliferation, and migration through inhibition of key fibrogenic pathways such as TGF-β/Smad and PDGF signaling. Paradoxically, although intrahepatic miR-192 expression declines during fibrosis progression in preclinical models, its serum levels are markedly elevated in MASLD patients and show a strong positive correlation with disease severity.121 This inverse relationship between tissue and circulating miR-192 may reflect compensatory secretion by stressed hepatocytes or selective release from activated HSCs, supporting its inclusion among the top-tier diagnostic miRNAs (with miR-34a and miR-122) for non-invasive MASLD staging.93

Another pivotal miRNA is miR-21, which serves as a master orchestrator of MASLD pathogenesis by integrating metabolic dysregulation, inflammatory signaling, and fibrosis progression into a coordinated regulatory network.93 Its expression displays a striking stage-specific upregulation in MASH patients compared to those with simple steatosis. Mechanistically, miR-21 promotes HSC activation through several synergistic pathways: (i) PTEN suppression leading to hyperactive PI3K/AKT signaling; (ii) inhibition of Sprouty2 (SPRY2) and hepatocyte nuclear factor (HNF)-4α sustaining ERK1/2 activation; (iii) SMAD7 silencing enhancing TGF-β signaling; and (iv) lipoprotein receptor-related protein 6 (LRP6) downregulation promoting Wnt/β-catenin-driven fibrotic responses.122, 123, 124 Clinically, circulating miR-21 levels correlate strongly with liver damage and predict disease progression independent of conventional risk factors, underscoring its dual role as a mechanistic mediator and a promising theranostic target in advanced MASLD.121 Notably, the lncRNA MEG3 participates in MASLD regulation largely through sequestration of hepatic miR-21.125

LncRNAs also play prominent roles in fibrogenesis, as exemplified by NEAT1. This lncRNA functions as a pleiotropic effector by acting as a multi-target miRNA sponge, forming an extensive regulatory network. Among its various miRNA targets, NEAT1 promotes fibrogenic progression via sponging of miR-506, while concurrently binding miR-139-5p to activate the c-Jun/SREBP-1c axis and modulate lipid metabolism.126,127 Hepatic NEAT1 expression is significantly upregulated in both high-fat diet-induced MASLD models and in patients with advanced MASH fibrosis, underscoring its potential role in disease progression.9 The breadth of NEAT1's miRNA interactions, spanning from metabolic to fibrotic pathways, establishes it as a master regulator capable of coordinating multiple pathological processes in advanced MASLD.

Another lncRNA, LFAR1, promotes hepatic fibrogenesis through a dual mechanistic cascade: it serves as a scaffold enhancing TGFβR1-mediated Smad2/3 phosphorylation, and acts as a transcriptional coactivator that directly binds Smad2/3 to potentiate TGF-β/Notch signaling networks.128 Although these findings reveal conserved pro-fibrotic pathways in murine models, the translational potential of LFAR1 remains limited due to the unresolved existence of a functional human ortholog, highlighting a critical gap in cross-species lncRNA biology.9

Beyond endogenous ncRNAs, engineered circRNAs have been developed as therapeutic platforms for liver fibrosis. For instance, the circRNA encoding human relaxin-2 (cRLN2) was developed as a novel circRNA therapeutic platform for liver fibrosis. In mouse models of CCl4-induced liver fibrosis, the nanoparticle-delivered cRLN2 exerted multi-targeted anti-fibrotic effects primarily by interfering with the TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway. cRLN2 significantly suppressed HSC activation and downregulated profibrogenic factors (TGF-β, FGF2, and PDGFs), while simultaneously promoting ECM degradation through the upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and downregulation of their inhibitors (TIMPs). Transcriptomic analyses further indicated that cRLN2 treatment partially restored global gene expression profiles and metabolic functions in fibrotic livers. Notably, cRLN2 demonstrated superior efficacy to relaxin protein treatment and exhibited excellent safety and tolerability in vivo, highlighting its promise as a platform for treating chronic fibrotic diseases.129

Emerging evidence also implicates piRNAs in fibrogenesis, as shown by piR-823. Upregulated during hepatic fibrogenesis, piR-823 plays a critical role in activating HSCs. Functionally, piR-823 promotes HSC proliferation and the production of fibrotic markers such as alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen type I (COL1A) via direct binding to the eukaryotic initiation factor 3B (EIF3B). This piR-823-EIF3B complex subsequently recruits TGF-β1 mRNA and enhances its translation, leading to increased TGF-β1 protein expression without affecting its transcription. Given that TGF-β1 is a master regulator of both inflammatory and fibrogenic responses, the piR-823/EIF3B axis represents a novel pathway regulating a core pro-inflammatory mediator in liver disease. This establishes a vicious cycle where activated HSCs further produce piR-823, driving fibrosis progression.53 Therefore, targeting the piR-823-EIF3B interaction presents a potential therapeutic strategy to disrupt the inflammatory and fibrogenic cascade in MASLD.

Taken together, these findings delineate a sophisticated ncRNA regulatory hierarchy governing fibrosis progression in MASLD (Fig. 1D). This ncRNA interactome not only provides novel pathophysiological insights but also establishes a framework for developing combinatorial diagnostic and targeted therapeutic strategies to disrupt fibrosis at multiple nodal points in advanced disease. NcRNA-mediated gut-liver axis crosstalk in MASLD

The gut-liver axis represents a bidirectional communication network that integrates signals from the intestinal microbiota, intestinal epithelium, and hepatic immune-metabolic systems. Disruption of this axis—manifested as increased intestinal permeability, microbial dysbiosis, and translocation of bacterial products—is increasingly recognized as a key driver of MASLD pathogenesis.134 Within this complex interplay, ncRNAs have emerged as critical molecular mediators that orchestrate host-microbiota crosstalk and influence disease progression (Fig. 1E).135

Emerging evidence reveals a sophisticated bidirectional crosstalk between the gut microbiota and the host in MASLD, mediated by miRNAs via EVs. Host-derived miRNAs, packaged into exosomes, can influence microbial composition, while bacterial miRNAs within outer membrane vesicles may modulate host gene expression.136 A study involves the probiotic Lactobacillus casei, whose supplementation is associated with the downregulation of host miR-144. This reduction enhances the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and OCLN), thereby improving gut barrier integrity and attenuating a critical pathological factor in MASLD.137 Conversely, the host miR-21, which is upregulated in disease progression and promotes hepatic inflammation and fibrosis, is implicated in modulating specific gut bacteria such as Oscillobacter, Prevotella, and Ruminococcus.138 This creates a vicious cycle where dysbiosis and increased gut permeability promote liver inflammation, which in turn alters miRNA expression to further disrupt microbial homeostasis, collectively driving MASLD pathogenesis.

Besides these endogenous ncRNA-mediated regulatory circuits, emerging evidence demonstrates the therapeutic potential of dietary ncRNAs acting through the gut-liver axis. Plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles have gained attention as natural carriers of functional ncRNAs. A recent study showed that oral administration of broccoli-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (BDENs) ameliorates high-fat diet-induced MASLD in mice. BDENs encapsulate 1659 miRNAs predicted to target pathways central to MASLD pathogenesis, including immune regulation, antioxidant defense, and fatty acid biosynthesis. Mechanistically, BDENs restored intestinal barrier integrity, normalized cecal bile acid profiles, and reshaped gut microbiota composition. These effects were accompanied by reduced serum LPS levels and suppressed hepatic inflammation, as evidenced by decreased F4/80, IL-6, and IL-1β expression.139 Overall, these findings suggest that BDENs and their miRNA cargos mitigate MASLD progression through coordinated modulation of the gut-liver axis, highlighting plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles as both a source of bioactive ncRNAs and a novel therapeutic strategy for MASLD.

Despite these advances, the current understanding of ncRNA-mediated gut-liver crosstalk in MASLD remains preliminary. Mechanistic studies are often limited by reliance on correlative analyses, and the causal relationships between specific ncRNAs, microbial taxa, and disease phenotypes remain incompletely defined. Moreover, substantial heterogeneity in study findings—stemming from differences in experimental models, intervention protocols, and the inherent dynamism of the gut microbiota—poses significant challenges for translating these discoveries into clinical applications. Future investigations utilizing gnotobiotic models, single-cell resolution technologies, and multi-omics approaches will be essential to dissect the causal architecture of this regulatory network and to establish a foundation for ncRNA-based diagnostics and therapeutics targeting the gut-liver axis in MASLD.

Diagnostic and therapeutic potential of ncRNAs in MASLD

Circulating ncRNAs as non-invasive biomarkers

Although ncRNAs predominantly operate within cells, they are also selectively packaged into various circulating carriers, including EVs, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) complexes, and ribonucleoprotein complexes (e.g., AGO2-bound), which protect them from degradation by ubiquitous RNases.140 Rather than being passive by-products of cellular turnover, these circulating ncRNAs exhibit hormone-like characteristics, mediating intercellular communication via cell-specific uptake mechanisms. This shift highlights their dual roles as functional signaling molecules and stable biomarkers, with distinct EV-derived ncRNA signatures reflecting tissue-specific pathological states. Virtually all cell types actively secrete ncRNAs, and among circulating species, extracellular miRNAs remain the best characterized to date.141 Of these, exosomal miRNAs (exomiRs) have arisen as pivotal mediators of systemic metabolic crosstalk in MASLD, using small EVs to deliver bioactive cargo across distant organs.142 ExomiRs originating from the liver or adipose tissue enter the circulation and influence the function of other organs, thereby contributing to systemic metabolic dysregulation and disease progression.143 For example, exomiR-155 secreted by adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) can be internalized by adipocytes, myocytes, and hepatocytes in vitro, where its overexpression disrupts cellular insulin signaling.144 Similarly, exomiR-122, a well-studied liver-derived exomiR, is released by hepatocytes and can modulate energy metabolism in peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle, underscoring its role in liver-extrahepatic communication during MASLD progression.145 Elucidating the mechanisms of such exomiR trafficking not only supports their utility as biomarkers of MASLD progression but also identifies them as promising targets for RNA-based therapeutics designed to intercept pathological inter-organ signaling.

Circulating miRNAs (particularly miR-122, miR-34a, miR-21, miR-193, and miR-192) show robust and consistent associations with MASLD/MASH progression, with miR-34a now included in the clinically validated NIS4 diagnostic panel.26 Their exceptional biomarker potential arises from unique physicochemical properties: high abundance in serum/plasma, nuclease resistance conferred by exosomal or protein-complex packaging, and stability throughout freeze-thaw cycles, facilitating sensitive detection via PCR or next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms.146 Importantly, these circulating miRNA profiles directly reflect tissue-level miRNA dysregulation, capturing real-time pathophysiological changes. This combination of analytical robustness and biological relevance has positioned extracellular miRNAs at the forefront of non-invasive diagnostic development, with growing applications in disease stratification, fibrosis staging, and treatment response monitoring in MASLD.147

Therapeutic potential of ncRNAs

Therapeutic targeting of miRNAs primarily follows two fundamental strategies: inhibition of pathogenic miRNAs using antagomiRs, and restoration of beneficial miRNAs via synthetic mimics. This approach aims to rebalance dysregulated molecular pathways at their source. However, the clinical translation of miRNA-based therapeutics for MASLD has encountered both encouraging advances and notable setbacks. Although preclinical studies have provided compelling proof-of-concept (such as exosome-delivered miR-199a-5p modulation significantly altering hepatic steatosis in animal models), clinical progress has faced substantial hurdles.148 The most advanced candidate, AZD4076 (RG-125), N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)-conjugated antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting the insulin resistance-associated miR-103/107 cluster, was discontinued during Phase II trials in 2017 due to safety concerns linked to related compounds in the development pipeline.93 Notably, miR-122-targeting therapies originally developed for hepatitis C virus (HCV), such as Miravirsen and RG-101, were also halted due to cases of drug-induced jaundice, despite miR-122's hepatoprotective role in MASLD.59 These clinical experiences highlight two major challenges: (i) the need for more advanced delivery systems that improve tissue specificity and limit off-target effects, and (ii) the complex, context-dependent functions of certain miRNAs that complicate their therapeutic targeting.

The modulation of lncRNAs and circRNAs for MASLD therapy currently focuses on two approaches: (i) knockdown using ASOs or siRNAs, and (ii) overexpression via viral vectors or plasmids, with efficacy highly dependent on subcellular localization.149 Nuclear-retained lncRNAs (e.g., MALAT1 and NEAT1) are more effectively targeted by ASOs, while cytoplasmic lncRNAs (e.g., DANCR) are more amenable to siRNA-mediated silencing.150 Similarly, circRNA targeting has shown preclinical promise: lentiviral overexpression of hsa_circ_004817 ameliorated hepatic lipid deposition, and siRNA-mediated knockdown of circRNA_002581 attenuated inflammation in MASH models.91,151 Beyond traditional plasmid- or viral vector-based overexpression, recent advances have enabled the in vitro synthesis of circRNAs for direct therapeutic administration. A landmark study demonstrated that synthetic RCRIN, a read-through circRNA downregulated in MASLD livers, when directly administered, significantly suppressed MASLD pathogenesis in mouse models, highlighting the feasibility of synthetic circRNA-based therapeutics.152 However, these strategies are constrained by limitations such as poor in vivo stability, off-target effects, and insufficient tissue specificity. Emerging solutions, including nanoparticle- and exosome-based delivery systems (e.g., GA-RM/GZ/PL nanoparticles for circRNA_0001805), improve targeting precision and efficiency, though further validation is required before clinical translation.153 Despite the considerable therapeutic potential of lncRNAs and circRNAs, given their ability to coordinately regulate multiple disease-relevant pathways, their successful translation from bench to bedside will require advances in delivery technologies, rigorous safety evaluation, and deeper mechanistic understanding of their roles in MASLD progression.

Advances in delivery systems for ncRNA therapeutics

The development of effective delivery platforms has become crucial for realizing the therapeutic potential of ncRNAs in MASLD, with recent innovations addressing key challenges in hepatic targeting, nucleic acid stability, and clinical translation (Fig. 2B). Lipid-based nanoparticles (LNPs) and GalNAc-conjugated oligonucleotides have shown exceptional promise for liver-directed delivery.154 LNPs employ optimized cationic lipid formulations (e.g., DLin-MC3-DMA) to efficiently encapsulate therapeutic RNAs, as demonstrated by the clinical success of FDA-approved therapies such as Patisiran for transthyretin amyloidosis.155 Their modular design enables further functionalization with targeting ligands (e.g., GalNAc for hepatocytes and anisamide for lung tumors) to enhance cell-type specificity.156 Notably, GalNAc serves as a carbohydrate ligand that binds with high affinity to the asialoglycoprotein receptor 1 (ASGR1), which is highly expressed and continuously recycled on hepatocytes, facilitating efficient uptake of phosphorothioate-modified ASOs and siRNAs via endocytosis.154 This strategy has facilitated clinical breakthroughs, such as Inclisiran for hypercholesterolemia, which exploits the liver's unique vascular permeability and receptor density to achieve effective cellular internalization prior to renal clearance.157

Therapeutic strategies for MASLD are further broadening with innovative delivery systems. Exosomes have gained attention as promising nanotherapeutic vehicles due to their innate biocompatibility and ability to be surface-engineered for tissue-specific targeting.158 Dual-functionalized exosomes engineered with both a 5-HT1D antibody and CD47 protein successfully deliver miR-29b to activated HSCs, bypassing ECM barriers while avoiding immune clearance, leading to significant attenuation of liver fibrosis.159 Similarly, Liu et al.160 engineered EVs with a hyaluronic acid (HA) ligand for CD44-targeted delivery and loaded them with therapeutic miR-181a-5p, generating a targeted nanovesicle (DPH-EVs@miR). This strategy effectively suppresses HSC activation and ECM deposition by blocking the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thereby improving liver function and mitigating fibrosis progression.

As these technologies continue to advance, the vision of clinically feasible ncRNA-based therapies for MASLD is increasingly achievable. By enabling precise delivery of ncRNAs to hepatic cell types, these next-generation systems offer considerable potential to address the multifactorial pathology of MASLD and may ultimately transform clinical management of this increasingly common metabolic disorder. The integration of novel delivery platforms with emerging ncRNA targets could finally achieve the long-standing promise of precision medicine in hepatology.

Challenges and proposed strategies of ncRNAs in MASLD

Despite remarkable progress in elucidating the regulatory roles of ncRNAs in MASLD, translating these mechanistic insights into clinical applications faces substantial challenges ranging from basic biology to clinical implementation.

One major bottleneck lies in the technical and biological hurdles associated with ncRNA-based therapeutics. The inherent instability of ncRNAs in biological fluids, coupled with their susceptibility to nuclease degradation, necessitates advanced delivery platforms to achieve sustained and controlled release at target sites. Although EVs and LNPs have emerged as promising carriers that protect ncRNAs from degradation and facilitate tissue-specific delivery, ensuring precise targeting while minimizing off-target effects remains a formidable challenge. ncRNAs operate within complex regulatory networks, and their regulation can inadvertently affect unintended targets, leading to unpredictable biological consequences.161 Consequently, thorough assessment of ncRNA specificity and optimization of delivery systems are essential requirements for safe and effective therapeutic translation.

Beyond these technical challenges, the path to clinical implementation is hindered by a lack of large-scale, well-designed clinical trials. Despite encouraging preclinical data demonstrating the efficacy of ncRNA modulation in MASLD models, robust clinical evidence remains limited. Current studies are restricted in scale and often lack longitudinal follow-up to assess long-term safety and potential adverse effects, including immunogenicity, unintended immune modulation, and off-target gene silencing. The intrinsic heterogeneity of MASLD, shaped by diverse genetic backgrounds, metabolic comorbidities, and environmental exposures, further complicates trial design and outcome interpretation. Addressing these gaps will require multicenter, randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols, well-defined patient stratification, and rigorous safety monitoring to establish the therapeutic window and clinical utility of ncRNA-based interventions.

MASLD encompasses a broad spectrum of clinical phenotypes, ranging from simple steatosis to advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis, with distinct underlying metabolic drivers and genetic susceptibilities.162 Notably, ncRNA expression profiles also exhibit marked heterogeneity across the disease stages, reflecting the dynamic nature of the regulatory networks they constitute.93 This dual heterogeneity underscores the need for precision medicine approaches that move beyond one-size-fits-all strategies. At the systems biology level, integrated multi-omics approaches offer a powerful framework for dissecting the complex ncRNA networks that regulate MASLD pathogenesis. Furthermore, the convergence of multi-omics profiling with artificial intelligence (AI) may offer a transformative path forward (Fig. 2C).163 Machine learning algorithms can integrate large-scale transcriptomic, epigenomic, and clinical datasets to identify patient-specific ncRNA signatures, predict treatment responses, and stratify individuals for targeted interventions. AI-driven approaches can also simulate the complex, non-linear interactions within ncRNA regulatory networks, enabling the identification of core network hubs that may serve as optimal therapeutic targets for specific patient subsets.164

Taken together, addressing the technical, clinical, and biological heterogeneity challenges will require concerted interdisciplinary efforts and continued innovation in delivery systems, multi-omics integration, and AI-driven analytics. Nevertheless, these collective advances hold great promise for translating ncRNA-based diagnostics and therapeutics into precision medicine strategies for MASLD.

Conclusions and future perspectives

MASLD has become the most prevalent CLD worldwide, driven by a complex interplay of metabolic, inflammatory, and fibrotic pathways. Over the past decade, ncRNAs, particularly miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, have emerged as one of the central regulators of these pathological processes. Rather than functioning in isolation, these ncRNAs form intricate regulatory networks that integrate and amplify disease signals across multiple cellular compartments and pathological stages. This review has examined the stage-specific roles of ncRNAs in MASLD pathogenesis, evaluated their diagnostic and therapeutic potential, and discussed the major challenges that must be overcome to translate these discoveries into clinical practice.

A key message emerging from this synthesis is that ncRNAs function as master coordinators across the core pathological axes of MASLD. In metabolic dysregulation, molecules such as miR-122, miR-33, lncRNA SRA, circRRM2, and the circ-SLC9A6-encoded peptide orchestrate hepatic lipogenesis, cholesterol balance, and insulin sensitivity. In the domain of cellular stress, miR-34a, circSCAR, Gm9795, and SNORA73 modulate oxidative damage, mitochondrial integrity, and ER stress. Inflammatory responses are regulated by ncRNAs such as miR-155, miR-223, MALAT1, and circSOD2, which govern Kupffer cell activation, cytokine release, and inflammasome-driven pyroptosis. During fibrogenic progression, miR-192, miR-21, lncRNAs NEAT1 and LFAR1, engineered circRNA cRLN2, and piR-823 regulate HSC activation and ECM deposition. Furthermore, ncRNAs mediate bidirectional gut-liver crosstalk, linking microbial dysbiosis to hepatic inflammation and metabolism, with plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (e.g., BDENs) emerging as natural sources of bioactive miRNAs for therapeutic intervention.

From a diagnostic perspective, circulating ncRNAs, particularly those packaged into EVs, offer several advantages as non-invasive biomarkers. Their high stability in biofluids, tissue- and disease-stage-specific expression patterns, and detectability by standard PCR or sequencing platforms make them ideal candidates for liquid biopsy. Notably, miR-34a has already been incorporated into the clinically validated NIS4 panel for identifying at-risk MASH patients. However, broader clinical adoption requires standardized protocols for sample collection, normalization, and data analysis, as well as large-scale prospective validation studies.

Therapeutically, ncRNAs present both opportunities and challenges. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that modulating pathogenic ncRNAs using ASOs, synthetic mimics, or CRISPR-based approaches can ameliorate steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in MASLD models. The FDA approval of resmetirom for MASH with fibrosis marks a milestone, but ncRNA-based therapies remain in early stages. Key hurdles include the inherent instability of ncRNAs in circulation, off-target effects due to their pleiotropic regulatory networks, and the need for efficient and tissue-specific delivery systems. Advances in lipid nanoparticles, GalNAc conjugates, and engineered exosomes have improved liver-targeted delivery, but further optimization is required to achieve cell-type specificity and minimize toxicity.

ncRNAs have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of MASLD as a network-driven disease. Their dual role as both mechanistic drivers and accessible biomarkers positions them at the forefront of precision medicine efforts. While significant challenges remain, the rapid pace of discovery in ncRNA biology, coupled with advances in delivery technologies and computational analytics, offers a realistic path toward clinical translation.

Looking ahead, several research priorities could guide the field. First, the field should transition from reductionist studies of individual ncRNAs toward comprehensive mapping of the interconnected ncRNA regulatory networks underlying MASLD pathogenesis. Second, using multi-omics approaches will be essential to elucidate the dynamic interactions among ncRNAs, host genetics, and environmental factors. Third, integrating AI with multi-omics data holds promise for identifying patient-specific ncRNA signatures, predicting treatment responses, and stratifying patients for personalized interventions. Finally, continued innovation in delivery platforms and rigorous clinical trials will be critical to establish the safety, efficacy, and therapeutic windows of ncRNA-based therapies.
