Pilot Study: Hyaluronic Acid Fillers in the Management of Scars After Skin Cancer Surgery
Authors: Harini Bala, Nisal Punchihewa, Philip Bekhor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Scarring after skin cancer surgery, particularly facial surgery, poses significant aesthetic and psychological concerns for patients. Surgical revision, although effective, can be associated with increased morbidity and recovery time. Hyaluronic acid (HA) fillers offer a potential nonsurgical alternative for scar revision, but their use in post–skin cancer scar revision is not well documented. This retrospective pilot study aimed to evaluate the safety, effectiveness, and patient satisfaction of HA fillers for the revision of scars following skin cancer surgery.Methods:A retrospective, single-center study was conducted on 10 patients who underwent HA filler treatment for scar revision following skin cancer surgery. These patients had previously declined further surgical revision due to its associated morbidity. The treatment involved multiple sessions of high-viscosity HA fillers, with intervals of 3 months between sessions. Patients’ satisfaction was assessed using a Likert scale.Results:All patients reported high satisfaction, with 6 patients rating their outcome as 5 out of 5 and 4 patients rating their outcome as 4 out of 5. No complications, including vascular occlusion or infection, were observed during or after the treatment.Conclusions:This retrospective pilot study demonstrated the potential utility of high-viscosity HA fillers for scar revision following skin cancer surgery, with high patient satisfaction and no observed complications.

Takeaways

Question: Do hyaluronic acid fillers provide a safe and effective nonsurgical alternative for facial scar revision following skin cancer surgery?

Findings: This retrospective study of 10 patients demonstrated that high-viscosity hyaluronic acid fillers effectively improved postsurgical facial scars, with high patient satisfaction and no complications. Treatments were performed over multiple sessions at 3-month intervals, offering a minimally invasive and safe approach to scar revision.

Meaning: Hyaluronic acid fillers are a safe, minimally invasive, and highly effective option for facial scar revision after skin cancer surgery, providing an alternative for patients seeking to avoid further surgical interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Facial scarring is a significant problem following skin cancer surgery. In Australia, many patients of varying ages are diagnosed with both melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancers. Skin cancer is typically treated with standard excisional surgery or Mohs micrographic surgery.1 Unfortunately, cutaneous surgery, especially surgery associated with the head and neck, carries a significant risk of disfigurement. Therefore, assessment of treatment effectiveness must include not only successful clearance of the malignancy, but also take into equal consideration the patient’s perspective, scarring, and aesthetic outcomes.

Physical disfigurement or scarring poses significant morbidity for patients.2 Post skin cancer surgery, the degree of scarring varies, and studies have shown that more than 50% of patients have concerns regarding their scars.1,3,4 A study that examined patient satisfaction after facial skin cancer surgery reported that most participants did not want to be seen by others if their scar was visible and that most participants felt self-conscious about their appearance.1

Conventional modalities for scar revision include techniques such as CO2 laser resurfacing, microneedling, dermabrasion, and surgical scar revision, each with varying downtime and risk profiles. Hyaluronic acid (HA) filler has been used to manage scars, particularly depressed acne scars or atrophic scarring secondary to intralesional triamcinolone injection.5–9 HA improves scars by acting as a dermal filler to restore volume and contour in atrophic and depressed scars, promoting local collagen production, enhancing hydration by attracting and retaining moisture, and mechanically supporting scarred tissue to improve elasticity and reduce visible irregularities.10

However, its use in revising scars, especially after skin cancer surgery, has not been widely reported. Given the capacity of HA fillers to elevate tissues, it is possible to investigate their use in depressed and atrophic scarring, such as that seen following full-thickness skin grafts, split-thickness skin grafts, and excisions, in restoring contour and structure. It is relatively safe, does not require patients to undergo further revision surgery, which is subsequently associated with morbidity, and is an in-clinic treatment that can be cost-effective compared with procedures such as laser and reconstructive surgery. This study aimed to evaluate the safety, effectiveness, and patient satisfaction of high-viscosity HA fillers for the revision of scars resulting from skin cancer surgery, providing a minimally invasive alternative to surgical revision.

METHODS

This retrospective, single-center case series was conducted to evaluate the use of high-viscosity HA fillers for scar revision following skin cancer surgery. Patients included in this study had scars from skin cancer surgery resulting in significant contour deformities or atrophy and had opted against further surgical revision. Patients were excluded if they had hypersensitivity to HA fillers, significant vascular compromise, or active infections. Our method involved using decanted high-viscosity filler (Voluma) delivered via a 31G BD ultrafine 0.25-mL syringe. Injections were typically made intradermally; however, depending on the site, they can also be injected directly onto bone or cartilage. (See Video [online], which displays the intradermal injection of HA filler for postsurgical scar revision, demonstrating a microbolus technique with reduced pressure to minimize risk and optimize contour restoration.) Aspiration was performed at each injection point, and small aliquots of 0.01–0.02 mL were injected. Needle repositioning was conducted after each injection. We corrected each defect across an average of 2–3 sessions, with undercorrection at the initial session and gradual increases over subsequent sessions to avoid compression of dermal vessels. A persistent white appearance posttreatment would mandate hyaluronidase; however, we did not need to use hyaluronidase in any of the cases performed. We found that in tighter scars, the filler can be conceptually comparable to a tissue expander, allowing for more volume in subsequent sessions. Unlike tissue expanders, HA fillers offer subtle volumization without applying prolonged mechanical pressure that could further compromise the already limited vascular supply of scar tissue. Treatment satisfaction was evaluated subjectively through a patient-reported Likert scale survey. Safety outcomes were assessed by the treating dermatologist during each session and follow-up visit.

RESULTS

At a single center in Victoria, Australia, 10 patients (3 men and 7 women) underwent HA filler therapy for scar revision following skin cancer surgery. Patients’ ages ranged from 18 to 72 years, and all were referred as a last-line option for HA treatment. The number of treatments varied depending on the size and complexity of the scars, ranging from 1 to 7 sessions. Across the cohort, the mean number of sessions required was 2.7 ± 1.8. Each session was spaced 3 months apart. Patients completed follow-up evaluations to rate their satisfaction using a Likert scale. Six patients rated their outcomes as 5 out of 5 (100%), whereas 4 patients rated theirs as 4 out of 5 (80%). No complications, including vascular occlusion or infection, were observed due to the treatment.

DISCUSSION

Surgical revision after skin cancer surgery, particularly on the face, has important considerations for patients. Aside from the technical challenges, the time, cost, and morbidity associated with surgery are significant factors when considering surgical scar revision. Increased scar lines, the potential for further scarring, and extended healing times are also critical factors that often deter patients from undergoing revision surgery. Other modalities, such as CO2 lasers and dermabrasion, could also be considered; however, these treatments require considerable recovery periods, which patients often find unsuitable.

HA filler is one of the most performed cosmetic procedures. In the world of aesthetics, it is used to treat various concerns, including fine lines and wrinkles, restoration of facial shape, and augmentation of facial structures such as lips, nose, jaw, and chin. In the last decade, its use beyond cosmetic procedures has slowly been explored. There have been case reports of its use in managing atrophic scarring secondary to acne and steroid injections.5 There have also been reports of its use in autoimmune and inflammatory skin conditions such as morphea.11,12 The use of filler for scar revision following skin cancer surgery has not been widely reported and has been limited to case reports.13 In our case series, we found the use of HA for postsurgical scars to be an excellent consideration for patients not wanting to undergo reconstruction. It has been our anecdotal experience that once correction has been achieved, the benefits are long-lasting. Possible reasons for longevity include treatment of immobile areas, collagen growth stimulation, and splinting into correct alignment, which may result in persistent changes to the surrounding structures. Additionally, none of our patients experienced any complications as a result of the treatment.

Complications of HA fillers include early events such as ecchymosis; vascular occlusion; and infection; and later sequelae, such as granulomas or delayed inflammatory reactions.14 In the context of scar revision, altered perfusion and vascular distortion within scar tissue may theoretically influence both the risk and presentation of these complications. Although bruising is common, vascular occlusion remains uncommon, with reported rates of 1:2000 to 1:10,000, and it is less frequent with HA fillers compared with autologous fat transfer.15

Early signs of intravascular compromise include persistent blanching that fails to resolve with massage, as well as later livedo-pattern discoloration and pustule formation.16 Prompt recognition of subtle ischemia postinjection and treatment with hyaluronidase remain essential.

Certain anatomical sites including the nasolabial fold, periocular region, nose, and glabella require heightened caution due to their complex vascular anatomy. These contain anastomoses between branches of the external and internal carotid arteries, including the dorsal nasal, angular, supraorbital, and supratrochlear arteries.14,17 Inadvertent intravascular injection at these sites can result in retrograde flow and subsequent anterograde embolization into the ophthalmic artery, posing a risk of visual loss.17 To minimize such risks, consensus guidelines emphasize the use of slow, low-pressure injections, microboluses, and micromovements within a single plane, with a strong understanding of local anatomy and avoidance of high-risk vascular territories.17–19

Future studies should include larger cohorts with longer follow-up periods to assess the long-term durability of high-viscosity HA fillers for scar revision. Limitations of this pilot study include the small sample size and the absence of standardized follow-up to evaluate long-term results. Addressing these factors in future research would help provide more robust evidence on the efficacy and safety of this treatment. However, the anecdotal impression of the investigators is that the corrections are surprisingly long-lasting following completion of the correction as measured in years, likely relating to collagen stimulation or splinting of the tissues in a better position with subsequent fat and connective tissue remodeling.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings of this case series suggest that high-viscosity HA fillers may serve as a viable, minimally invasive option for the revision of scars resulting from skin cancer surgery. Using BD micro-syringes, small boluses of 0.01–0.02 mL injected intradermally have been shown to be a safe technique in surgical scar revision. The microbolus technique and reduced pressure of injections reduce the likelihood of intravascular occlusion and complications. Additionally, treatment was administered at 3-month intervals, reducing the volume of product administered and further contributing to safety. In this small cohort of 10 patients, responses were favorable, with minimal morbidity and high patient satisfaction. However, these preliminary findings require confirmation in larger studies with longer follow-up to better establish efficacy and safety.
