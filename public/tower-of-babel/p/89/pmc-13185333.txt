Feasibility and acceptability of a mixed methods within-subjects comparative design in dance movement therapy for adults with personality disorders
Authors: S T Kleinlooh, R A Samaritter, B W Koes, R Hoekstra, J H Stubbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDance Movement Therapy (DMT) is an established treatment for adults diagnosed with personality disorders (PD). A central component of DMT is the creation of improvised dance material, which supports emotional expression and may enhance autonomy, self-efficacy, and well-being. However, structured dance-based micro-interventions derived from this practice have not yet been systematically described or evaluated. This study was the first attempt to examine the feasibility of implementing a within-subjects comparative study protocol and the acceptability of a structured dance-based micro-intervention, with the aim of informing future controlled trials.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods feasibility study comparing two DMT conditions delivered within ongoing open clinical groups: (a) DMT as Usual (DMT-AU) and (b) a structured dance-based micro-intervention (DMT-MI). Primary outcomes concerned the feasibility of the study protocol, assessed via recruitment, enrolment, retention, adherence, and questionnaire completeness, without evaluating intervention effects. Secondary outcomes concerned the acceptability of the DMT-MI, assessed through session ratings and semi-structured interviews with patients and dance movement therapists.ResultsAll feasibility indicators suggested that the comparative study protocol could be implemented in a real-world clinical setting and that the DMT-MI intervention was acceptable to both patients and therapists. Of the 27 eligible patients, 20 consented to participate (74.1%). Of these included patients19 completed the study (95.0%). Eighteen participants (90.0%) attended both intervention sessions, and 19 (95.0%) participated in the DMT-MI session. Questionnaire return was 100%, with 96% item-level completeness. Semi-structured interviews were completed with all patients (19/19) and both therapists (2/2).ConclusionsA within-subject comparative study protocol and structured DMT micro-intervention were feasible and acceptable when implemented within ongoing DMT group treatment for adults diagnosed with PD in a clinical setting. These findings can inform the design of future controlled trials. Future studies should address recruitment challenges arising from slow and unpredictable patient flow in long-term treatment programs and consider the high diagnostic heterogeneity and comorbidity within PD populations when planning sample sizes and study designs.Keywords: Dance movement therapy, Personality disorders, Complex interventions, Feasibility, Acceptability, Comparative design

Background

Individuals diagnosed with a personality disorder (PD) suffer from enduring inflexible patterns in their cognitions and emotions, affecting both their self-functioning and interpersonal functioning [2]. PDs are grouped into three clusters (5th ed.,DSM − 5-TR; [2]. Cluster A: Paranoid, schizoid, and schizotypal PD,Cluster B: Antisocial, borderline, histrionic, and narcissistic PD; Cluster C: Avoidant, dependent, and obsessive–compulsive PD. Other specified PD and unspecified PD are comparable to PD not otherwise specified (PD-NOS), commonly used in clinical practice [20, 28, 39]. PDs are internationally recognized as a mental priority [42]. According to the WHO World Mental Health Surveys, prevalence of PD in cross-national general populations across 13 countries is 6.1% for any PD and 3.6%, 1.5%, and 2.7% for Clusters A, B, and C, respectively [19]. In the Netherlands, among a population of 17.942.042 people in 2023, 1.4% (252.800) with PD were identified by general practitioners [31]. In Dutch mental health care, 30 to 50% of patients comply with the diagnosis of PD [1].

Dance movement therapy (DMT), one of the creative arts therapies (CAT’s), is recognised as an established treatment modality for individuals with PD in both the United Kingdom [21] and the Netherlands [1]. A systematic review of DMT for PD indicated that interventions predominantly involve body-oriented activities [23]. However, a survey among dance movement therapists working with PD populations demonstrated that dance-related activities are also frequently used in clinical practice [24]. The inclusion of dance-related activities distinguishes DMT from other body-oriented psychotherapies, such as psychomotor therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, and sensory-motor approaches, and contributes to DMT's distinctive professional identity [30, 35, 40]. To date, however, no clinical studies have examined the efficacy of DMT interventions for individuals with PD [23, 24]. There is an increasing demand for empirical research on action-based therapeutic approaches, including CATs, and on the applicability and delivery of specific therapeutic interventions [10]. The CAT’s are widely regarded as complex interventions, particularly when applied to populations with complex clinical needs such as PD. Multiple contextual factors influence how intervention components are delivered, experienced, and evaluated [22]. As a result, early-phase evaluation of feasibility and acceptability is strongly recommended during the initial stages of study and intervention development to identify methodological challenges and ensure that proposed designs are implementable in real-world clinical settings [5, 6, 8, 13, 34, 37]. Accordingly, the present study examined the feasibility of implementing a within-subjects comparative study protocol and the acceptability of a structured dance-based micro-intervention delivered within routine clinical DMT sessions for individuals with PD, as preliminary work to inform future efficacy trials. For this purpose, a dance-based DMT micro-intervention (DMT-MI), “the client as a choreographer,” was embedded within the usual DMT treatment programme. Micro-interventions are brief segments of a therapy session, thereby limiting the number of co-occurring variables. This approach can be chosen as it enables detailed micro-analysis of specific intervention components and potential mechanisms of change, which becomes increasingly complex when interventions are examined over extended timeframes [11], [18].

Objectives

The primary aim of this study was to assess the feasibility of implementing a within-subjects comparative study protocol within ongoing open clinical groups. The secondary aim was to assess the acceptability of a structured, dance-based micro-intervention to inform the design of future controlled trials. These aims were addressed by comparing two Dance Movement Therapy (DMT) conditions: (a) Dance Movement Therapy as Usual (DMT-AU) and (b) a structured, dance-based DMT micro-intervention (DMT-MI), both delivered within ongoing open DMT groups in a clinical setting. Feasibility was evaluated using predefined quantitative indicators, including recruitment, enrolment, retention, adherence, and questionnaire completeness. Acceptability of the DMT-MI was assessed using questionnaires and qualitative data obtained through semi-structured interviews with patients and dance movement therapists.

Methods

Study design

A mixed-methods approach (see Fig. 1) was used [7, 16], combining pre- and post-session within-subjects data collection with quantitative feasibility indicators, quantitative questionnaire data, and qualitative interviews to explore the experiences of patients and dance movement therapists. Evaluation of intervention effects was beyond the scope of this study. All participating patients received two DMT conditions in ongoing open DMT groups, enabling a within-subjects comparison in real-world clinical settings. During the DMT-AU session, the dance movement therapists followed their own objectives, whereas in the DMT-MI session, a series of specific steps was followed, inviting patients to create their own dance. Composing one’s dance can be seen as an aesthetic activity that enhances the client’s identity, sense of autonomy, self-efficacy beliefs, and feelings of self-control and well-being [10, 25, 26, 32]. The DMT-MI was described in a transferable and systematic way with a clear, consistent methodological line [33] and was based on two previous studies on DMT and PD [23, 24]. As the study was conducted within the usual treatment program, all consenting patients received both DMT conditions. Before and after both conditions, patients completed two questionnaires and rated both sessions immediately afterward. After participating in both DMT conditions, one-on-one interviews with the patients were conducted. Interviews were conducted with the participating dance movement therapists after they had delivered both conditions to all patients.

Baseline characteristics

Secondary parameters (baseline characteristics) were collected for additional information. They consisted of a) the diagnostic information of the clients, collected via the electronic client files by the dance movement therapists; b) the demographics of the patients (age, gender, nationality, client treatment duration; and c) the clinic’s overall therapeutical orientation, either mentalization based treatment (MBT) or schema-focused therapy (SFT), collected by the participating dance movement therapists and, d) the demographics of the dance movement therapists (age, gender, nationality, educational background, professional experience, and the clinical psychotherapeutic treatment context) were collected by the principal investigator.

Questionnaires

Quantitative data were collected to examine score distributions, variability, and the questionnaires' suitability for use in future controlled studies. The following measurement tools were used:

The session rating scale is a validated questionnaire designed for everyday clinical use and evaluating single clinical sessions (SRS; [12, 17]. This study explicitly used the SRS to assess clients’ experiences with DMT-AU and the DMT-MI. Patients were asked to rate their relationship with the therapist, whether the intended treatment goals were considered, whether the approach/method met their expectations, and the overall experience after both sessions.

Data analysis

The recruitment rate was calculated by the ratio (%) between the total number of recruited and eligible clients. The retention rate was calculated by the ratio (%) between the total number of patients who finished two sessions (DMT-AU and DMT-MI) and the total number of patients who enrolled in the study. The adherence rate was calculated by the ratio (%) between the total number of patients who participated during the DMT-MI and those who enrolled in the DMT-MI. The proportion of completed questionnaires was calculated by the ratio (%) between the total number of handed-out questionnaires per client and the total number of completed questionnaires per client. The willingness to participate in the semi-structured interviews was calculated by the ratio (%) between the total number of invited interviewees and the total number of successfully planned interviews.

Setting

This study was conducted in a Dutch outpatient hosting a PD programme within a clinic for bipolar disorders. Patient enrollment takes place through referral by general or mental health practitioners. Patients enter a waiting period or start treatment immediately after receiving an intake interview, a neuropsychological investigation, and meeting the criteria for PD according to the clinic’s diagnostic system, the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Personality Disorders (SCID-5-PD, [15]. Once in treatment, patients follow a one-year program of individual and group therapy within a specific clinical psychotherapeutic context, either mentalization-based treatment (MBT) [4] or schema-focused therapy (SFT) [46]. During the 4-day multidisciplinary treatment program, patients receive individual and group psychotherapy, visual art therapy, music therapy, and DMT. DMT sessions are offered weekly by a dance movement therapist specialized in either MBT or SFT.

Participants

To be eligible to participate in the study, the patients needed to meet the following criteria:

a) being diagnosed with a PD by the clinics’ diagnosis policy, b) receiving DMT, c) having signed the informed consent form. A client would be excluded from the study if unable to speak and read Dutch. The dance movement therapists offered the patients two DMT sessions.

Patient and public involvement

Clinicians and two dance movement therapists from the Institution contributed to recruitment strategies and supported communication regarding the study's organization with the client-participants.

Recruitment of the patients

The primary practitioners recruited the patients four weeks before the study at the clinic. All patients received the following information: a) a flyer with general information about the study, b) an invitation letter from the principal investigator with the purpose of the study, c) an explanation of the study procedures, d) an informed consent form, and e) the de-briefing procedure stating that the results of the study would be shared through open access publication. Patients could decide within two weeks whether to participate. If they had any questions, they could contact the primary practitioners.

Recruitment of dance movement therapists

The principal investigator purposively recruited the two dance movement therapists from the clinic for their expertise in DMT for PD clients. Before the study, the dance movement therapists received a half-day training on delivering the DMT-MI as designed by the principal investigator, and the following information: a) an invitation with an explanation of the purpose of the study, b) an explanation of the study procedures and handling of specific data, and c) an informed consent form. The principal investigator handled all information and communication.

Study procedures and data storage

Patients received both conditions, the DMT-AU and the DMT-MI. Only the patients who gave consent were included in the study procedures. The burden of participation consisted of filling in two short questionnaires before and three after both sessions. Filling out the questionnaires was estimated to take 10–15 min each time. Within two weeks after receiving the DMT-MI, patients were invited for a30-minute individual semi-structured interview with the principal investigator regarding the acceptability of the DMT-MI. The dance movement therapists were invited for a 45-min semi-structured interview. To protect the privacy of the included patients for the principal investigator, all patients received a unique participant’s code from the primary practitioners and were only known by the primary practitioners, the clients, and the dance movement therapists. The codes were used for all study documentation and reports. Two test days were planned for the MBT and SFT departments. Patients were asked to fill out the questionnaires and drop the envelopes with the questionnaires marked with the participants' unique code off into a post box. This box was sealed and stored at the clinic, after which all data was processed and stored on a secured server by the principal investigator. Only the principal investigator and the data manager could access the coded data. Appointments for client interviews were scheduled with individual patients according to their agendas and when they were at the clinic, to avoid extra time and cost. Planning the interviews with the dance movement therapists was organized in the same manner. All interviews were conducted in a room with one table, two chairs, and a recording device.

The DMT sessions

DMT group sessions were delivered within an ongoing open DMT group of 6–12 clients. In line with routine clinical practice, each DMT session lasted 75 min and consisted of a) a check-in followed by b) a short movement warm-up to develop clients’ readiness to move, c) a middle part in which the DMT session activities were offered, and d) a closure in which patients were invited to share their experiences with group members. The first DMT session consisted of the DTM-AU; in the second, the DMT-MI, ‘the client as a choreographer,’ was offered.

The DMT micro-intervention

The design and structure of the DMT-MI group session are based on findings from two previous studies on DMT and PD [23, 24]. The DMT-MI is delivered during the middle phase of the session and centres on clients creating their own choreography. The dance-movement therapist provides a clear, structured, and sequential framework to guide clients step by step through the choreographic process. Clients first explore and select three to five meaningful movements from earlier DMT sessions (5 min), then independently organize these movements into a short choreography with a clear beginning and ending, supported by dance elements and imagery (10 min). The choreography is then repeated and memorised (10 min). Afterward, clients teach their choreography to a group member, verbally and physically explaining movement qualities, imagery, and the use of space, time, and weight (15–20 min per client).

The DMT as usual session

The DMT-AU group sessions are delivered within an ongoing open DMT group of 6–12 clients. In line with routine clinical practice, each DMT session lasts 75 min. Sessions follow a standard structure consisting of: (a) an introduction phase (10 min), during which clients are welcomed and engage in a brief movement warm-up to support readiness for movement; (b) a central working phase (60 min), during which either DMT-AU interventions or the DMT micro-intervention (DMT-MI) are provided, depending on group allocation; and (c) a closure phase (5 min), in which clients are invited to reflect on their experience using a single word, gesture, or short sentence.

Training the DMT micro-intervention

To deliver the dance-informed DMT-MI in a structured, sequential manner, guiding patients through their choreographic process step by step, a half-day training was organized for the dance movement therapists. Two volunteers were asked to participate in the training, where dance movement therapists could practice implementing the micro-intervention under the supervision of the principal investigator. All involved had time for exploration, repetition, reflection, discussion, and feedback. The dance movement therapists received a booklet with all study procedures, a description of the micro-intervention, and a certificate of attendance, as the training was accredited by the Federatie voor Vaktherapeutische Beroepen [14]. All participants received a gift voucher for their efforts after the training.

Diagnostic data

Diagnostic information was extracted from the electronic client records by the participating dance movement therapists in accordance with the study protocol. All required diagnostic data were obtained without difficulty. Participants were diagnosed with PD’s primarily within cluster B (borderline personality disorder) and cluster C (avoidant personality disorder and obsessive–compulsive personality disorder). No participants were diagnosed with cluster A personality disorders. Overall, substantial heterogeneity in diagnoses and comorbid conditions was observed, with no two participants presenting identical diagnostic profiles. Eighteen of the nineteen participants were diagnosed with at least one comorbid condition, including post-traumatic stress disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, mood disorders, and eating disorders. In addition, 12 of the 19 participants received a diagnosis of personality disorder not otherwise specified (PD-NOS).

Demographics of patients

All demographic data were obtained from the electronic client records by the participating dance movement therapists in accordance with the study protocol, and no difficulties were encountered during data extraction. Of the 19 participants, the majority were female (17/19; 89.5%), with one male (1/19; 5.3%) and one participant identifying as non-binary (1/19; 5.3%). Participants ranged in age from 22 to 57 years. Most participants were Dutch (15/19; 78.9%), with additional nationalities including Turkish, Cape Verdean, Aruban/Dutch, and Italian (each 1/19; 5.3%). At the time of the study assessments, participants had been in treatment for 3–12 months, with the largest proportion receiving treatment for 9–11 months (8/19; 42.1%). Participants were recruited from two psychotherapeutic treatment programs: schema-focused therapy (SFT; 10/19; 52.6%) and mentalization-based therapy (MBT; 9/19; 47.4%).

Demographics of dance movement therapists

Both dance movement therapists have a master’s degree in DMT. At the time of the study, the male dance movement therapist (59) was affiliated with the MBT department and had 15 years of experience treating patients with PD. The female dance movement therapist (58) was affiliated with the SFT department and had 12 years of experience working with PD patients.

Study procedures

All included patients completed the questionnaires immediately before and after the DMT sessions between other treatment sessions in their program. Questionnaires were administered in rooms near the DMT studios reserved for participating patients during data collection, allowing assessments to be completed without disrupting scheduled treatment activities. No logistical or procedural barriers occurred during the delivery of either the DMT-AU or DMT-MI sessions within the clinic’s treatment program, and all planned semi-structured interviews were completed.

Discussion

Methodological considerations

This study assessed the feasibility of implementing a within-subjects comparative study protocol within ongoing open clinical groups and the acceptability of a structured, dance-based micro-intervention to inform the design of future controlled trials.

The findings demonstrate that the comparative protocol is feasible in a real-world setting, despite unpredictable factors such as treatment admissions, dropouts, discharges, and varying therapist availability. High recruitment, retention, and adherence rates, along with stable questionnaire completion, indicate that the protocol's methodological components can be successfully implemented and support its suitability for future effectiveness trials in similar clinical settings. The acceptability of the DMT-MI was explored through qualitative semi-structured interviews with both patients and dance movement therapists. Future studies may build on these findings by further examining patient and therapist perspectives and by integrating quantitative questionnaire data with qualitative insights to better understand the possible impact of DMT interventions on emotion regulation and self-efficacy.

Small sample size

Although power-based sample size calculations were not required for this feasibility study, the observed recruitment dynamics provide important information for planning future trials.

When preparing the study, the clinics’ maximum capacity for client treatment admissions was estimated at around 40–50 clients, with 8–10 patients per group. As the median sample size for feasibility and pilot studies in behavioral sciences is 30 participants per arm [5], the estimated sample size seemed promising for starting the recruitment and for future trials. However, when the recruitment and the test weeks were planned, only 27 patients were in treatment. Despite the high recruitment rate, a sample size of 20 participants would be insufficient for a future trial—postponing the recruitment and test weeks until clients' maximum treatment admission capacity was not an option, as it became evident that client enrollments, treatment dropouts, and discharges could not be easily predicted or guaranteed. Moreover, as the client's treatment duration is 12 months, the client flow is slow. Considering these factors, the clinic, the primary practitioners, and the principal investigator chose a timeframe in which all involved practitioners were available to recruit the participants and deliver the micro-intervention. Based on the findings, it is recommended that future studies consider alternative strategies to address the challenges of low and unpredictable client enrollment. This may include expanding the recruitment period to allow patients to enroll, conducting the study in a clinical setting with a guaranteed larger number of patient treatment admissions, or including ambulant patients. A second alternative could be to repeat the same study several times until the necessary sample size is reached. If the treatment concerns a 12-month open setting, the timing of testing within each client's treatment will vary, with assessments occurring at different points in their treatment journey (e.g., after three months, six months, or between 10–12 months). This would challenge the comparison of the outcome measures between clients. Third, collaborating with multiple clinics to increase the sample size would lead to more contextual variables, demanding enough financial, academic, and human resources to organize and execute the studies.

High heterogeneity in PD

The results show high heterogeneity in PD diagnosis and comorbidity with other mental disorders and PDs. Not one client's PD diagnosis was identical to another, and most patients were diagnosed with at least one co-occurring condition, which aligns with findings that PD is highly comorbid with other mental disorders or another PD [9, 19, 39]. The high number of assigned PD-NOS diagnoses aligns with research stating that ‘PD-NOS’ is commonly used in clinical practice [27, 39]. In effectiveness trials, intervention groups are preferred to consist of participants with one set of diagnoses. This may explain the high number of studies with insufficient power and the smaller number of such studies in the CAT’s field. [22]. Given the high heterogeneity and comorbidity in PD, as observed in this study, designing efficacy studies for PD is complex. Future research could focus on a single PD category, which seems almost impossible according to PD literature, as co-morbidity with another mental disorder and PD diagnosis is high. A second option is to investigate large sample sizes of PD patients to create subsamples with identical single PD diagnoses. Considering the high complexity of PD nosology [3, 27, 29, 38, 45] and the need for more studies and efficacy trials in the CAT’s for PD, a strong recommendation is to focus on the general characteristics of PD, for example, intra-and interpersonal distress [2, 43] and treatment foci as self-regulation and self -efficacy also referred to in previous studies on DMT and PD [23, 24].

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is that it evaluated the feasibility of implementing a within-subject comparative study protocol in a real-world clinical setting, embedded within ongoing open treatment groups for patients with PD. Conducting the study within routine clinical care allowed the protocol to be tested under realistic conditions characterized by fluctuating treatment admissions, discharges, and therapist availability. Despite these practical challenges, the study achieved high recruitment, retention, adherence, and questionnaire completion rates, indicating that the protocol's methodological components were feasible to implement. In addition, the inclusion of qualitative semi-structured interviews with both patients and dance movement therapists provided valuable insights into the acceptability of the DMT-MI and helped contextualize the feasibility findings.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, the sample size was small, which is typical for feasibility studies but limits the ability to draw conclusions regarding intervention effectiveness. The study was not powered to detect clinical effects and therefore primarily provides information on recruitment dynamics, implementation procedures, and data collection feasibility. Recruitment was also influenced by the limited and fluctuating number of patients in treatment at the clinic during the study period, which constrained the achievable sample size. Future studies may consider extending the recruitment period, recruiting from multiple clinical sites, or including outpatient populations to increase sample sizes.

Second, the clinical sample was highly heterogeneous, with participants presenting with different PD diagnoses and multiple comorbid psychiatric conditions. Such heterogeneity reflects the complexity of real-world clinical populations but may introduce variability in intervention responses and outcome measures. Designing controlled trials for PD is therefore challenging, particularly given the high prevalence of comorbid conditions and the frequent use of PD-NOS diagnoses in clinical practice.

Third, the study was conducted in a single specialized treatment centre, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other clinical contexts or treatment settings.

Taken together, these findings indicate that while the study protocol is feasible to implement in routine clinical care, future research should involve larger samples, potentially multi-site recruitment, and controlled study designs to evaluate the effectiveness of DMT micro-interventions for individuals with PD.

Conclusion

This study explored the feasibility of implementing a study protocol comparing DMT-as-usual and a DMT micro-intervention within an ongoing clinical group setting.

The findings indicate that the study procedures were feasible to implement within routine clinical care and were acceptable to participants and therapists. The study also identified several methodological considerations that may inform the design of future controlled trials of DMT interventions for individuals diagnosed with PD. In particular, future studies should address challenges related to sample size, the heterogeneity of PD diagnoses, and the high prevalence of comorbid mental disorders.

They could withdraw from the study at any time, for any reason, without consequences. The primary practitioners could withdraw a client from the study for urgent medical reasons. If patients withdrew themselves or were withdrawn from the study, data were not used and were destroyed.
