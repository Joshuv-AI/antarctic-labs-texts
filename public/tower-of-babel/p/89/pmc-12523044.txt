Predictors, trends and outcomes associated with urinary tract infections in intracerebral hemorrhage: insights from a National Inpatient Sample Study (2010–2022)
Authors: Binbin Tian, Chunbo Chen, Junfen Cheng, Jian Wang, Junde Mo, Guorong Zhong, Qiongru Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrinary tract infections (UTIs) are an important concomitant condition among patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). Data regarding predictors, outcomes and trends relating to UTIs in ICH are lacking.MethodsA retrospective analysis of data from the Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS) for the years January 1, 2010–December 31, 2022 was conducted, employing multivariable logistic regression to examine the associated factors and temporal trends of UTIs in patients with ICH.ResultsBetween 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2022, the NIS database contained an unweighted total of 311,581 hospitalizations (weighted estimate, 1,554,702) with ICH coded as either a primary or secondary diagnosis. Among these, 42,271 hospitalizations were combined with UTIs. Our analysis revealed a significant down-trend in the occurrence of UTIs among patients with ICH over the 12-year period, resulting in an overall occurrence rate of 13.6%. The identified associated factors for UTIs included advanced age (≥ 45), female individuals, Black individuals, Hispanic individuals, comorbidities (≥ 1), Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) ≥ 3, medium/large hospital and treatment in South regional hospital. Significant comorbidities that increased the odds of UTIs included deficiency anemia, depression, diabetes (with complications), congestive heart failure, fluid and electrolyte disorders, paralysis, psychoses, peptic ulcer disease excluding bleeding and weight loss. Patients who developed UTIs were associated with worse clinical outcomes, including more frequent conditions (pressure ulcer, pneumonia, transfusion, acute renal failure, deep vein thrombosis, delirium, malnutrition, sepsis), significantly longer hospitalization periods (median 10 vs. 5 days), and substantially increased healthcare expenditures (median $100,923 vs. $62,928). Interestingly, patients with UTIs were associated with lower in-hospital mortality (13.0% vs. 22.9%) (P < 0.001).ConclusionsThere is an association between UTIs and increased healthcare utilization in the setting of ICH. This study revealed several predictors/associated factors of UTIs occurrence with ICH among a nationally representative sample in the United States. These data may assist physicians and healthcare providers in identifying patients at increased risk for UTIs in ICH and, hence, initiate timely interventions.Keywords: Urinary tract infections, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Associated factors, Nationwide inpatient sample

Background

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), a devastating stroke subtype caused by cerebrovascular rupture and subsequent intraparenchymal blood extravasation, induces profound neurological deficits [1, 2]. Post-ICH immunosuppression, driven by hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activation and sympathetic nervous system hyperactivity, significantly compromises innate and adaptive immune responses, thereby amplifying susceptibility to nosocomial infections [3–9]. Among these, urinary tract infections (UTIs) represent a frequent complication in ICH, with reported incidence rates of 15.1–26.1%, and are independently linked to various adverse outcomes [10, 11].

UTIs considerably raise hospital readmission rates and lengthen stays, imposing billions in annual costs and straining healthcare resources [12, 13]. Moreover, these infections elevate the risk of organ dysfunction and are linked with serious clinical conditions, such as pyelonephritis, urosepsis, shock, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [11, 14, 15]. Evidence suggests that up to two-thirds of catheter-associated UTIs are preventable through targeted interventions [16]. However, studies on the risk factors for UTIs in patients with ICH are sparse, with a notable absence of large-scale, multicenter research.

In this study, we employed a comprehensive, nationally representative data set to assess the associated factors for UTIs among ICH patients.

Methods

Data source

The study utilized data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS), a component of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project funded by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. This database is the largest inpatient care repository in the United States, capturing approximately 20% of annual hospital admissions from over 1000 medical centers [17–19]. Consequently, the NIS incorporates both trend and discharge weighting parameters to adjust for hospital clustering effects and generate representative national estimates [20].The data set includes detailed patient information, such as demographics, institutional parameters, treatment outcomes, and financial metrics, coded according to the International Classification of Diseases standards. Institutional Review Board approval was not required this study used anonymized, publicly accessible data.

Data collection

The research spanned the period from January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2022. Hospitalizations of ICH were detected using diagnostic codes from both the ICD-9-CM (431) and ICD-10-CM (I61.0–I61.6, I61.8, I61.9) classification systems, excluding hospitalizations related to arteriovenous malformation and traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage. The study cohort encompassed hospitalizations, where ICH was documented either as the primary diagnosis or as a secondary diagnosis. UTIs were detected using diagnostic codes from both primary and secondary diagnosis , including ICD-9 codes (590, 590.1, 590.2, 590.3, 590.9, 595, 595.0, 595.3, 595.4, 595.8, 595.9, 597.0, 597.8, 599.0, 997.5) and ICD-10 codes (N39.0, N30.0, N30.00, N30.01, N303, N30.30, N30.31, N308, N30.80, N30.81, N30.9, N30.90, N30.91, N34, N34.0, N34.1, N34.2, N34.3, T83.021, T83.021A, T83.021D, T83.021S). Participants missing value and under 18 years of age were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Inclusion/exclusion process of UTIs in ICH patients

For subsequent analyses, hospitalizations were stratified based on the presence of UTIs diagnosis codes. Admissions with at least one UTI code documented in either the primary or secondary diagnostic fields were categorized as the UTI group, whereas those without such codes were categorized as the non-UTI group. The analysis included various variables: patient factors (age, gender, race); hospital characteristics (facility size, teaching status, hospital location); Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI); comorbidities, associated conditions and clinical metrics (hospitalization duration, total charge). Comorbidities and associated conditions were documented through specific diagnostic categories (Table 1).

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 was used for all analyses. Continuous variables were compared using independent t tests, and categorical variables were analyzed with chi-square tests. Temporal trends in UTIs occurrence from 2010 to 2022 were further evaluated using logistic regression with calendar year as a continuous independent variable. To identify factors associated with UTIs among ICH hospitalizations, we performed multivariable logistic regression analyses that incorporated patient demographics, hospital characteristics, comorbidities, and associated factors (Table 1). For each comorbidity and associated conditions, the odds of UTIs were explicitly compared between patients with and without the condition, adjusting for relevant covariates, thereby directly assessing whether these comorbidities or associated conditions were correlated with increased UTIs odds. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.001 given the large sample size [21].

Results

Temporal trend of UTIs in patients with ICH

Between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2022, the NIS database identified an unweighted total of 311,581 hospitalizations for ICH, representing a weighted national estimate of 1,554,702 (95%CI1,554,544–1,554,860) hospitalizations. UTIs were identified in 42,271 hospitalizations, representing an overall occurrence of 13.6% (Table 2). The occurrence declined from 16.4% in 2010 to 12% in 2022 (Fig. 2). Logistic regression using calendar year as a continuous variable confirmed a significant downward trend (B = −0.033, OR = 0.968, 95% CI 0.965–0.970, P < 0.001).

Temporal trends of UTIs in patients with ICH

Patient and hospital characteristics across the two cohorts

Demographic analyses revealed distinct age-related patterns across UTIs cohorts. Patients with UTIs demonstrated an older median age (73 years) than those without UTIs (68 years). Notably, the UTI group contained a substantially greater proportion of individuals aged ≥ 75 years (45.3% vs. 35.1%; P < 0.001). Sex-based stratification showed pronounced disparities, with females accounting for 65.1% of UTIs hospitalizations compared to 45.1% in the non-UTIs cohort (P < 0.001). Clinical metrics further differentiated the groups: 84.9% of UTI patients presented with CCI ≥ 3 versus 78.3% of non-UTIs (P < 0.001) (Table 2). Larger hospitals exhibited marginally higher UTI occurrence (68.7% vs. 67.5%), while geographic evaluations revealed elevated occurrence in South (41.6% vs. 39.4%) (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Clinical outcomes of UTIs in ICH

Patients with UTIs experienced a longer median length of stay (LOS) than those without UTIs (10 days vs. 5 days, P < 0.001) (Table 2). Consequently, UTIs were associated with increased healthcare costs, with hospital charges rising by a median of $37, 995 and total costs reaching $100,923, compared to $62,928 for patients without UTIs (P < 0.001). Despite these resource burdens, the in-hospital mortality was paradoxically lower in UTI patients (13.0% vs. 22.9%, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Multivariate analysis identified several significant predictors for UTIs, including older age (≥ 45 years), female individuals, Black individuals, Hispanic individuals, comorbidities ≥ 1, CCI ≥ 3, medium/large hospital and the South regional hospital (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Principal findings

This nationwide, multicenter health-economic study analyzed 311,581 unweighted ICH hospitalizations from 2010 to 2022 to evaluate UTIs occurrence, associated factors, and their adverse clinical outcomes and healthcare resource utilization. The analysis revealed significant disparities in patient demographics, hospital characteristics, comorbidities, and associated conditions between ICH hospitalizations accompanied by UTIs and those without UTIs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale, multicenter study examining the occurrence and associated factors of UTIs in patients with ICH.

Temporal trend

In our analysis, UTIs were diagnosed in 13.6% of hospitalizations, slightly lower than the previously reported rates of 15.1–26.1% [10]. Variability in UTIs incidence across studies may result from differences in follow-up duration, sample size, publication year, regional variations, UTI definitions (e.g., symptomatic vs. asymptomatic), and clinical practices [22, 23]. Previous studies had small sample sizes with limited evidence, whereas this study employed a sufficiently large sample to provide robust findings. Between 2010 and 2022, the occurrence of UTIs among patients with ICH demonstrated a significant decline, declining from 16.4% to 12.0%. Magill et al. indicated that healthcare-associated infections can be prevented through evidence-based interventions [24]. Additional prospective studies are necessary to substantiate these findings in the future.

Outcome

Although recent UTI research has expanded, few studies have examined its impact on population-level outcomes. The observation that UTIs were associated with lower mortality is likely influenced by survivor bias, unmeasured confounding, and potential clinical interventions [25, 26]. In addition, coding practices may capture less severe cases, influencing mortality rates. Future prospective studies using time-dependent models are essential to clarify the true relationship. Prior research reported that UTIs in ICH patients prolong hospital stays, increase healthcare costs, creating significant economic and psychological burdens on patients, families, and society [10]. They are also associated with some clinical conditions, including pneumonia, seizures, deep vein thrombosis, infection, and hydrocephalus [27]. Taken together, early identification of patients at risk for UTIs is essential for timely supportive care.

Predictors/associated factors

Elderly age is a risk factor for UTIs in ICH patients in our findings, likely associated with age-related immune decline, increased comorbidities, urinary retention, incontinence, frequent catheterization, frailty, and cognitive impairments, such as dementia [22]. Concordant with existing literature, our data identified that female sex as a significant predictor of UTIs, which may be linked to established anatomical and physiological vulnerabilities: shorter urethral length, proximity of the urethral meatus to the anus, and postmenopausal estrogen deficiency altering vaginal microbiota and mucosal immunity [22, 28].

Our results also revealed higher UTI rates in medium and large hospitals, consistent with Cao et al.’s observations. This finding possibly reflecting the larger patient populations in large hospital, increased complexity of medical conditions, and heightened reliance on urinary catheterization [29, 30]. Notably, the association between hospitals in the southern U.S. and a higher odd of UTIs remains unclear but is likely influenced by multiple factors, including antibiotic resistance, variations in healthcare access, socioeconomic factors, and regional differences in hospital practices [31, 32]. Moreover, Black/Hispanic race were associated with UTIs, potentially reflecting systemic disparities in healthcare access, socioeconomic barriers [33]. Cao et al. findings indicated that the association is not absolute, as UTIs is influenced by multiple factors, including hygiene, sexual activity, and comorbidities [30].

Our analysis demonstrated a significant correlation between the presence of multiple comorbidities and elevated rates of UTIs. This finding aligns with previous research by Chen and colleagues, which established that specific underlying conditions—particularly diabetes mellitus and dementia increase UTIs susceptibility [14, 34]. Diabetes mellitus was associated with an increased risk of UTIs, possibly driven by factors, such as chronic hyperglycemia, impaired leukocyte function, recurrent infections, and abnormalities in urinary tract anatomy and function [35, 36]. Zhu et al. conducted a multicenter study across 25 hospitals in China to investigate the prevalence, incidence, and risk factors of UTIs among immobile patients. They identified several independent risk factors, including advanced age, female sex, diabetes mellitus, prolonged hospitalization, treatment in medical wards, indwelling urethral catheterization, extended catheter use, glucocorticoid therapy, and the presence of an indwelling catheter. However, the findings are limited to the specific population of immobile inpatients [37].Research by Lin et al. showed significant connections between UTI frequency and several conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, coronary disease, elevated lipids, kidney dysfunction and depression [38]. In a similar investigation, Bausch and colleagues determined that compromised general health status, elevated comorbidity levels, concurrent immune deficiency, and advanced renal impairment requiring dialysis were linked to higher risk of UTIs following surgical procedures [39]. Previous studies were limited by small sample sizes and insufficient evidence, whereas our large sample size enhances the reliability and generalizability of these findings. These observations emphasize the importance of implementing effective preventive strategies to decrease UTIs incidence in vulnerable populations. Cao et al. demonstrated that proactive patient counseling reduces UTIs incidence in high-risk comorbidity groups [30].

Our investigation identified conditions associated with UTIs among ICH patients. Work by Jitpratoom et al. found a significantly higher incidence of post-stroke complications in UTI patients, including pneumonia, respiratory failure, sepsis, cerebral edema, seizures, hemorrhagic transformation, congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, acute kidney injury, and hyponatremia [40]. According to Zhao and colleagues, the probability of developing UTIs within 90 days after bladder cancer urostomy correlates with Nutritional Prognostic Index values, kidney filtration capacity (eGFR), and blood cell transfusions during surgery [41]. In addition, UTIs patient face increased risks of systemic infection and lung vessel blockage [42]. Prior research has been hampered by limited participant numbers, insufficient evidence base, and partial clinical conditions analysis. A particular advantage of our research was leveraging the NIS database to assess a large cohort of ICH patients while incorporating medical conditions into our multivariate logistic regression models.

Clinical and research implications

This study identifies high-risk subgroups for UTIs in ICH patients, such as female, older age, and diabetes, depression, psychoses etc., it is recommended to proactive patient counseling. Our analysis, leveraging the NIS database and multivariate logistic regression, links UTIs to associated clinical conditions (e.g., sepsis and acute renal failure), underscoring the need for vigilant monitoring. Integrating these evidence-based strategies may reduce UTIs incidence and enhance outcomes in post-ICH care. While observational, these findings provide a framework for future prospective studies.

Limitation

While leveraging the NIS database enabled large-scale analysis of UTIs in ICH patients, the reliance on administrative coding and the study’s retrospective design introduces limitations. Coding inaccuracies—such as misclassification of asymptomatic bacteriuria or failure to detect catheter-associated UTIs (CAUTIs) due to low ICD-10 sensitivity may overestimate or underestimate incidence and obscure risk factors [43]. In addition, the NIS database does not reliably distinguish CAUTIs due to inconsistent use of specific ICD codes, which precluded a subgroup analysis of CAUTI. Second, given the cross-sectional structure of the NIS and the inclusion of ICH hospitalizations identified from both primary and secondary diagnosis codes, the exact temporal relationship between ICH onset and UTIs occurrence could not be ascertained, which inherently constrains causal inference. Third, absence of clinical variables (e.g., cerebral hemorrhage size, cerebral hemorrhage location, catheter indwelling time, microbiological confirmation, and urinalysis) restricts severity stratification and mechanistic insights. These constraints underscore the need for prospective cohorts integrating longitudinal follow-up, microbiological validation, and granular clinical metrics to optimize UTIs management in ICH care.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the nationwide burden of UTIs in hospitalized ICH patients, revealing their high occurrence and association with substantial healthcare resource utilization. Identified associated factors underscore the urgency of prevention strategies, particularly for vulnerable groups. Clinicians must prioritize early identification of high-risk ICH patients and implement proactive measures to mitigate associated clinical burdens.
