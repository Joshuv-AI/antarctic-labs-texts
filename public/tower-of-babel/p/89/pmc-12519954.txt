The role of simple pulmonary function testing in unravelling the presence of respiratory muscular dysfunction in two patients with interstitial lung diseases
Authors: Pierantonio Laveneziana, Matthieu Le Mélédo, Thomas Gille
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Case history

A 50-year-old women and a 66-year-old man, both of whom had been diagnosed with pulmonary fibrosis, are referred for dyspnoea on exertion such as climbing two floors of stairs. They reveal that the symptoms appeared gradually. They are not smokers. Their current body mass index is 30 kg·m−2 for the female patient and 27 kg·m−2 for the male patient. Their Medical Research Council score for dyspnoea is 1–2. They both denied breathlessness while lying in the supine position (orthopnoea), while bending down or over to pick something up (e.g. to tie their shoes or to garden) (antepnoea) or while lying on left or right side (lateropnoea). On recent resting echocardiography, left ventricular ejection fraction was normal. Physical examination during spontaneous resting breathing was strictly normal: chest movement was equal bilaterally, there was no abdominal paradox, inspiratory neck muscle anomalies, or alterations at pulmonary and cardiac auscultation. On computed tomography (CT) their pulmonary fibrosis-related anomalies were stable over the past 6–12 months, as were the forced vital capacity (FVC), the 6-min walking distance and the desaturation during the 6-min walk test. Measurement of lung volumes (table 1) and lung transfer (or diffusing) capacity for carbon monoxide (TLCO or DLCO) indicated a “complex” restrictive ventilatory pattern.

What is the primary cause of their gradually appearing unexplained dyspnoea? Would pulmonary function tests (PFTs), including respiratory muscle evaluation, be an appropriate means to answer this question in these two patients with interstitial lung disease?

Answers

PFTs in an upright sitting position clearly showed a restrictive ventilatory defect as characterised by a reduction in total lung capacity (TLC) below the fifth percentile of the predicted value (lower limits of normality (LLN)), and a normal forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/vital capacity (VC) [1]. We noticed that this restrictive ventilatory defect was not “simple” or homogeneous, but rather “complex” or inhomogeneous (table 2) [1, 2]. In the “simple” or homogeneous restrictive ventilatory defect, lung volumes such as TLC, VC, residual volume (RV) and functional residual capacity (FRC) (and expiratory flow rates such as FEV1) are reduced to approximately the same extent [1, 2]. The RV/TLC and FRC/TLC ratios are usually normal [1, 2]. This pattern is typical of parenchymal restriction, i.e. decreased compliance of the lung parenchyma due to increased elastic recoil pressure. If DLCO is measured, KCO (defined as the DLCO/VA, where VA is the alveolar volume) is usually decreased along with DLCO because the lung parenchyma is affected [1, 2]. In the “complex” or inhomogeneous restrictive ventilatory defect, such as in our two patients, VC is reduced preponderantly compared to static volumes [1, 2], and the RV/TLC and FRC/TLC ratios are likely to be increased [1, 2]. This may indicate extra-parenchymal restriction. This can result from decreased compliance of the chest wall, including morbid obesity (which was not the case here), pleural involvement (which was not the case here) or respiratory muscle weakness [1–4]. In cases of extra-parenchymal restriction alone, the DLCO is reduced (by reduction of the exchange surface) but the KCO is high [1–4], as observed in our two patients (DLCO was ∼70% predicted in both while KCO was ∼115–120% predicted in both).

This “complex” or inhomogeneous restrictive ventilatory defect, combining a more pronounced and disproportioned reduction in VC compared to TLC, FRC or RV, an increased RV/TLC ratio along with a reduced DLCO but with KCO staying elevated, made our functional orientation switch more towards neuromuscular disorders [1–4].

In the presence of potential respiratory muscle weakness, a simple functional test available at PFT laboratories to orient towards diaphragmatic dysfunction is the evaluation of VC in a supine position (table 1) [5]. A difference of 24% in the 50-year-old women and of 13% in the 66-year-old man was found between the VC measured in an upright sitting versus supine position. This finding made us suspect diaphragmatic dysfunction, which was corroborated by the measurement of maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) that were found to be reduced in both patients (table 3). The diagnosis of the diaphragmatic dysfunction was confirmed by the invasive standard procedure involving electrical and magnetic phrenic nerve stimulation with transdiaphragmatic pressure (figure 1) [5].

State of the art and controversial issues

Respiratory muscle dysfunction is highly variable in patients with interstitial lung diseases, but when present it aggravates dyspnoea (especially on exertion) and exercise intolerance and impoverishes quality of life [6–9]. Therefore, PFTs play a crucial role not only in diagnosing and monitoring disease progression, but also in identifying early signs of respiratory muscle dysfunction, even in patients with relatively mild interstitial lung disease. In these patients, decreased lung volumes and maximal flow rates often signal restrictive ventilatory patterns, which may overlap with respiratory muscle dysfunction, leading to difficulties in distinguishing between them [1–4].

The observation of a “complex” or inhomogeneous restrictive ventilatory defect, characterised by a more pronounced and disproportioned reduction in VC compared to the other lung volumes (TLC, FRC and RV), an increased RV/TLC and/or FRC/TLC ratios along with a pattern of reduced DLCO with preserved or elevated KCO, may be very suggestive of extra-parenchymal restriction, such as respiratory muscle weakness [1–4].

Once suspected, respiratory muscle dysfunction (especially the diaphragm) can be confirmed by a number of tests [5]. Decisions about workup are generally made on the basis of the invasiveness and availability of testing [5]. Definitive diagnosis of diaphragm dysfunction can be obtained by phrenic nerve stimulation combined with diaphragmatic electromyography and/or twitch transdiaphragmatic pressure (twitch Pdi) measurement [5].

Simple and noninvasive PFTs, such as measurement of upright and supine VC which depends on activation of both inspiratory and expiratory muscles, are readily available and may support or refute the suspicion of respiratory muscle dysfunction, especially of the diaphragm (figure 1) [5]. The major limit of upright VC is that MIP decreases earlier than upright VC, this especially in neuromuscular diseases, thus making the suspicion difficult and postponing the correct diagnosis if relying exclusively on upright VC [5]. Unilateral diaphragm weakness is usually associated with a mild decrease in VC, to ∼75% of the predicted value, with a further 10–20% decrease in the supine position (15% representing twice the coefficient of variation of the measure could be considered as the upper limit of normality) (figure 1), while FRC and TLC are usually preserved in the seated position, but sometimes reduced in severe conditions [5]. In bilateral diaphragm weakness, VC usually reaches mean values of ∼50% predicted and can further decrease by 30–50% when supine [5].

It goes without saying that a measurement of upright and supine VC is important, as significant postural fall in VC when moving from a sitting to a supine position can suggest diaphragm dysfunction [5]. Now the question arises as whether a normal supine VC makes the presence of clinically significant diaphragmatic weakness unlikely. A study from our laboratory on the effects of posture on lung volumes in healthy individuals has recently pointed out that while FRC significantly decreased in the supine position, VC remained relatively stable due to compensatory increases in inspiratory capacity (IC) and decreases in expiratory reserve volume (ERV) [10]. The diaphragm has a protective function that prevents the lungs from being compressed by the abdominal contents [11]. Diaphragm atrophy can compromise this protective purpose [11]. When lying supine, the abdominal contents move towards the head, which should reduce end-expiratory lung volume, and this reduction should be disproportionately larger in patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction [11, 12]. Therefore, a reduction in ERV could indicate a problem with the diaphragm [12]. Healthy individuals also experience postural reductions in end-expiratory lung volume [10]. In our study of healthy participants, ERV significantly decreased in the supine position [10]. By contrast in patients with diaphragm dysfunction, supine ERV reductions may occur in addition to already reduced sitting FRC values, potentially leading to very low or nearly zero ERV [12]. In the study from our laboratory in healthy individuals, the observed reduction in ERV when moving to a supine position was compensated by an increase in IC [10], and this is thought to be due to the inspiratory muscles overcoming the added load from the change in posture [13]. By contrast, patients with diaphragm weakness may not show these compensatory changes in IC when supine due to a load–capacity imbalance [12]. The combination of the drop in FRC and the compensatory changes in IC explain why VC is relatively preserved in healthy individuals, with only small changes when moving from a sitting to a supine position [10]. It follows that relying solely on postural changes in VC may not be sufficient to detect diaphragmatic dysfunction, and postural changes in ERV and IC need to be considered, as they can be more indicative of changes than VC alone, as was the case for our male patient in whom the postural change in VC was only 13%. In his case the non-compensatory changes in IC (which decreased instead of increased) may explain the minimal variation of VC that could be misleading and hinders the diagnosis of respiratory muscle dysfunction.

The literature on respiratory muscle dysfunction in patients with interstitial lung diseases is scant [6, 8, 9, 14, 15]. Therefore, we do not know whether and to what extent the magnitude of the fall in VC in the supine position is correlated to respiratory muscle-related pressure in this population, as it clearly is in a wide range of neuromuscular disorders [5]. In addition, we do not know whether a significant reduction of VC at the diagnosis, as well as its change or rate of decline over time, would be recognised as being among criteria for initiating noninvasive ventilation and as being predictive of sleep disordered breathing, respiratory failure, prognosis, evolution and response to treatment, as it clearly is in a wide range of neuromuscular disorders [5]. One controversial point to highlight here is which noninvasive respiratory muscle test to use [5]. Conventionally, inspiratory and expiratory muscle strength has been evaluated by MIP and MEP at the mouth sustained for 1 s during a maximal static manoeuvre against a closed shutter [5]. However, MIP and MEP are highly effort-dependent and poorly reproducible, with a high coefficient of variation (∼25%). These tests may not always provide reliable results, especially in patients who are ill or experiencing dyspnoea [5]. Sniff manoeuvres (along with the pressure measured during sniff manoeuvres in the nostril (SNIP)) are considered easier and more natural to perform than MIP or MEP, and they offer better reproducibility [5]. However, they remain effort-dependent and can be challenging to interpret in patients with submaximal efforts or with dyspnoea [5].

The gold standard method of evaluating the mechanical function of the major inspiratory muscles, i.e. the diaphragm, is the measurement of the pressure generated by the diaphragm contraction in response to phrenic nerve stimulation, during which this pressure can be assessed using the difference between the oesophageal and the gastric pressures (twitch Pdi) [5]. Although techniques like transcutaneous electrical stimulation or magnetic phrenic nerve stimulation are useful and relatively easy tests that have become widely adopted for evaluating diaphragm function, they have the disadvantage of being time-consuming and challenging in terms of technical difficulty, discomfort and requiring specialised equipment [5]. Other novel means of evaluating diaphragm function are therefore welcome in the future.

Points to emphasise

An important point to emphasise is that a clear threshold for what constitutes a “normal” postural change in VC is not well-defined [5, 10]. A 20–30% reduction in VC when moving from sitting to supine is often and historically considered indicative of bilateral diaphragm weakness [16, 17]. A 15% postural change in VC has recently been proposed as the upper limit of normal [5, 18], but this threshold may not be sensitive enough to detect unilateral diaphragmatic paralysis, as highlighted by the European Respiratory Society (ERS) task force in 2019 [5], as well as by Brault et al. [19] and Laroche et al. [16]. A recent study suggested that a reduction in VC when moving from a sitting to a supine position of 8–12% may be a more accurate lower limit of normal in healthy subjects [10]. This is because the reduction in ERV when moving to a supine position is compensated by an increase in IC, this being thought to be due to the inspiratory muscles overcoming the added load from the change in posture [10, 13]. Patients with diaphragm weakness may not show these compensatory changes in IC when supine due to a load–capacity imbalance [12]. The combination of the drop in FRC and the compensatory changes in IC may explain why VC is relatively preserved in healthy individuals, with only small changes when moving from a sitting to a supine position [10]. A 15% postural change in VC (or even less) seems to be the best approach in a multimodal diagnostic setting as proposed by the ERS task force in 2019 [5], in which the presence of diaphragm dysfunction needs to be further confirmed by other tests (figure 1) [5]. A postural change in VC of more than 15% is higher than normal as defined by the ERS task force in 2019 [5], but not sensitive enough to identify mild forms of diaphragm dysfunction [5]. A 20% postural change in VC seems to be specific enough to independently confirm the presence of diaphragm dysfunction [5], but should be confirmed by other tests if possible [5]. Conversely, because of its very low sensitivity, it should never be used in a screening setting to rule out presence of diaphragm dysfunction (figure 1) [5].

Another point to emphasise is the absence of clearly defined lower limits of normality for noninvasive measures of global inspiratory and specifically diaphragmatic strength [5]. Generally and broadly speaking, unilateral and bilateral diaphragm paralysis can be expected to decrease MIP or SNIP in the ranges of 60% and <30% of the predicted values, respectively (figure 1) [5]. An important point to bear in mind here is that the agreement between SNIP and MIP is variable; therefore, it has been suggested that these variables should be regarded as complementary and not interchangeable in the evaluation of inspiratory muscle weakness [5]. MEP is generally preserved unless the underlying disease involves both the inspiratory and expiratory muscles (e.g. muscular dystrophy) [5]. Of note, MEP may be found to be mildly reduced (in the range of 70–80% of the predicted value) simply due to a restriction-related reduction of TLC which may affect the optimal length–tension relationships of the expiratory muscles at that volume [5].

It is worth noting that polysomnography could provide valuable additional information for the diagnosis of diaphragmatic dysfunction. During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, the diaphragm plays a crucial role in maintaining ventilation, as the accessory muscles of respiration are typically less active. Therefore, patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction may experience significant respiratory challenges during REM sleep, which can be detected through polysomnography. A definitive diagnosis, however, is reached only by assessing phrenic nerve stimulation and twitch Pdi [5].

Conclusions and future directions

Respiratory muscle dysfunction is an often underdiagnosed cause of unexplained dyspnoea in patients with interstitial lung diseases [6–9]. Although identifying respiratory muscle dysfunction is challenging, it plays a pivotal role in diagnosing, monitoring and guiding treatment, even in patients with relatively mild interstitial lung disease [6, 8, 9, 14]. In these patients, decreased lung volumes and maximal flow rates often signal restrictive ventilatory patterns, which may overlap with respiratory muscle dysfunction, leading to difficulties in distinguishing between them.

The suspicion of diaphragmatic dysfunction can be supported or refuted by simple lung and respiratory muscle function tests [2, 5], as was the case in our two patients. A suggested diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm for unilateral and bilateral diaphragm weakness is proposed in figure 1 and explained in greater detail in an ERS statement on this topic [5]. The definitive diagnosis of diaphragm dysfunction requires phrenic nerve stimulation and twitch Pdi measurement, which involves highly experienced physiology specialists and may, therefore, be not available at some institutions [5]. This may cause a significant delay in diagnosis of these conditions [5]. As such, new approaches to provide simple, noninvasive and easy-to-access procedures to diagnose diaphragmatic dysfunction are desperately needed [5]. Ultrasonography is being increasingly used to evaluate an altered function of the diaphragm, even in patients with interstitial lung diseases, and preliminary observations are very promising [6, 14, 15].

It goes without saying that the identification of respiratory muscle dysfunction in patients with interstitial lung diseases may help clinicians adjust treatment plans, including the implementation of pulmonary rehabilitation with the aim of strengthening the respiratory muscles and improving functional capacity, dyspnoea and quality of life [7].

Last but not least, more advanced cardiopulmonary exercise testing can unmask mechanisms of exertional dyspnoea [20, 21] by revealing, even in patients with the early stages of interstitial lung disease, early signs of respiratory muscle dysfunction [20, 22, 23], and improving early intervention and outcomes [23].
