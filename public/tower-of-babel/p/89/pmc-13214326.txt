Reciprocal influences between overdose experiences and naloxone access among people who use drugs utilizing a syringe services program: a cross-lagged panel model

Background

The opioid overdose epidemic continues to be one of the most pressing public health crises in the United States, with more than 80,000 people dying from drug overdoses in 2024 [1]. Opioid-related overdoses account for the majority of these deaths, largely driven by highly potent synthetic opioids such as fentanyl and its analogues [2]. This crisis disproportionately affects people who use drugs (PWUD), many of whom face structural barriers to care—including homelessness, poverty, stigma, and criminalization—that further elevate their overdose risk [3–8].

Naloxone is a lifesaving opioid antagonist medication that rapidly reverses opioid overdoses by restoring normal breathing, thereby preventing fatal outcomes [9]. Research suggests that nearly 40% of overdose events occur in the presence of a bystander, presenting critical opportunities for intervention if naloxone is available [10]. A substantial body of evidence now demonstrates that bystander naloxone administration and overdose education programs significantly reduce overdose mortality and have low rates of adverse events [11, 12]. However, despite being safe, effective, and endorsed by health authorities, access to naloxone remains inequitable, particularly among PWUD and those in underserved communities, such as racial and ethnic minorities, rural communities, and individuals experiencing homelessness [13–16].

Syringe services programs (SSPs) play a central role in addressing this gap, offering low-barrier, community-based naloxone distribution alongside other harm reduction services such as syringe exchange, overdose education, HIV and hepatitis C virus (HCV) testing, and wound care [17–19]. Studies have shown that SSP and other community-based naloxone distribution can significantly increase the availability of naloxone among people at risk of overdose and reduce overdose mortality [14, 20–22]. However, less is known about how individual-level overdose experiences influence subsequent naloxone access or, conversely, how receiving overdose education and naloxone affects future overdose preparedness and risk over time [23–25].

This study seeks to improve our understanding of the reciprocal influences between overdose experiences and naloxone access among PWUD utilizing services at an SSP in Miami, Florida. Specifically, we investigate the bidirectional relationship between (1) recent overdose experiences and naloxone receipt during SSP visits, and (2) naloxone receipt and having naloxone available when needed. By exploring these dynamics, we aim to shed light on how overdose experiences and naloxone access shape one another over time, with the goal of informing future harm reduction service delivery and improving outcomes for PWUD.

Methods

Setting

This study was conducted in Miami, Florida. In 2024, Miami-Dade County experienced 5,460 Emergency Medical Services (EMS) overdose responses and 375 fatal overdoses [26]. The county’s age-adjusted death rate due to drug overdoses has declined slowly in recent years, from 15.7 per 100,000 people in 2020 to 12.7 per 100,000 people in 2024 [26]. Paralleling national trends, the most common drugs involved in fatal overdoses in Florida in 2024 were fentanyl, followed by cocaine and methamphetamine [27]. Xylazine—a veterinary tranquilizer associated with heightened sedation and severe tissue necrosis—emerged as an adulterant in the Miami drug supply in the summer of 2022 and was detected in 56% of urine samples of PWUD utilizing services at the IDEA Miami SSP in 2023 [28, 29].

The IDEA Miami SSP has enrolled over 2,800 unique individuals in Miami-Dade County, Florida since its establishment in 2016 as Florida’s first legal SSP. Through its fixed site and mobile unit, IDEA Miami provides a comprehensive range of harm reduction services, including syringe exchange, overdose education and naloxone distribution, substance use disorder treatment, wound care, HIV, HCV, and sexually transmitted infection testing and treatment, medication management, and peer support. With regard to overdose education and naloxone distribution, every participant who presents to the SSP for syringe exchange or a related service is offered naloxone; staff inquire about their familiarity with naloxone administration and offer training on its use, as needed. As of February 2026, the SSP’s participants are predominantly male (71%) and are racially and ethnically diverse, with 73% identifying as White, 15% as Black, 10% as multiracial, and 39% as Hispanic. Forty-six percent of participants reported experiencing homelessness at the time of enrollment. In addition to its direct service provision, IDEA Miami leads statewide efforts to expand SSPs and promote harm reduction initiatives across Florida.

Design

This analysis used administrative data routinely collected by the IDEA Miami SSP. The analytic sample included all participants with at least three follow-up assessments (conducted at ≥ 3-month intervals, hereafter referred to as quarterly assessments) between January 2017 and April 2025, ensuring sufficient longitudinal data to provide within-person variability over time. While the SSP operates as an anonymous program, participants are assigned a unique participant ID upon enrollment and given a card with this ID number. This ID is recorded every time a participant visits the SSP for services, thus allowing for longitudinal analysis at the participant level. If participants do not have their card at subsequent visits, SSP staff can use an alternate unique identifier system to look up the participant’s ID number using their initials, mother’s initials, gender, ethnicity, and year of birth. If a participant reports losing their ID card at any point in time, SSP staff reissue the ID card based on the information provided above. By leveraging program records rather than research study recruitment and enrollment, this study captures real-world service interactions and outcomes among PWUD engaged in harm reduction services. The Institutional Review Board of the University of Miami determined that this study was not human subjects research due to its use of anonymous program data as part of routine program evaluation.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, race, ethnicity, and housing status, were obtained from the enrollment survey administered at participants’ first visit to the SSP; all of these measures were based on self-report. Overdose experiences (self-reported experience and/or witnessing of an overdose in the past 30 days) and whether the participant had naloxone available when needed in the past 30 days were captured through quarterly assessments, which are voluntary surveys administered every 3 months (i.e., they are not required for receipt of SSP services). Specifically, these items were assessed with the following questions:

In the past 30 days, have you overdosed due to injection drug use?

In the past 30 days, have you overdosed due to injection drug use?

In the past 30 days, have you witnessed an overdose due to injection drug use?

In the past 30 days, have you witnessed an overdose due to injection drug use?

In the past 30 days, how often have you had Narcan/Naloxone when you needed it?

In the past 30 days, how often have you had Narcan/Naloxone when you needed it?

Receipt of naloxone at the SSP was documented in daily exchange records by SSP staff. All measures were coded as binary variables (yes/no) for analysis.

Cross-lagged panel modeling

Data were processed and prepared using SAS OnDemand for Academics (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), including variable recoding, dataset merging, and construction of time-indexed variables. Analyses were conducted using Mplus (version 8; Muthén & Muthén, Los Angeles, CA). Cross-lagged panel models were specified to examine the bidirectional, longitudinal associations between overdose experiences and naloxone receipt, as well as between naloxone receipt and having naloxone available when needed [30].

Models were estimated using weighted least squares means and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimation, appropriate for binary dependent variables, and parameterized using the THETA method. For each research question, a series of nested models were tested, incrementally imposing constraints on autoregressive and cross-lagged paths to assess whether effects were stationary over time. Model comparisons were conducted using likelihood ratio tests and evaluation of fit indices, including the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and the Standard Root Mean Squared Residual (SRMR). Both unstandardized and standardized estimates were examined to interpret the magnitude and significance of autoregressive and cross-lagged effects.

Authors’ positionality

This analysis was led by a graduate student with five years of experience working with the IDEA Miami SSP, first as a medical student volunteer at its weekly student-run clinic and subsequently as a PhD student for her dissertation research. The analysis was guided by the Medical and Research Directors of the SSP and its lead statistician, all of whom have extensive expertise in harm reduction and related research.

Findings

Cohort

A total of 136 participants were included in the analytic sample (Table 1). The median age was 37 years (IQR: 29–44), and most participants were male (71%) and identified as White (77%). Approximately 41% identified as Hispanic. Most participants (69%) reported experiencing homelessness. Nearly all participants (96%) reported daily injection drug use, 73% reported needle reuse in the past 30 days, and 21% reported sharing needles in the past 30 days. The median interval between Time 1 and Time 2 was 6.2 months (standard deviation (SD) 7.3) and between Time 2 and Time 3 was 5.9 months (SD: 6.6).

Overdose experiences and naloxone access

Overdose experiences and naloxone access varied across the three time points (Table 2). At Time 1, 43% of participants reported experiencing or witnessing an overdose in the past 30 days, which declined to 31% at Time 2 and 28% at Time 3. In contrast, receipt of naloxone at an SSP visit remained relatively steady over time, at 51% of participants at both Time 1 and Time 3, with a slight decrease to 46% at Time 2. The percentage of participants reporting that they had naloxone available when needed increased over time, from 53% at Time 1 to 69% at Time 2 and 79% at Time 3.

Cross-lagged panel model fit

For both research questions, we tested a series of three nested cross-lagged panel models, incrementally imposing constraints on autoregressive (AR) and cross-lagged (CL) paths to assess whether effects were stationary over time. Model 1 was fully unconstrained, freely estimating all paths. Model 2 constrained the AR paths to be equal over time, while CL and residual paths remained freely estimated. Model 3 additionally constrained the CL paths to be equal over time.

For the first research question (i.e., overdose experiences and naloxone receipt), all models demonstrated excellent fit: chi-square tests were non-significant (p > 0.55), RMSEA = 0, CFI/TLI = 1, and SRMR values ranged from 0.022 to 0.045 (Table 3). Chi-square difference tests comparing each subsequent model to the baseline unconstrained Model 1 revealed no significant worsening of fit (Model 2 vs. 1: χ2 diff = 1.53, p = 0.465; Model 3 vs. 1: χ2 diff = 2.63, p = 0.622; Model 4 vs. 1: χ2 diff = 2.40, p = 0.301), supporting the adoption of the most parsimonious Model 3 as the final model for interpretation.

Cross-lagged panel model results

For the first research question (i.e., overdose experiences and naloxone receipt), standardized parameter estimates from the final cross-lagged panel model are presented in Fig. 1. Autoregressive paths showed significant stability over time: overdose experiences (OD → OD) ranged from 0.44 (p < 0.001) to 0.77 (p < 0.001), reflecting moderate to strong stability. Naloxone receipt (NR → NR) showed lower but still significant stability, with estimates ranging from 0.16 (p = 0.007) to 0.29 (p = 0.005). Cross-lagged paths revealed that experiencing and/or witnessing an overdose significantly increased the likelihood of receiving naloxone at a subsequent time point (OD → NR; 0.21, p < 0.001 to 0.41, p < 0.001). In contrast, receiving naloxone at a prior time point was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of reporting a subsequent overdose experience (NR → OD; − 0.18, p = 0.004 to − 0.28, p = 0.002). Contemporaneous correlations between overdose experiences and naloxone receipt within the same time point were small and nonsignificant, ranging from − 0.05 (p = 0.529) at Time 1 to 0.26 (p = 0.070) at Time 2. Unstandardized parameter estimates are provided in the Supplemental Materials.

For the second research question (i.e., naloxone receipt and having naloxone available when needed), standardized parameter estimates from the final cross-lagged panel model are presented in Fig. 2. Autoregressive paths showed low but significant stability over time for naloxone receipt (NR → NR), with estimates ranging from 0.13 (p = 0.036) to 0.25 (p = 0.035). In contrast, having naloxone when needed (HN → HN) demonstrated low and non-significant stability, with estimates ranging from 0.12 (p = 0.293) to 0.23 (p = 0.282). Cross-lagged paths revealed no significant effects from naloxone receipt to having naloxone at a subsequent time point (NR → HN; 0.03, p = 0.754 to 0.07, p = 0.754). However, the reverse pathway showed significant effects: having naloxone when needed significantly increased the likelihood of receiving naloxone at a future SSP visit (HN → NR; 0.19, p = 0.019 to 0.35, p = 0.011). Contemporaneous correlations between naloxone receipt and having naloxone within the same time point were strongest at Time 1 (r = 0.434, p < 0.001), with weaker and non-significant correlations at later time points (T2: r = 0.278, p = 0.091; T3: r = 0.245, p = 0.294). Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the reciprocal relationship between overdose experiences and naloxone access among PWUD utilizing harm reduction services. Our cross-lagged panel analysis showed that recent overdose experiences (including both self-experienced and witnessed events) were strongly predictive of receiving naloxone at subsequent visits to the SSP. Importantly, we also found evidence of a protective effect in the reverse direction: receiving naloxone at the SSP was associated with a lower likelihood of reporting overdose experiences at future time points. This finding may reflect several possible explanations: based on each participant’s needs, naloxone provision at the SSP could be accompanied with training on its use, which could encourage safer-use behaviors; or it could reflect differences in the risk profiles of participants who consistently engage with naloxone services at the SSP and those who do not. While this analysis does not account for important possible time-varying confounders, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, changes in the local drug supply, and changes in overall overdose mortality, taken together, these findings suggest that naloxone distribution at SSPs may play an important role in disrupting ongoing overdose risk cycles. They could do so by equipping participants with tools and supportive resources that reduce the immediate harms of opioid use.

Interestingly, we found no significant effect of receiving naloxone at an SSP visit on a person’s later likelihood of reporting naloxone access when needed, but we observed a significant reverse effect: participants who reported having naloxone when needed were more likely to receive naloxone at subsequent SSP visits. This counterintuitive pattern could suggest that individuals who are already more engaged and/or proactive about maintaining naloxone access may be more likely to seek resupplies at SSPs over time. An alternative interpretation is that having naloxone available during a critical moment could reinforce the perceived value of naloxone, thereby shaping future behaviors and increasing the likelihood of returning to the SSP for additional supplies. If so, this suggests that the psychological and behavioral impacts of overdose prevention experiences may play an important role in sustaining engagement with harm reduction services over time.

These findings build on and extend existing research that highlights the central role of SSPs in providing overdose prevention services to structurally marginalized populations [17]. While past studies have documented how naloxone distribution programs increase the capacity of communities to reverse overdoses and reduce mortality [11–14], few have examined the longitudinal, reciprocal dynamics between individuals’ overdose experiences, naloxone receipt, and overdose preparedness over time. Our results help to fill that gap by showing that overdose risk and naloxone engagement are not static: they evolve and interact over time, pointing to the need for continuous outreach and reinforcement to sustain overdose prevention benefits in high-risk populations. Expanding and supporting the capacity of SSPs to provide naloxone consistently is thus critical not only for increasing access to this life-saving medication but also for improving uptake and retention in harm reduction services over time—particularly in the face of structural barriers to accessing naloxone and substance use disorder treatment and evolving drug supply risks [3, 15, 16, 19, 31–33]. Likewise, efforts are needed to expand naloxone access to PWUD who are currently less likely to access SSP services [15, 16]; in the case of the IDEA Miami SSP, such groups include individuals who use drugs primarily by non-injection routes, individuals who live farther from the SSP and lack access to transportation, women, and racial and ethnic minorities.

This study has several strengths, including its use of real-world administrative program data, repeated longitudinal measures, and the application of cross-lagged panel modeling to disentangle directionality of relationships over time. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small, which may limit statistical power and the generalizability of our findings. Second, although the SSP’s administrative data provided detailed service and behavioral measures, we were limited to variables routinely collected by the SSP. Additionally, potential unmeasured confounders could influence the observed relationships; chief among these are time-varying contextual factors that occurred during the study time frame, including the COVID-19 pandemic, changes in the drug supply (e.g., the emergence of xylazine in 2022), and changes in overall overdose mortality. Likewise, because of the reliance on administrative program data rather than study data, a sampling strategy was not employed; thus, our findings may be biased based on who was more likely to complete the voluntary quarterly surveys. Third, outcomes such as overdose experiences and naloxone availability when needed were based on self-report, which may introduce recall or social desirability biases. Finally, this analysis does not account for fatal overdose experiences among SSP participants and we were unable to account for survivorship bias, which may artificially inflate the protective associations seen.

These findings highlight the dynamic, reciprocal nature of overdose risk and naloxone engagement over time. They reinforce the role of SSPs as ongoing points of access and re-engagement to enhance and sustain overdose prevention efforts [19, 31]. Future research should replicate these findings in larger and more diverse samples, account for potential time-varying confounders, utilize qualitative methods to explore potential mechanisms underpinning these relationships, and explore intervention strategies to strengthen naloxone access among those least likely to maintain it over time.
