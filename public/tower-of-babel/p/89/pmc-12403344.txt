Enhanced clinical outcomes of uncemented prostheses in revision surgery of distal femoral tumor prostheses: a retrospective study
Authors: Xiaoyuan Peng, Weiping Ji, Fan Yang, Yizhen Lin, Qingcheng Yang, Zhichang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rising incidence of cancer has highlighted the need for effective treatment strategies, particularly for bone tumors like distal femoral tumors. These tumors are among the most prevalent types of bone malignancies, often necessitating extensive surgical intervention to achieve optimal outcomes. Limb-salvage surgery, which involves resecting the tumor and reconstructing the limb with prosthetic devices, has become the standard approach for managing both malignant and aggressive benign tumors of the distal femur. This technique has largely replaced amputation, allowing patients to retain limb function and improve their quality of life. Despite advancements in surgical methods and prosthetic designs, complications such as aseptic loosening, periprosthetic fractures, and infections remain common, frequently requiring revision surgeries. The choice between cemented and uncemented prostheses in these revision procedures is crucial, as it significantly impacts patient outcomes and long-term functional recovery. This study aims to compare clinical outcomes between cemented and uncemented prostheses in distal femoral tumor revision surgeries, addressing existing gaps in the literature and providing guidance for clinical decision-making.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from 46 patients who underwent revision surgery for distal femoral tumor prostheses at Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital from 2019 to 2024. Patients were divided into two groups: 22 in the cemented prosthesis group (control) and 24 in the uncemented prosthesis group (experimental). Inclusion criteria required patients to be aged 20–50 years, with confirmed epiphyseal closure and clinical diagnosis of aseptic loosening after distal femoral tumor prosthesis replacement. Patients with severe systemic conditions or infections were excluded to ensure a homogeneous study population. Key parameters evaluated included operation time, intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay, and postoperative drainage duration. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) scoring system and the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scale. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, with significance set at a P value of ≤ 0.05, ensuring robust comparisons of clinical, radiological, and functional outcomes between the two prosthesis types.ResultsThe uncemented prosthesis group demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared to the cemented group. Operation time was significantly shorter (124.75 ± 12.54 min vs. 162.67 ± 19.17 min, P < 0.001), and intraoperative blood loss was markedly lower (412.45 ± 32.12 mL vs. 647.56 ± 37.45 mL, P < 0.001). Postoperative recovery indicators also favored the uncemented group, with shorter hospital stays (16.64 ± 2.67 days vs. 19.78 ± 2.86 days, P = 0.004) and reduced drainage duration (9.85 ± 3.52 days vs. 13.47 ± 3.17 days, P = 0.007). Functional outcomes, measured by MSTS scores, were significantly higher in the uncemented group (70.24 ± 7.35 vs. 56.70 ± 4.98, P < 0.001), and KPS scores showed similar trends (78.32 ± 3.87 vs. 62.45 ± 4.12, P < 0.001).ConclusionIn conclusion, this study demonstrates that uncemented prostheses provide superior clinical outcomes in revision surgeries for distal femoral tumors compared to cemented prostheses. The significant advantages include shorter operation times, reduced intraoperative blood loss, faster recovery, and improved functional outcomes, as indicated by higher MSTS and KPS scores. The biological fixation mechanism of uncemented prostheses promotes osseointegration, enhancing long-term stability and minimizing complications, such as aseptic loosening and infections. Although oncological outcomes, including disease-free survival and overall survival, were comparable between both groups, uncemented prostheses were particularly beneficial for younger patients with adequate bone stock. Despite limitations such as a small sample size and insufficient long-term follow-up, these findings offer valuable insights into the clinical application of uncemented prostheses and underscore the importance of personalized treatment strategies tailored to individual patient needs. Future research should involve larger, multicenter studies to validate these results and further explore the mechanisms behind the observed differences in outcomes.

Introduction

Cancer on the increase throughout the world has sharply been highlighting the importance of progress in tumor treatment strategies. Distal femoral tumors are one of the most frequent bone tumors, which usually involve long periods of surgery to secure optimal results. In recent decades, limb saving resection has become the gold standard for treatment of malignancy and aggressive, benign tumors of the distal femur. This method has largely replaced amputation and enables patients to preserve the activities of the limb and achieve a high quality of life after extensive resection of the tumor. [1, 2].

Tumor prostheses have been at the forefront of innovations in limb salvage surgery. However, despite their simplicity and success rate, aseptic loosening, periprosthetic fracture, and infection still represent issues that are not always manageable, often requiring revision procedures [3, 4]. The decision-making process regarding prosthesis type in revision surgery has significant influence on the patient outcome and his/her long-term functional recovery. Primary cemented, and cementless prostheses are the two common options, both with advantages and disadvantages.

Cemented prostheses have traditionally been favored, particularly for patients with poor bone quality or significant bone loss. These prostheses provide immediate mechanical stability and predictable short-term outcomes, making them especially suitable for older patients or those with compromised bone stock [5, 6]. However, cemented prostheses are associated with complications such as aseptic loosening, which remains one of the leading causes of implant failure [7, 8]. In addition, the use of bone cement introduces risks such as cement leakage and bone resorption, which can compromise long-term outcomes [9].

In contrast, uncemented prostheses have emerged as a promising alternative due to their ability to achieve biological fixation through osseointegration. This process allows host bone to grow into the implant surface, reducing the risk of aseptic loosening and enhancing long-term stability [10, 11]. Uncemented prostheses are particularly advantageous for younger patients with adequate bone stock, as they offer the potential for better survivorship and functional outcomes [12]. Recent advancements, including the development of 3D-printed customized uncemented stem prostheses, have expanded the indications for uncemented systems in complex revision cases. These innovations have demonstrated improved biomechanical performance, enhanced radiological stability, and higher patient satisfaction [13, 14].

Despite these advancements, the decision between cemented and uncemented prostheses remains complex and is influenced by various patient-specific factors, such as age, bone quality, and the extent of bone loss. While uncemented prostheses have demonstrated superior functional outcomes and lower complication rates in some studies, the lack of long-term comparative data limits their widespread adoption [15, 16]. Furthermore, complications such as infection, local recurrence, and periprosthetic fractures remain significant concerns that necessitate further investigation [17, 18].

The ongoing evolution of limb salvage surgery underscores the importance of personalized treatment strategies. Factors such as patient age, bone quality, and expected long-term outcomes should guide the choice of prosthesis type. This aligns with the principles of precision medicine, which aim to optimize treatment outcomes by tailoring interventions to individual patient characteristics [19].

This study addresses a critical gap in the literature by directly comparing the clinical outcomes, radiological stability, and complication rates of cemented versus uncemented prostheses in revision surgeries for distal femoral tumor prostheses. While existing studies have highlighted the advantages and disadvantages of each approach, few have provided comprehensive, comparative data specific to revision surgeries. By analyzing perioperative indicators, functional outcomes, and complication rates, this study not only enhances the understanding of prosthesis selection but also facilitates evidence-based decision-making in revision surgery. The findings aim to guide clinicians in optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes, contributing to the broader goal of precision medicine in orthopedic oncology.

Methods

Patient data

This study retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent revision surgery for distal femoral tumor prostheses at the Orthopedics Department of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University, between 2019 and 2024. Patients were divided into two groups: the Cemented Prosthesis Revision Group (control group) and the Uncemented Prosthesis Revision Group (experimental group). Inclusion criteria required patients to be aged between 20 and 50 years, with imaging confirming epiphyseal closure and clinical diagnosis of aseptic loosening following distal femoral tumor prosthesis replacement. In addition, patients were required to have healthy cortical bone in the proximal femur to ensure compatibility with uncemented stems. Exclusion criteria included severe cardiovascular disease (e.g., NYHA Class III or IV), significantly elevated blood glucose levels (> 11.1 mmol/L), severe osteoporosis (T-score ≤ −2.5), systemic tumor metastasis, an expected survival period of less than 18 months, active or chronic infections, or a history of infection. These criteria were established to ensure patient safety during surgery and postoperative recovery, as well as to minimize confounding factors that could compromise the reliability of the study outcomes. Patients with systemic comorbidities were excluded because such conditions can significantly influence perioperative risks, recovery trajectories, and functional outcomes, thereby complicating the interpretation of the results. However, this decision may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader patient populations, particularly those with complex medical histories.

Surgical methods

The surgical procedure for the control group involved the removal of the loosened cemented prosthesis through a standard longitudinal incision of the thigh skin and subcutaneous tissue. Specialized tools and ultrasonic bone knives were used to excise the damaged residual bone cortex and remove the bone cement. To fully expose the proximal femur and access the medullary canal, extensive soft tissue dissection was performed. A groove was created in the proximal femur to facilitate the removal of the medullary canal plug and residual bone cement. The medullary canal was reamed to a predetermined depth, exceeding the length of the original prosthesis stem, to accommodate the new cemented stem. After trial-fitting the prosthesis, the slotted bone block was reset and secured with a steel wire rope. Bone cement was then prepared and injected into the medullary canal under controlled pressure to ensure optimal filling. The new cemented prosthesis stem was inserted into the canal, and the modular prosthesis components were assembled. Care was taken to avoid cement leakage, which could lead to complications such as thermal necrosis or vascular compression. The wound was irrigated thoroughly before closure in layers.

The surgical approach for the experimental group was similar in its initial steps, involving a longitudinal incision to expose the distal femoral prosthesis. After soft tissue stripping, the residual host bone was exposed, and the loosened prosthesis was removed. Unlike the control group, proximal bone cement and medullary canal plugs were not removed, minimizing soft tissue disruption. Residual damaged bone cortex and bone cement were removed using ultrasonic bone knives to a depth exceeding the length of the uncemented stem by approximately 1 cm. A guide needle was inserted into the medullary canal, and the canal was gradually reamed to a diameter 1–2 mm smaller than the uncemented stem. This ensured a tight compression fit, which is critical for achieving primary stability and facilitating osseointegration. The uncemented stem was trial-fitted, and further reaming was performed if significant resistance was encountered. Once the stem was securely implanted, the modular prosthesis components were assembled. The surgical site was irrigated, and the wound was closed in layers.

Efficacy evaluation

The efficacy of surgical treatment was evaluated using a combination of clinical, radiological, and functional outcomes. Clinical indicators included perioperative factors such as operation time, intraoperative blood loss, duration of hospital stay, and drainage placement duration. Radiological assessments were conducted using X-rays and CT scans to evaluate prosthesis stability. Key radiological parameters included the length ratio of the extramedullary and intramedullary parts of the prosthesis, the ratio of prosthesis length to femoral length, and the ratio of intramedullary trunk diameter to femoral diameter.

Functional outcomes were assessed using the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) scoring system, which evaluates pain, function, emotional acceptance, support, walking ability, and gait. Each parameter is scored on a scale of 0 to 5, with a maximum score of 30. The Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scale was also used to evaluate overall functionality and quality of life.

In addition to functional and radiological outcomes, the incidence of complications was recorded. These included aseptic loosening, infection, periprosthetic fractures, and implant failure. Tumor-related outcomes, such as local recurrence, distant metastasis, disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS), were analyzed. Patient-reported outcomes, including pain relief (measured by the Visual Analog Scale, VAS) and satisfaction with postoperative recovery, were also evaluated.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including mean, median, standard deviation, and interquartile range. Group comparisons were conducted using independent t tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. A P value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data was handled using multiple imputation methods to minimize bias. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential interactions between patient characteristics (e.g., age, BMI, bone quality) and surgical outcomes. Graphical representations of key findings, including Kaplan–Meier survival curves for DFS and OS, were generated to provide a visual comparison of oncological outcomes between the two groups. This comprehensive methodology ensured a robust comparison of the clinical, radiological, and functional outcomes of cemented and uncemented prostheses in the revision surgery of distal femoral tumor prostheses.

Results

Patient features

Figure 1 illustrates the patient enrollment process for the study, which aimed to compare the outcomes of cemented and uncemented prostheses in revision surgeries for distal femoral tumor prostheses. Initially, 73 patients were identified as candidates for inclusion in the study. However, after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 27 patients were excluded for various reasons. Among these, six patients were excluded due to severe systemic comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hyperglycemia, and severe osteoporosis, which could significantly impact surgical outcomes. Three additional patients were excluded due to systemic tumor metastasis, while four patients were excluded because their expected survival period was less than 18 months. Furthermore, 14 patients were excluded due to active, chronic, or past infections, as these conditions could compromise the success of the revision surgery.

The enrollment process in the study. The final number of enrolled patients was 46, of which 22 in the Control Group and 24 in the Experimental Group

After these exclusions, a total of 46 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. These patients were divided into two groups based on the type of prosthesis used during revision surgery. The Cemented Prosthesis Revision Group (control group) included 22 patients, while the Uncemented Prosthesis Revision Group (experimental group) consisted of 24 patients. The figure highlights the rigorous screening process used to ensure that only eligible patients were included, thereby minimizing potential confounding factors and ensuring the reliability of the study outcomes. This careful selection process underscores the study's focus on maintaining a high standard of methodological rigor.

The baseline characteristics of the patients in the cemented prosthesis group (control group) and the uncemented prosthesis group (experimental group) were well-matched, with no statistically significant differences observed between the two groups. A total of 46 patients were included in the analysis, with 22 in the control group and 24 in the experimental group. The mean age of patients in the control group was 61.88 ± 11.09 years, compared to 62.24 ± 11.31 years in the experimental group (P = 0.913), indicating similar age distributions. Similarly, the Body Mass Index (BMI) was comparable between the two groups, with a mean BMI of 22.71 ± 2.85 kg/m2 in the control group and 23.83 ± 3.24 kg/m2 in the experimental group (P = 0.222).

Laboratory parameters further confirmed the comparability of the groups. For instance, the mean HbA1c levels were 6.18 ± 1.29% in the control group and 6.26 ± 1.09% in the experimental group (P = 0.821), indicating no significant differences in glycemic control. Similarly, serum creatinine levels were 78.43 ± 27.38 μmol/L in the control group and 82.88 ± 44.73 μmol/L in the experimental group (P = 0.689), reflecting comparable renal function. Serum uric acid levels were also similar, with 324.60 ± 84.39 μmol/L in the control group and 338.08 ± 89.52 μmol/L in the experimental group (P = 0.603). To enhance the interpretability of baseline characteristics presented in Table 1, standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated for each variable. While P-values for variables such as age, BMI, HbA1c, and serum creatinine were non-significant, Cohen’s d values indicate small to moderate effect sizes (ranging from 0.03 to 0.37), suggesting potential clinical differences between the uncemented and cemented groups.

Surgical indicators

Perioperative indicators were analyzed to compare the efficiency and safety of the two surgical approaches. Significant differences favoring the uncemented prosthesis group were observed (Table 2). The mean operation time in the experimental group was 124.75 ± 12.54 min, significantly shorter than the 162.67 ± 19.17 min observed in the control group (P < 0.001). This reduction in surgical time can be attributed to the simplified procedure associated with uncemented prostheses, which eliminates the need for extensive cement removal and preparation. The shorter operative time not only minimizes patient exposure to anesthesia but also reduces the risk of intraoperative complications, especially in patients with comorbidities.

Intraoperative blood loss was markedly reduced in the experimental group (412.45 ± 32.12 ml) compared to the control group (647.56 ± 37.45 ml, P < 0.001). This significant reduction reflects the less invasive nature of uncemented prosthesis surgeries, which require less dissection and preserve more surrounding soft tissue. Reduced blood loss is particularly beneficial for patients with the pre-existing anemia or those at risk of transfusion-related complications.

Postoperative recovery indicators further highlighted the advantages of uncemented prostheses. Hospital stays were shorter in the experimental group (16.64 ± 2.67 days) compared to the control group (19.78 ± 2.86 days, P = 0.004). A shorter hospital stay not only reduces healthcare costs but also minimizes the risk of hospital-acquired infections and enhances patient satisfaction. Furthermore, the duration of drainage placement was significantly reduced in the experimental group (9.85 ± 3.52 days vs. 13.47 ± 3.17 days, P = 0.007). This reduction indicates a faster resolution of postoperative inflammation and seroma formation, which facilitates earlier mobilization and rehabilitation.

Postoperative imaging follow-up indicators

Postoperative imaging assessments revealed significant differences in prosthesis stability between the cemented and uncemented groups. Radiological parameters, including the length ratio of extramedullary and intramedullary parts of the femoral prosthesis, the ratio of prosthesis length to femoral length, and the ratio of intramedullary trunk diameter to femoral diameter, were analyzed to evaluate the stability and integration of the prostheses.The length ratio of the extramedullary and intramedullary parts of the femoral prosthesis was significantly lower in the uncemented group (96.27 ± 28.08%) compared to the cemented group (108.51 ± 74.38%, P < 0.001). This indicates better alignment and integration of uncemented prostheses with the host bone, which is attributed to the biological fixation achieved through osseointegration. Similarly, the ratio of prosthesis length to femoral length was lower in the uncemented group (71.89 ± 6.62%) compared to the cemented group (80.75 ± 8.39%, P < 0.001), suggesting that uncemented prostheses are more effective in preserving the natural biomechanics of the femur. Furthermore, the ratio of intramedullary trunk diameter to femoral diameter was significantly higher in the uncemented group (35.06 ± 12.18%) as compared to the cemented group (25.50 ± 6.90%, P < 0.001), reflecting superior intramedullary fixation and stability.

Functional, tumor-related and patient-reported outcomes

The comparison of functional, tumor-related, and patient-reported outcomes between the cemented prosthesis group (control group) and the uncemented prosthesis group (experimental group) revealed significant differences favoring the uncemented group (Table 3). Among the major findings of this study, the significantly shorter operative time and superior functional outcomes observed in the uncemented group stand out as key contributions. The mean operation time in the uncemented group was 124.75 ± 12.54 min, significantly shorter than the 162.67 ± 19.17 min in the cemented group (P < 0.001). This reduction reflects the efficiency of the uncemented procedure, which avoids extensive cement removal and minimizes soft tissue disruption. Functional outcomes were particularly remarkable, with the uncemented group achieving MSTS scores of 70.24 ± 7.35 compared to 56.70 ± 4.98 in the cemented group (P < 0.001), indicating superior mobility, pain reduction, and overall functionality. Similarly, KPS scores were significantly higher in the uncemented group (78.32 ± 3.87 vs. 62.45 ± 4.12, P < 0.001), highlighting improved quality of life and physical independence. These findings establish the uncemented approach as a clinically advantageous option for revision surgeries.

Pain relief, assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), was also better in the uncemented group, with lower scores (2.9 ± 1.3) as compared to the cemented group (3.8 ± 1.5, P = 0.036), indicating reduced pain levels postoperatively. Tumor-related outcomes, including disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS), were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences (P = 0.906 and P = 0.823, respectively). This suggests that the type of prosthesis does not compromise oncological efficacy when appropriate surgical margins and adjuvant therapies are maintained.

The incidence of complications further highlighted the advantages of uncemented prostheses. The uncemented group exhibited fewer cases of wound complications, with no infections and only one case of wound non-healing. In contrast, the cemented group experienced higher complication rates, including one case of infection, two cases of wound non-healing, two cases of local tumor recurrence, two cases of tumor jumping lesions, and one case of distant metastasis. These findings underscore the superior functional recovery, reduced pain, and lower complication rates associated with uncemented prostheses, making them a favorable option for patients undergoing revision surgery for distal femoral tumor prostheses.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to compare the clinical effect of uncemented and cemented endoprosthesis revision in the distal femoral tumor prostheses. The results indicated that there were significant differences in surgical effects between uncemented and cemented groups, such as the relatively short operation time, the relatively small intraoperative blood loss, and the relatively fast recovery after operation of the uncemented group. In addition, the uncemented prosthesis had significant growth in postoperative stability and reduced complications rates including local tumor recurrence, distant metastases and infection. Also, uncemented patients have had higher MSTS scores according to better joint function recovery and better quality of life. These results offer practical guidance for the choice of prosthesis.

Building upon these findings, it is important to place this study in the context of existing literature to better understand how these results compare with prior research and contribute to the field of orthopedic oncology. The findings of this study align with previous research emphasizing the advantages of uncemented prostheses in orthopedic oncology. For instance, Kotz and Salzer demonstrated that modular uncemented prostheses provided better long-term stability and reduced complications as compared to cemented designs [6]. Similarly, Pala et al. highlighted that uncemented prostheses significantly reduced the risk of aseptic loosening by promoting biological fixation through osseointegration [20].

In contrast, cemented prostheses have traditionally been favored for patients with poor bone quality or significant bone loss, as they provide immediate mechanical stability. Yang et al. emphasized the benefits of cemented implants in elderly patients with osteoporosis, citing their ability to achieve early weight-bearing and reduce perioperative complications [21]. However, the current study suggests that uncemented prostheses may offer superior long-term outcomes, particularly for younger patients with adequate bone stock. The comparable oncological outcomes between the two groups align with studies such as those by Guo et al., which demonstrated that the type of prosthesis does not significantly influence DFS or OS when wide surgical margins and effective adjuvant therapies are achieved [22]. This underscores the importance of prioritizing functional recovery and quality of life in prosthesis selection. These comparisons highlight the need to further explore the mechanisms underlying the observed differences in outcomes, particularly the biological and mechanical factors that contribute to the success of uncemented prostheses.

The superior outcomes observed in the uncemented group can be attributed to several factors. First, uncemented prostheses rely on osseointegration, which allows host bone to grow into the implant surface, creating a stable biological fixation [20]. This process reduces the risk of aseptic loosening; a common complication associated with cemented implants. Additionally, the surgical technique for uncemented prostheses is less invasive, avoiding the extensive soft tissue dissection and removal of proximal bone cement required for cemented implants. This likely contributed to the shorter operative time, reduced intraoperative blood loss, and faster recovery observed in the uncemented group. Furthermore, patient-specific factors such as age and bone quality play a critical role in determining the suitability of uncemented implants. Younger patients with adequate bone stock benefit from the biological fixation mechanism, which promotes long-term stability and reduces complications. In contrast, older patients with compromised bone quality may require careful evaluation to ensure sufficient osseointegration potential. This highlights the importance of individualized treatment strategies in optimizing surgical outcomes. The lower complication rates in the uncemented group may also be linked to the absence of bone cement, which has been associated with adverse effects such as cement leakage, thermal necrosis, and localized immune reactions [23]. Furthermore, the materials used in uncemented prostheses, such as titanium alloys, exhibit excellent biocompatibility and promote vascularization, enhancing healing and reducing the risk of infection [24].

The higher MSTS scores in the uncemented group reflect better functional recovery, which may be attributed to the ability of uncemented prostheses to preserve more bone stock and promote natural joint mechanics [25]. This advantage is particularly relevant for younger patients, as their higher bone remodeling capacity supports the osseointegration process. For older patients or those with osteoporosis, additional measures such as preoperative bone density enhancement or customized implant designs may be required to achieve optimal outcomes. These findings underscore the need for tailored surgical approaches based on patient-specific factors, including age, bone quality, and activity level. This aligns with Miura et al.’s findings that biological long-stem prostheses reduce stress shielding and optimize stress distribution, creating a stable mechanical environment conducive to healing [26].

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to validate these results. Second, the follow-up period was insufficient to fully assess long-term prosthesis survivorship and late complications, such as mechanical failure or late aseptic loosening. Third, the study did not include biomechanical analyses, which could provide deeper insights into the performance of prostheses under physiological loads. Finally, patient-reported outcomes related to psychological and social aspects, such as quality of life and satisfaction, were not comprehensively assessed. The single-center design of this study, while ensuring methodological consistency, may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions with different surgical protocols or patient management strategies. Additionally, the retrospective nature of the study introduces inherent risks of selection bias, as patients with more severe systemic conditions or complex surgical histories may have been excluded. These factors highlight the need for multicenter, prospective studies to validate the results and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the clinical efficacy of uncemented prostheses in diverse settings. Addressing these limitations will not only improve the robustness of future studies but also enhance the reliability of evidence-based recommendations for clinical decision-making.

Despite these limitations, the results of this study provide valuable insights into the clinical application of uncemented prostheses and their potential to improve patient outcomes. These findings also have broader implications for clinical decision-making and the development of personalized treatment strategies. The results of this study have significant implications for clinical practice. The demonstrated advantages of uncemented prostheses in functional recovery, complication rates, and radiological stability support their broader adoption, particularly for younger patients with adequate bone stock. Recent advancements in 3D-printed customized prostheses have further expanded the indications for uncemented systems, allowing for precise adaptation to individual patient anatomy and bone quality [10]. These technologies enable the creation of prostheses with enhanced surface properties, promoting osseointegration and reducing the risk of aseptic loosening. Additionally, 3D printing facilitates the development of lightweight, biomechanically optimized implants that preserve natural joint mechanics and minimize stress shielding. In clinical practice, 3D-printed prostheses have shown promising results in managing complex cases involving significant bone loss or anatomical irregularities. For example, the use of patient-specific implants has demonstrated improved radiological stability and functional outcomes in revision surgeries. Moreover, this technology offers the potential for cost-effective production and faster turnaround times, which are particularly beneficial in resource-limited settings. Future research should focus on long-term evaluations of 3D-printed prostheses to fully understand their clinical impact and optimize their design for broader applications. The findings also highlight the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing patients undergoing revision surgeries. Surgeons must consider not only the mechanical and biological properties of the prosthesis but also the patient’s age, bone quality, and long-term functional goals. This comprehensive approach can help optimize outcomes and improve the quality of life for patients undergoing these complex procedures.

The exclusion of patients with systemic comorbidities, while necessary to ensure a homogeneous study population and reduce confounding variables, may impact the external validity of the findings. Patients with conditions such as advanced cardiovascular disease, poorly controlled diabetes, or severe osteoporosis often represent a significant proportion of those undergoing revision surgeries in real-world clinical settings. By excluding these patients, the study results may not fully reflect the outcomes achievable in a broader, more diverse patient population. Future studies should aim to include patients with varying degrees of systemic comorbidities to better understand how these factors influence surgical outcomes and to provide more comprehensive guidance for clinical decision making. In addition, the study underscores the need for personalized treatment strategies based on patient-specific factors, such as economic conditions, survival time, and activity level [27]. This aligns with the principles of precision medicine, which aim to optimize treatment outcomes by tailoring interventions to individual patient characteristics.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the superior efficacy of uncemented prostheses in the revision surgery of distal femoral tumor prostheses. Compared to cemented prostheses, uncemented prostheses offer significant advantages, including shorter operation time, reduced intraoperative blood loss, faster postoperative recovery, and superior functional outcomes, as evidenced by higher MSTS and KPS scores. These benefits are largely attributed to the biological fixation mechanism of uncemented prostheses, which promotes osseointegration and reduces the risk of aseptic loosening. Furthermore, radiological evaluations confirmed enhanced prosthesis stability, and the uncemented group exhibited a lower incidence of complications such as infection and local tumor recurrence. While oncological outcomes, including disease-free survival and overall survival, were comparable between the two groups, uncemented prostheses proved particularly advantageous for younger patients with adequate bone stock, offering better long-term survivorship and functional recovery. However, the study's limitations, including a relatively small sample size and insufficient long-term follow-up, highlight the need for further research through larger, multicenter studies. Overall, uncemented prostheses represent a promising option for improving surgical outcomes and quality of life in patients undergoing distal femoral tumor prosthesis revision surgery, emphasizing the importance of personalized treatment strategies based on patient-specific factors.
