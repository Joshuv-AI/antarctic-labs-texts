An Unusual Association of Trichobezoar with Refeeding Syndrome and Subacute Intestinal Obstruction in Postoperative Period
Authors: Rohit Kapoor, Divya Prakash, Sunita Singh, Amit Shukla, Rini Dixit
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTrichobezoar with Rapunzel syndrome is a rare condition seen in young females. Refeeding syndrome is a rare phenomenon that can occur in any chronically malnourished person after starting feeds. We discuss and review available literature of extremely rare cases of 11-year-old girl, with trichobezoar with refeeding syndrome with subacute intestinal obstruction in the postoperative period.

INTRODUCTION

Trichobezoar is rarely (<1%) seen in children, where accumulated hair within the stomach (most common site) and intestine is resistant to digestion and gets enmeshed over time causing various symptoms.[1] First, human bezoar was reported in 1779, after an autopsy for gastric perforation peritonitis.[2] The name “Rapunzel” comes from Greece fairy-tale for trichobezoar extending into the small intestine. Refeeding syndrome is well described but often forgotten condition affecting chronically malnourished patients. Although trichobezoar with Rapunzel syndrome has been reported by many in the literature,[2] refeeding syndrome occurring in the postoperative period in these patients is extremely rare. To the best of our knowledge, our case is the first such case to be reported in literature.

CASE REPORT

An 11-year-old girl was admitted from the outpatient department with complaints of occasional pain in the abdomen for 3 years with intermittent nonbilious vomiting and decreased appetite for 1 year. She had a habit of biting fingernails (onychophagia) and eating hair (trichotillomania). A large firm mass in the left hypochondrium, epigastrium, and right hypochondrium till the umblicus was palpable.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography-abdomen suggestive of heterogeneous intraluminal lesion with trapped air-foci consistent with bezoar (~30 cm) located in the stomach, extending up to DJ-flexure. There was no pneumoperitoneum.

Preoperative blood investigations revealed anemia (Hb – 9 g/dl), leukocytosis (total leukocyte count – 17,360/mm3) with neutrophilic predominance (77%), and low serum - albumin (1.5 g/dl) [Table 1]. She was started on intravenous antibiotics (3rd-generation cephalosporin), the night before surgery. She was taken up for exploratory laparotomy under general anesthesia. Gastrotomy over the anterior wall of the stomach, longitudinally along the gastric-axis was done and trichobezoar was removed in toto with its tail (Rapunzel syndrome) of length ~90 cm, complete specimen length ~125 cm, and weight ~1 kg [Figure 1]. Postoperatively, the child was nil orally (NPO) and transfused albumin. On 4th postoperative day (POD), orals were allowed and the next day, she developed facial and peripheral edema with high abdominal drain output, decreased urine output (<1 ml/kg/hr), dyselectrolytemia, hypoalbunemia (1.85 g/dl), and hypoglycemia ( Random blood sugar (RBS)-40 mg/dl) [Table 1]. She developed generalized tonic–clonic seizures with uprolling of eyes. Due to dyselectrolytemia and neurological manifestation after starting oral feeds in chronically malnourished child, refeeding syndrome was confirmed. The patient was kept nil orally, antiepileptics, total parental nutrition (TPN), and electrolytes correction were started and she improved. However, on 12th-POD, she had abdominal distension and not passing flatus/stools. X-ray abdomen showed multiple air-fluid levels with dilated proximal bowel, suggesting intestinal obstruction [Figure 1]. She was managed conservatively and oral upper gastrointestinal contrast follow-through showed grossly dilated proximal bowel with no contrast reaching the large bowel till 48 h. Next day, she passed a lot of stools with flatus. She was started orally and TPN tapered. She was discharged after 25 days of hospital stay. After 1-year of follow-up, the patient was healthy and on medications for trichotillomania/trichophagia after psychiatric consultation.

DISCUSSION

After the first description by Vaughan et al. in 1968, many cases of trichobezoars with Rapunzel syndrome have been reported.[2] Only 1% of patients with trichophagia develop trichobezoar. Trichobezoars are encountered in young females with psychological disorders which are often chronically malnourished. When trichobezoar extends from the stomach into the small intestine, it is called Rapunzel syndrome,[2] as in our case also, the tail was ~90 cm long extending up to the proximal jejunum.

Refeeding syndrome is defined as potentially fatal shift in fluids and electrolytes that may occur in chronically malnourished patients receiving artificial refeeding (enterally/parenterally). The hallmark biochemical feature of refeeding syndrome is hypophosphatemia. The syndrome is complex and features abnormal sodium-fluid balance; changes in glucose, fat, and protein metabolism; thiamine deficiency, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia. Excessive oral administration may lead to hyperglycemia and its sequelae of osmotic diuresis, dehydration, metabolic acidosis, ketoacidosis with lipogenesis, increased carbon dioxide production, hypercapnia, and respiratory failure.[3] As in our cases, the clinical features occurred as a result of functional deficit of these electrolytes and metabolic disturbances with cardiopulmonary and neurological manifestations. The National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE) guidelines recommend that refeeding has to be started at <50% of energy requirements in “patients who have eaten little or nothing for >5 days” and then gradually increased if no refeed occurs.[4] Therefore, in our case, feeding was restarted as per NICE guidelines which the patient tolerated. Moreover, there is a paucity of literature of refeeding syndrome occurring in the postoperative case of trichobezoar. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such case reported in the literature.

In our case, we also used water-soluble contrast, to manage adhesive intestinal obstruction in the early postoperative period as its high osmolarity, decreases edema, activates water movement into the small bowel lumen, and enhances smooth muscle contractility.[5]

In conclusion, refeeding syndrome rarely occurs in postoperative cases of trichobezoar. Hence, after surgery, it should always be kept in mind before starting orally and feeding should be done as per NICE guidelines. Furthermore, recurrences after surgery are rare but psychiatric consultation and regular follow-up after treatment, as done in our case, should always be considered.
