Alternative splicing dynamically regulates common carp embryogenesis under thermal stress
Authors: Shuimu Hu, Guopeng Tian, Yulin Bai, Ang Qu, Qian He, Lin Chen, Peng Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThermal stress is a major environmental factor affecting fish development and survival. Common carp (Cyprinus carpio) are susceptible to heat stress in their embryonic and larval phases, but the thermal stress response of alternative splicing during common carp embryogenesis remains poorly understood.ResultsUsing RNA-seq data from eight developmental stages and four temperatures, we constructed a comprehensive profile of alternative splicing (AS) during the embryogenesis of common carp, and found that AS genes and events are widely distributed among all stages. A total of 5,835 developmental stage-specific AS (SAS) genes, 21,368 temperature-specific differentially expressed genes (TDEGs), and 2,652 temperature-specific differentially AS (TDAS) genes were identified. Hub TDAS genes in each developmental stage, such as taf2, hnrnpa1, and drg2, were identified through protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. The early developmental stages may be more sensitive to temperature, with thermal stress leading to a massive increase in the number of expressed transcripts, TDEGs, and TDAS genes in the morula stage, followed by the gastrula stage. GO and KEGG analyses showed that from the morula stage to the neurula stage, TDAS genes were more involved in intracellular transport, protein modification, and localization processes, while from the optic vesicle stage to one day post-hatching, they participated more in biosynthetic processes. Further subgenomic analysis revealed that the number of AS genes and events in subgenome B was generally higher than that in subgenome A, and the homologous AS genes were significantly enriched in basic life activity pathways, such as mTOR signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway. Additionally, lncRNAs can play a regulatory role in the response to thermal stress by targeting AS genes such as lmnl3, affecting biological processes such as apoptosis and axon guidance.ConclusionsIn short, thermal stress can affect alternative splicing regulation during common carp embryogenesis at multiple levels. Our work complemented some gaps in the study of alternative splicing at both levels of embryogenesis and thermal stress in C. carpio and contributed to the comprehension of environmental adaptation formation in polyploid fishes during embryogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10838-6.Keywords: Cyprinus carpio, Alternative splicing, Thermal stress, Embryogenesis

Background

Thermal stress is a major environmental factor that affects the development and survival of aquatic organisms, especially fish [1, 2]. The common carp (Cyprinus carpio), a crucial economic freshwater fish species, is an allotetraploid with high tolerance to elevated temperatures compared to many diploid fishes [3]. Compared with adult fish, embryos and larvae are much more sensitive and fragile to thermal stress [4, 5]. Thermal stress induces a wide range of morphological and physiological abnormalities in common carp embryos and larvae, which lead to hatching unsuccess, growth retardation, and mortality increasing, which also affects many cell homeostasis pathways in response to external stimuli and stresses, such as DNA repair, apoptosis, oxidative stress, hormone synthesis, and thermal tolerance [6]. With the ongoing advancements in sequencing technologies, the application of genetic methods to analyze various stress resistance mechanisms has become increasingly widespread [6, 7]. In terms of thermal tolerance, genomic and transcriptomic studies of fish species have made some progress [8, 9] but studies about common carp embryos and other regulation mechanisms are still limited.

For eukaryotic genomes, alternative splicing (AS) events play a pivotal role in increasing the diversity of both transcriptomes and proteomes, maximizing the utility of limited coding sequences [9–11]. Alternative splicing has been studied in several fish species and under various environmental stresses, including temperature fluctuations, hypoxia, and salinity changes. Lu et al. conducted a comprehensive analysis of AS events in the genomes of four bony fishes, including Danio rerio, Oryzias latipes, Gasterosteus aculeatus, Takifugu rubripes, and Ictalurus punctatus, and revealed a significant negative correlation between AS frequency and genome size [12]. Many AS events associated with cold and hypoxic responses have been identified in catfish [13], rainbow trout [14], and tilapia [15–18]. Comprehensive alternative splicing profiles of spotted sea bass responding to salinity variations have also been constructed [17]. However, these studies are basically conducted at the adult fish level [19, 20]. AS events have been quite abundantly studied in the differentiation of human embryonic stem cells, early plant development, and stress responses [19–21], but relatively rare in fish embryos other than the model organism zebrafish [22, 23]. The role of alternative splicing in coordinating embryonic responses to environmental stressors, particularly in thermotolerance, remains an understudied aspect [24, 25]. As a classic allotetraploid species, the genome and transcriptome of the common carp have been extensively investigated at various biological levels. However, functional genomics at the subgenomic level of the common carp is still in its infancy, and research on alternative splicing mechanisms during embryogenesis under thermal stress remains scarce. Advancements in this area are essential for understanding the effects of heat stress and alternative splicing on common carp embryos.

The regulation of gene expression can be categorized into three main levels: regulation at the DNA level, transcriptional regulation, and post-translational regulation [24]. This complex regulatory particular emphasis on alternative splicing and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) as pivotal components [25, 26]. Alternative splicing research delves into the complex selection and splicing processes of coding elements after the formation of mRNA precursors [27, 28]. The studies of lncRNAs focus on non-coding sequences, traditionally regarded as "transcriptional noise". Although these transcripts cannot directly translate proteins with biological functions, they can influence the regulation of coding sequences by competitively binding and scaffolding RNA–protein complexes [26]. An increasing number of studies have highlighted the role of lncRNAs in alternative splicing regulation. Fefilova et al. identified the regulation of lncRNA Morrbid on the splicing of an oncogene Nras in mice [28]. Gonzalez et al. discovered that lncRNAs transcribed from a human gene FGFR2 promote its alternative splicing by preventing binding to repressive chromatin splicing complexes [29]. Additionally, some lncRNAs may collaborate with splicing factors to coordinate alternative splicing processes [30, 31]. However, comprehensive analyses integrating these two regulatory mechanisms are still in progress. Existing studies have mainly focused on major human diseases, leaving a research gap in understanding the interaction of these regulatory processes in aquatic animals at diverse developmental stages and environmental conditions.

Therefore, this study focuses on the effects of thermal stress on alternative splicing and the potential role of alternative splicing on common carp embryonic development. We explored the genome-wide and subgenomic distribution and expression of various AS events during common carp embryogenesis, concentrated on pivotal AS genes in response to thermal stress at each stage, and analyzed lncRNA-targeted alternative splicing regulation under heat stress. Our research will help to provide insights into the adaptive strategies and evolutionary potential of common carp under global warming scenarios, as well as to optimize aquaculture practices and improve the production and quality of common carp.

Methods

Data collection and transcript assembly

The Henan Academy of Fishery Sciences Hatchery Station in Zhengzhou, China, provided all fish and embryos used in this study. Three weeks before the experiment, 20 male and female common carps were selected and temporarily reared in two outdoor ponds (60 m2/pond, water temperature: 22.5 ± 1.5 ℃, pH: 7.2 ± 0.3, salinity: 0–0.5‰, natural light) until artificial insemination during the breeding season (late April). After anesthesia with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), fertilized eggs were derived from artificial insemination of sperm and eggs from one male and one female mature common carp. The incubation of fertilized eggs was located in four indoor water tanks (100 L/tank, 15 petri dish/tank, 100–200 eggs/petri dish). The water in the tanks came from outdoor ponds and two 25 W LED lights were installed indoors to simulate the natural light cycle every day (8:00–18:00). The incubation water temperatures in the experiments were set at 24 °C, 27 °C, 30 °C and 33 °C, controlled by temperature controllers (HAILEA HS-66A, China) and heating rods (WEINUO WN-B500W, China). The selection of developmental stages referred to the eight representative periods in the study of Jin et al. [32] (Fig. S1, P1-P8: morula stage, blastula stage, gastrula stage, neurula stage, optic vesicle stage, tail bud stage, muscle contraction stage, and one day post-hatching stage). Each time a petri dish was randomly selected and observed under the microscope. If more than 80% of the embryos have developed to the same stage, sampling can be taken at that stage. Embryos cultured at 24 ℃, 27 ℃, and 30 ℃ were collected in eight stages (P1-P8) with three biological replicates. Embryos cultured at 33 ℃ were only collected from P1 to P3 with three biological replicates because all common carp embryos failed to develop to the gastrula stage (P3) (Fig. 1A; Table S1).

TRIzol (Invitrogen, China) reagent was then used to extract the total RNA of 81 surviving samples of C. carpio at eight developmental stages and four temperatures. About 15 common carp embryos per sample were used for RNA isolation and RNA-seq libraries were created using the Illumina TruSeq Library Construction Kit, followed by paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovoSeq 6000 with 150 bp paired-end reads. A HiFi genome of the allotetraploid common carp (NCBI BioProject accession no. PRJNA823855) was used for the comparison of RNA-seq data. Trimmomatic (v0.38) [33] was used to filter raw reads of all samples to obtain clean reads. Next, STAR (v2.7.5a) [34] was used to index and compare clean reads to the common carp reference genome to generate.bam files. Finally, transcript assembly and expression calculation were performed by StringTie (v2.1.4) [35]. R was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA) on the whole gene expression data to assess the overall effects of temperature and developmental stage on gene expression in the embryos of common carp.

Alternative splicing and developmental stage-specific AS identification

All alternative splicing (AS) events and genes were identified and classified using rMATS (v4.2.0) [36]. The.bam files of three experimental groups (27 ℃, 30 ℃, and 33 ℃) were compared with those of the control group (24 ℃) at each developmental stage by rMATS, which divided all AS events into five categories: alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), skipped exon (SE), mutually exclusive exon (MXE) and retained intron (RI). In each developmental stage, genes with alternative splicing between at least one experimental group and the control group were considered AS genes. Stage-specific analysis was performed on all AS genes at 24 ℃ (control group) using PaGeFinder [37], and genes with DPM > 0.9 were considered to have significantly unequal contributions to different stages of AS gene expression. These genes were identified as developmental stage-specific AS (SAS) genes (Fig. S2).

Temperature-specific DEGs and DAS genes screening

DEseq2 [38] R package was used to identify differentially expressed encoding genes (DEGs) with |log2[foldchange]|≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 in temperature comparison groups at each developmental stage. The.MATS.JC.txt file generated by rMATS recorded the level of differential alternative splicing between two groups, and only those with FDR < 0.05 were considered differential alternative splicing (DAS) events. In each developmental stage, genes differentially expressed between at least one experimental group and the control group were considered temperature-specific DEGs (TDEGs) genes, and genes with differential alternative splicing between at least one experimental group and the control group were considered temperature-specific DAS (TDAS) genes (Fig. S2).

Analysis of HAS genes and lncRNA targeted AS genes regulation

The homologous gene (HLG) pairs and lncRNA data sets of C. carpio were derived from previous studies [39, 40]. Any AS gene in a homologous gene pair was considered a homologous alternative splicing (HAS) gene. HAS genes presented only on one subgenome were subgenome-specific HAS genes (subgenome A/subgenome B specific HAS genes), and on both subgenomes were paired HAS genes of C. carpio. DAS patterns of lncRNA target genes were graphed with rmats2sashimiplot (v4.0.2) [41]. Alluvial plots demonstrated the distribution and function of paired HAS genes on subgenomes of C. carpio and the relationship between lncRNAs and AS genes by ggalluvial [42] R package and ggsankey [43] R package, respectively.

Expression patterns, network construction, and enrichment analysis

ComplexHeatmap [44] R package was used to visualize the expression patterns of SAS genes and TDAS genes based on TPM values. Expression pattern clustering analysis of TDAS genes was performed by Mfuzz [45] R package. All PPI networks were constructed by STRING database[46] (Danio rerio as reference species) and Cytoscape (v3.10.0) [47]. The TDAS genes were further screened using cytohubba [48] plugin of Cytoscape, and the top 20 TDAS genes in the interaction network of each developmental stage were considered hub genes using MCC algorithm. All Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional annotation and analysis were performed using the OmicShare tools (https://www.omicshare.com/tools).

Experimental validation of qRT-PCR

A primer design tool (https://www.genscript.com/tools/real-time-pcr-taqman-primer-design-tool) was used to design primers for the inclusion isoforms of six differentially expressed TDAS genes (ccnb2, cdk2, cdt1, incenp, lmnl3, sgo1) and the internal control (β-actin) (Table S2). Reverse transcription was performed using extracted RNA and Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (gDNA digester plus, Yeasen, China), and Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) was used for qRT-PCR reaction in a CFX384™ real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA) with 10 μL reaction volume and technical triplicates. The cycling parameters were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, then 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, and 60 °C for 40 s. SPSS (v27.0) [49] was used to do statistical analysis on the relative expression of inclusion isoforms using 2−ΔΔCt method [50].

Results

Overview of AS events and dynamic expression of SAS genes in C. carpio

Before specific analysis, we preliminarily explored the overall effects of temperature and developmental stage on gene expression in common carp embryos. Principal component analysis (PCA) of gene expression data for all samples showed that the three replicates within each group were clustered together, indicating high biological consistency. Samples from each group tend to cluster together according to developmental stage rather than temperature, and the early development of common carp can be divided into two stages: P1-P4 and P5-P8. The impact of temperature on sample clustering varied in each phase, with the greatest dispersion among different temperature groups in P1 and P3, suggesting that high temperatures may have a greater effect during these stages (Fig. S3).

After ensuring the reliability of the RNA-seq data, we constructed a comprehensive AS profile of common carp embryos. Overall, in 81 samples of common carp embryos or larvae under thermal stress, a total of 367,029 AS events with 22,073 genes were identified, including 15,977 A3SS events, 17,947 A5SS events, 250,670 SE events, 14,390 MXE events, and 68,045 RI events. As embryonic development progressed, the number of both AS events and AS genes generally gradually increased with the highest proportion of SE events and the lowest proportion of MXE events at all stages (Table S3; Fig. 2A). Circos plot displayed the distribution of AS events and AS genes in the common carp genome (Fig. 1B). Regions with high density of AS events existed on many chromosomes, and genes in the ten regions with the highest AS event densities (nol11, r3hd1, lima1, cd27, znf84, hnrnpa1, pkhm1, yets4, zfan6, rtn4, ctnb1, and kifc3) were mainly associated with transcriptional regulation, cytoskeleton, stem cell pluripotency, immunity. Most AS genes only produced one AS event (70.8%), and very few genes could produce five types of AS events (0.5%) (Fig. 1C).

To investigate the potential role of alternative splicing on embryonic development of common carp, we screened 5,835 developmental stage-specific (SAS) genes using 24 samples from 24 °C. Then we mapped the expression profiles and co-expression networks of SAS genes (Fig. 2B and C). There were protein–protein interactions between the major contributing SAS genes at the same stage, but also between SAS genes playing important roles at different stages. In the early stages of common carp embryogenesis, SAS genes were mainly involved in the processing of genetic and environmental information. From P1 to P4, nucleic acid transport, cell cycle, protein modification, cell differentiation, and mRNA splice site selection became the main processes in order. In the later stages, SAS genes were more involved in molecular cellular processes and developmental processes, and from P5 to P8, chromatin modification, stem cell pluripotency, neurogenesis, and response to stimulus gradually dominated.

Temperature-specific analysis of DEGs and DAS genes

We further explored the effects of thermal stress on splicing and expression at various stages of common carp embryonic development. The number of genes expressed at 24 ℃ basically increased from P1 to P8. In particular, when the temperature rose from 24 ℃ to 27 ℃, the number of expressed genes in the P1 stage increased dramatically, exceeding all other development stages (Fig. S4). Next, a total of 21,368 TDEGs and 2,652 TDAS genes were identified using DESeq2 and rMATS, respectively (Table S4). The number of both TDEGs and TDAS genes was the most in the P1 stage, followed by the number of TDEGs in the P3 stage, and SE and MXE were the largest and least number of DAS events, respectively (Fig. 3A, Fig. S5). The P1 stage (morula stage) precedes the mid-blastocyst transition (MBT) when mRNA molecules in the embryo are predominantly of maternal origin [51]. Therefore, it was speculated that thermal stress may increase the abundance of expressed transcripts and cause differential expression and differential splicing of a large number of mRNAs before zygotic genome activation. After this stage, zygotic transcription begins, and alternative splicing events could continue to increase with embryonic development. The P3 stage (gastrula stage) is the termination period of common carp embryonic development at 33 ℃, thus differential expression and differential splicing of mRNA under heat stress may determine whether the embryo survives or not at this stage.

Heat shock proteins are important molecular chaperones that paticipate in a variety of biological processes under heat stress [52]. hsp90 co-chaperone cdc37-like 1 (cdc37l1) was identified as TDAS gene in multiple stages (P1, P3, and P6) and its main AS types were A3SS and RI. heat shock protein hsp 90-β (hsp90b) was a TDAS gene in the P3 stage, and its AS type was RI. hsp70-binding protein 1 (hpbp1) and hspb1-associated protein 1 homolog (hpap1) were up-regulated TDEGs and down-regulated TDEG in the P1 phase, respectively. These genes may regulate the thermotolerance of common carp embryos by producing functionally different isoforms or changing expression, and participate in cellular processes at different developmental stages. Among these TDAS genes and TDEGs, 1,447 genes underwent both differential expression and differential alternative splicing under thermal stress. These genes were significantly enriched in many cellular and environmental information processes, such as metabolic processes, gene expression, RNA processing, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and spliceosomes (Fig. 3B and C, Table S5).

Heatmap showed the expression patterns of TDAS genes under thermal stress at all stages, which were mainly divided into seven clusters (Fig. 4A and B). KEGG analysis revealed that many genes related to cell metabolic processes and splicing activities had temperature-specific alternative splicing throughout the early developmental stages. TDAS genes in the early stages were more involved in intracellular transportation, protein modification, and localization processes, while in the later stages, they were more inclined to biosynthesis processes (Fig. 4C and D). TDAS genes at the P1 stage were involved in a large number of activities related to environmental change response and basic life activities. Many TDAS genes participated in transcriptional repression processes such as histone acetylation, negative regulation of RNA metabolism and synthesis processes in the P2 stage, and more transcriptional dysregulation pathways were enriched from the P3 stage to the P4 stage, such as mRNA catabolic metabolism, apoptosis, cellular senescence, and necrosis (Table S6; Table S7).

Hub TDAS genes at eight developmental stages

To find out which genes play a central role in the thermal stress response of alternative splicing, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed to identify hub TDAS genes at each developmental stage (Fig. 5; Table S6). The majority of hub TDAS genes exhibited differential splicing at one to three developmental stages. In the P1 stage, kif2a, ccnb3, and ddx3 participated in mitotic and DNA replication processes and had many SE events. In the P2 stage, both drg2 and rnps1 had differential RI events, which were involved in RNA binding and regulation of cell growth and differentiation. In the P3 stage, cnot1, cep55, and ccnd1 underwent A3SS and SE events, which were associated with negative regulation of transcription and cell cycle. In the P4 stage, kdm1a and wsb1 with gene silencing and protein modification functions had differential SE events. In the P5 stage, ddx55 and traf6 participated in spliceosome assembly and congenital immune formation, which experienced SE and MXE events, respectively. In the P6 stage, rffl, noc3l, usf2, and ifrd1 were involved in embryogenesis, tissue differentiation, and signal transduction, which experienced differential SE events. In the P7 stage, med25, pno1, and pak2 were related to RNA and macromolecule metabolism and neurogenesis, which had differential SE events. In the P8 stage, mrnip and mysm1 occurred differential SE events, which can affect chromatin-binding and modifying effects. Several TDAS genes also existed in most developmental stages. For example, rbm39 was identified as a hub TDAS gene except the P2 stage, hnrnpl was a hub TDAS gene in six stages, and taf2 was a TDAS gene in five stages. Among them, certain hub TDAS genes may function in environmental stress response and multi-level damage repair in different stages, such as abi1, cdk2, etc.

Subgenomic characterization of AS genes during embryogenesis in C. carpio

To further explore the influence of alternative splicing at the subgenomic level, we compared the number and distribution of various AS genes between two subgenomes of common carp and analyzed the alternative splicing of homologous gene (HLG) pairs. Generally, the number of AS genes and AS events in subgenome B were higher than that in subgenome A, as were the number of TDAS genes and SAS genes (Table 1; Fig. 6A). The total expression of AS genes, TDAS genes, and SAS genes was also higher on subgenome B, despite some homologous chromosome pairs showed opposite results (Fig. S6).

In total, 9,811 of 14,085 HLG pairs of common carp contained at least one AS gene, including 1,778 pairs only on subgenome A, 2,049 pairs only on subgenome B, and 5,984 pairs on both subgenome A and B (Fig. 6B and C; Table 1). Similar to all AS genes, the number of various homologous alternative splicing (HAS) genes gradually increased from P1 to P8, with the most SE events and the least MXE events (Fig. S7). The total expression of paired HAS genes was significantly higher than that of subgenome-specific HAS genes, while the expression of paired HAS genes on subgenome B and subgenome B specific HAS genes was significantly higher than that of paired HAS genes on subgenome A and subgenome A specific HAS genes, respectively (Fig. 6D). Compared to subgenome-specific HAS genes, the expression patterns of paired HAS genes between two subgenomes also seemed to be more similar (Fig. S8). Paired HAS genes were more significantly enriched in many pathways that integrally regulate basal life activities. For instance, FoxO signaling pathway and mTOR signaling pathway regulate cellular physiological processes; the classical p53 signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway play an important role in cellular physiological processes; Hedgehog signaling pathway is crucial for embryonic development; the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway participates in innate immunity (Fig. 6A; Table S8). Subgenome A specific HAS genes were heavily involved in more specific biosynthesis and post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression, whereas subgenome B specific HAS genes were more inclined to function in nucleic acid metabolism regulation, multiple lipid metabolism, and apoptosis (Fig. S9).

LncRNAs regulating AS in the embryonic thermal stress interaction network

Based on the published embryonic heat stress network, we investigated the targeted synergy between lncRNA and AS genes under thermal stress. Only a small proportion of AS genes may be targeted by temperature-specific differentially expressed lncRNAs (TDELs), and only two genes, incenp, and lmnl3, were located in the intersection of all AS genes and lncRNA groups (Fig. 7B). incenp may help centromere localization in the early to mid-stage of mitosis [53], which had differential SE events under thermal stress. lmnl3 decreased the proportion of SE events, A5SS events, and RI events in the P1 stage, which was a main lamin protein in fish oocytes and early embryos, and could promote apoptosis [54] (Fig. 7A). The alluvial map demonstrated some SAS genes and TDAS genes that could be targeted and regulated by TDELs and their significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 7C; Table S9). In particular, trans-lncRNA-mediated regulation accounted for the vast majority, and different AS genes might act synergistically in the same key pathway. TDEGs, TDAS genes, SAS genes, and target genes of TDELs can simultaneously regulate many progresses including transcriptional dysregulation, biosynthesis, and metabolism biological processes, including apoptosis, axon guidance, glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, propanoate metabolism, and RNA polymerase composition.

To further confirm the reliability of the RNA-seq data and to evaluate the expression of AS genes at different developmental stages and under different temperatures, we verified the expression of six differentially expressed TDAS genes (ccnb2, cdk2, cdt1, incenp, lmnl3, sgo1) in three temperature comparison groups of the P1 stage and the P3 stage by qPCR. The results showed that these genes had similar expression patterns in RNA-seq analysis and qRT-PCR experiments, indicating the validity of our analysis (Fig. S10).

Discussion

Since the publication of the first high-quality genome of C. carpio [55], more and more breakthroughs have been made in tetraploid fish genomic resources, and common carp has been widely used as a model organism for tetraploid fishes to some extent. The improved genome quality and annotation of goldfish and other tetraploid fishes are often compared with common carp, and several characterization studies of polyploid evolution and origin have also selected common carp for validation [39, 56–59]. However, compared with model organisms such as Danio rerio, functional genomics research on common carp is still lacking in general, usually focusing on certain functions of organs or tissues in the adult fish stage [60–62], where more detailed studies are urgently needed. In previous studies, we preliminarily explored the thermal stress response of lncRNAs during common carp embryogenesis, mapped the expression profile of lncRNAs, and constructed heat stress regulatory networks of common carp embryos mediated by lncRNAs, exemplifying epigenetic regulation at the transcriptional level [40]. This study mainly focused on the thermal response of alternative splicing, another regulation mechanism at the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, in the early developmental stages of C. carpio. We identified and classified AS genes and events in all samples, completed the developmental stage-specific and temperature-specific alternative splicing analysis, screened out many hub TDAS genes, and innovatively explored the distribution and expression characteristics of AS genes at the subgenomic level of C. carpio, and finally parsed the integrated regulation of embryonic thermal stress by lncRNAs and alternative splicing based on existing datasets.

Widespread AS events during embryogenesis of C. carpio

Numerous reports have indicated that AS events occur abundantly and perform an important regulatory role during cell differentiation and embryonic development in many plants and animals [18–20]. In contrast to the preponderance of RI events in plants [63, 64], the number of SE events in common carp embryos accounted for an overwhelming advantage, which was similar to many fishes [17, 65]. AS genes and events were distributed in varying densities and widespread in various regions of common carp genome. Interestingly, an AS gene may produce more than one type of AS events or multiple AS events to utilize limited genes to express an extremely rich variety of proteins during common carp embryogenesis, which has been reported as a main reason for maintaining proteome diversity [66]. Meanwhile, some developmental stage-specific AS genes could specifically reflect the AS characteristics at certain stages during embryogenesis [23]. The expression of SAS genes changed dynamically with common carp embryogenesis, and their splicing products may interact at different developmental stages, which well characterized the key regulation of alternative splicing from cell division to organogenesis. Also, both increasing trends in the number of all AS genes and SAS genes suggested that the transcriptome characteristics become more complex as common carp embryos developed.

Heat-sensitive alternative splicing for embryo fate determination

It has been shown that the early stages of fish embryogenesis are important for environmental stress response and the regulation of alternative splicing [23, 67]. The P1 stage had the most TDEGs and TDAS genes, which might indicate the heat sensitivity of common carp embryos in the morula stage [39, 40]. More AS events and TDAS genes were activated after the blastula stage (P2) and continued to increase from the next gastrula stage (P3), the final period of embryonic arrest at 33 ℃, which had multiple transcriptional dysregulation pathways. Additionally, gene expression and alternative splicing traces related to oocyte and sperm activity were also found in the P1 stage, such as DAS of strbp and md2l2, suggesting that some influence of maternal transcripts still existed during the early stages of common carp embryos, as also found in many mammals and fish [23, 68–70].

In terms of transcriptional regulation, a large number of TDAS genes related to RNA polymerase and transcription initiation have been identified. taf2 was a hub AS gene in six stages, which correctly localized RNA polymerase II as a transcription initiation factor in combination with the core promoter [71]. Many hub TDAS genes could express mediators of RNA polymerase II transcription subunit, such as med27, med15, and med23 in the P1 stage, as well as med1 in the P5 stage, and med25 in the P6 stage, which might be involved in a variety of growth and differentiation processes and resistance pathways [71–73]. Post-transcriptional regulation was mainly manifested in the regulation of AS involving snRNAs [74], which constitute the major part of the RNA spliceosome in eukaryotic organisms. Genes in some high AS density regions and several hub TDAS genes were involved in the regulation of SR proteins and hnRNPs in different ways. hnrnpa1, hnrnpl, and hnrnpk, which belong to hnRNA family, had differential SE events in the vast majority during embryogenesis [75–77]. The protein encoded by srrm1 belongs to SR protein family, which could participate in mRNA splicing through spliceosome [78]. nek2, pak2h, and plk1 produce serine/threonine protein kinases regulating mitosis, apoptosis in dying cells, and a wide range of vital functions [79–81]. Multiple RNA-binding proteins perform essential regulation in RNA metabolism including post-transcriptional splicing processing, modification, and transport. For example, hub TDAS genes rbm39 and rnps1 participated in the co-localization of core spliceosome proteins and splicing of mRNA nuclear export and mRNA surveillance, respectively [82, 83]. AS differences in heat sensitivity may also regulate immunity and cell fate determination in common carp embryos. Several hub TDAS genes, such as kif2c, kif2a, kif23, cdk2, drg2, ccnb3, and ddx56, regulate mitosis, embryogenesis, or tissue differentiation in various ways [84–87], and the proteins encoded by traf6 are pivotal molecules in innate immunity [88]. Evidently, TDAS genes responded to thermal stress at multiple levels and pathways. Common carp embryos at early developmental stages were more sensitive to heat, and high temperatures at this stage might induce embryo mortality regulated by alternative splicing.

Contribution of HAS genes to embryonic resistance

Various studies on common carp are gradually advancing to the allotetraploid scale. In recent years, research on the evolution and dominance of common carp subgenomes has emerged in an endless stream, and flexible AS strategies at subgenomic level are being explored [39, 58]. Our results suggested that both genes in an HLG pair didn’t necessarily experience AS events at the same time, and any gene in an HLG pair may specifically alternative spliced on a particular subgenome. Although the number and total expression of diverse AS genes were slightly higher in subgenome B than subgenome A during common carp embryogenesis, whether this is due to subgenome dominance remains debatable.

Nevertheless, paired HAS genes exhibited a larger number and significantly higher expression than subgenome-specific HAS genes, which potentially indicated that AS regulation occurring on two subgenomes simultaneously was more dominant. Homologous genes underwent alternative splicing simultaneously to jointly regulate similar functions, which is consistent with the ubiquitous trans-splicing and co-expression between common carp subgenomes [56, 89]. On the one hand, the presence of HAS genes might enhance the resilience of common carp embryos in response to emergencies, such as gene silencing or functional alterations caused by mutations or environmental changes. For paired HAS genes, even if a gene incorrectly alternative spliced, its homologous gene may still correct the error by producing correct splicing products. For subgenome-specific HAS genes, even if they were adversely affected by alternative splicing in response to stresses like high temperatures, their homologous genes can still function properly, similar to the dosage compensation effect [90]. On the other hand, HAS genes may also co-splice to “double” expression in response to environmental stresses. Subgenome-specific AS of a gene does not mean that its homologous gene fails to express, and the spliced product of subgenome-specific HAS genes may still have similar functions as the original products, thereby exerting synergistic roles [91]. In summary, understanding the distribution, expression, and functional preferences of HAS genes in response to thermal stress during embryogenesis will help unravel the mystery of the robust adaptive capacity in common carp.

Multiple mechanisms combined in the regulation of embryonic thermal stress

The development of epigenetics has greatly enriched the genetic expression mechanism in organisms after the “central dogma” was proposed [92]. Heat stress regulation was not controlled by a separate mechanism, various genes collaborate synergistically across different regulatory levels. The response to embryonic thermal stress exhibits less gene overlap among lncRNA regulation and alternative splicing, suggesting distinct regulatory principles. However, it is essential to note that complete isolation is not observed. Specifically, 31 TDAS genes and 69 SAS genes were respectively identified as target genes of TDELs, and very few of them underwent temperature-specific differential expression at the same time, reflecting the possible regulation of gene expression and alternative splicing by lncRNAs. Such regulations were mostly trans, for example, nfya, a target gene of hub lncRNAs MSTRG.101646.1 and MSTRG.111162.1, encodes nuclear transcription factors [93], and its AS regulation affected trans-acting factors to influence gene expression.

Also, genes with different regulatory mechanisms can participate in common pathways of embryonic heat stress regulation and critical life activities. Numerous TDEGs and AS genes were gathered in protein processing such as endoplasmic reticulum and calcium signaling pathway, which synergistically regulated casp3 to express apoptotic proteins. Apoptotic proteins can act on lmnl3 to initiate apoptosis through the loss of nuclear membrane integrity [94]. Among them, casp3, traf2, perk, and ip3r were differentially expressed AS genes, and lmnl3 was present in all comparison groups (Fig. S11). The axon guidance pathway (Fig. S12) contains abundant activities of lncRNA targeting, alternative splicing, and differential gene expression. Genes involved in this pathway, such as slit1 and sema4d, may act on receptors (ephb and itfb1) and then participate in functions like axon repulsion through the MAPK signaling pathway during neural development [95]. Glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, especially the lacto and neolacto series, was crucial in embryonic development and influenced by a considerable number of TDEGs, TDAS genes, and target genes of TEDLs (Fig. S13). Similarly, transcriptional regulation of alternative splicing and lncRNAs were concurrently involved in the synthesis of many RNA polymerase subunits (Fig. S14), affecting the response of RNA transcription products to cellular demands [96]. In summary, genes can participate in the regulation of embryonic heat stress in different forms or cooperate on the same pathway, and diverse gene expression and regulatory mechanisms respond holistically to the complex thermal stress during common carp embryogenesis.

It follows that the potential effects of high temperature on alternative splicing and embryonic development are complex and multi-layered. The study highlights the contribution of different alternative splicing regulations to the thermal stress response during common carp embryogenesis, including the expression regulation of SAS and TDAS genes, differences at the subgenomic level, and synergistic regulation with lncRNAs. However, further detailed work and specific functional validation are still required, particularly in understanding the pathways related to hub TDAS genes. Higher-quality transcriptome sequencing and other advanced epigenetic metrics are expected to increase the depth of this study, such as third-generation sequencing, which can provide longer reads and more comprehensive views of the transcriptome.

Conclusions

In this study, we first explored the response of alternative splicing to thermal stress during common carp embryogenesis. Our findings revealed that AS genes and events were widely distributed in all stages during common carp embryogenesis, and the number continually increased with embryonic development. Developmental stage-specific AS genes were mainly involved in gene and environmental processing in the early stages (P1-P4), and mainly participated in molecular cellular processes and organ development in the later stages (P5-P8). The regulation of alternative splicing was more sensitive to high temperatures in the early stages of common carp embryo development. Thermal stress can cause a large number of differential splicing and differential expressions before zygotic genome activation, and the gastrula stage may determine whether the embryo survives or not. TDAS genes at various stages may regulate common carp embryogenesis at multiple levels, including transcriptional level regulation such as taf2 and med1, post-transcriptional level regulation such as srrm1 and hnrnpa1, and response to immune and environmental stresses such as cdk2, abi1, and drg2. Furthermore, the number of AS genes and events in subgenome B was generally higher than that in subgenome A, and the homologous AS genes were significantly enriched in many pathways about essential life activity, which complemented the possible explanations for common carp environmental adaptability. Ultimately, multiple regulation mechanisms, including lncRNAs and alternative splicing, could interact with each other to mutually enrich the mode of thermal stress regulation during common carp embryogenesis. Genes such as lmnl3 may regulate key pathways including apoptosis, embryonic development, and thermal stress under the combined effect of differential expression, DAS events, and lncRNA targeting. In summary, alternative splicing participates in various processes from biosynthesis to organogenesis during common carp embryogenesis, and thermal stress regulates gene splicing and expression of critical development stages. Our work complemented some gaps in the study of alternative splicing at both levels of common carp embryogenesis and thermal stress and contributed to the comprehension of environmental adaptation formation during polyploid fish embryogenesis.
