Selective head-and-neck cooling as a treatment method for concussions in elite male rugby union players: the Clinical Observed Outcomes with Local HEad-and-neck cooling After Diagnosed concussions (COOLHEAD) study protocol
Authors: James Brown, Michael Dunlop, Marelise Badenhorst, Cameron Owen, Niklas Marklund, Peter Andrews, Andrew Hay, Marco Bazo, Herman Rossouw, David Hanly, Craig Dyson, Daniel Jones, Keith Stokes, Simon P T Kemp, Matt Cross, Ben Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSports-related concussions (SRCs) typically occur when the brain is hyperthermic. Acute head-and-neck cooling should, therefore, reduce the brain’s metabolic demands, with the potential to improve recovery following an SRC. Elite ice hockey players who underwent head-and-neck cooling after sustaining a concussion (SRC) showed reduced return-to-play times, although further investigation is warranted. This paper aims to describe the methods proposed for investigating the clinical effects and feasibility of acute head-and-neck cooling in elite male rugby union players.A quasi-experimental study will be conducted in two professional male rugby competitions (clusters): the United Rugby Championship ‘intervention group’ and the PREM Rugby ‘standard care group’. Both groups will follow World Rugby’s standardised, graduated return-to-play concussion management protocols. In addition to this, within 30 min of the SRC, the intervention group will be offered head-and-neck cooling for 45 min. The quantitative phase of the study will collect return-to-play times and clinical outcomes in both groups (sample size calculated: 100 concussions per cohort). The qualitative phase will explore the experiences of players and medical teams with the intervention. Intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses, using appropriate regression modelling techniques, will adjust for possible confounders between the two groups, and thematic content analysis will be employed in the analysis of the respective phases.The Clinical Observed Outcomes with Local HEad-neck cooling After Diagnosed concussions study will provide evidence regarding acute head-and-neck cooling as a potential adjunct treatment to current concussion management in elite male rugby union.Keywords: Rugby, Male, Concussion

Introduction

Participation in contact sports is associated with a risk of sports-related concussion (SRC).1 Many of the pathophysiological processes involved in the acute, subacute and long-term consequences of head injuries are incompletely understood. It is widely accepted that the acute and subacute effects of a concussion are a result of biomechanically induced alterations in normal brain neurotransmitter and metabolic cascades, potentially involving axonal injury, blood flow changes and inflammation.1 An ‘energy crisis’ arises from a mismatch between the need for energy to restore these disruptions in the brain and a reduced ability to deliver or produce energy within the brain.2 3 The recovery and rehabilitation from an SRC has been identified as a priority area for future concussion research.1

In comparison to non-SRCs, SRCs occur when the brain is in a natural state of ‘hyperthermia’, as opposed to ‘normothermia’.4 5 The rise in core body and thus brain temperature is a fundamental consequence of the metabolic effect of physical activity. Core and brain temperatures of 39°C–40°C are commonplace in athletes, returning to resting temperatures in the hours postexertion.6,8 Temperature alone has a significant effect on many aspects of brain metabolism; for each 1°C increase in temperature, there is a 6%–10% increase in brain energy metabolism.9,11 The ‘detrimental metabolic synergy’, with potential clinical sequelae, of an SRC occurring at hyperthermia versus normothermia has only relatively recently been recognised.4 5 In animal studies of induced mild traumatic brain injuries, cognitive dysfunction was reduced if the hyperthermic injured subjects were acutely cooled postinjury to normothermia.4

Historically, in human medicine, whole body or ‘systemic cooling’ has been used as an adjunctive treatment modality for several clinical conditions, including severe traumatic brain injuries.11,15 Systemic cooling is a complex intensive care setting treatment that aims to bring patients’ core body temperature into, and maintain at, hypothermic temperatures for a period of time. Unfortunately, for several complex physiological and methodological reasons, there has been a lack of translation of the theoretical and animal-based beneficial effects of ‘systemic cooling’ into humans, and research in this field continues.11,15 However, a specific derivative of systemic cooling, ‘selective brain cooling’, has shown promising, early theoretical and clinical results across several clinical fields, including SRC.511 13 16,18 While the absolute levels of brain cooling are less than systemic cooling, the complications of systemic cooling are largely negated.5 11 13 16 Furthermore, many of the selective brain cooling modalities can be safely applied in a prehospital setting in conscious patients.11 13 16 Therefore, selective brain cooling is thought of by many as an alternative treatment modality for some clinical conditions.511 13 16,18

To date, the use of selective brain cooling, via direct head-and-neck cooling, as an acute treatment for SRC in elite sport has only been studied in one cohort.16 19 These studies in elite ice hockey not only demonstrated the treatment concept of augmented brain cooling on cessation of exercise-induced hyperthermia, but also a benefit of acute head-and-neck cooling (minimum 45 min cooling, initiated within 60 min of an SRC injury) in recovery. In comparison with the control group, the intervention group had fewer players who took an extended period of time (more than 14, 21, 28, 46 and 100 days) to return-to-play (RTP).16 Importantly, the device, PolarCap, was also found to be safe and well-tolerated, which is a consistent finding across studies that used similar devices in an acute setting.17 18 Although these findings are promising, they are based on a single study cohort, with acknowledged limitations.16 20 Thus, further research to build the evidence base around this potential acute concussion treatment is merited.

Elite rugby union has a relatively high rate of concussions and established standardised concussion recognition and graduated RTP (GRTP) protocols in place.21 22 This positions rugby union as an ideal environment to further investigate the effects of head-and-neck cooling in the acute management of SRC. Furthermore, the established professional male competitions globally allow the potential for well-structured intervention versus standard care trials. Comparable professional female competitions are yet to be established in women’s rugby union globally.

Aims and hypothesis

This protocol paper outlines the scientific rationale, study design, methodology and analysis of the Clinical Observed Outcomes with Local HEad-and-neck cooling After Diagnosed concussions study that will assess the effect of acute head-and-neck cooling in elite male rugby players who sustain an SRC on RTP time and clinical outcomes (symptom endorsement, cognitive assessment, balance assessment) during the first 72 hours, in comparison to standard SRC management.

We hypothesise that acute head-and-neck cooling initiated in the acute phase of an SRC injury will reduce RTP time (days lost due to injury) and reduce symptom endorsement, cognitive impairment and balance errors.

An additional aim of the study is to explore participants’ experiences with the intervention, as well as the barriers and facilitators to its implementation, to inform future interventions. Qualitative research can play a vital role in process evaluation by providing in-depth insights into individuals’ perspectives and experiences of interventions.23 Even if interventions are effective, their impact is diminished if they are not accepted and adopted by the relevant stakeholders.24 Understanding how stakeholders within the rugby system view and experience selective head-and-neck cooling may help us tailor interventions that address the needs of the target population.25

Methods

Design

A quasi-experimental study will be conducted in two professional male rugby competitions: the United Rugby Championship (URC; intervention group) and PREM Rugby (standard care group). The ‘clusters’ will be teams within the competitions. The URC was chosen as the intervention group due to the feasibility of a commercially available head-and-neck cooling device, PolarCap (figure 1), already being trialled. Thus, this is not a randomised controlled study, but a quasi-experimental study. Blinding is not possible in the context of this type of intervention. The PREM Rugby competition was chosen as the standard care group, due to it being a similar level of elite men’s rugby competition. Both competitions follow the same management protocols for the identification and management of SRC in elite rugby union, which are regulated by World Rugby. Thus, the intervention group will still receive standard care but has the additional option of acute head-and-neck cooling with the PolarCap.

PolarCap. (A) SmallCool, (B) PolarCap HeadCap, (C) Neoprene Cover.

A pragmatic, multimethod approach will be used to collect data in two phases (1) quantitative data collection of players’ clinical outcomes and RTP, in the intervention and standard care groups (quantitative study) and (2) qualitative data collection in the intervention group to explore players and the medical team’s experiences of the intervention as well as barriers and facilitators to implementation after completion of the competition (Qualitative study). Semistructured interviews will be used to collect qualitative data. The qualitative study will be informed by a pragmatic paradigm, which aligns with our goal of providing practical findings that may inform policy and practice.23

Sample size calculation

Quantitative study

The sample size was calculated using the following assumptions. This study employs a quasi-experimental design with hierarchical clustering. Each competition represents the overarching cluster (competition cluster), and the teams (team cluster) within each competition are nested within each competition cluster. The study power has been set at 80% and an alpha (type 1 error rate) of 0.05. Following an SRC, RTP time was the primary outcome used to calculate the sample size because (1) it is highly comparable between players and study arms and (2) a similar study in ice hockey assessed the effect on RTP time.

Publicly available RTP time (mean 19±4 days) from the PREM Rugby was used. No such data were available for the URC, so it was assumed that both competitions were similar. It was calculated that 40 SRCs were required in each group to achieve an expected reduction of 5 days in RTP time. In case the mean or SD was different in the URC, the required sample was increased to 100 SRCs in each group. In the 2024/2025 season, the PREM had 69 concussions in 93 matches (18.6 concussions per 1000 hours). In the same season, the URC had 99 concussions in 151 matches (16.3 concussions per 1000 hours). It was assumed that 50% of SRCs in the URC would use the PolarCap System, providing ~50 ‘cooled’ SRCs per season and meeting the required 100 SRCs in the Intervention group over two seasons. PREM Rugby (standard care group) should produce ~140 SRCs over two seasons, which should exceed the number required for the standard care group (also 100 SRCs).

Qualitative study

At least two players (n=32) and a medical staff member (n=16) from each team in the intervention group will be invited to participate. Data saturation is not a universally useful concept for all types of qualitative research.26 Instead, our focus will be on maximising variation in sampling and conducting an iterative analysis of the collected data.27

Participants and procedure

If a player is involved in an event in which an SRC is diagnosed or with the potential for an SRC, they are removed from the field and enter World Rugby’s Head Injury Assessment (HIA) protocol.22 The HIA protocol is a four-stage process used in elite adult teams (figure 2).

Head Injury Assessment (HIA) protocol for assessment of head impacts with the potential for concussion. Dashed arrows are when players enter the HIA process after a match: if they present soon after the match, they enter at HIA2, otherwise they enter at HIA3.

The first three HIA assessments (HIA1-3) are adapted from the Sports Concussion Assessment Tool and involve a trained medical doctor assessing symptoms, balance and cognitive modes.28 Players are identified by either team doctors, pitch-side medical personnel, match officials or the independent match-day doctor. Identification can occur through direct observation, video review or instrumented mouthguard data. Players who display obvious visible signs of SRC (termed as criteria 1 signs; table 1) are removed from play, diagnosed as concussed and do not require full completion of the off-field HIA1 screen assessment. Players classified as criteria 2 cases (table 1) are removed for a full off-field HIA1 assessment. Players classified as criteria 2 can either pass or fail the off-field assessment according to the medical doctor performing the assessment. Players who pass are allowed to return to the match, while those who fail are not permitted to return. Importantly, for players classified as criteria 2, the HIA1 is not diagnostic, and they will only be diagnosed with an SRC if they are deemed to be concussed at subsequent assessments (HIA2—post-match or HIA3 within 72 hours). The HIA3 is performed within 72 hours post-head-impact event, to identify a later diagnosis of concussion and to assess clinical progress.

Intervention

Device description

The PolarCap consists of an energy control unit, SmallCool (figure 1A), a soft and tight-fitting silicone HeadCap (figure 1B) and a Neoprene Cover (figure 1B). The tank of SmallCool is filled with coolant, and the HeadCap is connected to the SmallCool unit (figure 1). Once the power is turned on, the SmallCool pushes temperature-controlled coolant through the HeadCap. This results in heat exchange between the HeadCap and the player’s skin. The patented silicone-based HeadCap is moulded to ensure the best fit and is available in different sizes. Silicone is a substance that retains its properties even when chilled, and it is adaptable and tolerated by most people with only a small risk of hypersensitivity. HeadCap consists of a non-sterile, biocompatible silicone material and contains no latex. It is delivered prefilled, and no filling or purging is necessary before or after treatment. It is designed without any adhesive connection between the silicone and the patient’s skin and can easily be lifted during treatment if the operator needs access to covered body parts. A thermostat and its temperature probe control the coolant temperature. PolarCap maintains a controlled coolant temperature during the entire treatment period. The system automatically adjusts any deviations from the default temperature. The cooling process of SmallCool is set to keep the coolant in the tank at 0°C±1°C. The cooling unit (figure 1A) is a class IIa medical device with Conformité Européenne (CE) certification.

Eligibility

Inclusion criteria

Elite male rugby players participating in the United Rugby Championship (URC) or PREM Rugby (formerly known as Gallagher Premiership or the Premiership) during the 2025/2026 and 2026/2027 seasons who are identified with a potential or diagnosed sports-related concussion (SRC) during a match.

Exclusion criteria

Players who choose not to participate in the study or are deemed too unwell to receive cooling treatment by their medical team will be excluded from the study. This would include players who require immediate referral to a hospital, in keeping with the SCAT6 Red Flag criteria.28 In the intervention group, the player will be excluded from the study if the cooling treatment does not start within 30 min of the incident time or is interrupted during the 45 min treatment. A player who presents after the match (delayed SRC presentation) will be excluded from the match.

While prior studies with PolarCap and other selective head-and-neck cooling devices have not found any safety concerns,16,19 any adverse events will be documented during the application of the device. Adverse events will be defined as unintended disease or injury, or clinical signs, whether related to the use of PolarCap or not. This may include any signs, symptoms or conditions that were not recorded as pre-existing at baseline, or a worsening of a pre-existing condition.18 In addition, serious adverse events will be documented (hospitalisation or any instance of serious deterioration in health/development of concussion red flags28 once PolarCap was applied). As is routine in these competitions, any medical issues will be overseen by the player’s medical staff. Should any team within the ‘standard care’ arm be using PolarCap, they will be excluded from the analyses. Use of the PolarCap within the standard care arm will be confirmed through communication with the company.

Study procedure

Quantitative study

In the intervention group, a player classified as criteria 1, or a criteria 2 who fails the HIA1, will be eligible for cooling treatment (intervention) (figure 3). The medical staff of these players, who will receive PolarCap training in the competition preseason, will supervise cooling. Criteria 2 players who pass the HIA1 will be excluded from the study, as they would have returned to play and would not be able to commence cooling. Following cooling, the intervention group will receive standard care as part of the HIA protocol. The cooling intervention must be continued for at least 45 min but should not be repeated before RTP.

Qualitative study

Purposive sampling will be used to recruit players who participated in the URC and who either received treatment or declined to receive treatment. In addition, the medical team staff involved in the URC will be invited to participate. Semistructured interviews will be conducted with the medical team, online via Zoom, at the end of the URC competition, at a time convenient to them. Players in the intervention arm will be invited to participate in online interviews within 2 weeks of their HIA4. All interviews will be audio-recorded.

Data analysis

Quantitative study

Descriptive statistics will be calculated for all measured parameters. All outcomes (primary and secondary) will be compared between the intervention and standard care group using both p values and effect sizes from appropriate modelled comparison data. Interim analyses will be conducted after the first season of data collection (approximately July 2026), including primary and secondary outcomes, as well as any adverse events. These will be discussed and analysed by the study Steering Committee only.

For the intention-to-treat analysis, all SRCs that occur in players who have consented to participate in the study, regardless of whether they used the treatment as prescribed, will be included. If an entire team (cluster) from the intervention arm (URC) does not consent to the study, then this team (cluster) will be added to the standard care group.

For the per-protocol analysis, only players who complete the intervention as prescribed will be included in the intervention arm. As with the intention-to-treat analysis, any players in the standard care group who use selective cooling to treat their SRC must be excluded from the analysis.

Primary outcome: RTP time (in days)

Both the mean RTP time and the proportion of players with RTP>21 days will be compared between players in the intervention and standard care groups using a regression model, with the distribution to be selected based on the data-generating process. An RTP of 21 days was selected because the aforementioned elite ice hockey study found the largest effect of cooling in SRCs that took a longer time to recover. Pragmatically, a 21-day RTP is a concussion risk factor within World Rugby’s HIA protocol for the management of any subsequent SRC. We hypothesise that the intervention group will be associated with a lower mean RTP time or a smaller proportion of players with RTP>21 days than in the standard care group. All potential confounders from the demographic data (eg, age) and medical history data (eg, number of previous SRCs), as well as the permitted RTP pathway (7 or 12 days; table 2), will be considered to form an appropriate adjustment set to reduce bias. Referring back to the sample size analysis and based on expert opinion (experienced medical staff in rugby), a 25% reduction in the mean number of days to RTP (eg, a mean of 19 days in the standard care group and 14 days in the intervention group) will be considered clinically relevant regardless of the statistical analysis. A sensitivity analysis will compare the RTP time of concussed players who do not agree to be cooled with the RTP time of the standard care players’ concussions. We hypothesise that the RTP times of these two groups should be similar, as these are comparable cohorts.

Secondary outcome: symptom endorsement and severity, cognitive impairment and balance errors at HIA2 and HIA3 (relative to baseline)

Depending on the data-generating process of the specific outcome, these variables will be compared using Bayesian and/or regular inferential statistics. All the same covariates as for the primary outcome, as well as the specific test’s baseline score and time between HIA1, HIA2 and HIA3 assessments, will be added to the model as covariates. In most concussed rugby players, these secondary outcomes tend to worsen from baseline to HIA2/3. We hypothesise that, compared with the standard care group, the worsening in these outcomes will be attenuated in the intervention group. As part of the secondary outcome, we will also compare the proportion of players who are ultimately diagnosed as ‘not concussed’ after failing their HIA1 test (figure 3 asterisk). We hypothesise that the % of players in this category (HIA1 failed+diagnosis=‘not concussed’) will be lower in the treatment group, as the treatment will improve the clinical outcomes assessed in HIA2 and HIA3. In the rare case that an HIA3 assessment is conducted sooner than 24 hours after the event or more than 7 days after the inciting event, that HIA3 will be excluded from the analyses.

Qualitative study

Tertiary outcome: Audio recordings will be professionally transcribed. NVivo V.15 (Lumivero, Colorado) will be used to organise data. Thematic content analysis will be used to analyse the data.29 Two coders will code transcripts to generate discussion around codes and their meaning. Team discussions will be used to explore codes, categories and potential themes within the data. Themes will be considered in relation to the coded data extracts and the overall dataset. A thematic map will be developed, and the generated themes will be reviewed in regular research team meetings until the team is satisfied that the themes contain a comprehensive description of the data. We will adopt a relativist perspective towards rigour and trustworthiness, which recognises that the criteria for evaluation are study-specific and influenced by context.27

Consent

Written informed consent will be collected by the medical teams for all players in the intervention group, as well as for medical team staff willing to participate, prior to each season or treatment (eg, when a new player joins a team). Data for the standard care group are routinely collected in accordance with the World Rugby standard HIA protocol. If any specific player opts out of the HIA protocol (or the associated data for research), this specific player’s data are excluded from any export from World Rugby’s database. At the time of writing this protocol, there were no players from these two competitions who had opted out. Consent forms will be securely stored for a period of 5 years after the study’s completion.

Conclusions

Cooling of an athlete’s hyperthermic brain to normothermia after an SRC should reduce its metabolic demands and potentially the clinical sequelae of the injury. Based on this scientific rationale, this protocol paper outlines the methods proposed to investigate the clinical effects and feasibility of acute head-and-neck cooling in elite male rugby union players. Such transparency is especially important for studies of medical devices in the industry. The results of this study will be directly applicable to this mode of cooling in elite male rugby union. The findings will substantially add to the prior limited evidence base and thus enhance potential applicability to other sports that manage SRC. The study will also act as a methodological benchmark for future related studies.

Patient and public involvement

This project was conceptualised by MD, who is a medical advisor to the URC, and the team doctor of Edinburgh Rugby. MD facilitated a meeting in which previous authors of a study that investigated the PolarCap device were present along with experienced sports physicians and experts in both systemic and selective cooling. Six URC teams made use of PolarCap during the 2024/2025 season and their feedback was considered. MD and JB have also discussed the study concept with the medical department of World Rugby and World Rugby agreed that the data collected during the HIA protocol could be analysed as part of this proposed study. Regardless of the study results, all findings will be communicated via relevant conferences and meetings open to the public, as well as submitted to peer-reviewed journals for publication.
