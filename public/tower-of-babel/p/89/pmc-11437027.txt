Population structure and demographic history of two highly-trafficked species of pangolin in the Congo Basin
Authors: Iva Bernáthová, Markéta Swiacká, Loubassou Castella bath Shéba Vitel, Jen C. Tinsman, Pavel Hulva, Barbora Černá Bolfíková
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
African pangolins are hunted for their meat and for use in local traditional medicine, as well as for their scales, which are trafficked internationally, especially to growing Asian markets. Pangolin’s population genetic structure can be used to trace the geographic origins of trafficked scales, but substantial sampling gaps across pangolins’ ranges hinder these efforts. In this study, we documented population structure and dynamics in the two species of African pangolin, the white-bellied pangolin (Phataginus tricuspis) and the giant pangolin (Smutsia gigantea) in the underexplored Republic of Congo. Using the mitochondrial control region and two nuclear markers (beta-fibrinogen and titin), we identified high genetic diversity in both species. We document a distinct mitochondrial lineage of the white-bellied pangolin, which was most likely shaped by river barriers together with dynamics of forest refugia related to the climatic shifts during the Pleistocene. We detected population growth in the white-bellied pangolin coinciding with a dry period during the Pleistocene, suggesting some ability for this typically forest-dwelling species to persist under diverse environmental conditions. Using landscape genetics, we found all but one of the pangolins we sampled at bush meat markets originated locally. A single individual appeared to have been imported to Congo from Cameroon. These findings significantly contribute to our understanding of pangolin population biology and local trade dynamics. In addition, our data from a previously unstudied part of pangolins’ ranges will help us to better understand international wildlife trafficking patterns and to target conservation and protection strategies for these highly vulnerable species.

Introduction

Pangolins are the most trafficked wild mammals in the world1. Their scales and meat are in high demand especially in Asian countries2–5. Tens of tons of pangolin scales, representing hundreds of thousands of individuals, are illegally traded every year, often along with other wildlife products, to supply that demand6,7. As the numbers of Asian pangolin populations began to decline, poachers turned their attention to African species3,6, which have been traditionally hunted for local bushmeat consumption8–10 or for cultural and medicinal practices11–14. As a consequence of this overexploitation and habitat loss, all four species of African pangolins have been listed as either Vulnerable or Endangered on the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List15.

Despite the attention the pangolin trade receives, our knowledge of their population biology, especially in African species, remains poor16. However, assessments of population genetic diversity and demographic trends are essential for effective protection strategies and management plans16. Pangolin’s habitat preferences and naturally low densities make them difficult to monitor using traditional survey methods such as camera traps or transect method of sampling17–20 Molecular data can address gaps in our understanding of population biology and the current population status of pangolins21. Most genetic studies of pangolins are based on confiscated samples and are typically focused on species determination and evaluation of the geographic origin of traded animals22–29.

Studies assigning trafficked animals to their geographic origins depend entirely on our understanding of wild population genetic structure30. A phylogeographic study of the white-bellied pangolin (Phataginus tricuspis) identified six cryptic geographic lineages (Central African, Gabon, Western central African, Western African, Ghana, and Dahomey Gap lineages) with non-overlapping distributions, which have been used to trace the origin of illegally traded pangolins27,29,31. However, there are large gaps in sampling across this pangolin’s distribution, leaving the exact boundaries between some of these populations unknown. These omissions could introduce bias into the results when tracing the origin of poached animals. They also leave questions about the evolutionary history, uniqueness, and origins of these populations unanswered.

Within this study, we focused on a crucial gap between two of the cryptic geographic lineages, the Central African and West Central African lineages, in the Republic of the Congo (RoC), a country which was not included in previous studies. The RoC is situated at the edge of the Congo River Basin and sits between several major Pleistocene refugia, making it a site of rich, but often cryptic, biodiversity32,33. Ecological and genetic data for pangolins from the area are missing completely, and the population statuses of the three resident pangolin species are unknown. These species are the white-bellied pangolin, the black-bellied pangolin (P. tetradactyla) and the giant pangolin (Smutsia gigantea). White-bellied pangolin is the most abundant, and also the most traded species3,12. Where it has been studied, its numbers are estimated to be declining3,34. This species inhabits lowland forests and forest mosaics35–37 but also occurs in human-cultivated areas such as plantations and secondary forests38,39. It is predominantly nocturnal and forages on the ground and in trees for termites and ants35. In comparison, the giant pangolin is a terrestrial species inhabiting forests, forest-savannah mosaics and wooded savannah. The species is almost exclusively nocturnal40, and feeds on ants and termites41. Pangolin populations in the RoC are under constantly increasing hunting pressure because their meat is highly desired, and they are also used for spiritual or medicinal purposes12,42,43. This study aimed to provide insight into pangolin population structure and phylogeography, and into local pangolin trade dynamics.

Here, we use a combination of matrilinear and biparental genetic markers to evaluate the population structure and demographic parameters of two African pangolin species: the white-bellied pangolin and the giant pangolin. Our goals were to (i) clarify the phylogeographic history of pangolins in a poorly studied region, (ii) compare the population genetic parameters and trends between the two species, and (iii) evaluate the impact of hunting and local trade on pangolin populations at the genetic level.

Material and methods

Sampling and genotyping

Sampling was conducted in the rural settlements in the northern RoC (particularly targeting the surroundings of the Odzala-Kokoua National Park (OKNP)) and the main market in the capital city Brazzaville (Fig. 1). Samples were collected from local people associated with hunting and the bush meat trade in the RoC from May to June 2018 and in February 2022. In compliance with ethical guidelines, samples were provided voluntarily without any financial or other rewards. In total, we obtained 114 scales, 17 tissue samples (tongue or oesophagus) and three buccal swabs. The majority of the samples were taken from dead animals on the markets or in the villages except for one buccal swab, which was taken from a living individual. No animals were sacrificed for this study. Tissues and swabs were fixed in 96% ethanol; scales were dried and placed in plastic bags with silica gel. After transportation to the laboratory, all samples were stored in a refrigerator at − 20 °C.

Export (Nos. CG1125877, CG1125874, CG1125875 and CG1126091) and import (Nos. 18CZ028796 and 22CZ033624) CITES permits and veterinary permit (Nos. 3009329 and SVS/2022/094207-G) were obtained for all samples. Samples were stored and processed in the molecular genetics laboratory of the Faculty of Tropical AgriSciences, Czech University of Life Sciences Prague which has approval (No CZ 11712934) for storage and usage of animal material according to Section 48(1)(i) of Act No 166/1999s concerning veterinary care and amending certain related laws, as amended, pursuant to Article 17(1) of Regulation of the European Parliament and the Council (EC) No 169/2009 and Article 27(1) of Commission Regulation (EU) No 142/2011.

Genomic DNA from scales and tissues was extracted using the DNeasy Blood&Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). When extracting from the scales, a small Section (10–20 mg) was cut off from the base. If the scale tailing was available, extraction from that area was preferred. The piece of skin or keratin tissue was cut into smaller pieces and transferred to the tissue lysis buffer. For scales, 30 μl of dithiothreitol (DTT) was added during tissue lysis to enhance keratin lysis. DNA from buccal swabs was extracted using the Presto Buccal Swab gDNA Extraction Kit (GenAid).

In order to evaluate phylogeographic patterns and fine-scale population genetic structure, a mitochondrial region with high mutation rate and two polymorphic nuclear markers, to compensate for maternal inheritance of mtDNA, were selected. Two primer pairs were used to produce two mtDNA amplicons of the mtDNA control region (CR) with overlapping ends, i.e. DL44 and B123, using primer pairs PNG_DloopF and PNG_DloopR44 and L15997uni and panDH1597223, respectively. Intron 7 of beta-fibrinogen (FBG) gene was amplified using primers FBG7PangF and FBG7PangR31 and fragments of titin gene (TTN) using primers ManTTN3571F and ManTTN4272R31 Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) protocols for B1, FBG and TTN were modified from Gaubert et al.31. Annealing temperatures were as follows: 54–57 °C (B1), 56 °C (FBG and TTN). PCR conditions for DL fragments were adapted from Hsieh et al.23, annealing temperature was 56–57 °C.

The effectiveness of PCR reactions was verified using gel electrophoresis. Successfully amplified fragments were purified using Invisorb Fragment CleanUp Kit (Invitek Molecular GMBH) and sequenced using the BigDye Terminator v.3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit and analysed on a 3130 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Prague, Czech Republic). Raw sequences were edited and aligned in Geneious 10.2.6 (www.geneious.com) software using Clustal W45. The nuclear haplotypes were phased using Phase 2.1.146,47 software, the procedure was run separately for each species. CR, FBG and TTN sequences from the RoC were collapsed to haplotypes, and the haplotype data were submitted to GenBank (Supplementary Table S1).

Analyses of genetic variability and demographic analyses

The Templeton-Crandall-Sing (TCS) haplotype networks were calculated using TCS 1.2148 and visualized in TCS Beautifier49 for each species. Our novel RoC white-bellied pangolin CR sequences were compared to 104 previously published and georeferenced white-bellied pangolin CR sequences (GenBank accession numbers KX642071–KX642171; MG196296-MG196298; MF536683), 94 FBG sequences (Accession numbers KX642173-KX642267) and 95 TTN sequences (Accession numbers KX642269-KX642364; JN632925). To analyse the landscape genetic architecture, a spatial model-based clustering approach was employed using Geneland 4.0.450 software. Geneland assumes multinomial distribution of haplotypes and performs Bayesian clustering to generate Dirichlet cells centred on the ranges of these subpopulations. The parameters for the Geneland analysis were set as follows: 500,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations with thinning set to 100, five independent runs, and K values ranging from 1 to 10. The posterior probability of subpopulation membership for the most supported run was calculated for each pixel of the spatial domain, using a burn-in period of 50 iterations. An uncorrelated Frequency Model with an uncertainty on coordinate value = 2 was selected for the analysis. The average number of base differences per site (p-distances) among the Geneland clusters were calculated in MEGA1151. For the giant pangolin, only four comparative mitochondrial sequences were available (Accession numbers MF536684, MG196303, MG196301 and NC_036064). The giant pangolin was therefore excluded from haplotype network calculations based on FBG and TTN genes and from analysis in Geneland.

Summary statistics of genetic variability for CR samples from the RoC were calculated in DnaSp652. These included the number of haplotypes (Nh), haplotype diversity (Hd), number of polymorphic sites (Np), nucleotide diversity (π), raggedness statistics (r) and neutrality tests—Tajima’s D (D), R2, Fu's Fs (Fs). The statistical significance of neutrality was tested against the Standard Neutral Model (SNM). For nuclear markers, the following parameters were calculated in DnaSp652 the number of haplotypes (Nh), haplotype diversity (Hd) and nucleotide diversity (π).

To determine whether the RoC pangolins have experienced demographic or geographical expansion in the recent past, the mismatch distribution tests were assessed in DnaSP652. Population size changes in time for each species based on CR were estimated using coalescent-based Bayesian skyline plots (BSPs), calculated in BEAST 1.10.453, using sequences from this study and GenBank data. The substitution model of nucleotides was determined by jModelTest254,55. In both species, a strict molecular clock and HKY + gamma substitution model were used. For clock calibration, the black-bellied pangolin (P. tetradactyla) (Accession No. MF509825) was used as an outgroup for the white-bellied pangolin and Temminck’s pangolin (S. temminckii) (Accession No. MF536687) as an outgroup for the giant pangolin. We used divergence times from Gauber et al.56, (i.e., 13.3 Mya between white-bellied pangolin/black-bellied pangolin and 9.8 Mya between giant pangolin/Temminck’s pangolin). The MCMC procedure was run five times for each species with 10,000,000 iterations. Genealogy and model parameters were stored every 1,000 iterations, and the burn-in period was set to 1,000,000. The effective sample sizes (ESSs) were checked in Tracer 1.7.257 to be > 200. Log and tree files were concatenated using Logcombiner 2.6.758 and the results were then visualised in Tracer 1.7.257.

Using CR sequences of white-bellied pangolins, the relationship between genetic and geographic distances was examined via Genetic Landscape Shape Analysis in Alleles in Space 1.0 (AIS)59. A graph from the interpolated distances was created, where peaks represent samples with high genetic distances. The analysis was performed for the whole range of the species and for the RoC separately. The giant pangolin was excluded from the Genetic Landscape Shape Analysis due to small sample size.

Results

We sampled 112 white-bellied pangolins and 22 giant pangolins in the RoC. We obtained a total of 83 sequences of CR, of which 39 were 788 bp and 44 were 370 bp long (Supplementary Table S1). As for nuclear markers, we obtained 23 (354 bp) and 17 (533 bp) sequences of FBG and TTN (respectively) from white-bellied pangolin individuals (Supplementary Table S1). From the giant pangolin, we used 15 (779 bp), 11 (331 bp) and 4 (546 bp) sequences of CR, FBG and TTN (respectively) for further analyses (Supplementary Table S1). Sequence data have been deposited in the GenBank with the accession numbers that can be found in Table S1 in Supplementary information.

Population genetic structure of the species

The TCS haplotype network of the white-bellied pangolin (Fig. 2) using mtDNA CR showed a relatively complex topology with 95 haplotypes. The majority of individuals from the RoC did not share haplotypes with individuals from other phylogeographic lineages. One exception was a sample from Nakoaka in the northern RoC, which shared a haplotype with samples from the Yaoundé market and Bipindi in Cameroon (yellow in Fig. 2). This haplotype has five mutation steps to the closest haplotype from the RoC. Gabonian lineage (red in Fig. 2) represented by a single individual was closely related to haplotypes from RoC. Rest of haplotypes originating in Gabon were assigned to the Western central African lineage (yellow in Fig. 2). Haplotypes from the RoC (dark blue in Fig. 2) were connected to Central African haplotypes (purple in Fig. 2). The TCS haplotype network of the nuclear FBG gene (Supplementary Fig. S1a) indicated 12 haplotypes. The majority of the individuals from the RoC formed a discrete cluster of haplotypes. Nine individuals shared haplotypes with samples originating from Central Africa, and three specimens shared haplotypes with individuals from Western Africa/Dahomey Gap/Ghana. Analysis of the TTN gene (Supplementary Fig. S1b) indicated six haplotypes. Demographic histories of pangolins in the RoC

In white-bellied pangolins from RoC, we detected 50 different mitochondrial CR haplotypes in 83 individuals. This is relatively high observed haplotype diversity compared to other small mammals60 however, nucleotide diversity was low (Table 1). CR population neutrality tests reached negative values indicating population expansion (Table 1). The FBG dataset contained 12 haplotypes, and the TTN dataset indicated only 4 haplotypes. Haplotype diversity was high for FBG, and nucleotide diversity was low. Similarly, TTN exhibited moderate haplotype diversity and low nucleotide diversity. Two population neutrality tests on the FBG data, Tajima’s D and Fu’s Fs also indicated population expansion, but only Fu’s Fs was statistically significant (Table 1). Mismatch distribution analysis showed unimodal distribution in all three markers (Supplementary Fig. S3a–c), which indicates population expansion. For giant pangolins, we obtained 15 mitochondrial CR sequences and found eight haplotypes. Giant pangolin’s CR haplotype diversity was slightly lower than in the white-bellied pangolin, and nucleotide diversity was also low, indicating recent population expansion. Population neutrality tests also tended to indicate population expansion, but were not statistically significant (Table 1). The FBG gene exhibited eight haplotypes, while the coding region of TTN was monotypic (Table 1). FBG haplotype diversity was moderate in the giant pangolin, and nucleotide diversity was low. Descriptive statistics were not calculated for the TTN gene in the giant pangolin dataset due to the low number of sequences and the single haplotype found. Discussion

This research documented genetic variation and demographic history in an unstudied portion of two pangolin species’ ranges. We detected a differentiated mitochondrial lineage in white-bellied pangolins in the Republic of Congo (RoC) region which may improve efforts to trace the geographic origins of the illegal wildlife trade27,29,61.

The Congo lineage we described is a geographic neighbour to the Gabon, Western central and Central African lineages, as described by Gaubert et al.31. Mitochondrial haplotype network results (Fig. 2) reflect this proximity. However, Gabon lineage was not recognized in spatial genetic analyses (Fig. 3). Possible reasons could include the underrepresentation of this region in the analyses, as this lineage was represented by only a single individual, while the other samples from Gabon were assigned to the Western central African lineage. Further sampling and investigation of the original Gabon lineage proposed by Gaubert et al.31 is needed to confirm its occurrence in Gabon. The nuclear network provided lower resolution due to biparental inheritance; however, we still detected signal supporting the unique position of the Congo lineage (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Eastern boundaries of the Congo lineage are likely formed by the Congo and Ubangi Rivers, which separate our study area from the Central African lineage. Western boundaries and the strong barrier to gene flow between the Congo and Western central African lineages may have been facilitated by allopatric scenarios during dry interpluvial periods of the Pleistocene, in which forested areas contracted into disjunct refugia32,62. Sharp differentiation between these two regions was also observed in Tinsman et al.63 using whole-genome data. The clustering in this paper followed the banks of Ogooue and Ivindo Rivers. The idea that multiple factors contributed to the diversification of these lineages aligns with the refuge-river hypothesis, which explains the differentiation of several forest-dwelling taxa in the region33,64. Further sampling efforts are however needed to fully understand the geographic distribution of the Congo lineage.

The mitochondrial haplotypes of the Congo lineage are not shared with other known lineages, except a single sample which belonged to the Western central African lineage and clustered with specimens from Cameroon. This sample was from Nakoaka, a northwestern locality in our study area with a direct road connection to Cameroon (Fig. 1). It is likely that this animal was imported to the RoC, indicating one route for regional pangolin smuggling.

Necropsy sampling from hunted animals represents alternative to biopsies, which are not only cost effective, but most importantly reduce the necessity of ad hoc capture and handling, which always carries risk to the subject and is particularly stressful for pangolins65. However, one issue facing all studies relying on donated samples is the potential for bias due to the uncertain geographic origin of the samples. Our Genetic Landscape Shape Analysis revealed low genetic distances among pangolins from the RoC and from most of the localities around the OKNP, indicating that our samples originated near each other (Fig. 6). We also tested the samples from 2018 and 2022 separately, to ensure consistency in the observed patterns. With the notable exception of one sample from Nakoaka, we therefore assume that the pangolins collected in the villages around OKNP originated locally, similar to pangolins sold in bushmeat and traditional medicine markets in other African countries66.

Both the white-bellied and giant pangolins in our study exhibited high levels of haplotype diversity likely linked to their low mobility and high territoriality19,36 and landscape fragmentation, whether caused by climatic shifts or human activities67. Our findings mirror previous studies of the white-bellied pangolin29,31 and of the ground-dwelling Temminck’s pangolin in South Africa68. Although the overall population may tend to have high diversity, the hypervariable mtDNA marker and two nuclear markers used in this study do not fully reflect very recent changes to populations caused by anthropogenic pressure. For example, the Dahomey Gap lineage of white-bellied pangolins has low levels of genetic diversity and experienced a decline in effective population size approximately 200–500 years ago, likely due to overexploitation66.

High haplotype diversity in combination with low nucleotide diversity might also point to recent demographic or geographic population expansion. In the RoC white-bellied pangolins, recent effective population growth is supported by the analysis of mismatch distribution, neutrality tests, and Bayesian skyline plots. The expansion began approximately 500,000 years ago, coinciding with increased aridity and a reduction of forest cover in Africa69. This pattern is rather unexpected for a species that is typically associated with forest habitats and large trees36,38. The white-bellied pangolin may be capable of greater ecological flexibility than expected from a species that is seldom documented outside rainforest, (but see70). This flexibility is also indicated by their resilience in human-altered habitats1,20,36,38. The expansion pattern could also be region-specific, as illustrated by a star-like phylogeny in mitochondrial haplogroup from Western and Central Africa, contrasting with larger average haplotype distances in other haplogroups (Fig. 2).

In contrast to the white-bellied pangolin, the giant pangolin population did not display any signs of population size increase. Indeed, the Bayesian skyline plot indicated a slight decrease in the effective population size, with a wide confidence interval that could not reject neutral evolution, perhaps because of our small sample size. Similarly, because of the lack of comparative data for the giant pangolin, our assessment of the evolutionary history and population structure of this species was limited. Our preliminary work suggests high genetic diversity in giant pangolins in the RoC, but data from other localities are urgently needed to assess the status of this species.

Currently, we know that their large body size and terrestrial habit make giant pangolins particularly vulnerable to overexploitation39,41,71. They are also experiencing a recent increase in hunting pressure3,7. Our low sample size may be a result of hunters encountering the species less often due to population decline, or alternately that hunters sell the giant pangolin scales right away due to the high price they fetch in the illicit trade7,12. The majority of the samples came from the western periphery of the park typical by savannah, which may be more suitable habitat for giant pangolin. If that is the case, this area may have denser populations of giant pangolin and more trade in this species40,72. Alternatively, it could be that better quality roads along the eastern and northern edges of OKNP enable more efficient logistics to markets and thus higher profit12.

In conclusion, we identified a unique mitochondrial lineage of the white-bellied pangolin, with implications for tracing the illegal wildlife trade. Previous studies of confiscated pangolins may have failed to correctly localize individuals trafficked from the RoC due to a lack of available comparative data. Our samples will enable researchers to more accurately describe international trade in the white-bellied pangolin and to determine the extent to which pangolins from the Congo Basin are trafficked internationally. Our work highlights the importance of assessing population genetic structure across species’ ranges for understanding local, regional, and global trade dynamics, especially for taxa where cryptic diversity is probable.

We also began to unravel the unique evolutionary histories of pangolins in the Congo Basin. White-bellied pangolins and giant pangolins responded differently to climatic oscillations during the Late Pleistocene. Despite being considered predominantly a rainforest species, the white-bellied pangolin experienced population growth during a dry period, while the giant pangolin exhibited a non-significant trend of population decline. These findings indicate differing abilities to persist in human-modified landscapes between the two species. Our findings underscore the need for tailored conservation measures in both rural and urban areas to protect these vulnerable animals.
