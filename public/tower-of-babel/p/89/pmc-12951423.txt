Intraoperative seizure in the setting of tumor resection: illustrative cases
Authors: Thomas McCaffery, Josephine Buclez, Hithardhi Duggireddy, Razan R Faraj, Karen Salmeron- Moreno, Gustavo Pradilla, Tomas Garzon-Muvdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIntraoperative seizures pose a serious threat to patients undergoing neurosurgical procedures, potentially disrupting cases and increasing postoperative complications. While established risk factors, such as history of seizures and tumor location, are well documented, emerging evidence indicates hypocapnia, induced by controlled hyperventilation, may further reduce seizure threshold. Nevertheless, clinical data directly linking hyperventilation to intraoperative seizure incidence remain sparse in the current literature.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the cases of 3 patients with skull base tumors who experienced intraoperative seizures during elective craniotomy. In all cases, end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) levels fell below 27 mm Hg for extended periods. Seizure activity only resolved following EtCO2 normalization and anticonvulsant administration. Notably, the patients had surgeries where physiological levels of EtCO2 were maintained, resulting in successful surgeries with no seizure activity. All tumors were located at the skull base and involved main CNS arteries, raising the possibility that altered cerebral perfusion may have contributed to increased seizure susceptibility under hypocapnic conditions.LESSONSThese cases highlight hypocapnia as a potentially modifiable risk factor for intraoperative seizures and underscore the need for judicious management. Given the risk of seizure induction under reduced cerebral blood flow, strict hyperventilation guidelines should be followed when tumors are in critical vascular territories. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25585 Keywords: intraoperative seizure, tumor resection, hyperventilation, skull baseABBREVIATIONS: CBF = cerebral blood flow, EtCO2 = end-tidal CO2, ICA = internal carotid artery, PaCO2 = partial pressure of CO2, PCA = posterior cerebral artery, SCA = superior cerebellar artery

Intraoperative seizures are an uncommon yet disruptive intraoperative complication during craniotomies and pose significant risks to patient outcomes. The incidence of intraoperative seizures in elective supratentorial craniotomies ranges from 1% to 5%, but increases to 13% when motor evoked potential monitoring is used, especially in awake craniotomies.1–3 These complications are associated with premature termination of surgery, invalidation of functional brain mapping, and increased postoperative morbidity, including motor deficits and increased hospital stays.4,5 Thus, careful exploration of potential triggers and effective preventive measures must be studied. Historically, risk factors for developing intraoperative seizures include prior seizure history, high-grade histology, and tumor localization to the temporal or frontal lobe; however, recent studies have pointed to nonphysiological perioperative partial pressure of CO2 (PaCO2) levels as an additional risk factor of intraoperative seizures due to increased neuronal excitability.6

Controlled hyperventilation is a technique used by neurosurgeons during craniotomies to induce cerebrovascular constriction, thereby reducing cerebral blood flow (CBF), leading to the reduction of intracranial volume. Operative conditions (e.g., operative corridor) can be improved by reducing brain bulk and intracranial pressure; however, this is not without risk.7 While controlled hyperventilation can be utilized to mitigate the deleterious effects of increased intracranial pressure, the ensuing hypocapnia and respiratory alkalosis in this setting may generate a pro-ictal state. Hypocapnia shortens neuronal relative refractory periods, while alkalotic extracellular conditions accentuate voltage-gated Na+-channel activation and glutamatergic transmission. The reduction in CBF from controlled hyperventilation increases the risk of ischemia and facilitates seizure generation when paired with neuronal excitotoxicity.8 While no substantial evidence of a universal PaCO2 threshold has been shown to trigger seizures, studies have suggested that prolonged controlled hyperventilation with PaCO2 < 25 mm Hg is not recommended.9

Despite these mechanistic insights, there remains a paucity in the literature of clinical examples linking hyperventilation to intraoperative seizure, especially in the absence of overt aggravating risk factors, such as hemorrhage or hematoma. We present 3 illustrative cases of skull base tumor patients who developed intraoperative seizures at end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) nadirs of 22–27 mm Hg during craniotomy for tumor resection. We reported EtCO2 in lieu of PaCO2 as arterial blood gases were not monitored intraoperatively; however, past studies have demonstrated that EtCO2 can be considered as an acceptable surrogate measure of ventilation status and has been shown to correlate intraoperatively with PaCO2 in patients undergoing neurosurgical procedures.10 In all patients, the seizures were only abated following normalization of EtCO2 levels and anticonvulsant administration, which inevitably altered the surgical course. Thus, these cases highlight hypocapnia (EtCO2 < 35 mm Hg) as a modifiable risk factor for intraoperative seizure.11 We aim to provide insights into the relationship between hyperventilation and intraoperative seizures, guiding clinicians in optimizing patient care during complex neurosurgical procedures.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 34-year-old male with a past medical history of lymphoma (normal PET study by age 16) and obesity presented with a headache for evaluation following a motor vehicle accident. A head CT study, followed by an MRI examination, revealed a right cerebellopontine angle mass measuring 3.8 × 3.0 cm (Fig. 1A). It exerted right mass effect on the midbrain and pons, partial effacement of the fourth ventricle, significant crowding at the foramen magnum, and obliteration of the perimesencephalic cisterns, and there was a 12-mm cerebellar tonsillar herniation. There was no evidence of acute intracranial hemorrhage, large territorial infarction, or hydrocephalus. These imaging findings were consistent with a diagnosis of petroclival meningioma. The patient was not on any medications and denied any nausea, vomiting, weakness, numbness, and paresthesias, and there was no alteration or loss of consciousness. He was neurologically intact and was asymptomatic from the brain mass. There was no prior history of seizures. Because of the patient’s age and size of the tumor, resection was recommended through a combined transpetrosal approach.

Preoperative image demonstrating an enhancing mass centered within the nasopharynx and central skull base, measuring 4.9 × 3.9 × 3.5 cm, cranially extending into the inferior sphenoid sinus and eroding into the left clivus involving the left carotid canal, as well as the left petrous portion of the temporal bone. C: Case 3. Preoperative image demonstrating a 3.3 × 3.4 × 3.8–cm enhancing mass centered at the right cerebellopontine angle with significant mass effect and dorsal displacement of the adjacent pons.

The procedure involved an initial retrolabyrinthine approach followed by a right frontotemporal and retrosigmoid craniotomy. A pretemporal transcavernous approach was also performed to allow for the anterior petrosectomy. This was a staged approach with the last portion of the first stage being the anterior petrosectomy, which required relaxation of the brain. During the anterior petrosectomy, between 19:15 and 20:30, the EtCO2 ranged from 26.4 to 27.1 mm Hg (Fig. 2). Toward the end of the approach, the patient sustained an intraoperative seizure; the procedure was stopped after the seizure was controlled. The patient had tonic-clonic movements consistent with a generalized seizure per monitoring and the surgical team. He was given additional Keppra and propofol, and monitoring was continued. After closing, the patient remained intubated, underwent an immediate CT study, and was transferred to the ICU in stable condition. He did not require EEG monitoring overnight as he patient awoke briskly.

Graph depicting the EtCO2 during the procedure for case 1. The lowest recorded EtCO2 was 26.4 mm Hg.

The patient remained intubated and was brought back to the operating room for resection the next day. After the prior incision was reopened, the tumor was immediately visualized and dissection from the cerebellum and brainstem was initiated. The fourth cranial nerve was identified and freed. Further extracapsular dissection continued around cranial nerves VII and VIII, as well as IV, V, and VI, and vascular structures including the basilar artery, posterior cerebral artery (PCA), and superior cerebellar artery (SCA). The tumor’s base was found at Meckel’s cave and resected. A small fleck of tumor that was firmly adherent to the brainstem was left in place due to high surgical risk. An abdominal fat graft was harvested and used to fill the surgical defect. After closing, the patient was extubated and transferred to the ICU in stable condition. EtCO2 ranged from 41.2 to 28.7 mm Hg perioperatively. Postoperatively, the patient remained stable, had a negative continuous EEG study, and was started on 1000 mg of Keppra every 12 hours. Additionally, he had a successful recovery.

Case 2

A 57-year-old woman with a past medical history notable for anxiety, gastroesophageal reflux disease, mixed hyperlipidemia, hemithyroidectomy, and a history of breast cancer treated with lumpectomy and chemoradiotherapy in 2001 presented initially with left-sided ear fullness and recurrent symptoms of middle ear congestion. Multiple over-the-counter treatments, including antihistamines and decongestants, were trialed without improvement. Endoscopic evaluation raised suspicion for a mass, prompting an MRI study. Imaging revealed a large skull base mass involving the nasopharynx, clivus, and cavernous sinus and encasing the internal carotid artery (ICA), with further extension into the foramen ovale and lacerum (Fig. 1B). The patient denied significant pain, vision changes, or cranial nerve deficits, although she continued to experience aural fullness and hearing loss on the left side. Multidisciplinary review confirmed a fibrotic-appearing, expansile lesion on T2-weighted imaging. Otherwise, her neurological examination was intact.

She underwent initial endoscopic nasopharyngectomy with partial debulking of the mass without complications. During this first procedure, EtCO2 ranged from 31.0 to 39.9 mm Hg. Histopathology was consistent with clear cell carcinoma. At follow-up 2 months later, she reported numbness and intermittent pain in the left side of her mouth, but no new neurological deficits. Interval imaging demonstrated tumor debulking with residual tumor now measuring up to 3 cm with encasement of the left ICA, reduced when compared to prior imaging.

Subsequently, she underwent a planned open cranial approach for further resection. The patient experienced an intraoperative seizure after head fixation and standard exposure but prior to elevating the bone flap; this was confirmed clinically and via neuromonitoring. EtCO2 ranged from 47.6 to 22.6 mm Hg throughout the procedure with a 1-hour 15-minute period where it ranged from 22.6 mm Hg to 25.5 mm Hg (Fig. 3). The seizure was controlled with propofol, and the procedure was briefly continued; however, a second intraoperative seizure occurred on bone flap removal, prompting the surgical team to halt the procedure due to safety concerns. The bone flap was replaced, the wound was closed in layers, and the patient was extubated and transferred to the ICU in stable condition.

Graph depicting the respiratory rate and EtCO2 for case 2. The lowest recorded EtCO2 was 22.6 mm Hg, with the measurement of 4.2 mm Hg at 12:00 being a machine reading after the surgery had ended.

The surgical teams decided not to proceed with further surgery during the current admission. Her hospital course was otherwise uneventful. On the day of discharge, her pain was well controlled on oral medications. She tolerated a regular diet, ambulated well with minimal assistance, and had no difficulty with bowel and bladder movements. Her seizures were controlled at the time of discharge on Keppra 1000 mg every 12 hours.

Case 3

A 50-year-old male with a past medical history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, obstructive sleep apnea, and obesity presented with frequent headaches, gait instability, and dysphagia. The patient denied nausea, vomiting, double vision, weakness, or numbness in the face and extremities and had a normal neurological examination on presentation with no prior history of seizures. An MRI study was ordered and demonstrated a large 3.3 × 3.4 × 3.8–cm enhancing mass at the right cerebellopontine angle consistent with a posterior fossa petroclival meningioma with significant mass effect and dorsal displacement of the pons (Fig. 1C). There was minimal edema noted along the dorsal margin of the pons that extended to the superior margin of the fourth ventricle with partial effacement. The basilar artery was displaced laterally due to its proximity to the left anterior margins of the mass. A subsequent diagnostic cerebral angiography examination was performed for possible embolization of feeder vessels. The angiography study showed petroclival tumor blush supplied mainly by the meningohypophyseal trunk; however, the vessel was too small to safely catheterize and embolize.

The tumor resection was staged over 2 days. The first stage involved a right retrolabyrinthine approach and posterior petrosectomy. A frontotemporal craniotomy was then performed with dural elevation followed by a retrosigmoid craniotomy. Mannitol, Decadron, and Keppra were administered before the procedure. Hyperventilation was induced in the patient. Prior to the frontotemporal craniotomy and dural elevation during the first stage, the patient experienced an intraoperative seizure. During this time, an EtCO2 nadir of 26.8 mm Hg was reached between 13:00 and 13:15 (Fig. 4). No other complications were noted, and the procedure was continued following seizure control with Keppra 1000 mg. At the end of the first stage, the patient remained intubated and was transferred to the ICU in a hemodynamically stable condition. No seizures were noted on postoperative continuous EEG, and the patient was started on 4 mg of Decadron every 6 hours and 1000 mg of Keppra every 12 hours. A postoperative CT study demonstrated interval changes in the right sigmoid sinus with adjacent areas of pneumocephalus. A hyperdense rounded lesion was identified in the right cerebellopontine angle with an unchanged mass effect on the pons and fourth ventricle. A partial effacement of the right lateral ventricle was noted to have progressed from the preoperative MRI study. No unexpected hemorrhage, large territorial infarct, hydrocephalus, or entrapment was observed on postoperative imaging. The patient remained intubated and underwent the second stage of the procedure the following day.

Graph depicting the EtCO2 for case 3. The lowest recorded EtCO2 was 26.8 mm Hg.

The second procedure involved a right orbital craniotomy with an intradural resection of the middle and posterior fossae. An abdominal fat graft was harvested and placed over the epidural space to cover the defect at the end of the case. EtCO2 did not drop below 28.1 mm Hg throughout the second stage and the patient tolerated the procedure well. The patient was extubated 4 days later. A CT angiogram obtained on postoperative day 5 demonstrated luminal narrowing of the bilateral PCAs. Evolving infarctions in the pons and cerebellum were noted, and an eccentric nonobstructive filling defect was found at the junction of the right transverse and sigmoid sinuses. The patient was transferred for a cerebral angiogram on postoperative day 6 and was given intra-arterial vasodilators for management of posterior circulation vasospasm. On postoperative day 33, the patient was discharged to inpatient rehabilitation.

Discussion

Observations

Hyperventilation is frequently used during neurosurgical procedures to decrease elevated intracranial pressure for intraoperative brain relaxation.7 Hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia leads to vasoconstriction and subsequent reduction in CBF. While these effects are beneficial in reducing cerebral volume and softening tissue, the technique is not without limitations. In hyperventilation, alkalosis and hypocapnia act synergistically to induce a hypoxic state in the brain.11 Alkalosis increases oxygen-hemoglobin affinity, while hypocapnia induces vasoconstriction, decreasing cerebral perfusion.9,12 Hyperventilation is also thought to trigger seizures in patients with epilepsy. Neuronal membrane instability can be caused by mechanisms associated with respiratory alkalosis, hypocapnia, and reduced CBF. In these physiological states, neurons have an increased propensity to discharge spontaneously, potentially leading to epileptiform activity.13 The increased pH in alkalosis could also contribute to changes in neuronal excitability, decreasing the seizure threshold.9

In this report, we presented the cases of 3 patients with intraoperative seizures thought to be associated with hyperventilation during tumor resection, given that the patients did not have a history of seizures or recurrence of seizures after their recovery. Furthermore, there were no contusions or postoperative findings that would indicate any known direct causes of seizures in all 3 cases. Case 1 involved a patient with a diagnosis of a petroclival meningioma who underwent a right temporal and retrosigmoid craniotomy. The patient experienced an intraoperative seizure prompting the termination of the surgery. Hyperventilation was induced in the patient for a total of 30 minutes throughout the procedure, with an EtCO2 nadir of 26.4 mm Hg (Fig. 2). The resection was completed the following day without seizure activity; during the subsequent procedure, the EtCO2 did not drop below 28.7 mm Hg. Case 2 involved a patient presenting with a large skull base mass in the nasopharynx invading into the clivus, foramen ovale, and foramen lacerum into the cavernous sinus. The patient underwent an endoscopic nasopharyngectomy with partial resection of the clival malignancy without seizure activity. During this procedure, the patient’s EtCO2 remained above 31.0 mm Hg. The patient subsequently underwent a craniotomy to complete the resection and experienced two intraoperative seizures. Hyperventilation was induced in the patient for a total of 1 hour during the craniotomy, with an EtCO2 nadir of 22.6 mm Hg (Fig. 3). Case 3 was a patient with a diagnosis of a petroclival meningioma who underwent a right frontotemporal and retrosigmoid craniotomy. The patient experienced an intraoperative seizure just prior to elevation of the bone flap. Hyperventilation was induced in the patient for a total of 15 minutes, with an EtCO2 nadir of 26.8 mm Hg (Fig. 4).

All 3 patients had tumors situated in highly vascularized regions, which may have affected their preoperative CBF and increased their susceptibility to hyperventilation-induced seizures. The patient in case 1 had a tumor involving the foramen magnum with obliteration of the perimesencephalic cisterns (Fig. 1). These are highly vascularized spaces containing the basilar artery, PCA, SCA, and choroidal arteries. Prior studies have reported on petroclival meningiomas in the perimesencephalic cistern compressing the PCA leading to stenosis and blood flow disturbance.14 The patient in case 2 had a large skull base mass involving the nasopharynx, clivus, and cavernous sinus with ICA encasement (Fig. 1). In the skull base, the ICA is vulnerable to compression due to its rigid attachment to the dura mater; ICA encasement may have caused luminal narrowing, vessel occlusion, and even CBF reduction.15 The patient in case 3 had a large tumor at the right cerebellopontine angle with mass effect and dorsal displacement of both the adjacent pons and basilar artery (Fig. 1). As with case 1, displacement of the basilar artery in the patient may have also induced abnormalities of vascular dynamics in the region.

Hyperventilation is commonly utilized as a prophylactic measure in the management of severe traumatic brain injuries. Recent guidelines, however, advise against prolonged prophylactic hyperventilation (PaCO2 < 25 mm Hg), particularly in the first 24 hours for cases of reduced CBF.9,16 Therefore, similar precautionary guidelines could be considered for craniotomies depending on the location of the lesion and its involvement with cerebral vasculature. In cases such as the ones we presented in this report, the proximity of the lesions to critical neurovascular structures could serve as a contraindication for the use of hyperventilation for brain relaxation. Inducing hyperventilation in all 3 patients during craniotomy could have further exacerbated their reduced cerebral perfusion, leading to seizure induction. Of note, since arterial blood gases were not collected intraoperatively, our assessment was limited to using EtCO2 as a surrogate measure of hypocapnia, which has been noted in past studies to be an acceptable intraoperative marker, but may potentially underestimate PaCO2.10

Lessons

These cases underscore the need to scrutinize hyperventilation practices and explore how hypocapnia may modulate intraoperative seizure risk. While the present cases put forth an interesting clinical scenario, they also offer several learning opportunities to explore the physiological mechanisms underlying hyperventilation-induced seizure activity. Furthermore, given the observational nature of this case series, further clinical studies must be conducted to determine a causative relationship between hyperventilation and intraoperative seizures.
