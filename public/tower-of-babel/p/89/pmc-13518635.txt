Nonpenetrating Deep Sclerectomy versus Conventional Surgery in Primary Congenital Glaucoma: a Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Abdullah M Khan, Ibrahim AlJadaan, Abdullah M Al-Omair, Dalal M A Motabagani, Ahmed Mousa, Gorka Sesma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrimary congenital glaucoma (PCG) is a leading cause of irreversible childhood blindness, with elevated prevalence in consanguineous populations of the Middle East and Central Asia. Nonpenetrating deep sclerectomy (NPDS) is theoretically safer than conventional penetrating surgery, yet comparative evidence lacks systematic synthesis.MethodsWe systematically searched seven databases through December 2025. Eligible studies compared NPDS with conventional surgery in children with PCG aged under 4 years with ≥ 6 months follow-up. Two reviewers independently screened and extracted data; risk of bias was assessed with ROBINS-I and RoB 2.0. A random-effects meta-analysis [REML with Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) correction] was performed.ResultsFive studies [seven arms, 356 eyes; one randomized controlled trial (RCT), four observational] were included. Mean intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction was 10.34 mmHg (HKSJ 95% CI 7.54–13.14; 95% PI: 2.25–18.43 mmHg; I2 = 97.2%, indicating substantial between-study variability driven primarily by differences in baseline IOP [β = +0.61 mmHg; P = 0.004], adjuvant use, and surgical technique; P < 0.001). Surgical success was achieved in 77.1% of eyes (95% CI 65.3–85.8%). Visual acuity improved by 0.329 LogMAR (approximately three Snellen lines; 95% CI 0.220–0.438; P = 0.001). The composite complication rate was 13.2% (95% CI 8.7–19.4%), with I2 = 0% for hypotony, shallow anterior chamber, and hyphema. NPDS resulted in significantly fewer reinterventions (7.4% versus 14.1%; P = 0.046) and better corneal clarity preservation (92.4% versus 83.4%; P = 0.013) than non-NPDS procedures.ConclusionsEvidence comparing NPDS and conventional surgery in PCG remains limited. Surgery achieved a mean IOP reduction of 10.34 mmHg (HKSJ 95% CI 7.54–13.14), though estimates showed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 97.2%). While NPDS may be associated with lower reintervention rates and improved corneal clarity, no definitive differences in primary efficacy were observed. These findings require confirmation in well-designed prospective studies.Trial RegistrationPROSPERO registration: CRD420251121650.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01443-9.Keywords: Primary congenital glaucoma, Nonpenetrating deep sclerectomy, Trabeculotomy, Trabeculectomy, Intraocular pressure, CYP1B1, Pediatric glaucoma, Meta-analysis

Key Summary Points

Primary congenital glaucoma (PCG) requires early surgery to prevent irreversible optic nerve damage, with combined trabeculotomy–trabeculectomy and trabeculotomy as established standards; nonpenetrating deep sclerectomy (NPDS) had not been formally synthesized for this population.NPDS was developed in adults to reduce hypotony risk, but its comparative efficacy and safety against conventional penetrating surgery in children with PCG had not been systematically evaluated.In five comparative studies (356 eyes, seven arms), NPDS achieved a mean intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction of 10.34 mmHg (HKSJ 95% CI 7.54–13.14), a surgical success rate of 77.1%, and a visual acuity improvement of 0.329 LogMAR, broadly comparable to conventional surgery.NPDS was associated with statistically significant reductions in reintervention rates (7.4% versus 14.1%; P = 0.046) and better corneal clarity preservation (92.4% versus 83.4%; P = 0.013), though the evidence base is limited to five studies (two with serious risk of bias), and findings are hypothesis-generating.Heterogeneity was substantial for efficacy outcomes (I2 = 97.2% for IOP reduction; I2 = 70.9% for surgical success; I2 = 80.1% for VA improvement) and attributable to differences in success definitions, adjuvant use (MMC, Ologen, 5-FU), comparator mix, and geographic setting.Genotype-stratified prospective randomized controlled trials with standardized outcome definitions are needed before NPDS can be recommended as a routine alternative to conventional surgery in PCG.

Introduction

Primary congenital glaucoma (PCG) is a rare, severe bilateral disorder caused by developmental arrest of the aqueous humor outflow angle within the first 4 years, without ocular or systemic anomalies. It accounts for 2–15% of childhood blindness globally and is one of the most surgically challenging pediatric glaucoma disorders [1, 2]. Global prevalence is 1:10,000–20,000 live births in Western Europe and North America [3], but 1:2500 in Saudi Arabia [4–8], owing to consanguinity and pathogenic CYP1B1 variants (75–96% familial cases in Saudi Arabia; about 35% in European cohorts) [9].

Untreated PCG causes progressive optic nerve cupping, buphthalmos, and irreversible visual loss; early intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction is imperative during infancy’s critical visual development window [2].

Surgery is the cornerstone of PCG treatment [10]. Angle-based procedures such as goniotomy and trabeculotomy are standard first-line interventions with success rates of 75–90% at 1 year [11, 12]. Trabeculectomy and combined trabeculotomy–trabeculectomy (CTT) with mitomycin-C (MMC) provide dual pathway drainage but risk hypotony, shallow anterior chamber (SAC), and bleb complications. Nonpenetrating deep sclerectomy (NPDS) maintains the trabeculo-Descemet membrane to eliminate hypotony-related complications while maintaining adequate aqueous escape [10, 13–16].

Despite these theoretical benefits, NPDS has not been systematically reviewed for PCG. We conducted a systematic review comparing IOP reduction, surgical success, visual acuity (VA), complications, and reintervention rates between NPDS and conventional surgery in children with PCG. The review followed PRISMA 2020 and MOOSE guidelines and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251121650) [17, 18].

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective and retrospective comparative cohort studies, and case–control studies with simultaneous comparators. The eligible group were children under 4 years with confirmed PCG (isolated developmental aqueous outflow arrest, no associated abnormalities). Eligible interventions included NPDS with or without implant or antimetabolite augmentation. Comparators included standard surgical techniques, including goniotomy, trabeculotomy, penetrating trabeculectomy, CTT, and glaucoma drainage devices.

We incorporated studies in which IOP outcomes were reported at least 6 months later. There were no language restrictions.

Sources for Data and Search Strategy

A search of PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science (core collection), Cochrane CENTRAL, and Google Scholar was performed from inception to 31 December 2025. The search used MeSH terms and keywords including ‘primary congenital glaucoma’, ‘infantile glaucoma’, ‘buphthalmos’, ‘deep sclerectomy’, ‘non-penetrating’, ‘NPDS’ with ‘trabeculotomy’, ‘trabeculectomy’, ‘goniotomy’ and ‘comparative.’ We searched reference lists of studies and reviews, ClinicalTrials.gov and PROSPERO registry for ongoing and unpublished studies.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two reviewers (AAO, DM) independently performed title/abstract screening and complete-text screening using preset eligibility criteria, followed by full-text assessment. Differences were resolved by consensus or through a third reviewer (AK). Cohen’s κ quantified interrater agreement.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was mean IOP reduction (mmHg) from baseline. Secondary outcomes were surgical success, VA, complication rate, reintervention rate, and corneal clarity. Surgical success was extracted as reported by each included study, defined variably as IOP ≤ 21 mmHg (most common), ≤ 18 mmHg, or ≤ 16 mmHg, with or without adjunctive topical medication, and without the need for reintervention. When change-score standard deviations (SD) were not directly reported, they were estimated using SD change = √(SD pre2 + SD post2 − 2r SD pre SD post), with a pre–post correlation of r = 0.50 consistent with meta-analytic practice [19]. The sensitivity test to this assumption was applied across r = 0.30–0.90.

Risk-of-Bias Assessment

For nonrandomized studies, the risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I, and the categories were rated at the domain level (confounding, selection, classification, deviations from intended intervention, missing data, measurement, and reporting) and had an overall low, moderate, serious, or critical score. The single RCT was evaluated with RoB 2.0 [20, 21].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

We used R packages: meta (version 6.5-0) for random effects pooling with random-effects meta-analysis (REML) estimation and Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) variance; metafor (version 4.4-0) for meta-regression and leave-one-out analyses; and ggplot2 (version 3.4.4) for graphics. The mean difference (MD) of IOP reduction (mmHg) was pooled using a REML random effects model with inverse-variance weighting [22].

For binary outcomes (surgical success, complications, reinterventions, corneal clarity), analyses were performed on the logit scale with back-transformation for presentation using a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) as implemented in the metaprop function of the r meta package [23]. Binary outcomes were analyzed as single-arm pooled proportions (GLMM, logit scale); direct arm-to-arm comparative effect measures (risk ratio, odds ratio) could not be computed for all outcomes owing to incomplete reporting of paired arm-level data in three of five included studies. For outcomes where NPDS and non-NPDS arm-level data were available, between-subgroup Q-tests were performed as the primary comparative analysis.

Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) correction was applied (using t-distribution on k − 1 degrees of freedom) for small meta-analyses (k < 10) [24]. This method yields more accurate confidence interval (CI) and P-values compared with Wald tests for small studies.

Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic (significance threshold P < 0.10), I2 with 95% q-profile CI, h2 statistic, and τ2(REML). Results are reported within a 95% prediction interval. Six τ2 estimators (Dersimonian-Laird (DL), Hedges (HE), Hunter-Schmidt (HS), Sidik-Jonkman (SJ), Paule-Mandel (PM), and REML) were calculated, and REML was used for inferences [25]. All six τ2 estimators yielded qualitatively consistent results; REML was selected for inference on the basis of its favorable properties in small-k meta-analyses. A 0.5 continuity correction (cc) was applied to individual zero-event cells in logit-scale binary analyses (cc = 0.5, metaprop default); the infection rate estimate (1.3%) should be interpreted with caution given six zero-event arms and confirmed τ2(REML) instability in this sparse outcome.

For the primary outcome, six sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) all arms, (2) low/moderate ROBINS-I studies, (3) exclusion of Khamroeva [29], (4) RCT arms, (5) prospective only, and (6) exclusion of follow-up < 12 months. Studies were assessed using Leave-One-Out (L1O) analysis. Meta-regression was performed on three a priori continuous moderators: baseline IOP, follow-up duration, and age at surgery. All meta-regression findings should be considered exploratory, as the number of study arms (k = 7) is insufficient to support inferential conclusions from regression coefficients.

Prespecified subgroup analyses included NPDS-based procedures against non-NPDS modalities, Middle Eastern versus Western cohorts, CYP1B1-enriched compared with uncharacterized populations, and longer (≥ 18 months) compared with shorter (≤ 12 months) follow-up periods.

Ethical Approval

This study is a systematic review of published, anonymized data; as such, formal institutional review board (IRB) approval was not required under applicable regulations. In the interest of quality, transparency, and respect for ethical research principles, the study protocol was voluntarily submitted for institutional review. Exempted approval was granted under protocol number 25182-Rev.

Results

Study Selection

The systematic search yielded 847 records from the databases. A total of 624 unique records were screened for title and abstract after de-duplication, 589 of which were excluded from the study. We retrieved 35 full-text articles for eligibility assessment, of which 30 were excluded (wrong population n = 8, no NPDS arm n = 7, no conventional comparator n = 6, follow-up < 6 months n = 4, case series without comparator n = 3, duplicate cohort n = 2). Five studies (seven treatment arms and 356 eyes) met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Full-text inclusion was agreed upon among the raters [κ = 0.87 (95% CI 0.74–1.00)].

Study Characteristics

The study designs included one RCT [26], one prospective comparative study [27], two retrospective cohort studies [28, 29], and one multicenter registry study [30]. Sample sizes ranged from 40 eyes (Elabd [26], Khamroeva [29]) to 116 eyes (Rouland et al. [30]), with baseline IOP from 17.4 mmHg (Rouland) to 29.9 mmHg (Khamroeva). Follow-up was 6–36 months. NPDS approaches varied: Elhofi [27] used a 5 × 4.5 mm flap with Ologen and 5-fluorouracil; Elabd [26] used 6 × 6 mm superficial and 4 × 4 mm deep flaps with MMC 0.2 mg/ml × 2 min; Rouland [30] reported various NPDS variants across 16 centers. This technical heterogeneity causes clinical variability.

Risk of Bias

ROBINS-I ratings were serious for two studies (Mohammedsaleh et al. [28], Khamroeva [29]), mainly in the confounding subdomain owing to nonrandomized surgical studies. Two received moderate scores (Elhofi [27]; Rouland et al. [30]), and one received a low score (Elabd [26]; RoB 2.0). No article was scored critical. Three studies rated moderate for measurement domain owing to IOP assessment under anesthesia (Table 1).

Primary Outcome: IOP Reduction

The reduction in IOP from baseline was significant across all seven treatment arms (Table 2). The combined average IOP reduction from REML random effects was 10.34 mmHg (HKSJ 95% CI 7.54–13.14; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). High heterogeneity (I2 = 97.2%, Q = 238.98, DF = 6, P < 0.001; τ2 = 14.83; 95% prediction interval: 2.25–18.43 mmHg) indicated consistent IOP reduction direction, but magnitude varied by clinical context.

All six pretest sensitivity analyses showed significant pooled IOP reductions (P < 0.001). Low/moderate ROBINS-I studies (n = 5 arms, 316 eyes) showed MD of 11.82 mmHg (95% CI 7.74–15.90). The RCT-only estimate (MD = 9.50 mmHg; HKSJ 95% CI 6.56–12.44) was most conservative. L1O analysis (range, 9.15–11.01 mmHg) confirmed the pooled estimate, with no arm causing differences more than 1.5 mmHg.

Meta-regression showed baseline preoperative IOP as the significant moderator (β = +0.61 mmHg per mmHg increment; weighted r = 0.78; P = 0.004): higher baseline IOP produced larger decreases, indicating regression to mean effect. The follow-up duration showed a nonsignificant positive trend (β = +0.31/month; P = 0.068), while age at surgery was not significant (β = −0.04/month; P = 0.268) (Fig. 3).

Secondary Efficacy Outcomes

Standardized surgical success rates (Table 2) per IOP threshold criteria were achieved in 77.1% of eyes (HKSJ 95% CI 65.3–85.8%; I2 = 70.9%; Q-profile 95% CI for I2: 31.0–96.1%) (Fig. 4). The broad q-profile CI indicates uncertainty in heterogeneity estimation across seven arms. A high 95% prediction interval (41.2–94.2%) shows substantial context dependence. NPDS-based procedures showed a pooled success rate of 81.9% (HKSJ 95% CI 74.0–87.8%; I2 = 0%), versus 69.0% for non-NPDS procedures (HKSJ 95% CI 28.5–92.6%; I2 = 75.6%), without statistical significance (Q between = 2.36; P = 0.124).

VA from five arms in three studies (200 eyes) showed a pooled mean improvement of 0.329 LogMAR, equivalent to approximately three Snellen lines (HKSJ 95% CI 0.220–0.438; P = 0.001; I2 = 80.1%) as seen in Fig. 4. L1O analysis revealed Elabd 2023 CTT arm as the sole cause of VA heterogeneity, with exclusion reducing τ2 to zero and I2 to 0%, yielding a pooled estimate of −0.369 LogMAR (HKSJ 95% CI −0.403 to −0.336). The pooled MD remained consistent as predicted from pre-post correlation (range −0.329 to −0.330, r = 0.30–0.90).

Postoperative medication burden was low across arms, with a pooled mean of 0.40 medications per eye (HKSJ 95% CI 0.15–0.64). NPDS-based arms individually required 0.18–0.21 medications per eye (Khamroeva [29], Elabd NPDS, Mohammedsaleh et al. [28]). The Rouland 2025 registry reported 0.90 medications per eye; however, this is a registry-wide figure across all procedure types, including trabeculotomy in 31% of cases, and cannot be directly compared with NPDS-specific arms.

Results showed wide variability (I2 = 91.7%; 95% PI: 0.00 to 1.11 medications) with HKSJ CI (0.29 to 0.50) larger than Wald CI, indicating correction methods’ utility in small-scale meta-analyses.

Safety Outcomes

Table 3 and Fig. 4 summarize complications and reintervention data. A valuable observation for all complication categories was the homogeneity of rates: I2 = 0% for hypotony (5.2%; HKSJ 95% CI 3.7–7.2%), SAC (5.3%; HKSJ 95% CI 3.1–9.0%), hyphema (3.0%; HKSJ 95% CI 2.1–4.3%), and needling revision (7.0%; HKSJ 95% CI 4.6–10.5%). This uniformity in safety outcomes, compared with I2 = 70–97% for efficacy, shows complications are reproducible, while success rates may vary with targets or follow-up durations of IOP.

The composite complication rate was 13.2% (HKSJ 95% CI 8.7–19.4%; I2 = 5.1%), with a prediction interval of 8.2–20.3%, confirming safety reliability. Infection was extremely rare (one blebitis in 356 eyes; 0.3%), though the HKSJ estimate of 1.3% requires caution owing to REML instability in sparse cases.

Reintervention occurred in a pooled 10.3% of eyes (HKSJ 95% CI 6.9–15.1%; I2 = 22.8%) (Fig. 4). Reinterventions with NPDS-based procedures were significantly lower than non-NPDS-based procedures (7.4% versus 14.1%; Q = 3.97; P = 0.046).

Corneal clarity was preserved or enhanced in 88.7% of eyes (HKSJ 95% CI 82.2–93.0%; I2 = 33.4%; 95% PI: 72.9–95.8%), and NPDS-based procedures showed better corneal clarity preservation than non-NPDS procedures (92.4% versus 83.4%; Q between = 6.22; P = 0.013).

For the composite complication rate, meta-regression of IOP reduction resulted in a nonsignificant slope of β₁ = 0.039; standard error (SE) = 0.085; t = 0.462; and P = 0.664, showing no evidence that greater IOP reductions increase complication risk, consistent with NPDS’s physiologic outflow mechanism.

Subgroup Analyses

Discussion

Principal Findings

This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis of NPDS versus conventional surgery in PCG. The closest related synthesis is a Cochrane review of surgical interventions for PCG by Gagrani et al. [12], which excludes NPDS as a distinct intervention and lacks a focused NPDS versus conventional comparison, confirming no prior synthesis meets the present criteria.

Across procedures and sensitivity scenarios, surgery achieved a mean IOP reduction of 10 mmHg, including 9.50 mmHg in RCT estimates, confirming this is not an artifact of study design.

The safety profile showed remarkable consistency with I2 = 0% across all complication categories, a rare finding in surgical meta-analyses. With equivalent efficacy and reproducible safety, NPDS-based procedures showed advantages in reintervention rates (7.4% versus 14.1%; P = 0.046) and corneal clarity (92.4% versus 83.4%; P = 0.013).

Interpretations of Heterogeneity

The high heterogeneity in IOP reduction (I2 = 97.2%) was shown by meta-regression, with baseline IOP explaining most between-study variance (β = +0.61/mmHg; P = 0.004). This regression-to-mean effect in surgical meta-analyses shows populations with elevated baseline IOP (Elhofi 2020: 27–29 mmHg; Elabd 2023: 29 mmHg; Khamroeva [29]: 29.9 mmHg) have larger reductions than those with near-normal baseline IOP (Rouland et al. [30]: 17.4 mmHg) [30].

This meta-regression coefficient (β = +0.61; weighted r = 0.78), while methodologically informative, should be interpreted as exploratory; with k = 7 study arms, the model has insufficient degrees of freedom to support confirmatory inference.

This pattern denotes regression to the mean, not differential surgical success. The 95% prediction interval (2.25–18.43 mmHg) requires context to prevent misinterpretation. The lower limit of 2.25 mmHg indicates physiologically recoverable reduction limited by distance from therapeutic IOP, as seen in the Rouland et al. cohort (mean IOP 17.4 mmHg). A baseline IOP of 17–18 mmHg has a surgical ceiling of 4–6-mmHg reduction before normal range. The upper bound of 18.43 mmHg indicates large absolute reduction, similar to findings in high IOP cohorts (Khamroeva et al.: baseline 29.9 mmHg; Elabd et al.:29 mmHg). The anticipated IOP decrease was based on onset. Patients with baseline IOP of 25–30 mmHg may expect reductions of 12–18 mmHg, and those with 17–20 mmHg should expect 3–6 mmHg. Both were surgical successes physiologically. Future trials should measure percentage IOP reduction and absolute values, with prediction intervals stratified by baseline IOP category, not as a single pooled interval but as actionable recommendations for target IOP.

The I2 = 0% for all complication classes showed encouraging consistency. Results across studies in multiple countries suggest complication rates are not setting-dependent and are explainable to patients. The 95% prediction interval for composite complications (8.2–20.3%) represents the expected complication risk for future PCG surgeries.

Evidence Appraisal for NPDS versus Conventional Surgery

The primary efficacy endpoint showed comparable IOP reduction between NPDS and non-NPDS approaches (Q between = 2.36; P = 0.124), aligning with pressure reduction through trabeculo-Descemet membrane filtration [15, 16].

NPDS shows better safety with fewer reinterventions (−6.7 percentage points; P = 0.046) and higher corneal clarity (+9.0 percentage points; P = 0.013).

NPDS enhances corneal clarity and lowers reintervention rates beyond safety factors. Corneal opacification in infants (e.g., hypotony-related endothelial stress, IOP fluctuation post surgery) causes amblyopia during peak visual cortical plasticity, leading to irreversible deprivation amblyopia within a week [13, 31]. By preventing acute postoperative hypotony from full-thickness penetration, NPDS preserves the optical region during development. Pooled corneal clarity in NPDS (92.4% versus 83.4%; P = 0.013) should be a primary outcome in future trials and secondary in safety metrics.

It is important to note that corneal clarity data were available only for NPDS-versus-filtering-surgery comparisons [26, 27]; no included study reported corneal clarity outcomes for NPDS versus angle-based procedures, and the clarity advantage observed in our pooled analysis cannot be generalized to goniotomy or trabeculotomy comparisons. Additionally, baseline corneal haze grade was not systematically reported in any included study, limiting interpretation of postoperative clarity improvement in eyes with preexisting dense stromal opacity.

The VA improvement of 0.329 LogMAR pooled at three Snellen lines (HKSJ 95% CI 0.220–0.438; P = 0.001) is clinically significant. Early surgery enables better form vision during brain plasticity, establishing foundations for Vernier acuity, stereopsis development and amblyopia eradication [31].

The specific contribution of each mechanism remains indeterminate, but three pathways are presumed. IOP normalization is the primary mechanism: reducing IOP eliminates mechanical damage to the optic nerve and partially corrects axonal dysfunction in buphthalmic eyes, with best recovery before irreversible cupping [10]. Corneal clarity restoration is another mechanism: IOP reduction resolves stromal edema and epithelial endothelial stress, improving retinal image quality [13]. Since concurrent amblyopia treatment was not observed in all studies, the recorded VA improvements may underestimate recovery potential when combined with structured treatment.

The heterogeneity pattern shows a specific insight: the I2 of 80.1% across all VA arms reduces to I2 = 0% upon excluding the Elabd 2023 CTT arm (estimate −0.369 LogMAR; HKSJ 95% CI −0.403 to −0.336), indicating CTT as the sole variance source. The CTT arm in Elabd et al. (2023) showed lower VA improvement (−0.18 logMAR) compared with three NPDS arms (−0.35 to −0.38 logMAR) at 6 months follow-up. Elabd et al. attributed the inferior VA recovery in the CTT arm to postoperative complications (hypotony, SAC) rather than preoperative corneal clarity differences; however, baseline corneal haze was not reported by arm in this study, and differential preoperative opacity cannot be excluded as a contributing factor. CTT increases risk of early hypotony, SAC, and corneal endothelial stress complications disrupting corneal optical axis during amblyogenic window, while NPDS eliminates these mechanisms. VA recovery in all NPDS arms (I2 = 0, excluding CTT) shows three-line visual recovery is procedure-dependent for PCG nonpenetrating surgery. VA should be a first co-endpoint in PCG surgical trials, documented as LogMAR change with predefined follow-up periods, compared with amblyopia protocols.

CYP1B1 genotype’s association with surgical outcomes extends beyond missing data, warranting mechanistic study. CYP1B1-null mutations, predominant in Saudi Arabian PCG populations (75–96% of inherited cases) [5, 8], correlate with greater angle dysgenesis, higher baseline IOP and earlier presentation versus missense or heterozygous cases [6, 7].

The meta-regression tested this genotype–severity relationship: CYP1B1-null eyes with higher baseline IOP should show greater IOP decline, as confirmed by the moderator (β = +0.61 mmHg/mmHg; P = 0.004), regardless of surgical method. A differential benefit of NPDS in CYP1B1-null eyes needs further study. In eyes with missing or dysgenetic trabecular meshwork, angle-based surgery requiring functional trabecular tissue is less likely to maintain IOP control. NPDS establishes filtration through the trabeculo-Descemet membrane independently of trabecular function, potentially advantageous in genotypes with severe outflow disruption.

Without individual genotype data, this aligns with CYP1B1-null angle dysgenesis anatomy, suggesting testability in genotyped surgical cohorts. Genotype-stratified RCTs reporting CYP1B1 allelic status are needed to determine if NPDS advantages severe PCG phenotype in affected populations.

A nonsignificant trend toward lower composite complications (−7.1 pp; P = 0.062) supported this finding. The three independent outcome domains all favor NPDS, strengthening the overall inference.

The absence of SAC in NPDS arms versus 20% in trabeculectomy by Elhofi et al. (2020) is notable. SAC during penetrating filtration surgery risks complications in pediatric eyes, as the immature growing globe is vulnerable to hypotony-related maculopathy and choroidal effusion [13]. While NPDS eliminates this complication, a 5.3% pooled SAC rate across arms indicates persistent risk with penetrating comparators.

Further investigation of MMC augmentation is warranted. Rouland et al. found MMC augmentation was associated with increased reintervention rather than improved IOP outcomes [30]. This result requires careful consideration as it comes from a single retrospective registry study where MMC use was determined by surgeon preference, introducing potential confounding. Surgeons may have used MMC for more advanced cases, and no other studies provided direct NPDS comparison of MMC versus no-MMC augmentation. Pediatric antimetabolite literature provides biological grounds for caution against MMC in infant eyes, highlighting risks in trabeculectomy rather than supporting Rouland’s finding [13, 14]. These references describe penetrating filtration surgery, which differs from nonpenetrating surgery in antimetabolite action and risk profile. Studies by Kozobolis et al. and Anand et al. on adult nonpenetrating glaucoma surgery may not apply to pediatric cases owing to different scleral biomechanics and healing dynamics [15, 16]. The impact of MMC augmentation in NPDS for PCG remains unclear and requires prospective randomized evaluation before clinical recommendations can be made.

Postoperative medication load has important systemic risks in neonates: beta-blockers cause bradycardia and bronchospasm; brimonidine is contraindicated below age 2 years owing to central nervous system (CNS) depression; and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors affect acid–base balance in low-weight patients [32]. The requirement of 0.40 drugs per eye shows reduced topical therapy after surgery, with NPDS-based arms requiring 0.18–0.21 medications per eye; the higher Rouland registry figure (0.90) reflects a mixed-procedure registry average and is not a valid NPDS-versus-non-NPDS contrast. This difference stems from lower baseline IOP in the Rouland cohort, varying prescribing practices across 16 registry centers versus single-center series, and NPDS potentially providing greater IOP stability than penetrating procedures. A procedure reducing postoperative medication by 0.6–0.7 agents per eye offers systemic safety benefits beyond traditional outcomes, warranting medication count as a coprimary endpoint in future PCG surgical trials.

As noted in the subgroup analysis, the geographic comparison is structurally confounded and should not be interpreted as a population-level signal. The geographical success rate mismatch (80.8% Middle East versus 55.2% Western European registry (Q between = 23.83; P < 0.001)) cannot be explained by geography as an independent moderator, as the Western cohort is represented only by Rouland et al. [30]. The 25.6 percentage point difference is attributable to four factors. First, the lower baseline IOP in Rouland’s cohort (17.4 versus 23.7–29.9 mmHg) limits absolute success through regression to mean (β = +0.61; P = 0.004). Second, Rouland’s procedural heterogeneity across 16 centers introduces technical variability absent in single-center studies. Third, CONGLAU registry used stricter success criteria requiring reoperation-free IOP control at 12 months. Fourth, the inclusion of secondary glaucoma cases with PCG in Rouland’s cohort may have lowered success rates. These factors of baseline IOP, procedural variation, outcome criteria, and patient selection fully explain the geographic differences, precluding population-level surgical outcome comparisons.

The CYP1B1 subgroup analysis was exploratory and based on population-level genotype enrichment status rather than individual mutation data, which substantially limits its interpretive validity. The nonsignificant result (Q between = 2.22; P = 0.189) should not be interpreted as evidence against a genotype–outcome relationship; statistical power is insufficient (k = 2 per subgroup), and ecological assignment of genotype enrichment does not capture individual phenotypic severity. This analysis is hypothesis-generating only.

Strengths and Limitations

The study strengths include: comprehensive database searching; dual-reviewer screening and extraction; validated tools (ROBINS-I and RoB 2.0) for domain-level analysis; REML estimation with HKSJ variance correction; and reporting of 95% prediction intervals and CI for all outcomes.

These limitations are significant, and conclusions must be interpreted accordingly. The small number of studies (k = 5, 7 arms) limited statistical power for subgroup analyses and prevented publication bias testing. Serious ROBINS-I ratings in two studies were due to confounding by indication in nonrandomized surgical assignment. While a pre-post correlation (r = 0.50) is assumed for SD estimation, the VA pooled estimate’s robustness (r = 0.30–0.90) mitigates this limitation.

The variability in study definitions of success (with/without medication, IOP values between ≤ 16 and ≤ 21 mmHg) limits interpretation of combined success rate; 41–94% prediction interval (95%) should reference clinical use.

As geographic group comparison is uninterpretable (e.g., Western, one study), observed differences are explained by baseline IOP, procedural variation, outcome definitions, and patient selection.

The lack of individual CYP1B1 genotype data per study represents a key evidence gap, given the correlation between mutation status, disease severity and surgical prognosis in Arab patients with PCG [5, 6, 8].

There is a key limitation affecting generalizability: the difficulty of NPDS in infant eyes. NPDS requires dissecting the deep scleral flap to within 50–100 μm of Descemet’s membrane without perforation, a challenging task in infant eyes owing to reduced scleral rigidity and buphthalmic globe distortion [10, 13]. The studies did not report surgeon case volume, center NPDS experience, or perforation rates, preventing learning curve evaluation. Our safety benefits may only reflect expert center results, limiting the external validity for lower-volume settings or less experienced surgeons.

Implications for Practice and Future Research

NPDS represents a promising, safe, and technically reproducible surgical option for PCG, achieving IOP reductions comparable to conventional penetrating surgery. The safety and anatomical signals, fewer reinterventions and better corneal clarity preservation, are statistically consistent across studies; however, given the small evidence base (k = 5, 356 eyes) and absence of a statistically significant primary efficacy difference, these findings are best considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of superiority.

Current findings do not support routine MMC augmentation of NPDS in PCG; the signal from Rouland et al. is hypothesis-generating only and should not guide practice until randomized trials support it [30]. The safety data (I2 = 0%) help clinicians explain complication risks confidently, though efficacy data remain context-dependent. Multicenter prospective RCTs with genotype stratification are urgently needed, as CYP1B1 mutation status, a key disease severity modifier, could confound surgical outcomes [6, 7].

Standardization of outcome reporting (using the prespecified IOP thresholds, percentage reductions, logMAR VA, and number of medications administered) and follow-up intervals (12 and 24 months) would allow for future meta-analyses with far less heterogeneity. The grade certainty of evidence for all outcomes included in this review is currently low to moderate, reflecting the risk of bias in nonrandomized studies and imprecision from small sample sizes.

Conclusions

Pooling five comparative studies (seven arms, 356 eyes), this meta-analysis found that PCG surgery reduced IOP by an average of 10 mmHg (HKSJ 95% CI 7.54–13.14), with a directionally uniform effect across all arms and sensitivity analyses despite substantial magnitude heterogeneity (I2 = 97.2%). NPDS achieved IOP reduction comparable to conventional penetrating surgery and was associated with statistically significant reductions in reintervention rates and improved corneal clarity; the composite complication profile favored NPDS but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.062). The safety profile appeared similar across the included studies, although interpretation is limited by sparse outcome data. Individual complication categories (hypotony, shallow anterior chamber, hyphema, needling revision) showed I2 = 0%, suggesting directional consistency in safety outcomes across the included studies; however, with a small number of study arms (k = 5–7) and sparse event rates, I2 = 0% should not be interpreted as confirmation of homogeneity or absence of heterogeneity. Evidence for antimetabolite augmentation of NPDS in PCG remains inconclusive. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective, multicenter, genotype-stratified studies with standardized outcome reporting before informing definitive clinical recommendations.
