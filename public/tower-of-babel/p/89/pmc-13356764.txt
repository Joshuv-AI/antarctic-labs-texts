Thermal manipulation and heat stress: The transcriptomic landscape of the spleen in broiler chickens
Authors: Mohammad Borhan Al-Zghoul, Mu’taz Almaasfeh, Shadi Shahatit, Seif Hundam, Shirin Qaaty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Embryonic thermal manipulation (TM) has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance broiler thermotolerance, yet the transcriptional mechanisms by which it impacts immune organ responses to post-hatch thermal challenge remain poorly understood. Particularly given that heat stress (HS) compromises immune competence, suppresses lymphocyte proliferation, promotes apoptosis, and causes tissue damage, reducing organ size and functionality, leading to increased susceptibility to pathogens in heat-stressed chickens. Herein, we characterize the splenic transcriptomic profiles of broiler chickens subjected to embryonic TM (38.5°C, 18h/day, embryonic days 10-18) and post-hatch acute heat stress (AHS; 35°C, 12 h, day 22) or post-hatch chronic heat stress (CHS; 35°C, 8 days, days 14-22). TM did not impair hatchability and increased body weight measurements near marketing age. TM induced temporally dynamic transcriptional programs in the spleen across development, transitioning from immune activation and proliferative processes at embryonic and early post-hatch stages to ion homeostasis and transmembrane transport regulation by post-hatch day 22. Differential expression analysis across all experimental groups revealed that TM, AHS, and CHS each induced largely non-overlapping transcriptional profiles, indicating group-specific thermal responses. AHS elicited a proteostasis-driven response dominated by heat shock protein induction and glucose metabolism, whereas CHS produced markedly fewer DEGs. TM chickens under AHS exhibited recurrent upregulation of NAD, carbohydrate, phosphate, and DNA metabolism pathways, along with enrichment in hydrogen peroxide catabolism, suggesting that embryonic TM altered metabolic and antioxidant pathways. The identified genes and functional pathways reflected coordinated crosstalk among proteostatic, metabolic, and immunological networks that chickens use to maintain homeostasis under thermal challenge. Ultimately, understanding the molecular basis of thermoacquisition and heat adaptation is critical for improving breeding strategies that support poultry welfare and production in hot climate regions.

Introduction

The spleen is the largest peripheral immune organ, playing vital roles in immune responses and accounting for approximately 25% of total lymphoid tissue in chickens (Abdul-Careem et al., 2007; Chen et al., 2024). It is a major site for antigen filtration, lymphocyte activation, proliferation, and differentiation, contributing to innate and adaptive immunity (Lee et al., 2024). Heat stress (HS) adversely affects the immune system of broiler chickens as well as their growth (Hossain et al., 2026; Ohtsu et al., 2015). HS also compromises splenic mass and immune competence by elevating glucocorticoid levels, which suppress lymphocyte proliferation and drive apoptosis (Hossain et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025). As global temperatures continue to rise due to climate change, there is a pressing need for strategies to enhance thermotolerance in poultry production (Kumar et al., 2021).

Embryonic thermal manipulation (TM) has emerged as a potential strategy to improve thermotolerance and immune resilience (Al Amaz and Mishra, 2024). TM involves incubating eggs in controlled increases in temperature and humidity during specific developmental stages, which induces long-term physiological and molecular adaptations (Onagbesan et al., 2023; Ramiah et al., 2022a). Several studies have further demonstrated TM benefits on hatchability (Collin et al., 2007; Amaz et al., 2024), weight gain (de Barros Moreira Filho et al., 2015; Yalçin et al., 2008), thermotolerance acquisition (Loyau et al., 2015; Piestun et al., 2008), and overall positive immune response to HS (Al-Zghoul et al., 2023; Goel et al., 2023).

Gene expression analysis under thermal manipulation and thermal challenge reveals the molecular basis of heat adaptation in broiler chickens. Many signaling pathways are differentially regulated upon exposure to TM and HS in different tissues, including cytoskeleton organization, phosphorylation signaling, gap junctions, tissue remodeling, metabolism and energy generation, apoptosis, cell proliferation, oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune responses (Al-Zghoul et al., 2026b, 2026a; Juiputta et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). Among these, several heat shock proteins (HSPs), antioxidants, energy, and metabolism-related genes (e.g., HSP70, SOD, CAT, ANGPTL4, PLCB4, IL15, TLR4) are differentially modulated to preserve cellular homeostasis (Hossain et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025).

In the spleen, HS triggers IL15- and TLR4-mediated pro-inflammatory responses and immune cell proliferation, and TM influences splenic immune responses, affecting cytokine and HSP expression levels in HS-chickens (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020; Leão et al., 2024; Ohtsu et al., 2015; Renaudeau et al., 2012). Transcriptomic analysis of Ethiopian indigenous chickens exposed to HS revealed enrichment of immune-related pathways, highlighting key hub genes (e.g., GADD45B, FOS) (Park et al., 2019). Proteomics analysis of the spleen under HS identified downregulation of innate immunity proteins within TLR and NOD-like receptors signaling pathways, alongside activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and apoptotic and necroptotic cell death programs (Ma et al., 2019). Finally, stress-responsive and immune-modulating genes are impacted by several environmental factors, such as exposure to embryonic or early-age TM, humidity, and HS duration, which shape the expression profile in chickens (Nawaz et al., 2021; Vitorino Carvalho et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2025).

Despite these advances, the genetic architecture underlying spleen-specific responses to embryonic TM and subsequent post-hatch acute and chronic HS remains incompletely explored. Here, the objectives of this study were two-fold. We investigated the temporal dynamics of TM during embryonic, early post-hatch, and finisher developmental stages. We also characterized the transcriptional profiles of TM, AHS, and CHS using a two-factorial experimental design and bulk RNA-seq of the spleen transcriptome.

Materials and methods

The experimental design

All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Jordan University of Science and Technology (ACUC number: 16/4/12/265; Date: 27/04/2025). A total of 384 hatching eggs from 35-week-old commercial ROSS broiler breeders were collected from a local hatchery in Irbid, Jordan (mean egg weight = 62.36 ± 3.63 g). Eggs were visually inspected, and those exhibiting morphological abnormalities, including misshapen, soiled, cracked, or irregularly sized eggs, were excluded. The remaining eggs were surface-disinfected using 70% alcohol. To avoid thermal shock to the blastoderm and synchronize development, the eggs were gradually preheated to 25°C for 6 hours prior to incubation. They were then placed with their large ends up on plastic trays and incubated in commercial Type I HS-SF incubators (Masalles Company Inc., Barcelona, Spain). After seven days of incubation, we performed egg candling, removing 20 eggs due to infertility or embryonic death.

Until embryonic day 10, all eggs were maintained under standard incubation conditions of 37.8°C and 56% relative humidity; thereafter, they were randomly allocated to two groups of 182 eggs housed in separate incubators. The control group (Con-N) remained at 37.8°C and 56% relative humidity, whereas the thermally manipulated group (TM-N) was exposed to 38.5°C and 65% humidity for 18 hours daily from embryonic days 10 to 18. Eggs were automatically rotated every hour throughout incubation, which is a practice shown to support embryonic development (Oliveira et al., 2015).

Chicken management and heat stress challenges

Following hatching, a total of 344 day-old broiler chicks were transferred to the Animal House at Jordan University of Science and Technology. The birds were randomly allocated to 43 multi-tier wire cages: 169 chicks in the TM group (21 cages) and 175 in the control group (22 cages). Cages predominantly housed 8 birds each, with each individual cage serving as one experimental unit. Chickens were reared for 33 days according to standard management guidelines (Aviagen, 2009). Ambient temperature was maintained at 35°C during the first week, with a photoperiod of 23 h of light and 1 h of darkness. During post-hatch days 19 to 22, the temperature was maintained at 21°C. Diets were corn-soybean meal-based rations formulated to meet National Research Council nutrient requirements (National Research Council, 1994); (National Research Council, 1994). The diets were delivered in three phases: a starter diet from days 1 to 10 providing 3,000 kcal/kg and 23.0% crude protein; a grower diet from days 11 to 22 providing 3,100 kcal/kg and 20.0% crude protein; and a finisher diet from day 23 to the end of the trial providing 3,175 kcal/kg and 18.1% crude protein. Feed and water were available ad libitum.

Chickens were subjected to acute heat stress (AHS) and chronic heat stress (CHS) challenges following the same approach described in our previous work (Al-Zghoul et al., 2026a). Briefly, in both experiments, the chickens originated from two primary groups: TM-N and Con-N. Chickens from each group were randomly allocated to the AHS, CHS, or thermoneutral subgroups. This was to investigate the physiological and molecular responses to short- or long-term HS, noting that AHS and CHS experiments were conducted independently.

Regarding the AHS protocol, the AHS challenge was initiated on post-hatch day 22. 40 chickens from each group (TM-N and Con-N) were randomly assigned to two subgroups of 20 chickens each, housed in four pens of five birds each. This yielded four subgroups: Con-N, TM-N, Con-AHS, and TM-AHS. Con-AHS and TM-AHS groups were exposed to AHS (i.e., 35 ± 0.5°C for 12 hours), whereas Con-N and TM-N groups were maintained at thermoneutral conditions (i.e., 21 ± 0.5°C).

Regarding the CHS protocol, the CHS challenge was initiated on post-hatch day 14. Similarly, 40 chickens from each group (TM-N and Con-N) were randomly assigned to two subgroups of 20 each, housed in four pens of five birds each. This also yielded four subgroups: Con-N, TM-N, Con-CHS, and TM-CHS. Con-CHS and TM-CHS groups were exposed to CHS, with constant exposure at 35 ± 0.5°C for eight consecutive days from post-hatch day 14 to 22, mimicking prolonged environmental HS. In contrast, Con-N and TM-N groups were maintained at thermoneutral conditions (i.e., 21 ± 0.5°C).

Physiological measurements

Body weight (BW) and body temperature (BT) were recorded from 20 randomly selected chickens per subgroup at several time points post-hatch from day 1 to day 33. Similar chickens were not repeatedly measured across sampling days. For BT, rectal temperature was measured by inserting a thermocouple probe 2-3 cm into the cloaca, connected to a J/K/T thermocouple meter via a rat rectal probe (Kent Scientific Corporation, CT, USA).

Sample collection and RNA sequencing

We collected samples from the Con-N and TM-N groups on embryonic day 19, post-hatch day 7, and post-hatch day 22, as well as from all experimental groups (i.e., Con-N, TM-N, Con-AHS, TM-AHS, Con-CHS, TM-CHS) on post-hatch day 22. For spleen tissue isolation, six chickens per group were selected at random and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Then, the spleen tissue was excised, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and placed in TRI Reagent tubes (Zymo Research, CA, USA). The tubes were stored in a CryoCube F570 Series Ultra-Low Temperature Freezer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at -80°C till further RNA extraction. Our sampling strategy enabled the investigation of developmental temporal dynamics of TM as well as responses following HS protocols. Specifically, embryonic day 19 and post-hatch day 7 were selected as additional sampling points to assess the developmental periods of late embryogenesis and early juvenile stages, and to study transcriptional changes before hatching and early post-hatch extended TM-related effects, before the initiation of AHS and CHS challenges.

We extracted total RNA from the spleen tissue using the RNeasy Plus Kits (QIAGEN, Hilden) after tissue homogenization with the Bead Ruptor Elite-Bead Mill Homogenizer (OMNI International in Kennesaw, GA, USA). Then, we evaluated RNA quality using gel electrophoresis, a Biotek PowerWave XS2 Spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA), and a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) per the manufacturer's kit (Qubit™ RNA IQ Assay Kit). For library preparation, three replicates per group with RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 8.0 were selected for RNA sequencing. The libraries were generated using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold Kit (Illumina, CA, USA) per the manufacturer’s protocol (TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Reference Guide, 1000000040499 v00). Bulk RNA-seq was carried out by Macrogen Inc., Korea, with an Illumina sequencer, resulting in 101 bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatics analysis

We used Trimmomatic to filter RNA-seq data (Bolger et al., 2014). The following parameters were used: Sliding window: 4:15, Trailing: 3, Leading: 3, and MINLENG: 36. We then used Salmon to quantify transcript counts in quasi-mapping mode (Love et al., 2018; Patro et al., 2017). We used tximport to get the final gene-level expression matrix (Soneson et al., 2015). The reference transcriptome for indexing was downloaded from https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-115/fasta/gallus_gallus/cdna/ using Gallus gallus GRCg7b release 115.

Regarding differential gene expression, we used DESeq2 (log2 fold change ≥ 1; q-value < 0.05) (Love et al., 2014). Pairwise comparisons between all the experimental groups were performed using the Wald test to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Overrepresentation enrichment analysis (ORA) of functional pathways was performed in clusterProfiler (adjusted p-values < 0.05) using Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Processes (BP) database and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Gene Ontology Consortium, 2004; Kanehisa et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2021). The background set included all genes in our expression matrix, totaling approximately 16,000 genes.

To investigate semantic relationships and functional clusters within GO BP terms in ORA, we utilized ViSEAGO (Brionne et al., 2019). We used the org.Gg.eg.db package (ID: 9031) to map gene-to-GO annotations. Then, topGO was used for enrichment analysis, followed by merging enriched BP terms and calculating semantic similarity matrices using the Wang method to account for GO topology (algorithm = elim, statistic = fisher, nodeSize = 10, p-value < 0.01). Hierarchical clustering of enriched BP terms was performed with Ward.D2 aggregation and dynamic tree cutting. ViSEAGO identified functional modules that facilitated pairwise comparisons of the groups’ expression programs. We used ggplot2 for visualizations (Wickham, 2016).

Reverse-transcription quantitative PCR

We tested several DEGs using RT-qPCR to validate RNA-seq results. We selected eight top-ranking DEGs at random. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA extracts using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix per manufacturer's protocol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, DE, USA). The qPCR reaction was performed in duplicate using BlasTaq™ 2X qPCR MasterMix (Applied Biological Materials Inc., BC, Canada) on a Rotor-Gene Q MDx 5plex system (Qiagen, Germany) using a total volume of 20 µL (10.0 µL MasterMix, 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2.0 µL diluted cDNA, and nuclease-free water). We used ACTB and GAPDH for gene expression normalization with publicly available primers (Vitorino Carvalho et al., 2020). The two reference genes yielded similar results, so we only reported ACTB. For gene sequences and primer design, we used NCBI and RefSeq annotations (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/) and Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) with the G. gallus reference genome (taxid: 9031). For each primer pair, at least one primer spanned an exon-exon junction. See Supplementary Table 12 for primer sequences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, 2023). We used a two-sample test for proportions to compare hatchability percentages between the Con-N and TM-N groups. Also, we used a two-way ANOVA test and post hoc Tukey’s test to compare BW and BT between the groups (BW ∼ group × day; BT ∼ group × day). For comparison at day 22 for AHS, a one-way ANOVA (BW ∼ group; BT ∼ group) was applied. Each chicken was considered an independent observation, as measurements were not repeated on the same birds throughout the experiment. Regarding RT-qPCR results, we compared relative expression levels within each group to the Con-N group using either a parametric or a nonparametric test, with the Shapiro-Wilk test used to assess normality (p-value < 0.05).

Results

The effects of TM, AHS, and CHS on the physiology and molecular biology of chickens

During embryonic development, chickens were assigned to either a thermoneutral or a thermal manipulation incubation, and then, they were maintained under control conditions or exposed to AHS or CHS post-hatch, yielding a total of six experimental groups: Con-N, TM-N, Con-AHS, TM-AHS, Con-CHS, and TM-CHS. TM did not adversely affect the hatchability of fertile eggs. In the control group, 175 out of 178 fertile eggs successfully hatched (98.3% hatchability, with 3 embryonic deaths), while in the TM group, 169 out of 176 fertile eggs hatched (96.0% hatchability, with 7 embryonic deaths). The hatchability rates were statistically similar between the two groups (χ² = 0.96, p-value = 0.33).

After hatching, from day 1 to day 33 post-hatch, BW increased in all groups, with significant differences between TM-N and Con-N (p-value < 0.0001), and a marginally insignificant interaction term between group and day (p-value = 0.055; Supplementary Fig. 1A). At day 33 post-hatch, TM-N chickens showing an average increase of 138.07 g compared to Con-N chickens (mean weigh in TM-N = 2200.87 ± 256.82 g and in Con-N = 2062.80 ± 201.06 g). BT did not show significant changes between Con-N and TM-N groups (40.73 ± 0.42°C, 40.72 ± 0.46°C, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 1B). Exposure to CHS in both groups of Con-CHS and TM-CHS reduced BW (Con-CHS: p-value = 0.017; TM-CHS: p-value < 0.0001) and increased BT in comparison with Con-N (p-value < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). However, exposure to AHS resulted in only a slight increase in BT, without significantly affecting BW. At day 22 post-hatch, average BW across conditions was 1207.50 ± 122.21 g in Con-N, 1221.25 ± 141.21 g in TM-N, 1191.88 ± 111.06 g in Con-AHS, 1209.38 ± 89.14 g in TM-AHS, 1111.25 ± 84.67 g in Con-CHS, and 1091.88 ± 84.09 g in TM-CHS.

To characterize the transcriptional temporal dynamics of TM, we performed differential expression analysis between TM-N and Con-N within specific embryonic, juvenile, and adult developmental stages. At embryonic day 19, 37 DEGs were identified (11 upregulated, 26 downregulated), with GO enrichment revealing a predominant upregulation of immune-related biological processes, including humoral immune response, response to bacteria, alongside enrichment in transition metal ion transport (Fig. 1A-B). At post-hatch day 7, the transcriptional landscape shifted markedly, with 63 DEGs detected (39 upregulated, 24 downregulated), and GO analysis revealed an enrichment in cell division-associated processes, including mitotic sister chromatid segregation, nuclear division, and chromosome segregation, suggesting an acceleration of proliferative activity in splenic tissue (Fig. 1C-D). On the other hand, AHS activated the cellular unfolded protein response to mitigate the effects of HS, with pathways involved in heat response, protein stabilization, and chaperone cofactor-dependent protein refolding (Con-AHS vs Con-N; Fig. 5A). Additionally, Con-AHS affected glucose metabolic processes (Con-AHS vs Con-N). In addition to proteostasis-related pathways, Con-AHS vs TM-N showed an enrichment in signal release and negative regulation of apoptosis (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, we observed a recurrent enrichment of key clusters of metabolic processes, including DNA, NAD, carbohydrate, and phosphate metabolism, when comparing TM-AHS to all other groups (TM-AHS vs Con-N, TM-AHS vs TM-N, and TM-AHS vs Con-AHS; Fig. 5A). Notably, analysis using KEGG database of TM-AHS vs Con-AHS revealed additional metabolic pathways, with enrichment in glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate, fructose and mannose metabolism, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (see Supplementary Fig. 3D). Additionally, TM-AHS vs Con-N showed a unique involvement of hydrogen peroxide catabolism (Fig. 5A). In the TM-AHS vs Con-AHS comparison, protein folding and stabilization pathways were enriched; however, these pathways were not detected in TM-AHS vs Con-N and TM-AHS vs TM-N comparisons (Fig. 5A). Together, these results suggest context-dependent transcriptional and metabolic regulatory programs shaped by developmental thermal history and later thermal challenges.

CHS produced minimal significant pathways compared to the thermoneutral condition (Con-CHS vs Con-N; Fig. 6A). However, our analysis detected an enrichment in phosphate and inorganic anion transmembrane transportation (Con-CHS vs Con-N). The catabolism of hydrogen peroxide was also enriched in Con-CHS vs TM-N and TM-CHS vs TM-N, which indicated the involvement of oxidative homeostasis pathways (Fig. 6A). Also, Con-CHS vs TM-N showed an enrichment in cellular modified amino acid catabolism, and TM-CHS vs TM-N showed an enrichment in protein-containing complex assembly (Fig. 6A). Finally, comparing TM-CHS vs Con-CHS showed the involvement of immune system processes.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the splenic transcriptomic landscape of broiler chickens subjected to embryonic TM and post-hatch AHS and CHS using bulk RNA-seq, with several key DEGs validated with RT-qPCR. TM, AHS, and CHS each induced distinct transcriptional programs with minimal overlap, indicating that each condition included specific molecular pathways rather than a global thermal response. These findings suggest that the spleen is a thermally responsive immune organ with transcriptional profiles influenced by early developmental conditioning, with potential implications for broiler thermotolerance strategies.

Embryonic TM exerted temporally dynamic transcriptional effects in the spleen, with early immune and proliferative activation programs and later regulatory changes on ion homeostasis and transmembrane transport pathways. Also, the effects of embryonic TM were detectable on day 22 post-hatch. Metal ion signaling and membrane potential changes are fundamental to the modulation of adaptive immunity, lymphocyte activation thresholds, T-cell receptor signal transduction, and macrophage polarization (Bohmwald et al., 2021; Feske et al., 2015; Rodriguez et al., 2025). TM-driven alterations in splenic ion regulation may lower the immunological activation threshold for subsequent thermal responses. However, further experimental investigation is required to establish these functional associations. Our results align with prior evidence that embryonic thermal conditioning in chickens results in stable post-hatch effects on gene regulation and physiological parameters, reflecting developmental plasticity of the spleen (Ramiah et al., 2022b; Amaz et al., 2025). Notably, TM increased post-hatch BW near marketing age (i.e., day 33) without adversely affecting embryonic growth, with no changes in hatchability rates. This is consistent with prior evidence that TM during critical incubation windows can be beneficial, increasing poultry weight, growth factor expression levels, and white blood cell counts (Ansari-Pirsaraei et al., 2025; Ncho et al., 2021; Yalcin et al., 2022).

The transcriptional impact of TM became particularly pronounced when developmentally conditioned groups were subsequently exposed to post-hatch thermal challenge. TM-AHS chickens showed a recurrent enrichment of metabolic process clusters, including NAD, carbohydrate, and phosphate metabolism, that were absent in other comparisons. This pattern may indicate altered energy sensing and metabolism in thermally manipulated chickens in response to acute thermal stress. NAD metabolism is particularly relevant as an essential cofactor for energy transduction and stress signaling through NAMPT-dependent salvage pathways and NAD-consuming enzymes, maintaining homeostasis under metabolic and proteotoxic stress (Audrito et al., 2019; Groth et al., 2021; Revollo et al., 2004). Furthermore, hydrogen peroxide catabolism was enriched in TM groups under both AHS and CHS. In poultry, HS disrupts redox balance by driving excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which in turn activates a coordinated antioxidant defense network involving catalase, glutathione peroxidase, SOD, and Nrf2-mediated vitagene induction to restore cellular homeostasis (Habashy et al., 2018; Surai et al., 2019). Splenic immune cells are particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage, and research suggests that TM positively impacts the oxidative response of thermally stressed chickens, promoting immune cell survival and function (Saleh et al., 2020). Our results suggest that oxidative homeostasis pathways were a consistent mechanistic feature of TM-mediated splenic resilience to HS. Collectively, embryonic TM can induce epigenetic thermal adaptations that extend into post-hatch development (Al Amaz and Mishra, 2024), and our data can suggest that such adaptations also manifest in the spleen with metabolic and antioxidant changes upon exposure to later stressors.

AHS induced the most extensive transcriptional response observed in this study, and pathway enrichment was dominated by protein stabilization and glucose metabolic processes, which is a signature consistent with the canonical heat shock response driven by HSF1-mediated transcriptional activation of the proteostasis network (Akerfelt et al., 2010). This proteotoxic burden reflects the well-characterized consequence of an acute thermal challenge on cellular protein homeostasis, in which rapid temperature elevation triggers widespread protein misfolding, prompting HSP-mediated chaperone induction to prevent protein aggregation and support cell survival (Balakrishnan et al., 2023; Shehata et al., 2020). On the other hand, the markedly reduced DEGs in TM-AHS vs Con-N suggest that TM substantially attenuated HS-induced proteotoxic damage. Most pathways enriched in TM-AHS groups included DNA, NAD, carbohydrate, and phosphate metabolism across all three pairwise contrasts, indicating a shift toward energy and biosynthesis management. Enhancing energy generation and maintaining metabolic stability are essential to preserving homeostasis during critical periods of high temperatures.

CHS produced markedly fewer DEGs relative to AHS, which may reflect heat acclimation under prolonged exposure to the extended HS protocol or reduced technical sensitivity. It is important to note that the physiological and immunological consequences of CHS on the broiler spleen are well established, leading to lymphocyte depletion and impaired systemic immunity (Hirakawa et al., 2020). Also, Yang et al. (2025) showed that chronic exposure to HS induces more pronounced transcriptomic changes than acute exposure, highlighting conserved stress-responsive genes across tissues, including HSPs, antioxidants, and structural regulators (Yang et al., 2025). However, their CHS protocol lasted only a few hours, compared to a few days in our study, which limits direct comparisons. Thus, additional transcriptomic data across multiple CHS timepoints in the spleen would be required to resolve the temporal changes associated with prolonged heat exposure. In our study, the mechanistic divergence between AHS and CHS yielded the highest counts of DEGs, reflecting different gene regulatory networks in response to different HS protocols.

Limitations of this study include the relatively small number of biological replicates per group for RNA-seq, which may reduce statistical power to detect subtle transcriptional changes. Furthermore, during embryonic life, tissue was sampled at only a single time point; while this captures fully established late-term adaptations right before pipping, it provides a developmental snapshot rather than a continuous trajectory, masking the exact onset of these changes. This lack of resolution in stage-specific transcriptional transitions highlights the need for denser time-series sampling across multiple days within each developmental stage. Additionally, analysis of the CHS groups was conducted at a single post-hatch time point, precluding assessment of temporal responses over the course of prolonged heat exposure. Future studies incorporating multi-omics technologies, such as epigenomic profiling (Chanthavixay et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020), and genetic editing techniques, such as CRISPR-Cas9 (Li et al., 2022; Oishi et al., 2016), to perturb candidate genes linked to heat tolerance will be essential to resolve the mechanisms by which early thermal manipulation encodes lasting immune resilience in the broiler spleen. Furthermore, breed-specific responses to thermal challenges should be considered in future studies, as adaptation strategies differ substantially across genetically distinct lines and indigenous breeds, with implications for investigating spleen biomarkers and selecting simultaneously for heat and disease resilience (Goor et al., 2017; Nawaz et al., 2024).

Conclusion

In this study, the transcriptional splenic profiles of thermal manipulation and heat stress were analyzed. Various functional pathways were involved, affecting ion homeostasis, NAD and carbohydrate metabolic processes, DNA repair, heat shock proteins, antioxidant defense, and immune functioning. Our findings open avenues for integrating multi-omics approaches to resolve how early thermal developmental plasticity encodes lasting splenic immune resilience, with direct implications for breeding broilers. Poultry management programs should incorporate effective, scalable strategies that preserve the genetic diversity of the species and sustain poultry heat resilience and immune competence, given the challenges of climate change.
