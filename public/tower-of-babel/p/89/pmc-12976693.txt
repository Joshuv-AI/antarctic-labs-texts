A Review of the Variations in Design Features in Diaphyseal Engaging Tapered Fluted Titanium Femoral Stems in Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Joseph X. Robin, Theodor Di Pauli von Treuheim, Nathan A. Huebschmann, Ran Schwarzkopf, Joshua C. Rozell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For femoral reconstruction in revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA), cementless, diaphyseal engaging femoral components are the most commonly-used implants. At present, there are no reviews that directly compare the design features of these implants. We performed a manual review of the designs of commercially available diaphyseal engaging femoral stems. We compiled and compared the design features of these implants. Clinical outcomes of modular and monoblock stems were also compared. We identified five modular and four monoblock stems in the manual review of commercial companies manufacturing these stems. Distal stem taper varied from 2° to 3.5°, and the number of splines varied from 8 to 16. The stems varied in their stem lengths, offsets, and surface finish. Although there are no clinically significant differences in the restoration of leg length between monoblock compared to modular stems. The modular stems appear to perform slightly better with respect to subsidence and restoration of leg length. A source of concern for modular stems are mechanical implant failures that occur almost exclusively at modular junctions. Current evidence does not support any difference in dislocation rate, intraoperative or postoperative fracture, aseptic loosening, re-revision rates, or clinical outcomes between monoblock and modular stems. With the knowledge of the distinct features of implants, surgeons must make choices associated with specific design characteristics that could be pivotal to the success of the operation. Our understanding of design differences will help us minimize chances of failure and choose patient-specific implants that will lead to a high rate of success.

INTRODUCTION

Every year, over 50,000 revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) cases are performed in the United States with nearly half of these cases involving revision of the femoral component1). The volume of rTHA cases is estimated to double over the next decade2). With the impending increase in the volume of cases, surgeons will be faced with numerous decisions regarding the selection of implants. Compromised metaphyseal bone is common in femoral component revisions3,4). Therefore, a cementless diaphyseal-engaging tapered fluted titanium stem (TFTS) femoral component is often the preferred choice.

Ream-only femoral canal preparation and circular cross-sectional geometry are the instrumentation used for a cementless diaphyseal engaging TFTS. The stems gradually taper distally to facilitate stability of the axis in the diaphysis. Additionally, circumferential splines and flutes oriented longitudinally along the stem facilitate rotational stability within the endosteal bone5). This combination contributes to the stability of the primary implant and allows for the ongrowth of host bone onto the roughened surface of the implant. This osseointegration facilitates long-term stability.

Cementless diaphyseal-engaging stems are of two types: monoblock and modular. The Wagner SL Revision stem was the first monoblock cementless diaphyseal-engaging tapered fluted stem that became commercially available in 19876). In 2000, the first modular diaphyseal-engaging tapered-fluted femoral stem with subtrochanteric or mid-stem modularity became available with the Zimmer ZMR7). This allowed surgeons to adjust the femoral stem height, offset, and version, once the distal stem was prepared and fixed.

Although there is a dearth of a large registry or a prospective study that compares modular versus monoblock stem designs, various studies have demonstrated similar performance and outcomes for the two types. Even though many surgeons find the additional level of modularity useful in the revision setting8), the added modular junction could lead to rare instances of complications and failures that are not observed with monoblock designs. Despite the availability of various popular cementless diaphyseal-engaging TFTS, no report has reviewed and summarized the different design features and rationale of the available stem options. The purpose of our review is to summarize the design features and rationale of the current diaphyseal-engaging TFTS femoral components and to review the available evidence on the outcomes of monoblock versus modular stems.

METHODS

We performed a manual review of medical device companies with commercially available diaphyseal-engaging TFTSs for the femoral component of rTHA. Stems were classified as either monoblock or modular. The design features and sizing options of commercially available stems were compiled and summarized (Tables 1, 2). We identified and reviewed literature of retrospective studies associated with component characteristics, including design rationale, material, growth surface, and tapered fluted geometry. We also searched several databases for long-term series of patients who had undergone rTHA with a diaphyseal-engaging TFTS. We focused on patient series that reported on the most commonly utilized stems in our institution over the last 20 years. We did not enforce a strict time frame in an effort to be as exhaustive as possible and to obtain information on implants from the most comprehensive group of series. We also selected series that reported clinical and/or radiographic outcomes of tapered-fluted modular and monoblock implants with explicit documentation on the implant make and model (Supplementary Table 1)8-74). We searched the references from each article to identify additional series. Other studies examining outcomes, including subsidence, restoration of leg length, implant failure, dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, aseptic loosening, re-revision, and clinical outcomes between monoblock and modular stems were also reviewed to determine if differences or trends exist in the literature. All the selected studies were retrospective studies.

1. Design Rationale

Prosthetic femoral implants are subject to various combinations of axial, torsional, and bending forces. Cementless diaphyseal-engaging TFTS implants contributes to initial stability as they rely on the contact of the outer surface of the implant with the adjacent endosteal bone that permits osseointegration. The various features of the designs are an improvement on the technical issues of prior iterations of femoral component designs.

2. Component Characteristics

1) Titanium

Prior to the current use of tapered fluted designs, cylindrical, monoblock, extensively porous, coated stems made of cobalt chromium were the revision stems of choice. Although these stems were known for their impressive long-term survival, proximal bone stress shielding and thigh pain were prevalent75). This was believed to be due to the extensive mismatch in the stiffness of the stem and the native bone. Stiff stems are less likely to undergo bending deformation and could transfer load to the femur. The transition to titanium stems was considered an improvement as it addressed many of the issues plaguing the previous generation of implants.

The effect of a stiff implant on the proximal femoral bone has been well documented in the literature: high elastic modulus leads to high cortical and cancellous bone loss. The elastic modulus of cobalt chromium is about eight- to ten-fold that of bone, while titanium is about two- to five-fold76). With increasing porosity, titanium’s elastic modulus decreases and further approaches that of bone. Stem stiffness reduces with increased porosity, which increases micromotion at the implant-bone interface and decreases ingrowth77). When titanium reduces stem stiffness, it not only decreases proximal bone stress shielding but also is thought to decrease thigh pain.

2) Growth surface

The majority of the distal stems and monoblock stems have rough porous designs ranging from 5 to 11 micrometers in diameter. This porosity supports osseointegration with the host bone in the form of ongrowth on the implant. On increasing the percentage of porosity and pore depth, the strength of the biologic fixation of the osseointegrated implant is reinforced.

3) Tapered fluted geometry

The extent of femoral fixation that can be achieved varies greatly depending on the morphology of the patient’s femur and the dimensions of the stem78). The minimum length of contact between the stem and the bone to attain stability in fixation is reported to be anywhere from 1.5 to 2.5 cm9,79). This is a major improvement from cylindrical designs that were thought to require 5 to 7 cm of endosteal contact to support primary fixation80). Compared to a cylindrical implant, the tapered fixation concept is theorized to not only enhance mechanical stability and improve the distribution of load over a short segment of femur but also to simultaneously promote osseointegration81). The proximal to distal stem taper avoids creating a focal stress-riser when loaded and therefore increases torsional failure strength and the risk of periprosthetic fractures79). In Paprosky type III and type IV femoral bone defects, tapered stems have been found to be more beneficial than cylindrical stems because the tapered stem is better in engaging a short isthmic segment10,81,82). The use of tapered stems have now been extended to type I, II, and IIIA Paprosky defects, given its success with more deficient femoral reconstructions83). Although the tapered geometry does not fully protect against subsidence in type IV bone defects, the large proximal stem diameter provides “secondary stability” that is thought to protect against progressive subsidence that could necessitate revision surgery11,79). In this scenario, more aggressive taper geometry imparts improved “secondary stability”. Tapered fixation theory has also been shown to be beneficial in those patients with osteoporotic bone and concomitant fractures because it allows for stable fixation for immediate weight bearing without the risk of subsidence79,84). Obtaining a large surface area for fixation is probably the most difficult in femurs where the isthmus is more proximal or in instances where the femur has a short radius of curvature78). For these patients, curved stem designs or short aggressive taper designs may be more suitable.

With respect to the flute and spline geometry of tapered stems, Pierson et al.83) conducted a biomechanical study to compare various taper angles and spline widths to determine optimal subsidence and torsional resistance. They reported increased axial stability with broad spline configurations and more aggressive taper angles but no difference in the rotational stability across taper and spline geometry83).

3. Outcomes of Monoblock versus Modular Stems (Table 3)

1) Subsidence

One of the concerns of the rTHA procedure is the subsidence (Fig. 1) of the femoral component as it can lead to leg length discrepancy (LLD), impingement, fracture, instability, and aseptic loosening. There are mixed observations in the literature regarding subsidence following the use of monoblock or modular stems. Furthermore, each system has a different tactile feel to the reamers. Hence, a lack of experience in using a specific stem may result in undersizing for fear of fracturing the femur during diaphyseal preparation. Some studies that compared subsidence following rTHA have observed no difference when comparing modular and monoblock designs. For instance, no statistically significant difference in overall subsidence or the incidence of subsidence >5 mm between stems was observed in a retrospective review of 271 rTHAs that included 91 Redapt (Smith & Nephew; monoblock), 90 Reclaim (DePuy; modular), and 90 Restoration Modular (Stryker; modular) stems. There were also no differences in the subsidence rates in subgroup analyses based on femoral bone loss (low grade, high grade)12). There was no significant difference in subsidence rates between patients with modular, monoblock, or fully porous-coated cylindrical stems in a retrospective review of 335 consecutive patients undergoing femoral component revision13). We noted comparable clinical and radiographic outcomes for rTHA with no significant difference in subsidence rates between stem types in a meta-analysis of 3,671 patients with 1,718 patients having received modular and 1,953 having received monoblock stems85).

Other studies, however, have demonstrated differences in subsidence rates. A retrospective review of 186 patients—106 Restoration Modular (Stryker), 80 Redapt (Smith & Nephew)—demonstrated significantly high mean subsidence and a high proportion of subsidence >5 mm following the use of modular stems14). Several studies have shown modular stems to be advantageous. In a retrospective review of over 200 patients with a minimum six-year follow-up, there was significantly less LLD and subsidence in the modular (Link MP; Waldemar Link) group compared to the LLD and subsidence of the monoblock (Wagner SL; Zimmer) group. There was an LLD mean difference of 3.3 mm and a low subsidence mean difference of 1.3 mm with modular stem designs15). A large meta-analysis of over 3,000 patients (2,451 modular, 734 monoblock) from 46 studies demonstrated a significantly greater pooled subsidence rate in the monoblock (12.4%) than in the modular (5.9%) group86). Another meta-analysis of over 2,000 hips of patients (1,430 modular, 758 monoblock) with a minimum two-year follow-up reported significantly greater clinically relevant subsidence, defined as >10 mm, in the monoblock (4.5%) than in the modular (1.0%) group87). In summary, although mixed results have been reported, the current literature suggests that the use of modular stems may afford low rates of clinically relevant subsidence and average distance of subsidence than those of monoblock stems in rTHA. However, stem undersizing appears to be the most pivotal factor related to subsidence and is probably dependent on the “learning curve” of preparing and sizing the femoral canal for these implants.

2) Restoration of leg length and offset

The restoration of leg length and offset following rTHA may affect the clinical outcomes and the satisfaction of patients with the operation. Thus, it is important to understand whether there are differences in the ability of surgeons to restore leg length and offset with monoblock versus modular stems. The increase in leg length was less significant with modular than with monoblock stems (0.64 mm vs. 3.76 mm) in a retrospective review of 76 patients, half of whom received modular and half of whom received monoblock stems. However, there was no significant difference in offset between the groups16). Another study of 145 femoral revisions with modular (n=67) or monoblock (n=78) stems demonstrated significantly low postoperative LLD following the use of modular stems, but no significant differences in hip offset or the proportion of patients with LLD >1 cm17). The findings from these studies suggest that although modular stems may afford greater ability to restore leg length and minimize LLD with rTHA, these differences are probably not clinically significant. The restoration of leg length and offset should, therefore, not be the primary reason to guide surgeons in their choice of monoblock versus modular stems for rTHA.

3) Mechanical implant failure

Modularity and the flexibility to adjust offset and length after diaphyseal fixation does not come without a cost. There have been multiple reports of documented fatigue fracture at the modular junction with an increase in follow-up (Fig. 2). Some of the reported risk factors for implant mechanical failure, include excess body weight18,88-90), patients with high levels of physical activity, lack of proximal osseous support18,88-91), presence of a stress riser within the stem, and a small cross-sectional area of the stem19,92). A recent retrospective analysis with 8 to 15 years of follow-up documented a 1.4% mechanical failure rate with the MP tapered fluted modular titanium stem (Link MP; Waldemar Link)93). Four implant failures (5.2%), all at the taper junction and all with lateralized necks, were observed in a retrospective single institution consecutive series of 89 modular tapered revision stems with a 7-year follow-up. However, standard offset implants did not demonstrate any failure18). Van Houwelingen et al.11) reported an 18.5% implant fracture rate in 65 patients at the mid-stem modular junction with 5 to 10 years of follow-up with ZMR (Zimmer) standard design implants. In response to this, Zimmer discontinued this body and replaced it with a large “XL body” junction (14 to 19.5 mm) without subsequent reports of implant failure. Van Houwelingen et al.11) also postulated that the location of implant failure is influenced by the lack of stout lateral bony support at this level through extended trochanteric osteotomies (ETOs) and sliding trochanteric osteotomies. A finite element analysis documented that the loss of the proximomedial bone increases stress in the revision component by 82% with doubling in stress in the setting of unhealed osteotomies92). Fretting failure can also occur at the site where the morse taper of the body couples with the stem, which increases premature cracks and accelerates fatigue failure by more than two-fold19). Lakstein et al.19) retrospectively reviewed 165 patients with modular stems and found a 3.6% failure rate. Implants failed 1 to 2 mm proximal to the body-stem junction with evidence of fretting fatigue failure near the fracture surface. Modular implants also exhibit in vivo hydrogen embrittlement in the modular junctions leading to fretting-crevice corrosion94). Nonetheless, measures can be taken to minimize the risk of implant failure. In high body mass index (BMI) patients and those with poor proximal bone stock, allograft supplementation and maximizing the size of the body, known as “filling the gap”, can be utilized19). Although a majority of modular mechanical failures occur at the modular junctions, there are rare reports of mid-stem failures. Rueckl et al.95) reported two cases of Restoration Modular (Stryker) mid-stem failure due to fatigue fracture. Bicanic et al.96) reported a mid-stem failure of an MP Link (Waldemar Link) prosthesis.

In contrast, mechanical implant failure of monoblock stems is exceedingly rare. Alqahtani et al.20) reported no mechanical failure rates at the 2- to 7-year follow-up in 100 patients treated with a Redapt (Smith & Nephew) stem for 47.6% Paprosky type 3A, 11.1% type 3B, and 1.6% type 4 femoral bone loss. There were no cases of implant failure with a follow-up of almost five years in another multicenter retrospective review that included 81 patients with 82.7% Paprosky type 3A, 13.6% type 3B, and 3.7% type 4 bone loss21).

In summary, modular tapered fluted stems have the added disadvantage of mechanical failure that occurs most frequently at the site of modularity. Risk factors are circumstances where the implant experiences high stress, such as in active patients with a high BMI. Furthermore, the lack of proximal bone support imparts additional risk for failure and patients with multiple risk factors are advised to be careful.

4) Dislocation

Dislocation following THA or rTHA is a rare but feared complication97,98). Recurrent dislocation rates are high following an initial prosthetic hip dislocation97,98). Especially in the setting of rTHA, all efforts should be made to minimize the risk of dislocation and further complications. Modular stems were introduced to provide the surgeon with more options to restore offset and length. Despite this proposed benefit, the current literature demonstrates no difference in the dislocation rates based on the use of modular or monoblock stems following rTHA. Koutalos et al.86) report a rate of 5.4% and 6.8% that is not significantly different for modular and monoblock stems, respectively. Dislocation following rTHA with either modular or monoblock stems was also examined in the previously cited meta-analysis of over 2000 patients by Wang et al.87). In an analysis of eight studies in which dislocation was reported, there was no significant difference in the dislocation rate (3.5% vs. 3.4%) or the odds of dislocation between the modular and monoblock groups. Similarly, the meta-analysis by Zampogna et al.85) including 3,671 patients found no statistically significant difference in the odds of dislocation at a mean 46.9 months of follow-up in patients who underwent rTHA with either modular or monoblock stems. Tables 1 and 2 reveal that monoblock and modular stem offset options are similar since monoblock necks provide a range of at least 4 mm that appears to confer the prerequisite modularity to achieve stability.

In summary, modular stems provide surgeons flexibility by uncoupling diaphyseal fixation and proximal metaphyseal sizing along with femoral anteversion. Although this modularity is postulated to help improve hip stability under challenging circumstances, meta-analyses have demonstrated no difference in dislocation risk between monoblock and modular tapered fluted stem designs.

5) Intraoperative and postoperative fracture

Although one large meta-analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference in the risk of intraoperative fracture rate with the use of either modular or monoblock stems in rTHA85), another meta-analysis demonstrated significantly high odds of intraoperative fracture with modular stems (9.2%) compared to that with monoblock stems (7.6%)86). In their meta-analysis, Wang et al.87) also found a significantly high odds ratio of 2.72 for intraoperative fracture with modular stems as well as a significantly high rate of ETO with the use of monoblock stems. The authors suggest that this association may be due to ETOs being indicated in cases of proximal femoral varus remodeling or excessive femoral bowing to facilitate straight reaming and safe monoblock stem placement. Although making an ETO for stem placement is not necessary in our experience, it may help surgeons who have limited experience using monoblock stems in complicated femoral revision. ETOs are less necessary for modular stems where uncoupling of the proximal and distal parts of the modular stems allows for optimization of fit and separate fixation in these regions. To prevent high intraoperative fracture rates with modular stems, the authors reasoned that surgical exposure is enhanced while protecting the bone from intraoperative fracture when an ETO is performed. Regarding postoperative fractures, both meta-analyses found no significant difference in the odds of periprosthetic fracture between modular and monoblock stems85,87). In summary, there is some evidence that intraoperative fracture risk may be high with modular stems that demosntrate no difference in postoperative fracture risk; however, further research is needed to confirm these findings as well as the role of ETO in mitigating the risk of intraoperative fractures.

6) Aseptic loosening

Given the flexibility of modular stems to adjust length and offset, in theory, there may be a more precise recreation of normal hip forces, improved implant stability, and low risk of aseptic loosening. However, the current literature does not support a difference in aseptic loosening with the use of either modular or monoblock stems. In a meta-analysis of over 2,000 hips, six studies reported on the incidence of aseptic loosening and found that the stem type did not significantly increase the odds of aseptic loosening87). The previously cited retrospective review of 145 patients undergoing rTHA reported no significant difference in the rate of aseptic loosening following rTHA between modular (4.5%) and monoblock (6.4%) stems17). Thus, currently there is no evidence to support one femoral stem design over the other with respect to risk mitigation against aseptic loosening.

7) Re-revision

Since there is an increase in morbidity including infection and instability with subsequent re-revisions99), the primary objective is mitigation of these risks in this setting. The cost of revision THA implant has been reported to account for 36% of the total hospital cost100). There is no clear evidence to support the use of either monoblock or modular stems with respect to long-term implant survival and the effect on re-revision rates. Yacovelli and Hozack101) examined consecutive patients who underwent femoral component revision and observed no significant differences in stem re-revision rates among 91 patients who received a Redapt (Smith & Nephew) stem, 90 who received a Reclaim (DePuy) stem, and 90 who received a Restoration Modular (Stryker) stem. In their meta-analysis that included six studies reporting on re-revisions, Wang et al.87) demonstrated no statistically significant difference with respect to re-revisions between patients receiving either modular or monoblock stems for any reason or re-revisions specifically for aseptic loosening. In summary, modular and monoblock tapered fluted stems do not differ in the re-revision rate. However, from a value-based care perspective, monoblock designs are preferred due to the high cost associated with modular stem designs.

8) Clinical outcomes

More importantly from a patient perspective, we need to understand if patient-reported clinical outcomes differ between modular and monoblock stems. The Harris hip score (HHS)102) is a rating scale from 0 to 100 consisting of four subscales (pain: 0-44, function: 0-47, range of motion: 0-5, absence of deformity: 0-4), with higher scores indicating better function. The HHS is commonly used to assess clinical outcomes following THA and rTHA. In the meta-analyses by Zampogna et al.85) and Wang et al.87), with 3,671 and 2,188 patients, respectively, there were no significant differences in postoperative HHS scores between patients who revealed modular versus monoblock stems. These findings suggest that any radiographic differences associated with modular versus monoblock stems probably do not impact pain and function following rTHA.

LIMITATIONS

The present review has several limitations. First, there was a great deal of heterogeneity in the stems that were assessed in the studies cited in this review. Several of the included studies were retrospective studies or meta-analyses that included retrospective studies. The references did not include randomized controlled trials. Therefore, stem selection was not standardized and was subject to the personal preference of surgeons and patient-specific factors. Second, it is difficult to control for several outcome-associated variables in the reviewed studies, including surgical approach, femoral head size, liner type (e.g., fixed bearing, dual mobility, constrained), surgical indication, patient follow-up, patient comorbidities, and surgeon experience with the chosen stems. These limitations are somewhat inherent to retrospective studies and limit the conclusions regarding outcomes of monoblock versus modular stems. Another limitation is that this was not a formal systematic review. However, the purpose of our review is to provide a comprehensive, narrative summary of the variations in design features of diaphyseal-engaging TFTSs for the femur, the design rationale as well as an overview of the current evidence for monoblock and modular stems with respect to several clinical outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Variations are present in the design of both monoblock and modular diaphyseal engaging TFTSs for the femoral component of rTHA. There are several options available for distal taper, number of splines, stem length, offset, and pore diameter across stems and for each model. Modular stems have been shown to perform slightly better than monoblock stems with respect to subsidence and the restoration of leg length. However, the differences in the restoration of leg length are not clinically significant. Mechanical implant failures occur almost exclusively at modular junctions and, therefore, are a source of concern for modular stems. Current evidence does not support any significant differences between modular and monoblock stems with respect to dislocation rate, intraoperative or postoperative fracture, aseptic loosening, re-revision rates, or clinical outcomes.
