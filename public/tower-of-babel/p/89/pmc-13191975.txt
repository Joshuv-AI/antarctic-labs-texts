Anxiety and lifestyle behaviors among Lebanese university students during armed conflict: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rawane Rawda El Balah, Nicole Fakhoury Sayegh, Aya El Hajj, Nour El Helou, Tatiana Papazian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolitical instability and armed conflict impose substantial psychological and behavioral challenges on young adults. University students may experience heightened anxiety alongside disruptions in eating behaviors (EB) and physical activity (PA) during periods of active conflict.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the associations between anxiety, EB, PA and conflict-related strain among Lebanese university students exposed to war-induced stressors, and to identify the main factors associated with anxiety.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 401 students (mean age = 21 ± 2.2 years) during the most recent armed conflict in Lebanon (September- December 2024). Participants were recruited via snowball sampling from multiple Lebanese universities through an online survey diffused on social media platforms. Validated instruments were used, including the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, the short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), and an adapted EB questionnaire. Additional questions assessed sociodemographic, anthropometric measurements, conflict-related stressors, lifestyle changes and other relevant factors.ResultsClinically significant anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 10) was observed in 43.6% of students and was more prevalent among females (p = 0.006). Anxiety was significantly associated with employment status (p = 0.014) and smoking status (p = 0.016), whereas EB, PA, and body mass index (BMI) showed no significant associations. In multivariable binary logistic regression, anxiety was associated with full-time employment (OR = 2.62; 95% CI: 1.20–5.73), direct exposure to bombings (OR = 2.77, 95% CI: 1.29–5.92), perceived academic functioning impairment (concentration or motivation) related to online classes (OR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.00-4.99), stress-related changes in appetite (decreased appetite: OR = 3.63, 95% CI: 1.91–6.90; increased appetite: OR = 3.37, 95% CI: 1.88–6.04).ConclusionAnxiety was highly prevalent among Lebanese university students during active conflict and was primarily associated with war-related stressors, contextual academic and occupational pressures rather than with EB, PA, or BMI. These findings highlight the importance of context-sensitive mental health support within university communities during periods of socio-political instability and educational disruption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27172-z.Keywords: Anxiety, Conflict exposure, Political instability, Eating behavior, Physical activity, University students

Introduction

Lebanon has experienced repeated cycles of armed conflict that have profoundly affected the psychological well-being and social functioning of its population. From the 16-year civil war (1975–1990) to the recurrent invasions of 1982, 2000, and 2006, repeated exposure to conflict, displacement, and economic disruptions has left enduring mental health consequences. The most recent escalation, which began in late 2023 and intensified throughout 2024, affected large areas of the country, with particularly intense bombardments in the southern suburbs of Beirut and several regions in South Lebanon and the Bekaa. This period was marked by thousands of civilians casualties, extensive internal displacement, and significant damage to infrastructure and insufficient public and mental health services [1]. Previous studies conducted in Lebanon following major armed conflicts, particularly the 2006 war, have reported a high prevalence of anxiety disorders, depressive symptoms, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) across different subgroups of the Lebanese population [2]. University students may be especially vulnerable, as they navigate academic demands while simultaneously confronting political instability, economic hardship, and uncertainty about their future. These cumulative stressors have been shown to negatively affect psychological well-being, academic performance, and daily functioning [3].

The university period represents a critical transition from adolescence to young adulthood, characterized by increasing autonomy, new academic responsibilities, financial pressures, and the need to establish new social networks [4]. This transition often coincides with changes in lifestyle behaviors, including eating behavior (EB) and physical activity (PA). Greater independence in food choices, long academic days, and limited financial and time resources may promote reliance on convenient, energy-dense foods, while academic workload, screen time, and irregular schedules may contribute to reduced PA and increased sedentary behavior [4, 5]. Such habits may lead to fatigue, impaired concentration, weakened immunity, and an increased risk of anxiety and depression, which in turn affect students’ food choices and academic performance [6, 7]. In Lebanon, most research on university students’ health has examined nutrition, physical activity (PA), or mental health as separate outcomes, typically in relatively stable or post-crisis contexts. Evidence addressing the coexistence of anxiety and lifestyle behaviors during periods of active conflict remains limited. Therefore, the present study adopts a cross-sectional design to study the relationships between anxiety, EB, and PA among Lebanese university students during the last period of active conflict, while considering both habitual behaviors and conflict-related changes in appetite and PA.

Methods & materials

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between October and December 2024, during the period of armed conflict and active political instability in Lebanon. Due to safety concerns, ongoing bombardments, and university closures, students were not physically present on campuses, and higher education was delivered entirely through online platforms in all Lebanese academic institutions. Consequently, data collection was conducted via an online self-administered questionnaire, which allowed safe participation and data collection within a limited time frame, under constrained conditions. Eligible participants were Lebanese students, aged between 18 and 26 years, healthy, and without any physical handicaps. Exclusion criteria included the use of antidepressant medications, the presence of chronic illness, and a diagnosed mental health or eating disorder. These criteria were applied to reduce potential confounding parameters related to pre-existing clinical conditions. Participants were recruited via snowball sampling on social media platforms (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp). This recruitment approach was selected due to movement restrictions, safety concerns, and the absence of on-campus access. As a result, the sample represents a digitally reachable and socially connected subgroup of Lebanese university students rather than a nationally representative population.

Sampling methods and sample size

The sample size was calculated according to the standard formula used to estimate a proportion in a cross-sectional study, with an estimate prevalence of 72.2% of anxiety among Lebanese university students [8] and with the desired margin of error set at 5%. Using this formula, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be approximately 288 participants.

Ethical consideration

The research protocol was approved by the ethical committee of Saint Joseph University (USJ) (CER-2025-181) before data collection and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964). Participants were informed about the purpose of the study before participation and provided informed consent by selecting “I agree” on the first page of the survey hosted on the online platform Google Forms. Only consenting individuals were able to proceed. The survey was anonymous, with no personal identifiers collected. Participants were informed they could withdraw at any time without penalty.

Data collection and measures

Data was collected anonymously via an online survey available in Arabic, English, and French. The questionnaire included several parts as detailed below:

Sociodemographic parameters

Age, gender, marital status, place of residency, type of university (private or public), field and year of study, monthly family income, crowding index, and employment status were compiled in addition to questions assessing alcohol consumption, tobacco use and sleep patterns. Vulnerability to conflict-related factors was assessed using a set of self-reported questions developed specifically for the Lebanese context of the ongoing conflict. These items captured key dimensions of exposure relevant to university students, including bomb attacks, forced displacement from home, disruptions related to online learning, as well as changes in EB, PA, and consumption of alcohol and tobacco. Although several validated instruments are available to assess war-related or extreme traumatic experiences such as the Extreme Experiences Scale (EES) [9] and the Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ) [10], these tools were primarily developed for post-war contexts, refugee populations, or clinical evaluations of trauma-related disorders [9, 10], hence they could not cover the scope of this research.

The crowding index, calculated as the total number of household members divided by the number of rooms (excluding kitchen and bathrooms), was used as a measure of household density, which may reflect socioeconomic status and living conditions. It was categorized as not crowded (≤ 1), moderately crowded ([1–2]), and severely crowded (≥ 2) [11].

Anthropometric variables

Self-reported anthropometric measurements such as weight in kilograms and height in centimeters were requested from all participants. Body mass index (BMI) was then calculated and categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) as follows: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m2) [12]. Participants were also asked to report any weight fluctuations over the past six months, before the start of the political unrest and conflict.

Eating behavior

Eating behavior (EB) was assessed using a 10-item brief dietary screening instrument designed to capture overall dietary patterns relevant to chronic disease prevention and adapted to reflect regional dietary habits and public health nutrition recommendations [13–15]. The questionnaire assessed the frequency of consumption of recommended food groups (whole grains, fruits and vegetables, dairy products, legumes, poultry, and fish) as well as foods to limit (red and processed meats, fast or fried foods, sugary snacks, sugar-sweetened beverages, and added fats). Each item was scored on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (least healthy) to 3 (most healthy), based on consistency with international dietary recommendations, relevant to the Lebanese context. Total score ranged from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating healthier EB. Participants were categorized into three levels of diet quality: poor (0–10), mediocre [11–20], and acceptable [21–30]. This adapted tool was selected for its brevity, ease of administration, and suitability for large population-based studies, particularly in settings where comprehensive dietary assessment tools may be impractical. Internal consistency of the adapted EB questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which indicated acceptable reliability (α = 0.70). The instrument was used as a brief dietary screening tool to capture overall dietary patterns relevant to the Lebanese context rather than a comprehensive psychometric scale. Findings related to EB should be interpreted as exploratory. All participants were well-versed in English or French and, therefore, completed the survey in either of these languages.

Physical activity assessment

Physical activity (PA) was assessed using the short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), a validated tool for diverse populations [16], which evaluates frequency and duration of walking, moderate, vigorous activities, as well as sedentary behavior. The total score is calculated based on frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per day) of activities in each domain. The responses are used to estimate weekly PA energy expenditure (in MET-minutes per week). Participants were classified into three levels [17]:

Psychological Health

Anxiety levels were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7) scale, a validated 7-item self-report instrument by Spitzer et al. [18], which evaluates symptoms such as nervousness, excessive worry, restlessness, and irritability, over the past two weeks. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all, 3 = nearly every day), with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. The intensity of anxiety was classified as minimal (0–4), mild [5–9], moderate [10–14], or severe [15–21]. The GAD-7 has demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy, with a sensitivity of 0.83 and a specificity of 0.84 for detecting generalized anxiety disorder. A cut-off score of ≥ 10 is commonly cited in literature to identify the presence of clinically significant anxiety symptoms [18].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, EB, PA levels, and anxiety severity. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Associations between categorical variables were assessed using cross-tabulations. When more than two categories were present and more than 20% of expected cell counts were < 5, Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation was applied to estimate p-values. Effect sizes for associations between categorical variables were reported using chi-square (χ²). Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between anxiety outcomes and selected independent predictors. Overall model significance was evaluated using the Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients. Model adequacy was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The explained variance was assessed using Cox & Snell R2 and Nagelkerke R² statistics, with increases in these values indicating improved model fit. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.

Results

A total of 401 participants completed the questionnaire and were included in the final analysis. The sample size was predominantly females (75.6%), with males representing 24.4% of participants. Nearly half of the participants resided in Mount Lebanon (49.6%), followed by Beirut (35.9%), while the remainder were distributed across other governorates in smaller proportions. More than half were undergraduates (56.1%), while 38.9% were graduate students, and the majority were enrolled in health sciences faculties (59.9%). Regarding socioeconomic status, 42.6% of participants reported a monthly income or allowance exceeding 500 USD, whereas 20.4% reported less than 150 USD. Most participants were full-time students (66.1%), while 34.0% combined their studies with part- or full-time employment. Household living conditions, assessed using the crowding index, indicated that 56.6% lived in moderately crowded households (1–2 persons per room), 38.7% in non-crowded households (≤ 1 person per room), and 4.7% in severely crowded conditions (≥ 2 persons per room). Based on the GAD-7, 43.6% of students presented clinically significant anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 10), while 56.4% were not prone to anxiety (GAD-7 < 10). All results are summarized in Table 1.

Table 2 presents the distribution of sociodemographic characteristics, BMI categories, and lifestyle behaviors according to anxiety status, defined as GAD-7 score ≥ 10. Overall, 43.6% were classified as having clinically significant anxiety, while 56.4% did not meet this threshold. Gender distribution differed significantly by anxiety status (p = 0.006), with females comprising a higher proportion of students suffering from this condition (82.3%) compared to those without anxiety (70.4%). In contrast, BMI categories did not differ significantly between students with and without anxiety (p = 0.795). Normal BMI was the most prevalent category in both groups, followed by overweight and obesity. Self-reported weight change over the previous six months was not significantly associated with anxiety (p = 0.095). Although a higher proportion of students with anxiety reported weight gain (46.3%) compared to those without anxiety (39.8%), this difference did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, EB categories were comparable across anxiety groups (p = 0.178), with most participants classified as having mediocre EB, regardless of anxiety status. Only a small proportion of students achieved an acceptable EB score in both groups. Alcohol consumption showed a statistically significant association with anxiety (p = 0.036), with approximately 51% of participants reporting no alcohol use. Smoking status was significantly associated with anxiety (p = 0.016). Students with anxiety were more likely to report being smokers (24.6%) compared to those without anxiety (15.0%). PA levels did not differ significantly according to anxiety status (p = 0.216). Low, moderate, and vigorous PA levels were similarly distributed among students with and without anxiety. In contrast, employment status was significantly associated with anxiety (p = 0.014). Students combining their studies with full-time employment represented a higher proportion of those with anxiety (21.7%) compared to those without anxiety (12.4%), whereas part-time employment was more common among students without anxiety.

Table 3 presents the associations between anxiety status and conflict-related exposures, perceived burden of online learning, and conflict-induced behavioral shifts during the data collection. Several factors were significantly associated with anxiety among university students. Students directly exposed to bombings showed a substantially higher prevalence of anxiety (41.1%) compared to those not directly affected (19.9%) (p < 0.001). Similarly, being forced to leave home due to bombardments was significantly linked to anxiety, with 30.3% of displaced students reporting anxiety versus 16.8% among those who were not displaced (p = 0.001). Perceived impact of online classes was also significantly associated with anxiety. Students who reported that virtual classes were more stressful had a markedly higher prevalence of anxiety (58.3%) compared to those reporting no change (27.4%) or less stress (14.3%) (p < 0.001). Similarly, those who reported that remote classes had a strong negative impact on concentration or motivation, showed higher prevalence of anxiety (40.6%) compared to those reporting “moderately” (27.4%), “a little” (21.1%), or “not at all” (10.9%) (p = 0.003). Behavioral changes during the conflict were also significantly related to anxiety. Changes in appetite showed a strong association, with higher prevalence among students reporting increased (52.0%) or decreased urge to eat (33.1%) compared to those reporting no change (14.9%) (p < 0.001). Similarly, modifications in PA were also linked to anxiety (p = 0.007); students reporting decreased status exhibited a higher prevalence of anxiety (68.6%) than those reporting no change (21.7%) or increased activity (9.7%). Finally, changes in alcohol and tobacco consumption were significantly associated with anxiety (p = 0.042). Anxiety was more prevalent among students reporting either increased consumption (12.0%) or decreased consumption (5.1%) compared to those reporting no change (82.9%).

A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors independently associated with anxiety (GAD ≥ 10) among university students (Table 4). Variables included in the model were selected based on theoretical relevance, evidence from prior literature, and factors showing an association with anxiety in bivariate analyses. The Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients was significant (p < 0.05), indicating that the model with predictors performed better than the null model. Model adequacy was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which indicated acceptable fit (p > 0.05) and in the fully adjusted Model, the explained variance increased to 21.9% (Cox & Snell R2) and 29.4% (Nagelkerke R2), suggesting substantial improvement in predictive ability. Employment status was significantly associated with anxiety. Compared with students who were not working, those engaged in full-time employment had higher odds of anxiety (OR = 2.62; 95% CI: 1.20–5.73). Direct exposure to bombings was independently associated with increased anxiety, with exposed students having approximately 2.8 times the odds of anxiety compared to those not exposed (OR = 2.77; 95% CI: 1.29–5.92). Academic disruption also showed a significant relationship with anxiety. Students who reported that online classes affected their concentration or motivation “a lot” had more than twice the odds of anxiety compared to those reporting no impact (OR = 2.24; 95% CI: 1.00-4.99). Changes in appetite demonstrated the strongest association with anxiety. Students reporting decreased appetite had over three times higher odds of anxiety (OR = 3.63; 95% CI: 1.91–6.90), while those reporting increased appetite also had significantly higher odds (OR = 3.37; 95% CI: 1.88–6.04) compared with students reporting no change. All other covariables were not significantly associated with the presence of anxiety (GAD ≥ 10) after adjustment in the multivariable model.

Discussion

Lebanon’s prolonged history of political instability and conflict increases the likelihood of exposure to traumatic events among the general population, and university students are not exempt from this risk. Previous research conducted among this subgroup of the population, the Lebanese students, has also documented substantial associations between sociopolitical stressors and poor mental health outcomes, including anxiety and burnout, in contexts of socioeconomic and political unrest [20].

This study is the first cross-sectional investigation conducted among Lebanese university students during a period of active conflict. The sample consisted predominantly of female students (75.6%), which aligns with findings from a recent UK undergraduate study reporting higher female participation in online surveys studies [21]. Gender was significantly associated with anxiety, with females reporting higher prevalence compared to males. This is consistent with a substantial body of literature indicating that women are generally more susceptible to anxiety disorders due to a combination of biological, hormonal, and psychosocial factors that influence stress responsiveness and emotional regulation [22]. Polyvianania et al. (2025) found that 34.1% of Ukrainian university students experienced moderate-to-severe anxiety twenty months after the start of the Russian Ukrainian war, with female students being particularly vulnerable [23].

BMI categories were not significantly associated with anxiety in this study. This contrasts with previous research, such as those published in 2022 by Wang et al., who found higher prevalence of anxiety among obese college students. Their study also demonstrated that calorie restriction significantly alleviated anxiety symptoms in obese students, suggesting that weight status and dietary regulation can directly influence psychological well-being [24]. The discrepancy may be explained by differences in sample composition, as most of our participants were within the normal BMI range, whereas Wang et al. focused on obese students. It is also plausible that, in Lebanon, external stressors such as conflict, academic pressure, and socioeconomic instability exert a stronger influence on anxiety than anthropometric measurements, thereby masking potential associations. Weight changes also reflected anxiety-related behavior: students reporting increased or decreased appetite, suffered more from weight gain (46%) or loss (33%) over the past six months. This pattern is consistent with evidence from a systematic review among university and college students, which showed that stress and anxiety may lead to weight fluctuations (gain or loss), depending on individual coping mechanism, emotional eating tendencies, and appetite regulation [28].

The lack of significant associations between habitual EB and anxiety may be due to limited variability, as most participants had similar EB scores. University students, particularly those in health-related fields, tend to possess greater nutrition knowledge and awareness compared to the general population, which may attenuate the expected association between EB and anxiety. Indeed, higher nutrition literacy has been shown to protect against emotional eating and stress-induced dietary changes [25, 26]. Similar outcomes have been observed in previous studies, where overall dietary patterns were not significantly linked to anxiety; this may be due to non-modifiable factors, such as genetic predispositions or other personal and cultural differences, exerting a stronger influence on anxiety [27].

Typically, individuals with healthier dietary patterns often engage in regular PA; however, this relationship is not always linear. Interestingly, no significant association was observed between PA and anxiety (p = 0.216). In the present study, low-to-moderate PA predominated and overall engagement was below the WHO guidelines, which recommend at least 150 min of moderate-intensity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity per week [28]. A possible explanation is that university students did not engage in their usual sports activities because of safety issues, displacement, fear of bombardment, and prolonged residence in shelters and safe zones. Those factors may have influenced and disrupted usual lifestyle and behavioral patterns, potentially overshadowing the protective effects of PA on mental health. While numerous studies have documented the beneficial impact of PA on reducing anxiety levels among university students, the relationship is multifaceted and shaped by individual, environmental, and psychosocial factors. In contexts of active conflict, these conditions may substantially alter the anxiety-PA relationship, as persistent fear, perceived insecurity, and ongoing uncertainty, even in the absence of direct exposure to bombings, may have intensified anxiety levels beyond the protective effects of PA. Kowalkowska et al. reported that PA was associated with emotional eating in males and cognitive restraint in females, suggesting that psychological traits and gender-specific factors may modulate the EB-PA relationship [29]. Moreover, Sun et al. (2024) suggest that PA may exert both direct and indirect benefits for anxiety reduction, mediated through lifestyle habits and dietary behavior [30]. Studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period of heightened stress, highlighted a significant negative correlation between PA levels and mental health issues, such as anxiety and depression, among college students in China during the COVID-19 pandemic [31]. Importantly, anxiety experienced during armed conflict differs fundamentally from anxiety during public health crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic, as it is driven by persistent threats to safety, uncertainty, and trauma rather than temporary restrictions or health-related fears. These contextual differences may reduce the detectable protective effects of PA, particularly when assessed using self-reported measures at a single time point. Although changes in PA were significantly associated with anxiety in bivariate analysis, this association did not persist after adjustment for other variables. This suggests that the observed relationship may be attributable to confounding by more proximal conflict-related stressors, such as direct exposure to bombings, appetite changes during conflict, and academic disruptions, which remained independently associated with anxiety in the multivariable model. It is plausible that, during periods of acute conflict, the psychological burden imposed by direct stressors may outweigh the influence of PA, thereby diminishing its potential protective effect against anxiety. In such circumstances, lifestyle behaviors such as PA may become secondary to more immediate concerns related to safety and survival.

Changes in alcohol and tobacco consumption, whether they increase or decrease, were also linked to anxiety, despite the relatively low overall prevalence of these behaviors in this sample. Marmaru Melkam et al. reported a significant association between substance use and anxiety among Ethiopian youth [32]. In Lebanon, cultural norms typically frame alcohol consumption as a social or celebratory practice rather than habitual use. Notably, more than half of the participants reported abstaining from alcohol, reflecting religious and cultural norms in Muslim communities that limit its use. Nonetheless, the observed associations suggest that even moderate changes in alcohol and tobacco consumption may reflect attempts to cope with psychological distress [33], as reported by Abbas et al. with 37% of displaced Palestinians and Syrians in Lebanese refugee camps relying on alcohol and cigarettes as a coping mechanism [34].

In the present study, the frequency of consumption of specific food groups of the EB questionnaire was not statistically associated with anxiety (Supplementary Table 1). These findings align with a recent publication by Boutros et al., in Lebanese university students [35]. Consistently, a cross-sectional analysis from the Mitchelstown cohort reported no significant associations between dietary measures and anxiety after adjustment for potential confounders, despite observing links between diet quality and general well-being [27]. This suggests that in a cross-sectional protocol, diet-anxiety relationships may be weak or non-detectable when dietary exposure is assessed using general diet quality indicators rather that stress-sensitive behavioral measures.

Employment status emerged as a significant correlate of anxiety, with students engaged in full-time employment exhibiting higher odds of anxiety compared with part-time or non-employed peers. This finding aligns with evidence demonstrating that employment intensity constitutes a significant factor of psychological distress. Moxham et al. reported that nursing students who combined academic training with employment experienced higher levels of anxiety, largely attributable to burnout, fatigue, and limited time for recovery [36]. In the Lebanese context, full-time employment may further reflect heightened financial pressure and household responsibilities, which are exacerbated by ongoing socio-political instability, a potent driver of anxiety [37].

The findings indicated a high prevalence of anxiety, which was independently associated with conflict-related stressors, particularly with changes in appetite, exposure to bombings, and perceived stress related to online learning. Direct exposure to bombings and forced displacement were strongly associated with anxiety. From a theoretical perspective, anxiety in such settings reflects a sustained state of anticipatory threat. Alotaibi’s framework on future anxiety among war-affected youth, conceptualizes this phenomenon as the cumulative effect of prolonged exposure to violence, chronic uncertainty, and perceived insecurity, which together undermine individuals’ sense of predictability and control over the future [38]. This persistent disruption of future-oriented cognitions may, in turn, manifest as enduring anxiety even in the absence of direct trauma.

Challenges in online learning, particularly those affecting concentration or motivation, have been shown to contribute to heightened psychological distress among students. This aligns with research showing that obstacles related to online learning, including difficulties with engagement and academic worries, are linked to increased psychological distress among students [39]. A meta-analysis of studies on remote learning during the COVID-19 pandemic similarly documented high prevalence of anxiety and stress among students engaged in online education, reinforcing that remote academic environments can contribute to mental health burden [40]. Despite contextual differences, these findings suggest that academic disruption and increased cognitive demands associated with virtual academic platforms contribute to anxiety across the student. Besides, in conflict settings, unstable internet access, electricity shortages, and non-conductive home environments may further exacerbate stress and impair learning.

Importantly, habitual lifestyle characteristics (EB scores and baseline PA levels) were not significantly associated with anxiety (p > 0.05). In contrast, conflict-related behavioral changes, particularly changes in appetite, were strongly associated with anxiety, while changes in PA were significant in bivariate analysis but did not remain independently associated after adjustment. These results suggest that anxiety during active conflict may be more strongly influenced by acute environmental stressors and perceived threat than by stable lifestyle characteristics [41]. Changes in appetite emerged as the strongest behavioral correlation of anxiety in this sample. Appetite dysregulation is a well-established response to stress and anxiety and may serve as an acute psychophysiological indicator that is not fully captured by conventional dietary quality indices [42]. It is possible that in conflict settings, where food availability, safety, and daily routines are disrupted, traditional measures of EB lack sensitivity to stress-related changes in food intake. Under such conditions, self-reported appetite changes may represent a more responsive indicator of stress-associated eating patterns.

Strengths and Limitations

The study was the first to provide region-specific insights into anxiety and perceived conflict-related stressors among university students in the Middle East during an active armed conflict period. Data were collected during political instability in Lebanon, which represents a rare and timely opportunity to document anxiety and related stressors among university students under real-world conditions. The use of a validated anxiety screening tool (GAD-7) and a widely used PA questionnaire (IPAQ-SF) enhanced comparability of key outcome measures; however, the adapted EB screening tool was used for descriptive and exploratory purposes and was not validated as a psychometric scale in this context. Additionally, the study demonstrated the feasibility of conducting mental health research among students during periods of severe disruption, when traditional face-to-face data collection was not possible.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the use of non-probability snowball sampling through social media introduces substantial selection and coverage bias. The sample was predominantly female and largely composed of students enrolled in health-related fields, which may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias, as these students may have greater mental health awareness and different lifestyle behaviors compared to the broader student population. Additionally, the majority of participants resided in Beirut and Mount Lebanon, limiting representativeness of the entire population of Lebanese university students. Although students from the South and heavily affected Beirut suburbs were proportionally fewer in our sample, bombardments and conflict-related stressors occurred across multiple Lebanese governorates during the study period. Nevertheless, the underrepresentation of students from the most severely affected areas may have led to underestimation of anxiety prevalence. As a result, findings cannot be generalized to the broader population of Lebanese university students. Besides, self-reported measures may have introduced recall or social desirability bias, potentially leading to over- or underestimation of certain variables such as BMI, EB, mental health, or PA levels. Additionally, smoking and sleeping patterns were not investigated by validated tools and alcohol intake was not fully quantified. The accuracy of the crowding index may have been compromised by conflict-related displacement too. Many families were temporarily forced to share housing or reside in public shelters, potentially biasing household density estimates and limiting the reliability of this socioeconomic indicator. Weight-change data should be interpreted as a general indicator of recent lifestyle fluctuation rather than a conflict-specific outcome. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits the ability to assess temporal relationships between conflict exposure, behavioral changes, and anxiety.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional research found a high prevalence of anxiety among Lebanese university students during a period of conflict, war, and political instability. The results obtained suggest thatcontextual academic and occupational pressures, rather than lifestyle behaviors, were the primary factors associated with anxiety severity. Measurement limitations, behavioral constraints imposed by insecurity, and restricted variability in EB and PA may have contributed to these findings. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to better capture temporal relationships between conflict exposure, psychological distress, and behavioral responses. Improved and validated measures of conflict exposure, EB, and lifestyle factors, along with more representative sampling strategies and inclusion of key psychosocial confounders, are needed to strengthen inference and inform targeted interventions in conflict-affected university populations.

At the national level, policy makers should reinforce food-security initiatives, particularly during periods of armed conflict, to ensure that all vulnerable population subgroups have access to affordable and nutritious foods. In parallel, efforts should be made to provide safe and accessible public spaces, such as parks, to promote PA and help mitigate anxiety among affected communities.
