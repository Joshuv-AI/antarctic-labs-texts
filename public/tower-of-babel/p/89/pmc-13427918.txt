Patient-reported headache before and after surgery for pituitary adenomas: a longitudinal single-centre study
Authors: Per Sveino Strand, Benjamin Minh-van Richoz, Lisa Millgård Sagberg, Ole Solheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo what extent pituitary adenomas cause headache is controversial and the impact of transsphenoidal surgery on headache remains uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, single-centre study of patients undergoing surgery for pituitary adenomas between 2014 and 2024. Headache was assessed using the EORTC QLQ-BN20 module, question four, at baseline, one month, and six months postoperatively. Demographic, clinical, and surgical data were analysed for associations with headache outcomes.ResultsOf 207 eligible patients, 134 completed all assessments (63% male; median age 55 years). Preoperatively, 50% reported no headache, 33% a little, 8% quite a bit, and 9% very much headache. Headache was more common in females, but there was no relation to tumour related factors. At one month, 31% improved, 52% remained unchanged, and 16% worsened. At six months, 34% improved, 56% remained unchanged, and had 10% worsened compared to preoperatively. Among patients with preoperative headache (n = 67), 52% reported improvement at one month and 55% at six months. Among patients with no headache preoperatively, 25% and 12% reported having headache at one and six months, respectively. No demographic, tumour-related, or perioperative variables were significantly associated with headache change.ConclusionIn this prospective longitudinal study, approximately half of patients with preoperative headache reported improvement following pituitary surgery, while a minority experienced worsening. No clear clinical predictors of headache before surgery or outcome after surgery were identified, suggesting a multifactorial nature of headache in patients with pituitary adenomas.Keywords: Pituitary adenomas, Headache, Surgical outcomes, Tumour

Introduction

Pituitary adenomas are common intracranial tumours, and due to the increasing use and availability of neuroimaging the reported incidence is increasing [19].

Headache is a common symptom in patients with pituitary adenomas, reported in 33–70% before treatment [1, 21] and guidelines from the Endocrine Society suggest that surgery for pituitary incidentaloma should be considered in patients with unremitting headache [7]. However, headache is also common in the general population with an estimated one-year prevalence of 65%, complicating causal interference [12]. Thus, the effect of pituitary surgery on headache symptoms remains debated. Although some studies describe persistent or worsening symptoms after surgery, most previous studies find that a majority have less headache after surgery [2, 4, 6, 9, 10, 14, 17, 18, 22, 23]. Few prospective studies have been published on the topic so far. In a recent systematic review of 111 patients with nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas and 75 patients with Rathke’s cleft cysts, only one of thirteen studies were prospective, and after surgery 77%, 19%, and 5% experienced improvement, no change, or deterioration, respectively [22]. In the prospective studies in the literature, worsening of headache ranges from 0 to 17% after surgery [2, 4, 17].

Reported predictors of headache outcome have been inconsistently observed, which makes it difficult to predict which patients that might experience relief after surgery. A better understanding of headache before and after surgery might help guide expectations for surgeons and patients prior to adenoma surgery.

We longitudinally assessed patient-reported headache before and after undergoing transsphenoidal pituitary adenoma surgery at a tertiary care centre, aiming to quantify postoperative changes and explore potential clinical predictors.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This was a prospective, single-centre cohort study conducted at St. Olavs University Hospital, Trondheim, Norway, between January 2014 and December 2024. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing surgery for histologically confirmed pituitary adenomas were eligible. Data was registered in a prospective local tumour registry, based on informer consent. Patients unable to complete questionnaires or lost to follow-up were excluded. Clinical variables included age, sex, ASA class, operative details, postoperative complications (including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak), and endocrine outcomes.

Patient-reported headache was assessed using question 4 of the EORTC QLQ-BN20 (“Did you have headaches?”), rated on a four-point scale (not at all, a little, quite a bit, very much) [20]. Questionnaires were filled out 1–3 days prior to surgery, and repeated one and six months after surgery by structured phone interviews conducted by study nurses. Changes were categorized as improvement, no change, or worsening from baseline headache status.

Tumour volume registration was done as described in earlier studies where the largest diameter in coronal (d1), sagittal (d2) and transversal (d3) planes were used to calculate the tumour volume using the formula π/6*d1*d2*d3 [8]. Volume estimations and scoring of Knosp grades were done by a resident in neurosurgery and, if needed, a consultant neurosurgeon was consulted [15].

All patients were operated under general anaesthesia, with microscopic transsphenoidal surgery, and some with endoscope assistance. Follow-ups after surgery was routinely done by an endocrinologist three months after surgery.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were done using SPSS statistics version 30.0 [3]. Descriptive statistics summarized demographics and outcomes. Changes in headache were categorized as improvement, no change or worsening from baseline headache status. Associations between categorical and continuous variables and headache change were assessed using chi-square tests and Mann–Whitney-U tests, respectively. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Assuming that missing data were missing at random, we performed complete data analyses.

Ethics

A study-specific ethical approval was obtained by the Regional Ethical Committee (REK 823997). All patients provided written consent to participate in research. The study was done according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

In the study period, 207 patients were operated for a pituitary adenoma at our institution. In total, 134 patients completed questionnaires on HRQoL prior to, 1 and 6 months after surgery. Of these, 63% were male; median age 55 years, range 18–88. Most patients had a low ASA-score and were primary surgeries. Only 31 and 11 patients presented with acromegaly or Cushing’s disease, respectively. Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. No statistically significant differences in baseline data or tumour volume between patients included and excluded in the study were found.

Discussion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that approximately 50% of patients operated for pituitary adenoma report headache preoperatively. The prevalence was higher in female patients, but no tumour related risk factors were identified. Among patients with preoperative headache about 50% report improvement after surgery but 1/5 and 1/10 report worse headache at 1 and 6 months, respectively. Approximately 1/10 patients without headache before surgery report headache at 6 months, but usually milder symptoms. In patients with headache at baseline, no tumour or patient related factors were predictive of change in headache symptoms after surgery. While surgery may be associated with an improvement in headache symptoms in many patients, worsening can also occur and predicting the course of headache symptoms remains difficult.

Reported changes in headache symptoms following surgery varies widely; from 53–93% improvement [9, 11, 13, 17], to persistent headache in 12–20% at 6–12 months [9, 14, 22, 23], underscoring that tumour resection does not necessarily ensure improvement of headaches. Our findings mirror this variability; while most patients experience stable or improved headache status, a notable subset also reported worsening.

Although headache is common in the general population, an overrepresentation of headache symptoms among patients with pituitary tumours has been reported [8]. The observed prevalence of headache in our patients at baseline (50%) is also higher than in the general population (14%) assessed with the headache question in the BN-20 instrument [12]. The highest prevalence of pre-treatment headache has been reported in younger females with a preexisting headache disorder [2, 4, 17], suggesting that headache susceptibility may play a role. However, no clear clinical, radiological or endocrinological risk factors have been established, as also seen in the present study, and the mechanisms behind headache in pituitary patients are not well understood. Suggested pathophysiology include dural stretching, trigeminal irritation, hormonal hyper—or hyposecretion, mass effect or alterations of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, but none are confirmed. A diversity of possible mechanisms might explain why surgical decompression does not uniformly relieve symptoms. Our findings, like prior studies, suggest that patients with hormonally active adenomas do not consistently experience better or worse headache trajectories compared to those with non-functioning tumours [4, 6, 23]. Tumour volume and invasion, often assumed to correlate with headache burden, were also not predictive of headache outcome. This might suggest the dominance of non–mass effect mechanisms in many patients and potentially also be coloured by the high prevalence of primary headache disorders in the general population. Without formal preoperative headache phenotyping, distinguishing tumour-related headaches from unrelated primary headache syndromes remains challenging. Future studies should include screening for preexisting headache disorders, headache phenotyping prior to surgery and include control groups to better conceptualize change in headaches over time.

No reliable clinical predictors of headache change were identified in this study. Previous studies have variably implicated tumour size, cavernous sinus invasion, and hormonal function, but findings remain inconsistent, emphasizing the need for future studies on the topic [4, 13, 23]. The Pituitary Society International Guidelines suggests that “risks and benefits of surgery be considered for patients with severe headache refractory to anti-headache medication after evaluation in a multidisciplinary clinic, including by an expert neurologist” [5], whereas a review suggested that “severe headaches are indications for surgery” [16]. However, our findings, in addition to the existing literature, indicate that it remains difficult to predict which patients who will benefit from surgery, with regards to headaches. Patients should be counselled that surgery is frequently followed by an improvement of headache, but outcomes remain variable and unpredictable. Preoperative headache classification, or screening for co-existing headache disorders, by a neurologist may guide patients’ expectations.

Our results are also consistent with previous work from our group [8]. In a retrospective analysis on headache in pituitary adenoma patients, we found that most of both patients who underwent treatment and those who did not receive any treatment, reported an improvement of headache over time. Also, in that study, there were no association between radiological, clinical, or endocrine markers and headache. The current prospective longitudinal study strengthens this observation; despite a comprehensive evaluation of clinical variables, tumour volume, Knosp grade, hormonal status, and perioperative variables, no clinical predictors reliably forecast headache improvement or worsening.

Headache is often a dynamic and remitting symptom and before-after studies, especially with retrospective assessments are prone to both recall bias and improvement due to regression to the mean. Strengths of the current study include the prospective, longitudinal design, standardized questionnaire, and relatively large sample size. Still, the four-point scale to assess headache may be too coarse of a measurement leading to a floor-ceiling effect where true changes may not be detected. Further, the scale might be interpreted differently by different patients, as some may answer based on headache frequency, and others on headache intensity. This might make it less clear how to interpret headache status. Even longer follow up of both operated and non-operated patients would better capture longer term dynamics of symptoms. Further, although we found no differences in baseline data between patients with complete questionnaires and those who were excluded, one cannot be certain that data are missing completely at random. In addition, the current study did not report on the use of medical treatment for headache before or after surgery, which may be a confounding factor.

Conclusion

In this prospective longitudinal study, approximately 50% of patients with preoperative headache reported improvement after transsphenoidal pituitary surgery, while a minority worsened. Approximately 1/10 patients without headache before surgery report some headache at 6 months. No clinical predictors reliably forecast headache outcome, suggesting a multifactorial aetiology of headache in pituitary adenomas.
