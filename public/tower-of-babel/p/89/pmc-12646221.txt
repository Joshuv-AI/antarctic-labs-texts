Fluorescein-based dyes are not valid reporters of oxidative stress in bacteria, and conclusions based on their use must be reconsidered
Authors: Sergey Korshunov, James A. Imlay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceOver the past two decades, workers have considered the possibility that intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) mediate the bactericidal effects of many stresses, including those imposed by natural or manmade antibiotics. Because ROS are difficult to detect directly, investigators infer their presence by their effects upon other molecules. The most widespread approach has been to use fluorescein-based dyes that become fluorescent when oxidized. Investigations using this method have concluded that ROS underpin myriad killing effects, with implications for the directions of subsequent studies. However, the current paper reveals that those results have been driven by artifacts. Therefore, it is necessary to put aside prior conclusions and to reexamine oxidative-stress hypotheses using alternative methods.

When bacteria grow in oxic environments, they must cope with reactive oxygen species (ROS). Superoxide and hydrogen peroxide are produced when molecular oxygen steals electrons from redox-active cofactors of enzymes (1, 2). These ROS can then oxidize the iron cofactors of metalloenzymes, eliminating their activity and interrupting the pathways to which they belong (3, 4). Reactions between loose iron and hydrogen peroxide also produce hydroxyl radicals, which can oxidize DNA (5). Cells have evolved to minimize these events by synthesizing superoxide dismutases, peroxidases, and catalases, which lower superoxide and H2O2 concentrations to subtoxic levels.

However, the titers of these scavenging enzymes are calibrated to barely avoid toxicity from normal ROS production (4); therefore, circumstances that accelerate ROS production can overwhelm these defenses. In this way the exposure of cells to hyperoxia or to redox-cycling antibiotics can block growth and elevate mutagenesis (6, 7). This tight balance has prompted workers to wonder whether other stresses, too, might somehow thereby owe their toxicity to accelerated ROS formation. This question is difficult to answer a priori, because the primary sources of ROS inside bacterial cells are unsettled. The respiratory chain was considered to be a likely candidate, but H2O2 production was undiminished in Escherichia coli mutants deficient in that chain (8). The cell contains many other redox enzymes, and without further information it is hard to know whether cell stresses might predispose some of them to transfer electrons to oxygen.

This issue received much attention after the publication of papers that implicated ROS in cell killing by several important clinical antibiotics (9–11). Strikingly, these various antibiotics attack disconnected cell processes—translation, cell-wall synthesis, and DNA replication—seemingly precluding a simple, unified explanation. Subsequent studies suggested that ROS production might mediate bacterial killing by an even wider array of stressors that are of substantial interest to microbiologists: heat (e.g., ref. 12), cold (13), metal overload (14), nanoparticles (15), organic solvents (16), addiction cassettes (17), type VI secretion systems (18), phage infection (18), antimicrobial peptides (19), bile salts (20), heavy metals (21), high and low pH (22), various natural products (23), solar radiation (24), carbon monoxide (25), and more. Consequently, the idea has been broached that bacterial stress per se might trigger a common molecular mechanism of ROS production (26, 27). No such mechanism has been identified.

To detect oxidative stress, most of these studies relied upon the use of antioxidants and/or redox-sensitive dyes. These methods originated in eukaryotic studies, and it seemed reasonable to apply them as well to bacteria. Antioxidants are small organic molecules that can chemically degrade superoxide, H2O2, and/or hydroxyl radical in vitro, and when their addition to stressed cells increases survival, a role for ROS has been inferred. However, eukaryotic systems did not provide good control systems to validate this approach. In fact, recent work with E. coli has demonstrated that these compounds are not nearly reactive enough to lower ROS levels inside cells (28). For example, when putative antioxidants were added to mutants that lack scavenging enzymes, these compounds were unable to suppress the phenotypic growth defects of these cells or to boost the activities of their ROS-damaged enzymes. Instead, while antioxidants can indeed suppress cell killing by various stresses, this effect apparently derives from the fact that they slow cell growth.

Redox-sensitive dyes, then, comprise the most relied-upon indicator of ROS stress (29). Most are reduced derivatives of dichlorofluorescein diacetate. Fig. 1 depicts their principle of action. Passive diffusion of the dye across the cell membranes brings it into the cytoplasm, where esterases trap it by hydrolyzing its acetyl groups. Univalent oxidation of the dye by intracellular superoxide or hydroxyl radicals would generate a radical species. Subsequently, either comproportionation of two radicals, or spontaneous electron transfer to oxygen, would produce an oxidized form that is highly fluorescent. A similar effect could be achieved if the reduced dye were divalently oxidized by H2O2. These dyes were first applied to the detection of ROS in mammalian cells. By one estimate they have been used in more than 6,000 papers (30).

(A) Standard model of intracellular dye oxidation. The reduced, acetylated dye H2CDCFDA diffuses passively into cells, where nonspecific esterases (31) release a hydrophilic form (H2CDCF) that will not escape the cell. The prevailing model is that ROS oxidize the dye to its fluorescent product, CDCF*. (See SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for structures.) (B) Determination of cellular content of oxidized and reduced dye species. Dye-loaded cells are recovered by centrifugation, and the dye forms are extracted with chloroform. Immediate fluorescence measurements determine the amount of oxidized dye. Treatment with horseradish peroxidase/H2O2 oxidizes the remaining reduced dye, and a second fluorescence measurement determines the total dye that was extracted.

However, their use was not without objection. Fluorescein dye oxidation by other compounds, including nitrogen species such as NO2 or peroxynitrite, was detected in vitro. Cytochrome c appeared to oxidize the dyes directly. The high-valent compound I heme intermediate of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) can do so as well. While none of these non-ROS species are abundant in most bacteria, these observations raised the possibility that other species might similarly interfere with the assay. This point has been made repeatedly in cautionary papers authored by ROS specialists (30, 32–37).

Nevertheless, over the past two decades many microbiologists have relied upon fluorescein-based dyes as ROS probes. The focus of the present work was to determine whether these dyes are reliable indicators of elevated ROS. Instead, the data show that common intracellular electron carriers, including flavins and quinones, can directly oxidize such a dye without any ROS involvement. Further, both the reduced and oxidized forms of the dye are continuously ejected from cells by protonmotive force (PMF)-driven pumps; when various stresses impair metabolism, the energy charge drops, these pumps slow, the oxidized dye accumulates inside the cells, and the cell signal is artifactually elevated. Finally, when authentic ROS stress is imposed upon cells, the dye fails to detect it. These facts preclude the use of such dyes as ROS sensors.

Results

ROS Are Not the Cause of Dye Oxidation in Unstressed Cells.

The standard model for ROS detection by fluorescein dyes is depicted in Fig. 1A. The dye enters passively, and cellular esterases remove acetyl groups to create charge that prevents passive exit from the cell. Oxidation of the dye creates a highly fluorescent product. Experimenters typically detect its production by washing, resuspending, and measuring the fluorescence of intact cells or by flow cytometry. In most reports that claim to detect oxidative stress, after ~1 h incubation the dye signal in stressed cells is twofold to fourfold higher than in unstressed cells. Workers have inferred a proportionate increase in the concentration of oxidant.

We monitored the ability of cells to oxidize the reduced fluorescein-based dye 5-(and 6)-carboxy-2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2CDCFDA) to CDCF*, its fluorescent product. (The asterisk denotes a fluorescent compound. Chemical structures are depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S1.) H2CDCFDA was chosen because of its chemical stability and its use in prior studies of putative ROS production by antibiotics (11, 38). Growing cells were treated with the dye under full aerobic conditions.

To quantify the degree of dye oxidation, we used chloroform to extract the dye from E. coli. Dye fluorescence was determined both before and after treatment of this extract with horseradish peroxidase/H2O2 (Fig. 1B). In the presence of H2O2, compound I of HRP quantitatively oxidizes H2CDCF to CDCF* (39), so that this protocol yields the cellular content of both oxidized and total dye. Fig. 2A shows that whereas the original, purchased chemical was only 0.5% oxidized, an additional 4% of the dye was oxidized when recovered from cells. These data confirm that cells contain an oxidant that converts H2CDCF to CDCF*.

Cells were loaded with 20 μM H2CDCFDA for 20 min. The cells were harvested, the dye extracted with chloroform, and the intracellular levels of oxidized and reduced dye determined. Where indicated, 1 mM of the cell-permeable chelator dipyridyl (dipy) was added to the culture shortly before the dye, in order to eliminate intracellular hydroxyl radical production via Fenton chemistry. (B) Rate constants were determined in vitro at 37 °C for the oxidation of H2CDCF by oxygen species and select redox-active biomolecules (Materials and Methods). *Ref. 40.

In publications workers broadly attribute this oxidation to “ROS.” As used here this term subsumes superoxide (O2−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and the hydroxyl radical (HO•), all of which are formed at some amount inside aerobic cells. However, these species have very distinct reactivities. Workers have noted that O2, O2−, and H2O2 react sluggishly with fluorescein-based dyes in vitro, but it has not been clear whether the rates are so low that dyes cannot detect the levels of these oxidants that pertain in vivo. Because the actual intracellular concentrations of these oxygen species are known for E. coli, this question can be answered. We quantified the rate constants at which each of these species oxidizes H2CDCF (Fig. 2B). The constant for molecular oxygen (<0.001 M−1 s−1) can be evaluated in terms of its quantity inside fully aerated cells (210 μM at 37 °C), revealing that molecular oxygen would require >2 d to oxidize 4% of the H2CDCF. Therefore, O2 cannot be the oxidant that converts H2CDCF to CDCF* inside cells. Similar calculations can be made for O2− and H2O2, whose intracellular concentrations are approximately 0.2 nM and 50 nM, respectively (4). These levels would require 80 d and 1.3 y, respectively, to oxidize 4% of the dye, showing that they, too, are not the primary oxidants of H2CDCF inside the cell.

The hydroxyl radical is an extremely powerful oxidant and reacts with H2CDCF, as it does with many biomolecules, at nearly diffusion-limited rates (40). Accordingly, hydroxyl radicals are often regarded as the species most likely to be detected in biological systems by fluorescein dyes. This oxidant is generated inside cells by the Fenton reaction between unincorporated iron and H2O2 (5):[1]FeII+H2O2→FeIII+OH−+HO.

Therefore, the rate of hydroxyl radical formation is proportional to the concentrations of loose Fe(II) and H2O2, multiplied by the rate constant. Direct EPR measurements indicate that inside wild-type E. coli the loose-Fe concentration is typically near 20 μM in defined medium and 60 μM in complex medium (41, 42) and that this iron pool is Fenton-active (43). The rate constant for the Fenton reaction depends upon how the metal is coordinated, and the higher rates at physiological pH project to be approximately 20,000 M−1 s−1 at 37 °C (44). These values suggest that hydroxyl radicals are formed inside aerobic E. coli at a rate near 60 nM/s. Hydroxyl radicals have short lifetimes due to their immediate reactions with biomolecules—primarily the amino acid residues of proteins and the bases of nucleic acids. Accordingly, these targets compete with H2CDCF for oxidation, which has led to concerns that dyes will be ineffective in vivo (33). Our calculations (Materials and Methods) suggest that the 3 μM H2CDCF we detect inside cells will intercept less than 0.002% of hydroxyl radicals, so that 1.5 d would be required to oxidize 4% of the initial H2CDCF. Therefore, although H2CDCF readily detects hydroxyl radicals in vitro, in unstressed cells the observed CDCF* signal should not arise from this source.

This argument by itself does not preclude the possibility that Fenton chemistry will lead to oxidation of the dye in an indirect way. Ferryl species are intermediates in the Fenton reaction, and carbonate radicals and organic radicals are generated when hydroxyl radicals attack other species. Each of these species could plausibly oxidize the dye. This possibility was tested by the use of 2,2’-dipyridyl, a cell-permeable iron chelator that blocks Fenton chemistry (5) (Fig. 2B). Dipyridyl was added to E. coli in quantities fully sufficient to block DNA damage by exogenous H2O2 (5), but it did not suppress the CDCF* signal that arose when H2CDCF was added to cells.

Indeed, rigorously anoxic cells—which cannot generate any ROS—created a CDCF* signal of the same magnitude as oxic cells (Fig. 2B). Collectively, these data show that an intracellular oxidant other than ROS is responsible for the basal production of CDCF*. Further, the level of any ROS would have to rise by an extreme amount for it to create the elevated signal reported in many studies. For that reason we searched for other biomolecules that might oxidize H2CDCF.

Other Biomolecules Can Oxidize H2CDCF.

The conversion of H2CDCF to CDCF* is likely to occur by univalent oxidation. We confirmed that metal species such as Cu(II) and ferricyanide [Fe(CN)63−] are both capable of doing so (Fig. 2A). Oxidized cytochrome c, a high-potential univalent electron carrier, also oxidized it, as others have noted (32). Activity was also detected for manganese-containing superoxide dismutase and the resting state of the heme enzyme horseradish peroxidase. Collectively, these examples demonstrate that H2CDCF can be directly oxidized by three different redox-active biological metals and that they can do so in the context of metalloproteins.

Nitrite (NO2−) exhibited some oxidizing action. Strikingly, the two major univalent electron carriers of cells—flavins and quinones—each directly converted H2CDCF to CDCF*. As expected, NAD+, which is an obligatory divalent oxidant, did not. Inverted membrane vesicles were able to produce CDCF*, and this conversion required that quinones be present in the membranes (Fig. 3). SI Appendix, Supplementary Text S1 shows that the cellular content of quinones and flavins, in conjunction with the rate constants in Fig. 2, are high enough to plausibly explain the cellular CDCF* signal.

The upshot is that non-ROS molecules found inside cells are capable of oxidizing H2CDCF. It is incorrect to regard H2CDCF as a specific probe of ROS.

Cells Actively Export Both H2CDCF and CDCF*, and Metabolic Stresses Impair That Process.

While the preceding data suggest that CDCF* is not a marker of ROS, they do not explain why some stresses can elevate the cellular CDCF* signal. One deficiency of CDCF* analysis has been that the dye signal is not typically normalized to the total dye content (H2CDCF + CDCF*) of the cell. This normalization is standard for other probes used inside cells. The lack of normalization may have seemed acceptable to workers, because H2CDCF and CDCF* were believed to be trapped inside cells once they were deacetylated.

However, we observed that both species are continuously excreted by E. coli (Fig. 4). When cells were loaded with either (reduced) H2CDCFDA or (preoxidized) CDCFDA, the deacetylated forms continuously flowed back out. The efflux of both forms occurred at a speed high enough to potentially disrupt analyses (t1/2 = 90 s), either by reducing the amount of reduced dye available to be oxidized in the cell, or by directly preventing the accumulation of the fluorescent oxidized form. The similarity of the two rates suggested that a common mechanism was responsible. This process was blocked by the uncoupling agent carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP), indicating that it is driven by the PMF.

Both CDCF* and H2CDCF are continuously exported from wild-type (MG1655) cells. Cells were cultured in glucose medium for 20 min with either dye; the dye was then washed away and its export was monitored. Blue: H2CDCFDA was loaded, and export of (deacetylated) H2CDCF was monitored. Red: CDCFDA was loaded, and export of CDCF* was monitored. In both cases efflux was abolished by the protonophore CCCP.

E. coli, like all bacteria, contains a variety of nonspecific PMF-dependent efflux pumps (45, 46). To test whether they mediate dye excretion, cells were treated with already-oxidized CDCFDA, so that the picture was not complicated by issues of redox state. Known inhibitors of these efflux pumps (47) caused CDCF* to accumulate (Fig. 5A). The same result was obtained with a strain that lacked nine of these pumps (Fig. 5B). Efflux was almost entirely dependent upon TolC, the outer membrane channel of most Resistance Nodulation Cell Division (RND-type) efflux pump complexes. Analysis indicated that the AcrAB pump was primarily responsible for CDCF export. In its absence CDCF rose to a higher level; secondary TolC-dependent pumps prevented accumulation to as high a concentration as in the tolC mutant. Notably, when the acrB mutant was exposed to the reduced dye H2CDCF, it also accumulated far more than in wild-type cells, without any change in the CDCF/H2CDCF ratio. Thus, the AcrAB pump affects intracellular dye concentration rather than its oxidation (Fig. 5C). AcrAB is a multidrug efflux pump that exports compounds of diverse structures; it is named for its ability to export acriflavine, which fluorescein dyes resemble (48) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The pump directly removes drugs from the periplasm, thereby minimizing their entry into the cytoplasm; in addition, it facilitates drug efflux from the cytoplasm either by operating downstream of cytoplasmic exporters (49) or by possessing a channel open to the cytoplasm (50).

This linkage between these pumps and dye signals suggested that metabolic stresses, which can diminish the PMF, may slow those pumps and cause H2CDCF and CDCF* to accumulate inside wild-type cells. If so, those stresses would elevate the intracellular CDCF* signal and cause experimenters to misconstrue the result as indicating a high degree of ROS stress. Indeed, the addition of cyanide, which suppresses respiration, strongly raised the CDCF* signal when cells were treated with CDCFDA alone (again, to moot issues of H2CDCF oxidation) (Fig. 6A). So did SOD deficiency, presumably because superoxide poisons enzymes of the TCA cycle (51), which ramifies to lesser PMF production (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). A ubiCA strain with a small quinone pool, which suffers from a diminished ability to generate PMF, also exhibited an elevated dye signal (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Fig. 6 shows that even the quality of growth substrate had large effects upon CDCF* signal. Dye accumulation was high when cells were grown on alanine, a relatively poor carbon source. Dye content and efflux rates were measured when cells were cultivated in media that produce a range of doubling times: Luria broth (LB)/glucose (22’), minimal glucose (60’), and minimal alanine (130’) media. The dye-efflux rate was diminished, and the dye content substantially elevated, in the less nutritious media (Fig. 6B).

The signal was also elevated in cells stressed by cold or high temperature (Fig. 6C). Similar experiments have led workers to suggest that cold and heat elevate ROS production (12, 13); however, in these experiments, too, we treated cells with preoxidized CDCFDA rather than H2CDCFDA, so that oxidation was not involved.

Such control experiments using preoxidized dyes are a straightforward test of whether a given stress may create an artifactual signal by slowing the efflux pumps. Notably, the nonspecificity of efflux pumps ensures that this effect also pertains to other popular fluorescein dyes, including dihydrodichlorofluorescein and hydroxyphenyldichlorofluorescein (HPF) (52) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). It seems likely that these pumps will also act upon other structurally similar redox markers, such as dihydrorhodamine and dihydroethidium (36).

Clinical antibiotics have been proposed to be sources of intracellular ROS stress, based substantially upon H2CDCF experiments. However, aminoglycosides can collapse the PMF (53), raising the possibility that the increase in dye signal arose from pump failure rather than ROS formation. Indeed, we observed that when cells were exposed to CDCFDA, kanamycin caused CDCF* to accumulate intracellularly (Fig. 6D). This effect occurred even when the experiment was conducted in the absence of oxygen, again precluding any role for oxygen species.

In short, the CDCF* signal of stressed cells reflects impairment of (H2)CDCF pumps, rather than any increase in intracellular ROS.

H2CDCF Fails to Detect Authentic Oxidative Stress in E. coli.

The preceding data demonstrate that cell stresses can drive the intracellular accumulation of CDCF*, without any involvement of ROS. A separate question is whether authentic ROS stress can also boost the signal. We tested this idea by examining mutant cells lacking catalase and peroxidase. These mutants accumulate 1 to 2 μM endogenous H2O2. This level is 10 to 20 times the level in wild-type cells, and it is sufficient to inactivate enzymes and disrupt key metabolic pathways (54). To enhance the sensitivity of the experiment, H2CDCF oxidation was tested in catalase/peroxidase mutants that also lacked TolC.

Fig. 7 shows that the oxidation of H2CDCF was no greater in the H2O2-stressed cells than in their scavenger-proficient parents. Thus, the dye does not detect the level of H2O2 to which one wants a detector molecule to respond—a level sufficient to impair core cellular functions.

Reduced dye (H2CDCFDA) was added for 20 min, and the cellular H2CDCF and CDCF* content was then determined. Amino acids were included in the medium to bypass biosynthetic defects of the catalase/peroxidase mutant.

The hydroxyl radical is regarded as the ROS most likely to oxidize H2CDCF. It is also most relevant to cell killing: Unlike superoxide and H2O2, hydroxyl radicals can directly generate lethal lesions in DNA (5). The calculations in the first section of the Results raised the question, however, as to whether H2CDCF would be able to detect even a high level of hydroxyl radical. The addition of exogenous H2O2 by itself does not produce enough hydroxyl radicals to kill most wild-type cells. However, the combination of added H2O2 and cystine can do so (41). Imported cystine is rapidly reduced in a glutathione-dependent process to cysteine (55), which accelerates the Fenton reaction by rapidly reducing intracellular Fe(III) back to Fe(II):[2]Fe(II) + H2O2 → Fe(III) + OH− + HO.

[3] cysteine+FeIII→0.5 cysteine +FeII.

This combination killed >90% of cells within 3 min (Fig. 8A), and it produced ample polymerase-blocking DNA lesions. The involvement of Fenton chemistry was confirmed by the ability of dipyridyl to block both effects.

The polymerase-blocking lesions were quantified by PCR (Fig. 8B) (41). Approximately 1.6 such lesions were detected per 10 kb of single-strand DNA, indicating that the genome accumulated 1,500 impassible lesions. Yet despite the overwhelming lethality of this treatment, the CDCF* signal inside H2CDCF-loaded cells was unchanged (Fig. 8C). Some thought explains why H2CDCF could not detect this hydroxyl-radical storm. DNA bases react as quickly with hydroxyl radicals as does H2CDCF, with rate constants exceeding 109 M−1 s−1 (56); therefore, because 0.01% of DNA bases were damaged, one expects only 0.01% of the dye to have been oxidized by this treatment. As 0.5% of the dye was already oxidized at the outset of the experiment, such a tiny increase in dye oxidation would be far too little to elevate the CDCF signal above baseline.

This nonresponsiveness of H2CDCF indicates that this dye is incapable of detecting hydroxyl radicals even if they are generated at a pace sufficient to kill E. coli.

Discussion

Fluorescein-based dyes have been very widely employed as detectors of oxidative stress, primarily because the manipulations are straightforward. The availability of commercial kits has popularized their use. However, the present study extends others’ concerns (30, 32–37) by demonstrating that these dyes lack the specificity, sensitivity, and freedom from artifacts that an assay requires. They produced false positives when metabolic impairment or membrane disruption diminished the cellular energy charge; similar results were observed even when stressed cells were exposed to a preoxidized dye, demonstrating that the elevated signals are not diagnostic of ROS. Further, the dyes produced false negatives even when authentic oxidative stress was so severe as to kill most cells. Consequently, one must revisit claims that various treatments engender oxidative stress inside bacteria, whenever those conclusions rested substantially upon fluorescein data. Conversely, negative results from dye-based experiments may have led to erroneous conclusions that ROS were uninvolved.

Fig. 9 presents a revised view of the behavior of reduced fluorescein dye inside E. coli. It is significantly oxidized by an as-yet unknown oxidant(s) that is not ROS, and when efflux pumps are slowed, the oxidized dye accumulates inside the cell, giving a positive signal.

Revised model of dye behavior inside bacterial cells. The reduced, acetylated dye diffuses passively into the cell, where esterases convert it to its hydrophilic form. Redox-active moieties inside the cell, which may include quinones, metal centers, and flavins, oxidize the dye to its fluorescent form. In unstressed cells both the reduced and oxidized dye forms are continuously excreted by TolC-dependent pumps such as AcrAB. Any stressor that slows metabolism or injures the membrane will diminish the PMF, impairing these PMF-driven pumps and causing the accumulation of fluorescent dye inside the cell. ROS are not involved.

Because TolC-mediated dye efflux is driven by PMF, the simplest model for how stresses impair it is by directly dissipating PMF itself. Indeed, PMF poisons did trigger dye accumulation. However, if ATP-driven cytoplasmic efflux pumps lie upstream of periplasmic TolC action, then it is possible that defects in ATP production can create the same outcome. The synthesis of pumps is also responsive to growth conditions, adding another variable (57).

It is germane to consider whether the results from E. coli are likely to pertain to other organisms. The inability of H2CDCF to detect a lethal level of Fenton chemistry in E. coli indicated that a detectable level of dye oxidation does not happen even when a fatal level of DNA oxidation has occurred; presumably, the same problem would be true in other bacteria. Background oxidation by quinones, flavins, or metal centers would also seem likely. And drug-efflux systems, including AcrAB, are widely distributed among bacteria.

It would be premature to extend our conclusions to eukaryotic cells, where this method has been widely used. However, the results here do caution that dyes can be oxidized by non-ROS biomolecules and that it would be wise to normalize cellular dye signals to dye content.

In any case, if investigators still wish to use reduced dyes (e.g., H2CDCFDA) to detect ROS in stressed cells, they should consider performing a parallel experiment using the complementary preoxidized dye (e.g., CDCFDA). This control may reveal that the stress causes a change in dye loading.

Some Other Tests of Oxidative Stress Are Similarly Flawed.

Unfortunately, several alternative experimental approaches have also turned out to be flawed. These are discussed here so that workers do not move from one problematic method to another. One frequent test of oxidative stress has been whether cell survival is improved by the addition of putative antioxidants—small molecules that can degrade ROS in vitro. Yet while typical antioxidants—N-acetylcysteine, thiourea, ascorbate, and others—can scavenge oxidants in test tubes or in growth medium, they have virtually no impact upon the levels of ROS inside bacteria (28). The reason is that cytoplasmic scavenging enzymes are orders of magnitude more efficient, so that even in principle such antioxidants would have only a minute effect upon ROS lifetime. Instead, many “antioxidants” improve cell survival in the face of lethal stresses simply by slowing cell growth. Slow-growing cells are more resistant to many killing agents. This mechanism of resistance is easily revealed by the fact that the protective effects of antioxidants can be observed even under anoxic conditions, when ROS are necessarily absent.

Substantial attention has been given to the specific hypothesis that clinical antibiotics exert some of their toxicity by stimulating ROS production in the target cell (9, 10). That conclusion was challenged by other groups (58, 59). Here we found that kanamycin increased the CDCF signal not by driving H2CDCF oxidation but by disrupting its efflux from the cell, and that the same effect occurred in the absence of oxygen. Others have pointed out that the elevated fluorescent signals in cells treated with norfloxacin and ampicillin—the other two drugs in the original study—were misleading because they trigger filamentation and the CDCF signals were measured by flow cytometry (60, 61). Flow cytometry naturally yields larger per-cell signals when cell-size corrections are not used. To finally resolve this point, in Fig. 10 antibiotic-stressed cells were loaded with H2CDCF, and the resultant CDCF* signal was appropriately normalized to biomass. The data show that these drugs do not increase the dye signal.

Cell viability was quantified by plating.

To detect oxidative stress in bacteria, some investigators have borrowed from the eukaryotic literature and relied upon presumptive markers of lipid peroxidation. The detection of malondialdehyde—a.k.a., thiobarbituric-acid reactive species (TBARS)—is accomplished through the addition of a dye to cell extracts after a heat treatment. One problem with this approach is that the TBARS assay is not specific, also reacting to by-products of proteins, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids (62). More importantly, the lack of polyunsaturated fatty acids in most bacteria make it very unlikely that any lipid peroxidation occurs at all, even during genuine oxidative stress (63).

Validated Tests of Oxidative Stress.

If those methods must be discarded, what can experimenters turn to? The primary action of excess superoxide or H2O2 in the bacterial cytoplasm is to inactivate a subset of key enzymes—and, therefore, the activity of those enzymes is a good guide as to whether hazardous levels of ROS are present. Dehydratases with [4Fe-4S] clusters, such as aconitase, fumarase, and isopropylmalate isomerase, are all good markers of oxidative stress, and these enzymes are present in a wide range of organisms (64).

An even simpler approach is to monitor the defensive regulons that bacteria induce when they perceive elevated levels of intracellular H2O2. Cytoplasmic H2O2 directly activates the OxyR transcription factor in E. coli, triggering the induction of scavenging enzymes (KatG, AhpCF), proteins that control the iron pool (Dps, YaaA, Fur), and others (thioredoxin 2, glutaredoxin 1, etc.) (65). The degree of induction of these genes is often more than 10-fold, making the signals quite clear by RT-PCR, gene fusion, or RNA seq methods. OxyR is widespread among bacteria; others use PerR, which exhibits a similar sensitivity to H2O2 and controls an overlapping set of genes (66). The doses of H2O2 that induce these regulons (67) are much less than is needed to kill a cell, so any lethal stress that does not induce these genes is very unlikely to act through ROS. HyPer is a chimeric protein in which the sensory domain of OxyR has been conjugated to YFP, providing fluorescent readout (68) [It should be noted that although the SoxRS regulon was once believed to respond to superoxide, it does not (69), and it should not be monitored as a proxy of superoxide stress].

Other approaches employ specialized mutant strains. Catalase/peroxidase mutants, which lack H2O2-degrading enzymes, steadily release H2O2 into the external medium, allowing the rate of intracellular H2O2 production to be measured directly (70). Additionally, such strains may also be expected to be hypersensitive to stresses whose toxicity is mediated by ROS; conversely, strains that constitutively activate the OxyR/PerR regulons may be expected to be resistant (71).

Finally, protein carbonylation (72) is a consequence of excessive Fenton chemistry inside cells. These modifications can be detected by protein derivatization and western blot. One point of caution is that the proteins in slow-growing cells are older than those in faster-growing cells, and so the former may display more carbonylation even if the actual damage rate is unchanged.

Used in combination, these methods have accurately identified a variety of bona fide ROS-generating stressors, including redox-active drugs, hyperoxia, photochemistry, and the high-level expression of autoxidizable enzymes (7). In all these cases subsequent biochemical investigations identified and reproduced in vitro the underlying mechanisms that produce the ROS. In contrast, purported occasions of oxidative stress flagged by dye or antioxidant analyses have generally failed to be tracked to any molecular mechanism of ROS formation.

The invalidation of redox dyes and antioxidants as ROS sensors must call into question the conclusions that were drawn from investigations that relied upon them. This is unfortunate. While the withdrawal of this evidence does not by itself disprove the involvement of oxidative stress, it seems inevitable that many stressors that had been tentatively linked to ROS will turn out to have no connection to them at all.

Growth and Media.

Cells were grown in LB/glucose or defined minimal A media (73) at 37 °C. LB/glucose medium contained (per liter) 10 g bactotryptone, 5 g yeast extract, 10 g NaCl, and 0.2% glucose at pH 7. Defined media contained minimal A salts (pH 7) plus either 0.2% glucose, 1% bactotryptone, or 40 mM alanine as a carbon source, as indicated in figure legends. Where indicated, amino acids were supplied at 0.5 mM. For anaerobic experiments, all media and solutions were moved into a Coy anaerobic chamber and were held there for at least 24 h before use to ensure the full removal of oxygen. Reagents for anaerobic experiments were dissolved in anoxic solvents in the chamber. All experiments were performed when cells were at mid-exponential growth phase (approximate OD600 of 0.3 for rich media and 0.25 for defined media) after at least four generations of growth. In some experiments, cell viability was determined by dilution and plating on LB plates. For this purpose cells were diluted 1:50 twice into sterile 100 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.2, at room temperature, and then 50 μL of cells were mixed with 5 mL of LB top agar and immediately spread upon an LB plate. Colonies were counted after 20 h.

Determination of Rate Constants for In Vitro Oxidation of the Dye.

Reactions of H2CDCF with potential oxidants were performed in the dark to avoid any photochemistry. Reactions were at 37 °C. Acetylated dye (CDCFDA) cannot be oxidized to a fluorescent form, and therefore the dye must first be deacetylated to H2CDCF before its reactivity with oxidants can be measured in vitro. Since commercial H2CDCF was not available, we de-esterized H2CDCFDA using porcine liver esterase. Ten microliters of 10 mM dye in ethanol were mixed with 4 U of esterase in a 1 mL reaction in our standard KPi/EDTA buffer (100 mM potassium phosphate, 0.25 mM EDTA, pH 7.2) under anoxic conditions at 37 °C for 2 h. The final amount of H2CDCF produced by this reaction was determined by oxidation with hydrogen peroxide in the presence of peroxidase, as described below.

The reactions of H2CDCF with potential oxidants were performed in the dark to avoid any photochemistry. Reactions were at 37 °C. The H2CDCF (0.1 or 2 μM, with the higher concentration used for slower reactions) was mixed with the corresponding chemical in KPi/EDTA buffer. Aliquots (0.7 mL) were collected periodically, added to 1.4 mL of KPi/EDTA buffer, and the fluorescence of the oxidized dye was measured in a Shimadzu RF-mini 150 fluorometer using 490 nm excitation/520 nm emission filters.

Reactions with cytochrome c, flavin mononucleotide, ubiquinone-1 (CoQ1), ferricyanide, manganese-dependent SOD, and horseradish peroxidase (without H2O2) were performed under oxic conditions with 10 μM of the oxidant. Nitrite was used at 10 mM, NAD+ was tested at 0.6 mM, and H2O2 was used at concentrations up to 30 μM. The reaction with 10 μM copper(II) chloride was tracked in an anaerobic chamber, to avoid reoxidation of copper and the formation of ROS; this reaction did not include ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). The reaction with oxygen was performed in air-saturated buffer, and a value of 210 μM O2 was used in calculations of the rate constant. This rate was low, and it was subtracted from the other reactions that were performed under oxic conditions. The dye appeared to be fully stable under anoxic conditions.

The xanthine–xanthine oxidase system (100 micromolar xanthine, plus xanthine oxidase to generate 1 micromolar/min superoxide) was used as a source of superoxide. The steady-state superoxide concentration was estimated to be 0.22 μM using an established formula that equates the rate of superoxide formation (determined in a parallel reaction by cytochrome c reduction) with the rate of spontaneous dismutation (74). Exposure to superoxide was performed for 5 min in the presence or absence of bovine Cu-Zn SOD (1,000 U/mL); because oxygen was necessarily present and H2O2 is also produced by the reaction, only the SOD-sensitive component of the oxidation rate was ascribed to superoxide. The oxygen- and H2O2-driven dye oxidation reactions are slow, so the great majority of the oxidation rate was mediated by superoxide.

Dye Loading and the Determination of Intracellular Dye Content.

Experiments tested the loading of both reduced and oxidized dyes. The reduced dye H2CDCFDA and the oxidized dye CDCFDA were added to cell cultures to final concentrations of 20 μM. When used, the HPF concentration was 5 μM. Cells were then incubated in the dark for 20 min at 37 °C. Aerobic cultures were shaken at 200 rpm.

After the incubation with the dye, 50 mL of cells were centrifuged (4,000 rpm × 3 min, 4 °C). Anaerobic experiments employed a centrifuge in the anaerobic chamber. Pellets were quickly resuspended in 2 mL of ice-cold washing buffer (100 mM KPi plus 0.25 mM EDTA, pH 7.2). EDTA was included to block extracellular oxidation by contaminating trace metals. This suspension was then diluted with the same ice-cold buffer to total volume of 30 mL and centrifuged for 3 min at 4,000 rpm. This washing procedure was performed again, and after the final spin cells were resuspended in 1 mL of ice-cold washing buffer. The density of this cell suspension was determined by diluting 20 μL of cells into 1 mL of the buffer and measuring the absorbance at 600 nm. Fifty μL of chloroform was added to 1 mL of the dense suspension, and the suspension was vigorously vortexed for 30 s. The mixture was left in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. This incubation time was sufficient to extract at least 90% of cytosolic dyes. The mixture was then centrifuged (13,000 rpm × 5 min).

The resulting supernatants were mixed with the KPi/EDTA buffer (0.7 mL of the extract plus 1.4 mL of the buffer), and the fluorescence of the oxidized dye was measured.

To quantify reduced dye in the cell extract, we exploited the ability of horseradish peroxidase to directly oxidize dihydrofluorescein-based dyes (39). The initial fluorescence of the dye-containing cell extract was measured, and then 0.5 mM of hydrogen peroxide plus 25 μg/mL of horseradish peroxidase were added. Fluorescence was again measured after 2 min. The final fluorescence indicated the total amount of the dye in the sample. In this way the fractional dye oxidation was determined. Calibration of the fluorescent signal was performed using known concentrations of commercial CDCF, the deacetylated oxidized dye.

The exception was that HPF determinations were performed in buffer adjusted to pH 7.5, and the HRP reaction was allowed to continue for 10 min prior to measurement of fluorescence.

All signals were normalized per OD. Control extracts from cell cultures without exposure to any dyes were generated and processed in parallel, so that the minor fluorescent background from the cells themselves could be subtracted from the dye signals. Absolute concentrations of dye were calculated from the relationship that 1 L of 1 OD cells contains 0.5 mL cytoplasm (75).

Where indicated, 1 mM dipyridyl was added to cells prior to the addition of reduced dye. Dipyridyl is a cell-permeable iron chelator, and at this dose it blocks virtually all intracellular hydroxyl radical production from Fenton chemistry (5).

For some experiments, 40 μM of NMP or 25 μM of PAbN (from 100× stock solutions in water) or 10 mM potassium cyanide (100× stock in water) were added to cells 2 min prior to the addition of the dye.

Dye Oxidation by Respiratory Quinones in Membrane Vesicles.

Inverted membrane vesicles were prepared from cells grown in LB medium supplemented with 0.2% glucose and 0.5 mM uracil. Uracil was necessary because some experiments used quinoneless mutants, and quinones are obligatory electron acceptors for dihydroorotate dehydrogenase of the uracil biosynthetic pathway. Wild-type cells were grown aerobically to ensure that ample ubiquinone was present, while the ubi men quinoneless mutant was grown anaerobically, as it is not viable under oxic conditions. When 200 mL cultures reached OD600 of 0.2, they were centrifuged, and the pellet was washed with cold 100 mM KPi buffer (pH 7.2) and resuspended in 3 mL of the same buffer. The cells were lysed by French press, generating inverted membrane vesicles. The lysates were diluted with ice-cold KPi buffer plus 0.5 mM EDTA to a total volume of 10 mL. The resulting suspension was centrifuged using a Beckman L8 70 M ultracentrifuge (100,000× g 1 h). The supernatant was discarded, and the vesicle pellet was moved to the anaerobic chamber and resuspended in 1 mL of anoxic cold 100 mM KPi/0.5 mM EDTA buffer by pipetting for 3 min. The vesicles were stored on ice and were stable for days. The protein concentration was determined using Coomassie blue reagent.

The oxidation of the reduced dye H2CDCF by vesicles was performed both aerobically and anaerobically. The mixture contained 5 μM of H2CDCF and vesicles (0.1 mg of protein/mL) in 100 mM KPi buffer (pH 7.2) plus 0.5 mM EDTA. Incubation was performed at 37 °C. At intervals, 1 mL of the mixture was removed and filtered by syringe (0.22 μm filter) to minimize background fluorescence from the vesicles themselves. Filtrate (0.7 mL) was mixed with 1.4 mL of KPi/EDTA buffer, and the fluorescence was measured as described in the previous section. Parallel control reactions were performed without vesicles.

Tracking the Dye Excretion Process.

Cells were grown in minimal A medium supplemented either with 40 mM alanine, 0.2% glucose or 0.2% glucose plus 1% bactotryptone. Yeast extract was omitted because it creates a significant background signal; the presence of bactotryptone supported a growth rate equivalent to that in LB. Cells were grown for at least four doublings, to approximately 0.25 OD. Fifty mL of cells were washed twice with 4 °C buffer (100 mM KPi, pH 7.2, 0.25 mM EDTA) and resuspended in 10 mL of the same buffer. Cells were shaken in a water bath at 37 °C for 20 min to use up possible residual nutrients. Twenty μM CDCFDA or H2CDCFDA were added, and cells were maintained at room temperature for 20 min. Cells were then washed as described above and resuspended in 15 mL of minimal A salts, prewarmed to 37 °C. The culture was split into two flasks, and 60 μM of the uncoupler CCCP were added to one of them. Two mL of cell suspension were syringe-filtered immediately, and filtrates were stored in the dark on ice. After that, a proper amount of corresponding energy source (40 mM alanine, 0.2% glucose, or 0.2% glucose with 1% tryptone) was added to the cell suspensions, and aliquots were filtered every 60 to 90 s and stored on ice. To determine the total amount of the dye in the cell culture, an aliquot of cell suspension (cells plus medium) was mixed with 50 μL of chloroform and the full extraction of all dye was performed as described above. The signals of oxidized and reduced dyes were measured as described in the previous section, with HRP/H2O2 treatment being necessary to quantify the reduced dye. The dye concentration remaining within the cell at each time point was calculated by subtracting the dye signal of the filtrate from the total dye signal of the culture.

Dye Responses to Cell Stress.

The heat-stress treatment was designed to match that of Marcen et al. (12), who reported that a large dye signal arose in heated cells. Cells were grown in aerobic LB/glucose to OD600 of 0.25. A 35 mL aliquot was collected, washed, and resuspended in 0.5 mL of 100 mM KPi, pH 7.2. The 0.5 mL cell suspension was then added to 5 mL of the same buffer that had been preheated to 52 °C. A control culture was resuspended at room temperature. After 4 min of exposure to the heat, both 5-mL cultures were added to 30 mL of LB-glucose medium preheated to 37 °C. Cells were then loaded with 20 micromolar of the oxidized acetylated dye CDCFDA as described above and incubated for 20 min. The intracellular content of both reduced and oxidized forms of the dye were then determined after chloroform extraction, as detailed above. To determine the viability of the cells, aliquots were removed at the beginning of the exposure to the heat and right before resuspension in LB, and colony formation was determined by plating.

Cold stress was performed by growing 35 mL cells in LB/glucose to OD600 of 0.25 and then immersing the culture either in water-ice mix or 37 °C for 5 min. Twenty micromolar oxidized acetylated dye (CDCFDA) was added for 20 min, and then the dye content was determined by standard means.

Kanamycin stress was performed by adding kanamycin (20 μg/mL) to cells growing in aerobic LB/glucose. After 25 min, 20 micromolar oxidized dye (CDCFDA) was added, and incubation continued for 20 min. The cellular content of the oxidized dye was then determined.

Ampicillin and norfloxacin stress were applied to SSK155, a tolC mutant. The strain was grown in aerobic minimal A glucose/tryptone medium to 0.15 OD600. Ampicillin (4 μg/mL) or norfloxacin (0.2 μg/mL) was added. Cells were incubated for 20 min at 37 °C in a shaking water bath, and then the reduced dye H2CDCFDA was added. Incubation continued for 20 min, and then cells were harvested and their contents of reduced and oxidized dye were determined. Small aliquots were removed before and after the antibiotic incubation, and cell survival was determined by plating.

Superoxide stress was imposed by growing sodA sodB mutants lacking superoxide dismutase to 0.15 OD in aerobic minimal A glucose/tryptone medium. A wild-type strain was used in parallel. Oxidized dye (CDCFDA) was added for 20 min, and the dye content was then determined.

Hydrogen peroxide stress was imposed by growing katG katE ahpCF mutants lacking H2O2-scavenging enzymes to 0.15 OD in minimal A glucose/tryptone medium. Previous studies have shown that approximately 1 μM H2O2 accumulates in these cells. A wild-type strain was used in parallel. Reduced dye (H2CDCFDA) was added for 20 min, and the dye content was then determined.

Hydroxyl-Radical Stress.

The rapid intracellular production of a lethal dose of hydroxyl radicals was imposed by adding to cells a mixture of cystine and H2O2. Upon the addition of cystine, its rapid import and reduction inside the cell produces a high cysteine content, which drives the Fenton reaction upon H2O2 addition (41). The addition of dipyridyl blocked Fenton reactions both inside and outside the cells, whereas the addition of cell-impermeable EDTA prevented Fenton reactions only in the external growth medium (SI Appendix).

In parallel experiments the amount of replication-blocking DNA damage produced by this treatment was measured using RT-PCR (76) (for details, see SI Appendix). After comparing band intensities of the untreated and treated cells, the Poisson distribution was used to calculate the mean number of replication-blocking lesions in the 10 kb piece (per single strand). This value was then extrapolated to the entire 4.2 Mb double-stranded genome.

Mutants were constructed by P1 transduction (73) with subsequent selection on LB plates with an appropriate antibiotic (12 μg/mL of tetracycline or 30 μg/mL of kanamycin).
