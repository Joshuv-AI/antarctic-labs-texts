Optimizing revascularization in the high-risk heart: Impella™-facilitated percutaneous coronary intervention in multivessel coronary artery disease with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and mitral regurgitation
Authors: Cristina Aurigemma, Jerzy Sacha, Pia Lanmüller, Aleksandra Gąsecka, Luca Baldetti, Ee Ling Heng, Stanislaw Bartus, Nikos Werner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPercutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is considered for patients at high risk for peri-operative mortality and complications during coronary artery bypass grafting, with further consideration of mechanical circulatory support (MCS) to prevent haemodynamic decompensation in patients with complex coronary artery disease and reduced ventricular function. However, current risk models do not account for crucial factors such as frailty or severity of valvular disease, potentially underestimating surgical risk. As such, the surgeon’s clinical judgment may more accurately reflect a given patient’s mortality risk. Here, we present a clinical scenario of a frail, inoperable 73-year-old male with mitral regurgitation and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction who underwent successful complex high-risk PCI supported by an Impella™ microaxial flow pump. A multidisciplinary approach was taken in determining the patient’s anatomical and clinical risk profile, with consideration of both conventional risk scores as well as the patient’s frailty. The multivessel procedure was carefully planned to minimize intra-procedural risk while achieving the most complete and durable revascularization possible. Planned early use of MCS played a central role in maintaining haemodynamic stability during the intervention. The patient recovered without vascular or device-related complications, and at 6 months, demonstrated improved left ventricular ejection fraction to 45%. This scenario highlights the feasibility and safety of this approach when performed in an experienced centre; however additional studies are needed to define optimal patient selection, procedural protocols, and follow-up pathways. Until then, meticulous multidisciplinary evaluation, tailored procedural planning, and rigorous post-procedural care remain the cornerstones of success.Keywords: High-risk percutaneous coronary intervention, Frailty, Mechanical circulatory support, Multidisciplinary procedural planning

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is the guideline-recommended revascularization option for patients with complex multivessel coronary artery disease (CAD) and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) to improve long-term survival (class I recommendation),1,2 whereas percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) may be considered in selected patients who are at high or prohibitive surgical risk (class IIb recommendation).1,3 High surgical risk is defined as a >8% risk of peri-operative mortality and complications based on the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) or EuroScore II risk models. These risk models, however, do not include numerous crucial risk factors such as frailty or the morphology of valvular heart disease, and therefore may underestimate the surgical risk profile. In real-world practice, the number of patients treated with PCI vastly outnumbers those treated with CABG in the setting of multivessel CAD and reduced LVEF, with the CABG to PCI ratio ranging from 2:1 to 10:1, depending on the healthcare system.4 In the recent analysis of the British Cardiovascular Intervention Society database, over 30% of the PCI procedures performed between 2006 and 2017 were defined as high-risk (HR-PCI), including nearly 50% in patients aged 65–79 years.5 Performing PCI in patients who have been deemed unsuitable for CABG is inherently high risk, as the very factors that led to surgical ineligibility, such as advanced age, frailty, chronic kidney disease (CKD), or concomitant complex CAD and severe mitral regurgitation (MR), also adversely impact PCI outcomes.6-9

The use of a microaxial flow pump (mAFP), such as Impella™ CP (Abiomed, Danvers, MA, USA), may be considered to prevent haemodynamic decompensation in selected patients with reduced LVEF undergoing PCI for complex CAD, although it should not be the default strategy.1,4-10 The first randomized clinical trials comparing Impella-protected HR-PCI to unprotected HR-PCI in the contemporary era are currently ongoing [PROTECT IV (NCT04763200) and CHIP-BCIS3 (ISRCTN17730734)]. Contemporary registry-based data clearly show that the use of Impella greatly facilitates the ‘state-of-the-art’ PCI, even in patients at extreme risk, including those with frailty, very low LVEF (≤30%), and advanced CKD. Moreover, ventricular unloading with mechanical circulatory support (MCS) may be particularly beneficial in cases of severe volume overload due to MR.11 Here, we present a clinical scenario involving a frail, inoperable patient with MR who underwent successful MCS-supported complex PCI.

Case presentation

Patient presentation and background

A 73-year-old male was referred to our tertiary cardiology unit for evaluation of coronary revascularization in the setting of advanced ischaemic cardiomyopathy. He was an active smoker with long-standing arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus treated with oral hypoglycaemic agents, and pre-obesity (body mass index 29 kg/m²). His medical history was notable for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease stage KDIGO IIIA (estimated glomerular filtration rate of 45 mL/min/1.73 m²), carotid atheromatosis, prior spinal canal decompression, right hip arthroplasty, and benign prostatic hypertrophy.

Five years earlier, during pre-operative assessment for orthopaedic surgery, he underwent myocardial perfusion scintigraphy, which demonstrated a moderate basal inferolateral defect with partial reversibility. Coronary angiography at that time revealed a 20–30% stenosis in the distal left main artery (LM), a 40% stenosis in the distal left anterior descending artery (LAD), and an 80% stenosis in the proximal posterior descending branch of a dominant circumflex (Cx) artery. Transthoracic echocardiography performed during that period showed normal LVEF and no significant valvular abnormalities. Given the distal location and calibre of the affected vessels, no revascularization was attempted.

Since that coronary angiography, the patient had not undergone further cardiology follow-up. Because of new-onset effort dyspnoea corresponding to New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III, he underwent transthoracic echocardiography. The examination revealed a markedly dilated left ventricle (LV) with an end-diastolic diameter of 68 mm and eccentric hypertrophy, global hypokinesia, and akinesia of the apical anterolateral wall. LVEF was severely reduced at 31% (Simpson biplane method). Diastolic filling was pseudonormal, with an E/e′ ratio of 13.5, indicating elevated filling pressures. The mitral valve leaflets were tethered, producing moderate central functional regurgitation. The right ventricle (RV) was enlarged but had preserved longitudinal systolic function, with mild tricuspid regurgitation and an estimated pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) of 44 mmHg.

Following the detection of LV dysfunction and valvular disease, the patient underwent coronary angiography at a spoke centre. The examination demonstrated diffuse and severe coronary calcification: calcified 70% stenosis of the LM, producing a pressure drop upon catheter engagement; 50% calcified stenosis in the proximal LAD, with signs of old ulceration; 95% distal LAD stenosis; and severe stenoses in the first (90%) and second (95%) diagonal branches. The dominant Cx had 40% proximal stenosis and significant distal stenosis in the fourth obtuse marginal branch. The small, non-dominant right coronary artery had critical proximal stenosis (95–99%).

Given the combination of severe LV systolic dysfunction and complex three-vessel disease involving the unprotected LM, the patient was referred to our cardiac surgeons for evaluation. The SYNTAX I score was 30. The SYNTAX II score predicted a 4-year mortality of 48.9% with PCI and 47.6% with CABG. The STS score predicted a surgical mortality of 1.7% and morbidity of 16.8%, while the EuroSCORE II was 8.1%. The patient appeared mildly frail [estimated Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) score 5], considering his advanced age, multiple comorbidities, and reduced functional capacity. After discussion by our multidisciplinary Heart Team, and taking into account the advanced age, chronic lung disease, and chronic kidney disease, surgical revascularization was deemed prohibitively risky. The consensus decision was to proceed with mAFP-protected (Impella CP) HR-PCI to treat the LM–proximal LAD segment.

Procedure strategy

The patient was admitted in good general condition. Pulmonary auscultation was clear, with no signs of right heart congestion or peripheral oedema. The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus tachycardia, left atrial enlargement, and non-specific repolarization abnormalities. Laboratory results included creatinine of 1.5 mg/dL, urea of 55 mg/dL, and glucose of 132 mg/dL; other haematology and biochemistry results were unremarkable. Repeat echocardiography confirmed marked LV dilatation (indexed end-diastolic volume of 104 mL/m²), severe systolic impairment [LVEF of 27%, stroke volume index of 35 mL/m², and LV outflow tract velocity time integral (LVOT-VTI) of 19 cm], previously noted wall motion abnormalities, grade III diastolic dysfunction (E/A >2, E/e′ of 27), significant biatrial enlargement, moderate multi-jet functional MR, mild tricuspid regurgitation, RV fractional area change of 15%, TAPSE of 20 mm, S′ of 12 cm/s, and PAP of 50 mmHg.

Despite the absence of pulmonary oedema, the combination of complex coronary anatomy and an adverse haemodynamic profile indicated a high risk of peri-procedural instability (Figure 1). It was therefore decided to perform PCI with ventricular assist device support. As part of procedural planning, the patient underwent lower limb arterial Doppler ultrasonography to assess the iliac–femoral axis, which revealed no significant atheromatous disease.

Interventional procedure summary

The intervention was performed the day after admission. Right femoral artery access was obtained under ultrasound guidance, and LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) was measured with a pigtail catheter, yielding a value of 34 mmHg (Figure 2). In view of the markedly elevated LVEDP, an Impella CP device was implanted. Two ProGlide™ (Abbott, Santa Clara, CA, USA) sutures were pre-deployed for haemostasis, and a 14 Fr sheath was inserted using the single-access technique, enabling introduction of both the support device and PCI catheters through the same arterial entry.12

The Impella was positioned across the aortic valve into the LV, providing active support. Anticoagulation was maintained with unfractionated heparin (activated clotting time of 250–300 s). The revascularization strategy was to treat the proximal-to-mid LAD and subsequently the proximal LAD and LM, using overlapping stents and a provisional approach to the LM–LAD–Cx bifurcation, achieving extensive, though incomplete, revascularization (Figure 3). Lesion preparation achieved satisfactory expansion without rotational atherectomy. Stent implantation was first performed in the proximal-to-mid LAD, followed by implantation of a second overlapping stent from the proximal LAD to the LM ostium. Proximal optimization was carried out, the Cx branch was treated with kissing balloon inflation, and a final re-proximal optimization was performed. During balloon inflations, the patient developed transient hypotension and loss of pulsatility, both of which were fully compensated by the Impella. No arrhythmias or other complications occurred. The final angiogram demonstrated TIMI 3 flow in all treated vessels, with no residual dissection.

Weaning and recovery

At the end of the procedure, Impella support was gradually reduced under SmartAssist™ monitoring. With stable blood pressure and satisfactory native output at minimal support, the device was removed, and haemostasis was achieved using the pre-deployed ProGlide sutures. After device removal, a final angiogram was performed via a 4 Fr catheter inserted into the right femoral artery, with the ProGlide closure sutures pre-positioned around the catheter. This angiographic assessment confirmed intact vessel integrity, with no evidence of extravasation or bleeding. Following this confirmation, the 4 Fr catheter was removed, and the ProGlide sutures were securely tightened to achieve definitive haemostasis. The patient’s recovery was uneventful. He was discharged 24 h after the procedure on optimal heart failure therapy and dual antiplatelet therapy. No vascular or device-related complications occurred.

Follow-up and outcomes

At 45 days, the patient reported improved exercise tolerance (NYHA II), no angina, and no readmissions. Echocardiography showed modest LVEF improvement (low thirties), reduced LV volumes, and stable mild-to-moderate MR. At 6 months, he remained clinically stable in NYHA II, with LVEF increased to 45%, reduced LV volumes, and MR improved from moderate to mild.

Discussion

Patient selection and surgical risk

The European and American guidelines for the management of patients with chronic CAD endorse CABG in individuals with significant LM coronary artery involvement, particularly in the context of multivessel disease, severe LV dysfunction, or diabetes (class I recommendation).1,3 In the present case, in which coronary disease primarily affected the LM, LAD, and diagonal branches, a single left internal mammary artery-to-LAD anastomosis may have provided a reasonable alternative to PCI. Prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach in high-risk surgical populations, showing a reduced incidence of myocardial infarction.13,14 However, the presence of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease substantially increases procedural complexity, often rendering patients unsuitable for surgery.

In this setting, CABG with mAFP support has emerged as a novel strategy to achieve complete revascularization in patients with elevated STS risk scores.15 A growing consensus supports the expanded use of mAFP during cardiac surgery, with the aim of reducing peri-operative morbidity and mortality.16,17 Pre-operative cardiac MRI is a viable option to assess the extent of myocardial scar tissue. Segments with scar burden exceeding 75% and without evidence of viability are generally deemed unsuitable for revascularization. Risk stratification tools such as EuroSCORE II and the STS Score are widely used to predict short- and intermediate-term mortality after cardiac surgery. Nevertheless, these scores do not incorporate frailty, which is a significant contributor to peri-operative morbidity and mortality.18,19 Elderly patients with multiple comorbidities, severe LV dysfunction, heart failure symptoms, and frailty generally have poor biological reserves. Although commonly used risk scores may suggest acceptable operative risk, long-term outcomes after cardiac surgery are often compromised by age-specific factors not captured in these scores, such as mobility limitations or target vessel quality. In this context, the surgeon’s clinical judgment may more accurately reflect the substantial mortality risk extending up to 180 days after surgery.20

Current revascularization recommendations are typically based on composite endpoints that do not always reflect patient preferences.21 Thus, shared decision-making is essential, particularly in the setting of advanced age, poor LV function with a propensity for haemodynamic decompensation, significant comorbidities, and frailty, all of which make surgical treatment especially challenging for both surgeons and patients. However, complex PCI with mAFP support enables high-quality procedures in anatomically and clinically demanding cases, translating into improved long-term outcomes.22,23 Therefore, a less burdensome percutaneous approach supported by a mAFP represents an appropriate option, acceptable to Heart Teams and consistent with patients’ expectations regarding reasonable procedural invasiveness.8,22-24

Device selection and vascular access in patients with multivessel coronary artery disease, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, and mitral regurgitation

When planning HR-PCI, particularly in patients with severely reduced LV function and complex coronary anatomy, careful device selection and vascular access strategy are paramount to ensure procedural safety and haemodynamic stability. An intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) may provide modest afterload reduction and enhanced coronary perfusion in patients with mild haemodynamic compromise; however, its utility in HR-PCI is limited by a lack of robust outcome improvement and low augmentation capacity (approximately 0.5 L/min). In contrast, Impella mAFP devices offer active LV unloading and greater circulatory support (up to ∼5.5 L/min depending on the model), making them more suitable in cases of extensive myocardial dysfunction and unprotected LM or last-patent-conduit interventions. Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, while providing full cardiopulmonary support, is typically reserved for patients with refractory cardiogenic shock or biventricular failure due to its high afterload and complex cannulation requirements.25,26

The PROTECT II trial demonstrated superior haemodynamic stability with Impella 2.5 compared with IABP in HR-PCI. While no statistically significant difference in major adverse events was observed at 30 days, follow-up analyses at 90 days showed a trend toward improved outcomes with Impella, reaching statistical significance in per-protocol and selected post hoc analyses.22,27 For the first time, the most recent European guidelines explicitly recommend the use of an mAFP in selected patients undergoing HR-PCI at experienced centres (class IIb recommendation).1 Furthermore, in patients with significant functional MR, MCS with an Impella device offers a compelling mechanistic option. In functional MR, the LV is subject to chronic volume overload and elevated end-diastolic pressure; this raises left atrial and pulmonary venous pressures, which elevates RV afterload and further compromises RV reserve. RV dysfunction further limits haemodynamic adaptability during ischaemia or haemodynamic stress. The Impella system unloads the LV by reducing LVEDP and end-diastolic volume, improving forward flow, decreasing pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, and thus reducing the downstream load on the RV.28

Vascular access strategy is equally critical, particularly in procedures involving large-bore sheath insertion. Pre-procedural imaging of the femoral and iliac arteries (via duplex ultrasound or computed tomography) allows assessment of vessel size, calcification, and tortuosity to guide access-site selection and reduce the risk of complications. Ultrasound-guided puncture significantly improves first-pass success and reduces bleeding and vascular events compared with fluoroscopic guidance, as demonstrated in the FAUST trial.29 In addition, pre-closure techniques using suture-mediated or plug-based devices are essential for achieving safe haemostasis after large-bore access.30 Comprehensive bleeding mitigation strategies, including optimal anticoagulation, echo-guided puncture, and post-procedural access surveillance, are associated with improved clinical outcomes in patients undergoing MCS-supported PCI.30,31

Single-access technique

The single-access technique, wherein both the mAFP and PCI catheters are introduced through a single femoral arterial access point, has emerged as a practical strategy to streamline HR-PCI. Initially proposed to minimize vascular complications associated with dual arterial punctures, this technique has shown promise in reducing bleeding risk, shortening procedural time, and simplifying post-procedural management, especially in frail or comorbid patients.12,32 The technique requires placement of a sheath large enough to accommodate both the MCS device and a secondary introducer for coronary guide catheters (Figure 4). Limitations include potential interference between catheters, limited guide catheter size (commonly up to 7 Fr), and more complex troubleshooting in the event of device malfunction.33 Nevertheless, when performed by experienced operators and combined with ultrasound-guided access and suture- or plug-mediated closure, single-access PCI can be executed safely, with low complication rates. Its use is particularly advantageous in patients with challenging vascular anatomy or prior interventions, in whom preserving access sites is critical for procedural success and future interventions.

Extent of revascularization and support impact

The extent of coronary revascularization in HR-PCI is a major determinant of long-term clinical outcomes. MCS, particularly with Impella devices, has been shown to enable more complete revascularization by maintaining haemodynamic stability during prolonged or high-risk phases of the procedure. Early evidence from the PROTECT II trial demonstrated that, compared with IABP, Impella 2.5 provided superior haemodynamic support and enabled treatment of more extensive coronary disease segments.27 However, initial operator caution limited the complexity of procedures performed. Subsequent real-world data from the PROTECT III registry and other observational studies revealed that greater operator experience and familiarity with Impella allowed for more aggressive and extensive revascularization strategies without a corresponding increase in complications.23

The R-IMP IT registry further confirmed that a greater extent of revascularization in Impella-supported PCI was independently associated with a lower incidence of major adverse cardiac events at one year, underscoring the prognostic relevance of achieving complete or near-complete revascularization in complex coronary disease.34 The RESTORE EF study further demonstrated that Impella-protected HR-PCI resulted in significant improvement in LVEF at follow-up, with the greatest gains observed among patients who underwent more extensive revascularization.35 Complementary evidence confirmed that both the degree of revascularization and the use of haemodynamic support were independent predictors of post-procedural LVEF recovery.36 Together, these findings suggest that mAFP-assisted PCI not only enhances procedural safety, but also facilitates more extensive revascularization, which in turn translates into improved ventricular function and clinical outcomes.

Weaning from Impella after protected high-risk percutaneous coronary intervention

Procedural ischaemia during PCI poses a risk of transient worsening of LV systolic and diastolic function. Therefore, the weaning process requires a thorough assessment of biventricular function and congestion status before deciding whether to remove the device or not. Classic parameters of end-organ perfusion should always be checked during HR-PCI, including mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), urinary output (the use of urinary catheters is encouraged), and serum lactate before and after the procedure.37 If a central venous catheter or a pulmonary artery catheter is inserted, trends in central venous saturation or mixed venous saturation, together with central venous or pulmonary arterial pressure monitoring, respectively, may also be useful.

Distinct red flags during HR-PCI may help identify patients at highest risk of haemodynamic compromise, such as frequent loss of pulse pressure, ventricular arrhythmias, worsening pulmonary congestion and respiratory failure, and residual clinical and/or ECG signs of myocardial ischaemia.38 During the vulnerable process of device weaning, the ability of the LV and RV to compensate for the progressive reduction in Impella unloading and systemic flow augmentation should be checked every 1 to 2 P-level reduction. The LVOT-VTI is a simple yet very useful surrogate marker of the LV stroke volume.39 This allows repeated intra-patient assessment throughout the HR-PCI procedure. This marker can be coupled with assessment of the trans-mitral filling pattern, as a decreasing LVOT-VTI trend associated with worsening trans-mitral filling patterns during P-level reduction usually suggests a high risk of weaning failure. The SmartAssist system provides a powerful complement to this assessment. Briefly, this system provides real-time values and trend graphs for estimated LVEDP, measured Impella flow, and native LV cardiac output (CO—calculated as CO minus Impella flow). A steady or abrupt increase in the LVEDP signal, with unchanged or reduced native LV CO during P-level downtitration, suggests exhausted contractile reserve of the LV. A final observation period at P2 should be performed for several minutes prior to pump removal.

Identification of high-risk markers for weaning failure should prompt consideration of enhance weaning with inotropes and/or vasodilators. In cases of uncertainty or borderline haemodynamics, a slower bailout weaning process with background inotropic stimulation can be planned in the ICU.

Left ventricular ejection fraction recovery in high-risk percutaneous coronary intervention with Impella

The Impella device provides a platform that enhances the tolerance of the failing LV to complex and prolonged percutaneous revascularization procedures, which are often complicated by transient peri-procedural myocardial ischaemia, translating into further loss of systolic function, and/or ventricular arrhythmias. The unique Impella effect of LV unloading and increased systemic flow does not rely on intrinsic LV pulsatility and differs from that of IABP, as it provides full support even in cases of severe myocardial stunning or re-entrant ventricular arrhythmias. Thus, the Impella device provides superior haemodynamic support compared with IABP in this setting.27 This, in turn, may enhance the chances of complete revascularization, an important achievement that has been shown to predict LVEF improvement40 and better mid-term cardiovascular outcomes.41 The benefit of myocardial revascularization on LV systolic functional recovery may require up to 90 days to become evident.27,35 Therefore, the clinical pathway for patients undergoing HR-PCI does not terminate at the time of hospital discharge, and a post-discharge follow-up plan should always be defined before the end of hospitalization.

Importantly, in patients with systolic LV dysfunction, the synergistic effect of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) should not be overlooked. The completeness of GDMT component prescription should be verified prior to discharge and, if necessary, optimized. Similarly, follow-up visits should prompt GDMT uptitration, according to current guidelines and good clinical practice.42 Finally, some HR-PCI patients may meet criteria for advanced heart failure, and ischaemic aetiology marks a very dismal prognosis in this cohort of patients.28 Patients that fail to recover myocardial function after HR-PCI should be promptly referred to specialized, advanced heart failure clinics for potential further escalation, including long-term MCS devices and/or heart transplantation.

Summary

This case illustrates how Impella-supported HR-PCI may represent a viable and effective revascularization strategy in a frail patient with severely depressed LV function, complex multivessel CAD, including unprotected LM, and functional MR with prohibitive surgical risk. The positive outcome observed in this case can be attributed to the interplay of several key elements. A thorough pre-procedural evaluation helped define the patient’s anatomical and clinical risk profile. This was followed by a multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion that considered not only conventional surgical risk scores, such as the STS score and EuroSCORE II, but also the patient's frailty, an increasingly recognized yet often underrepresented factor in procedural decision-making. The procedure was carefully planned to minimize intra-procedural risk while aiming for the most complete and durable revascularization possible. Within this framework, the early use of MCS played a central role, helping to maintain haemodynamic stability during the most vulnerable phases of the intervention (Figure 5).

The literature supports the concept that MCS during HR-PCI enables more complete and more complex revascularization. The PROTECT II trial demonstrated improved haemodynamic stability with Impella 2.5 compared with IABP. Subsequent real-world registries (e.g. PROTECT III, R-IMP IT) have shown that the extent of revascularization in Impella-supported PCI correlates with improved LVEF recovery and reduced major adverse cardiac events at one year. Of particular note is the subgroup of patients with functional MR and/or RV dysfunction. MR imposes a volume load on the LV, and RV dysfunction limits compensatory reserve during ischaemic insult or haemodynamic stress. LV unloading plus stable systemic perfusion with Impella may mitigate these pathophysiological stresses and facilitate ventricular recovery.43

Patient selection bias is inherent. Frailty remains an under-recognize, but important risk modifier.44 Standard surgical risk scores omit this parameter, yet frailty is strongly predictive of adverse outcomes after both surgery and PCI. Tools such as the 5-m gait speed test or the CFS may assist risk assessment but have not been universally adopted. Shared decision-making with the patient is essential, particularly regarding expectations for quality of life, recovery potential, and procedural risk. A dedicated risk stratification tool for complex PCI, the CHIP-BCIS score, has recently been proposed.45 This score provides a practical framework for integrating various dimensions of procedural complexity, including coronary anatomy, ventricular dysfunction, comorbid burden, and the anticipated need for MCS. Importantly, the inclusion of MCS adds three points to the total score, not only as a marker of technical difficulty, but also as a signal of the potential for abrupt haemodynamic deterioration if support is not employed proactively.

Conclusion

In selected patients with complex multivessel coronary disease, severely reduced LVEF, and functional MR who are deemed inoperable, HR-PCI supported by an mAFP such as the Impella may provide a viable revascularization alternative. This strategy appears feasible, safe in experienced centres, and potentially effective in achieving ventricular recovery and symptomatic improvement. Future studies are needed to validate this approach in larger cohorts and to define optimal patient selection, procedural protocols, and follow-up pathways. Until then, meticulous multidisciplinary evaluation, tailored procedural planning, and rigorous post-procedural care remain the cornerstones of success.
