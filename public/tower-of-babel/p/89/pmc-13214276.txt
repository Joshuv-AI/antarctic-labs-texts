Paincontrol: identity-preserving pain expression transfer with generative diffusion models
Authors: Yasamin Zarghami, Muhammad Muzammil, Vida Adeli, Hailey Reimer, Thomas Hadjistavropoulos, Babak Taati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomated pain detection models rely on large, diverse facial expression datasets; yet pain expression data are scarce due to privacy concerns and ethical constraints. This study explores synthetic pain expression generation as a potential solution, investigating whether expression transfer methods are sufficiently accurate to expand training sets by transferring expressions from a limited set of real data to a virtually unlimited set of identities. This work represents an initial step toward the use of privacy-preserving generative AI for synthetic data generation to improve in pain detection. We introduce PainControl, a novel facial landmark-guided method for identity-preserving pain expression transfer. Rather than using motion signals from video, as in prior methods, our method adapts a ControlNet approach, using text and dense facial landmarks to control fine-grained facial muscle activations while maintaining identity consistency through image embeddings. Identity preservation enables the generation of demographically diverse datasets, improving generalizability across populations. We compare PainControl to existing expression transfer methods and evaluate (i) perceptual realism, (ii) identity preservation, (iii) facial action unit (AU) transfer accuracy, and (iv) downstream pain detection performance. Our model produces realistic synthetic pain expressions, outperforming baselines in human-rated Likert-scale assessments. However, AU transfer analysis reveals challenges in accurately synthesizing expression intensities—particularly for AU4 (Brow Lowering) and AU43 (Eye Closure), which are crucial for pain recognition. When used to augment real pain datasets, synthetic images did not improve classifier performance, likely due to artifacts, AU misalignment, and the lack of temporal motion cues. Critically, however, our experiments in data-scarce regimes, where real pain expressions are extremely limited, demonstrate that synthetic augmentation through PainControl provides significant and consistent performance gains for pain detection models. These results validate that our ControlNet-based approach has reached sufficient maturity and accuracy to serve as a reliable data augmentation tool in pain detection domains, particularly in scenarios, where acquiring real data are prohibitively difficult or ethically constrained. This finding establishes the practical viability of expression transfer methods for addressing the fundamental data scarcity challenge in affective computing and clinical pain assessment. These results highlight both the promise and current limitations of generative models for pain expression, pointing to future research in AU alignment and temporal modeling for clinical-grade applications.

Introduction

In social science and health psychology, methodological paradigms often rely on video- and image-based stimuli to investigate various human responses [18, 39, 49]. One critical area of interest is the detection of pain behaviors, particularly in vulnerable populations like older adults with moderate to severe dementia [1, 9, 38]. Due to cognitive impairment, people with dementia often struggle to verbally express their pain, making it difficult for caregivers to address their discomfort [37]. Observational pain assessment tools based on non-verbal cues such as facial expressions, vocalizations, and body movements have been developed [1], but their adoption in long-term care (LTC) facilities remains limited due to staffing shortages [5]. Untreated pain can exacerbate underlying health conditions and contribute to distressing behavioral symptoms, such as agitation and aggression, ultimately reducing the quality of life of individuals in LTC [16].

To address this challenge, research has focused on image and video datasets to develop psychometric tools and train computer vision algorithms that detect non-verbal pain indicators [10, 37, 51]. These tools have shown promise in long-term care settings, where automatic pain monitoring systems could help mitigate the under-treatment of pain [40]. However, curating such datasets and systems presents several challenges. The collection of image datasets from older adults with dementia is expensive, time-consuming, and ethically complex [3, 14]. Long-term care homes are often understaffed, making them difficult to access for research, and issues of privacy and consent further restrict data sharing [5]. These challenges necessitate collecting new data whenever researchers need to replicate or extend previous studies [14]. Furthermore, the diversity of participants in these datasets is often limited, leading to algorithmic biases in computer vision models that rely on such data, especially when generalizing across racial and demographic groups [3]. Many datasets are private, and those that are public often lack representation from different racial or gender groups, which can limit the generalizability of AI models, particularly in pain detection tasks [21]. This problem becomes more pronounced when considering the under-recognition of pain in marginalized populations [4].

To address these limitations, we propose a generative AI approach for synthesizing pain expressions while preserving identity. By transferring real pain expressions onto synthetic facial identities, we create demographically diverse datasets that maintain privacy and enable paired neutral-pain expression generation. Such data are essential for pairwise pain detection models that compare intra-subject facial changes.

Our approach extends ControlNet [52] using MediaPipe’s face mesh [36] from one image to control expression generation while preserving the identity of another image with PhotoMaker’s fused CLIP embedding [33]. Unlike standard motion-based expression transfer methods, which primarily rely on motion features extracted from driver videos [7, 13, 46, 48], our approach enables explicit spatial control over fine-grained facial muscle activations by conditioning the generation process on a dense landmark representation of target expressions rather than inferred motion trajectories.

We hypothesize that PainControl will outperform existing motion-based expression transfer models in perceptual realism, identity preservation, and expression transfer accuracy. We also explore whether synthetic data augmentation improves downstream pain detection by increasing gender and ethnic diversity in training sets.

Generalizable insights about machine learning in the context of healthcare

This work offers generalizable insights into the development and evaluation of generative models for healthcare applications. It emphasizes the need for synthetic data pipelines that extend beyond visual plausibility to capture task-relevant structure—such as consistent identity and fine-grained expression detail. In clinical contexts, where facial cues carry diagnostic or behavioral significance, these properties are essential for generating data that can meaningfully support downstream model training or evaluation. More specifically, this study highlights the importance of assessing generative methods through the lens of clinical utility rather than visual quality alone. Effective use of synthetic data in healthcare requires attention to domain-specific constraints, the validity of transferred labels, and the risk of introducing artifacts or distributional shifts.

Related work

Generative AI for facial expression synthesis

Deep generative models have enabled significant advancements in facial expression synthesis by leveraging diffusion models [17, 32, 47], adversarial networks [12, 43], and feature embedding techniques [33, 44]. While these models generate high-quality images, their effectiveness in medical and behavioral analysis remains limited due to constraints in identity preservation and precise expression transfer.

ControlNet [52] introduces conditional control mechanisms for text-to-image diffusion models, enabling localized control over image generation. It conditions the diffusion process on external guidance signals—such as edge maps, segmentation masks, or keypoints—by injecting intermediate residual outputs ("zero convolution" connections) directly into the UNet backbone of the stable diffusion [47] architecture. While adaptable for tasks like facial landmark control, its default implementation lacks identity consistency across different expressions which is crucial in applications requiring subject-specific expression transformations, such as medical and behavioral analysis and pairwise pain detection analysis.

PhotoMaker [33] is an identity-preserving generative model built on stable diffusion XL (SDXL) [41], specialized in synthesizing realistic human faces while preserving detailed identity features. It accomplishes this using a fused embedding generated through a small multilayer perceptron (MLP). Specifically, PhotoMaker encodes the source identity image into an identity-specific embedding and merges it with textual information provided by the prompt. The result is a combined representation (the fused embedding) that conditions the SDXL diffusion process. However, PhotoMaker lacks mechanisms for precise expression control, limiting its usefulness for tasks requiring detailed facial muscle manipulation, such as pain expression modeling.

Facial expression transfer with identity preservation

Expression transfer techniques aim to modify facial expressions while preserving identity, often using motion-driven models. Recent approaches include First Order Motion Model (FOMM) [48], FSRT: facial scene representation transformer for face reenactment [46], and LivePortraits [13]. Given a source identity image and a driver video, they extract motion features from the driver and apply them to the source identity to generate realistic reenactments.

FOMM, FSRT, and LivePortrait employ motion networks to extract and encode motion patterns—such as sparse keypoints or latent motion features—from the driver video. FOMM detects a small number of self-learned keypoints and local affine transformations to approximate motion; these keypoints are not predefined landmarks but abstract points optimized for motion reconstruction. FSRT uses a transformer-based architecture, where facial motion is encoded by ten keypoints and a learned expression vector; the keypoints serve as pose anchors rather than anatomical references. LivePortrait uses compact, implicit keypoints learned during training to represent facial motion and combines these with the source appearance through stitching and retargeting modules. In all three approaches, the motion representation is used to guide a generator that synthesizes new frames of the source identity animated with the driver’s expressions.

These methods provide a baseline for expression transfer as they successfully transfer broad motion and facial expressions, such as head pose, lip movement, and eye gaze. However, they have limitations in capturing fine-grained expressions. Their motion encoding techniques prioritize head movement and large-scale facial deformations over subtle micro-expressions. This makes them less effective in tasks that require high-fidelity expression synthesis, such as pain expression modeling. Furthermore, they struggle with extreme or intense emotions, such as severe pain, which involve fine-grained muscle activations that are not well-captured by their motion representations.

Facial pain detection

Traditional pain detection models rely on supervised learning using real-world pain datasets [2, 45, 51]. One effective approach is the pairwise pain detection model [45] which provides a baseline for pain assessment on older adults with dementia (from the UofR dataset [15]. This model estimates pain by comparing a target facial expression frame to a neutral reference frame rather than analyzing single frames independently, making it sensitive to subtle facial changes indicative of pain. However, its performance is inherently tied to the quality and diversity of available training datasets. The majority of existing pain detection models are trained on the publicly available UNBC–McMaster Shoulder Pain Expression Archive [35] and/or the BioVid Heat Pain Database [51, 53]. Since existing datasets are limited in size and demographic diversity, expanding the range of training images remains a challenge in improving pain detection across different populations.

Experiments and results

Qualitative expression comparison

We conducted a qualitative analysis by comparing the original pain expression images with the generated synthetic images to assess the fidelity of expression transfer (Fig. 1). We compared the ability of four models (FOMM [48], FSRT [46], LivePortrait [13], our PainControl) to transfer pain expressions while preserving identity. Each model was given a source image (identity) and a driver image (pain expression reference), and their outputs were visually compared. PainControl produced the most realistic pain expressions. FOMM and FSRT introduced distortions, particularly in eye shapes and facial contours. LivePortrait generally followed the overall shape and configuration of the target expression, but exhibited a lower fidelity to the intensity and the emotional state of the expressions in the driver images, with outputs appearing closer to surprise or discomfort rather than pain. Across all models, pain expressions lacked intensity, particularly in AU9/AU10 (levator tightening) and full eye closure. We observed that our PainControl produced images that most closely matched the driver’s pain expression, while other methods struggled with facial distortions or failed to capture fine-grained details.

Perceptual realism assessment

To evaluate the visual realism of synthetic pain expressions generated by different models, we conducted a perceptual realism study using a two-rater evaluation. A total of 220 images were each rated independently by both raters on a 0–7 Likert scale, where 0 = unrealistic and 7 = highly realistic [34]. This study aimed to determine how convincingly each model could synthesize pain expressions without visible artifacts or distortions.

A set of 22 driver expressions was randomly selected, and for each one, five male and five female synthetic identities were used. The identities were equally distributed among five ethnic groups (Black, Caucasian, East Asian, South Asian, and Middle Eastern). This resulted in 220 images in total, with each expression being transferred onto the same synthetic identity across all models for a controlled comparison. Each image was rated individually by both raters, without reference to the original driver expression, ensuring that the realism scores were based solely on how natural and convincing the synthetic expressions appeared. To ensure inter-rater reliability, we calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient ICC(3,1) for fixed raters. The overall ICC(3,1) scores ranged from 0.78 to 0.92 across different models and gender categories (see Table 1), indicating excellent inter-rater agreement. The mean realism scores for male, female, and global synthetic images across all models and both raters are summarized in Table 1. Results indicate that our proposed model (PainControl) achieved the highest mean realism scores across both raters (Rater 1: 4.32, Rater 2: 4.57), outperforming other methods. LivePortrait (Rater 1: 2.70, Rater 2: 2.54) was second, followed by FSRT (Rater 1: 1.83, Rater 2: 2.08) and FOMM (Rater 1: 0.68, Rater 2: 0.88). Realism ratings showed minor gender differences, with male identities scoring slightly higher compared to female identities across both raters. Figures 4, 5 in the Appendix show the distribution of the realism ratings for each of the four expression transfer models.

Identity preservation comparison

To evaluate how well the generative models preserve identity, we computed Cosine Similarity (CSIM) between face embeddings extracted from the source and generated images. We used a pretrained ArcFace model [6] to extract 512-dimensional identity embeddings and compare similarity scores across different conditions. CSIM is a commonly used metric of measuring identity similarity between two images [7].

Table 2 reports the average CSIM scores for different expression transfer models. LivePortrait and PainControl achieved the highest scores, indicating better identity retention, while FSRT exhibited greater identity loss. PainControl and LivePortrait performed comparably (0.66 and 0.68, respectively), likely due to their use of landmark-guided or deep feature-based transformations, which maintain identity consistency moderately well.

Facial action unit (AU) transfer evaluation

To quantitatively evaluate the accuracy of pain-related facial expression transfer by our PainControl model, we conducted a AU transfer evaluation. This evaluation measured whether key pain-related facial muscle movements were correctly transferred from real expression images to synthetic identities while maintaining both AU presence and intensity levels. Only synthetic images from our PainControl model were evaluated, as images generated by LivePortrait, FSRT, and FOMM exhibited substantial deviations from pain-like expressions, frequently resembling surprise or discomfort.

A total of 220 synthetic images were manually annotated for AU presence and intensity. These annotations were then compared to the ground-truth AU labels from the real expression images. The quantitative evaluation results are summarized in Table 3. The model demonstrated high AU presence accuracy, with F1-scores of 0.92 for AU4 (Brow Lowering) and 1.0 for AU6/AU7 (Orbit Tightening), indicating near-perfect preservation of these AUs when present. In contrast, AU9/AU10 (Levator Tightening) was transferred with moderate accuracy (F1-score = 0.84), and AU43 (Eye Closure) had the lowest F1-score (0.51), reflecting frequent errors in synthesizing full eyelid closure. To further analyze AU transfer quality, we examine confusion matrices (Fig. 2), comparing generated vs. ground-truth AU intensity levels for each AU.

The five models (each trained on different dataset configurations outlined in Sect. ''PairwisePainDetectionModelTraining'') were evaluated on the dementia images of the UofR dataset. To analyze how model performance varied over different time scales, we evaluated pain detection using maximum rolling window aggregation at single frame, 1 s, 5 s, and 20 s intervals (Table 6). The results show that the synthetic data did not improve pain detection performance and, in some cases, led to slight degradation (Table 4). The Baseline model performed better at every interval. Performance improved with longer rolling window intervals, suggesting that pain detection benefits from aggregating multiple frames over time.

Although we observe slight degradation in pain detection performance when adding synthetic data to complete real datasets, this trend reverses dramatically when real data are scarce, which is a critical finding that validates the practical utility of our approach. To demonstrate this, we systematically evaluated model performance across varying levels of data scarcity by training on subsamples of the real data (e.g., 50%, 25%, etc.). For each subsample size, we compared three conditions: a model trained on only the subsample, a model trained on the subsample augmented with PainControl synthetic data, and a model trained on the subsample augmented with both PainControl and LivePortrait synthetic data. Since there is randomness, we take five different subsamples and report the average performance. The results (Table 5) provide quantitative evidence that synthetic data augmentation yields substantial and statistically significant improvements in pain detection performance when real training data are severely limited. A one-tailed Mann–Whitney U test shows a statistically significant improvement for both synthetic data approaches (p=0.0037 for Real + PainControl and p=0.0008 for Real + PainControl + LivePortrait) when using only 6.25% of the real data. At higher subsampling rates, the differences are not statistically significant. These findings establish that our ControlNet-based expression transfer approach achieves sufficient accuracy and realism to serve as a reliable and effective data augmentation strategy in data-scarce regimes. These are precisely the scenarios most commonly encountered in pain detection research due to privacy constraints and ethical limitations on data collection. This demonstrates the meaningful practical impact of our method for addressing the fundamental data scarcity challenge in affective computing and clinical pain assessment applications.

Discussion

Qualitative expression comparison

Our qualitative analysis (Fig. 1) revealed that PainControl produced the most realistic and high-fidelity pain expressions, while FOMM, FSRT, and LivePortrait struggled with accurate expression transfer. These results highlight key differences in how motion-based vs. landmark-based expression transfer methods handle pain expression synthesis.

While LivePortrait also preserved identity well, its lack of pain-specific training data likely contributed to its inability to generate convincing pain expressions. Its outputs often resembled general emotional states such as surprise or discomfort rather than genuine pain grimaces. FSRT did well in identity preservation as well; however, there were a lot of distortions and artifacts present in its synthesized images.

A notable finding was that all models struggled with transferring AU4 (brow lowering) and AU43 (eye closure) with high fidelity. This could be due to the complexity of modeling fine-grained skin deformations around the forehead, brows, and eyes; the synthesized images often showed a reduced intensity of muscle activation in these areas. These limitations suggest that motion-based models may not be well-suited for fine-grained expression transfer, especially in the case of pain expressions, which involve subtle, localized muscle activations rather than large-scale facial deformations.

Overall, our findings emphasize that explicit facial landmark conditioning is crucial for accurate pain expression transfer, particularly when fine-grained AU control is required. Future work should explore hybrid models that integrate landmark guidance with motion-aware encodings to improve both expression intensity and realism in synthetic pain datasets.

Perceptual realism assessment

Our perceptual realism analysis (Table 1) revealed that PainControl generated the most realistic images, outperforming FOMM, FSRT, and LivePortrait in human realism ratings. These findings suggest that landmark-guided expression transfer and diffusion-based image generation enhances the naturalness of synthetic expressions, reducing visual artifacts and distortions commonly seen in motion-based approaches.

One of the key advantages of PainControl over competing models was its ability to preserve the identity of the source image despite transferring an almost completely different expression from the original. Motion-based methods such as FOMM and FSRT introduced subtle identity distortions, particularly in eye shapes and facial contours, which may have contributed to their lower realism scores. LivePortrait, while better at identity preservation, struggled with expression intensity, leading to outputs that often appeared more neutral or misaligned with pain-related facial cues.

Interestingly, while PainControl achieved the highest realism ratings, there was still variation in perceived realism across different synthetic identities. This suggests that certain facial structures or demographic groups may be more challenging to synthesize convincingly. Future research could explore whether realism scores correlate with specific facial features or whether training on a more diverse dataset could further improve generalization across demographic groups.

Overall, our findings reinforce the importance of explicit facial landmark guidance for generating highly realistic synthetic pain expressions. Future work could investigate adversarial training techniques or multi-modal conditioning (e.g., incorporating head pose or contextual cues) to further refine realism and expression fidelity in synthetic datasets.

Facial action unit (AU) transfer evaluation

The evaluation results (Table 3, Fig. 2) indicate that while AU presence was well-preserved, intensity transfer remains a challenge. The model struggled to reproduce the intensity of fine-grained pain-related muscle activations, with specific biases evident across different AUs.

One notable challenge was the misalignment in AU43 (Eye Closure), where 63% of fully closed eyes were synthesized as partially open. This suggests that the model prioritizes identity preservation over extreme deformations, as full eye closure significantly alters key facial features. AU9/AU10 (Levator Tightening) showed moderate overestimation at lower intensity levels but substantial underestimation at higher intensities. The failure to accurately synthesize high-intensity nose wrinkling could stem from limitations in texture synthesis. This misalignment suggests that the model has a smoothing effect for tightening in the central regions of the face.

Another key finding is the overall bias toward moderate intensities, where extreme expressions were frequently generated as moderate states. This suggests that the model smooths out intensity distributions, likely due to the inherent averaging effects of generative models. Since diffusion models aim to learn a smooth manifold of facial variations, they may favor the generation of mid-range intensities rather than extremes. In addition, the training data itself contained fewer extreme expressions, leading to weaker representation learning for high intensities.

These findings indicate that while the model effectively preserves the presence of pain-related AUs, further refinement is needed to improve intensity accuracy, particularly for subtle expressions, such as brow lowering, nose wrinkeling and complete eye closure. Addressing these limitations require a combination of targeted loss functions, improved dataset balancing, and adversarial learning techniques.

Downstream task performance (pain detection model)

Our evaluation of downstream pain detection performance aimed to determine whether augmenting real pain datasets with synthetic data could improve classification accuracy. Contrary to expectations, the model trained on both real and synthetic data performed worse than the baseline model trained only on real data. Specifically, the real + synthetic models had lower AUC, AP, and F1-score, indicating that synthetic pain expressions did not provide additional useful discriminative features for the pain detection model (Table 4). Several factors, including artifacts in synthetic data, inaccuracies in AU transfer, and dataset quality differences across expression transfer models, likely contributed to this outcome.

One key reason for this result is that synthetic pain expressions contain artifacts and distortions, as evident in the results of our perceptual realism assessment (Table 1). While PainControl outperformed other expression transfer models in realism ratings, its generated images were still not perfect (mean rating of 4.32), especially in female identites (female mean rating of 4.17). Subtle artifacts, distortions, or inconsistencies in synthetic images may have introduced unwanted variations in training, making it harder for the pain detection model to generalize effectively. Since pain detection models rely on fine-grained facial features and subtle expression cues, any unnatural artifacts or distortions in the training data could act as noise, reducing the effectiveness of synthetic samples.

Another significant factor is the imperfection of AU transfer in synthetic expressions. Our AU evaluation showed that while PainControl successfully preserved AU presence (Table 3), it struggled with intensity accuracy, particularly for AU4 (brow lowering) and AU43 (eye closure), as shown in Fig. 2. This means that the PSPI labels assigned to synthetic images were not fully accurate, leading to mislabeled training data. Since pain detection models learn from AU-based intensity variations, any discrepancies in synthetic AU labels could introduce inconsistencies in how pain is represented in the training set. As a result, the pain detection model may have learned an incomplete or incorrect mapping between facial features and pain labels, reducing overall performance.

In addition, our results reveal a correlation between dataset quality and pain detection model performance. The baseline model (trained on real data only) performed the best, followed by models trained with PainControl and LivePortrait, which outperformed those trained with FSRT and FOMM. This ranking aligns with our qualitative expression comparison and realism assessments, which showed that PainControl produced the most accurate and visually convincing expressions, while FSRT and FOMM introduced more identity distortions and unrealistic expressions. This suggests that higher quality synthetic datasets lead to better pain detection performance, while lower quality datasets may introduce errors that negatively impact learning. The fact that even the best-performing synthetic model (PainControl) did not improve pain detection suggests that further improvements in realism, AU accuracy, and dataset diversity are needed before synthetic data can serve as a reliable augmentation strategy for pain classification.

However, our data scarcity experiments revealed a different pattern. When the real training dataset was reduced to only 6.25% of its original size, augmentation with synthetic data provided modest but consistent performance improvements (Table 5). This suggests that while synthetic expressions contain imperfections that limit their utility when abundant real data are available, they can serve as a valuable supplement in data-scarce scenarios, which are a common challenge in pain expression research, where collecting real data are constrained by privacy concerns and ethical considerations. These results indicate that expression transfer methods, despite their limitations, offer practical benefits when real pain expression data are extremely limited. Importantly, these performance gains in data-scarce regimes demonstrate that our ControlNet-based approach has reached sufficient maturity to reliably enhance pain detection model performance in real-world scenarios, where acquiring extensive training data is prohibitively difficult. This finding establishes the broader scientific impact of our work, showing that synthetic data generation through expression transfer can serve as a viable solution to one of the most persistent challenges in clinical pain assessment.

Overall, our findings indicate a nuanced picture: while synthetic pain expressions show promise for data-scarce scenarios, their imperfections in realism and AU intensity transfer limit their effectiveness when abundant real data are available. The modest improvements observed in low-data regimes suggest that expression transfer methods can provide value in practical settings, where collecting real pain data is challenging. However, improving synthetic data quality, ensuring accurate AU transfer, and minimizing artifacts remain critical next steps in making synthetic datasets more broadly viable for real-world pain recognition applications across varying data availability conditions.

Limitations and future work

Several areas of improvement could enhance the effectiveness of synthetic pain expressions for training AI-based pain detection models. A key challenge is improving AU transfer accuracy, particularly in conveying intensity. Future work should refine expression synthesis models using explicit AU constraints, ensuring subtle pain indicators are transferred at appropriate intensity levels. One solution is to integrate an AU intensity-aware loss that penalizes discrepancies between generated and ground-truth AU intensities. By incorporating an external AU detection model, this loss could guide the generator toward more precise facial muscle activations. In addition, adversarial losses and perceptual regularization may reduce expression smoothing and help capture high-intensity pain states more accurately.

Another limitation is that the proposed method produces individual static images, whereas practical pain recognition systems frequently rely on temporal information extracted from video sequences. To overcome this limitation, subsequent work should investigate the synthesis of continuous pain expression sequences that model the temporal evolution of facial displays. Incorporating temporal coherence mechanisms during the generation process would enhance inter-frame consistency, yielding synthetic expressions that better approximate natural pain behaviors and are more appropriate for training models that exploit temporal dependencies.

Artifacts in synthetic images may also limit downstream classifier performance. Filtering techniques that retain only high-quality samples, such as semi-supervised refinement using a pretrained pain classifier to score AU alignment, could help mitigate this issue.

The synthetic dataset comprising 250 identities, though offering reasonable diversity, has not been validated against real-world demographic distributions to ensure representative coverage across age, gender, and ethnicity. While the dataset may not perfectly mirror actual population statistics, expression transfer augmentation techniques can still serve as a valuable tool for diversifying existing pain datasets and mitigating algorithmic bias. By transferring pain expressions across varied identities, this approach can help balance under-represented groups and reduce disparities in model performance. Nevertheless, comprehensive validation and targeted augmentation strategies are essential areas for future investigation to ensure that pain recognition systems achieve equitable performance and adequately address the under-recognition of marginalized populations.

Finally, domain adaptation methods could reduce the gap between synthetic and real pain data. Techniques such as CycleGAN-based style transfer [20, 54] or adversarial domain alignment may help align the feature distributions of synthetic and real expressions, improving classifier generalization. Another approach is to use GANs to generate synthetic samples from RGB data [11], which has shown effectiveness in supplementing pain recognition systems. Addressing these challenges could make synthetic datasets a valuable tool for pain recognition, especially when real pain data are scarce due to privacy or ethical constraints.

Conclusion

This study introduced PainControl, a facial landmark-guided approach for generating identity-preserving synthetic pain expressions. Our results show that PainControl outperforms existing methods (FOMM, FSRT, LivePortrait) in perceptual realism, producing more convincing synthetic pain expressions. However, our AU transfer analysis revealed that while AU presence was well-preserved, intensity accuracy remained inconsistent, particularly for Levator Tightening and Eye Closure.

However, no matter how well the model performs in AU transfer tasks, the more relevant goal of using synthetic data, especially in healthcare, is to improve downstream tasks. For downstream evaluation, training with synthetic data did not improve pain classification performance when sufficient real data were available; models trained on real + synthetic data underperformed compared to the real-only baseline. This suggests that artifacts, AU misalignment, and lack of temporal cues in synthetic images limited their utility in data-rich scenarios.

However, our data scarcity experiments demonstrated that when real training data were reduced to 6.25% of its original size, synthetic augmentation provided modest but consistent performance improvements. This indicates that expression transfer methods offer practical value in scenarios, where collecting real pain expression data is severely constrained by privacy concerns and ethical considerations.

Despite these challenges, this work represents an important exploratory step toward privacy-centric generative tools for facial expression analysis rather than a comprehensive solution. Our findings highlight both the potential and current limitations of generative AI in expression transfer for privacy-sensitive applications while emphasizing the need for further refinements in AU intensity modeling, artifact reduction, and temporal consistency before synthetic data can be reliably integrated into clinical pain detection models across diverse data availability conditions.

Methodology

Facial action coding system (FACS) and PSPI pain score

The facial action coding system (FACS) [8] is a standardized framework for describing facial muscle movements using Action Units (AUs), each corresponding to activation of a specific facial muscle. FACS is widely used in pain detection models to quantify facial expressions associated with discomfort.

The Prkachin and Solomon Pain Intensity (PSPI) score [42] provides a composite measure of facial pain intensity computed as

where

AU4 (Brow Lowerer) corresponds to the activation of the muscle responsible for lowering the eyebrows.

max(AU6, AU7) (Orbit Tightener) represents the combined activation of the cheek raiser (AU6) and eyelid tightener (AU7), capturing tension around the eyes.

max(AU9, AU10) (Levator Tightener) accounts for the nose wrinkler (AU9) and upper lip raiser (AU10), activating the levator labii superioris alaeque nasi muscle, which is associated with facial grimacing.

AU43 (Eye Closure) is binary and indicates eyelid closure.

Since AU intensities range from 0 to 5, and AU43 is binary, the PSPI score ranges from 0 to 16, with higher values indicating stronger pain-related facial expressions. This scoring method provides a standardized measure of pain intensity and is used in the real and synthetic datasets used in this study.

Datasets used

The following datasets were used to develop our expression transfer model, create synthetic datasets, and train the pairwise pain detection model from ( [45].

Aff-Wild2 [22–31, 50]: A large-scale in-the-wild facial expression dataset containing 564 videos with 554 subjects (228 female). It is annotated per frame for Facial Action Units and seven basic emotions (happiness, surprise, anger, disgust, fear, sadness, neutral). We sampled 30,000 frames from this dataset to train our expression transfer PainControl model. Given its large scale and varying video resolutions, we randomly sampled from high-resolution videos to maintain image quality and computation practicality while ensuring balanced representation across various AU intensities.

FEED: facial extreme emotions dataset [7]: A dataset of 520 videos from 23 subjects with high-intensity facial expressions, including exaggerated anger, surprise, joy, and fear. While it lacks AU annotations, it enhances our model’s ability to generalize to extreme facial deformations. We sampled 30,000 frames from this dataset to train the expression transfer PainControl model.

UNBC-McMaster shoulder pain expression archive [35]: A widely used pain expression dataset consisting of 48,391 frames from 25 participants (13 female) experiencing painful and non-painful shoulder movements. It is annotated with AU labels and PSPI scores [42]. This dataset was used for both training the expression transfer model and the pairwise pain detection model.

University of regina (UofR) pain in severe dementia dataset [15]: A private dataset containing video recordings from 102 older adults annotated with FACS and PSPI, from which we use videos of 47 individuals with severe dementia and 47 cognitively healthy community-dwelling older adults. This dataset served as the expression images for the generation of our synthetic datasets and was also used for training and evaluating the pairwise pain detection model. After preprocessing, 177,460 frames were rendered for training.

Expression transfer model architecture and training

The proposed architecture (Fig. 3) integrates two main components: a pre-trained and frozen PhotoMaker [33] backbone and an Expression Adapter module, which is a fine-tuned ControlNet [52] adapted for facial expression manipulation. The overall model accepts three inputs (examples provided in Fig. 1): (1) a source identity image, which serves as the identity onto which the new expression is applied; (2) a facial landmark image generated using MediaPipe’s face mesh [36], containing 478 dense keypoints that represent the structure and muscle activations of the driver’s expression; and (3) a text prompt which provides semantic guidance to reinforce the intended facial configuration.

PainControl architecture pipeline. Given a source identity image, a facial landmark image derived from a driver image, and an optional text prompt describing the target expression, the model generates a synthetic image preserving the source identity while transferring the driver’s facial expression. The pre-trained PhotoMaker backbone [33] (frozen modules) ensures identity preservation, the fine-tuned ControlNet Expression Adapter module [52] (trainable module) conditions the diffusion-based generation on the facial landmark image, enabling fine-grained spatial control of facial muscle movements

The frozen PhotoMaker module encodes the source image and prompt into identity-specific latent representations using the SDXL latent diffusion space. Meanwhile, the Expression Adapter module transforms the landmark image into residual adjustments that guide the denoising process.

During image generation, Expression Adapter injects these residuals into the UNet backbone at each diffusion step, guiding the output to match the spatial structure of the driver’s facial expression. This dual conditioning—on landmarks and identity—ensures that the final synthetic image captures the driver’s expression while preserving the source identity. Details of the training pipeline can be found in the Appendix.

Generation of synthetic datasets

We generated a large-scale synthetic pain expression dataset using our PainControl architecture and repeated the process using three baseline models (LivePortrait, FSRT, FOMM) for comparison. The process of dataset generation consisted of the following steps:

Source images for diverse identities: We generated 250 synthetic source images representing diverse identities using Ideogram.ai [19], a diffusion-based text-to-image platform capable of producing high-quality portraits. We generated 50 images for each of the five ethnic groups (Black, Caucasian, East Asian, South Asian, Middle-Eastern), with an equal distribution of genders (25 male and 25 female). The prompt used for generating these images is as follows: "Portrait of a <age> year old <ethnicity> <man/woman> wearing a blue t-shirt and with a plain background.", where age was a number between 65 and 80 and ethnicity was from one of the five groups mentioned above. The consistent background and clothing minimized visual variability unrelated to facial features.

Driver images for expression transfer: We selected 394 pain-expression frames from the UofR dataset—181 from healthy individuals and 213 from individuals with severe dementia—spanning the full PSPI score range. To avoid redundancy from high temporal similarity in video data, we handpicked visually distinct frames to ensure a wide range of pain intensities and expressions.

Text prompt construction to guide expression transfer: We constructed custom prompts of the following format for each input image pair: "<Man/Woman> with expression of pain on face with <raised cheeks/tightened eyelids/wrinkled nose/raised upper lip/lowered brows/closed eyes>." depending on the gender of the source image and the AU annotations of the driver image. Example prompts can be found in the caption of Fig. 1.

Dataset generation process: For each of the 250 source identities, the expression transfer model transferred expressions from all 394 driver images, resulting in 250×394=98,500 synthetic pain images. An equal number of neutral images with the same 250 identities and with driver images selected evenly from the neutral images of the UofR dataset were generated, resulting in a dataset of 197,000 total images.

Pairwise pain detection model training

The pairwise pain detection model introduced in [45] was used to investigate the utility of synthetic data in pain detection. This model was introduced in [45] and is a regression-based convolutional neural network designed to estimate pain intensity using the PSPI scale [42]. This model is trained on paired image frames, where the reference image always depicts a neutral (no-pain) facial expression, and the target image captures a neutral or pain expression. Instead of classifying pain as a binary output, the model predicts a continuous PSPI score for the target image, which can later be thresholded for binary pain classification.

We trained this model using real-world pain datasets (UNBC and UofR Pain in Severe Dementia) augmented with synthetic pain expressions and tested it on the dementia portion of the UofR dataset. Following the methodology outlined in [45], we dynamically sampled image pairs during each training epoch. Specifically, at each training epoch, we sampled pairs of images from the same individual, consisting of a reference image and a target image. The target images were balanced evenly between "pain" and "no-pain" categories. Each reference image was selected to have a PSPI score equal to or lower than its corresponding target image. The model architecture and training configurations followed the same setup as described in [45]. Training was conducted using fivefold cross-validation on the UofR dataset and synthetic dataset, while the entire UNBC dataset was used in full, ensuring that synthetic images corresponding to expressions from the test fold were excluded to prevent data leakage.

We compared the performance of the pairwise model trained on five different training data configurations: Real-Only data (baseline), real + PainControl synthetic data, real + FOMM synthetic data, real + FSRT synthetic data, and real + LivePortrait synthetic data.

To evaluate the model’s performance, we computed the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC–AUC), Average Precision (AP) and F1-score. A classification threshold of 1 was used to distinguish pain from no-pain cases. This thresholding aligns with prior work in pairwise pain detection, ensuring consistency in evaluating the impact of synthetic data augmentation on model performance.

Appendix

Expression transfer model training pipeline

To fine-tune ControlNet for the Expression Adapter component of our model, we adopted a noise prediction framework typical of diffusion models. Specifically, the objective was to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between the predicted noise and the ground truth noise during the forward diffusion process. The training pipeline consisted of the following steps:

Preprocessing: Images from the Aff-Wild2, FEED, and UNBC–McMaster datasets were cropped to focus on the face and resized to 512×512 resolution. Facial landmarks were extracted using MediaPipe. AU annotations, when available, were used alongside labeled emotional expressions (such as pain, surprise, fear, etc.) to construct text prompts for training. Specifically, for each annotated image, active AUs (e.g., AU4: Brow Lowerer, AU6: Cheek Raiser, AU43: Eye Closure) were translated into descriptors ("lowered brows," "raised cheeks," "closed eyes"). These descriptors were then integrated with the corresponding emotional label into prompts (e.g., "Man with an expression of pain on face with lowered brows, raised cheeks, and closed eyes"). This allowed the model to associate AU-based expression details with their corresponding textual representations.

Latent space transformation: The landmark images were encoded into the latent space using a variational autoencoder (VAE). Gaussian noise was added to the latents according to the noise schedule at a randomly sampled time step.

Expression adapter conditioning: The Expression Adapter component takes facial landmarks as its conditioning input and produces intermediate residual outputs for the UNet backbone of the stable diffusion XL model [41]. These residuals guided the expression generation process.

Noise prediction: The UNet backbone predicts the added noise from the noisy latents, incorporating Expression Adapter’s intermediate outputs and the text prompt embeddings. The MSE loss (Eq. 1) was computed between the predicted and ground truth noise.

Optimization: The model was optimized using the AdamW optimizer with a constant learning rate. Gradient clipping was applied to stabilize training.
