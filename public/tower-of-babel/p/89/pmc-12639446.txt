Neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix (NECC): A retrospective study of 175 Chinese patients
Authors: Ning Su, Xiao Li, Dian Wang, Min Wang, Shuping Yan, Haifeng Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNeuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix (NECC) is rare and aggressive. We analyzed a dual-center Chinese cohort to describe clinicopathologic features, outcomes, and treatment patterns, and to identify prognostic factors.MethodsWe retrospectively included patients with pathologically confirmed NECC treated from June 2012 to June 2021. Variables included age at diagnosis, symptoms, high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV), FIGO 2009/2018 stage, tumor histology, tumor diameter, depth of stromal invasion (DOI), lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), lymph-node metastasis (LNM), and treatments. Follow-up followed NCCN guidance. Survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression.ResultsWe analyzed 175 patients. hrHPV was positive in 54.3 % (95/175); 84.0 % (147/175) presented with abnormal vaginal bleeding. By FIGO 2018, 39.4 % (69/175) had stage IIB–IVB disease. On univariable Cox regression analysis, younger age, higher FIGO stage (2009 and 2018), pure NECC histology, deeper DOI, larger tumor diameter, and positive LNM were associated with worse survival. In contrast, multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed that FIGO 2009 stage and DOI were independent predictors of both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS); tumor histology was independently associated with OS. Survival did not differ between abdominal and laparoscopic radical hysterectomy. Among surgically treated patients, adjuvant radiotherapy did not improve PFS or OS across early-stage, locally advanced, or late-stage subgroups.ConclusionsIn this cohort, primary-tumor factors—especially DOI—and FIGO 2009 stage were independent prognostic factors, whereas surgical route and postoperative radiotherapy showed no survival benefit; these findings can inform staging considerations and treatment decisions for NECC.Keywords: Neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix, Retrospective cohort, Radiotherapy, Progression-free survival, Overall survival

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of cervical malignancies, the most common histologic subtypes are squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma (AC), accounting for approximately 80 % and 15 % of cases, respectively [1]. The remainder comprises several rare entities, including neuroendocrine carcinoma, gastric-type adenocarcinoma, and mesonephric adenocarcinoma [1]. Neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix (NECC) is categorized into small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC), large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), and undifferentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma, representing about 80 %, 12 %, and 8 % of NECCs, respectively [2], [3]. Compared with SCC and AC, NECC more often presents at an advanced stage with nodal or distant metastases [2] and shows lower responsiveness to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [4]. Despite multimodal therapy, outcomes remain worse than for SCC or AC [4], [5].

Because NECC is uncommon, adequately powered prospective trials are difficult to conduct. We therefore performed a retrospective cohort study of 175 patients with NECC to describe presentation, treatment patterns, and survival, with the aim of providing data to inform clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

Between June 2012 and June 2021, 184 patients with pathologically confirmed NECC were identified at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University and the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Nine were excluded for incomplete records; 175 were included in the analysis. Clinicopathologic variables—age at diagnosis, presenting symptoms, high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) status, FIGO 2009 and 2018 stage, tumor histology, tumor diameter, depth of stromal invasion (DOI), lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), lymph-node metastasis (LNM), and treatment modality (surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy)—were retrieved from the medical records. For staging comparisons, each patient was staged according to both FIGO 2009 and FIGO 2018, and survival was then evaluated separately under each system. Lymph-node status was coded as pathologic (p) when based on surgical pathology and radiologic (r) when based on imaging alone; this p/r designation was recorded and used for descriptive staging under FIGO 2018. Use of immunotherapy (PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) was not included in the present analysis to avoid overlap with a separate retrospective series from our group that specifically evaluated immunotherapy in NECC during a similar time frame [6].

Follow-up

After completion of initial therapy, patients with NECC were scheduled for follow-up every 2–3 months in year 1, every 3–4 months in year 2, every 6 months in years 3–4, and annually thereafter. Assessments included pelvic examination and, when clinically indicated, imaging such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT). Disease progression was defined as radiographic progression per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 when applicable, or clinical/pathologic progression based on medical record documentation. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval from diagnosis to the first occurrence of disease progression or death from any cause, whichever came first. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause; patients without an event were censored at last contact. Vital status and follow-up information were obtained from outpatient visits, electronic medical records, and telephone follow-up. Data were administratively censored on January 31, 2022.

Statistical analysis

Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models were fitted for PFS and OS. Multivariable models included prespecified covariates—FIGO stage, age at diagnosis, tumor histology, DOI, tumor diameter, and LNM; variables associated with the outcome in univariable analyses (two-sided P < 0.05) were considered for inclusion. To avoid collinearity, FIGO 2009 and FIGO 2018 stages were modeled in separate Cox models. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs), and two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with SPSS, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Stage migration from FIGO 2009 to FIGO 2018 in the NECC cohort (n = 175) was visualized with a Sankey diagram created in Python 3.11 (pandas 2.2.2; Matplotlib 3.8.4).

Results

hrHPV status was positive in 95 (54.3 %), negative in 20 (11.4 %), and not documented in 60 (34.3 %). Among hrHPV-positive cases (n = 95), HPV18 alone accounted for 58.9 % (56/95), HPV16 alone 24.2 % (23/95), HPV16/18 co-infection 4.2 % (4/95), and other high-risk types 12.6 % (12/95) (Table 1). Presenting symptoms were abnormal vaginal bleeding (84.0 %), abnormal discharge (9.1 %), and pelvic pain (4.6 %); 2.3 % were asymptomatic.

Pure NECC occurred in 69.1 % of patients (SCNEC 115; LCNEC 6); 30.9 % had mixed histology. DOI was < 2/3 in 54.2 % and ≥ 2/3 in 45.8 %. Tumor diameter was ≤ 4 cm in 61.0 % and > 4 cm in 39.0 %. LVSI and LNM were positive in 44.9 % and 33.3 %, respectively (Table 1).

148 patients underwent surgery: 145 had radical hysterectomy with lymphadenectomy and 3 had hysterectomy. Among all surgical patients (n = 148), 78 (52.7 %) received adjuvant chemotherapy, 62 (41.9 %) received adjuvant chemoradiotherapy, and 8 (5.4 %) underwent surgery alone. Of the 62 receiving chemoradiotherapy, 59 had undergone radical hysterectomy and 3 total hysterectomy. Among the 145 radical hysterectomies, 78 (53.8 %) were abdominal radical hysterectomies (ARH) and 67 (46.2 %) were laparoscopic radical hysterectomies (LRH); 32/67 (47.8 %) LRH procedures occurred after the Laparoscopic Approach to Cervical Cancer (LACC) trial publication (October 31, 2018) [7], representing 22.1 % (32/145) of all radical hysterectomies. Of the non-surgical patients, 17 (9.7 %) received chemoradiotherapy, 7 (4.0 %) received chemotherapy, and 3 (1.8 %) received no antitumor treatment.

Patterns of distant metastases

Distant metastases were identified in 88 of 175 patients (50.3 %). In this study, distant lymph-node metastasis denotes nodal involvement beyond the pelvic and para-aortic basins (e.g., supraclavicular, mediastinal). The most frequent sites were lung (n = 46), liver (n = 32), bone (n = 28), distant lymph nodes (n = 25), and brain (n = 11); other sites included kidney/adrenal, breast, skin, spleen, and pancreas. Among patients with distant disease, 46/88 (52.3 %) had multiple metastatic sites (≥2).

FIGO 2009 model (Table 3): Higher FIGO 2009 stage and deeper stromal invasion remained independent predictors of worse outcomes—PFS: HR = 2.668 (95 % CI 1.247–5.712, P = 0.011); OS: HR = 2.994 (95 % CI 1.305–6.868, P = 0.010) for stage, and PFS: HR = 1.852 (95 % CI 1.125–3.047, P = 0.015); OS: HR = 2.180 (95 % CI 1.197–3.969, P = 0.011) for DOI. Association between radiotherapy and survival

Among patients who underwent radical hysterectomy with lymphadenectomy (n = 145), 78 (53.8 %) received adjuvant chemotherapy, 59 (40.7 %) received adjuvant chemoradiotherapy, and 8 (5.5 %) underwent surgery alone. Analyses were stratified by FIGO 2018 stage as early (IA, IB1, IB2, IIA1), locally advanced (IB3, IIA2, IIB), and late (III–IV). In univariable Cox models comparing chemoradiotherapy vs chemotherapy, no association was observed with PFS or OS: early (PFS: P = 0.286; OS: P = 0.340), locally advanced (PFS: P = 0.712; OS: P = 0.622), and late (PFS: P = 0.319; OS: P = 0.415).

Discussion

NECC is uncommon and often presents at an advanced stage; it appears less responsive to chemotherapy and radiotherapy and has high risks of recurrence and distant spread [8], [9]. Prospective trials are rare; therefore, well-annotated retrospective cohorts may inform risk stratification and management. In this dual-center series of 175 patients, we examined clinicopathologic correlates of progression and survival.

Prior reports indicate that hrHPV is common in NECC, with HPV18 predominating [10], [11]. Among hrHPV-positive tumors (n = 95), HPV18 was detected in 60/95 (63.2 %) and HPV16 in 27/95 (28.4 %); these detection rates include co-infections and are consistent with NeCTuR [11]. Although a causal cascade from hrHPV to NECC is unproven, genomic studies describe frequent viral integration near 8q24.21 (c-MYC) in cervical neuroendocrine carcinomas [11]. Exploratory proteomics from our group showed p16 upregulation in NECC versus adjacent tissue, supporting a biological link that warrants further study [12]. The frequent coexistence of squamous and/or glandular components in roughly one-third of cases also aligns with an HPV-related, lineage-plastic spectrum.

For cervical cancer, lymph-node status was incorporated into the FIGO staging system in 2018 [13]. Compared with the 2009 system, FIGO 2018 upstages any tumor with regional nodal metastasis to stage IIIC regardless of primary tumor size or depth of stromal invasion. In our cohort, higher stage by either FIGO 2009 or FIGO 2018 correlated with worse outcomes on univariable analysis; however, in multivariable models, only FIGO 2009 remained independently prognostic, whereas FIGO 2018 did not. This pattern is consistent with prior NECC reports—e.g., a 72-patient Chinese SCNEC cohort in which earlier FIGO 2009 stage correlated with superior OS and DFS [14], a U.S. series of 188 cases showing better 5-year disease-specific survival for stage I–IIA versus later stages (36.8 % vs 8.9 %) and for tumors < 2 cm versus ≥ 2 cm (67.4 % vs 34.4 %), with pelvic or para-aortic nodal involvement not prognostic [15], and a 293-patient study where FIGO stage (IIB–IV vs I–IIA) and tumor size (≥4 cm vs <4 cm) were independent predictors while nodal metastasis was not [16]. Notably, these studies used FIGO 2009, which does not account for nodal status.

Evidence specifically testing FIGO 2018 in NECC remains limited. In a 172-patient cohort of cervical neuroendocrine tumors, FIGO 2018 showed a stepwise association with outcomes (5-year OS: I 74.8 %, II 56.2 %, III 41.4 %, IV 0 %; 5-year PFS: I 63.8 %, II 54.5 %, III 30.8 %, IV 0 %) and remained independently prognostic (per-stage increase: OS HR 2.053 [95 % CI 1.591–2.650], P < 0.001; PFS HR 2.067 [1.622–2.633], P = 0.001; multivariable OS HR 1.726 [1.263–2.404], P = 0.006; PFS HR 1.731 [1.316–2.279], P < 0.001) [17]. By contrast, in a surgical SCNEC cohort directly comparing systems, univariable analysis linked LNM, parametrial involvement, and FIGO 2009 stage with OS, whereas FIGO 2018 was not significant (P = 0.082); in multivariable analysis, only FIGO 2009 remained significant (P < 0.001), with LNM (P = 0.058) and FIGO 2018 (P = 0.062) borderline [18]. When stage IIIC was refined by local invasion (IIIC-T1 cervix/vagina; IIIC-T2 parametrial; IIIC-T3 pelvic/abdominal cavity), 5-year OS was 83.3 %, 30.0 %, and 0 %, respectively (P = 0.010). Similarly, refining stage IIIC by nodal burden and location—IIICN1a (pelvic nodal disease with LNM ratio ≤ 0.20), IIICN1b (pelvic nodal disease with LNM ratio > 0.20), and IIICN1c (para-aortic nodal involvement)—yielded 5-year OS of 80.0 %, 26.7 %, and 0 %, respectively (P = 0.016). Here, the LNM ratio denotes number of metastatic lymph nodes/total number of resected lymph nodes. These findings indicate that integrating nodal status with local extent improves risk discrimination within FIGO 2018 [18].

Collectively, these data suggest that classifying all nodal involvement as stage IIIC may obscure the prognostic contribution of primary-tumor features in NECC—particularly tumor size, DOI, LVSI, and patterns of local extension. Under a blanket IIIC label, a T1 tumor with shallow DOI, focal LVSI, and a single micrometastatic pelvic node is classified the same as a bulky T3 tumor with deep DOI, extensive LVSI, and multiple nodes including para-aortic, despite clearly different risks and management needs. Heterogeneity is further amplified by differences in nodal burden (number or ratio of positive nodes), metastatic volume (micrometastasis vs macrometastasis), and nodal station (pelvic vs para-aortic), which a binary “node-positive” variable does not capture and which correlate with the biology of the primary.

Under FIGO 2018, a subset of patients is upstaged to IIIC on the basis of radiologically detected nodal metastases (IIIC[r]) [13]. Although this aligns staging with current practice, relying on imaging alone can misclassify patients. Reactive or inflammatory nodes may mimic metastasis on size or FDG uptake, leading to false positives and potentially unnecessary nodal irradiation or systemic intensification [19], [20], [21]. Conversely, micrometastases often fall below imaging resolution, generating false negatives and underestimation of risk [22]. Diagnostic performance also varies across modalities and centers—criteria for “suspicious” nodes, PET/CT versus CT/MRI availability, and reader expertise—introducing non-biologic variability into stage assignment [23], [24]. Finally, an IIIC(r) label neither quantifies nodal burden (number/ratio, pelvic vs para-aortic) nor incorporates primary-tumor determinants (tumor size, DOI, LVSI) that are strongly prognostic in NECC [25], [26]. Where nodal status would alter management, pathological confirmation should be sought when feasible; reports should specify r vs p status and nodal burden, and prognostic models should augment FIGO 2018 with tumor size, DOI, LVSI, and quantitative nodal metrics.

Mixed histology (NECC with squamous and/or glandular components) was common. Consistent with prior studies, mixed histology showed better survival than pure NECC on multivariable analysis [27]. Among patients who relapsed, sampled recurrent sites in our series were uniformly pure NECC; this should be interpreted cautiously given sampling limitations but raises the hypothesis that the NECC component may dominate at recurrence.

Like other highly aggressive cancers, distant metastases frequently occur in NECC patients. In addition to the typically affected organs such as lung, liver, and bone, NECC uniquely produced brain metastases [28]. Based on previous studies, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines emphasized that an MRI or CT examination of the brain was essential for NECC patients even when an early-stage disease was assumed [29]. The mechanisms underlying the relatively frequent brain metastases in NECC remain unclear. In our latest study, we used quantitative proteomics to identify key enzymes in biosynthetic and catabolic pathways linked to tumor recurrence or metastasis [12]. Among the up-regulated proteins, ALDH9A1 could catalyze the dehydrogenation of gamma-aminobutyraldehyde into gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). GABA has been shown to enhance the proliferation of brain metastases in breast cancer [30]. These preliminary results may hopefully evoke more discussion as to how NECC cells migrate to the brain and survive in an environment that is totally different from the original tumor site (i.e., the cervix).

The 2018 LACC trial challenged the benefits of minimally invasive surgery for early-stage cervical cancer [7], [31]. Compared with open surgery, minimally invasive radical hysterectomy was associated with worse disease-free survival (DFS) and OS and higher recurrence rates; subsequent reports also described increased peritoneal carcinomatosis in some series [31], [32]. In response to these data, the NCCN 2025 guideline considers open radical hysterectomy the standard approach, while LRH may be considered only in carefully selected early-stage cases after explicit counseling [33]. In light of LACC, we stratified outcomes by surgical route. In our NECC cohort, neither PFS nor OS differed significantly between ARH and LRH on univariable analysis (P = 0.529 and 0.692), and the association remained non-significant after adjustment for age, tumor diameter, and DOI (Table 5). Several factors may explain the absence of a detectable difference. First, LACC enrolled patients with SCC, AC, or adenosquamous carcinoma of the cervix; NECC was not included, limiting direct applicability to this histology [7]. Second, NECC exhibits aggressive biology with early systemic spread, so survival may be driven more by tumor biology and systemic therapy than by surgical approach [2]. Third, postoperative adjuvant chemoradiotherapy was administered in 62 patients (35.4 %), which could have mitigated route-related disadvantages [2]. Fourth, after the LACC publication (on October 31, 2018), LRH was likely used more selectively (e.g., for smaller, lower-risk tumors), introducing confounding by indication. Finally, contemporary technical modifications (e.g., avoidance of uterine manipulators, early vaginal closure, protected specimen extraction) may reduce mechanisms proposed to underlie LRH inferiority [32]. In our series, among patients who underwent radical hysterectomy, 67 (46.2 %) received LRH; of these, 32 (47.8 %) were performed after October 31, 2018 (pre-LACC: 35/67, 52.2 %). Consistent with NCCN 2025, open radical hysterectomy remains our default approach, with LRH reserved for carefully selected early-stage cases after informed consent [33].

Radiotherapy benefits in common types of cervical cancer are clear, but its efficacy in NECC is controversial. Dong et al. found that post-surgical radiotherapy improved OS in patients with distant metastasis using SEER data [34]. Moreover, Salvo et al. showed that combining pelvic radiotherapy and chemotherapy improved survival compared with chemotherapy alone in 71 patients with late-stage NECC [35]. In contrast, Zhou et al. analyzed data from 208 patients with NECC and found that surgery was most effective for early stage tumors, with no added benefits from adjuvant or radical radiotherapy, especially in stage I patients [36]. A meta-analysis of 13 studies with 400 patients with NECC found that routine postoperative radiotherapy did not enhance PFS or OS in early stage NECCs [37]. In addition, a meta-analysis of 1904 patients with NECC found that adjuvant radiotherapy was ineffective for treatment outcomes [38]. In our cohort, radiotherapy did not improve the prognosis of early-stage, locally advanced, or late-stage tumors with metastases in patients undergoing radical hysterectomy and lymphadenectomy. Radiotherapy details are rarely reported in these articles, including techniques, treatment volume, dose, and equipment, which may affect treatment benefits; thus, negative results should be interpreted cautiously to prevent faulty decision-making regarding the use of radiotherapy in NECC patients.

The treatment of NECC is challenging, particularly in the recurrent or metastatic setting [39]. In 2022 (Version 1), the NCCN Guidelines first listed pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy, with or without bevacizumab, as a first-line option for PD-L1–positive (combined positive score [CPS] ≥1) recurrent, persistent, or metastatic cervical cancer, following the FDA’s October 13, 2021 approval based on KEYNOTE-826 [40]. By contrast, NECC-specific data on PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors remain limited to small series and case reports. Towner et al. described a PD-L1–positive patient with recurrent small-cell NECC who received ipilimumab plus nivolumab and maintained stable disease for 83 weeks at cycle 14 [41]. Suresh et al. reported stage IVB, PD-L1–positive NECC achieving a complete response with atezolizumab plus carboplatin/etoposide, interval resection, and atezolizumab maintenance, with PET showing resolution of cervical and hepatic lesions at 14 months [42]. Another report detailed metastatic SCNEC with multiple brain metastases deriving durable benefit from serplulimab after EP chemoradiation (complete intracranial response, partial extracranial response; PFS 15 months; OS>49 months) [43]. Immunotherapy was not analyzed in this cohort because most patients were treated prior to the routine availability or reimbursement of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in our region; additionally, to prevent double-counting, cases receiving checkpoint blockade were reported in a separate series [6]. In that real-world study of recurrent/metastatic high-grade NECC, earlier (first-line) integration of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in advanced, non-surgical patients was associated with numerically longer survival, PD-L1 expression was not predictive, and a tumor mutational burden–high (TMB-high) case showed prolonged benefit—findings that support biomarker-informed, prospective evaluation of immunotherapy in NECC [6].

This study included a relatively large NECC cohort with standardized data abstraction, enhancing internal consistency. Nonetheless, its retrospective, dual-center design (June 2012–June 2021) is prone to information and selection biases, and nonrandom treatment allocation (surgical route; adjuvant radiotherapy/chemotherapy) may confound survival comparisons despite stratification. Incomplete capture of radiotherapy details (technique, target volumes, dose/fractionation, image guidance) and systemic regimens limits evaluation of dose–response and modality-specific effects. Finally, surgical-route analyses span the pre- and post-LACC periods; temporal selection (e.g., preferential LRH for smaller or lower-risk tumors) and evolving techniques (avoidance of manipulators, early vaginal closure) may bias effect estimates even after subgrouping by age, tumor size, and depth of invasion.

Because NECC is rare, prospective trials are unlikely [39]. A multi-institutional pooled retrospective registry with a shared, pre-specified analysis plan is warranted, emphasizing standardized re-abstraction of radiotherapy and systemic-therapy details; uniform nodal-burden metrics (lymph-node ratio and LODDS [log odds of positive nodes]); central/digital pathology review; and internal–external validation. This approach enables consistent capture of treatment details, refined nodal characterization, and external assessment of risk models without prospective studies.

Conclusion

In this dual-center Chinese cohort, DOI was independently associated with PFS and OS, while tumor histology was independently associated with OS. FIGO 2009 stratified risk better than FIGO 2018 in multivariable models. Within the radically operated subset, surgical route (LRH vs ARH) and postoperative chemoradiotherapy vs chemotherapy were not associated with survival. These findings support combining tumor-intrinsic factors and quantitative nodal metrics with FIGO staging for risk counseling and study design.
