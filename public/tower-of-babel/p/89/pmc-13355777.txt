A blended intervention for adjustment disorder: Results from a feasibility trial
Authors: Sara Fernández-Buendía, Laura De la Coba-Cañizares, Jorge Grimaldos, Diana Castilla, Sandra Alonso-Guillén, Pim Cuijpers, Soledad Quero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite the clinical relevance and high prevalence of AjD, there is limited research on psychological treatments for this disorder. The scientific literature suggests that low-intensity interventions, such as blended treatments, could be a promising option given how well they would fit with the characteristics of the disorder. The aim of this study was to analyze the feasibility and potential efficacy of a blended intervention for AjD.MethodAn open-label, single-group feasibility trial was conducted. After completing the eligibility assessment, 41 participants with an AjD diagnosis received the blended intervention. This combined a 7-module internet-based CBT intervention with videoconference sessions with a therapist every 12 days. Patients were assessed at four time points: pre-treatment, post-treatment, and at 3- and 12-month follow-ups. Feasibility was assessed both quantitatively and qualitatively, including measures of adherence, satisfaction and expectations, participants' opinions, preferences, therapeutic alliance, and usability. In addition, different clinical measures were included.ResultsThe treatment proved to be well accepted and valued by the participants, showing high adherence rates, a preference for the blended format, and high expectations, satisfaction, usability, and therapeutic alliance scores. Overall, the participants reported positive opinions, both quantitatively and qualitatively, emphasizing the importance of the therapist's support. The dropout rate was 14.6%. The intervention was effective in improving both primary and secondary measures.DiscussionThis blended intervention for AjD appears to be a viable and effective option for addressing the symptomatology of the disorder. Introduction

Adjustment Disorder (AjD) is considered one of the most diagnosed psychological problems in clinical practice (Evans et al., 2013; Reed et al., 2011). However, unclear diagnostic criteria in both the American Psychological Association's (APA) Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) and the World Health Organization's (WHO) International Classification of Diseases (ICD) may have limited research into this disorder (Bachem and Casey, 2018; Morgan et al., 2022; O'Donnell et al., 2019). This is a major concern given its clinical relevance and high prevalence (Zelviene and Kazlauskas, 2018).

The most recent version of the WHO manual, the ICD-11, incorporated updated diagnostic criteria offering greater detail on the diagnosis of AjD (WHO, 2018), which requires the presence of one or more stressors (a divorce, the diagnosis of an illness, a family or work conflict…), followed by preoccupation with this stressor or its consequences and a failure to adapt (WHO, 2018). Thanks to this new definition, new assessment tools were also developed (e.g., Einsle et al., 2010), enabling a more specific and precise assessment of the disorder.

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that there are effective psychological interventions for this disorder, highlighting Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (Baños and Miragall, 2024; Cowansage et al., 2025), there are several barriers to access prevent these treatments from reaching individuals in need (Harvey and Gumport, 2015). This has led to the emergence of new treatment formats that are low intensity but have greater reach (Kazdin, 2017). In the specific case of AjD, several authors indicate the appropriateness of these types of treatments given the transient nature of the symptoms of the disorder (e.g., Bachem and Casey, 2018; Domhardt and Baumeister, 2018; Maercker et al., 2015). Examples include internet-based or blended interventions, which combine face-to-face and internet-based elements (Erbe et al., 2017).

Previous literature points out that internet-based interventions could be an encouraging and effective option for AjD (e.g., Eimontas et al., 2018; Lindsäter et al., 2018; Moser et al., 2019; Rachyla et al., 2020). This treatment format offers multiple advantages (e.g., it allows reaching more people, it promotes learning by giving patients the possibility to repeatedly access treatment content, and it can lead to cost savings) (Andersson and Titov, 2014). However, one of its main limitations is the considerable attrition rates, which are around 31% on average (Melville et al., 2010). An example of an internet-based intervention for AjD was tested by Rachyla et al. (2020). In this study, the efficacy of a self-applied CBT treatment called TAO (acronym for Adjustment Disorder Online in Spanish) accompanied by weekly 10-min telephone calls was compared with a wait-list control group. The results showed that the intervention was effective in reducing AjD symptomatology with a total of 76.5% of participants completing the whole treatment. The dropout rate was 23.5%. Although it was lower than the mean mentioned above (Melville et al., 2010), it is crucial to find new strategies to ensure that the maximum number of individuals can benefit from evidence-based interventions.

The blended format includes the advantages present in internet-based interventions while maintaining the figure of the therapist (Erbe et al., 2017). The therapist constitutes a source of support and provides the opportunity to personalize the treatment to a greater extent, which could reduce the attrition rates compared to a fully internet-based treatment (Fernández-Álvarez et al., 2017; Patel et al., 2020; Rasing et al., 2020). There are multiple studies conducted with other mental disorders that suggest that blended interventions are well valued by patients (e.g., Etzelmueller et al., 2018) and can be as effective as face-to-face therapy (e.g., Cuijpers et al., 2019; Nunes-Zlotkowski et al., 2024). To the authors' knowledge, only one study has tested a blended intervention for AjD (Leterme et al., 2020). In this study, a total of 120 patients with the anxious subtype of AjD were assigned to a blended CBT treatment group (online treatment combined with face-to-face sessions), to a face-to-face CBT treatment group or to the wait-list control group. Blended therapy combined a self-applied intervention via a hospital computer and 10-min face-to-face sessions with a nurse. The results showed that both face-to-face and blended groups were effective in reducing symptoms of anxiety, depression, worry, and perceived stress. However, the blended group achieved a greater reduction in certain secondary measures of anxiety and depression, being slightly more effective than the face-to-face and control groups.

Building on this evidence, the present study tested a videoconference-based blended format, a modality not previously examined for AjD, which may offer distinct mechanistic advantages over both fully self-applied and in-person approaches. Compared to the original TAO intervention, the addition of videoconference sessions was hypothesized to contribute through four key mechanisms: (1) therapeutic alliance, by providing a relational context largely absent in fully self-applied interventions and consistently linked to treatment outcomes (Flückiger et al., 2018); (2) treatment personalization, by enabling the therapist to adapt the pacing and focus of the intervention to each patient's evolving needs (Patel et al., 2020); (3) accountability and adherence, as regular scheduled contact with a therapist has been associated with reduced dropout in internet-based formats (Fernández-Álvarez et al., 2017; Rasing et al., 2020); and (4) emotional processing, as the synchronous and interpersonal nature of videoconference sessions may facilitate the elaboration and reappraisal of stressor-related experiences that define AjD. Furthermore, compared to the face-to-face blended format tested by Leterme et al. (2020), a videoconference-based approach may extend accessibility to patients who face geographical, mobility, or scheduling barriers, while preserving the core therapeutic benefits of synchronous human contact. Indeed, meta-analytic evidence indicates that video-delivered psychotherapy produces large pre-post effect sizes and outcomes equivalent to in-person therapy, with particularly pronounced effects when CBT is the treatment modality (Fernandez et al., 2021).

In the present study, we conducted a feasibility trial of a blended intervention that combined an internet-based self-applied treatment (TAO) with 30-min videoconference sessions with a therapist every 10 to 12 days. This intervention was called TAO blended. The main objectives of this work were: (1) to explore the appropriateness of recruitment and data collection methods (including whether they reach the target sample, how restrictive the eligibility criteria are, patients' interest in participating, and the time needed to collect data), (2) to analyze the participants' expectations and preferences, (3) to assess their satisfaction and acceptance of treatment, and (4) to study the reasons for dropping out of treatment. In line with previous research on blended interventions (Rasing et al., 2020), it was expected that the dropout rate would be 17% or less and that treatment adherence would be 70% or higher. Additionally, we explored preliminary changes in symptom levels associated with this intervention both at post-treatment and during follow-ups. This treatment was expected to be feasible and effective in reducing the symptoms of AjD. We hope findings from this trial will facilitate the design of future Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs).

Method

Trial design

An open-label feasibility clinical trial was conducted with a single intervention group following an intra-group design with four measurement points: pre-treatment, post-treatment and follow-ups at 3 and 12 months. This study was pre-registered in Clinical Trials before the start of the trial (NCT05464121), and received approval by the Ethics Committee of the Universitat Jaume I in March 2022 (CD/42/2022). Details about the procedure of this study are specified in the published protocol (Fernández-Buendía et al., 2024).

Participants

Setting

The study was carried out at the Universitat Jaume I de Castellón, Spain. However, given that the entire treatment was administered via the Internet, the participants came from different cities in Spain.

Eligibility criteria

To be eligible to participate in the study, participants had to meet the following inclusion criteria: a) be of legal age (>18 years); b) meet the diagnostic criteria for AjD as defined by ICD-11: c) exceed the cut-off point of 47.5 on the Adjustment Disorder New Module-20 (ADNM-20) (Lorenz et al., 2016); d) be able to read and understand Spanish; e) have access to the Internet and be able to use a computer; f) have an email address and; g) sign the informed consent form. Potential participants were excluded if they: a) were at risk of suicide or showed self-destructive behavior; b) had a diagnosis of another serious mental disorder or a medical condition that precluded their participation in the study (psychotic, bipolar or personality disorder, substance abuse or dependence, dementia, or severe disability); c) were receiving another psychological treatment for AjD; or d) experienced a change in medication during the study period.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited from November 2022 to March 2025. The dissemination was carried out through publications and videos on the social networks of the research group and the university (e.g., WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn), using information posters in places near the Universitat Jaume I, through the institutional email of the same university and publications on the website of the Official College of Psychologists of the Valencian Community. In addition, patients were referred from the Mutua Umivale Activa insurance company or from the Emotional Disorders Clinic of the Universitat Jaume I.

Participants interested in the study contacted the clinical team by email or telephone. They received a response within 24 h to complete the telephone and videoconference eligibility assessment conducted by a trained psychologist. Those who met the study's inclusion and exclusion criteria signed an informed consent form before being assigned to the treatment group. They were informed clearly about the study details. In addition, they were told that their participation was voluntary, that they would not receive any financial reward for completing the treatment, and that they could leave the study at any time.

Intervention

This integrated blended intervention (TAO blended) alternated the use of a self-applied internet-based treatment called TAO (Adjustment Disorder Online) with videoconference sessions by a therapist. The structure of this blended treatment can be found in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Structure of the blended intervention for Adjustment disorder.Fig. 1

Structure of the blended intervention for Adjustment disorder.

The self-applied TAO intervention was delivered through the Psychology and Technology web platform (https://psicologiaytecnologia.labpsitec.es/). On this platform, patients had access to the treatment content, which was organized into 7 sequential modules. Each module was estimated to take 60 min, totaling 420 min needed to complete the online intervention. All treatment modules followed the same structure: 1) objectives, 2) module content, 3) self-test questions, and 4) homework between modules. The content of each module was available in text format and included various multimedia resources (images, videos) and downloadable PDFs. Some example screenshots are shown in Fig. 2. In addition to the main content, the platform also offered an option to review completed modules, a calendar to track pending homework and days on which the participant accessed the platform, and graphs displaying various clinical measures for participants to monitor their progress after the treatment. The platform also included an option to alert the therapist if a patient is at risk of suicide and another feature to send email reminders to participants who have not accessed the platform for 15 days.Fig. 2Screenshots of the self-applied intervention TAO on the Psychology and Technology web platform.Fig. 2

Screenshots of the self-applied intervention TAO on the Psychology and Technology web platform.

The videoconferences were conducted by two trained CBT therapists with a master's degree and one year of clinical experience. The individual sessions took place via Google meet software every 10–12 days and were planned to last 30 min each. Their objective was to answer any questions from the previous module, explain the next module to the participants, and motivate them to continue with the treatment. The complete treatment was planned to last a total of 12 weeks.

Several measures were implemented to promote and monitor adherence to the treatment model. Therapists were provided with a written protocol document specifying a structured agenda for each videoconference session, which standardized the content, sequencing, and key therapeutic tasks to be addressed across the intervention. In addition, weekly clinical supervision sessions were held throughout the trial, led by a therapist with extensive expertise in the treatment of AjD and in the delivery of technology-based interventions. These sessions provided a structured space to review case progress, discuss clinical decision-making, and ensure consistency in the application of the treatment protocol across therapists.

Regarding the treatment content, it was mainly based on CBT, although it also included some exercises and components adapted from positive psychology and Neimeyer's (2000) meaning reconstruction approach to grief. Overall, the treatment featured a psychoeducation component about the symptomatology of AjD, emotional regulation techniques to manage the unpleasant emotions that may arise from experiencing stressful events, problem-solving and exposure techniques, and an important component on elaborating the stressful event, along with a relapse prevention module. A more detailed description of the treatment content for each module can be found in the study protocol (Fernández-Buendía et al., 2024).

Outcomes

All the instruments, as well as evaluation moments and administration formats, were described elsewhere (Fernández-Buendía et al., 2024). Measures were collected at: pre- and post-treatment, and two follow-ups at 3 and 12 months.

Sociodemographic and screening measures

The eligibility assessment consisted of two phases: a) a pre-screening interview via telephone with questions on inclusion/exclusion criteria, and b) two videoconference sessions in which the therapist administered a semi-structured screening interview and the Adjustment Disorder New Module - 20 questionnaire (Einsle et al., 2010) to confirm the diagnosis of AjD according to ICD-11. Other interviews such as the Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-IV (SCID-I; First et al., 1996) and the Anxiety Disorders Interview Schedule (ADIS-IV; Brown et al., 1988) were used respectively for the differential diagnosis of mood and anxiety disorders. Sociodemographic data were also collected at this point, and psychiatric medication intake was recorded throughout the study.

Primary outcomes: Feasibility measures

Adherence to the intervention (including the number of completed modules, number of times participants access the treatment, number of times they review the contents, total time spent in the treatment, and dropout rates) was measured with usage data from the web platform. Attendance rates and the duration of videoconference sessions were recorded by the therapists. The reasons for participants dropping out were assessed by an independent researcher via telephone, using a qualitative interview designed by the clinical team (Table S1 in Supplementary material). An Intervention opinion questionnaire, developed for this study, was used to assess participants' format preferences before and after the intervention, their satisfaction with and usefulness of the different components and modules of the treatment, their opinion on the blended format and videoconference sessions (both quantitatively and qualitatively), and any adverse effects experienced during the treatment (open-ended question in the post-treatment assessment). This Intervention opinion questionnaire was completed online by participants, and can be found in Table S2 in the Supplementary material.

The Expectations and satisfaction questionnaire adapted from Borkovec and Nau (1972) was used to analyze participants' expectations and satisfaction before and after treatment. Finally, the Working Alliance Inventory for guided Internet Interventions (WAI—I; Gómez Penedo et al., 2020) to evaluate the therapeutic alliance, and the Usability System Scale (SUS; Bangor et al., 2008; Brooke, 1996; Castilla et al., 2023) to analyze the usability and acceptance by the users of the treatment platform, were included.

Secondary outcomes: Efficacy measures

The Adjustment Disorder New Module-20 (ADNM-20; Einsle et al., 2010) was used to assess AjD symptomatology. The Loss and Stress Inventory (ISL; Quero et al., 2019) was included to analyze how the person feels in relation to the loss of a person or situation as a consequence of a stressful event. Positive and negative affect was measured with the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS trait; Díaz-García et al., 2020; Watson et al., 1988) and positive psychological change after experiencing a difficult event was assessed with the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI; Tedeschi and Calhoun, 1996; Weiss and Berger, 2006). Anxiety and depression symptomatology was assessed using the Anxiety Overall Severity and Impairment Scale (OASIS; González-Robles et al., 2018; Norman et al., 2006) and the Depression Overall Severity and Impairment Scale (ODSIS; Bentley et al., 2014; Mira et al., 2019).

Other outcomes

Other efficacy measures were included to assess the meaning in life and the quality of life of the participants using the Purpose-in-Life Test-10 Items (PIL-10; Crumbaugh and Maholick, 1964; García-Alandete et al., 2013) and the Quality of Life Inventory (QLI; Frisch et al., 1992; Mezzich et al., 2000). Lastly, the change in some measures after each treatment module (mood at the time and changes in mood after completion of the module, perceived self-efficacy to cope with the stressful event, and degree of acceptance and openness to future experiences) were also analyzed.

Sample size

A total of 41 participants received the blended intervention. To estimate the preliminary efficacy, an estimation of the sample size was made considering the changes in AjD symptoms between pre- and post-test as the main measure. A significance level of 5%, a statistical power of 0.80, a bilateral contrast, and a moderate effect size of d = 0.50 according to Cohen (1988), were established for the calculation. The initial estimate for the sample size was 34 participants. However, after taking into consideration an estimated dropout rate of 17% (Rasing et al., 2020), the required sample amounted to 41 participants.

Analysis

Sociodemographic and clinical data related to psychosocial stressors, as well as primary feasibility outcomes were described narratively using descriptive statistics. Adherence was estimated using treatment platform usage data and was also described narratively. Treatment dropouts were determined based on missing data.

Qualitative data was analyzed using a thematic analysis approach as it allows us to capture patients' subjective experiences (Braun and Clarke, 2006). The analysis was conducted from an essentialist–experiential epistemological stance, assuming that participants' accounts provide meaningful access to their experiences with the blended treatment. An inductive approach guided the identification of codes and themes, focusing on the explicit content of the data. Thus, themes were not predefined by the questions; instead, patterns were derived directly from participants' responses, and multiple codes could be assigned to the same extract. This approach aligned with the aims of the study, prioritizing clarity, transparency, and the identification of practically relevant themes. Interview responses were transcribed verbatim during the telephone calls, ensuring that participants' wording was captured as accurately as possible. The open-ended responses from the online questionnaire were used directly for the analysis, as they were written by participants themselves. All data were subsequently translated into English for publication using a careful translation–revision process to preserve meaning and tone.

The following phases were followed: (1) familiarization with the transcriptions, (2) creating codes, (3) grouping codes into themes and subthemes, (4) reviewing of themes and subthemes, and defining and naming them for further refinement, and (5) creating the report (Braun and Clarke, 2006). Each phase was performed by two researchers (SFB and JG), both clinical psychologists with experience in AjD and Internet-based interventions. This background may have influenced the interpretation of certain responses. To minimize the risk of bias, coding was carried out independently, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, reflecting on how potential biases might influence each stage of the process. To provide a more detailed overview of the results, the frequency of the subthemes and their corresponding codes was calculated based on the number of participants mentioning each idea. A checklist covering the most important aspects of qualitative research (Beg, 2025) can be found in Table S3 of the supplementary material.

To explore preliminary changes in symptom levels associated with this intervention, repeated measures analyseswere conducted for each outcome using linear mixed models (LMM). An intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was performed because it provides a conservative estimate of the treatment effect by including all participants. Time was the sole fixed factor, with four measurement occasions: pre-treatment, post-treatment, 3-month follow-up, and 12-month follow-up. LMM accounts for missing data by employing full information maximum likelihood (FIML) parameter estimation. Consequently, all available data from both complete and incomplete cases were included, allowing all 41 participants to contribute to the analyses. The model was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), and a compound symmetry covariance structure was selected, assuming variance homogeneity across time points and uniform covariance among all repeated observations. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Within-group effect sizes were calculated as d = MD/SD, where MD is the mean difference between two measurement occasions, and SD is the standard deviation of the change scores. The SD was approximated as: SD=SE√df, where SE represents the standard error of the MD, and df denotes the degrees of freedom for the corresponding comparison (df = n - 1; cf. e.g., Cumming, 2012). Cohen's (1988) guidelines were used to interpret effect sizes: 0.20 (small), 0.50 (medium), and 0.80 (large). Standard errors and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were also calculated for each comparison. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 29.

Results

Flowchart of participants and appropriateness of different methods of recruitment

Out of a total of 152 participants that showed interest in participating in the study, 95 completed the initial pre-screening and 92 responded to the full eligibility assessment. Of these, 44 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria of the study or met some exclusion criteria, with the absence of an AjD diagnosis being the main reason for not being included in the study. In addition, 7 participants refused to complete the eligibility assessment, although the reasons could not be analyzed due to lack of response from participants.

A total of 41 participants met the eligibility criteria, responded to the pre-treatment assessment, and were assigned to the single-treatment group. The treatment was completed by 35 participants, but only 31 answered the full post-treatment assessment and 6 more responded partially. Data loss was greatest at the 3- and 12-month follow-ups, with a total of 16 and 7 participants fully responding to the assessments, respectively. Regarding the first theme related to the therapeutic relationship, participants generally emphasized the importance of having a therapist present. Almost the entire sample highlighted the necessity of human contact during the intervention, as well as receiving emotional and professional support. In terms of perceived usefulness, more than half of the respondents found the blended format effective for their improvement, noting that it provided the motivation needed to continue treatment. Finally, regarding the experience with the blended format, it was positive for most participants. More than half commented on the organization of treatment. Although many reported a positive experience, three participants expressed concerns about the purpose of the videoconference sessions (n = 1, 3.1%), felt that the time to complete the modules was too short (n = 1, 3.1%) or expressed a preference for an in-person format rather than videoconferencing for sessions with the therapist (n = 1, 3.1%). Less than half of the participants noted that the two elements of the blended format (face-to-face and online) complemented each other, providing continuity to the intervention, facilitating accessibility to treatment, and promoting autonomous and self-managed work.

When comparing pre-treatment outcomes with post-treatment and the two follow-ups, a significant reduction in AjD symptomatology was observed. The same was true of the other symptoms, with a decrease in the distress regarding the loss, negative affect, and anxious and depressive symptomatology. Only in the case of depressive symptomatology were no statistically significant differences observed between pre-treatment and follow-ups. Similarly, a significant increase was found in post-traumatic growth, quality of life, positive affect, and sense of life. The calculated effect sizes ranged from small to large. We wanted to point out that these changes cannot be fully attributed to the intervention given the absence of a control condition. As for the differences between post-treatment and follow-ups, and between the two follow-ups, they were not significant, suggesting the maintenance of changes in all variables.

Second, measures of post-module efficacy related to current mood, post-module mood, current self-efficacy, post-module self-efficacy, acceptance of the stressful event, and openness to new experiences were also collected. Table S8 in the Supplementary material shows the results of the repeated measures analysesunder LMM for these post-module measures (n = 41). Overall, all of these measures improved significantly across modules, with the exception of current mood, which showed no significant differences.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to analyze the feasibility of a blended intervention for AjD that alternated an internet-based program with videoconference sessions.

First, we found that a high percentage of participants who showed interest in receiving treatment ended up completing the eligibility assessment (60.5%), while a smaller number were assigned to the intervention (27%). So far, there are no studies on blended interventions for AjD reporting treatment reach data. However, the initial uptake figures were comparable to studies conducted with other mental disorders (e.g., Rahmadiana et al., 2021), whereas intervention uptake was lower (Nunes-Zlotkowski et al., 2024). Among the participants who did not meet the eligibility criteria in our study, the main reasons were not meeting the ICD-11 criteria for AjD (62.7%), followed by not exceeding the cut-off point on the ADNM-20 questionnaire (15.7%). Although this second criterion was considered helpful in the diagnosis of AjD, it may have slowed down the recruitment of the sample. In any case, the recruitment methods used made it possible to reach the target sample over a period of about two and a half years, with the most successful methods being dissemination through LinkedIn and recommendations from other people who knew about the project.

In addition to those who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 7 participants (13.7%) declined to complete the eligibility assessment. Although the reasons could not be recorded, it is possible that the temporary nature of the AjD symptoms may have played a role. Future studies should address this question and explore the reasons for not participating. Most participants who started treatment completed it (85.4%), so the rates of modules completed and videoconference session attendance were high, indicating excellent adherence that exceeded the pre-defined success criterion (≥70%). The dropout rate was 14.6% Although this figure is higher than that found in the blended treatment for AjD of Leterme et al. (2020), it is very similar or even lower than in blended interventions for other mental disorders (e.g., Mathiasen et al., 2022; Nunes-Zlotkowski et al., 2024) and meets the success criterion established in this study (≤17%). In addition, some previous self-applied interventions for AjD reported significantly higher dropout rates (e.g., Eimontas et al., 2018; Moser et al., 2019; Rachyla et al., 2020). In the study by Rachyla et al. (2020), this same treatment (TAO) was tested, but in a fully self-applied format via the Internet, with minimal therapist contact. The dropout rate was 23.5%, and the authors suggested that the therapeutic support could be a relevant factor in participant adherence.

In terms of fidelity to the protocol, in general, the days allocated to each module and the total treatment duration matched the initial recommendations. However, some individuals required more time, especially for modules 2 and 3. The average length of the videoconference sessions was within the suggested 30 min, although in some cases they were longer, especially for sessions 2 and 3. In these modules, participants learn exposure techniques and problem-solving skills (module 2) and begin elaborating on the stressful event (module 3). Therefore, the findings indicate a need to adjust the time assigned to each module and the duration of the sessions, particularly for these modules.

The treatment was well appreciated by the participants. Expectations before starting treatment were high, and their opinion even improved by the end of the intervention. In addition, blended was the preferred treatment format both before and after treatment. The therapeutic alliance averages and the usability of the internet-based program were excellent. Participants considered both the blended format and videoconference sessions to be satisfactory, useful and helpful in preventing dropouts. All the modules were also positively rated. The text was the most highly valued element of the online program, although this is probably due to the fact that most of the treatment was presented in this format. These results align with the findings of previous blended interventions for AjD (Leterme et al., 2020; Servant et al., 2016) and other mental disorders (Nunes-Zlotkowski et al., 2024), suggesting high acceptability of the blended format.

The modules and videoconference sessions with the highest satisfaction and usefulness scores were related to the elaboration of stressful events and relapse prevention. It is not surprising, considering that elaboration addresses, at least partly, the core symptoms of the disorder and has already shown evidence in other stress and trauma-related disorders (Peri et al., 2016). However, some authors have suggested that, within the exposure technique (Jaycox and Foa, 1996), elaboration could generate rejection or worsen symptomatology in some specific cases. In fact, in this study, only one adverse effect was reported, related to a worsening of symptoms after elaborating on the stressful event.

The qualitative results were consistent with the quantitative ones. The most repeated topic was the importance of therapist support and therapeutic relationship, which included the essentiality of human contact and emotional and professional support. Less frequently, participants also mentioned that the blended format was effective in reducing their symptoms and generally reported positive experiences. Similarly, previous qualitative studies conducted with patients with depression who received a blended intervention found mostly positive opinions toward the format, describing it as “contemporary”, “innovative” or “motivating” and suggesting that it increases the efficacy of the treatment and facilitates the integration of the contents (Schuster et al., 2018). Other authors also found greater acceptance and perceived usefulness of this format versus the fully online or face-to-face format (Etzelmueller et al., 2018).

Some patients who dropped out of treatment reported doing so because they needed more therapist support, lacked time, felt confused regarding treatment completion instructions, or faced personal conflicts unrelated to the intervention. Negative feedback mainly focus on the lack of face-to-face contact with the therapist and the lack of personalization. Participants requested increased contact with the therapist (longer and more in-person sessions), greater personalization, and a reduction of tasks between sessions, as well as updating the platform's functions. This replicates the results of Fernández-Álvarez et al. (2017) with patients with emotional disorders, suggesting that the lack of therapeutic support or lack of content specificity were the most frequent reasons for dropout. Similarly, Patel et al. (2020) found that personal support was an essential component of treatment success as it allowed for treatment personalization.

As a secondary objective,we explored preliminary changes in symptom levels associated with this intervention both at post-treatment and during follow-ups. Participants showed significant reductions in AjD symptoms, anxious and depressive symptomatology, negative affect, and the interference caused by the stressors. In addition, significant increases in quality of life, positive affect, and participants' sense of life were found, which is of particular interest given that these variables may indicate a greater ability of participants to cope with difficult situations (Truskauskaite-Kuneviciene et al., 2022). Overall, improvements were maintained at 3- and 12-month follow-ups for most variables. It is important to note that, given the absence of control group, these changes cannot be totally attributed to the intervention. This replicates the findings of a previous study by Rachyla et al. (2020), which also reported improvements in nearly all measures of symptomatology and in posttraumatic growth, quality of life, and positive affect, maintained at follow-ups. A secondary analysis also revealed a significant effect on the participants' sense of life (Quero et al., 2023). The only prior blended intervention for AjD also found improvements in all variables of anxiety, depression, worry, and perceived stress, which were maintained for most variables at 6 months (Leterme et al., 2020).

In sum, it seems that this treatment is feasible and shows preliminary changes in symptoms levels. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first blended treatment for AjD that combines the use of an internet-based intervention with videoconference sessions with a therapist. It is also the first in Spanish language. Therefore, the results of this study could provide relevant information for future clinical trials. In addition, these findings highlight the important role of therapeutic support. While some patients considered it sufficient, others required more guidance or a higher degree of personalization of the treatment. It is possible that a stepped-care approach that allows the intensity of contact to be adjusted to the needs of each individual may address the heterogeneity that exists among AjD patients (Baumeister et al., 2022).

Limitations and future research

The study has several limitations. First, the recruitment rate was modest: 41 participants over two and a half years, which is insufficient for future RCTs. This rate likely reflects the reliance on a single center with a limited patient flow. For future studies, it will be necessary to expand recruitment methods, for example through collaboration with hospital or primary care services, as well as to increase the number of clinicians involved to accelerate participant enrollment. Second, the sample was predominantly female, with a high level of education and relatively high functioning, which limits the generalizability of the results. Individuals with lower educational levels or less access to technology might respond differently to the blended format, and their underrepresentation in this sample is a significant limitation for the external validity and scalability of TAO Blended. Future studies should incorporate recruitment strategies aimed at increasing sociodemographic diversity. Third, although a cut-off point exists for the Spanish version of the ADNM-20 (Quero et al., 2024), this study was conducted prior to its publication. Furthermore, the diagnosis of AjD was based on ICD-11 criteria, while the interviews for differential diagnosis relied on DSM-IV, introducing some heterogeneity into the diagnostic procedures. Fourth, the participants were individuals interested in receiving a blended intervention, which may have influenced their ratings. Furthermore, adverse effects were assessed solely through an open-ended question at the end of treatment, a common procedure in feasibility studies but one that may underestimate adverse events. Future studies should incorporate standardized measures and periodic assessments. Fifth, the absence of session recordings and independent ratings prevented rigorous verification of adherence to the treatment model. Although therapists followed a structured protocol and received weekly supervision, it would be necessary to include independent fidelity assessments and validated monitoring tools to strengthen internal validity and examine the relationship between adherence and outcomes. Sixth, no a priori success criteria were established for some feasibility indicators, which limits the interpretation of certain results beyond a descriptive assessment and underscores the need to define explicit benchmarks in future studies. Regarding thematic analysis, it is worth noting that it allowed for the identification of clear patterns, but it is not designed to generate theory or capture subjective experience with the depth offered by other qualitative approaches. Additionally, although the aim was to achieve depth and richness rather than a rigid notion of saturation, the small number of participants who dropped out of the intervention limited the depth with which their experiences could be explored. Finally, the absence of a control condition limits the interpretation of the efficacy findings, as symptom reductions may partly reflect spontaneous remission, regression to the mean, or non-specific effects rather than the specific effects of TAO Blended. In addition, the high rate of data loss at follow-up prevents the drawing of robust conclusions regarding long-term treatment effects. Therefore, these results should be considered preliminary and useful for informing future RCTs rather than as definitive evidence of treatment effectiveness.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Universitat Jaume I in March 2022 (CD/42/2022). The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant laws and institutional guidelines were followed and respected throughout the trial. The privacy rights of human subjects were observed. All participants who decided to take part in the study were clearly and precisely informed about what the research consisted of and how their data would be treated. They were also informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could leave the study at any time. All of them signed an informed consent form before starting the treatment. As for the platforms used for data collection and treatment, all complied with European Union data protection regulations, ensuring data security and protection. In addition, in the case of the treatment platform (Psychology and Technology), participants had a username and password known only to them. The resulting databases were anonymized using codes. Similarly, personal information was organized separately from clinical information. Only members of the research team had access to these data.
