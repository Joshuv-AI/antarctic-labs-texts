Efficacy of bupivacaine liposome transversus abdominis plane block in enhancing postoperative recovery following laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection
Authors: Mei Li, Wenwei Wang, Yiping Yang, Yangyang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effect of bupivacaine liposome transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block on laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection patients for clinical practice.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial involved 60 patients (aged 57–84 years, weighing 41–84 kg, mean BMI 21.9 kg/m², ASA Class I/II) undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection with TAP block. Patients were divided into bupivacaine (Bup) and bupivacaine liposome (Bup + Lip) groups, each with 30 patients. Primary outcomes were postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) scores and sufentanil use. Secondary outcomes included recovery room stay, time to initiate oral intake, 72-h recovery quality, pain control satisfaction, and adverse event incidence.ResultsThe study included 28 patients in Bup group and 27 patients in Bup + Lip group in the modiffed intention-to-treat (mITT) population. Compared to the Bup group, the Bup + Lip group had significantly lower VAS scores for both rest and movement at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively (P < 0.05), reduced total sufentanil use (P < 0.05), shorter time to initiate oral intake (P < 0.05), and improved 72-h recovery quality (P < 0.05) and pain control satisfaction (P < 0.05). The Bup + Lip group also had a significantly lower incidence of adverse events within 72 h postoperatively (P < 0.05).ConclusionBupivacaine liposome TAP block significantly improves postoperative pain control, reduces recovery time, and enhances recovery quality and patient satisfaction with low adverse event incidence in laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection patients. It is an effective postoperative analgesic strategy recommended for clinical application.Keywords: Bupivacaine liposome, Transversus abdominis plane block, Laparoscopic colorectal Cancer resection, Postoperative recovery

Introduction

Laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection is a minimally invasive surgical procedure characterized by minimal trauma, rapid recovery, and low postoperative complication rates, making it the standard treatment for colorectal cancer. However, studies have shown that 30–73% of patients experience incisional pain, visceral pain, and shoulder pain due to CO2 pneumoperitoneum following laparoscopic cholecystectomy [1, 2]. These pains can adversely affect the respiratory, circulatory, digestive, endocrine, and immune systems, leading to physiological impairments and psychological distress, thereby reducing the quality of postoperative recovery.

In recent years, multimodal analgesic strategies have emerged as a key area of research to alleviate postoperative pain and enhance the quality of recovery in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection. Among these strategies, nerve block techniques such as the transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block effectively reduce pain from abdominal surgical incisions by blocking the conduction of abdominal wall nerves. Numerous studies have confirmed that TAP can significantly decrease postoperative pain and accelerate recovery in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection [3–6]. However, the currently used long-acting local anesthetics such as ropivacaine and bupivacaine have a duration of action in TAP of only 4 to 7 h, which is insufficient for optimal analgesia.

Bupivacaine liposome is a novel local anesthetic formulated using multivesicular liposome technology and possesses sustained-release characteristics. It has been approved for clinical use in local infiltration and brachial plexus nerve block anesthesia, with an extended duration of action up to 72 h. This significantly reduces dependence on opioid medications, enhances patient satisfaction, and has minimal side effects, making it an ideal postoperative local analgesic [7–13]. While data on the use of bupivacaine liposome in regional anesthesia are limited, studies have shown that its use in ilioinguinal nerve block is safe and provides long-lasting analgesia, reducing the need for opioids and other analgesic drugs [14, 15]. Foreign data have also confirmed its safety and efficacy in TAP [16–20], but there is a lack of relevant clinical data in China. This study aims to investigate the impact of bupivacaine liposome TAP block on postoperative recovery in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection, providing a reference for clinical practice.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled trial compared the efficacy of bupivacaine with bupivacaine liposome transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block on laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection patients. The study protocol was implemented in accordance with Good Clinical Practice principles and the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Taizhou First People’s Hospital (Approval No: 2023-KY066-02). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before enrollment. An independent assessment committee evaluated efficacy independently and blindly. This study does not include any robotic surgery.

This study involved 60 patients who underwent laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection in the Department of Surgery at Taizhou First People’s Hospital from March 2023 to June 2024. The patients’ age range was 57–84 years, with a mean age of 71 years (± 6 years), weight range of 41–84 kg, and mean body mass index (BMI) of 21.9 kg/m² (± 1.7 kg/m²), all classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Class I or II. Exclusion criteria: Patients with severe visual or hearing impairments or inability to communicate effectively; those with unsuccessful or suboptimal nerve block anesthesia; those with postoperative cognitive dysfunction; those with surgery duration exceeding 5 h, blood loss exceeding 400 mL, conversion to open surgery, or those who underwent another surgical anesthesia within 24 h after surgery.

Randomization and anesthesia management

Patients were randomly assigned into two groups using a random number table: the bupivacaine group (Bup) and the bupivacaine liposome group (Bup + Lip), with 30 patients in each group (n = 30). Upon entering the operating room, all patients had a central venous access established. Standard monitoring included electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), and pulse oximetry (SpO2). Fifteen minutes before anesthesia induction, all patients received bilateral TAP blocks under ultrasound guidance. The Bup group received 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine (Shanghai Harvest Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., State drug approval no: H31022839) at each block site, while the Bup + Lip group received 20 mL of bupivacaine liposome (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., State drug approval no: H20223899) diluted in saline (containing 133 mg of bupivacaine liposome) at each site. All patients underwent a standardized anesthesia induction and maintenance protocol: oxygen denitrogenation for 3 min, followed by intravenous injection of propofol 1.5–2.5 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.4 µg/kg, and cisatracurium 0.2 mg/kg for rapid induction, with subsequent mechanical ventilation after tracheal intubation. Anesthesia was maintained with a propofol infusion of 3–10 mg/kg/h and remifentanil infusion of 0.2–0.5 µg/kg/min, with intermittent intravenous administration of sufentanil and cisatracurium. All procedures were performed by anesthesiologists who had been trained for this trial and were of attending physician rank or higher. No other analgesics or antiemetics were administered intraoperatively or postoperatively, except for sufentanil. Postoperatively, all patients were provided with electronic patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA, formulation: 100 µg of sufentanil citrate diluted in 100 mL of saline, with a loading dose of 5 mL, background infusion of 0 mL/h, and a single dose of 4 mL every 10 min). After extubation, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU).

An independent statistician generated a randomization sequence, which was stratified according to the participating centers. The resultant sequences were securely stored in opaque, sealed envelopes and maintained by an impartial third-party custodian. Throughout the study, all researchers, investigators, and participants remained blinded to the sequence prior to randomization and subsequent allocation.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes included the visual analog scale (VAS) scores for rest and movement at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively, as well as the total amount of sufentanil used within 48 h postoperatively. The total sufentanil usage encompassed amounts administered in the PACU, via PCIA, and in the ward. If patients reported unbearable pain upon returning to the ward, an anesthesiologist was consulted to administer an appropriate dose of sufentanil, without the use of any other analgesics.

The secondary outcomes included the duration of stay in the recovery room, the time to initiate oral intake postoperatively (defined as the time of flatus or when the patient feels hungry or thirsty and can start drinking), satisfaction with pain control at 72 h postoperatively, quality of recovery, and the incidence of adverse events such as nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and fatigue. Pain control satisfaction was assessed using the Houston Pain Questionnaire (HPQI, with a score of 100 for complete satisfaction and 0 for complete dissatisfaction) [21]. The quality of postoperative recovery was evaluated using the QoR-40 scale, which assesses five domains: emotional state (9 items), physical comfort (12 items), self-care ability (5 items), psychological support (7 items), and pain (7 items).

During the course of the study, efficacy evaluation was performed by the investigators in accordance with the predetermined protocol. These investigators carried out routine blinded re-assessments, and the resultant data were utilized for the final statistical analysis. Additionally, the efficacy endpoints determined by the investigators were meticulously recorded.

Statistical analyses

The modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, comprising all subjects who received a minimum of one dose of the study intervention (either bupivacaine or bupivacaine liposome) and for whom data were obtainable for at least one assessment time point, was incorporated into the analysis to evaluate the primary and secondary outcomes. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics with SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data that followed a normal or approximately normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation ( ± s). Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests, while intragroup comparisons were conducted using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical data were presented as frequencies or percentages, and compared using the chi-square test. P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 60 patients who underwent laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection were assessed for eligibility and inclusion criteria for randomization (Fig. 1). Moreover, 30 patients each were randomly allocated to receive Bup or Bup + Lip intervention. Two patients in the Bup group were excluded: one had a surgery duration exceeding 5 h (n = 1), and one had blood loss exceeding 400 mL (n = 1). Three patients in the Bup_Lip group were excluded: one underwent conversion to open surgery (n = 1), and two had a surgery duration exceeding 5 h (n = 2). The remaining 55 patients successfully completed the trial, with 28 patients in the Bup group and 27 patients in the Bup + Lip group, and included in the mITT analyses.

Discussion

This study found that bupivacaine liposome TAP block can significantly improve the quality of postoperative rapid recovery and satisfaction with pain control in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer. Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) integrates anesthesia, analgesia, minimally invasive surgery, and other comprehensive measures during the perioperative period, employing a series of evidence-based methods and involving multidisciplinary clinical collaboration. This approach can reduce postoperative complications and mortality rates, shorten hospital stays, and lower hospital costs [22–24]. The core idea is to optimize a series of medical actions to control the patient’s perioperative pathophysiological response, minimize patient suffering, promote early organ function recovery, and help patients return to their preoperative state as soon as possible [25]. Over the past decade, numerous authoritative studies have confirmed the clinical significance of ERAS and its role in promoting postoperative recovery, with continuous updates to relevant guidelines [26–31]. In colorectal surgery, the ERAS concept has been extensively researched and widely applied. In 2013, some authoritative organizations conducted a detailed evidence-based medical review of perioperative management for intestinal cancer surgery and reached a consensus on guidelines. Anesthesia plays a significant role in many aspects of ERAS, such as patient education, preoperative preparation, anesthesia methods, anesthetic drugs, fluid management, temperature monitoring, and postoperative analgesia [27]. Under the ERAS concept, regional anesthesia has been widely recognized and promoted due to its long-lasting analgesia, anti-inflammatory effects, improved tissue perfusion, protection of gastrointestinal function, reduction of chronic pain, satisfactory analgesia, and facilitation of early patient activity and intake. It has become a safe and effective method for rapid recovery and is recommended by some ERAS guidelines [32–36]. Therefore, in thoracic and abdominal surgical procedures, ERAS advocates the combination of general anesthesia with local or regional anesthesia, including spinal anesthesia, TAP, erector spinae plane block, and incisional local infiltration anesthesia, among others.

General anesthesia combined with TAP is a commonly used anesthesia method under the ERAS concept for laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer. The TAP is located between the transversus abdominis and internal oblique muscles, with over 80% of the abdominal muscles, skin, and parietal peritoneum innervated by spinal nerves T7 to L1. These nerves branch in the TAP after exiting the intervertebral foramina to innervate the anterior abdominal muscles and skin [37]. Local anesthetics injected into the TAP can block the nerves in this area, reducing abdominal pain. Numerous studies have confirmed that TAP can significantly reduce postoperative pain and accelerate recovery in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer [3–6]. However, the currently used long-acting local anesthetics such as ropivacaine and bupivacaine have a duration of action in TAP of only 4 to 7 h, which is far from ideal for achieving optimal analgesia. Clinical methods to extend the duration of action include the combined use of dexamethasone, dexmedetomidine, and other drugs, but the effect typically does not exceed 12 h. Alternatively, continuous drug administration via catheter can be used, but this involves inconvenience in operation and potential complications such as catheter displacement, dislodgement, and nerve damage. Therefore, there is a need for a local anesthetic drug with a longer duration of action that is safe and cost-effective.

Liposomal bupivacaine, developed by Pacira Pharmaceuticals in 2011, is a novel local anesthetic formulated using multivesicular liposome technology. After injection, the release of bupivacaine is staggered due to the varying times at which the vesicles rupture, resulting in a sustained-release analgesic effect [38, 39]. Compared to levobupivacaine, it has lower peak plasma concentrations and a longer time to peak when administered locally at the same dose, with fewer toxic side effects. Currently, domestically produced liposomal bupivacaine has been approved for marketing.

Most clinical research data are available for local infiltration, demonstrating that a single incisional infiltration of liposomal bupivacaine after procedures such as hemorrhoidectomy, bilateral breast augmentation, and total knee arthroplasty can alleviate pain for up to 72 h, significantly reduce dependence on opioid medications, enhance patient satisfaction, and have no significant side effects, making it an ideal postoperative local analgesic [7–13]. Data on the use of liposomal bupivacaine for regional nerve blocks are limited, but relevant clinical studies have found that it can be safely used for ilioinguinal nerve blocks in hip replacement surgery or adductor canal nerve blocks in knee replacement surgery, with excellent analgesic effects [14, 15, 40, 41]. Foreign studies have also shown that implementing TAP in surgeries such as rectal cancer is safe and can provide long-lasting analgesia, reducing the use of opioids and other analgesic drugs [16–20]. This study found that in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer, TAP block with liposomal bupivacaine resulted in significantly lower VAS scores for rest and movement at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively compared to the levobupivacaine group, and significantly improved satisfaction with postoperative pain control. This indicates that using liposomal bupivacaine for TAP block in abdominal surgery is safe and can provide sustained analgesia for over 48 h, significantly enhancing patient satisfaction with postoperative pain control.

The use of large doses of opioids significantly increases the risk of perioperative adverse events such as constipation, dizziness, sedation, fatigue, hot flashes, increased sweating, delirium, itching, reduced bowel motility, and nausea and vomiting [42–46]. Nausea and vomiting, the most common side effects of opioids, can affect the quality of postoperative recovery; reduced bowel motility can significantly delay the time to initiate oral intake, hindering rapid postoperative recovery. This study showed that the total postoperative usage of sufentanil was significantly reduced in the liposomal bupivacaine group, with a marked decrease in the incidence of adverse events such as nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and fatigue, and a significant shortening of the time to initiate oral intake, leading to a significant improvement in recovery quality. This suggests that TAP block with liposomal bupivacaine can substantially reduce the use of postoperative opioid analgesics, thereby reducing their associated side effects and accelerating the postoperative recovery quality of gastrointestinal surgery patients.

Epidural anesthesia and TAP block with bupivacaine liposome are two different methods for postoperative analgesia in laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection. Epidural anesthesia involves injecting local anesthetics into the epidural space, which can block a wide range of nerve fibers, providing effective analgesia for a relatively long time. It can cover a larger area of innervation compared to TAP block, including not only the abdominal wall but also the visceral nerves in some cases. However, epidural anesthesia has several potential drawbacks. It requires more technical expertise for placement, and there are risks such as accidental dural puncture, epidural hematoma, and infection. In addition, it may cause more systemic side effects like hypotension, urinary retention, and motor blockade, which can affect the patient’s early mobilization and overall recovery.

On the other hand, TAP block with bupivacaine liposome targets the nerves in the transversus abdominis plane. It specifically blocks the nerves innervating the anterior abdominal wall, effectively reducing pain from abdominal surgical incisions. The use of bupivacaine liposome provides a sustained-release effect, lasting up to 72 h, which can significantly reduce the need for opioid medications. Compared to epidural anesthesia, TAP block is relatively easier to perform, has a lower risk of serious complications, and has fewer systemic side effects. It allows for better preservation of motor function, facilitating early patient mobilization and faster return of gastrointestinal function, as evidenced by the shorter time to initiate oral intake in this study. In summary, while epidural anesthesia has its own analgesic advantages, TAP block with bupivacaine liposome offers a more targeted, safer, and potentially more beneficial option for postoperative analgesia in laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection patients in terms of reducing side effects and promoting rapid recovery.

Limitations of the study

The dosage of the opioid analgesic sufentanil used intraoperatively was based on the experience of the anesthesiologists and was not strictly controlled to ensure patient postoperative awakening, resulting in no significant difference in intraoperative usage between the two groups. Further research is needed to determine whether liposomal bupivacaine can reduce the intraoperative use of opioids.

Conclusion

TAP block with liposomal bupivacaine can significantly improve the quality of postoperative rapid recovery and satisfaction in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer, providing a valuable option for perioperative analgesic management in abdominal surgery patients.
