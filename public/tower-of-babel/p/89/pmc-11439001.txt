Novel soliton solutions and phase plane analysis in nonlinear Schrödinger equations with logarithmic nonlinearities
Authors: Du’a Al-zaleq, Lewa’ Alzaleq
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates a generalized form of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation characterized by a logarithmic nonlinearity. The nonlinear Schrödinger equation, a fundamental equation in nonlinear wave theory, is applied across various physical systems including nonlinear optics, Bose–Einstein condensates, and fluid dynamics. We specifically explore a logarithmic variant of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation to model complex wave phenomena that conventional polynomial nonlinearities fail to capture. We derive four distinct forms of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with logarithmic nonlinearity and provide exact solutions for each, encompassing bright, dark, and kink-type solitons, as well as a range of periodic solitary waves. Analytical techniques are employed to construct bounded and unbounded traveling wave solutions, and the dynamics of these solutions are analyzed through phase portraits of the associated dynamical systems. These findings extend the scope of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation to more accurately describe wave behaviors in complex media and open avenues for future research into non-standard nonlinear wave equations.

Introduction

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation is a widely-studied equation that arises from the study of nonlinear phenomena in dispersive and inhomogeneous media, such as nonlinear optics1, Bose–Einstein condensates2, fluid dynamics3, plasma physics4, magnetic spin waves5,6, modeling of deep water7, and many others. In optics, the nonlinear Schrödinger equation appears in Manakov system, which is a model of wave propagation in optical fibres. This equation models a variety of nonlinear optical effects in a fiber such as self-phase modulation, optical solitons, second-harmonic generation, four-wave mixing, ultrashort pulses, stimulated Raman scattering, etc. Additionally, for water waves, the nonlinear Schrödinger equation is used to describe the evolution of the envelope of modulated wave groups.

For many physical systems, the function F is a polynomial of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u|^2$$\end{document}|u|2 (see, e.g.,9–11) or a ratio of polynomials of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|u|^2$$\end{document}|u|2 (see, e.g.,12). The Schrödinger equation that describes non-Kerr effects is a non-integrable model and cannot be solved analytically by the inverse scattering transform method11,13,14. However, bright, dark, and gray solitons can still exists for the Schrödinger equations in non-Kerr media11–13,15.

Recent advancements in soliton physics and wave guiding have significantly broadened our understanding of nonlinear wave phenomena across various physical systems. Notably, the generation of Brillouin-Kerr soliton frequency combs in optical microresonators has been demonstrated, achieving stable repetition rates and narrow linewidths through a simplified approach utilizing a Brillouin laser and a broad linewidth diode laser as the input pump16. Additionally, the self-structuring of cold atom clouds under optical feedback has revealed diverse phases, including hexagonal, stripe, and honeycomb, with potential applications in understanding dipolar supersolids and motivating further studies on new stripe supersolid phases17. Another innovative contribution is the introduction of a novel wave guiding mechanism using a periodic array of slits, demonstrated for surface plasmon polaritons and surface gravity water waves, which supports low-loss propagation and higher on-axis intensity without spatial confinement, applicable to various types of waves18. Furthermore, the first synchronized generation of bright and dark solitons in a passive microresonator, facilitated by cross-phase modulation, has been reported, with implications for optical signal processing and telecommunications due to the resultant stable light states with frequency comb-like spectra19. Moreover, the evolution of periodic wave trains through surface gravity water wave packets has been theoretically and experimentally studied. The findings demonstrate that, in the linear regime, low-steepness waves unfold a Talbot carpet, while increasing wave steepness leads to a transition through the Akhmediev breather solution and eventually to asymmetric wave breaking. These phenomena are effectively modeled using the Dysthe equation20.

It was shown that Eq. (1.3) has soliton-like solutions, known as Gaussons, for a number of cases, and their interactions were studied. This equation admits applications to quantum mechanics24,25, quantum optics26, quantum gravity27, nuclear physics28, theory of Bose–Einstein condensation29, modelling magma transport30, information theory31, transport and diffusion phenomena32, and others.

It is important to keep in mind that the Klein–Gordon equation is connected to the Schrödinger equation for the quantum wave function. The Klein–Gordon equation is the most straightforward relativistic equation for wave function, in contrast to the Schrödinger equation, which is based on a nonrelativistic expression for the energy of a particle. The Klein–Gordon equation may be derived from the Schrödinger equation by taking into account a spinless particle in a vacuum with no potential energy.

Bartkowski and Górka33 recently took into consideration the following relativistic version of equation (1.3), which is the Klein–Gordon equation with a logarithmic nonlinearity,1.4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \left( \Delta -\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial ^2}{\partial t^2}\right) u=\frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar ^2}u+\epsilon u\log |u|^2. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Δ-1c2∂2∂t2u=m2c2ħ2u+ϵulog|u|2.It was demonstrated by the authors that traveling wave solutions existed for Eq. (1.4) in the one-dimensional case. In34, the authors explored further versions of the Klein–Gordon equation that had various logarithmic nonlinearities; from these, they were able to obtain several types of solitary waves using the auxiliary equation technique.

Overall, the current understanding of the logarithmic nonlinear Schrödinger equation is that it is a challenging equation to solve exactly, but it has been used to model a wide range of physical systems and has led to a deeper understanding of the behavior of nonlinear waves. One of the main challenges in solving the logarithmic Schrödinger equation is that the logarithmic nonlinearity term is not well understood and can lead to singularities in the solutions. This makes it difficult to obtain exact analytical solutions, and most studies have focused on numerical and approximate analytical solutions (see, e.g.35,36).

Conclusions

This study presents a significant extension to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation by incorporating logarithmic nonlinearities, thereby revealing a diverse array of solitonic solutions, including bright, dark, kink-type solitons, and periodic solitary waves. The key contributions of this paper lie in the introduction of these novel soliton solutions, which offer a more comprehensive understanding of nonlinear wave phenomena compared to traditional polynomial nonlinearities. Unlike previous studies, which predominantly focus on polynomial nonlinearities, our solutions leverage logarithmic nonlinearities to capture wave behaviors with greater complexity and versatility. The derived solutions not only broaden the theoretical framework of soliton dynamics but also have practical implications for various fields such as nonlinear optics, Bose–Einstein condensates, and fluid dynamics. By analyzing these solutions through the lens of phase plane dynamics, we highlight the intricate behaviors near hyperbolic and non-hyperbolic points and their impact on wave interactions. This analysis reveals a spectrum of dynamical structures, including saddle and center points, which provide new insights into the potential complexity of wave interactions and stability. These advancements underscore how logarithmic nonlinearities can lead to richer and more nuanced wave behaviors, setting our work apart from existing literature. The findings suggest promising avenues for future research into nonlinear wave equations with non-standard forms of nonlinearity, potentially offering new applications and deeper insights into various physical and engineering systems.
