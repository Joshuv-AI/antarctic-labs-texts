Investigating the role of oncogenic FAM83A as a prognostic biomarker in lung adenocarcinoma: Insights from smoker and non-smoker cohorts
Authors: Prithvi Singh, Aanchal Rathi, Mohammad Masood, Md.Imtaiyaz Hassan, Mohammad Mahfuzul Haque, Ravins Dohare, Anas Shamsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) in smokers and non-smokers presents distinct clinical characteristics, including differences in genetics, treatment response, and prognosis. To explore these differences, we conducted a meta-analysis using publicly accessible LUAD datasets, identifying meta-differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in smokers and non-smokers. A total of 29meta-DEGs were discovered, with seven (CLDN2, CLDN18, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, FAM83A, HLF, and PLA2G1B) demonstrating prognostic significance in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Among these, FAM83A was particularly noteworthy for its amplification in LUAD samples and its negative correlation with immune cell infiltration. Functional enrichment analysis revealed key pathways, such as cytochrome P450 metabolism and cell–cell adhesion, which may be critical in LUAD progression. Our findings highlight FAM83A as a potential prognostic biomarker with significant implications for treatment strategies, especially concerning immune modulation. This study offers a more comprehensive insight into the molecular differences in LUAD in smokers and non-smokers and lays the groundwork for targeted therapies tailored to these subgroups.

Introduction

The Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) estimated that in 2020, there were 19.3 million new cancer cases worldwide, with lung cancer (LC) being the second most prevalent, comprising 11.4% of the total cases. However, with an expected 1.8 million deaths (18%), LC remained the leading cause of cancer-related deaths. By 2040, the global number of cancer cases is expected to reach 28.4 million, reflecting a 47% rise compared to 20201. In both sexes, LC was recognized as both the leading and third most prevalent cancer type. Based on current data, it is estimated that approximately 2.2 million new LC cases were reported globally in 2020. These cases accounted for 14.3% (1.4 million) of all new cancer diagnoses in males and 8.4% (0.8 million) in females2.

Global study highlights cigarette smoking as the leading cause of LC. Additionally, around 600000 deaths worldwide are linked to passive smoking, and nearly one-third of adult smokers are constantly exposed to it3. However, notably, according to some findings from the study, 15% of patients with LC who have undergone testing have never smoked, as have about 53% of female patients. Studies have indicated that approximately 25% of instances of LC worldwide that are evaluated have no clear smoking connection. A variety of other variables therefore cause LC in addition to smoking4, 5. Furthermore, the percentage of non-smokers diagnosed with LC has increased over time. This also indicates the presence of distinct mechanisms of carcinogenesis, as well as differences in clinicopathological features, epidemiology, and the disease's progression, when compared to LC in smokers5, 6. Several investigations on the identification of signature genes and the molecular mechanism behind LC have been carried out; however, they have only been applied to specific sexes (male smokers, female non-smokers), as well as individual patients who were either smokers or non-smokers7, 8, 9, 10.

The highest frequency of mutations was observed in the following order: TP53, EGFR, KRAS, PIK3CA, ALK, and PTEN. Missense mutations were common, with ever-smokers having higher rates of TP53 and EGFR mutations11, 12, 13. Based on further studies, non-smokers' LC is more likely than smokers' to have EGFR mutations14. Targeted therapies, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), can effectively block the kinase activity of the carcinogenic EGFR and ALK proteins15. Most exposure to passive smoking comes from the opposite side of smoke released from the burning tip of the cigarette. Peripheral smoke is hazardous as it contains elevated concentrations of carcinogenic compounds, including benzene, nicotine, ammonia (NH3), carbon monoxide (CO), and other toxicants. It is believed that long-term exposure to passive smoking poses health risks to non-smokers that are similar to those posed by light smoking. Exposure to environmental tobacco smoke causes the symptoms of chronic lung illnesses, allergies, and angina in adults who already have a medical history of these conditions. Acute symptoms experienced by healthy individuals include headaches, nausea, and irritation of the ocular and nasal mucosal membranes16. As evidenced by multiple studies, individuals with a family history of LC are at an increased risk for the disease, suggesting that LC in non-smokers may be attributed to a genetic predisposition.17. Certain germline mutations (GMs) have been detected in families where non-smokers have a high incidence of LC. The GM in EGFR is the most notable of them18.

As our understanding of the molecular biology of cancer has expanded, significant distinctions between LC in smokers and non-smokers have been discovered. According to several studies, LC in non-smokers differs sufficiently from smokers' LC in terms of biology and epidemiology to require consideration of a distinct entity19. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), particularly lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), is more prevalent in non-smokers, light smokers, and former smokers. In contrast, heavy smokers are more likely to develop lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC)20. However, there is still much to learn about the specific molecular pathways that explain the development and spread of LC in smokers and non-smokers of both sexes.

Therefore, we conducted an integrated analysis of LUAD patients including both males and females, smokers and non-smokers, to delineate molecular distinctions between these groups. The aim was to identify hallmark genes through meta-analysis of publicly available messenger RNA (mRNA) expression datasets using a combining p-value–based algorithm. These meta-differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subsequently examined through protein–protein interaction network (PPIN) construction, functional enrichment, and survival analyses to uncover potential prognostic biomarkers linked with overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS). Genetic, epigenetic, and immune infiltration characteristics of these biomarkers were further validated using cBioPortal, UALCAN, and TIMER. This multi-omics approach allowed us to not only identify reliable molecular signatures but also strengthen their clinical relevance by focusing on survival-filtered prognostic genes. Importantly, distinguishing LUAD in smokers versus non-smokers carries significant clinical value, as smoking-related tumors often harbor tobacco-specific mutations, while non-smoker LUAD may be shaped by alternative environmental carcinogens or genetic predispositions. The novelty of our study lies in integrating multi-cohort data with survival-based filtering, thereby providing a robust foundation for biomarker discovery. We hypothesize that distinct molecular signatures and pathways, with unique mutational, promoter methylation, and immune infiltration profiles, drive LUAD in smokers and non-smokers. By capturing these differences, our study enhances the understanding of LUAD biology and lays the groundwork for the development of precision diagnostics and therapies tailored to smoking status.

Materials and methods

LUAD-associated mRNA profile extraction and meta-analysis

National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI)-gene expression omnibus (GEO)21 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) was queried utilizing “LUAD” and “lung adenocarcinoma” as keywords for retrieving mRNA expression dataset(s) related to LUAD. All search results were filtered further in accordance with the following inclusion criteria: (1) the type of dataset(s) must be “expression profiling by array” with all its samples belonging to homo sapiens; (2) there must be accessibility of both pre-processed and raw expression values for the dataset(s); (3) the dataset(s) must encompass ≥25 patient samples; (4) there must be segregation of patient samples into smokers and non-smokers within the dataset(s); (5) dataset(s) must be deposited to GEO within last 10 years (i.e., 2012-2022). Exclusion was done for those studies that lacked case reports, abstracts, non-human samples, review articles, and cell-line-based experimental design. We fetched the series matrix files of the dataset(s) as per the inclusion and exclusion criteria followed by further quality checks (QCs). Each probe ID within dataset(s) was assigned its respective HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) gene symbol(s) as per the official sequencing platform Bioconductor package. p-value and log2(foldchange) value of all genes across individual datasets were computed via limma22 R package followed by obtaining common genes between all individual datasets utilizing the intersect function. Fisher's method of combining p-value was used thereafter to obtain p-value and Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)-p-value for all the common genes. Next, we averaged log2foldchange values across all individual datasets to obtain a single log2foldchange values for the common genes. All the genes with a BH-p-value<0.05 and log2(foldchange)>1 were categorized as meta-DEGs.

PPIN and functional enrichment analyses of meta-DEGs

We inputted all the meta-DEGs to STRING v12.0 web-based tool23(https://string-db.org/) to construct a PPIN at medium confidence (corresponding to an interactionscore>0.4) and afterwards visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.124. CytoNCA plugin25 was used to analyze PPIN topological properties. All the meta-DEGs were given as an input to Enrichr web-based tool26, 27 (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) for assembling top 5 significant (i.e., p-value<0.05) Reactome pathway, gene ontology (GO)-biological process (BP), GO-molecular function (MF), GO-cellular compartment (CC) terms.

Prognostic analysis of meta-DEGs

We accessed Kaplan-Meier (KM) plotter web-based tool28(https://kmplot.com/analysis/) to explore the prognostic significance of meta-DEGs across The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD cohort. We generated KM plots of only those meta-DEGs which reported significant OS and RFS trends across TCGA-LUAD cohort as per parameters discussed previously29.

Validation of prognostic meta-DEGs via cBioPortal and UALCAN

We accessed the cBioPortal30, 31, 32(https://www.cbioportal.org/) to examine the putative copy number alterations (CNAs) and mutations of prognostically significant meta-DEGs. We selected LUAD dataset (TCGA, Firehose Legacy) to perform our analysis. We queried UALCAN web-based tool33, 34(https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) thereafter for examining promoter methylation level of prognostically significant meta-DEGs based on individual cancer stages and patient smoking status across TCGA-LUAD cohort.

Tumor infiltration analysis

TIMER web-based tool35(https://timer.cistrome.org/) was queried to discover the correlation between prognostically significant meta-DEGs expression levels and CD8+ T cells, B cells, dendritic cells (DCs) across TCGA-LUAD cohort. p-value<0.05 was regarded as the statistically significant threshold along with Spearman’s test used for computing correlation coefficient.

LUAD-associated mRNA profile extraction and meta-analysis

As per the abovementioned inclusion and exclusion criteria, we chose datasets with accession numbers GSE43458, GSE50081, and GSE75037. We retained only smoker and non-smoker tumor patient samples in these three datasets and eliminated any other patient samples for further analysis. Patient sample information corresponding to all these datasets is summarized in Table 1. We found a total of 8915 common genes among all three individual datasets followed by Fisher's method of combining p-value wherein we ended with computation of p-value, BH-p-value, and averaged log2foldchange values for all these common genes. A total of 29 meta-DEGs (24 downregulated + 5 upregulated) corresponding to BH-p-value<0.05 and log2(foldchange)>1 were identified. Discussion

The dysregulation of specific genes in LUAD is associated with cancer progression, with distinct patterns observed in smoker and non-smoker patients. Researchers found that gene expression profilings (GEPs) varied significantly between these two groups, with smoking patients showing more complex disease mechanisms36. This was further supported by scientists, who identified unique gene expression patterns in smokers, including upregulation of cell cycle regulators37. These results aligned with researchers' identification of a gene expression signature (GES) characteristic of smoking, which included cell cycle genes associated with mitotic spindle formation38. Scientists also identified potential genes related to LUAD, including those involved in mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)37. Taken together, these studies indicate that the dysregulation of certain genes in LUAD is affected by smoking status and may contribute to cancer progression.

The PPIN analysis revealed 11 nodes, with CYP3A5 and PIGR having the highest equal degrees (Fig. 3). The functional enrichment analysis highlighted most significant pathway, GO-BP, GO-MF, GO-CC terms such as cytochrome P450-arranged by substrate type, Ca2+-independent cell–cell adhesion via plasma membrane cell-adhesion molecules, serine-type endopeptidase activity, bicellular TJ, indicating potential molecular associations in the context of meta-DEGs (Fig. 4). TP53 mutations are known to be prevalent in smoking-related LUAD and are linked to loss of genomic stability and regulation of apoptosis. KRAS mutations, which are also common in smoking-related LUAD, drive tumorigenesis by triggering essential signaling pathways, including the MAPK and PI3K-AKT pathways. These mutations could modulate the pathways identified in our study, thus providing further insight into the genetic drivers of DEGs in smokers39, 40.

Cytochrome P450 enzymes are essential in the progression of LUAD, with specific subtypes being associated with different aspects of the disease. For instance, CYP24A1 has been shown to enhance cancer cell proliferation in mutant KRAS-dependent LUAD, independent of its catalytic function41. Similarly, the expression of CYP1A1, CYP2E1, and CYP3A has been associated with poor survival outcomes in advanced adenocarcinoma42. The role of CYP in the metabolism of carcinogens and anti-cancer agents is also significant, with CYP19 (aromatase) and CYP24 being frequently expressed in NSCLC43. Furthermore, the presence of high levels of pulmonary cytochrome P450IA, particularly in current smokers, is associated with an increased risk of developing LC, particularly the peripheral adenocarcinomatous type44. These findings highlight the intricate and varied roles of CYP enzymes in LUAD.

The key carcinogenic mechanisms in smokers is the DNA damage induced by the inhalation of tobacco smoke, which contains a variety of carcinogens. This encompasses compounds like polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and nitrosamines, which are metabolically activated in the body through cytochrome P450 enzymes. The interaction between CYP enzymes and tobacco smoke is critical in the context of LUAD in smokers. The CYP4B1 gene, which we identified as a significant DEG in our study, plays a role in the metabolism of these carcinogenic substances. It is known that CYP enzymes, particularly CYP1A1, CYP2A6, and CYP4B1, metabolize components of tobacco smoke, facilitating their activation into more reactive metabolites that can bind to DNA and induce mutations. These mutations can impair key genes, such as TP53 and KRAS, which are commonly mutated in smokers with LUAD.

CLDN18 and CLDN2 are integral membrane proteins (IMPs) located at TJs and exhibit Ca2+-independent cell-adhesion activity45, 46, 47, 48. This activity is modulated by intracellular Ca2+ levels, which are crucial in the activation of cell adhesion molecules (CAMs)49. The expression of CLDN2 and CLDN18 in LUAD has been investigated, highlighting their involvement in cancer proliferation, motility, and malignant potential. CLDN2 expression is enhanced by the EGFR/MEK/ERK/c-Fos pathway, which facilitates cell proliferation and motility50. In contrast, CLDN18 inhibits cell proliferation and motility by suppressing the PI3K/PDK1/Akt signaling pathway51. CLDN18 has been recognized as a regulator of yes-associated protein (YAP) activity, controlling organ size, progenitor cell (PC) proliferation, and tumorigenesis52. The claudin (CLDN) protein family is essential for the formation and function of TJs, which are vital for preserving epithelial barrier integrity. These proteins regulate paracellular permeability and ion selectivity, with their interactions being key to assembly53. The roles of CLDN18 and CLDN2 in the bicellular TJ pathway in LUAD are complex and multifactorial. CLDN18, a critical component of TJs, has been shown to regulate lung stem and PC homeostasis and tumorigenesis, possibly through its interaction with YAP52. Furthermore, the lung-specific isoform CLDN18.1 has been shown to reduce malignancy and associated signaling pathways in LUAD, including the IGF1R and AKT phosphorylation54. Conversely, CLDN2, another member of the CLDN family, has been linked to the progression of LUAD, with its overexpression correlating with a poor prognosis55. The studies indicate crucial roles for CLDN18 and CLDN2 in LUAD development and progression may involve the modulation of the bicellular TJ pathway, highlighting the need for further research to fully understand their contributions.

HABP2, a regulator of hyaluronan-mediated cancer dynamics, is overexpressed in LUAD and is associated with increased uPA activation, migration, and extravasation56. The TMPRSS2 gene, which encodes transmembrane serine protease, has been recognized as a prognostic factor in LUAD, with reduced expression linked to poorer OS57. On the other hand, the MMP7 gene is upregulated in LUAD and is associated with LN metastasis, being regulated by COX-2 and promoting tumor proliferation and invasion58. Both genes are involved in the serine-type endopeptidase activity pathway, with MMP7 being a member of the matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) family, which is associated with the immune response (IR) in cancer59. Additional research is required to comprehensively understand the interaction between these genes and their role in LUAD.

In our study, 29 meta-DEGs were evaluated for prognostic significance across TCGA-LUAD cohort (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Higher FAM83A expression correlated with worsened OS and RFS, while lower expression of CLDN18, CLDN2, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, HLF, and PLA2G1B was associated with poorer outcomes. Notably, FAM83A showed higher HR values, suggesting a pronounced prognostic impact. In multiple studies, increased expression of FAM83A is consistently linked to poorer OS in LUAD patients60, 61, 62. However, the influence of smoking status on this correlation is not explicitly addressed in these studies. Further research is needed to determine if smoking status could potentially modify the prognostic significance of FAM83A in LUAD.

FAM83A overexpression is also linked to higher mutation, amplification, and deletion frequencies, suggesting a potential role in LUAD progression62. This is further corroborated by findings that FAM83A plays a role in cancer proliferation, migration, and the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) of LUAD cells60. FAM83A influences PD-L1 expression levels in LUAD patients and may serve as a key biomarker for immune escape and PD-1/PD-L1-targeted therapies63. However, the specific correlation between FAM83A alterations and smoking status in LUAD patients remains to be fully elucidated. Our study also looked at genetic changes in specific genes (CLDN18, CLDN2, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, FAM83A, HLF, PLA2G1B) in 584 LUAD patients. The OncoPrint figure (Fig. 7) indicates that 16% of patients had alterations in these genes, with FAM83A being altered in 9% of cases. The barplots (Fig. 8) show the types of alterations, with FAM83A having a 7.95% amplification frequency. Specifically, FAM83A showed increased amplification in patients with LUAD, suggesting a potential role in the development of the disease.

Our study also explored how smoking and cancer stage affect the level of gene changes (Fig. 9). The observed alterations in gene expression across different cancer stages provide valuable insights into the potential roles of CLDN18, CLDN2, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, FAM83A, HLF, and PLA2G1B in LUAD progression. The significant decrease in the expression of CLDN2, and PLA2G1B with advancing cancer stages suggests a potential tumor-suppressive role for these genes in the early stages of LUAD. Conversely, the notable increase in HLF expression implies a possible oncogenic function, indicating its involvement in promoting the progression of LUAD with increasing cancer severity. These findings highlight the interaction of these genes at various stages of the disease, establishing a basis for further research into their specific roles in the development and progression of LUAD.

The scatterplots presented in Fig. 10 illustrate the correlations between the expression levels of key prognostic genes (CLDN2, CLDN18, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, FAM83A, HLF, PLA2G1B) and the composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in LUAD. Positive correlations indicate that higher gene expression is associated with increased levels of infiltrating immune cells (CD8+ T cells, B cells, DCs), suggesting potential interactions between these genes and the IR against cancer. Notably, FAM83A showed negative correlations, suggesting its potential role in suppressing immune cell infiltration. Additionally, both CLDN2 and CLDN18 exhibited negative correlations with tumor purity, implying that higher expression of these genes is associated with a less pure TME in LUAD. FAM83A impacts the recruitment and activity of immune cells in the TME via chemokine and cytokine profile alteration. Correlation of FAM83A expression with decremented CD8+ T cells levels support the fact that an immunosuppressive TME is promoted by FAM83A. FAM83A may play a role in immune evasion by reducing the presence of immune cells such as T lymphocytes and macrophages (MPs) in the TME, potentially impairing the IR against the tumor. Given its role in immune suppression, FAM83A could serve as a promising biomarker for immunotherapy resistance in LUAD patients. Thus, FAM83A multifaceted role favors tumor evasion and LUAD progression64.

This study advances existing knowledge of LUAD by providing a comprehensive analysis of GEPs in both smokers and non-smokers, highlighting the distinct molecular mechanisms that may contribute to the progression of LUAD in these two groups. The inclusion of smoker and non-smoker patient samples allowed us to uncover important differences in gene regulation, offering a clearer understanding of how smoking influences LUAD biology. A key contribution of this study is the identification of FAM83A and other meta-DEGs as novel prognostic biomarkers. Specifically, we found that higher expression of FAM83A was associated with worsened OS and RFS in LUAD patients, establishing its potential as a prognostic indicator. This finding is particularly significant as FAM83A has not been extensively studied in the context of LUAD, and its involvement in immune modulation further underscores its relevance in cancer progression. Our analysis reveals that FAM83A correlates with altered immune cell infiltration, particularly in suppressing the recruitment of CD8+ T cells, B cells, and DCs, which suggest a potential role in immune evasion and an immunosuppressive TME. This novel insight into FAM83A’s role in immune modulation opens new avenues for exploring its potential as a therapeutic target in LUAD.

In addition to FAM83A, we identified other meta-DEGs such as CLDN18, CLDN2, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, HLF, and PLA2G1B, which have significant prognostic value. Notably, the dysregulation of these genes was found to correlate with poorer OS and RFS, further reinforcing their importance in LUAD survival outcomes. By integrating these findings with genomic alteration data from TCGA and examining the impact of smoking and cancer stage on gene expression, this study provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the molecular differences between smokers and non-smokers with LUAD.

The results of our study could have several implications for clinical decision-making. Biomarkers such as FAM83A, which exhibit differential expression between smoker and non-smoker LUAD patients, can be utilized for stratified diagnostic panels. These biomarkers, in combination with smoking status, could enhance early detection accuracy by tailoring diagnostic strategies to specific patient subgroups. The strong association of FAM83A with poor OS and RFS highlights its potential as a prognostic indicator. Patients with high FAM83A expression could be identified as high-risk, enabling closer monitoring and aggressive treatment regimens. Similarly, low expression of CLDN2, CLDN18, and PLA2G1B could serve as negative prognostic markers.

The involvement of FAM83A in immune evasion and its correlation with TME suppression presents an opportunity for therapeutic intervention. FAM83A may serve as a candidate for targeted therapies aimed at modulating the tumor immune landscape, particularly in conjunction with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). By delineating the molecular differences between smoker and non-smoker cohorts, the study provides a potential foundation for personalized treatment approaches. For instance, non-smokers exhibiting high EGFR pathway activity may benefit from targeted TKIs, while smokers with elevated FAM83A levels might require therapies addressing immune suppression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study underscores the molecular heterogeneity of LUAD between smokers and non-smokers, characterized by distinct epidemiological, genetic, and clinicopathological features. By analyzing 209 patient samples from three GEO datasets, we identified 29 meta-DEGs, among which seven (CLDN2, CLDN18, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, FAM83A, HLF, and PLA2G1B) emerged as significant prognostic markers. Survival analysis revealed that reduced expression of CLDN18, CLDN2, CYP4B1, CYP4X1, HLF, and PLA2G1B correlated with poor outcomes, whereas elevated expression of FAM83A worsened survival, consistent with its frequent amplification across TCGA-LUAD patients. Functional enrichment and immune infiltration analyses suggested that these genes may have stage-specific roles in LUAD progression, with FAM83A showing a particularly strong link to immune evasion. Furthermore, our findings indicate that environmental carcinogens, including air pollution and radon, may contribute to the molecular profiles of non-smoker LUAD through modulation of genes such as CYP4B1.

Although the study is limited by reliance on public datasets, potential analytical biases, and absence of experimental validation, it provides a strong foundation for future investigations. Experimental confirmation through qRT-PCR, IHC, or functional assays, along with integration of multi-omics data, will be crucial to validate these biomarkers and refine their clinical relevance. Taken together, our results highlight novel molecular distinctions in smoker versus non-smoker LUAD and identify candidate biomarkers, particularly FAM83A, CLDN2, and PLA2G1B, that may guide precision diagnostics, prognostic assessment, and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies in LUAD.
