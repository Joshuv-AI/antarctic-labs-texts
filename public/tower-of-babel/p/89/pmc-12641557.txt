Anthrax in Koraput, India: A 15 year retrospective analysis of epidemiological trends, seasonal patterns and vaccine coverage
Authors: Hari Ram Choudhary, Rachita Pradhan, Matrujyoti Pattnaik, Debaprasad Parai, Arun Kumar Padhi, Swati Pattnaik, Santosh Kumar Panigrahi, Y. Selvamani, Alex Joseph, Jaya Singh Kshatri, Harpeet Kaur, Debdutta Bhattacharya, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to its potential use as a bioterrorism agent, anthrax remains a disease of global public health concern, particularly in endemic regions. In India, anthrax is enzootic, especially in the eastern and southern states. Periodic outbreaks in both animals and humans have been reported from tribal and rural areas with poor infrastructure, where livestock rearing practices and socio-cultural traditions contribute to disease transmission. In this study, we analyzed anthrax outbreaks in Koraput district over last about one and a half decades and identified key epidemiological patterns and the vaccine coverage to guide future prevention and control measures. Anthrax in Koraput shows a recurrent seasonal pattern linked to environmental spore viability and intensified human–animal contact. The study highlighted that a sustained, high-coverage livestock vaccination implemented through an integrated One Health framework with community engagement and inter-sectoral coordination is critical to interrupt transmission and protect vulnerable tribal and rural populations.

Introduction

Anthrax is a zoonotic disease caused by the spore-forming bacterium Bacillus anthracis, which primarily affects herbivorous animals but can also infect humans through direct or indirect contact with infected animals or animal products [1]. The disease manifests in cutaneous, inhalational, and gastrointestinal forms in humans, with the cutaneous form being the most common [2,3]. Due to its potential use as a bioterrorism agent, anthrax remains a disease of global public health concern, particularly in endemic regions.

India is one of the countries where anthrax is enzootic, especially in the eastern and southern states such as Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and Jharkhand [4,5]. Periodic outbreaks in both animals and humans have been reported from tribal and rural areas with limited veterinary and healthcare infrastructure, where livestock rearing practices and socio-cultural traditions contribute to disease transmission [6]. Communities that are adjacent to forests are typically affected, and outbreaks are primarily recorded from eastern states like Odisha [7]. Despite ongoing surveillance and control efforts, underreporting, limited diagnostic facilities, and a lack of inter-sectoral coordination continue to impede effective anthrax control in India.

An epidemiological assessment of anthrax outbreaks can provide valuable insights into the spatial distribution, temporal trends, risk factors, and public health response mechanisms in India. Such an assessment is essential for informing One Health-based strategies that integrate human, animal, and environmental health approaches to reduce the burden of the disease. This manuscript aims to analyse anthrax outbreaks in Koraput district over the last about one and a half decades and identify key epidemiological patterns and the vaccine coverage to guide future prevention and control measures.

Materials and methods

We undertook a retrospective analysis of the outbreak data related to anthrax among humans and animals reported by the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) [8], National Digital Livestock Mission (NDLM) and District Veterinary Department, Odisha during January 2010 to December 2024 in the Koraput district of Odisha, which is highly endemic to anthrax. The record of livestock vaccination against anthrax being carried out by the Directorate of Animal Husbandry & Veterinary Services, Govt. of Odisha over the period of 15 years was also collected and analyzed.

Koraput district which comes under the Eastern Ghat Highland Agro climatic Zone, spans ∼8807 km2, making it the third largest in Odisha, with just ∼16 % urban population. Koraput has a population of 1.61 million in 2023 with a sex ratio of 1032 and more than 50 % of the population is tribal belonging to various primitive tribal communities like Paraja, Gadaba, Kandha etc. The district has a total of 2 subdivisions, 14 blocks. The district which shares its border with Chhattisgarh and Andhra Pradesh has livestock as the major source of income.

Data were analyzed using statistical package STATA 17.0. Descriptive statistics were employed for summarizing outbreak patterns, case fatality rate and seasonal patterns. The relationship between vaccination coverage and anthrax cases was also analyzed.

The data used for analysis was obtained from the publicly accessible IDSP weekly outbreak database; therefore, no ethical permission was required, and no personal or sensitive information was accessed, ensuring compliance with ethical standards.

Results and discussion

A total of 80 outbreak episodes were reported during the 15 years involving 515 humans and 421 livestock from 10 blocks of the district. A total of 32 human deaths related to anthrax were reported during this period. Anthrax outbreaks among livestock peaked during 2013 and 2017, with 408 human cutaneous anthrax cases and 22 deaths related to anthrax recorded by IDSP (Table 1). The outbreak of Human and livestock anthrax cases reduced to zero in 2020 and since then there have been some sporadic cases reported from only 3 blocks of the district (Fig. 1). The maximum number of outbreaks (n = 27) was observed in the dry-heat period and a few in the rainy season, suggesting a strong seasonal influence on anthrax outbreaks (Fig. 2). Four types of animal species viz. buffalo, goat, sheep, and cow were mainly infected with anthrax. Among the 421 cases in livestock, highest was reported among cattle (80 %), and maximum reported cases were in 2017. Anthrax in other animals like sheep and goats was reported in 2015. During these 15 years, 515 human cases and 32 anthrax-related deaths were reported in the 10 blocks of Koraput district (Fig. 3). First report of gastrointestinal anthrax was reported in 2023 which was linked to a social dinner of tribal communities residing in two blocks of the district. The exposure was linked to consumption of meat from an unvaccinated dead sheep. Twenty-six individuals from those who attended that dinner developed symptoms like abdominal pain, diarrhoea and vomiting within seven days. Rectal swabs were collected from those suspected cases, of which one was tested positive. Thirteen animal deaths, primarily sheep, were reported during the same period. The annual case fatality rates during 2010–2014 varied significantly, ranging from 0 % to 25 %, with the highest rates (16.7 %–25 %) reported during 2010–2012. Temporal association was observed between scaling up of livestock vaccinations and the subsequent decline in anthrax cases (Fig. 1). Following a peak in both human and animal cases between 2014 and 2016, intensified vaccination efforts led to a substantial coverage reaching a maximum in 2020. This period corresponded with a marked reduction in reported cases, suggesting a protective effect of widespread vaccination. Our analysis provides a comprehensive, district-level overview of anthrax-related disease outbreaks, offering insights into emerging patterns, seasonal trends and correlation with livestock vaccination. The annual case fatality rates (CFR) in Koraput have significantly declined since 2020, highlighting the prompt public health measures [7]. CFRs due to human anthrax in Koraput were nil since 2020 in comparison to CFRs reported from outbreaks in Ghana (CFR =36 %), Thailand (CFR = 4 %) and Zambia (CFR = 19 %) [[9], [10], [11]]. This may be due to early case detection, case reporting, sample collection, sample testing and timely antibiotic treatment. Furthermore, ongoing anthrax elimination initiatives by ICMR-Regional Medical Research Centre Bhubaneswar in collaboration with state health and veterinary department, including community-based surveillance, community awareness through information education communication (IEC) and behavioral change communication (BCC) campaigns, intersectoral coordination, and advocacy for free livestock vaccination against anthrax, have strengthened early detection and management of anthrax in the district [12].

The cultural and behavioral practices of the tribal communities living in Koraput district and low livestock vaccination were identified as a major reason behind occurrence of human anthrax cases [7]. One of the most effective preventive strategies in controlling anthrax among animals—and, by extension, humans is livestock vaccination and the use of the Sterne spore vaccine has shown marked success in reducing outbreak frequency when systematically implemented [13]. States like Tamil Nadu and Odisha that have invested in regular, pre-monsoon livestock vaccination campaigns such as parts of Odisha and Tamil Nadu report fewer human cases, underlining the need for consistent vaccination drives [14,15]. Our analysis revealed an inverse relationship between livestock vaccination and reported cases of anthrax. However, annual vaccination coverage remains suboptimal in these regions due to inadequate staff at the block and gram panchayat level veterinary departments, resistance from livestock owners, the absence of livestock during the daytime due to regular grazing, poor accessibility to remote hilly terrains, and other logistical issues.

There is an increased risk of anthrax exposure and underreporting of cases to the concerned departments since most of the communities in the district have extremely poor levels of understanding and information about the disease that affects both humans and animals [6]. States that have adopted interdepartmental coordination mechanisms, involving animal husbandry, tribal welfare, public health, and forest departments, have demonstrated more effective outbreak containment and risk communication [16]. Anthrax, being an ancient disease, remains endemic and enzootic in many parts of the world, mainly due to inadequate public health services, ineffective policies, insufficient research, and poor coordination in addressing sporadic outbreaks [17]. A coordinated One Health approach combining human, animal and environmental departments is crucial to improve early detection, case reporting and response capacity. Findings from the study highlight the need for operationalizing inter-sectoral collaboration through organized surveillance networks and targeted interventions. Increasing the vaccination coverage of livestock and implementing sustained community engagement strategies can enhance the vaccine coverage and significantly decrease the risk of potential future outbreaks.

Conclusion

The study highlights that anthrax continues to be a significant zoonotic threat in Koraput, Odisha, particularly in areas with high human-animal interaction and inadequate veterinary infrastructure. The clear seasonal pattern and correlation between increased livestock vaccination and reduced outbreak incidence underscore the importance of sustained preventive strategies. Strengthening routine animal vaccination, enhancing community awareness, and adopting a comprehensive One Health approach are essential for effective anthrax control. Collaborative efforts between public health, veterinary, and local governance systems will be crucial in mitigating the disease burden and protecting the livelihoods of vulnerable tribal communities.
