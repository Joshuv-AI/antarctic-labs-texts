Long-term HBsAg clearance, relapse, and safety following tenofovir or entecavir discontinuation in non-cirrhotic, HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis
Authors: Onuma Sattayalertyanyong, Wimolrak Bandidniyamanon, Julajak Limsrivilai, Watcharasak Chotiyaputta, Supot Nimanong, Tawesak Tanwandee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stopping nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) can foster hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) clearance, yet comparative data for tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) and entecavir (ETV) are scarce. We compared HBsAg loss, virological relapse (VR), clinical relapse (CR), and safety after stopping TDF or ETV. Ninety-eight non-cirrhotic, HBeAg-negative adults with viral suppression on TDF or ETV for ≥ 3 years were prospectively followed for ≤ 4 years after allocation to TDF discontinuation (TDF-D; n = 39), ETV discontinuation (ETV-D; n = 27), or continued therapy (n = 32). Median follow-up was 146 weeks. HBsAg clearance occurred in 7 patients (10.6%): 12.8% with TDF-D, 7.4% with ETV-D, and none with continued therapy. All clearers had end-of-treatment HBsAg < 100 IU/mL, which was the only independent predictor of clearance (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.18–0.83, p = 0.02). By week 144, cumulative VR was 83% with TDF-D versus 56% with ETV-D (p = 0.002); CR was 50% versus 27% (p = 0.02). Six TDF-D patients had severe ALT flares, two were hospitalized, and one died. NA discontinuation yielded greater HBsAg clearance than continued therapy, mainly within the first year, without a significant difference between TDF and ETV. However, TDF cessation caused earlier and more severe relapses. Careful candidate selection and intensive monitoring are essential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40972-y.

Introduction

Over the past 3 decades, the prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) has declined significantly due to universal newborn vaccination programs. However, up to 240 million individuals worldwide remain chronically infected, facing a 15- to 20-fold increased risk of liver-related events and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Indeed, HBV accounts for at least 50% of HCC cases1,2. HBV DNA levels and hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBeAg) positivity are key contributors to chronic HBV (CHB) progression toward cirrhosis and heightened HCC risk3,4. Many current CHB patients were born before universal vaccination. They are often HBeAg-negative but have significant viral loads, fluctuating HBV DNA and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), progressive inflammation and fibrosis, and low spontaneous remission rates5. While suppressing HBV DNA reduces the relative HCC risk from 16.5- to 1.2-fold, achieving hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) seroclearance can further decrease HCC incidence from 196 to 37 per 100 000 person-years6. Consequently, HBsAg loss (functional cure) is the primary treatment goal in current guidelines5,7,8.

Long-term use of nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs), including tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), tenofovir alafenamide, and entecavir (ETV), effectively reduces necroinflammation and fibrosis in CHB with minimal adverse effects9. These agents serve as first-line therapies due to their high potency and robust genetic barrier5,7. However, HBsAg seroclearance on NA therapy is rare (< 1% annually), and extended treatment is often required, particularly in HBeAg-negative patients5,10. This necessity raises concerns about adherence, cost, and potential adverse events, such as nephrotoxicity and osteoporosis11.

Current guidelines permit NA discontinuation in select non-cirrhotic or compensated cirrhotic HBeAg-negative CHB patients after a few years of undetectable HBV DNA, provided they undergo close monitoring for flares5,8. Some studies suggest that stopping NAs can prompt HBsAg seroclearance in 2%–40% of patients over 3–5 years or at annual rates below 10%12–22. However, NA withdrawal may cause severe ALT flares and can lead to hepatic decompensation, particularly in patients with cirrhosis and, although rarely, even in non-cirrhotic patients15,23. Two randomized controlled trials reported disparate outcomes regarding HBsAg loss after end of treatment (EOT). Berg et al. noted 10% and 19% seroclearance rates at 1 and 3 years, respectively, among Caucasian patients who stopped TDF, compared with 0% in those who continued TDF14. By contrast, Liem et al. identified a 2.2% seroclearance rate at 1 year in a predominantly Asian cohort17. A meta-analysis further showed that 70% of patients developed virological relapse (VR; HBV DNA > 2000 IU/mL) and 50% experienced clinical relapse (CR; HBV DNA > 2000 IU/mL with ALT elevation) within 1 year off therapy. Only 1 cirrhotic patient in that analysis progressed to hepatic decompensation16. To our knowledge, no study has directly compared discontinuing TDF versus discontinuing ETV.

This study aimed to assess the long-term consequences of discontinuing TDF or ETV in comparison with continued NA therapy in non-cirrhotic, HBeAg-negative CHB patients.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 98 HBeAg-negative CHB patients included, 39 were in the TDF-D group, 27 were in the ETV-D group, and 32 continued NA therapy (15 on TDF and 17 on ETV). Baseline characteristics were similar across all groups. The mean age was 60.2 ± 10 years, and 66% of patients were male. The mean transient elastography measurement was 5.7 ± 1.9 kPa. According to METAVIR staging, 17.4% had F0 fibrosis, 59.4% had F1, and 23.3% had F2. Baseline ALT levels were within normal limits in all groups, with a median of 19.5 (interquartile range [IQR] 15–26) IU/mL. The median EOT HBsAg level was 2.7 (IQR 1.8–3.3) log10 IU/mL. The median treatment duration before enrollment was 11.2 (IQR 9.2–13.2) years. The median consolidation time was 6.4 (IQR 3.5–9.7) years in TDF-D and 9.7 (IQR 7.6–10.9) years in ETV-D (p = 0.003). Cumulative rate of HBsAg loss after treatment discontinuation and predictors of HBsAg loss

At a median follow-up of 146 weeks, 7 of 66 patients (10.6%) who discontinued therapy achieved HBsAg loss. Five were in the TDF-D group (12.8%) and 2 were in the ETV-D group (7.4%; p = 0.47). No patients in the continued NA therapy group achieved HBsAg loss (Supplement Fig. 1). HBsAg clearance occurred between 12 and 48 weeks post-discontinuation. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression identified the EOT HBsAg level as the sole predictor of HBsAg loss (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.18–0.83; p = 0.02; Table S2). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for EOT HBsAg was 0.959 (95% CI 0.91–1; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2). The optimal cutoff was 1.815 log10 IU/mL (< 65 IU/mL), yielding 100% sensitivity, 88.1% specificity, and 89.4% accuracy (p < 0.001). Cumulative rate of VR and CR after treatment discontinuation and predictors

Figure 3A,B illustrate the cumulative incidences of VR and CR. By weeks 48, 96, and 144, the TDF-D group had higher VR rates (66.9%, 71.6%, and 83%, respectively) than the ETV-D group (40.7%, 55.8%, and 55.8%, respectively; p = 0.002). Similarly, CR was more frequent in TDF-D (38.6%, 45.7%, and 49.8%) than in ETV-D (22.2%, 26.5%, and 26.5%; p = 0.02). The median time to VR was 12 weeks (range 12–48) in TDF-D and 72 weeks (range 12–84) in ETV-D (p = 0.002). No clinical or virological flares occurred in the continued therapy group.Fig. 3The cumulative rate of virological (A) and clinical relapse (B) in TDF-D, ETV-D, and continued group.

The cumulative rate of virological (A) and clinical relapse (B) in TDF-D, ETV-D, and continued group.

Univariate and multivariate analyses identified treatment with TDF as the sole independent predictor of VR (OR 2.17, 95% CI 1.12–4.2; p = 0.02). For CR, TDF treatment (OR 3.2, 95% CI 1.32–7.72; p = 0.01) and older age (OR 1.05 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.09; p = 0.03) were each significantly associated with a higher risk of relapse. Other factors, including ALT levels, treatment duration, consolidation time, prior NA resistance, and end-of-treatment HBsAg levels, showed no significant associations in the multivariate models (Tables S3 and S4).

Retreatment rate after NA discontinuation

Among patients who discontinued NA therapy, retreatment was required more frequently in the TDF-D group than in the ETV-D group (23/39 [59.0%] vs 9/27 [33.3%]; p = 0.05; Fig. 1). Among patients who required retreatment, the time to NA re-initiation was shorter in the TDF-D group than in the ETV-D group (median 12 [IQR 9–44] vs 40 [IQR 36–60] weeks; p = 0.02).

Outcomes of HBsAg loss, VR, and CR based on HBsAg level

Among 66 patients who discontinued therapy, 17 (25.8%) had EOT HBsAg levels below 100 IU/mL. The cumulative rate of HBsAg loss at weeks 24, 48, 96, and 144 was 14%, 50%, 50%, and 50%, respectively, in patients with EOT HBsAg < 100 IU/mL. No patient with EOT HBsAg ≥ 100 IU/mL achieved HBsAg clearance (p < 0.001; Fig. 4A).Fig. 4Cumulative rate of HBsAg loss (A), virological relapse (B), and clinical relapse (C) by by qHBsAg level < 100 IU/ml.

In contrast, the cumulative rates of VR at weeks 24, 48, 96, and 144 were 28%, 28%, 43%, and 43%, respectively, in those with EOT HBsAg < 100 IU/mL. Among patients with EOT HBsAg ≥ 100 IU/mL, the VR rates at those same time points were 58%, 69%, 79%, and 82% (p = 0.01; Fig. 4B). The cumulative rate of CR at weeks 24, 48, 96, and 144 remained 17% in patients with EOT HBsAg < 100 IU/mL. However, it rose from 17 to 38%, 44%, and 47% in those with EOT HBsAg ≥ 100 IU/mL (p = 0.17; Fig. 4C).

Safety

Adverse events (AEs) occurred in 45 of 98 patients (45.9%) during follow-up. The highest incidence was observed in the TDF-D group (30 of 39; 76.9%), followed by the ETV-D group (15 of 27; 55.6%), and then the continued therapy group (4 of 32; 12.5%) (p < 0.001; Table 3). Grade 4 AEs were reported only in TDF-D (15.4% of patients) and did not occur in the ETV-D or continued groups (p = 0.02). Discussion

This prospective study suggests that discontinuing NAs in non-cirrhotic, HBeAg-negative CHB patients may lead to higher HBsAg clearance rates than continuous therapy, particularly among those treated with TDF. However, VR and CR were significantly more frequent in the discontinuation groups. Notably, TDF cessation was associated with an earlier and more severe onset of VR and CR compared to ETV discontinuation. Our findings also indicate that patients with EOT HBsAg levels below 65 IU/mL are more likely to achieve HBsAg loss after stopping therapy.

Current guidelines for HBeAg-negative CHB allow treatment discontinuation if patients attain HBsAg loss or have 3 separate occasions of undetectable HBV DNA at least 6 months apart8. Additionally, the European Association for the Study of the Liver recommends discontinuing NAs in non-cirrhotic patients who have maintained HBV DNA suppression for over 3 years5. Despite these recommendations, evidence is still limited regarding optimal patient selection, monitoring strategies following NA cessation, and retreatment criteria.

In our study, HBsAg loss was observed more frequently among patients who discontinued NA therapy, with a cumulative rate of 10.6% over 3 years. Most seroclearance events occurred within the first year post-discontinuation. This finding aligns with several Asian studies that examined TDF or ETV discontinuation in HBeAg-negative CHB, which reported HBsAg clearance rates of about 10%–15% over extended follow-up19,20,24–29. We also noted that the TDF-D group had a higher clearance rate than the ETV-D group (12.8% vs. 7.4%), although this difference was not statistically significant. A retrospective study in Taiwan yielded similar results, reporting HBsAg loss rates of 15.4% for TDF and 10% for ETV after 3 years19. Another retrospective cohort, which included both HBeAg-positive and HBeAg-negative patients, showed significantly higher clearance with TDF (12%) compared to ETV (7.7%) over the same timeframe (p = 0.02)25. By contrast, an observational study in Hong Kong, which followed 184 HBeAg-negative patients on ETV for 48 weeks post-therapy, found no cases of HBsAg seroclearance30.

Several Asian studies have documented substantially lower HBsAg clearance rates—ranging from 2 to 6%—after TDF or ETV discontinuation over 1 to 5 years of follow-up15,17,22,31–34. In one investigation, Jeng et al. noted that ETV withdrawal led to a threefold higher rate of HBsAg clearance than TDF discontinuation (6.1% vs. 1.9%), although 44% of the patients in that study had underlying cirrhosis15.

By contrast, Western populations have shown significantly higher HBsAg clearance rates of 19%–30% following NA discontinuation14,21,35,36. These differences suggest that patient ethnicity and HBV genotype may play a major role in HBsAg loss. In support of this, a multicenter study by Sonneveld et al. reported a fivefold higher rate of HBsAg clearance in non-Asian patients compared with Asian patients (22.4% vs. 4.7%; p < 0.001) at 3 years31. A recent meta-analysis further confirmed that non-Asians were 5 to 6 times more likely to achieve HBsAg clearance than Asians, regardless of end-of-treatment HBsAg levels37.

Off-therapy management in CHB aims to achieve a functional cure, defined as HBsAg loss. Discontinuing NAs often triggers viral reactivation, ranging from a mild HBV DNA rebound to severe ALT flares38. This elevation in HBV DNA and/or HBsAg can stimulate both innate and adaptive immune responses, potentially clearing HBV-infected hepatocytes39. However, the exact mechanisms underlying this immune reactivation and resulting functional cure are not yet fully understood.

A German prospective study demonstrated that NA discontinuation can alter T-cell phenotypes and enhance HBV-specific T-cell responses40. Similarly, Jeng et al. found that HBsAg loss was 7.4 times more likely in patients who did not restart therapy after CR, compared with those who resumed treatment15. Supporting this observation, a recent prospective single-center study in Spain showed that HBV-specific CD8 + T-cell function was threefold more potent in patients who remained off therapy than in those who required retreatment at 12 weeks post-discontinuation35.

In this study, HBsAg seroconversion occurred in 57% (4 of 7) of patients who achieved HBsAg loss, a proportion similar to other reports citing rates of 50%–75% among individuals who clear HBsAg14,27,35. One study documented no instances of HBsAg seroconversion at 18 months post-discontinuation, although this was a rare finding26. To date, no studies have identified factors specifically associated with HBsAg seroconversion following clearance.

We found that the EOT HBsAg level was the only statistically significant predictor of HBsAg clearance (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.18–0.83; p = 0.02). The optimal cutoff of 65 IU/mL yielded an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.96 (p < 0.001). Of note, all patients who achieved HBsAg loss had EOT HBsAg levels below 100 IU/mL, echoing multiple previous studies that underscore EOT HBsAg < 100 IU/mL as a key determinant of post-discontinuation HBsAg loss15,20,22,25,26,28,31,34,36. In contrast, a retrospective cohort of 194 Chinese patients who stopped ETV therapy found only a nonsignificant trend toward lower EOT HBsAg levels as a predictor of HBsAg clearance. Instead, the ALT/qHBsAg ratio at EOT emerged as the significant factor (hazard ratio 1.009, 95% CI 1.003–1.016; p = 0.004)29. A similar analysis in our cohort showed no association between ALT/qHBsAg and HBsAg clearance in either the TDF-D or ETV-D groups.

Two additional parameters—male sex and longer treatment duration—were observed more frequently in patients who achieved HBsAg loss. However, neither reached statistical significance in our multivariable models (Table 2). Previous studies from Taiwan have identified longer therapy duration as a significant predictor of HBsAg clearance20,25. Other reported predictors include older age, non-Asian ethnicity, HBV genotype C, baseline HBV DNA, and EOT HBc-related antigen levels26,28,31,32,34,41.

Although discontinuing NA therapy may enhance HBsAg clearance, it is associated with a high risk of VR. In our study, VR occurred in 83% of patients in the TDF-D group and 56% in the ETV-D group over 3 years. Moreover, VR not only appeared more frequently but also occurred earlier in TDF-D than in ETV-D (p = 0.002). These findings align with earlier studies reporting that TDF discontinuation results in earlier and more frequent VR, often reaching 50% within the first year and up to 70%–80% at 3 years19,25,42,43.

We also noted that patients on TDF who had an EOT HBsAg below 100 IU/mL seemed to exhibit higher VR rates (Figs. 3A and 4B). However, our multivariate analysis showed TDF discontinuation as the sole independent risk factor for VR (hazard ratio 3.17, 95% CI 1.50–7.20; p = 0.006; Table S3). Previous studies have identified younger age, HBV genotype B, TDF cessation, and EOT HBsAg levels as predictors of VR19,20,42–44. In contrast, Seto et al. observed a 91% VR rate within 1 year in 184 HBeAg-negative patients who stopped ETV, yet found no link between VR and EOT HBsAg levels30.

Similar to VR, we observed that CR occurred more frequently and earlier in patients discontinuing TDF compared with ETV. Over 3 years of follow-up, the cumulative CR rate was 49.8% in the TDF-D group versus 26.5% in the ETV-D group (p = 0.02). Although slightly lower than previously reported data, our findings align with earlier evidence that over half of patients in both groups eventually experience CR after 3 or more years, with a higher and earlier rate in TDF-treated individuals19,25,43. Unlike VR, we did not detect a clear association between EOT HBsAg < 100 IU/mL and CR in the TDF cohort (Figs. 3B and 4C). In multivariate analysis, TDF discontinuation remained the only independent predictor of CR (hazard ratio 6.5, 95% CI 2.23–19.88; p = 0.0006; Table S4). Previous studies have also identified multiple factors for heightened CR risk, such as younger age, HBV genotype B, Asian ethnicity, TDF withdrawal, and low EOT HBsAg levels19,20,34,42,43.

The mechanism behind the higher and earlier VR and CR rates following TDF discontinuation compared with ETV remains unclear. One hypothesis involves differing immunomodulatory effects of TDF and ETV. As a nucleotide analog also used against HIV, TDF exerts immunosuppressive properties by reducing cytokines such as interleukin-8, CCL3, and tumor necrosis factor-α45. By contrast, ETV appears to promote an immune-activating effect, reducing regulatory T cells and boosting Th1 cytokines (e.g., interferon-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α, and interleukin-12)46. Consequently, when TDF is stopped, an initially suppressed immune system may rebound robustly, triggering severe and early HBV relapse.

We observed a 35.9% incidence of severe AEs (grades 3 and 4) in the TDF-D group, all occurring within 12 weeks of therapy cessation. By contrast, the ETV-D group had a severe AE rate of 14.8%, manifesting after 12 weeks. Additionally, 6 of 39 patients (15.4%) in the TDF-D group developed severe hepatitis B flares. Among these, 2 patients (5.2%) experienced hepatic decompensation 12 weeks after stopping therapy, and 1 (2.5%) progressed to liver failure and died 8 weeks after decompensation. Most earlier studies reported no cases of hepatic decompensation and death post-NA discontinuation. However, some of these studies had short follow-up durations (18–24 months) or primarily enrolled Caucasian populations, both of which may yield lower observed rates of severe events14,17,19,21,35,47.

Investigations in Asian cohorts suggest that 0.85%–3.3% of patients experience hepatic decompensation after TDF or ETV cessation15,18,20,22,24–26,28,33. Two studies including cirrhotic patients found higher decompensation rates among cirrhotic patients than non-cirrhotics. Jeng et al. reported a 3% decompensation rate in cirrhotic patients (44% of the cohort) but no events in non-cirrhotics15. Similarly, Hirode et al. reported a 4.3% decompensation rate in cirrhotic patients versus 0.8% in non-cirrhotics26. Few studies have compared TDF and ETV cessation directly. Chen et al. noted a 3.3% decompensation rate with TDF and 1.1% with ETV, although this difference was not significant25. By contrast, Huang et al. reported comparable rates of decompensation following TDF or ETV discontinuation33. Most patients with hepatic decompensation improved after restarting NA therapy, and under 1% progressed to liver failure and death15,18,22,25,26,28,33. In our cohort, 2 patients (5.2%) in TDF-D developed decompensation, and 1 (2.5%) died within 12–24 weeks post-therapy. All had low EOT HBsAg (Table 4).

Although this prospective, controlled study features long-term follow-up, it has several limitations. First, it is not a randomized controlled trial. However, after enrolling eligible volunteers, the baseline characteristics of both the TDF-D and ETV-D groups were similar. Second, the single-center design and Thai population limit the generalizability of our findings, primarily to other Asian cohorts. Third, at 20 weeks post-enrollment, a high incidence of severe adverse events prompted amendments to the protocol—namely, more frequent follow-ups and earlier NA retreatment. Ideally, these measures would have been instituted in earlier protocols, and it is unclear whether they might influence HBsAg loss rates. Finally, we did not perform HBV genotyping. Nevertheless, in Thailand, approximately 70% of CHB cases involve genotype C and about 30% genotype B48. Past studies suggest no significant correlation between HBV genotype and HBsAg dynamics35,49,50.

Conclusions

Our single-center study indicates that NA withdrawal in HBeAg-negative CHB patients yields a higher rate of HBsAg loss than continued NA therapy (10.2% vs. 0%). An EOT HBsAg level below 100 IU/mL strongly predicts HBsAg clearance. However, discontinuation frequently causes VR and CR, particularly in patients on TDF, whose earlier and more severe relapses can lead to hepatic decompensation and death. TDF discontinuation emerged as a key predictor for both VR and CR. These results suggest that off-treatment strategies may be more appropriate in patients receiving ETV who have low HBsAg levels. In TDF-treated patients with low HBsAg who choose to stop therapy, intensive monitoring is essential within the first 24 weeks post-discontinuation. NA withdrawal should be considered in specialized centers and in motivated patients who can be monitored more frequently, with immediate retreatment in cases of CR.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective, single-center, controlled study was conducted at Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Thailand. Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with HBeAg-negative CHB who had received TDF or ETV for at least 3 years. They also had undetectable HBV DNA for at least 2 consecutive years prior to enrollment.

Exclusion criteria included evidence of advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis, defined as transient elastography > 10 kPa or METAVIR stage > F3. A prior history of hepatic decompensation was also an exclusion, indicated by total bilirubin > 2 mg/dL, international normalized ratio > 1.7, serum albumin < 3.5 g/dL, or platelet count < 100 000/mm3. Additional exclusion criteria were HBeAg positivity, co-infection with hepatitis C virus or HIV, pregnancy, lactation, an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 50 mL/min/1.73 m2, active malignancy, or use of immunosuppressive agents.

One hundred patients were screened between April 2019 and February 2020. Two were excluded (1 for HBeAg positivity and 1 who withdrew consent), leaving 98 for the final analysis. Eligible patients were allocated into three groups: TDF discontinuation (TDF-D, n = 39), ETV discontinuation (ETV-D, n = 27), and continued NA therapy (n = 32; 15 on TDF, 17 on ETV). Allocation was based on the type of NA used prior to enrollment and the shared decision between the treating physician and the patient.

Follow-up and monitoring

Patients in the TDF-D and ETV-D groups were followed every 3 weeks until week 12, then every 12 weeks up to week 48. After the first year, they were followed every 3–6 months until 3 years post-discontinuation. Patients in the continued group underwent monitoring every 12 weeks during the first year and then every 3–6 months, similar to the discontinuation groups. HBV DNA levels were measured every 12 weeks (Fig. 1). When ALT was elevated, additional evaluations were HBV DNA quantification and quantitative HBsAg (qHBsAg) measurement. Patients with confirmed ALT elevation were followed weekly.

Following 2 severe ALT flares within the first 20 weeks—resulting in 1 death and 1 hospitalization—the retreatment threshold was changed to HBV DNA > 2000 IU/mL plus ALT > 2 × ULN.

Patients continuing NA therapy who showed a rise in HBV DNA > 1 log IU/mL for > 4 weeks were evaluated for compliance and additional HBV DNA testing. If viral resistance was suspected in a fully compliant patient with detectable HBV DNA, a resistance profile was obtained, and a different NA class was prescribed.

All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (Si 113/2019) and registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20190609001; first registered on 08/06/2019). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study assessments

Baseline demographic data included age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, smoking, alcohol use, and treatment adherence. Laboratory evaluations included serum qHBsAg measured by the ARCHITECT HBsAg II assay (Abbott Diagnostics, Sligo, Ireland; detection limit, 0.05 IU/ml). HBV DNA was assessed by the Cobas HBV quantitative nucleic acid test (Roche Diagnostics, Pleasanton, CA, USA; detection limit, 10 IU/ml). Standard liver function tests and anti-HBs antibodies (Elecsys Anti-HBs II assay; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany; detection limit, 10 IU/ml) were also measured at the end of the study. VR was defined as HBV DNA > 2000 IU/mL, while CR was defined as HBV DNA > 2000 IU/mL with ALT > 2 × the ULN.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of HBsAg seroclearance in patients who discontinued TDF or ETV compared with those who continued therapy. Secondary outcomes included the proportions of HBsAg seroconversion, VR, and CR in both groups, as well as predictors of HBsAg seroclearance. Adverse events following discontinuation were also recorded, including hepatic decompensation (total bilirubin > 5 mg/dL and international normalized ratio > 1.5), acute-on-chronic liver failure, and death.

Statistical analyses

Age, sex, body weight, treatment duration, baseline biochemical data, and viral parameters (including ALT, HBV DNA, and EOT HBsAg) were compared among patients. Fisher’s exact probability test or chi-square test was applied for categorical data, and the independent Student t-test was used for continuous data. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on the normality of distribution. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonparametric analysis.

Logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of HBsAg seroclearance, VR, and CR. A forward stepwise approach was employed in the multivariable model, and results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant. Log-rank tests and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to compare HBsAg seroclearance, VR, and CR among the groups. All data analyses were conducted using SAS OnDemand for Academics (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
