Loeffler endocarditis in hypereosinophilic syndrome, the paradox of absent peripheral eosinophilia: a case report
Authors: Astrid B M Heymans, Jessica Manders, Caroline Jaarsma, César Magro-Checa, Mark R Hazebroek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLoeffler endocarditis is a cardiac disease characterized by infiltration of eosinophils in hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES), progressing through acute necrotic, thrombotic, and fibrotic stages. It can ultimately lead to heart failure and restrictive cardiomyopathy. Early recognition, multidisciplinary approach, and accurate treatment are crucial to prevent these complications.Case summaryA 51-year-old man presented with dyspnoea and chest discomfort. Imaging revealed a large left ventricular (LV) thrombus, a reduced LV ejection fraction (LVEF 28%), and a typical ‘double-V pattern’ of endomyocardial fibrosis. Despite characteristic imaging findings of Loeffler endocarditis, the absence of peripheral eosinophilia reduced clinical suspicion. Coronary disease was excluded by CT coronary angiography. Extended bloodwork for infectious, autoimmune, oncologic, and HES-related diseases remained negative. Myeloperoxidase tested positive; however, there were no clinical findings suggestive of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Subsequently, a bone marrow biopsy confirmed eosinophilia, establishing the diagnosis of Loeffler endocarditis. The patient was treated with anticoagulation, heart failure therapy, and prednisone. At 6-month follow-up, LVEF improved slightly to 35% but the typical V-shaped LV thrombus persisted, and heart failure symptoms (NYHA III) remained.DiscussionLoeffler endocarditis is an eosinophil-mediated disease but may present without peripheral eosinophilia, particularly in longstanding cases of HES. This underscores the importance of cardiac imaging, bone marrow evaluation, and multidisciplinary collaboration in suspected cases to ensure timely diagnosis and treatment, thereby preventing disease progression.

Introduction

Loeffler’s endocarditis is a cardiac disease caused by eosinophilic infiltration, progressing through three stages, (i) acute necrotic, (ii) thrombotic, and (iii) fibrotic, potentially leading to restrictive cardiomyopathy.1 This case of Loeffler endocarditis showed typical cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) findings but lacked peripheral eosinophilia. In the absence of an underlying aetiology, a bone marrow biopsy was performed, revealing extensive eosinophilia and confirming the diagnosis of Loeffler endocarditis in the context of a longstanding hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES). The patient received multidisciplinary care with initiation of heart failure therapy, anticoagulation, and prednisone.

Case presentation

After referral by his general practitioner, a 51-year-old man presented to the cardiology outpatient clinic for an index evaluation with a 5-week history of progressive dyspnoea, chest discomfort, and orthopnea. He lost 6 kg over 4 months but denied fever, night sweats, or any systemic symptoms. His medical history included partial commissural fusion of the aortic valve cusps. Physical examination showed normal vital signs, a Grade 2 systolic murmur, bibasal crackles on lung auscultation, and no lower limb oedema. Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with incomplete left bundle branch block. Blood tests revealed an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 66 (N > 90) ml/min/1.73 m2 and elevated troponin T at 16 (N < 12) ng/L, but no further abnormal blood values, with a normal leukocyte (8.0 × 109/L), and eosinophil count (0.30 × 109/L). NT-proBNP, measured by the general practitioner, was 1320 (N < 125) ng/L.

The echocardiogram demonstrated a reduced left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF), mild concentric LV hypertrophy, severe left atrial dilatation, and a possible large apical thrombus (Figure 1, Supplementary material online, Video S1). CMR showed a LVEF of 28%, a LV end-diastolic volume index of 113 ml/m2, and a moderately enlarged right ventricle (right ventricular end-diastolic volume index 74.1 ml/m2) with a severely reduced function of 18.5%. No increase in myocardial T2-weighted signal intensity indicated the absence of oedema/inflammation. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images showed midventricular and apical subendocardial LGE, resulting in the typical ‘double-V sign’: a three-layered appearance of dark myocardium, hyperenhanced fibrotic endocardium, and an overlying dark thrombus at the ventricular apex (Figure 2, Supplementary material online, Video S2). Due to the strong suspicion of Loeffler endocarditis based on these imaging findings, this case was managed through a multidisciplinary approach integrating expertise from cardiology, pulmonology, rheumatology, and haematology to assess HES and its underlying cause. Differential diagnosis included malignancy and infectious and autoimmune diseases. A CT thorax-abdomen revealed no malignancies but multiple enlarged lymph nodes. A biopsy of lymph node Station 7 showed a reactive node with no granuloma or evidence of malignancy. Serological testing was positive for antibodies against myeloperoxidase [12 (N < 3.5) IU/ml]; however, there were no clinical findings of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis or any other autoimmune disease (Table 1). Notably, peripheral eosinophilia was never observed, either before admission or during hospitalisation. However, due to strong suspicion of Loeffler endocarditis, a bone marrow biopsy was performed, revealing a high eosinophil count without signs of myeloproliferative disease. Genetic testing for FIP1L1-PDGFRA mutation was negative. All these tests supported the diagnosis of Loeffler endocarditis in the context of an idiopathic HES.

The patient was initiated on therapeutic dalteparin, later switched to acenocoumarol, and heart failure therapy was slowly introduced during hospitalization due to hypotension and pneumonia. Prednisone (60 mg daily) was started but tapered off after 2 weeks due to side effects and questionable efficacy in the fibrotic stage. At 6-month follow-up, CMR and echocardiography showed slight improvement in LVEF (35%) with persistent thrombus despite up-titration of heart failure medication (metoprolol 25 mg, Entresto 24/26 mg twice daily, eplerenone 50 mg, dapagliflozin 10 mg) and anticoagulation. According to current guidelines, cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) was not indicated given a QRS of 127 ms with a non-left bundle branch block pattern. The patient remained symptomatic with dyspnoea on minimal exertion, NYHA III, with potential consideration for heart transplantation in the future.

Discussion

Loeffler endocarditis, an eosinophilic endomyocardial disease associated with HES, is characterized by persistent hypereosinophilia leading to systemic tissue damage through eosinophil-mediated inflammation and toxic granule release.2 In cases of Loeffler endocarditis, cardiac damage typically evolves through three stages: (i) acute necrotic, (ii) thrombotic, and (iii) fibrotic.3 The initial necrotic stage is often asymptomatic and may be detected incidentally during evaluation for hypereosinophilia. Eosinophil infiltration damages the endo- and myocardium through release of toxic granules, cytokines, or inflammatory cell recruitment.4 Damaged and disrupted endothelium can also activate and adhere platelets, contributing to thrombus formation in one or both ventricles, typically developing at the apex, marking the thrombotic stage.5,6 The fibrotic stage involves endocardial fibrosis, possibly leading to restrictive cardiomyopathy and heart failure symptoms. The different stages may overlap, and the degree of eosinophilia does not directly correlate with cardiac dysfunction severity.3,7

Diagnostic workup includes, among other things, analysis of eosinophilia and the underlying cause of HES, as well as investigation into the endocardial and myocardial damage, requiring a multidisciplinary approach. CMR is pivotal for both diagnosis and follow-up of Loeffler endocarditis, enabling detection of stage-specific features such as inflammatory damage of the first stage and subsequent endomyocardial fibrosis of the third stage on late enhancement imaging and intracardiac thrombus of the second stage.8 The previously described typical double-V pattern can be observed.

Notably, the absence of peripheral eosinophilia, as seen here, is rare and has been reported in only a few cases.9–11 Possible explanations include sequestration of eosinophils, advanced fibrotic disease with subsided eosinophilic activity, prior glucocorticoid exposure, and clonal vs. reactive forms of HES.12 Its absence should not preclude the diagnosis when imaging is typical, as this may delay recognition and allow further disease progression. Due to the high suspicion on CMR, a bone marrow biopsy was performed, where eosinophilia was confirmed. Late-stage HES was diagnosed and treated accordingly.

Treatment should address both HES and resultant heart failure and be initiated in a truly multidisciplinary manner, integrating expertise from multiple specialties.13 High-dose glucocorticoids are the first-line therapy for HES, typically initiated with prednisone 1 mg/kg/day or equivalent, aiming to achieve rapid eosinophil depletion and prevent disease progression.14 However, their efficacy in the absence of peripheral eosinophilia remains uncertain, as illustrated in our case, where treatment response was limited and adverse effects emerged. Once the fibrotic stage is reached, eosinophils in other compartments may not reflect ongoing inflammation or predict response to immunosuppression. Management at this stage should prioritize anticoagulation and optimal heart failure therapy. Other immunosuppressive and targeted biologic therapies, such as mepolizumab, have been employed to control eosinophilic inflammation and prevent organ damage. Nonetheless, their utility appears limited in advanced, fibrotic stages of HES, where eosinophil-driven inflammation has already caused irreversible organ damage, and the risk–benefit ratio may be unfavourable.15 Anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist or low-molecular-weight heparin is recommended in cases of thrombus and/or embolic events, along with medication for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction in cases of cardiac involvement.9

In conclusion, this case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of late-stage Loeffler endocarditis without peripheral eosinophilia. It underscores the value of CMR and the need for clinical awareness when characteristic CMR findings are present. Multidisciplinary management is essential for accurate diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. Ultimately, this case highlights the limited treatment options once fibrosis has developed, reinforcing the importance of early recognition and timely intervention.
