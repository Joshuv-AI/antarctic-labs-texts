Evaluation of risk factors for childhood myopia progression: A systematic review of the literature
Authors: Chun-Wen Chen, Jing-Yan Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Myopia is a global public issue with a dramatically increasing incidence. Myopia is currently characterized by its earlier onset, quick development in preschool (0–5 years old), and continued progression particularly during the coronavirus-19 epidemic phase. It has been established that myopia experienced during childhood earlier resulted in vision impairment. In order to intervene in myopia development and offer a novel tool for earlier detection, the review attempts to identify known environmental and genetic risk factors for juvenile myopia (6–18 years old). Medline, Embase, and Web of Science databases were thoroughly searched for articles on myopia that had been published within the previous 10 years. The searches were carried out separately by two experts. The study's inclusion criteria were met by 28 articles. All studies that examined the link between risk factors and myopia were recruited. Parental myopia, near work, time spent outdoors, and a high level of education are all significant risk factors for juvenile myopia. It is clear that there is a strong environmental connection, especially in high myopia; nevertheless, more research is needed to identify any potential links between myopia and screen use. Myopia's genesis and mechanism are ambiguous and unclear. Further genetic studies could aid in examining genes to comprehend the development of myopia.

A longer axial length, known as axial myopia, causes light to focus in front of the retina. Myopia is a widespread public health issue, and its incidence remains rather high. Participants who are myopic have rapidly expanded to comprise 30% of the global population.[12] According to Holden et al.,[3] there will be 50% more myopic people worldwide in 2050 than there were in 2000 (22%). In 2050, 10% of the population is predicted to have high myopia. More importantly, high myopia would have elusive social and economic costs.[1] As children get older, a number of complications of high myopia-related diseases including retinal detachment, macular degeneration, glaucoma, and other irreversible visual impairment also develop.[4] Therefore, there is a specific need to control axial elongation and axial myopic progression worldwide.

The prevalence of myopia in preschool and school-age children is rising with 6.3% in 2011 and 12.7% in 2020 in Hong Kong.[56] In Taiwan, the prevalence among myopic schoolchildren increased rapidly from 1983 with 5.4% [95% confidence interval (CI), 3.5%–7.2%] through 2017 with 25.4% (95% CI, 21.3%–29.5%).[7] Interestingly, Hae Ri Yum[8] indicated that the rate of myopic progression accelerated after the coronavirus-19 (COVID-19) period compared to the pre-COVID-19 period in children treated with low-concentration atropine. Therefore, it is essential to regulate environmental risk factors, even in children who received effective preventative interventions.[7] In general, epidemic studies had indicated potential factors, like time spent outdoors,[9] reading time,[710] diet structure,[11] paternal myopia, and education levels.[12] One study reported other risk factors, such as light intensity, short sleeping duration, and a reading distance of fewer than 25 cm.[13] However, they have proved that high levels of education and less outdoor time led to myopia development but lack a systematic analysis for bias factors. It is much vital to identify potential factors and evaluate myopia control efficacy, which benefits preventing myopia development and a young onset age of myopia. We performed a systematic review to summarize modifiable risk factors associated with myopia in preschool children (0–5 years old) and adolescents (13–17 years old) to find a potential tool for establishing a more effective myopic prevention plan.

Methods

A literature search was undertaken using keywords (gene, genetic environmental factor, inheritance, high myopia, shortsightedness, and refractive error) in Medline, Embase, and Web of Science within 10 years up to August 2022. Mesh terms such as myopia, near-sightedness, and risk metrics were also included. No language restrictions were placed on the article. Reference lists of included studies were reviewed for additional articles. Inclusion criteria included all retrospective, prospective, cross-sectional cohort studies and randomized clinical trials (RCTs). The study selection and exclusion process can be viewed in Fig. 1. Exclusion criteria were defined as unrelated subject interest, amblyopia, strabismus, and animal studies. Two separate analysts independently performed the searches to maximize capture. Data were extracted by two reviewers independently for authors, year of publication, sex, sample size, subject age, outcomes, and study type [Table 1].

Results

A total of 1193 articles were searched according to the keywords and mesh terms. After removing duplicates, the title and abstracts of 1193 records were screened and a further 657 articles excluded at this stage. The full text of the remaining 321 articles was reviewed, articles that did not meet the study criteria were excluded, and a total of 28 articles published between 2012 and 2022 were included. We identified these studies and presented reliable risk factors of myopia in children to provide potential prevention for early-onset high myopia in the light of genetic studies and environmental factors, respectively. Genetics studies

The pathological mechanism of myopia is still ambiguous. Some novel techniques showed that genetic factors were essential on myopia. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) gradually showed many myopic correlated gene mutation sites and mutation bodies.[32]

Many studies have indicated that parental myopia may accelerate childhood myopia onset. Swierkowska et al.[33] indicated that differential methylation of identified candidate genes of CG dinucleotides might play a role in high-myopia pathogenesis. Alterations in levels of promoter regions of PCDHA10, ADAM20, PAG1, ZFAND6, ETS1, ABHD13, LIG4, SBSPON, SORBS2, SLC25A3P1, TANC1, LMOD3, ATXN1, FARP2, and OR6B3 may affect gene expression and disruption of their function and thus contribute to the high-myopia phenotype. Still, the role of PCDHA10 gene methylation and sequence variants remains inconclusive.

Besides, plenty of biological cellular pathways associated with refractive error were identified, including axon regeneration, extracellular matrix differentiation, and scleral fibroblast proliferation. Bilbao-Malavé et al[33]. found that the methylated molecules COL8A1 and rs13095226 were related to choroid neovascularization and axial elongation in high myopia, respectively. In addition, the methylated molecule rs634990 was highly associated with myopic maculopathy. Other basic information was also associated with myopic macular maculopathy, including the age of onset, sex, smoking, and birth type.[34]

More fundamental human studies should be developed to identify the unclear conclusion. Liao et al.[14] analyzed 1831 myopic children who were divided into four groups, including no paternal myopia group, parents with moderate and high myopia, parents with moderate myopia, and parents with high myopia. They found that parents with moderate myopia, moderate, and high myopia groups presented more significant axial elongation and myopia development. Myopic children had a 13.98 odds ratio (OR) for moderate myopia parental group and a 25.71 OR for high myopia paternal group compared to normal children (P < 0.001). Another multiple-center cohort study investigated the association between parent genetics and ocular metrics in 6–72 months early-onset myopic children. The risk of being myopic for one and two parent myopia was 1.42 and 3.39 times compared with healthy children. Besides, children with paternal history had a longer axial length (AL) and a greater spherical equivalent (SE) in various countries. Interestingly, this increase in SE was not related to CRC.[15] These studies showed that parental myopic children had a higher risk of transforming high myopia.

The efficacy of parental refractive status differs among children. For example, preschool children who had parental myopia were at the risk of moderate and high myopia (OR = 1.23 and 1.19) compared with no parental myopia history. Adolescents were at myopic risk of 2.17 and 4.02 times.[16] Children with parental history had the risk of myopia by 1.76 times (P < 0.001).[19] Therefore, it is much vital to take effective action to control myopia development in terms of a gene.

Siblings’ refractive status

As myopia was related to genetic factors, siblings’ refractive status should be identified. In a survey investigated in Shanxi Province, the prevalence of myopia was 34.7%, surpassing much more than nonmyopes, which was only 8.5% in 1813 participants.[17] The prevalence of moderate and high myopia was higher in children who had myopic siblings (86.1% and 78.7%) than parents (20.5% and 48.0%). The high myopia risk of 6.3% was significantly higher in children who had myopic siblings than parents at 3.8%. We should pay more attention to myopic history children to avoid myopia development and severe complications.[17]

Age

Children with longer onset of myopia and greater initial SE are at a risk of retinal degeneration alterations. In general, myopic children who were younger than 6 years old have a higher risk of high myopia. The refractive status, AL, CRC, and lens thickness will change with aging in the process of emmetropia.

Liu et al.[18] analyzed 830 myopic children aged 40.83 ± 3.43 months. After 2 years, they reported that the risk of longer initial AL and AL/CRC on myopia was 4.91 and 2.98 times compared with healthy subjects (P < 0.001). McCullough et al.[19] found that the younger age of myopia onset was highly associated with progressive myopia. They indicated that the duration of myopia development was the most important predictor of high myopia in later childhood.

Sex

Many studies have compared the prevalence of myopia in adolescents and found that boys were more likely to be myopic.[2021] Bikbov et al.[20] showed that a higher prevalence of myopic pathology blindness was associated with male gender (p < 0.001; OR = 2.32; 95% CI 1.47–3.66). However, one study reported that the incidence of myopia in females was higher than males. Li et al.[22] found that girls had a higher prevalence than boys of hyperopia in grade 1 (P = 0.596) but a higher prevalence than myopic boys in grade 6 (P < 0.001).

Environmental factors

Diet habits

Daily eating habits and nutritional structure may play a vital role in childhood myopia. In Singapore, a birth cohort study[23] recruited 467 children and reported that multiple chemicals like macronutrient and micronutrient intake were not related to myopia. There was no significant difference in SE and AL between the whole grain intake group and normal group. Finally, they concluded that myopia development in 9-year-old children was not associated with dietary habits. Lin et al.[24] performed a population-based retrospective cohort study that included 35,538 patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) and 71,076 patients without DM. Compared with healthy subjects, patients with DM had a significantly higher hazard ratio for myopia. However, the rate of myopia development did not differ significantly. These findings show that DM may be a critical risk factor among patients younger than 60 years. Another study indicated that high salt and sugar intake accelerated the progression of myopia and high myopia. The risk of myopia was higher in high-salt and sugar intakers compared to vegetarians, respectively (OR, 2.10 versus 1.39, 2.40 versus 1.32, P < 0.05).[16] Most retrospective studies had indicated the association between diet and myopia. Berticat et al.[25] identified that high-carbohydrate girls were easier to be myopic. Based on human studies, Cordain et al.[35] believed high-load carbohydrate diets would accelerate myopia development. This possible mechanism included insulin destruction, chronic hyperinsulinemia, and high concentration Insulin―like the Growth Factor-1 (IGF-1) signal pathway.

Serum vitamin D levels

The mechanism of vitamin D-induced myopia is still unclear with lack of RCT supporting data.[36] Yazar et al.[26] analyzed 23.4% of the myopia population and indicated 25 (OH)D₃ level was lower compared with controls. In addition, the increased myopia risk was associated with a lower level of 25 (OH)D₃ (≤50 nmol/L; OR = 2.07, P < 0.001). Meanwhile, the protective role of a high level of 25 (OH)D₃ might explain ocular sun exposure on the retina. Several systematic reviews found no association between the total level and myopia.[3738] Therefore, more RCTs would be needed to investigate the potential role of 25 (OH)D₃ in myopia development.

Sleeping time

Previous studies showed that the relationship between myopia and sleeping duration was almost ambiguous. Several retrospective studies[273940] indicated that myopes slept later and poorer than nonmyopes. Qu et al.[27] showed that myopia was positively associated with a late bedtime on weekends. For instance, the myopic risk in those students who slept after 11:00 pm was 1.97 times than before 10:00 pm (P = 0.003), whereas the risk of bedtime after 10:00 pm was 1.52 times than before 10:00 pm (P = 0.003). Lee et al.[28] analyzed myopic adults for 22 years and found that sleeping time was not related to the change of SE. Children who slept later on weekends showed axial elongation of 0.003 mm (95% CI = -0.000 to 0.003) per year. The SE and axial elongation were 0.006 D (95% CI = 0.001 to 0.011) and 0.003 mm (95% CI = 0.001 to 0.005) each year with 1 hour late for wakening time on weekends. However, earlier weakening time on weekdays was not correlated with myopia development. Conversely, the sleeping time during weekdays was not correlated to AL change. They believed that sleeping time was not related to myopia development, which was distinguished from the other study. Li et al.[29] retrospectively analyzed 10,142 children and showed the risk of myopia significantly decreased in those who slept for more than 9 hours compared with less than 9 hours. The sleeping duration with 10 hours was associated with the decreased risk of myopia compared with 9 to 10 hours (OR: 0.77 versus 0.87). Meanwhile, late wakening time after 7:00 am increased the risk of myopia compared with after 6:30 am (OR = 1.36). The study found that the risk of myopia and high myopia in preschool children who slept for less than 7 hours was 4.16 and 9.79 times compared with more than 9 hours every night.[16] We speculated that less sleeping time may be an independent risk factor of myopia.

Screen time

Electronic devices like mobile phones and computers were frequently used when children were not allowed to go to school because of the epidemic of COVID-19. Children had to spend a lot of time gazing at the screen to take notes for 8 hours every day. IG et al.[12] concluded that there is currently no evidence that time using digital devices is more dangerous than a similar amount of time reading, but more work in this area is required. Meanwhile, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recognized the impacts on myopia development of extreme screen time on school children, possibly combined with marked deprivation of time outdoors, have the potential to be severe. Screen time control is an area that requires more attention. The study indicated that the risk of myopia and high myopia was 2.83 and 5.01 times in those who use electronic screens for more than 3 hours compared with less than 1 hour (P < 0.001).[16] This demonstrated that using electronic devices for too long would accelerate myopic development in preschool children and adolescents. However, we were not sure about the specific mechanism of myopia progression when children devote themselves to homework or mobile phone for a long time.

Outdoor time

Many studies proved that time spent outdoors was an important factor for myopia prevention as people spent more time viewing subjects in the distance, reducing abnormal accommodation to control axial elongation. Interestingly, families imitated natural light outdoors using indoor lamination of a light bulb but lack a standard examination method. The risk of myopia may decrease by 50% after 90 minutes of outdoor activities every day. The myopia risk for less outdoor time was 1.21 times comparing those exceeding 2 hours, but no significant difference was found in high myopia.[16] The two major factors were light and near work activities outdoors.

Rose KA et al.[41] assessed the efficacy of increasing time spent outdoors at school among 6-year-old children in Guangzhou. They compared the addition of 40 minutes of outdoor activity group (952 children) with usual activity (951 children) over 3 years. The prevalence rate of myopia was 30.4% in the intervention group and 39.5% in the control group [difference of -9.1% (95% CI, -14.1%, -4.1%); P < 0.001]. There was also a significant difference in SE for the intervention group (-1.42 D) compared with the control group (-1.59 D) (P = 0.04). However, AL elongation was not significantly different between the intervention group (0.95 mm) and the control group (0.98 mm) (P = 0.07).

Light exposure

Enough ocular light exposure and limited near work time could prevent myopia and high myopia development. Rose KA et al.[41] postulated that brighter light outdoors during daylight hours led to more dopamine release in the retina, which in turn inhibited axial elongation. This hypothesis has been supported by animal experiments demonstrating that bright light inhibits the development of form-deprivation myopia under laboratory conditions and that the protective effect involves D2-dopamine receptors in chickens, monkeys, and tree-shrews.[94243] However, no significant difference was found between outdoor time and near-distance work for myopes and nonmyopes, respectively (2.25 h versus 2.30 h, 4.32 h versus 4.21 h; P > 0.05). The result was almost consistent with one cohort study conducted in Singapore.[20]

Li M et al.[44] assessed light levels, the duration, timing, and frequency of light exposure of 483 Singaporean children aged 9 years (42.2% myopic) with a questionnaire, the FitSight watch, and a 7-day activity diary. The average light levels were 458 ± 228 lux, ≥1000 lux (37 ± 19 min/day), 76% <5000 lux, and 1.7 ± 1.0 light exposure episodes/day. Greater reported time outdoors was associated with lower odds of myopia (OR = 0.82, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.95/hour increase daily; P = 0.009). Light levels, timing, and frequency of light exposures were not associated with myopia, SE, or AL (P > 0.05). They concluded that reported time outdoors was protective against myopia but not light levels or specific light measures.

Peripheral refraction

Currently, the evidence of peripheral refraction in myopic progression has been the focus of much research.[45464748] Many parents disagree to use spectacles to control myopia and thus choose atropine therapy. Sun et al.[45] compared central and peripheral RPR of myopic children (n = 209) who received atropine treatment and those who did not use atropine along the horizontal meridians for both nasal and temporal fields (10° to 30°). No statistically significant differences were found in spherical equivalent (SE) and astigmatism of the three refractive groups in either the nasal or temporal retina. They found significant differences in RPR between the atropine group with moderate myopia and controls in the nasal 30° and temporal 30° fields (t 36 = 2.37, P = 0.02; t 2.84 = 2.84, P = 0.01). The findings identified that using atropine is effective in controlling myopia progression.

Apart from single defocus glasses, Ortho-K lens also managed myopia progression. Jyoti M Damani et al.[46] compared peripheral myopic defocus induced by Ortho-K lens with single vision spectacles and soft-contact lenses (SCLs). They found a significant interaction between relative peripheral refraction (RPR) and the target distance among different refractive modalities (P = 0.02). Single overnight Ortho-K lens led to relative peripheral myopia for both distance (mean RPR ± SE: -0.92 ± 0.21D and -1.04 ± 0.22D) and near viewing (-0.71 ± 0.17D and -0.76 ± 0.20D). Comparisons of RPR indicated statistical significance along both temporal and nasal meridian (±20 and ±25°, P < 0.05). RPR with SCL and spectacles were similar (P > 0.05). The Ortho-K lens designs lead to high relative peripheral myopia for both distance and near viewing and might offer beneficial effects on myopia control.

However, peripheral refraction and its relationship with myopia development was unknown in continual follow-up. Lin-Song Qi et al.[47] prospectively analyzed cycloplegic peripheral refraction at 0°, ±10°, and ±20° along the horizontal visual field of 85 nonmyopic boys for 2 years. They found the baseline RPR did not differ between students with and without myopia (P > 0.05). At the 2-year follow-up, the RPR at ±10° and 20° nasal was significantly more hyperopic in the myopic group than in the nonmyopic group. They concluded that central refraction change is significantly correlated with changes in RPR at 20° nasal, 10° nasal, and 20° temporal, and students who develop myopia have more relative peripheral hyperopia. More baseline central refraction and relative peripheral hyperopia at 10° nasal are protective of myopia onset. Sara Silva-Leite et al.[48] evaluated the peripheral defocus for myopia progression control and impact on visual function in 17 myopic young adults. They showed no significant differences between the monofocal and perifocal lenses measured visual contrast sensitivity (VCS) and light disturbance (LD). The result showed an average myopic defocus of -0.42 ± 0.38 D (P < 0.001) in the nasal retina at 25° but not in the temporal retina. RPR changes may provide a novel myopia control direction.

Discussion

The relevant myopia risk factors for childhood myopia contributed to genetic alterations and environment. The main reason for myopia development was continuing near work and less outdoor time.[1230] The risk factors were as follows: paternal myopia, sibling myopia, longitudinal near work, screen time, less sleeping time, and outdoors time.[1224363941] For instance, girls in rural areas become myopic contributed to education pressure and body development. Conversely, myopic subjects were more male rather than female.[2] The fluctuating trend could not indicate the direct biological association between sex and myopia.

We demonstrated that two vital risk factors for myopia were less time spent outdoors and near work activities. Some objective metrics like light levels and intensity of near work should be considered altogether. Children have to wear a newly specific device[49] like Clouclip to monitor the light intensity and identify myopic shift. The Clouclip device (Glasson Technology Co Ltd, Hangzhou, China) has a built-in infrared distance sensor (measurement range: 15 to 60 cm) and a light intensity sensor (measurement range: 1 to 655336 lux) for detecting the working distance and ambient light intensity in real time. Based on objective data, Wen et al.[49] provided novel evidence to support the association between light level and myopia. They indicated that myopic children were found to be exposed to light intensities more than 3000 lux (0.68 ± 0.50 hours vs 1.02 ± 0.53 hours, P = 0.012) and >5000 lux (0.42 ± 0.35 hour vs 0.63 ± 0.31 hour, P = 0.004) for shorter durations than nonmyopic children each day. Additionally, the myopic children spent more time at a distance of <20 cm (1.89 ± 0.61 hours) than nonmyopic children (1.52 ± 0.77 hours, P = 0.019) each day. The time spent outdoors with higher light intensity (>3000 lux (OR = 0.27, P = 0.009); >5000 lux (OR = 0.11, P = 0.008) and a working distance of <20 cm (in a circumstance of >3000 lux (OR = 1.17, P = 0.038) or in that of >5000 lux (OR = 1.12, P = 0.046)) were the independent protective factors and risk factors, respectively. However, the causality and the dose-effect relationship need to be investigated further. Concerning the progress through schooling, children were eligible to perform increasingly near work, whereas decreasing the time spent outdoors. According to this pattern, it would simplistically be expected that myopic shifts and perhaps the progression of high myopia would increase as children enter higher-year grades.

To the best of our knowledge, this systematic review has evaluated associated risk factors for juvenile myopic patients. The results can be of great significance in future investigations. The novel protective metrics evaluation for preventing axial elongation and myopia development and various aspects of investigation contributed to early-onset myopia are two considerable strengths of this study. Limitations can be listed as follows: First, as it was a systematic review, selection bias might be common. Second, a relatively small number of RCTs associated with myopic risk factors analysis were included. Third, exploring possible reasons for myopic maculopathy and retinal alterations was significant in the future.

In this review, we found many independent risk factors such as parental myopia, time spent outdoors, near work time, and less sleeping duration. Besides, children who have bilateral parents’ myopia and less outdoor time may exposed higher risk of severe myopia, especially in those children who experienced excessive near work and less sleep. By this way, we suggested that spending more time outdoors and reducing near work duration could obviously control myopia progression. In daily life, it is of significance for children to sleep more to prevent myopia. Meanwhile, using proper correction therapy including Ortho-K lens, atropine, and multiple soft lens that induced RPR to lead to peripheral hyperopia.

In conclusion, excessive near work, less outdoor time, and less sleeping time may be three main risk factors for myopia. However, further research was required to identify predictable risk factors to control myopia development.
