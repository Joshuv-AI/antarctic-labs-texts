First reported mortality from XDR Acinetobacter Baumannii Meningitis following posterior lumbar interbody fusion in Iran: a case report
Authors: Mohsen Nabiuni, Fatemeh Zarghami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial meningitis is a rare but serious complication following neurosurgical procedures. Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) has emerged as an opportunistic pathogen, causing serious infections worldwide. The increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant strains presents growing challenge in healthcare settings. The occurrence of multidrug-resistant A. baumannii meningitis after Posterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion (PLIF) surgery is exceptionally uncommon.Case presentationWe report a 59-year-old osteoporotic female who developed A. baumannii meningitis resistant to our available antibiotics following PLIF for L5/S1 spondylolisthesis. Although a minor durotomy occurred during surgery due to sever adhesion in the inflamed tissue and no cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage was observed postoperatively. The patient experienced fever and headache on first day after discharge which was managed with ondansetron, and then gradual loss of consciousness six days after discharge occurred, leading to her readmission. Despite administration of intravenous antibiotics and two courses of intrathecal antibiotic therapy via an External Ventricular Drain (EVD), her condition progressively deteriorated, and she ultimately succumbed to the infection.ConclusionThis case highlights the critical importance of maintaining dura integrity and adhering to strict operating room sterilization protocols. As resistant organisms become increasingly prevalent, healthcare providers must exercise heightened vigilance in the early recognition and management of infections following neurosurgical procedures.Keywords: PLIF, Spondylolisthesis, Post neurosurgical meningitis, Acinetobacter Baumannii, Case report

Background

Bacterial meningitis, typically resulting from intraoperative dural tears with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, is an infrequent but fatal serious complication of spinal surgery [1]. Post-neurosurgical meningitis (PNM) ranges from 0.1% to 1.5%, rising to 22% in patients requiring shunts for CSF drainage or intracranial pressure monitoring devices [2–4]. Common pathogens include Staphylococcus, Klebsiella sp, Acinetobacter sp, Enterococcus, E. coli, and Pseudomonas sp [3, 5–7]. PNM caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) gram-negative bacilli, such as Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), is particularly concerning due to their high mortality rates [3, 8, 9]. A. baumannii has been associated with traumatically injuries [10] or various neurosurgical procedures, such as craniotomies and is considered an opportunistic pathogen found in health care setting. To the best of our knowledge there have been no prior reports of fatal meningitis with extensively drug-resistant (XDR) A. baumannii following posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) surgery in Iran. Here, we present a case of PNM in a 59-year-old woman after PLIF for L5/S1 spondylolisthesis, caused by XDR A. baumannii.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old woman presented with an 8-year history of severe low back pain, neurogenic claudication occurring after walking approximately 10 steps. The condition was progressively lead to worsening lower limb weakness (right leg: 3/5, left leg: 4/5), accompanied by radicular pain radiating to the right lower extremity. Conservative treatment had been unsuccessful. Her medical history included hypothyroidism, osteoarthritis, and hypertension; she had no prior surgeries, did not smoke, and did not consume alcohol. Physical examination revealed limited lumbar flexion and extension and tenderness over the L4, L5, and S1 spinous processes. Diagnostic evaluation included lumbosacral radiographs (flexion and extension views) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) demonstrated L4–L5–S1 spondylolisthesis and multilevel lumbar canal stenosis from L2 to L5. Electromyography with nerve conduction velocity (EMG-NCV) studies confirmed L4–L5 radiculopathy. Bone densitometry confirmed severe osteoporosis with reduced bone mineral density and a low T-score, indicating high fracture risk. Given the severity of symptoms, spinal instability, and underlying osteoporosis, the surgical team recommended spinal fusion at L4–L5–S1 with adjacent vertebral involvement to enhance stability. After discussing the diagnosis and treatment options, the patient opted for surgery due to significant functional impairment and poor quality of life. We performed bone densitometry for the patient during hospital stay and evaluated the patients’ bone mass with DEXA scan for regions of hip-spine and radius. There was no sign of osteopenia or osteoporosis. Hence the patient was a suitable candidate for the PSF (posterior spinal surgery) surgery.

Patient was allergic to penicillin. So, we avoided all penicillin family drugs and cephalosporins. According to the results of consultation with infectious disease specially, patients received 2 g of meropenem, every 8 h in addition to amikacin, 500 mg every 12 h (both IV).

Preoperatively, she received Meropenem 1 g, Clindamycin 600 mg, Dexamethasone 8 mg, and Pantoprazole 40 mg, stat. Laboratory findings showed elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (21 mg/dL) and creatinine (1.5 mg/dL), while other parameters were within normal limits.

Under general anesthesia, a midline incision from L2 to S1 was made. Dissection through fascia, subfascial layers, and paraspinal muscles enabled excision of the L2–S1 spinous processes. A total bilateral laminectomy was performed at L3–L5, with partial laminectomy at L2, foraminectomy for L2–L5 nerve roots, and discectomy at L5/S1. Spinal stabilization involved pedicle screw fixation, bone cement augmentation, partial reduction of L5/S1 spondylolisthesis, and posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) using an 8 mm 3D-printed cage at L5/S1. Bone chip allografts were placed over the transverse processes from L2 to S1 to promote fusion. Because of sever adhesion due to inflamed tissue a minor dural tear with the size of almost 1-mm tear in the posterior area occurred which was promptly repaired with one stitch using 5 − 0 Prolene suture. Strict aseptic protocols were followed, with gloves changed twice intraoperatively. The wound was closed in layers, and a sterile dressing was applied. Postoperative radiographs confirmed proper implant placement and alignment (Fig. 1).

X-ray images following PLIF surgery displays the positioning of pedicle screws and interbody cages, augmented with bone cement from the L2 -S1 levels

On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the patient experienced headache, nausea, and vomiting upon sitting. Symptomatic management with intravenous normal saline (1 L) and ondansetron improved oral intake and caffeine tolerance. Lab results revealed leukocytosis (WBC 13,200/mm³; PMN 87%), hemoglobin (Hg) 10.2 g/dL, BUN 22 mg/dL, creatinine (Cr) 1.2 mg/dL, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) 59 mg/L. On POD2, the patient remained afebrile, though the headache persisted. Acetaminophen was added. Labs showed WBC 13,600/mm³ (PMN 88%), Hg 10.7 g/dL, and BUN 24 mg/dL. Over the following days, she remained hemodynamically stable, with no significant changes in vitals or laboratory findings. On POD5, the patient reported significant pain relief and was discharged in good condition with stable vitals. Labs at discharge included WBC 13,600/mm³ (PMN 85%) and Hg 9.9 g/dL.

On POD6, the patient developed a high-grade fever and transient loss of consciousness. Upon admission to emergency medical services (EMS), she was febrile (40 °C) with altered consciousness, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 13, blood pressure (BP) 175/110 mmHg, pulse rate (PR) 95 bpm, oxygen saturation (SpO₂) 96%, and respiratory rate (RR) 21 bpm. Neurological examination was normal with no meningeal signs (negative neck stiffness, Kernig’s, and Brudzinski’s). On POD6, due to the first appearance of discharge (yellowish clear fluid, similar to serous) from surgery site, a simple discharge culture was sent to laboratory and the results came back negative. The patient underwent broad spectrum of antibiotic therapy and conservative management.

A full diagnostic workup—including brain computed tomography (CT) scan, chest radiography, electrocardiogram (ECG), echocardiography, ultrasonography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, bilateral doppler ultrasound were performed, Laboratory testing, including blood and urine analyses—excluded respiratory infections, endocarditis, intra-abdominal/pelvic abscess, urinary tract infections, and deep vein thrombosis. After fluid resuscitation, her GCS improved to 15. Lab tests showed WBC 16,900/mm³ (PMN 85%), CRP 105.8 mg/L, K 3.2 mEq/L, and venous blood gas (VBG) with pH 7.39, PCO₂ 32 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 19.4 mEq/L. Suspecting postoperative infection, blood and urine cultures were obtained, and empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (Meropenem 2 g and Vancomycin 1 g q8h) were started.

On POD7 and POD8, the patient experienced a headache but remained hemodynamically stable (BP 130/85 mmHg, PR 88 bpm, SpO₂ 97%, RR 16, Temperature (T) 36.6 °C. She was conscious but drowsy, with a normal neurological exam. The surgical site remained clean with no signs of cellulitis. Examination for COVID-19, influenza, and urine cultures were negative. Antibiotics were continued, the patient developed a high-grade fever (39.4 °C), Serious discharge was observed at the surgical site and in response to the evolving clinical picture, with consultation with department of infectious disease, Amikacin (500 mg TID) was added to expand antibiotic coverage.

By POD9, with a T 38.4 °C and WBC 18,570/mm³, PR: 80/minute; BP: 170/100, she became increasingly irritable but there was no sign of nuchal rigidity or stiff neck. Her GCS dropped to 10 and the results of the CT (without injection) showed no signs of brain abscess or any hemorrhage/posterior fossa haematoma. On POD9, due to continuation of discharge which prompted patient to receive would exploration surgery. There was a collection of 60 cc of blood-like fluid with no sing of pus and there was no evidence of CSF leakage, even with Valsalva maneuver. That collection was drained from beneath the skin above the fascia (Fig. 2) and it was sent for pathology study. Beta-transferrin testing was negative which ruled out CSF leakage. Additional culture was sent from the deep wound debridement. The results showed blood cultures identified a multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter strain resistant to multiple antibiotics multiple antibiotics including: Piperacillin/Tazobactam, Gentamicin, Doxycycline, Co-trimoxazole, Ciprofloxacin, Ceftazidime, Cefotaxime, Cefepime, Ampicillin/Sulbactam, and Amikacin. The patient had clinical SIRS (Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome) positive criteria (T: 39.4; tachycardia: PR: 92) which was a significant factor to consider explorative surgery. After the second surgery (wound exploration), vancomycin was added to the antibiotic regiment (1 gram every 12 h, IV), waiting for antibiogram after second would culture. After adding vancomycin, patient became partially better with GCS up to 13 and the clinical SIRS became negative with still active laboratory positive SIRS. However, the patient response to therapy was not optimal.

The surgical field image, taken during the re-opening of the skin and subcutaneous tissue, reveals no evident purulent discharge

As a result, Amikacin was discontinued, and intravenous (IV) Colistin was initiated with a 300 mg loading dose followed by 150 mg maintenance. By POD10, patient was started deterioration with periods of loss of consciousness and the patient remained systemically ill but alert and responsive, able to move extremities on command despite pain. Vital signs were stable and continued labs showed worsening leukocytosis (WBC 20,210/mm³) and markedly elevated CRP (166 mg/L), consistent with ongoing systemic inflammation.

On POD11 until POD13, the patient had stiff neck and positive Brudzinski sign and surgical wound cultures confirmed Acinetobacter baumannii. While and intravenous antibiotics were continued and patients; medical condition deteriorated. She became unresponsiveness and required intubation. We started the treatment based on the order of the consultation and the positive culture for Acinetobacter with administration of IT antibiotics (colistin and Amikacin). Intrathecal antibiotic administration was recommended via catheter or external ventricular drain (EVD). Due to recent spinal surgery and manipulation of the spinal column and infection risk, catheter insertion was considered too risky; thus, EVD placement via a frontal approach was performed. Semi-transparent, high-pressure CSF collected for analysis while Intrathecal Colistin (500,000 units) was administered, followed by sterile closure. On POD14, EVD output was 12 mL/24 hours, prompting a second dose of intrathecal antibiotics. Finally, CSF cultures confirmed extensively drug-resistant A. baumannii. On the morning of POD15, the patient developed bradycardia progressing to ventricular tachycardia. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) restored spontaneous circulation temporarily, but unfortunately a second cardiac arrest occurred later that day and despite resuscitation efforts, the patient did not survive.

Discussion

We report the case of a 59-year-old osteoporotic woman with spondylolisthesis and lumbar stenosis who underwent PLIF surgery and subsequently developed meningitis caused by extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Acinetobacter baumannii, resulting in death. This case underscores the challenges posed by multidrug-resistant infections in neurosurgical and intensive care settings.

Post-neurosurgical meningitis (PNM) can be life-threatening, occurring in 0.1% to 22% of spinal surgeries [2–5, 11]. Common symptoms include fever, headache, and altered consciousness [2, 8]. The classic triad of fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status is present in less than 50% of cases [5, 8], and is even less common after spinal surgery [9] as seen in our patient. Symptoms typically emerge around 7.9 ± 3.1 days after surgery (POD8 in our case). Some patients initially improve, then later present with fever, confusion, seizures, or severe headaches [3]. In our case, we manage headaches conservatively with hydration and acetaminophen due to the absence of other concerning symptoms [12]. Still, the onset of fever and confusion raised concerns for infection.

PNM can be either bacterial or aseptic [6] with early differentiation being challenging [13]. Diagnosis relies on clinical symptoms and positive CSF cultures [12]. Common pathogens include Staphylococcus, Klebsiella, Acinetobacter, Enterococcus, E. coli, and Pseudomonas [3, 5–7]. In 2017, the World Health Organization (WHO) identified carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa as critical threats to human health, due to their resistance profiles and adverse impact on patients clinical outcomes [14]. A. baumannii is particularly concerning in intensive care and neurosurgical units because of its persistence and multidrug resistance characteristics, that contributes to increased mortality and significant postoperative risks [8, 9, 15].

The rise of antibiotic-resistant bacteria complicates routine treatments and makes early diagnosis, prevention, and appropriate management challenging [5, 11, 16, 17]. Effective prevention strategy includes rigorous sterilization, meticulous intraoperative management to any avoid dural tears, and prophylactic antibiotic use [6]. Survival analyses have shown that patients receiving prophylactic antibiotics have significantly lower mortality rates at 42 and 90 days [18].

Treatment often involves broad-spectrum antibiotics with good blood-brain barrier penetration immediately after lumbar puncture [1]; however, in our case, lumbar puncture was not performed due to the risk of iatrogenic infection and recent surgery. The commonly used antibiotic combination for PNM is Meropenem and Vancomycin [15]. In a review of PNM cases, 10 of 21 patients with incidental durotomy responded well to IV antibiotics without mortality [2]. However, despite initiation of Meropenem, Vancomycin, and later Amikacin, our patient’s condition worsened, requiring surgical exploration. Although she was initially alert after the re-operation, she lost consciousness on POD11 and required intubation. This clinical manifestation was similar to a reported case of dural tear during transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion for spinal stenosis involved persistent fever leading to a second surgery; treatment with Vancomycin and Meropenem for Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection led to improvements in mental status and headaches [1].

Following confirmation of XDR A. baumannii meningitis, we treated the patient with IV Meropenem, Vancomycin, and Colistin. Studies support the use of Polymyxin plus Meropenem to reduce 30-day mortality in multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial (GNB) infections [13, 19]. Additionally, Colistin combined with Vancomycin (IV) showed no significant difference in 30-day mortality, clinical response, or nephrotoxicity compared to Colistin (IV) alone [20]. American guidelines recommend intrathecal or intraventricular antibiotics if IV therapy fails within 48–72 h, especially for MDR pathogens [21, 22]. Patients with carbapenem-resistant GNB meningitis treated with combined systemic and IT therapy have shown improved survival compared to control groups [23]. Colistin has re-surged as a key treatment for carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii [24]. In a neurosurgical ICU case series, five multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii MRAB-infected patients were successfully cured with intraventricular/intrathecal Colistin [25]. However, in another series of post-craniocerebellar surgery patients with MRAB, only one survived; two died despite receiving Colistin and prior broad-spectrum antibiotics [26]. In another case, a 36-year-old man with craniofacial trauma and CSF leakage caused by Colistin-sensitive MRAB became afebrile within 48 h and had negative CSF cultures after five days of intrathecal Colistin [27].

For infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter species, colistimethate sodium or polymyxin B (administered both intravenously and intraventricularly) is recommended [22]. IT Colistin and polymyxin B for 21 days following IV antibiotics has been shown to successfully eradicate A. baumannii from blood and CSF [15]. In our case, after the initial treatments failed, IT colistin was administered via an EVD, a method that can help avoid renal toxicity associated with IV use [25]. While polymyxins are considered a last-resort treatment for MRAB, monotherapy risks resistance development. Combining Polymyxin B with other agents, such as Closantel, may offer a promising treatment strategy [28] though further studies are required.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first death reported due to XDR A. baumannii meningitis following PLIF. Antibiotic regimens based solely on blood culture results may be insufficient due to poor CSF penetration, delayed clearance of infected CSF, and the involvement of resistant organisms [1]. Despite surgical re-exploration and combined IV and IT antibiotic therapy, systematic sepsis and neurological deterioration developed worsened the prognosis and resulted in a fatal outcome.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the complexity of proper managing of post-neurosurgical bacterial meningitis, particularly caused by highly resistant pathogens like A. baumannii following spinal surgeries. It underscores the importance of preventing dural tears, considering prolonged drainage, and tailored antibiotic therapy. The patient’s rapid deterioration emphasizes the need for prompt monitoring and early recognition and immediate intervention in postoperative infections. Physician awareness and education are vital for timely diagnosis and management. Continued research into novel agents, emerging therapeutic options and best combination therapy regimen is essential to combat the growing threat of XDR infections in neurosurgical settings.
