Functional nutrition: a non-pharmacological approach to supporting cognitive Health
Authors: Mukhtar Tultabayev, Umyt Zhumanova, Tamara Tultabayeva, Aruzhan Shoman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents evidence that functional nutrition serves as a nonpharmacological method for supporting cognitive health. Specific nutrients and dietary patterns known for their supportive effects on cognitive ability and neuroprotection, such as omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants (vitamins C and E), polyphenols, magnesium, B vitamins, and flavonoid-rich foods, are proposed. This study aimed to assess the efficacy of a functional nutrition protocol enriched with omega-3 fatty acids, magnesium, B vitamins, antioxidants, and polyphenols as a non-pharmacological strategy for maintaining cognitive health and alleviating perceived stress. The methodology of this study is based on an interdisciplinary approach that integrates elements of nutritional science and digital behavior analysis to examine the impact of functional nutrition on cognitive functions under conditions of digital overload. The results demonstrate that such targeted dietary interventions offer a promising and specific non-pharmacological means of enhancing cognitive resilience in the context of prolonged and intensive use of digital technologies.Keywords: functional nutrition, cognitive functions, digital stress, neuroprotection, antioxidantsSubject terms: Health care, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

The ongoing digitization of modern society has fundamentally transformed the ways in which people live, learn, work, and communicate. From early childhood to adulthood, individuals are increasingly exposed to screens—computers, smartphones, tablets, and other digital devices—for extended periods. As digital technologies continue to permeate all aspects of life, concerns about their impact on mental and cognitive health have significantly increased. Numerous studies have identified correlations between excessive screen time and negative outcomes, such as reduced attention span, impaired memory, mental fatigue, emotional instability, and diminished executive functions1–3. These cognitive effects are particularly pronounced in children, adolescents, and knowledge workers, who often operate in highly stimulating digital environments4. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses confirm a clear dose-dependent relationship between intensive use of digital devices and reduced cognitive control, particularly among young individuals5,6. Consequently, digital stress can be regarded as a nonviral plague of the 21st century.

Prolonged digital stimulation appears to exacerbate oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and dysregulation of neurotransmitter systems, thereby creating a biological environment that impairs neuroplasticity and cognitive resilience7,8. In this context, functional nutrition—defined as the targeted use of nutrients and bioactive compounds to optimize physiological functions—has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological strategy for neuroprotection and cognitive enhancement (Gravesteijn et al., 2022;9.

Compelling evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and longitudinal studies demonstrates that specific nutrients exert direct beneficial effects on brain health. Long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, particularly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), support synaptic membrane integrity and reduce neuroinflammation10,11. B vitamins (B6, B9, and B12) regulate homocysteine metabolism and facilitate neurotransmitter synthesis12. Magnesium modulates stress responses and NMDA receptor activity13, whereas antioxidants (vitamins C and E) and polyphenols counteract oxidative damage and improve cerebral blood flow14,15.

The concept of digital overload and its impact on cognitive functions has garnered significant attention in recent years, particularly as reliance on digital devices has become an integral part of daily life. Numerous empirical studies have confirmed that prolonged and excessive interaction with screens—especially when combined with multitasking, fragmented attention, and information overload—negatively affects various aspects of cognitive function. These include reduced sustained attention, working memory, information processing speed, and executive control. Specifically, studies have shown that individuals heavily engaged in media multitasking perform worse on tasks requiring cognitive control and attention filtering1. Moreover, chronic digital stimulation is linked to increased mental fatigue, emotional irritability, and heightened risks of cognitive burnout.

Against this backdrop, research into functional nutrition as a countermeasure has emerged as a promising interdisciplinary approach that integrates neuroscience, nutritional science, and behavioral health. Studies, including RCTs, have demonstrated a positive association between omega-3 fatty acid intake and improvements in attention, memory, and mood regulation. In particular, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) stands out for its neuroprotective effects and role in reducing neuroinflammation associated with cognitive decline16. In addition to omega-3 s, B vitamins—especially B6, B9 (folate), and B12—support cognitive health by regulating homocysteine levels, facilitating neurotransmitter synthesis, and promoting myelin formation. Deficiencies in these vitamins are linked to cognitive impairments, particularly in older adults, as confirmed by meta-analyses by Smith et al.,12 and others. Antioxidants, such as vitamins C and E, play a critical role in combating oxidative stress—a biological process exacerbated by both aging and environmental factors, including prolonged screen exposure. Oxidative stress damages neuronal tissues and impairs cognitive processes. Consequently, a diet rich in antioxidants found in colorful fruits, vegetables, and nuts is closely associated with better cognitive outcomes17.

Polyphenols and flavonoids, bioactive compounds found in berries, dark chocolate, green tea, and certain vegetables, have also gained recognition for their ability to enhance memory and learning by increasing cerebral blood flow and reducing inflammation. The MIND diet (Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay), which combines principles of the Mediterranean and Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diets, has shown remarkable results in slowing cognitive decline, as described in studies by15. This dietary model emphasizes the consumption of green leafy vegetables, whole grains, legumes, and healthy fats while limiting processed foods and saturated fats. Several longitudinal studies have confirmed that adherence to such diets is correlated with slower cognitive aging and a reduced risk of neurodegenerative disorders18.

Furthermore, research on neuroinflammation and its interaction with digital stress indicates that certain nutrients can modulate inflammatory cytokines in the brain. For example, magnesium regulates the stress response system and reduces the production of cortisol, a hormone that is often elevated due to constant digital stimulation and psychological stress. As reported by Barbagallo and Dominguez13, magnesium intake is associated with improved mood stability and cognitive clarity.

Thus, despite the growing body of evidence supporting the neuroprotective role of these nutrients when studied individually, few studies had examined their combined effect within a structured dietary intervention specifically aimed at alleviating cognitive symptoms induced by digital overload.

Therefore, the present 8-week randomized controlled parallel-group trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a functional nutrition protocol enriched with omega-3 fatty acids, magnesium, B vitamins, antioxidants, and polyphenols as a non-pharmacological approach to preserving cognitive health, reducing perceived stress, and improving cognitive performance in young adults who reported high daily screen time and symptoms of digital overload.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was an 8-week parallel-group trial conducted from October 2024 to March 2025. As the intervention consisted solely of dietary recommendations using commonly available foods and supplements not classified as medicinal products, and did not involve invasive procedures, formal ethics committee approval was not required under applicable national regulations (ON PUBLIC HEALTH AND HEALTHCARE SYSTEM. Code of the Republic of Kazakhstan dated July 7, 2020 № 360-VI.). Nevertheless, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, including full information on study purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences.

Participants and recruitment

Eighty-six healthy young adults (aged 18–35 years; mean age ± SD: 25.4 ± 4.2 years; 52% women) were recruited through university mailing lists, social media advertisements, and posters.

Inclusion criteria: High self-reported daily screen time (confirmed by the built-in smartphone tracker during a 7-day screening week); Score ≥ 10 on the Digital Overload Screening Scale (DOSS19); No diagnosed neurological or psychiatric disorders; No chronic diseases requiring regular medication; No regular use of dietary supplements or omega-3 preparations in the previous 3 months; Exclusion criteria: pregnancy, lactation, vegan diet (due to difficulty achieving target DHA levels without supplementation), and known allergy to fish or nuts.

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 with an expected moderate effect size f = 0.25, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, two groups, and two measurements, yielding a minimum of 34 participants per group. To account for up to 10% dropout, 43 participants were enrolled in each group (total N = 86).

Distribution by groups

After baseline assessment, participants were randomly allocated (1:1) to either the intervention group or the wait-list control group using a computer-generated sequence. Allocation concealment was ensured by sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. Outcome assessors (psychologists) and the statistician were blinded to group assignment. Participants and the dietitian were not blinded due to the nature of the dietary intervention.

Intervention

The intervention group followed an 8-week structured functional nutrition protocol designed to achieve the following daily targets: Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA + DHA) ≥ 1.6 g (from fatty fish ≥ 3 servings/week + 1000 mg algal DHA capsules if needed); Magnesium 320–420 mg (nuts, seeds, spinach, whole grains + 200 mg magnesium citrate if needed); B-vitamin complex at or above recommended daily allowances (B1 1.2–1.5 mg, B6 2–5 mg, folate 600–800 µg, B12 6–10 µg); Vitamin C ≥ 500 mg and vitamin E ≥ 100 mg from natural sources; Polyphenols ≥ 500 mg (berries 150–200 g/day, dark chocolate ≥ 70% cocoa 20–30 g/day, green tea 3–4 cups, vegetables).

Participants received: A detailed 7-day menu cycle with recipes and shopping lists; Weekly 30-minute individual consultations with a certified dietitian; Access to the validated MyFitnessPal mobile application for daily food logging; Weekly review of records and personalized feedback from the dietitian; The control group was instructed to maintain their usual dietary habits and lifestyle. After study completion, they were offered the full intervention protocol.

Outcome measures

All assessments were performed at baseline and at week 8 by qualified psychologists.

Primary outcome: Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, version 8.120).

Secondary outcomes: Sustained attention and impulsivity: Conners’ Continuous Performance Test 3 (CPT-3; Conners, 2014) – primary variable: detectability (d′); Working memory: Digit Span forward and backward (WAIS-IV; Wechsler21; Everyday cognitive failures: Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ22); Perceived stress: Perceived Stress Scale-10 (PSS-1023; Dietary intake and adherence: MyFitnessPal records + revised Dietary Quality Index (DQI-R).

Additional covariates

During the 8-week period, participants in both groups maintained weekly diaries of physical activity (International Physical Activity Questionnaire – short form, IPAQ-SF) and sleep quality (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, PSQI). Weekly data on physical activity (IPAQ-SF) and sleep quality (PSQI) were recorded by participants in their diaries and analyzed to confirm the absence of significant changes. Mean MET-min/week values from the IPAQ and global PSQI scores showed no significant changes in either group (p > 0.35 for all within- and between-group comparisons), and there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups at any time point (all p > 0.40). Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0. Normality was confirmed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group differences in change scores (week 8 – baseline) were analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with baseline values and average screen time as covariates. Within-group changes were assessed with paired-sample t-tests. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated between increases in nutrient intake and cognitive improvements. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Intention-to-treat analysis was performed using last-observation-carried-forward for the two participants who dropped out.

Within-group analysis confirmed significant improvements in the intervention group (paired t-tests, all p < 0.001) and no significant changes in the control group (all p > 0.20).

It is important to note that adjustment for average daily screen time, as well as for levels of physical activity and sleep quality (as covariates in ANCOVA), did not alter the significance of the results: all between-group differences remained highly significant (p < 0.001). This indicates that the improvements in cognitive functions and reduction in perceived stress were attributable specifically to the functional nutrition protocol, rather than to changes in physical activity or sleep.

Correlations with nutrient intake

Increased intake of EPA + DHA (r = 0.69), magnesium (r = 0.61), and polyphenols (r = 0.53) showed moderate-to-strong positive correlations with MoCA score improvement (all p < 0.01). Regression analysis revealed that EPA + DHA intake was the strongest predictor of working-memory improvement (β = 0.52, p < 0.001).

Safety and tolerability

No adverse events were recorded. The dietary protocol was well tolerated by all participants.

Physical activity and sleep parameters

As shown in Table 3, levels of physical activity (IPAQ MET-min/week) and sleep quality (PSQI global score) remained stable in both groups throughout the entire study. There were no significant within-group changes (all p > 0.65) and no significant between-group differences (all p > 0.45 in ANCOVA). This confirms that the observed improvements in cognitive functions and reduction in stress were not explained by changes in these confounders.

Discussion

The present 8-week trial demonstrates that a structured functional-nutrition protocol enriched with omega-3 fatty acids, magnesium, B vitamins, antioxidants, and polyphenols can substantially improve cognitive performance and reduce perceived stress in young adults experiencing high levels of digital overload. Compared with the wait-list control group, participants receiving the dietary protocol exhibited moderate-to-large improvements in global cognitive function (MoCA), sustained attention (CPT-3), working memory (Digit Span), everyday cognitive failures (CFQ), and perceived stress (PSS-10), with Cohen’s d effect sizes ranging from 0.68 to 0.82. These findings provide preliminary evidence that targeted dietary strategies may serve as an effective non-pharmacological tool for supporting cognitive resilience in the context of intensive digital-device use.

The magnitude of the observed cognitive improvements warrants attention. A mean increase of 2.4 points increase on the MoCA within only eight weeks is clinically meaningful, particularly in a non-clinical sample of young adults who typically perform near ceiling on this instrument. Similarly, the 28% reduction in self-reported cognitive failures and the substantial decrease in perceived stress suggest that functional nutrition may influence not only affect objective neuropsychological performance but also subjective daily-life functioning and emotional well-being.

These results align with prior evidence linking individual neuroprotective nutrients to cognitive health. Long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, particularly DHA, are integral components of neuronal membranes and have repeatedly been associated with enhanced attention and memory16. Magnesium plays a critical role in NMDA-receptor regulation and stress-response modulation13, whereas polyphenols improve cerebral blood flow and exert anti-inflammatory effects14. The moderate-to-strong correlations observed in the current study between increased intake of these nutrients and cognitive improvements support the hypothesis of additive or synergistic effects when delivered within a structured dietary regimen.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the intervention duration was relatively short (8 weeks); longer-term studies are needed to determine whether benefits are sustained after active support ends and to assess potential cumulative effects over months or years. Second, the study was conducted in a relatively homogeneous sample of educated young adults in an urban setting, which may limit generalizability to older individuals, those with clinical cognitive impairment, or populations with diverse cultural and dietary backgrounds. Third, although adherence was rigorously monitored using validated food-logging software and weekly dietitian review, self-reported intake remains susceptible to recall bias. Future studies would benefit from objective biomarkers such as plasma omega-3 index, erythrocyte magnesium, and serum polyphenol metabolites. Finally, the absence of an active control condition (e.g., general healthy-eating advice) precludes definitive conclusions regarding the specificity of the observed effects.

Despite these limitations, the high retention rate (98%), blinded outcome assessment, detailed documentation of nutrient targets, and inclusion of both objective and subjective measures strengthen confidence in the findings. Particularly important is that physical activity and sleep quality, measured weekly, remained stable and comparable between the groups (Table 1; all p > 0.45), which rules them out as alternative explanations for the intervention effects. Thus, the differences in outcomes are highly likely attributable specifically to the enriched functional nutrition protocol (omega-3 fatty acids, magnesium, B vitamins, antioxidants, and polyphenols).

The intervention was pragmatic, low-cost, and well accepted by the participants, indicating strong potential for scalability and real-world implementation.

In conclusion, this study provides promising evidence that targeted functional nutrition can mitigate the cognitive and psychological consequences of digital overload in young adults. Although further research in larger and more diverse populations with extended follow-up is required, the current results support the integration of evidence-based dietary strategies into broader public-health and workplace-well-being programs aimed at preserving cognitive health in the digital era.

Conclusions

The findings of this study highlight the profound and multifaceted impact that functional nutrition can exert on cognitive function, particularly in the increasingly prevalent context of digital overload. As society grows ever more reliant on digital technologies for communication, education, and work, the cognitive strain arising from constant screen exposure, multitasking, and information overload has emerged as a significant public-health concern. This trial demonstrates that targeted dietary strategies can serve as both a protective and restorative force, mitigating the adverse effects of digital saturation while simultaneously enhancing mental performance.

The results confirm that incorporating neuroprotective nutrients — including omega-3 fatty acids, B vitamins, antioxidants, magnesium, and polyphenols — produces statistically and clinically significant improvements in attention, memory, executive functions, and overall cognitive clarity. These benefits are evident not only in objective neuropsychological assessments but are also reflected in participants’ subjective experiences of reduced mental fatigue, improved emotional regulation, and greater stress resilience. The evidence supports the conclusion that functional nutrition should be recognized as a valid and effective approach to supporting cognitive health, especially in populations experiencing prolonged digital engagement.

Beyond direct cognitive benefits, the study uncovers a network of indirect pathways through which nutrition influences broader human health. These include enhanced stress regulation, improved sleep quality, greater emotional stability, and potentially favorable shifts in gut microbiota composition — all of which interact synergistically to sustain cognitive vitality. This multilayered mechanism of action underscores that functional nutrition is not a narrow or isolated intervention but a holistic component of an integrated cognitive-health model.

The observed improvements in global cognitive function, after accounting for the stable levels of physical activity and sleep (Table 1), suggest that targeted dietary strategies represent a promising and specific non-pharmacological approach to supporting cognitive resilience under conditions of intensive digital device use. The data confirm that these improvements persist after statistical adjustment for physical activity and sleep, which remained stable and equivalent in both groups, highlighting the specific contribution of functional nutrition to supporting cognitive resilience.

Although longer-term studies in more diverse populations are needed to confirm the sustainability and generalizability of these findings.
