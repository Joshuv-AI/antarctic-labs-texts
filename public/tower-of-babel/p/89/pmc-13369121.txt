CerS6 orchestrates mitochondrial–immune crosstalk via BNIP3-dependent mitophagy suppression and mtDNA–STING/NLRP3 activation in acute lung injury
Authors: Guoqing Luo, Dinghui Li, Zhiwei Wang, Guorui Li, Yifan Zuo, Wanli Jiang, Yi Liu, Boyang Li, Xiaoping Xie, Ning Li, Bohao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lung injury (ALI) is characterized by impaired alveolar epithelial barrier function and mitochondrial dysfunction in type II alveolar epithelial cells (AT2 cells). Ceramide synthase 6 (CerS6), a key enzyme in sphingolipid metabolism responsible for generating C16:0 ceramide, has not previously been implicated in ALI. This study reveals that CerS6 expression is significantly upregulated in AT2 cells during lipopolysaccharide (LPS)- and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced ALI. Specific knockout of CerS6 in AT2 cells attenuates apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and barrier disruption in an ALI mouse model while preserving mitochondrial function. Mechanistically, CerS6 directly interacts with the mitophagy receptor BNIP3, disrupting its binding to LC3 and thereby inhibiting mitophagy. The impaired mitochondrial clearance mechanism promotes cytoplasmic release of mtDNA and activates the STING/NLRP3 signaling cascade. RNA-Seq further confirmed that CerS6 knockdown suppressed the STING/NLRP3 pathway and upregulated BNIP3. Notably, CerS6 metabolite C16:0 ceramide also participated in these effects, reinforcing the pivotal role of sphingolipid metabolism in linking mitochondrial stress to innate immune activation. In summary, this study establishes CerS6 as a core driver of mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance in acute lung injury, and indicates that both CerS6 and C16:0 ceramide may serve as potential therapeutic targets.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06227-9.Keyword: CerS6, C16:0 ceramide, Acute lung injury

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) and its severe manifestation, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), remain major clinical challenges. A 2016 epidemiological study reported that about 10% of critically ill patients develop ALI or ARDS, with mortality rates reaching 30–50% [1]. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, the incidence of ALI/ARDS has risen further [2]. ALI/ARDS can result from diverse etiologies—including infection, aspiration, surgery, and systemic disorders—and is typically marked by refractory hypoxemia, persistent dyspnea, and diffuse pulmonary infiltrates [3]. Current management mainly relies on supportive care to sustain vital functions, underscoring the urgent demand for more targeted therapeutic strategies.

Type II alveolar epithelial cells (AT2 cells) are pivotal for preserving alveolar integrity, repairing tissue injury, and modulating immune responses. AT2 cells are essential for maintaining alveolar homeostasis by producing pulmonary surfactant to reduce surface tension, facilitating alveolar fluid clearance, serving as progenitors for epithelial repair after injury, and participating in local immune regulation through cytokine secretion. During acute lung injury, dysfunction or loss of AT2 cells compromises these functions, thereby promoting alveolar collapse, edema formation, impaired regeneration, and amplified inflammatory responses [4, 5].

Mitochondria act as key hubs regulating cellular energy metabolism, redox homeostasis, and cell fate decisions [6]. Beyond their canonical role in ATP synthesis, they orchestrate calcium dynamics, produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), and participate in programmed cell death [7, 8]. In AT2 cells, mitochondrial function is indispensable: surfactant production and secretion are highly energy-dependent processes, and AT2 cells also serve as progenitors for alveolar type I (AT1) cells during lung regeneration. Consequently, maintaining mitochondrial integrity within AT2 cells is crucial for preserving alveolar barrier stability and facilitating post-injury repair [9]. Accumulating evidence suggests that mitochondrial derangements in AT2 cells—such as defective mitophagy, excessive ROS buildup, and leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—provoke epithelial apoptosis, inflammation, and disruption of the alveolar barrier, contributing to ALI and chronic pulmonary disorders [9, 10]. Mitophagy acts as a protective mechanism that eliminates dysfunctional mitochondria, restrains ROS and mtDNA release, and thereby supports AT2 cell survival and recovery after injury.

Sphingolipid metabolism is disturbed during ALI, leading to the buildup of ceramides—bioactive lipid mediators that induce oxidative stress and apoptosis [11–13]. Importantly, ceramides comprise multiple molecular species with distinct fatty acyl-chain lengths, and accumulating evidence suggests that their biological effects are chain-length dependent in the lung. In healthy and diseased lung tissues, C16- and C24-ceramides represent two of the most abundant ceramide species, largely reflecting the activity of CerS5 and CerS6 (preferentially generating C16 species) and CerS2 (preferentially generating very-long-chain species such as C22–C24) [14, 15]. Functionally, long-chain ceramides (e.g., C16:0) are frequently associated with cellular stress phenotypes, including mitochondrial dysfunction and pro-apoptotic signaling, whereas very-long-chain ceramides (e.g., C24) have been linked to membrane structural homeostasis and may exert context-dependent protective or anti-apoptotic effects in lung cells [16]. Consistent with this concept, in COVID-19–related lung injury, an increased C16:0/C24 ceramide balance has been associated with pulmonary microvascular and endothelial barrier injury, and exogenous enrichment of C16:0 ceramide can recapitulate barrier-disruptive effects in human lung endothelial models [16]. Together, these findings support the notion that ALI may involve not only a global rise in ceramide abundance but also a pathogenic shift in ceramide chain-length distribution, which could influence epithelial/vascular integrity, mitochondrial stress responses, and inflammatory amplification.

Ceramide synthase 6 (CerS6) catalyzes the synthesis of C16:0 ceramide and is therefore a central enzyme in this metabolic pathway. Previous studies have demonstrated that CerS6-derived sphingolipids influence mitochondrial fission and contribute to obesity-associated insulin resistance [17], while CerS6 activation has been linked to high-fat diet–induced mitochondrial stress [18]. Ceramides have been widely implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction, pro-apoptotic signaling, and amplification of inflammatory responses [19, 20]. Among the members of the ceramide synthase family, CerS6 preferentially generates C16:0 ceramide, a species frequently linked to cellular stress signaling and mitochondrial perturbation [21]. Therefore, dysregulation of CerS6 provides a mechanistically plausible route through which increased C16:0 ceramide may contribute to mitochondrial and inflammatory injury. Nevertheless, whether CerS6 directly drives mitochondrial impairment in AT2 cells and thereby amplifies the pathogenesis of ALI remains to be clarified.

Our study reveals that during ALI, CerS6 expression and its product C16:0 ceramide levels increase in AT2 cells. Conditional deletion of CerS6 in Sftpc-lineage epithelial cells attenuates inflammation, oxidative stress, and pro-apoptotic signaling in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI, while improving barrier function-related markers. Furthermore, modulating CerS6 in primary AT2 cells alters cytoplasmic mitochondrial DNA abundance and activation markers of the cGAS-STING and NLRP3 pathways. These data support a model in which CerS6 may exacerbate mitochondrial stress and innate immune activation, at least in part, by interfering with BNIP3-mediated mitochondrial autophagy.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval No. WDRM-20250004) and were performed in compliance with the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines. Male C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice were obtained from the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Animals were maintained in a temperature- and humidity-controlled specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to sterilized chow and autoclaved water. Bedding, cages, and water bottles were sterilized before use. To generate AT2 cell–specific CerS6 conditional knockout mice, CerS6-floxed mice (C57BL/6JGpt-Cers6em1Cflox/Gpt, stock #T015208, GemPharmatech, Nanjing, China) were crossed with Sftpc-IRES-iCre transgenic mice (C57BL/6JGpt-Sftpcem1Cin(IRES-iCre)/Gpt, stock #T004715, GemPharmatech, Nanjing, China). In the CerS6flox allele, loxP sites flank exon 2 of the Cers6-206 transcript (106-bp coding sequence); Cre-mediated excision of exon 2 in Sftpc-expressing AT2 cells generates a recombined Δexon 2 allele predicted to disrupt CerS6 function (loss-of-function). The resulting Sftpc-IRES-iCre+;CerS6flox/flox offspring were used as the conditional knockout (cKO) group, whereas CerS6flox/flox littermates lacking Cre expression served as controls. Genotypes were confirmed by PCR using specific primers (Table S1). Expected amplicon sizes were 361 bp for the CerS6flox allele, 260 bp for the wild-type allele, and both bands for heterozygotes; the iCre transgene yielded a 413 bp fragment only in positive animals.

For the LPS-induced acute lung injury model, phenol-extracted lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Escherichia coli O111:B4) was freshly dissolved in sterile saline and administered via intraperitoneal injection. In mechanism and histopathology analyses, mice received a single 10 mg/kg LPS dose. Unless otherwise specified, samples were collected 12 h post-challenge. For time-course experiments, mice were sampled at 0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h post-LPS administration (10 mg/kg). The high-dose LPS regimen (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) was used exclusively for 7-day survival analysis and not for molecular or histological endpoint detection. An alternative acute lung injury model was established via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP). The procedure was as follows: Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3–4% for induction and 1.5-2% for maintenance in oxygen). A 2-cm midline abdominal incision was performed under sterile conditions to expose the cecum. The distal third of the cecum was ligated below the ileocecal valve. A single puncture was made using a sterile 22-gauge needle, followed by gentle expulsion of a small amount of feces. The cecum was repositioned into the abdominal cavity. The peritoneum and skin were closed with interrupted sutures. Postoperative care included warm sterile saline (1 mL subcutaneous injection) for recovery. Animals were monitored continuously for 12 h. Following LPS or CLP stimulation, lung tissue was immediately harvested: samples for biochemical analysis were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C; samples for histological evaluation were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Survival analysis

To evaluate the effect of CerS6 deficiency on survival outcomes, mice in the CerS6-C and CerS6-CKO groups (n = 10 per group) were monitored under standard conditions with unrestricted access to food and water. After administration of LPS (40 mg/kg), daily mortality was recorded for seven consecutive days, and survival rates were calculated accordingly to determine the 7-day survival percentage for each group.

Collection and analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

After LPS challenge, mice were euthanized, and the thoracic cavity was opened to expose the trachea and lungs. A sterile catheter was inserted into the distal segment of the right main bronchus, and both the trachea and catheter were secured using surgical sutures. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed by gently instilling 1 mL of sterile saline into the trachea, followed by three sequential lavages. The collected bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent biochemical assays. Total protein concentration in BALF was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (#ST2222, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). For cytological evaluation, the cell pellet was resuspended in saline, spread onto glass slides, and stained with Giemsa solution (#C0133, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The total number of cells, macrophages, and neutrophils were quantified manually under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Evans blue staining

Pulmonary epithelial barrier permeability was evaluated using Evans blue (EB) dye extravasation. Briefly, after LPS stimulation, mice received an intravenous injection of 5% Evans blue solution (1 mL/kg) via the tail vein. Five minutes after injection, the abdominal aorta was incised for exsanguination, and a catheter was inserted into the pulmonary artery through the right ventricle. The pulmonary circulation was perfused with 10 mL of ice-cold saline to remove intravascular dye, after which the lungs were excised and weighed. Lung tissues were homogenized in 1 mL of 50% trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was diluted 1:3 with ethanol and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The absorbance of the final supernatant was measured at 630 nm using a microplate reader, and vascular permeability was expressed as the ratio of Evans Blue absorbance in lung tissue to that in serum.

Construction of adenoviral overexpression vectors

The adenoviral vector carrying the target overexpression construct was generated using standard molecular cloning techniques. Briefly, the backbone plasmid was digested with suitable restriction endonucleases, and linearized fragments were purified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The target gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using specific primers, and the correctly sized fragment was excised and purified. The purified insert and vector were ligated using homologous recombination or T4 DNA ligase, and the ligation products were transformed into competent E. coli cells. After incubation for 12–16 h, individual colonies were selected, verified by colony PCR, and confirmed through sequencing. Plasmids with validated sequences were isolated using an endotoxin-free purification kit and transfected into 293A cells with an appropriate transfection reagent to produce recombinant adenoviruses. High-titer viral stocks were subsequently obtained through serial amplification and purified by ultracentrifugation. The purified adenoviruses were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for later use.

Isolation and treatment of primary AT2 cells

Primary AT2 cells were isolated and cultured following previously reported procedures [22]. Briefly, mice were disinfected with 75% ethanol prior to dissection, and lung tissues were excised and subjected to enzymatic digestion with trypsin and collagenase. Red blood cells were removed using an erythrocyte lysis buffer, and the resulting suspension was filtered sequentially to enrich AT2 cells based on cell size. A 74-μm mesh was used to remove coarse debris and larger cell types, such as AT1 cells and macrophages, followed by 38-μm filtration to eliminate remaining contaminants. The filtrate was then passed through a 19-μm membrane and further purified by immunomagnetic adsorption using IgG-coated culture plates. The purified AT2 cells were seeded in Ham’s F-12K medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 72 h. Cell morphology and viability were routinely monitored, and the purity of AT2 cells was verified by flow cytometric detection of surfactant protein C (SP-C). AT2 cells maintained for 72 h exhibited suitable growth and purity for subsequent experiments, whereas prolonged culture beyond 144 h led to spontaneous differentiation and reduced experimental reliability (Figure S1A–B). For gene manipulation, AT2 cells were transduced for 12 h with an adenovirus expressing CerS6 (MOI = 100; Shanghai Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) or with a control empty vector (Ad-NC). For gene silencing, siRNAs targeting CerS6 or BNIP3 (50 nmol/L) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 for 4 h, followed by an additional 24 h of culture before analysis. The corresponding siRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

C16:0 ceramide (d18:1/16:0) treatment

C16:0 ceramide (d18:1/16:0; Cayman Chemical, Cat. #10,681) was dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution. After brief vortex mixing, the stock solution underwent gentle sonication, aliquoting, and storage at − 20 °C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. For cellular delivery, working solutions were prepared by stepwise dilution of the DMSO stock solution into pre-warmed medium (containing low-concentration, fatty acid-free BSA, 0.2% w/v) to facilitate dispersion of this highly hydrophobic lipid and carrier-assisted delivery. To minimize local precipitation/agglomeration, first dilute the stock solution into a minimal volume of BSA-containing medium, then adjust to final volume by gentle mixing. The final DMSO concentration was maintained at ≤ 0.1% (v/v) under all conditions.

Primary AT2 cells were treated with 5 μM C16:0 ceramide for 6 h. The solvent control contained matching concentrations of DMSO (≤ 0.1%) and fatty acid-free BSA (0.2% w/v). To assess cytotoxicity, cells treated with solvent or varying concentrations of C16:0 ceramide for 24 h were analyzed for viability using the CCK-8 assay. Concentrations exhibiting ≥ 90% viability relative to the solvent control were deemed non-cytotoxic and used in subsequent experiments.

To minimize interference from extracellular ceramide non-specific membrane binding in intracellular lipidomics analysis, treated cells were placed on ice and washed three times with ice-cold PBS. They were then briefly washed twice with ice-cold PBS containing 0.1% (w/v) fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA), followed by a final PBS wash to remove residual albumin. Cells were immediately harvested and subjected to UPLC–MS/MS quantification of intracellular ceramide species as described in section "Detection of Cellular Ceramides".

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-μm sections. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate histopathological injury. The severity of lung injury, including neutrophil infiltration and pulmonary edema, was independently scored by two blinded pathologists. For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using sodium citrate buffer under microwave heating. Samples were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against CerS6 (Santa, 1:100) and ZO-1 (ProteinTech, 1:100) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBST, sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies, followed by tyramide signal amplification (TSA, Servicebio, 1:500) at room temperature for 10 min. The second primary antibody, SFTPC (Abclonal, 1:100), was then applied, and the TSA amplification procedure was repeated. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium, and fluorescence images were captured using a laser confocal microscope.

TUNEL staining

Paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by treatment with proteinase K at 37 °C for 30 min. After rinsing three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), 50 μL of TUNEL reaction mixture (Catalog. No. 11,684,817,910, Roche, Shanghai, China) was added to each section and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 2 h. The slides were then washed three times with PBS on a decolorization shaker, counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature, and again washed three times with PBS. After gentle drying, sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium, and apoptotic nuclei were visualized under an inverted fluorescence microscope (DAPI channel: excitation 330–380 nm, emission 420 nm; FITC channel: excitation 465–495 nm, emission 515–555 nm).

For TUNEL quantification, lung sections were imaged under identical microscope settings (same exposure, gain, and threshold) across all groups. In each section, 5–10 randomly selected non-overlapping fields (40 ×) were analyzed using ImageJ by an investigator blinded to group assignment. TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells were identified by co-localization of TUNEL signal with DAPI-stained nuclei, and apoptosis was expressed as TUNEL-positive nuclei/total DAPI-positive nuclei (%). For each mouse, field-level values were averaged to generate one biological replicate for statistical analysis.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) staining

For tissue ROS detection, paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, and the tissue area was circled with a hydrophobic barrier pen to prevent reagent overflow. The sections were incubated with PBS-diluted ROS working solution (#S0063, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. After incubation, slides were washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4) on a decolorization shaker under light-protected conditions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature, followed by another series of PBS washes. Sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium, and fluorescence images were captured under a fluorescence microscope.

For measurement of intracellular ROS levels, primary AT2 cells were treated with 5 μM CM-H₂DCFDA probe (#S0035, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following 6 h of LPS stimulation. Cells were incubated with the probe at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed with PBS to remove residual dye, and fluorescence signals were visualized and recorded using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Mitophagy and mitochondrial morphological assessment

Mitophagy and mitochondrial morphology in AT2 cells were evaluated using MitoTracker Green (#C1048, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and LysoTracker Red (#C1046, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) fluorescent probes. Following the indicated treatments, both dyes were diluted in pre-warmed culture medium to a final concentration of 1 μM and added to the cells for co-staining. For mitochondrial morphology analysis, cells were stained with MitoTracker Green alone under the same conditions. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, the staining solution was replaced with fresh medium, and fluorescence images were captured using a laser confocal microscope.

Measurement of superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

Total superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured in lung tissue homogenates using a commercially available assay kit (#A001-3–2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) based on the water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST-1) method. Briefly, freshly collected lung tissues were homogenized on ice, and the resulting lung homogenates were used for SOD activity determination according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically, and SOD activity was calculated based on the kit protocol.

Isolation of mitochondria

Mitochondria were isolated from mouse lung tissues and cultured AT2 cells using a commercial mitochondrial extraction kit (#SM0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. For lung tissues, freshly harvested lungs were rinsed in ice-cold PBS, minced on ice, and homogenized in isolation buffer using a Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove nuclei and debris. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet mitochondria. The mitochondrial pellet was washed once with washing buffer and centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain mitochondria-enriched fractions. For primary AT2 cells, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, harvested by gentle scraping, resuspended in lysis buffer on ice for 15 min, and homogenized (~ 100 strokes) with a glass homogenizer. The lysate was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet mitochondria. The mitochondrial fraction was lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors for use in Western blot analysis. COX IV was used as the loading control for mitochondrial fractions, whereas β-actin was used as the loading control for total cell lysates. The enriched mitochondrial fraction was used for immunoblotting to detect mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins (e.g., p-DRP1/DRP1 and FIS1).

Cytosolic mtDNA isolation and quantification

Cytosolic fractions were prepared to quantify mtDNA released into the cytoplasm. Briefly, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in an isotonic cytosolic extraction buffer. The plasma membrane was selectively permeabilized on ice using digitonin (40 μg/mL, 10 min), followed by gentle mixing. Lysates were centrifuged at 700 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet nuclei and unbroken cells. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove intact mitochondria, and the resulting mitochondria- and nuclei-depleted supernatant was collected as the cytosolic fraction. All steps were performed on ice or at 4 °C with protease inhibitors.

DNA from the cytosolic fraction was purified using the Ezup Column Animal Genomic DNA Purification Kit (#B518251, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytosolic mtDNA abundance was quantified by qPCR using primers targeting the mitochondrial D-loop region and NADH dehydrogenase subunit 1 (ND1). GAPDH was quantified as a nuclear DNA marker and used for normalization where indicated. All primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

To exclude mitochondrial and nuclear contamination, the purity of subcellular fractions was validated by immunoblotting (Figure S6D): COX IV (mitochondrial marker) was enriched in mitochondrial fractions but undetectable in cytosolic fractions, while Histone H3 (nuclear marker) was detected in whole-cell lysates but not in cytosolic fractions. Only cytosolic preparations meeting these criteria were used for mtDNA quantification.

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in AT2 cells was evaluated using JC-1 dye (#MA0338, Meilunbio, Liaoning, China). After the indicated treatments, the JC-1 staining solution (200×) was diluted to a working concentration of 1× with culture medium, added to the cells, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were gently washed twice with JC-1 buffer to remove excess dye, and fluorescence signals were immediately observed under a laser confocal microscope. The ratio of red (JC-1 aggregates) to green (JC-1 monomers) fluorescence was used to indicate changes in mitochondrial membrane potential.

Real-time cell metabolism assay

The real-time metabolic profile of AT2 cells was analyzed using an XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, USA). During the assay, oligomycin was first injected to inhibit ATP synthase and determine ATP-linked oxygen consumption, followed by addition of the mitochondrial uncoupler carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP) to evaluate maximal respiratory capacity, reflected by the maximal oxygen consumption rate (OCR). Subsequently, rotenone and antimycin A, inhibitors of mitochondrial complexes I and III respectively, were applied to block the electron transport chain and quantify non-mitochondrial respiration. All parameters were automatically recorded and analyzed using the Seahorse XF software according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Lung tissues and primary AT2 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Catalog #G2002, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Catalog #GK10009, GLPBIO, California, USA). Equal amounts of total protein (30 μg per sample) were separated by 10%–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Germany). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and band intensities were quantified with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Detailed information on the primary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table S2. For immunoblotting, β-actin was used as the loading control for whole-cell lysates, whereas COX IV served as the loading control for mitochondria-enriched fractions.

For co-immunoprecipitation, AT2 cells were lysed in immunoprecipitation buffer containing 2% octyl-β-D-glucoside, and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was incubated overnight with anti-CerS6 or anti-BNIP3 antibodies, followed by addition of Protein A/G Plus Magnetic Beads (MaqPoly) for 4 h at 4 °C. The bead–antibody–antigen complexes were washed with Tris buffer, eluted with Laemmli loading buffer, heated at 100 °C for 10 min, and analyzed by Western blotting.

Immunoblot signals were quantified from the original blot images using ImageJ densitometry. For each blot, the same-sized region of interest (ROI) was applied to all lanes, and background subtraction was performed uniformly before quantification. Target band intensity was then normalized to the appropriate loading control (β-actin for whole-cell lysates; COX IV for mitochondrial fractions) and, for phospho-proteins, additionally normalized to the corresponding total protein.

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from lung tissues or AT2 cells using TRIzol reagent (#G3013, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with a reverse transcription kit (#RK20429, Abclonal, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted on a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using SYBR Green Master Mix (#RK21203, Abclonal, Wuhan, China). The relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to β-actin as an internal control. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (#G3013, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA libraries were prepared with the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Preparation Kit (Pre-mixed Version) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) by OE Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.

Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were processed using fastp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 [23], and gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) [24] using HTSeq-count [25]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in R software (v3.2.0) to evaluate biological reproducibility among samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2, using thresholds of Q < 0.05 and fold change > 2 or < 0.5.

Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was conducted in R (v3.2.0) to visualize gene expression profiles across samples, and radar plots of the top 30 DEGs were generated using the ggradar package. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Reactome, and WikiPathways, were performed in R based on the hypergeometric distribution. Significantly enriched terms were visualized as bar plots, chord diagrams, and bubble charts.

Detection of cellular ceramides

Cell samples were transferred into glass vials, weighed, and mixed with 10 μL of internal standard solution and 200 μL of ultrapure water. The mixtures were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles between liquid nitrogen and an ice bath, followed by sonication to disrupt cell membranes. After vortexing for 5 min, samples were centrifuged, and the resulting pellets were dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen at room temperature. The residues were reconstituted in 100 μL of dichloromethane/methanol (1:1, v/v) and analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS; 1290–6470 system, Agilent Technologies, USA). For experiments assessing intracellular delivery of exogenous C16:0 ceramide, vehicle- and C16:0 ceramide-treated cells were processed in parallel using identical washing, extraction, and UPLC–MS/MS settings.

Data analysis

For ANOVA-based analyses, global ANOVA outputs, including F values, degrees of freedom, and P values, were reported in the figure legends and supplementary statistical tables. All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments (n ≥ 3). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.3; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test, whereas one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The corresponding ANOVA data for this article can be found in Supplementary Table S5.

Results

CerS6 is upregulated in AT2 during acute lung injury

To profile the ceramide synthase (CerS) family during ALI, we analyzed a public time-course transcriptomic dataset of LPS-challenged mouse lungs (0–72 h; GSE193876). Most members of the CerS family were unchanged or downregulated over time, whereas CerS6 showed a distinct induction and peaked at 12 h (Fig. 1B, Figure S3A). This pattern was validated in our LPS time-course model: lung qPCR confirmed a selective increase of CerS6 (Fig. 1C, Figure S3B; schematic in Fig. 1A), and immunoblotting demonstrated that CerS6 protein was induced by LPS with a maximal level at 12 h (Fig. 1D).

CerS6 is selectively induced during ALI and activates the CerS6–C16 ceramide axis in lung and AT2 cells. (A) Schematic of the LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI) time-course design and sample collection. (B) Time-course expression profile of ceramide synthase family genes (CerS1–CerS6) in a public transcriptomic dataset of LPS-challenged mouse lungs (0–72 h; GSE193876). CerS6 shows distinct induction with a peak at 12 h. LPS exposure induced pronounced lung injury characterized by alveolar septal thickening, interstitial edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2D). In contrast, Sftpc-lineage epithelial CerS6 conditional deletion markedly alleviated these pathological changes, as evidenced by H&E staining (Fig. 2D).

Consistent with histological findings, CerS6 deficiency significantly reduced lung injury scores, decreased lung wet-to-dry ratios, and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (Fig. 2E–G), indicating that AT2-derived CerS6 contributes to endotoxin-driven pulmonary inflammation.

We next examined cell death. TUNEL staining revealed extensive apoptotic cell death in LPS-treated lungs, whereas CerS6-deficient mice exhibited markedly fewer TUNEL-positive cells (Fig. 2H). Immunoblot analysis further demonstrated that LPS robustly induced the pro-apoptotic protein BAX and the execution-phase marker cleaved caspase-3; importantly, both were substantially attenuated in CerS6-deficient lungs (Fig. 2I), supporting a protective role of CerS6 deletion against LPS-induced apoptosis. Given that oxidative stress represents an early amplifier of inflammatory and apoptotic cascades in ALI, we evaluated reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and antioxidant capacity. LPS markedly increased pulmonary ROS levels, whereas CerS6 deficiency significantly blunted this elevation and restored superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity (Fig. 2J-K).

Finally, to assess disease severity under lethal conditions, mice were challenged with a high dose of LPS (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and monitored for 7 days. Sftpc-lineage epithelial CerS6 cKO mice exhibited significantly improved survival compared with control littermates (Fig. 2L). These findings collectively indicate that CerS6 promotes inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress in the lung during ALI.

The alveolar epithelial barrier is essential for normal lung function, and the extent of barrier disruption closely correlates with the severity of acute lung injury. Immunoblotting showed that LPS exposure markedly reduced the expression of tight junction–associated proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, claudin-3, and claudin-5, in CerS6flox/flox control mice, whereas Sftpc-lineage epithelial CerS6 conditional deletion partially restored their levels (Fig. 3A). Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence staining of ZO-1 provided supportive tissue-level evidence that CerS6 deficiency attenuated LPS-associated alterations in barrier-related staining patterns in AT2 cell regions (Fig. 3B).

Given prior evidence that CerS6-derived C16:0 ceramide contributes to mitochondrial fission and stress responses [17, 18], we next investigated whether CerS6 influences mitochondrial homeostasis in vivo. To further dissect the role of CerS6 during LPS challenge, we performed RNA-seq on primary AT2 cells stimulated with LPS in the presence of CerS6 knockdown or control siRNA. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation between siNC and siCerS6 groups under LPS stimulation, with expected biological variability among replicates (Figure S5C–D). In addition, genes linked to inflammatory monocyte recruitment (Ly6c1), metabolic stress (Pdk4), and inflammatory signaling (Spp1 and Sting1) were also downregulated (Fig. 6A–B). Conversely, Bnip3 [28], a mitophagy-promoting gene, and Sp6, a gene implicated in epithelial regeneration, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 6A–B), suggesting a transcriptional shift toward a less inflammatory and more reparative program. Consistent with this shift, analysis of mitochondrial-associated gene expression (log2(FPKM + 1)) revealed that mitochondrial gene expression profiles exhibited a trend toward mitochondrial fusion and repair following CerS6 silencing (Figure S5E), though no significant differences were observed.

Given that both CerS6 and CerS5 can contribute to C16:0 ceramide synthesis [17], it remained unclear whether CerS5 might compensate for CerS6 loss during LPS challenge. RNA-seq and RT–qPCR validation showed that CerS6 knockdown did not induce compensatory upregulation of CerS5 (Fig. 6K). Moreover, CerS6 silencing significantly reduced LPS-induced intracellular C16:0 ceramide accumulation (Fig. 4A). Together with our time-course RT–qPCR analysis showing that LPS selectively induced CerS6, but not CerS5, in primary AT2 cells (Fig. 1G), these results support the conclusion that CerS6—rather than CerS5—is the principal driver of C16:0 ceramide remodeling in AT2 cells during LPS stimulation.

Overall, these findings indicate that CerS6 promotes inflammatory signaling and ceramide accumulation during ALI, while CerS6 silencing suppresses the activation of inflammatory pathways and contributes to restoring sphingolipid metabolic balance in AT2 cells.

CerS6 and its product C16:0 ceramide activate the cGAS–STING–NLRP3 inflammatory axis in AT2 cells

To delineate the mechanism by which CerS6 amplifies inflammatory responses, we first examined transcriptomic alterations associated with CerS6 silencing during LPS stimulation. As described above, RNA-seq revealed that CerS6 knockdown reduced Sting1 expression, suppressed pathways related to cytosolic DNA sensing (cGAS–STING) and NOD-like receptor signaling, and markedly downregulated interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) (Fig. 6A–J), suggesting that CerS6 may regulate these innate immune programs.

The cGAS–STING pathway senses cytosolic DNA derived from exogenous sources (e.g., viral/bacterial DNA) or endogenous danger signals such as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), thereby initiating downstream inflammatory signaling. Because mitochondrial injury promotes mtDNA release into the cytosol, we hypothesized that CerS6 facilitates LPS-induced mtDNA leakage and subsequent activation of the cGAS–STING axis.

To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed CerS6 in primary AT2 cells and confirmed overexpression efficiency by immunoblotting and RT–qPCR (Figure S6A–B). We further verified that CerS6 overexpression increased intracellular C16:0 ceramide levels (Figure S6C). As expected, LPS stimulation significantly elevated mtDNA abundance in the cytosolic fraction. Importantly, CerS6 knockdown attenuated this increase, whereas CerS6 overexpression further enhanced cytosolic mtDNA accumulation (Fig. 7A). Successful cytosolic fractionation was confirmed (Figure S6D).

Consistent with these findings, CerS6 overexpression potentiated LPS-induced activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, as indicated by increased cGAS protein abundance and enhanced phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 (Fig. 7B). Immunofluorescence further showed that LPS promoted STING puncta formation—a hallmark of STING activation—and this effect was augmented by CerS6 overexpression (Fig. 7C). Accordingly, transcription of ISGs was further increased by CerS6 overexpression during LPS challenge (Fig. 7D).

Because cytosolic mtDNA can also function as a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) to engage NOD-like receptor pathways, and because STING signaling can directly or indirectly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [32, 33], we next investigated whether CerS6 modulates NLRP3 activation. LPS robustly induced the NLRP3 axis in AT2 cells, as reflected by increased levels of NLRP3, cleaved caspase-1, mature IL-1β, mature IL-18, and phosphorylated NF-κB (Fig. 7E). CerS6 overexpression further amplified these responses, whereas CerS6 knockdown markedly suppressed them. To address whether cGAS–STING signaling functionally contributes to NLRP3 inflammasome activation in our system, we pharmacologically inhibited STING using H-151 during LPS stimulation and assessed downstream inflammatory readouts. As shown in Figure S8, LPS robustly increased cGAS expression and TBK1 phosphorylation, accompanied by enhanced NLRP3 expression and caspase-1 activation. Importantly, STING inhibition with H-151 markedly attenuated TBK1 phosphorylation and reduced NLRP3 and cleaved caspase-1 levels, supporting that STING activity is required for full induction of the NLRP3 inflammasome response under LPS challenge [34]. Notably, combining H-151 with CerS6 knockdown did not further suppress NLRP3 and caspase-1 activation beyond either intervention alone, suggesting that CerS6 promotes NLRP3 activation largely through the same STING-dependent pathway rather than via an additional parallel mechanism.

Given that the CerS6–C16:0 ceramide axis is dynamically remodeled during LPS stimulation, we next asked whether the CerS6-derived lipid product mediates activation of the cGAS–STING–NLRP3 cascade. A key rescue experiment showed that exogenous C16:0 ceramide restored activation of the cGAS–STING–NLRP3 pathway in CerS6-deficient cells (Figure S7A–C). Figure S7D confirmed efficient intracellular delivery, and additional analysis indicated that C16:0 ceramide primarily acts as an amplifier under LPS challenge rather than an initiator (Figure S7E).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CerS6 and its product C16:0 ceramide enhance LPS-induced inflammatory responses in AT2 cells by promoting cytosolic mtDNA accumulation and activating the cGAS–STING–NLRP3 inflammatory axis.

CerS6 suppresses mitophagy by interacting with BNIP3 and disrupting BNIP3–LC3 recruitment

To further clarify how CerS6 drives mtDNA–cGAS/STING–NLRP3 activation, we next investigated its impact on mitochondrial homeostasis. Because cytosolic mtDNA release is a key upstream trigger of this inflammatory cascade, and given that CerS6 silencing increased autophagy-related signatures in our transcriptomic analyses (Fig. 6C–D), we focused on mitophagy, the selective autophagic removal of damaged mitochondria.

BNIP3 and BNIP3L (NIX), members of the BCL-2 family of BH3-olny proteins, function as mitophagy receptors by binding LC3/GABARAP to tether depolarized mitochondria to autophagosomes [28]. This process limits excessive mitochondrial ROS production and prevents mtDNA leakage. RNA-seq and RT–qPCR validation showed that CerS6 knockdown in AT2 cells significantly upregulated Bnip3 and Bnip3l expression (Fig. 8A–B), suggesting that CerS6 may negatively regulate BNIP3-dependent mitophagy.

Under LPS stimulation, the co-localization between BNIP3 and LC3 was markedly reduced; this reduction was further exacerbated by CerS6 overexpression, whereas CerS6 knockdown restored BNIP3–LC3 co-localization (Fig. 8D). Consistent with these findings, LPS stimulation increased expression of fission-related markers (phosphorylated Drp1 and Fis1) and reduced the fusion-related protein MFN1. CerS6 depletion partially reversed these alterations; however, concurrent BNIP3 knockdown diminished the corrective effect of CerS6 silencing on mitochondrial dynamics markers (Fig. 9B). Functional analysis using Seahorse extracellular flux assays further supported this observation. CerS6 knockdown improved LPS-impaired mitochondrial respiration, including basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, and maximal respiration, whereas these improvements were blunted when BNIP3 was simultaneously silenced (spare respiratory capacity showed a non-significant trend) (Fig. 9C).

Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is closely linked to mtDNA release, we next quantified cytosolic mtDNA levels. LPS markedly increased cytosolic mtDNA abundance, which was reduced by CerS6 knockdown. Notably, BNIP3 co-silencing partially reversed this reduction (Fig. 9D). Since cytosolic mtDNA activates innate immune signaling pathways, downstream events were assessed. CerS6 depletion suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of TBK1, IRF3, STING, and NF-κB p65, thereby attenuating activation of the cGAS/STING–NLRP3 axis. Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated that STING puncta formation was regulated by CerS6. Importantly, BNIP3 co-silencing largely restored pathway activation that had been suppressed by CerS6 knockdown (Fig. 9E–H).

Collectively, these findings indicate that BNIP3 participates in the regulation of CerS6-mediated mitochondrial dynamics, mtDNA release, and downstream inflammatory signaling during LPS challenge, supporting a model in which CerS6 amplifies STING–NLRP3 activation by suppressing BNIP3-dependent mitophagy.

Disscussion

This study identifies ceramide synthase 6 (CerS6) as a key regulator of alveolar epithelial injury in acute lung injury (ALI). Following LPS or CLP stimulation, CerS6 expression and its product C16 ceramide are significantly upregulated in AT2 cells. Knocking out CerS6 in AT2 cells mitigates apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses while preserving barrier integrity and mitochondrial homeostasis. Mechanistically, CerS6 inhibits mitochondrial autophagy by interacting with BNIP3 to block its recruitment to LC3. The resulting accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria triggers excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage into the cytoplasm, thereby activating the STING-NLRP3 inflammatory axis. Collectively, these findings establish CerS6 as a molecular hub linking mitochondrial stress to innate immune activation during ALI.

This study expands previous reports on CerS6 as a regulator of mitochondrial dynamics in metabolic diseases. Hammerschmidt et al. demonstrated that CerS6-derived sphingolipids promote mitochondrial fission via Mff, contributing to obesity-induced insulin resistance [17]. Subsequent studies revealed CerS6 association with high-fat diet-induced mitochondrial stress [18]. Our findings align with clinical observations of elevated C16:0 ceramide levels in COVID-19-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome patients [16], establishing CerS6 as a mechanistic bridge linking ceramide metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, and pulmonary epithelial inflammatory injury. A recent study by Zhu et al. further supports the concept that CerS6 links ceramide metabolism to mitochondrial stress-associated innate immune activation [35]. In diabetic kidney disease, the authors showed that CerS6 and mitochondrial Cer(d18:1/16:0) accumulation promoted mtDNA release and cGAS-STING signaling in podocytes, at least in part through a VDAC1-related mechanism. These findings are conceptually consistent with our observations that CerS6 serves as a key node connecting ceramide remodeling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and innate immune activation. Importantly, however, our study extends this framework to acute lung injury and identifies a distinct mechanistic context in alveolar epithelial cells, in which CerS6 aggravates mitochondrial–immune crosstalk through BNIP3-dependent mitophagy suppression, ultimately driving mtDNA-STING/NLRP3 signaling. Thus, although both studies support a pathogenic role for CerS6 in coupling mitochondrial damage to inflammatory signaling, they differ in disease setting, cell type, and proximal mechanism, highlighting the broader but context-specific relevance of CerS6 across organ injury states.

We further demonstrated that CerS6 and its metabolite C16:0 ceramide jointly contribute to this inflammatory amplification effect. Although CerS5 can also synthesize C16:0 ceramide, data indicate that LPS-induced C16:0 accumulation in AT2 cells primarily depends on CerS6. Knockdown of CerS6 reduces C16:0 ceramide levels without altering CerS5 expression, while exogenous supplementation of C16:0 restores activation of the cGAS/STING-NLRP3 pathway in CerS6-deficient cells. Notably, C16:0 ceramide alone did not induce robust inflammatory activation but primarily exerted an amplifying effect under LPS-induced mitochondrial stress conditions. These findings underscore the importance of C16:0 ceramide as a key downstream effector molecule in CerS6-driven inflammatory signaling.

Beyond lipid remodeling, CerS6 was found to disrupt mitochondrial quality control. Knockdown of CerS6 enhanced BNIP3 expression, restored mitochondrial autophagy flux, and improved mitochondrial membrane potential and respiratory function. Immunoprecipitation and colocalization analyses confirmed an interaction between CerS6 and BNIP3/BNIP3L. Functional assays demonstrated that BNIP3 silencing partially reversed the protective effects of CerS6 depletion.

This incomplete reversal indicates that BNIP3-dependent mitochondrial autophagy represents an important but non-exclusive mechanism node. Other mitochondrial autophagy receptors or parallel mitochondrial stress pathways may also participate in CerS6-mediated effects. Furthermore, CerS6 knockdown increased TFAM expression and enhanced PGC1α binding, suggesting CerS6 may concurrently regulate mitochondrial clearance and biogenesis. Collectively, these findings support a model where CerS6 modulates mitochondrial integrity through multi-tiered mechanisms, thereby influencing downstream innate immune activation.

In this study system, the most consistent biochemical and functional readouts point to the mtDNA-cGAS/STING-NLRP3 pathway, a conclusion further validated by STING inhibition experiments. Therefore, our data support the model that CerS6-mediated mitochondrial stress primarily promotes inflammasome activation via STING-dependent signaling, rather than solely activating NOD receptors.

From a translational medicine perspective, these findings identify CerS6 as a potential therapeutic target for limiting ALI-associated pulmonary inflammation and epithelial barrier dysfunction. AT2 cells are critical for pulmonary surfactant production and alveolar regeneration, processes highly dependent on mitochondrial homeostasis. By maintaining mitochondrial autophagy and inhibiting STING-NLRP3 pathway activation, CerS6 suppression may protect epithelial integrity and promote repair. Consistent with this, CerS6 knockdown increases expression of the epithelial repair factor Sp6, further substantiating its restorative potential. Thus, pharmacologically reducing ceramide levels by inhibiting CerS6 or modulating sphingolipid metabolism may serve as an effective strategy against infection- or sepsis-associated lung injury. Indeed, Zhou et al. recently identified FF-C1, a natural metabolite from Fusobacterium foetens, which noncompetitively inhibits CerS6 and reduces ceramide levels, thereby mitigating the progression of steatohepatitis [36]. Although its efficacy in ALI remains unvalidated, our findings suggest that targeting CerS6 may offer therapeutic benefits for acute pulmonary inflammation.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Although Sftpc-driven Cre recombination primarily targets AT2 cells, recombination during injury may not be fully confined to all epithelial states, necessitating further optimization of cell-type specificity via lineage analysis. Additionally, while pharmacological and genetic data support a STING-dependent mechanism, future studies employing conditional STING knockout in AT2 cells would more effectively strengthen causal inference. Interestingly, in both in vivo and in vitro ALI models, ultra-long-chain ceramides (C22, C24) were elevated compared to controls. Given the typically protective role of these ceramides in the lung, we hypothesize this may represent a reactive protective mechanism.

In summary, this study reveals that CerS6 disrupts BNIP3-dependent mitochondrial autophagy, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, mtDNA release, and activation of the STING-NLRP3 inflammatory cascade in acute lung injury.

CerS6 and its product C16:0 ceramide synergistically amplify epithelial inflammatory injury by coupling sphingolipid metabolism with mitochondrial-immune cross-regulation. These findings establish CerS6 as a metabolic-immune checkpoint in the lung and highlight its inhibition as a potential strategy to restore mitochondrial homeostasis and mitigate inflammatory responses in ALI and related diseases.
