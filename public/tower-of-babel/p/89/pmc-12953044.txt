Pharmacogenetics of CYP3A5 in Pediatric Patients With Eosinophilic Esophagitis
Authors: Janelle Noel‐MacDonnell, Norah Almahbub, Erin Boone, Wendy Wang, Dilyara Cheranova, Lisa Harvey, Valentina Shakhnovich, Rachel Chevalier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is an atopic inflammatory disease with limited treatment options. Though swallowed topical steroids are commonly used, they are not effective in all patients. We investigated the presence of CYP3A4/5 and ABCB1 (P‐glycoprotein) mRNA in esophageal tissue and its differential expression by genotype in pediatric patients. Esophageal tissue biopsies were obtained from pediatric patients undergoing standard of care endoscopy. Levels of CYP3A4, CYP3A5, and ABCB1 mRNA were measured using droplet digital PCR and compared across patients with and without EoE. Genotypes for common, clinically relevant CYP3A5 variants were evaluated in a cohort of individuals with and without EoE. Levels of CYP3A4 and ABCB1 in the esophagus were negligible in all patients. CYP3A5 mRNA expression was highest with two wild type alleles (*1/*1) and lowest with no wild type alleles. Further investigation into the function of esophageal CYP3A5 and its ability to metabolize budesonide delivered to the esophagus is warranted.

Introduction

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a life‐long, inflammatory disease affecting all ages and characterized by eosinophilic infiltration of the esophagus [1]. EoE is estimated to occur in approximately 34.4 cases out of 100,000, and its incidence has been increasing in the last decade [2, 3]. The pathophysiology is an allergen‐driven Th2 T cell response that triggers infiltration of eosinophils into the esophagus leading to active inflammation and symptoms such as dysphagia, odynophagia, and in younger patients, vomiting, abdominal pain, and poor growth. Diagnosis is made when 15 or more eosinophils per high powered field (hpf) are seen on histopathology, but other chronic signs of inflammation such as basal cell hypertrophy, edema, eosinophilic microabscesses, or fibrosis are common as well. Chronic inflammation results in fibrosis causing esophageal strictures and poor esophageal motility. Potential triggers for EoE include food and/or environmental allergen exposures. Patients frequently have many triggers leading to difficult‐to‐treat and refractory disease. Although the EoE population is thought to be primarily Caucasian, others have demonstrated that the local EoE population may mirror the diversity of the local general population [4].

A subset of patients with EoE will histologically respond to proton‐pump inhibitors (30%–50%) [5, 6, 7]; however, the remaining cases are managed with either locally acting topical (swallowed) glucocorticoids, dietary food group eliminations, an anti‐IL‐4 and ‐13 monoclonal antibody (dupilumab), or a combination of these strategies. These treatment options have varying levels of success, and therapy adherence is particularly challenging for children. Dupilumab treatment, for instance, can be expensive, invasive, and difficult to obtain insurance approval, especially before trying other therapies [2]. Whereas elimination diets are difficult to implement and can lead to further issues with linear growth, weight gain, and psychosocial development.

Among corticosteroids, oral budesonide is valued by clinicians for its extensive first‐pass metabolism, which prevents systemic drug exposure and significantly reduces the risk of serious adverse events associated with other oral steroids (e.g., growth failure and iatrogenic adrenal insufficiency) [8, 9]. However, more than a quarter of patients with EoE fail to achieve disease remission with budesonide therapy [10, 11, 12], possibly in part due to medication not reaching its intracellular target. Currently, clinicians rely on a “trial and error” approach to assess budesonide efficacy, which requires patients to undergo repeated endoscopic procedures every 8–12 weeks to evaluate esophageal healing. Though relatively safe, these procedures are invasive, time consuming, expensive, and result in increased life‐time exposure to general anesthesia, which is associated with negative cognitive effects, especially in the developing child [1]. Currently, no method exists to preemptively guide dosing for swallowed topical steroids and predict the potential for response.

Budesonide is a substrate for the cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP3A4 and CYP3A5, as well as the cell membrane transporter, P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) [13, 14]. These proteins frequently co‐locate in the body and together reduce the intracellular concentration of xenobiotics [15]. CYP3A5 is a polymorphically expressed enzyme, with marked variability in enzyme activity due to single nucleotide variations (SNVs) in the gene encoding the protein. The reference allele, CYP3A5*1, shows increased metabolism of substrates, including budesonide [16]. The CYP3A5*1 allele is found at highest rates in the Black/African American population (60%), less frequently in the Asian and Hispanic populations, with lowest frequency in Caucasians (10%) [17, 18]. Other clinically relevant CYP3A5 alleles (i.e., *3 (NG_007938.2:g.12083A>G, rs776746, splice defect), *6 (NG_007938.2:g.19787G>A, rs10264272, splice defect), and *7 (NG_007938.2:g.32228dupT, rs41303343, p.T346fs)) result in null enzyme activity [16, 19]. Of these alleles, CYP3A5*3 is the most frequent with an allele frequency of 92% in European populations [20].

Reduced‐function alleles have also been identified for CYP3A4; specifically, CYP3A4*22 is the most prevalent and is associated with decreased enzymatic activity in vivo for tacrolimus, cyclosporine and statins, with frequencies between 3% and 10% in European and African populations [21]. Although rare, CYP3A4*6, *20, and *26 may be fully dysfunctional based on predicted protein structures [22]. P‐gp (also known as MDR1 and encoded by the gene ABCB1) is well known for contributing to cancer drug resistance when overexpressed. While the gene is highly polymorphic, its significance in genotype‐guided therapy has not yet been established [23].

As a first step to determining esophageal enzyme contribution to metabolism of budesonide, we investigate the expression of CYP3A4, CYP3A5, and ABCB1 (P‐gp) mRNA in pediatric patients diagnosed with eosinophilic esophagitis. We hypothesize that esophageal budesonide metabolism via CYP3A4/5 or efflux by P‐gp contributes to treatment failure. Since CYP3A5 has clinically relevant genotypic variations affecting drug metabolism, we also determined genotype and examined its effects on mRNA expression. Finally, we evaluate a subset of EoE patients with available clinical data to assess for genotype influence on treatment response.

Methods

Sample Procurement

Patients presenting for standard of care endoscopy were approached in same‐day surgery. All patients consented or assented to study participation as appropriate for their age group. All esophageal tissue biopsy samples were collected during standard‐of‐care endoscopy for gastrointestinal symptoms as determined by their primary gastroenterologist. Study samples were only obtained in the same locations as diagnostic biopsies, as ordered by the primary gastroenterologist. Primary gastroenterologists gathered samples from the mid and distal esophagus or only the distal at the provider's discretion. Samples for the biorepository reflected this original order and thus a subset of patients has samples banked from both locations or solely distal. Biopsies were immediately flash frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen until use. All samples were stored in an IRB‐approved biorepository within the Children's Mercy Research Institute (CMRI).

Patients with > 15 eos/hpf on esophageal biopsy were included as EoE patients. Control samples were obtained from patients undergoing standard‐of‐care endoscopy for gastrointestinal symptoms who had no pathologic findings on their biopsies. These patients would be diagnosed with disorders of gut‐brain interaction (DGBI) and are used as controls since asymptomatic patients do not undergo endoscopy. Patients with DGBI do not have inflammatory alterations in their esophageal epithelium.

To capture genetic diversity, patients from various self‐identified racial backgrounds were included in the study. This strategy was designed to enrich the sample set for CYP3A5*1 genotypes, which are more frequently found in individuals of African ancestry, and consequently resulted in a participant demographic that differs from those typically described in EoE studies. Nevertheless, as EoE is most commonly observed in Caucasian males, the sample set still lacked full racial representation; most notably, there were no Asian patients included.

Clinical Data Collection

Demographics including age at diagnosis and sample collection, sex, and race were obtained via the CMRI biorepository data. Patients' EoE medications and dietary restrictions were recorded. Clinical chart review allowed assessment of disease state at the time of tissue collection. For patients with a diagnosis of EoE, the EoE Endoscopic Reference Score (EREFS) [24] (Table S1) was obtained from endoscopy reports. Histological data were reviewed for eosinophil counts and common signs of EoE‐related inflammation (Table S1). To evaluate clinical response, patients with EoE were included if they had undergone ≥ 2 endoscopies with biopsies: one prior to budesonide initiation and one after ≥ 12 weeks of therapy, each with an associated clinic visit that assessed patient‐reported outcomes. Budesonide dosing and formulation were not standardized in this retrospective study but typically ranged from 0.25 mg to 1.00 mg BID based on age. Specific doses and formulation can be found in Table 2. Patient‐reported outcomes were assessed on a binary scale (1 = present, 0 = absent) and included: abdominal pain, pain with eating, throat pain, throat itching, throat clearing, waking with pain, food impaction, dysphagia, choking, nausea, vomiting, regurgitation, non‐cardiac chest pain, nighttime cough, feeding refusal, and cutting food into small bites.

DNA/Total RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis

DNA and total RNA were extracted from the tissue biopsies using AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) as follows: frozen esophageal biopsy samples (< 10 μg) were immersed into 350 μL buffer RLT/β‐ME and homogenized for 30 s with a PRO200 homogenizer on ice. Next, the homogenized lysate was transferred to the DNA spin column and centrifuged for 30 s at full speed. The flow‐through was saved and applied to a new 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tube and the DNA spin column stored at ambient temperature until completion of the RNA extraction steps. RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer's protocol with three additional steps of RPE buffer washing. DNA extractions were performed per the manufacturer's instructions without any modifications. RNA and DNA quantities and purity were determined on a BioTek Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using Gen5 software (v.3.10). RNA integrity was verified on agarose gel electrophoresis and total RNA (500 or 1000 ng) was reverse transcribed using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

TaqMan Genotyping of CYP3A5

All samples were TaqMan genotyped for CYP3A5*3, *6, and *7. Genotype calls were defaulted to CYP3A5*1 if a sample returned homozygous reference for all CYP3A5 alleles tested. TaqMan genotyping was performed on a QuantStudio 12K Flex Real‐Time PCR System (v. 1.5.0; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the TaqMan OpenArray PGx Express Panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Data was manually examined using Taq‐Man Genotyper Software (v1.6.0).

Gene Expression by Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR)

Gene expression was determined by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) using the Bio‐Rad QX200 System (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). For each cDNA sample, a target TaqMan gene expression assay (ABCB1 [P‐gp], CYP3A4, CYP3A5) was combined with a normalizer gene expression assay (GAPDH, PPIA, or RPS18) for a total of nine different target‐normalizer gene combinations. A list of all gene expression assays used can be found in Table S2. Gene expression was determined as a ratio of target mRNA copies to normalizer mRNA copies. Reactions were combined with 1× TaqMan gene expression assay mix (one target gene, one normalizer gene), 1× ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP), diluted (1:10 for 500 ng input and 1:20 for 1000 ng input) or stock cDNA (0.5 μL or 1.0 μL) and reagent grade water to a final volume of 21 μL. Water and cDNA volumes were adjusted to achieve between 100 and 5000 copies/μL for the normalizer gene. Stock cDNA was used when comparing the target genes expression to PPIA due to differences in expression levels of PPIA compared to the other normalizer genes within the esophagus. Reactions mixed in a ddPCR 96‐deep well plate and set on a QX200 AutoDG Droplet Generator for droplet formation using Droplet Generation Oil for Probes (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The plate was then heat‐sealed with pierceable foil and cycled on a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler with the following protocol: initial denaturing at 95°C for 10 min, 40× cycling at 94°C for 30 s and 60°C for 1 min, enzyme deactivation at 98°C for 10 min, droplet hardening at 4°C for 30 min, and final hold at 10°C. Droplets were read with the QX200 Droplet Reader and results analyzed using the QuantaSoft Analysis Pro Software (v1.0.596).

Statistical Analysis

All clinical and expression data were descriptively summarized. Verification of expression levels between medial and distal tissue type was conducted for patients with both sample types. Bivariate analyses were completed to compare patients without esophageal pathology to EoE participants. Furthermore, differences in mRNA expression between the number of functional CYP3A5 allele(s) (i.e., two non‐*1 alleles, presence of one *1 allele, and presence of two *1 alleles) were investigated. Due to the small number of samples with two CYP3A5*1 alleles, nonparametric statistical tests (Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis with Dwass, Steel, Critchlow‐Fligner Method for pairwise comparisons) were completed for all comparisons. Furthermore, multivariable regression was completed to assess mRNA expression variability due to clinical factors. All analysis was completed in SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Demographics

Sixty‐nine patients were included for genotype and mRNA analysis including 24 non‐EoE and 45 patients with EoE (Table 1). Ages for patients with EoE (median (M) = 9.0, interquartile (IQR) = 5.6, 13.4) and non‐EoE patients (M = 12.8, IQR = 8.6, 16.6) were distributionally similar (p = 0.094). Median age of diagnosis of EoE was 5 years (range 0.8–17.8). Male and female ratios were as expected with EoE showing a male predominance (~59%) though not statistically different between EoE and non‐EoE patients (p = 0.245). Race/ethnicity demographics were skewed from the general population as discussed in methods (p = 0.001). Patients were on a variety of medications and diet therapy, as is common for EoE patients (Table 2).

Genotype

Results of CYP3A5 genotyping are shown in full in Figure 1. Five (7%) patients were homozygous for CYP3A5*1/*1. The most common genotype of our cohort was CYP3A5*3/*3 at 68%. Patients with at least one CYP3A5*1 allele made up 27% of this set of patients.

mRNA Expression

Three different normalizer genes were evaluated (RPS18, GAPDH, and PPIA) with PPIA providing the most discriminative results (Figure S1, Table S2). Twenty‐four patients had both distal and medial esophageal tissue samples. There was no difference in the distribution of expression for ABCB1 (P‐gp), CYP3A4, or CYP3A5 when comparing different tissue locations (Figure 2). Without location‐specific expression differences, the location with the largest number of samples (distal, n = 69) was selected for further analysis.

CYP3A5 mRNA expression in mid (n = 27) and distal (n = 69) esophageal biopsy tissue. Due to convenience sampling, numbers are low, especially among patients with functional CYP3A5 alleles.

CYP3A4 and ABCB1 had negligible expression for all tested patients irrespective of EoE diagnosis (Figure 3). Statistical differences in the distribution of CYP3A5 mRNA/PPIA expression ratio between EoE and non‐EoE patients were observed (p = 0.002) (Figure 3C). This was also the case with increasing number of CYP3A5*1 functional allele(s) (p = < 0.0001) (Figure 4), specifically for differences between no functioning alleles (CYP3A5*3, *6, *7) compared to one functioning allele (p = < 0.0001) and no functioning alleles compared to two functioning alleles (p = 0.0007). Expression for one versus two functional allele(s) was similar (p = 0.153). Overall, neither sex nor EREFS score had significant differences in CYP3A5 mRNA expression (Figure 5A–C; p = 0.746 and p = 0.616, respectively). No differences in CYP3A5 mRNA expression were present with the histological parameters spongiosis (p‐value = 0.424), papillary elongation (p‐value = 1.000), fibrosis (p‐value = 0.324), nor microabscesses (p‐value = 0.525). However, among the four patients that had degranulation, expression was lower (p = 0.032). Patients who identified as Black or African American (n = 16) had higher expression of CYP3A5 (p = < 0.0001). Moreover, of the 19 patients with at least one *1 alleles, 14 identified as Black/African American, three identified as white, one as Hispanic, and one as mixed race (Figure 5B).

In multivariable regression, EoE diagnosis (p = < 0.0001), genotype utilizing number of CYP3A5*1 functional allele(s) (p = < 0.0001), as well as the interaction between these two factors (p = 0.0003) had significant effects on CYP3A5 mRNA expression (R 2 = 0.71, F = 30.71, p < 0.001). Specifically, when the number of functional *1 alleles in the genotype is used as the reference, decreases in expression for no functioning alleles (β = −23.6; SE = 2.5) and one functioning allele (β = −15.0; SE = 2.6) were observed. This decrease also held true for EoE diagnosis versus non‐EoE patients (β = −13.0; SE = 3.1). These results suggest that both CYP3A5 genotype and EoE diagnosis contribute to the downregulation of expression, though genotype has a slightly greater impact.

Clinical Outcomes

The subset of patients evaluated for clinical outcomes included 39 patients; 38% female; median age 5 years (IQR 2.3–13.6). Race distribution was 7.7% Black/African American, 2.6% Latino, 89.7% White. Genotypes found were CYP3A5*1/*1 (n = 3), *1/*3 (n = 3), *3/*6 (n = 1), *3/*3 (n = 32). Functional CYP3A5*1 alleles were racially varied (2 Black/AA, 2 White, 1 Latino). Overall, 29/39 patients (74.3%) achieved histologic remission with budesonide. Among patients with a CYP3A5*1 allele, only 3/6 (50%) achieved remission, suggesting a possible trend toward reduced response in functional allele carriers, particularly at lower doses. No significant association with eosinophil counts, EREFS, symptom scores, or pathology scores.

Discussion

This study investigates the expression of drug metabolizing enzymes and transporter mRNA based on genotype in the esophageal epithelium of pediatric patients, both with and without a diagnosis of eosinophilic esophagitis. Additionally, the novel use of ddPCR with small and precious tissue biopsy samples obtained during esophagogastroduodenoscopy was employed to evaluate this method for ABCB1, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5 gene expression.

Compared to qPCR, which uses the Delta–Delta Ct method to quantitate expression, ddPCR provides absolute quantification, achieving higher precision and sensitivity even with variable contamination between samples [25]. However, qPCR has a broader dynamic range (up to 6 orders of magnitude 100 to 1 × 107 copies/μL) compared to ddPCR [26, 27] (5 orders of magnitude 1 to 10,000 copies/μL). Because of variable esophageal biopsy specimen processing and storage, we chose two normalization genes, RPS18 and GAPDH. However, when CYP3A5 non‐functional allele expressors (i.e., CYP3A5*3/*3) were tested with the normalizer genes GAPDH and RPS18, they reached the limit of detection (LOD) for some of the lower concentration samples. Additionally, when increasing the amount of sample input, the normalizer gene would oversaturate the reaction, pushing quantification outside of the linear range. Therefore PPIA, which has similar expression levels to CYP3A5 in esophageal tissue, was tested as an additional normalizer gene and run with stock cDNA to maintain linearity without oversaturation. Our experiments underscore the importance of using a normalizer gene that fits within the dynamic range of the target gene, when measuring expression levels with ddPCR.

Patients with CYP3A5*1 alleles expressed detectable mRNA in the esophagus with almost no presence of CYP3A4 or ABCB1 mRNA. These findings are consistent with similar reports of CYP3A4/5 and ABCB1 expression in the esophagus [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. Additionally, genotype correlates with CYP3A5 mRNA expression in a predictable fashion depending on the number of functional alleles. If CYP3A5 metabolism is happening locally in the esophagus, these variations may alter response rates in a way that can be predicted by genotype. EoE diagnosis, including the severity as predicted by eosinophilic degranulation, also contributes to changes in CYP3A5 mRNA expression encouraging future study into the effects of esophageal tissue inflammation in EoE in larger populations.

As a general limitation, the CYP3A5 expression assay may not equally represent all the nonfunctional alleles as genotypes with later alternative splicing variants, frameshifts, or SNVs could still produce a full length or longer transcript that is captured by this assay. Additionally, it should be noted that mRNA expression levels do not always proportionally equate to protein function; SNVs resulting in decreased or null function could still produce a full‐length transcript. Additionally, alternative splicing or frameshift variants that occur downstream of the CYP3A5 assay target (CYP3A5 Exon 3–4) may also be captured in expression levels.

Published studies have compared the efficacy of oral viscous budesonide to placebo [34], as well as differences between corticosteroids such as fluticasone and budesonide [35]. However, clinical practice reveals substantial variability in the preparation of these formulations. A recent survey of pediatric gastroenterologists identified 19 distinct mixing vehicles used routinely, underscoring the heterogeneity in administration methods [36]. Notably, the only commercially available formulation, Eohilia (Budesonide oral suspension, Takeda), demonstrates a response rate comparable to home‐prepared formulations, raising important questions about formulation standardization and therapeutic optimization [37]. Due to this high variability, it is difficult to evaluate patient response rates related to formulation retrospectively.

Though further investigation into the presence and function of metabolizing enzymes and transporter proteins is needed, genotype‐informed prescribing may allow physicians and patients to make better informed decisions regarding therapeutics and/or dosages. The importance of precision medicine for these patients cannot be understated. Some of these patients (who are all pediatric) have had > 20 lifetime endoscopies. One patient in this cohort was noted to have 22 endoscopies by age 10. The results in this study are a step to improving budesonide dosing for patients.

This study made use of biobank samples collected between 2017 through 2023. Clinical outcomes in retrospective studies are inherently challenging to interpret. At the time of data collection, validated symptom questionnaires were not available, and patient‐reported outcomes depended on clinician inquiry, introducing potential variability. Additionally, the small sample size—particularly the limited number of patients with functional CYP3A5*1 alleles—combined with incomplete retrospective data, constrains the strength of our conclusions. An important observation in this cohort is that all individuals carrying two CYP3A5*1 alleles self‐identified as Black/African American. This finding is consistent with the known population genetics of CYP3A5; however, caution is warranted in interpretation. While allele frequency often correlates with ancestral background, genotype—not self‐reported or perceived race—should serve as the basis for pharmacogenetic evaluation and clinical decision‐making. Reliance on race as a proxy for genetic variation risks reinforcing inaccurate assumptions and may obscure the underlying genetic determinants that inform personalized therapy. Nevertheless, the observed trend toward lower rates of histologic remission among patients carrying CYP3A5*1 alleles suggests a possible pharmacogenetic influence on budesonide response. These findings underscore the need for larger, prospective studies to clarify the role of CYP3A5 genotype in treatment outcomes for EoE.

Additionally, our study is limited by our local population demographics, so not all genetic backgrounds are well represented (particularly lacking Native American and Asian patients who are a minority in our area), despite our best efforts to include a variety of known CYP3A5 genotypes. Prospective studies with larger patient populations could further investigate the relationship between CYP3A5 genotype/mRNA expression and patient‐reported outcomes. Additionally, since these samples were collected before dupilumab was approved and/or in widespread use in pediatric patients, this study is also limited by not having patients with exposure to this medication. Because dupilumab may be used concurrently with budesonide, future studies could investigate whether dupilumab affects expression of CYP3A5.
