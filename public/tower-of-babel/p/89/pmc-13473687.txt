Tobacco rattle virus triggers local defense and systemically coordinates taxoid biosynthesis in Taxus baccata revealed by integrated metabolic analysis
Authors: Seyed Ali Hosseini Tafreshi, Mansour Shariati, Mohammad Reza Mofid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The clinical demand for paclitaxel necessitates novel strategies to enhance its production. While recent breakthroughs have elucidated the complete biosynthetic pathway in heterologous hosts, optimizing production within the native Taxus plant remains crucial. Here, we investigated the unexplored potential of using Tobacco rattle virus (TRV) as a controlled biotic elicitor in Taxus baccata seedlings. Using hydroponics and agroinfiltration, we found that TRV did not systemically infect or silence genes but triggered a localized hypersensitive response. Despite this, TRV significantly increased paclitaxel, baccatin III, and 10-deacetylbaccatin III levels in shoots, with the response depending on the infiltration method. Machine learning revealed exceptionally strong positive correlations among shoot taxoids (r > 0.94) and identified baccatin III levels as the primary predictor of paclitaxel yield. Metabolic flux analysis quantified a preferential enhancement of early pathway steps and revealed a relative bottleneck in the final conversion to paclitaxel. These results demonstrate that TRV acts as a potent, growth-neutral biotic elicitor in T. baccata, where local defense couples with systemic metabolic reprogramming. Our integrated analysis establishes a novel virus-mediated strategy to complement pathway-engineering approaches for sustainable taxane production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49431-0.

Introduction

Taxus baccata L. is a renowned medicinal plant, primarily due to its production of taxane diterpenoids, with paclitaxel (Taxol®) being a frontline chemotherapeutic agent for various cancers, including breast, ovarian, and lung cancers1. However, the ever-increasing clinical demand for paclitaxel is in stark contrast to its extremely low natural abundance in yew tissues and the slow growth of the plant, which drives the continued search for innovative biotechnological methods to enhance its production2.

Elicitation—the use of biotic or abiotic stimuli to trigger plant defense responses and thereby enhance the production of secondary metabolites—is a well-established biotechnological strategy to boost the yield of valuable phytochemicals in in vitro cultures3. In Taxus species, suspension cell cultures represent a promising platform for taxoid production, and a wide array of elicitors has been explored to stimulate this process. These include not only signaling molecules such as methyl jasmonate4, but also biotic agents like fungal cell extracts and culture filtrates, which have been shown to significantly increase paclitaxel and 10-deacetylbaccatin III yields in T. baccata suspension cultures5. While viral infections are known to alter plant secondary metabolism and phytohormone biosynthesis, often as part of the disease syndrome6, the deliberate and controlled use of viral pathogens as elicitation tools to enhance the production of specific valuable metabolites—particularly in Taxus species—remains unreported and constitutes a novel biotechnological approach.

Tobacco rattle virus (TRV) is a bipartite RNA virus that remains one of the most extensively used and efficient vectors for Virus-Induced Gene Silencing (VIGS), a powerful reverse genetics tool for transient gene knockdown in plants7,8. Its popularity stems from key advantages such as a broad host range, efficient systemic movement, and mild symptom development in susceptible hosts, making it suitable for functional genomics studies in numerous angiosperm families, including Solanaceae, Brassicaceae, Papaveraceae as well as other families9–13. Successful VIGS depends critically on the virus achieving systemic infection and replication to generate sufficient double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), the trigger for RNA silencing14. However, while TRV's utility in angiosperms is well-documented, its interaction with, and potential as a functional tool in, gymnosperms—such as the economically important Taxus species—remains completely unknown.

Beyond its utility as a VIGS vector, viral infection constitutes a potent biotic stress that reprograms plant metabolism, affecting diverse pathways including those of secondary metabolites15. Plants perceive viral pathogens and activate intricate defense signaling networks, which frequently involve the modulation of key phytohormones such as salicylic acid (SA), a central regulator of antiviral defense16, and jasmonic acid (JA). This hormonal reprogramming leads to the production of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins and antimicrobial secondary metabolites known as phytoalexins17,18. In yew (Taxus spp.), taxoids are classified as phytoalexins, and their biosynthesis can be upregulated by various elicitors, including fungal derivatives and exogenous application of SA19,20. Given that viral infections are known to alter phytohormone profiles and secondary metabolic pathways6,15, we hypothesized that TRV infection could act as a novel biotic elicitor in T. baccata, activating defense-related metabolic shifts and potentially enhancing taxoid biosynthesis.

Therefore, to directly test our hypothesis that TRV can act as a biotic elicitor, we investigated its interaction with T. baccata seedlings under controlled hydroponic conditions. The central objective of this study was to evaluate the growth and metabolic response of yew seedlings to TRV infection, focusing on its potential to enhance taxoid biosynthesis. To conclusively determine whether TRV could establish a systemic infection and function as a VIGS vector in this gymnosperm host—a prerequisite for attributing any metabolic effects to gene silencing rather than elicitation—we employed TRV vectors harboring a fragment of a T. baccata gene (phytoene desaturase, pds) alongside empty vector controls. This comparative approach was designed to disentangle the effects of viral infection per se from those of potential virus-induced gene silencing. Furthermore, to quantitatively decode the complex metabolic reprogramming and identify the principal factors governing taxoid accumulation, we integrated traditional statistical methods with machine learning (Random Forest regression). We anticipated that our findings could pioneer novel virus-mediated strategies to stimulate the production of high-value taxanes in yew biotechnological systems.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Uniform Taxus baccata L. seedlings were generated using an optimized protocol combining in vitro embryo culture and hydroponics, as previously described21. Briefly, mature seeds were scarified with concentrated sulfuric acid, washed under running water for one week to remove germination inhibitors, and surface-sterilized. Zygotic embryos were excised aseptically under a light microscope and cultured on solid MS medium22 supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) activated charcoal. After germination in darkness, seedlings were transferred to a hydroponic system containing a tenfold diluted Johnson's nutrient solution23. Plants were maintained under a 16/8 h photoperiod at 25 °C for one month prior to agroinfiltration. This protocol yielded healthy, uniform seedlings within approximately three months.

TRV vector constructs and agroinfiltration

The standard bipartite TRV vectors, pTRV1 and empty pTRV2, were used. The construction of the recombinant TRV vector harboring a 390-bp fragment of the T. baccata phytoene desaturase (pds) gene (pTRV2-Tbpds390) was performed as previously described24. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3103 harboring pTRV1 was mixed 1:1 with cultures containing either empty pTRV2 or pTRV2-Tbpds390.

Two different agroinfiltration buffers/methods were employed and compared: Method M1, based on Velasquez et al.25 using an induction medium with MES/MgCl₂, and Method M2, modified from Chung et al.26 using a citrate-sucrose buffer. Bacterial suspensions (OD600 = 0.3) were infiltrated into the leaves of hydroponically-grown seedlings using a needleless syringe.

Experimental design and plant maintenance

Following agroinfiltration, seedlings were arranged in a factorial design with two main factors: (1) infiltration method and (2) TRV construct type. The TRV constructs were: Mock (infiltration buffer only), Empty TRV vector (pTRV1 + empty pTRV2), and TRV vector containing the pds fragment (pTRV1 + pTRV2-Tbpds390). The combination of these factors resulted in six distinct treatment groups (Table 1). A total of 30 seedlings were used in this experiment, with five biological replicates (n = 5) per treatment group. Plants were maintained in the hydroponic system for two months post-infiltration (mpi) under the same growth conditions described in section "Plant material and growth conditions". Assessment of viral movement and gene silencing

At two months post-infiltration (2 mpi), total RNA was extracted from newly formed, non-inoculated leaves using an optimized CTAB-based protocol specifically developed for Taxus baccata tissues27. The quantity and purity of the isolated RNA were assessed spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop), ensuring A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios were consistently ≥ 2.0. RNA integrity was further verified by visualizing sharp, distinct bands of ribosomal RNA (25S, 18S, and 5S) on a denaturing agarose gel. cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 µg of this high-quality, DNase-treated RNA using reverse transcriptase.

Analysis of systemic viral movement

Systemic movement of TRV was assessed by conventional PCR using specific primers targeting the TRV1 and TRV2 genomic components (TRV1-F: 5′-CTTGAAGAAGAAGACTTTCGAAGTCTC-3′, TRV1-R: 5′-GTAAAATCATTGATAACAACACAGACAAAC-3′; TRV2-F: 5′-CGGGCTAACAGTGCTCTTG-3′, TRV2-R: 5′-CTCCCTTGGTTCGTCGTAAC-3′). PCR reactions were performed in a 25 µL volume containing 1X PCR buffer, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 2 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 µM of each primer, 1 µL of cDNA template (~ 1 ng/µL), and 1.5 U of Taq DNA Polymerase. The thermal profile consisted of an initial denaturation at 94°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 50°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Amplification products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

The primer pairs for TRV1 and TRV2 were validated previously (Hosseini Tafreshi et al., 2012). RT-PCR specificity was confirmed using three controls: TRV plasmid DNA (positive), un-inoculated healthy plant cDNA (negative), and TRV-inoculated leaf cDNA (positive control for reaction efficiency).

Quantitative analysis of gene expression (RT-qPCR)

Potential silencing of the endogenous phytoene desaturase (pds) gene was assessed by RT-qPCR. Gene-specific primers for T. baccata pds (Tbpds) and the 18S rRNA reference gene were designed outside the region cloned into TRV. Primer sequences were: Tbpds-F: 5′-TCCAGCGCCGTTGAATGGAATTTG-3′, Tbpds-R: 5′-AACTCGATCTGGTATGCCCTGCTT-3′; 18S rRNA-F: 5′-TGCTAACTAGCTACGCGGAGGTTT-3′, 18S rRNA-R: 5′-AGATTTCCCGAACCTTTCGGACCA-3′. Reactions were performed in triplicate using Power SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix on a StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System. The thermal profile was: 95°C for 3 min, followed by 50 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 60°C for 20 s, and 72°C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis confirmed specificity. Relative expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with 18S rRNA for normalization and mock-inoculated plants as the calibrator.

Measurement of growth parameters

At 2 mpi, the following growth parameters were measured: plant height (shoot and root length), fresh weight of shoots and roots, and the number of leaves and roots per seedling. For dry weight determination, tissues were dried in an oven at 65 °C until a constant weight was achieved.

Extraction and HPLC analysis of taxoids

Dried shoot and root tissues were ground separately to a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. For taxoid extraction, approximately 50–80 mg of each powdered sample was sequentially extracted twice with 5 mL of methanol under constant agitation (300 rpm) for 24 h at room temperature in the dark. The combined methanol extracts were partitioned against n-hexane to remove lipophilic contaminants, concentrated under vacuum at 50 °C, and the resulting residue was re-dissolved in 500 µL of HPLC-grade methanol. The final extract was filtered through a 0.22 µm PTFE syringe filter prior to injection.

Taxoid quantification was performed by High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) using a Sykam system equipped with a reverse-phase C18 column (Alltech, 250 mm × 4.0 mm) and a UV detector. The column was maintained at 25 °C. A binary gradient of acetonitrile (solvent A) and water (solvent B) was applied at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min as follows: 0–5 min, 35% B (isocratic); 5–15 min, 35% to 25% B (linear gradient); 15–20 min, 25% B (isocratic); 20–30 min, 25% to 20% B (linear gradient); 30–50 min, 20% B (isocratic). Detection was carried out at 227 nm. Commercial standards of paclitaxel (Calbiochem), baccatin III (Sigma), and 10-deacetylbaccatin (Sigma) were used to generate calibration curves (R2 ≥ 0.999) for absolute quantification.

Statistical and machine learning analyses

All growth and metabolic data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). The effects of infiltration method (M1, M2) and TRV construct type (Mock, Empty, pds) on growth parameters and taxoid content were evaluated using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD test for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Gene expression data were analyzed by ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 5.0.

To quantitatively assess the key predictive factors for tissue-specific paclitaxel accumulation and to elucidate the coordination patterns among taxoid metabolites, we employed machine learning approaches on the original experimental dataset (n = 30 samples per metabolite).

Correlation analysis: A comprehensive correlation matrix was constructed using Pearson correlation coefficients to examine relationships among all six measured taxoid metabolites (three in shoot tissues: paclitaxel, baccatin III, 10-deacetylbaccatin; three in root tissues: paclitaxel, baccatin III, 10-deacetylbaccatin). The resulting matrix was visualized as a heatmap.

Feature importance analysis: To identify the relative importance of different factors in predicting paclitaxel accumulation in each tissue, we constructed two separate Random Forest regression models—one for shoot tissue and one for root tissue. For the shoot tissue model, the target variable was shoot paclitaxel content, and the predictor variables were: baccatin III in shoots, 10-deacetylbaccatin in shoots, infiltration method (encoded), and TRV construct type (encoded). For the root tissue model, the target variable was root paclitaxel content, and the predictor variables were: baccatin III in roots, 10-deacetylbaccatin in roots, infiltration method (encoded), and TRV construct type (encoded). This separate modeling approach was designed to test our hypothesis of decoupled tissue-specific regulation.

Both models employed 200 decision trees with a maximum depth of 5 to prevent overfitting. Categorical variables (infiltration method and TRV construct) were encoded using integer labels. Feature importance scores were extracted from each trained model using the Gini importance metric, representing the relative contribution of each variable to the prediction accuracy. Model performance was evaluated using R2 (coefficient of determination), RMSE (root mean squared error), and MAE (mean absolute error).

Model validation and overfitting assessment: To evaluate model robustness and assess potential overfitting, we performed fivefold cross-validation. The dataset was randomly partitioned into 5 equal-sized folds, with each fold used once as validation data while the remaining 4 folds were used for training. This process was repeated 5 times, and performance metrics were averaged across all folds.

All quantitative analyses, including machine learning and metabolic flux calculations, were performed in Python (v3.9) using Scikit-learn (v1.0.2), Pandas (v1.4.0), and NumPy (v1.22.0) libraries, with additional custom scripts for flux ratio analysis.

Metabolic flux ratio analysis

To quantitatively assess the relative enhancement of specific enzymatic steps in the taxoid biosynthetic pathway following TRV elicitation, we performed a metabolic flux ratio (RFR) analysis based on steady-state metabolite concentrations in shoot tissues, where a coherent and upregulated metabolic response was clearly observed28. This approach leverages the principle that, under approximated steady-state conditions, the ratio of product to precursor concentrations provides a proxy for the relative flux through that biochemical conversion.

Consistent with this finding, RT-qPCR analysis revealed no significant reduction in the transcript levels of the endogenous phytoene desaturase (pds) gene in plants infiltrated with the TRV vector harboring the pds fragment (pTRV2-Tbpds390), compared to either mock-inoculated controls or plants receiving the empty TRV vector (Fig. 1C). This confirms that under our experimental conditions, VIGS was not established in T. baccata seedlings. Critically, there was no detectable difference between the two infiltration methods (M1 and M2) in terms of systemic viral movement or the efficiency of gene silencing attempt; both methods failed to facilitate these processes in yew.

Localized hypersensitive response and absence of detrimental growth effects

Despite the lack of systemic infection, all TRV-inoculated plants (both empty and pds-containing vectors) exhibited a clear phenotypic response at the inoculation sites. Distinct necrotic lesions developed specifically where the agroinfiltration was performed (Fig. 2). Visual assessment indicated that the necrotic response appeared more pronounced in plants infiltrated using Method M1 compared to Method M2, suggesting a method-dependent intensity of the local defense reaction. This localized cell death is a hallmark of a hypersensitive response (HR), indicating that T. baccata recognized TRV and activated an effective defense mechanism that contained the virus at the point of entry.

Importantly, this defensive reaction did not compromise overall plant health or development. A comprehensive assessment of growth parameters at 2 mpi showed no significant differences among any treatment groups for shoot height, root length, shoot/root fresh and dry weights, root number, or leaf number (Fig. 3A–H). TRV elicitation significantly enhances taxoid accumulation in shoot tissues

Despite the lack of systemic viral movement or gene silencing, HPLC analysis revealed a profound, taxoid-specific metabolic reprogramming in the aerial tissues of TRV-inoculated T. baccata seedlings. The effect was consistent across all three major taxoids analyzed but varied in magnitude and showed dependence on both the infiltration method and, in some cases, the viral construct.

TRV elicitation significantly enhances taxoid accumulation in shoot tissues

Paclitaxel content in shoots increased significantly (p < 0.05) in all TRV-inoculated plants compared to their respective mock controls, irrespective of the vector construct (empty or pds-containing) or infiltration method (Fig. 4A). However, the elicitation potency was method-dependent. The increase was substantially greater in plants infiltrated using Method M1 than in those treated with Method M2. A similar, significant elicitation pattern was observed for baccatin III (Fig. 4B). All TRV-inoculated plants exhibited higher baccatin III levels than mock controls. Again, Method M1 proved to be a more effective elicitation protocol than Method M2. Notably, within the M1 treatment group, plants inoculated with the pds-containing vector (M1-pds) accumulated significantly more baccatin III than those inoculated with the empty vector (M1-Empty), suggesting a potential synergistic interaction between the viral elicitation and the presence of the specific vector construct under these infiltration conditions.

The response of 10-DAB mirrored that of baccatin III (Fig. 4C). Its content was significantly boosted by TRV inoculation, with Method M1 inducing a stronger response than Method M2. Furthermore, the M1-pds treatment triggered a significantly higher accumulation of 10-DAB compared to the M1-Empty treatment, reinforcing the observation that the pds-containing vector could enhance the elicitation of certain taxoid precursors when delivered via the M1 protocol.

Root taxoid profiles show distinct and tissue-specific trends

In contrast to the consistent upregulation observed in aerial tissues, the taxoid response in roots was highly tissue-specific and displayed more variable trends, indicating a compartmentalized metabolic response to viral elicitation (Fig. 4D–F).

Paclitaxel content in roots exhibited a decreasing trend following TRV inoculation compared to mock controls (Fig. 4D). This reduction was most evident in plants infiltrated using Method M1, where both empty and pds-containing vector treatments led to lower paclitaxel levels. No significant difference was observed between the two vector types within the M1 method. Method M2 induced a milder, non-significant decreasing trend.

The response of baccatin III in roots was strikingly different (Fig. 4E). A significant increase in baccatin III content was detected specifically in the roots of plants infiltrated with TRV vectors using Method M1. Both the empty (M1-Empty) and pds-containing (M1-pds) vectors elicited this positive response to a similar degree. In contrast, Method M2 did not induce any significant change in root baccatin III levels.

The content of the biosynthetic precursor 10-DAB in roots remained remarkably stable across all treatments (Fig. 4F). No statistically significant differences were observed between any TRV-inoculated group (regardless of vector type or infiltration method) and their respective mock controls. This indicates that the viral elicitation did not alter the pool of this specific taxoid intermediate in root tissues under our experimental conditions.

Discussion

TRV fails as a VIGS vector but emerges as a potent biotic elicitor in T. baccata

This study provides the first evidence that while Tobacco rattle virus (TRV) is ineffective as a virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) vector in the gymnosperm Taxus baccata, it functions as a powerful biotic elicitor, significantly enhancing the biosynthesis of valuable taxoid metabolites. Our molecular analyses confirmed the absence of systemic TRV movement and the lack of target gene (pds) silencing, demonstrating that the standard VIGS mechanism is not operational in yew under our conditions. This outcome contrasts sharply with its well-documented efficacy in numerous angiosperm families9,10,12 and directly addresses the previously unknown interaction between TRV and gymnosperms. The key and novel finding, however, is that this incompatible viral interaction triggered a strong local defense response—visually scored as hypersensitive necrotic lesions—and, more importantly, a systemic metabolic reprogramming that led to a substantial increase in paclitaxel, baccatin III, and 10-deacetylbaccatin in aerial tissues. This decouples the virus's role from that of a gene-silencing tool and redefines it as a novel elicitation agent for this medicinally important plant. Importantly, our experimental design included an empty TRV vector control (pTRV1 + empty pTRV2) to distinguish the effects of Agrobacterium infiltration from those of TRV-specific sequences. The observation that both empty and pds-containing TRV vectors similarly enhanced taxoid accumulation (Fig. 4A–C) confirms that the elicitation response is primarily attributable to the viral components or the Agrobacterium-mediated delivery system, rather than the specific pds insert.

T. baccata mounts an effective local defense and systemic signaling

The necrotic lesions observed at inoculation sites, coupled with the absence of viral RNA in distal leaves, indicate that T. baccata activated a robust hypersensitive response (HR) to contain TRV. HR is a well-characterized plant resistance mechanism where localized programmed cell death limits pathogen spread31. Our results align with studies showing that resistant plants can use HR to successfully restrict virus movement32,33. The lack of systemic infection directly explains the failure of VIGS, as systemic viral movement and replication are prerequisites for generating sufficient dsRNA to trigger silencing14. Beyond containment, the HR is a potent initiator of defense signaling.

An integrated view of metabolic reprogramming: machine learning reveals pathway coordination and key drivers

The transition from a localized hypersensitive response (HR) to a systemic enhancement of shoot taxoid levels implies the activation of long-distance signaling networks, likely involving salicylic acid (SA) or jasmonic acid (JA) pathways—key regulators of plant defense and secondary metabolism6,15. To move beyond descriptive statistics and uncover the complex, predictive relationships underlying this response, we integrated a machine learning approach using a Random Forest model34. This quantitative framework powerfully reinforces and extends our biological interpretation, offering novel insights into pathway coordination and the key drivers of taxoid accumulation.

First, the machine learning analysis revealed exceptionally strong positive correlations (r > 0.94) among paclitaxel, baccatin III, and 10-deacetylbaccatin (10-DAB) levels within shoot tissues. This tight coupling is not merely a statistical observation but provides robust, quantitative evidence for the coordinated upregulation of the entire taxoid biosynthetic pathway in aerial tissues. It strongly supports the model that TRV elicitation activates a broad, systemic transcriptional or signaling program, rather than independently modulating individual enzymatic steps. This system-wide metabolic reprogramming is consistent with the established role of SA, a known elicitor of taxoid biosynthesis19, which accumulates during HR and systemic acquired resistance18, and suggests a defense-related hormonal signal orchestrates the pathway.

Second, the Random Forest model provided a quantitative ranking of predictor importance, yielding a nuanced understanding of the factors controlling paclitaxel accumulation. Contrary to what might be expected, the levels of pathway intermediates—specifically baccatin III in shoots—emerged as the strongest predictor of shoot paclitaxel yield (feature importance = 0.645), followed by 10-deacetylbaccatin in shoots (importance = 0.290). The infiltration method and the specific TRV construct type, while significant, showed comparatively lower importance scores (0.036 and 0.028, respectively). This computational insight underscores a critical biological principle: once the initial elicitation signal is perceived, the intrinsic metabolic flux through the pathway—reflected in the pool sizes of key precursors—becomes the primary determinant of the final paclitaxel yield, rather than the specific parameters of the initial trigger. This finding aligns perfectly with the observed tight correlation among shoot taxoids and reinforces the model of a coordinated, systemic upregulation of the entire pathway upon defense activation.

Finally, the decoupled metabolic response between shoot and root tissues was clearly quantified by the model, evidenced by weak or even negative inter-organ correlations (e.g., paclitaxel in shoots vs. roots: r = − 0.96). This highlights the profound tissue-specific nature of the induced defense. The concurrent decrease in root paclitaxel and increase in shoot taxoids under the most effective elicitation protocol (Method M1) may reflect a strategic reallocation of defensive resources or the activation of independent regulatory circuits in different organs. Such spatial complexity in metabolic regulation is a layer of sophistication that traditional statistical analysis alone might not have elucidated as clearly or quantitatively.

Viral elicitation in context: from evolutionary arms race to biotechnological application

Our findings can be interpreted within the broader framework of the continuous evolutionary arms race between plants and viruses, where metabolic reprogramming represents a key biochemical battlefield35. This dynamic mirrors a fundamental principle in host–pathogen interactions across biological kingdoms, wherein pathogens deploy strategies to modulate host processes, and hosts evolve sophisticated countermeasures36. In our study, T. baccata's recognition of TRV and the subsequent HR represent a successful defensive tactic to limit viral spread. The significant, systemic increase in taxoid production reveals an additional, proactive layer of defense: the plant not only contains the local threat but also amplifies its chemical arsenal of phytoalexins throughout its aerial tissues. This aligns with the established concept that pathogen challenge can drive the upregulation of secondary metabolic pathways as a form of induced chemical defense17. Thus, we provide a concrete example of how a controlled viral encounter can be harnessed to trigger a beneficial defensive metabolic shift, amplifying the production of valuable compounds and illustrating a positive outcome in the complex dialogue of plant-virus interactions.

The significant enhancement of taxoid production places TRV among other effective biotic elicitors known for Taxus species, such as fungal cell extracts and culture filtrates5 and the signaling molecule methyl jasmonate4. This strongly supports the classification of taxoids as phytoalexins—antimicrobial compounds whose biosynthesis is upregulated as an integral component of the plant's innate immune response37. From a biotechnological perspective, our discovery that a viral pathogen can act as a potent, growth-neutral elicitor opens a novel and strategic avenue for metabolic engineering. The use of a well-characterized virus like TRV, which is renowned for inducing only mild symptoms even in susceptible hosts and is an established, controllable vector in plant biology7,9, or its defined molecular components, could be developed into a precise elicitation protocol. Its inherent mildness minimizes the risk of severe pathogenicity and catastrophic tissue damage, while still providing a potent defense trigger—functioning analogously to a ‘vaccination’ that primes the plant's metabolic defense systems without compromising viability. Such a protocol could be optimized for established in vitro production platforms, such as cell suspension or hairy root cultures of Taxus species, which are promising systems for industrial taxoid production38,39. The method-dependency of the response, quantitatively benchmarked by our traditional analysis showing a greater increase in shoot paclitaxel with Infiltration Method M1 over M2, provides a crucial and immediately actionable parameter for optimizing and scaling up this novel elicitation strategy.

Decoding a metabolic bottleneck for strategic optimization and biotechnological translation

Our integrated metabolic flux (RFR) analysis provides a critical, system-level insight: TRV elicitation does not uniformly enhance the taxoid pathway but creates a specific late-stage bottleneck in the terminal steps leading from baccatin III to paclitaxel (RFR_Step 2 < 1). This bottleneck likely resides in one or more of the final modifications, which include the attachment of the phenylisoserine side chain by baccatin III: 3-amino, 13-phenylpropanoyltransferase (BAPT), followed by its hydroxylation and N-benzoylation catalyzed by N-benzoyl transferase (DBTNBT)40. This precise localization of the constraint transforms the biotechnological challenge from one of general pathway enhancement into a mission of targeted metabolic engineering focused on these terminal enzymes.

The immediate application of our finding is clear. The optimized TRV-M1 elicitation protocol serves as a powerful tool to maximize the production of baccatin III in established Taxus in vitro cultures. Since baccatin III is the direct precursor for industrial semi-synthesis, our strategy offers a viable route to bolster the supply chain for this critical intermediate.

Furthermore, our work establishes a vital functional link with contemporary breakthroughs in pathway elucidation. The recent comprehensive mapping of the oxidative steps forming the highly oxygenated tetracyclic core skeleton41 defines the complex early-to-mid pathway that our TRV elicitation potently activates. Our identification of the subsequent bottleneck in the final side-chain assembly completes this functional map by pinpointing the next critical barriers for optimization.

This integrated understanding has direct implications for the future of engineered production. For cutting-edge heterologous systems, such as the total biosynthesis of baccatin III in Nicotiana benthamiana42, our results provide pre-validated, high-priority engineering targets. Concerted efforts can now rationally focus on optimizing the expression and activity of BAPT, DBTNBT, and related side-chain supply enzymes in these next-generation biofactories. Thus, our study transcends the demonstration of a novel elicitor; it decodes a principal metabolic constraint and delivers a rational framework for maximizing paclitaxel yield, effectively bridging the gap between native pathway induction and the optimization of synthetic production platforms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that while Tobacco rattle virus (TRV) cannot establish systemic infection or gene silencing in Taxus baccata, it acts as a potent biotic elicitor. The virus triggers a localized hypersensitive response and a systemic, coordinated upregulation of taxoid biosynthesis in shoot tissues, significantly increasing paclitaxel, baccatin III, and 10-deacetylbaccatin III levels without impairing plant growth.

Our integrated machine learning analysis provided quantitative insights into this response, revealing tightly coordinated pathway induction in shoots and identifying pathway intermediate levels as key predictors of paclitaxel yield. The tissue-specific and method-dependent nature of elicitation was clearly quantified, with Infiltration Method M1 proving superior.

These findings establish TRV-mediated elicitation as a novel, growth-neutral biotechnological strategy—akin to a metabolic 'vaccination'—to enhance taxane production in intact Taxus plants. This approach is complementary to recent breakthroughs in microbial and plant-based heterologous synthesis. While our method optimizes the native production machinery, advances such as the recent discovery of the complete baccatin III biosynthetic gene set offer a parallel route for engineered production. Together, these strategies represent a convergent and promising framework for overcoming the long-standing supply challenges of paclitaxel.

While our study establishes TRV as a novel biotic elicitor in Taxus baccata, the mechanistic basis of systemic signaling remains to be elucidated. Future studies should investigate the roles of long-distance signaling molecules such as salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) by quantifying their levels in both local (infiltrated) and systemic (new) leaves following TRV elicitation. Additionally, expression analysis of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins and taxane pathway transcription factors (e.g., TCJAZ, TCMYC) using qRT-PCR would provide deeper insight into the regulatory networks governing systemic metabolic reprogramming. Such mechanistic dissections will complement the phenomenological findings of the present study.
