Helicobacter pylori promotes hepatocarcinogenesis by abrogating the protective effect of intestinal Bacteroides acidifaciens in females
Authors: Qiuyu Jiang, Fulin Nian, Lin Xu, Shengdi Wu, Feng Zhang, Fansheng Meng, Zhixue Chen, Wenqing Tang, Xizhong Shen, Ling Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) exhibits sexual dimorphism, with a lower incidence observed in females. However, the mechanisms underlying the disruption of this protective effect remain inadequately understood. Helicobacter pylori (Hp) is associated with HCC and can cause gut microbiota imbalances that promote HCC progression. This study explored how Hp might influence female susceptibility to HCC via the gut-liver axis, focusing on gut bacteria and their metabolites.MethodsA Hp-infected DEN + CCl₄-induced HCC mouse model was established, and a cohort of 186 HCC patients was analyzed. Fecal metagenomics and serum metabolomics were employed to identify Hp-responsive gut microbes and metabolites. The therapeutic potential of Bacteroides acidifaciens (Ba) and its metabolite 4‑hydroxybenzyl alcohol (4‑HBA), alone or combined with Hp eradication, was evaluated in mouse models and in mechanistic cell-based assays.ResultsHp increased tumor burden and fibrosis especially in female mice. Hp-positive female patients exhibited larger tumors, more advanced disease stages, higher cirrhosis incidence, and poorer overall survival compared to Hp-negative females. Hp also reduced gut microbiota diversity and decreased female-enriched Ba. The Ba-specific metabolite 4-HBA, which is higher in females and reduced by Hp, suppressed TGF‑β/SMAD signaling by binding to TGFBR2, thereby inhibiting hepatic stellate cell activation and HCC cell proliferation. Ba/4-HBA alleviated Hp-induced liver pathology in both sexes, with the combination of Hp eradication and Ba/4-HBA treatment proving more effective than eradication alone in females.ConclusionsHp exacerbates hepatic fibrogenesis and HCC in females by depleting Ba and its metabolite 4-HBA, which inhibits TGF-β/SMAD signaling through binding to TGFBR2. Supplementation with Ba/4-HBA, particularly when combined with Hp eradication, tend to be a promising microbiota–metabolite-targeted strategy for attenuating female HCC progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08590-4.Keywords: Helicobacter pylori, Bacteroides acidifaciens, 4‑hydroxybenzyl alcohol, Hepatic fibrosis, Hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Liver cancer ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with its incidence anticipated to rise to 1.4 million cases by 2040 [1]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which constitutes approximately 80% of primary liver cancers, typically arises in the context of chronic liver disease. HCC exhibits a notable epidemiological characteristic of sexual dimorphism, characterized by a higher incidence and mortality rate in men compared to women [2, 3]. This disparity cannot be attributed solely to behavioral or occupational factors, but is closely linked to sex differences in endocrine, immune, and gut microbial functions [4]. Evidence from animal models and cohort studies indicates that estrogens may exert protective effects by modulating metabolic homeostasis and fostering a more tolerant immune microenvironment [5–7]. Additionally, gut microbiota demonstrates sex-linked variations in composition and metabolic profiles [8], which may further modulate sex-related susceptibility to HCC.

The gut-liver axis acts as a core regulator of liver disease progression. Gut microbiota and their metabolites control intestinal barrier function, mucosal immunity and hepatic stellate cell activation [9, 10]. Targeting the gut microbiota, such as probiotics or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), has shown promise in alleviating liver injury and improving HCC outcomes [11, 12], highlighting the microbiota as a key intervention target. Sex hormones are pivotal regulators of gut microbiota composition, providing a mechanistic basis for sex-specific microecological differences. Clinically, healthy females exhibit enrichment of Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia, and Bacteroides [13, 14], which could contribute to sex-biased protection against HCC.

Helicobacter pylori (Hp), classified as a Group 1 carcinogen, is causally associated with chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric cancer. Notably, accumulating studies have showed sex disparities in Hp infection. Although there is no significant difference in Hp infection prevalence between females and males across all age groups, young females exhibit a lower Hp infection rate than young males in the same age bracket [15]. Such protective effect disappears after menopause, suggesting that estrogen may be involved in the prophylactic mechanism. In Hp-infected models, male mice showed an increase in bacterial load and inflammatory cascade after infection, while female mice had relatively milder gastric lesions [16, 17]. This sex-specific discrepancy may be attributed to a stronger immune response in females, including more efficient phagocytosis and higher CD4⁺ T cell and B cell activity, which contribute to better pathogen control [18, 19]. Hp is traditionally perceived as confined to gastric manifestations, emerging evidence suggests broader systemic effects [20], including diabetes [21], dementia [22], and cardiovascular disease [23]. Previous studies also indicated that Hp infection was significantly related to increased total cholesterol in males [24] and was an important risk factor for carotid atherosclerosis in young males, but not in females [25]. Notably, among patients with HCC, the prevalence of Hp infection was 68.9% in those with hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related HCC and 33.3% in those with HBV-negative HCC, both significantly higher than in control groups (P < 0.001) [26]. However, most evidence remains observational, and whether Hp disrupts female-specific HCC protection via the gut-liver axis remains unknown.

Based on the observed epidemiological indicators and the biological plausibility of the gut–liver axis, we propose the hypothesis that Hp may elevate the risk of HCC through interconnected microecological and metabolic pathways, potentially altering sex-related susceptibility. Consequently, this study synthesizes clinical and mechanistic evidence to evaluate whether Hp-induced gut–liver interactions influence male–female disparities in HCC susceptibility, thereby providing insights for future clinical translation and intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Patient and samples

A total of 186 tumor tissue samples were collected from patients with HCC who underwent surgical resection and were pathologically confirmed at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China) during 2005 to 2010. Based on their history of preoperative 13C-urea breath test results, the samples were categorized into 107 Hp-negative and 79 Hp-positive cases. All tissue samples were then fixed, paraffin-embedded and subsequently processed for staining. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Zhongshan hospital (B2025-442). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the commencement of clinical data analysis. All research was conducted according to both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul.

Propensity score matching (PSM)

PSM was performed on the original dataset consisting of 186 patients (107 Hp-negative and 79 Hp-positive cases). The stratification was conducted by gender: the male cohort included 60 Hp-negative and 39 Hp-positive cases, while the female cohort included 47 Hp-negative and 40 Hp-positive cases. Imbalanced baseline variables (P < 0.05) were balanced using propensity score matching. Specifically, in the female cohort, key variables including T stage, TNM stage, BCLC stage, cirrhosis history, and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) levels were matched, resulting in a paired sample of 17 Hp-negative versus 17 Hp-positive cases. For the male cohort, although no statistically significant differences were observed in the original baseline characteristics (P > 0.05), the identical PSM criteria and procedures were followed for consistency. This process yielded a paired sample of 28 Hp-negative versus 28 Hp-positive cases. After matching, all variables showed P > 0.05 and standardized differences (SMD) < 0.1, indicating adequate covariate balance between groups. Baseline characteristics of before and after PSM analysis were supplemented in Table S1 & S2. All paired samples were used for subsequent immunohistochemistry (IHC) assays and survival analyses.

Animal models

All animals were on a C57BL/6J genetic background. Mice were housed under standard conditions (12 h light–dark cycle) in the specific pathogen-free animal facility of Fudan University, with free access to food and water.

For the diethylnitrosamine (DEN) and carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄)-induced HCC model [27], both male and female mice at 2 weeks of age received a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of DEN (25 mg/kg; Sigma, N0756). Starting at 6 weeks of age, mice were administered CCl₄ (2 mL/kg, i.p.) twice per week for 18 weeks.

For Hp infection [28, 29], 6-week-old mice were fasted for 8–12 h and then injected intraperitoneally with ranitidine (0.25 mg/mouse; MCE, HY-B0693). One hour later, the mice were orally gavaged with Hp strain PMSS1 (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mouse). This procedure was repeated every other day for a total of five administrations. For Hp eradication, a combination of lansoprazole (40 mg/kg [30]; MCE, HY-13662), amoxicillin (28.5 mg/kg [31]; MCE, HY-B0467A), and clarithromycin (14.3 mg/kg; MCE, HY-17508) was administered daily by oral gavage for 2 weeks. Successful Hp infection and eradication in mice were confirmed by the gastric mucosal urease test (red: positive; yellow: negative).

For B. acidifaciens intervention, mice received oral gavage of B. acidifaciens (2 × 10⁸ CFU/mouse) every other day starting at 8 weeks of age for a duration of 16 weeks.

For 4-Hydroxybenzyl alcohol (4-HBA) intervention, mice were orally administered 4-HBA (200 mg/kg) every other day from 8 weeks of age for 16 weeks.

All mice were euthanized at 24 weeks of age for subsequent experimental analyses.

Cell culture

Human HCC cell lines (Huh-7 and MHCC97-H) and the human hepatic stellate cell line LX-2 were acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 16140071) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma, 381074), and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Additional reagents used in the study included recombinant human TGF-β1 (Procell, PCK354) and Bacteroides acidifaciens (Mingzhoubio, B98300).

Other materials and methods

Details of histological staining, immunohistochemical staining (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF), western blot, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), RNA extraction and quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR), CCK‐8 and colony formation assays, cell viability assay and drug molecular docking analysis are described in the Supplementary materials and methods.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized with means and SDs or medians and IQRs. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent group t-tests compared means of normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normal data. One-way ANOVA compared multiple groups, using Dunnett’s or Tukey’s test for independent data. Two-way ANOVA compared more than two groups and variables, followed by Sidak’s test. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables, and Spearman correlation analysis for correlation analyses. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to create survival curves and the log-rank test was used to compare survival distributions. Statistical significance was determined by a p-value < 0.05. Data analysis and visualization were done using R version 4.1.0.

Results

Hp infection exacerbates hepatic fibrogenesis and HCC progression with a female bias

We established a primary HCC model in both male and female mice using DEN in combination with CCl4, followed by a superimposed two-week Hp infection (Fig. 1A). The colonization of Hp was verified through the rapid urease test (Fig. S1A). In comparison to the control group, Hp infection significantly exacerbated the HCC burden, as demonstrated by an increase in tumor count, larger tumor volumes, and a higher number of Ki67-positive cells within the tumors. This pro-tumorigenic effect was particularly pronounced in female mice (Fig. 1B). Consistently, Hp infection accelerated the progression of liver cirrhosis during HCC development, as indicated by intense Masson and Sirius red staining, which correlated with elevated levels of the fibrosis marker α-SMA and the fibrosis inducer TGF-β, as observed in IHC staining. This heightened susceptibility was similarly observed in female mice (Fig. 1C, D; Fig. S1B). These results indicated that Hp infection abolishes the inherent protective effect of females against HCC and fibrosis, resulting in comparable malignancy and fibrosis severity between females and males after infection.

To evaluate clinical concordance, we first conducted analysis involving 186 patients with HCC, comprising 79 Hp-positive (Hp+) and 107 Hp-negative (Hp-) individuals. The presence of Hp infection was generally correlated with increased tumor size (T stage, P = 0.045), more advanced TNM (P = 0.013) and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage (P = 0.022) and a higher incidence of cirrhosis (P = 0.005). (Table 1). Stratified analyses revealed that these associations were statistically significant only in female patients, characterized by larger tumor size (T stage, P = 0.004), more advanced TNM (P < 0.001) and BCLC stage (P = 0.018), a higher incidence of cirrhosis (P = 0.003) and elevated gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) levels [55 (40.75, 85.75) vs. 40 (29.5, 60.5), P = 0.008], but not in male patients (Tables 2 and 3). The differing effects of Hp on males in animal models versus clinical samples likely stem from differences in study design (interventional vs. retrospective); the complex causes of human HCC (like HBV [32, 33] and NAFLD) with unrecorded variables; variations in gut microbiota [34–36]; Hp strain virulence; genetics among humans, unlike the controlled conditions in experimental mice and so on. To further eliminate the influence of confounding factors and solely investigate the effect of Hp on HCC, we performed Propensity Score Matching (PSM) separately for the male and female cohorts. Ultimately, we obtained 34 patients (17 Hp- and 17 Hp+) in the female cohort and 56 patients (28 Hp- and 28 Hp+) in the male cohort, respectively (Table S1-S2). We further evaluated the impact of Hp infection status on patient prognosis. The results showed that Hp+ patients exhibited lower overall survival (OS) [HR = 2.24 (1.20–4.18), P = 0.008] and this trend was particularly pronounced in female patients [HR = 3.41 (1.19–9.72), P = 0.015], whereas no significant effect was observed in male patients [HR = 1.74 (0.80–3.78), P = 0.151]. Histopathological examination of clinical specimens consistently indicated heightened expression of α-SMA and TGF-β in association with Hp infection. Furthermore, qPCR analyses of murine liver specimens corroborated the increased mRNA expression of fibrosis-related markers (Acta2, Timp1 and Col1a1), with these effects being more pronounced in females (Fig. 1F; Fig. S1B, C). Collectively, these findings indicate that Hp exacerbates liver cirrhosis and HCC initiation with a clear female bias.

Hp infection alters the gut microbiota and reduces key taxa linked to protective effects in females

Based on the aforementioned observations, we conducted a further investigation into the mechanisms by which Hp mediates these effects. Our initial hypothesis posited that Hp might directly infiltrate the liver and disrupt its microenvironment. Nevertheless, immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. S2A) revealed an absence of Hp in hepatic tissues across all groups, indicating that its pathogenic effects are more likely mediated indirectly through extrahepatic mechanisms.

Building on existing evidence that implicates the gut microbiota in the progression of HCC and the perturbations in intestinal ecology induced by Hp, we conducted metagenomic profiling to elucidate variations in microbial community structure. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a distinct separation in the gut microbial compositions of male and female mice in the uninfected state, highlighting significant baseline sex-related differences. However, following Hp infection, the microbial community structure in female samples exhibited a substantial shift, resulting in a convergence of post-infection distributions between males and females, thereby eliminating the previously observed sex-based clustering (Fig. 1G; Fig. S3A). This observation is substantiated by the Simpson diversity index, which indicates that Hp reduces the diversity of gut microbiota in both female and male mice (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these findings suggest that Hp infection reduces gut microbiota diversity and alters the female gut microbial composition to resemble a male-like configuration. This remodeling diminishes sex-specific microecological differences, consequently undermining the protective effects typically observed in females and resulting in a similar susceptibility to HCC.

In further analysis of the differences in microbial composition between male and female subjects within the control group at the genus level, it was observed that Akkermansia, Parabacteroides and Bacteroides were significantly enriched in females (LDA > 4, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1I). These genera have been identified as potential female-protective taxa, corroborating findings from previous studies [37–39]. To elucidate the core microbiota alterations induced by Hp infection, we conducted a comparative analysis of pre- and post-infection profiles within each sex and subsequently intersected the results. This approach identified 19 taxa consistently influenced by Hp infection (Fig. 1J). Among these, the genera Phocaeicola, Bacteroides, Duncaniella and Muribaculum were prioritized due to their consistent pre- to post-infection trajectories across both sexes in response to Hp infection (Fig. 1K). The intersection of three differential sets revealed a sex-divergent core microbiota, comprising the Bacteroidaceae family, the Bacteroides genus and the species Bacteroides acidifaciens (Ba), which exhibited a higher baseline abundance in females compared to males but experienced a significant decline following Hp infection (Fig. 1L–M). Taken together, these findings indicate that Helicobacter pylori infection may reduce the prevalence of the female-enriched bacterium Bacteroides acidifaciens, thereby attenuating its protective effects against hepatic fibrosis and tumorigenesis. This reduction may ultimately contribute to a more pronounced pathogenic impact in females.

Hp decreases Bacteroides acidifaciens and its metabolite 4‑hydroxybenzyl alcohol to facilitate HCC progression

To investigate the potential involvement of Ba in the observed sex-biased protective effect, we administered Ba during the establishment of the Hp-infected female murine HCC model (Fig. 2A). The presence of Hp colonization was verified using the rapid urease test (Fig. S4A). Administration of live Ba via oral gavage, as opposed to heat-killed Ba, significantly inhibited Hp-induced fibrosis and tumor proliferation (Fig. 2B, C; Fig. S4B, C), suggesting a viability-dependent mechanism. In line with these findings, co-culturing TGF-β–stimulated LX-2 cells with live Ba or Ba supernatant resulted in a significant downregulation of multiple fibrogenic markers at both protein and mRNA levels, whereas heat-killed Ba did not produce similar effects (Fig. 2D, E). Furthermore, live Ba and Ba supernatant were observed to suppress viability, colony formation, and proliferation in LX-2 and HCC cell lines (Huh7 and 97 H), while heat-killed Ba exhibited no significant impact (Fig. 2F-H). Considering the extensive alterations in metabolic pathways indicated by metagenomic analysis (Fig. S3B), we propose that Ba-derived metabolites, rather than the bacterial components, are responsible for mediating its anti-fibrotic and anti-tumor activities.

We conducted widely targeted metabolomics analysis of serum using a similar grouping and intersection methodology as applied in our metagenomics study, leading to the identification of 19 distinct metabolites (Fig. 3A, B). Among these, only four metabolites—4-Hydroxybenzyl alcohol (4-HBA), 2-Pentyl-3-phenyl-2-propenal (ACA), Eicosanoyl-EA (EEA), and 5-Acetylamino-6-amino-3-methyluracil (AAMU)—exhibited a pattern analogous to that of Ba, characterized by higher baseline levels in females compared to males and a significant reduction following Hp infection (Fig. 3C). Further correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations (Cor: 0.43–0.64, P < 0.05) between these four serum metabolites and both Bacteroides and Bacteroides acidifaciens (Fig. 3D), indicating a potential functional relationship.

To identify a specific metabolite derived from Ba, we conducted a comprehensive profiling of the Ba culture medium using a widely targeted metabolomics approach. Our analysis revealed a significant elevation of 4-HBA (Fig. 3E, F) but not ACA, EEA or AAMU, suggesting its potential role as a Ba metabolite. Subsequent in vitro assays demonstrated that all four candidate metabolites inhibited the TGF-β-induced fibrotic phenotype in LX-2 cells (Fig. 3G, H; Fig. S5). Notably, 4-HBA uniquely exhibited an additional capacity to inhibit the viability and proliferation of both LX-2 and HCC cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3I–K; Fig. S6–S8). Collectively, these findings implicate 4-HBA as the principal Ba-derived effector responsible for mediating anti-fibrotic and anti-tumor effects. It is posited that Hp infection may lead to a reduction in Ba abundance and 4-HBA levels, thereby diminishing female-specific protection and facilitating the progression of HCC.

4-HBA competitively binds to TGFBR2 to inhibit the TGF‑β/SMAD pathway and mitigate HCC progression

Expanding upon this protective mechanism, we administered 4-HBA orally after Hp infection in female murine HCC models (Fig. 4A). The presence of Hp colonization was verified using the rapid urease test (Fig. S9A). In comparison to the control group, 4-HBA significantly mitigated the tumor burden and pathological deterioration induced by Hp. This was evidenced by a reduction in both tumor number and volume, as well as an improvement in hepatic fibrosis (Fig. 4B, C; Fig. S9 B, C).

To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we first observed that fibrosis and tumor phenotypes exhibited parallel changes across various interventions: both conditions were exacerbated following Hp infection and mitigated following interventions with Ba or 4-HBA. TGF‑β/SMAD signaling pathway is a well-established and central mediator of hepatic fibrosis [40, 41] and a promoter of tumor cell proliferation [42–44]. The binding of TGF‑β to its receptor TGFBR2 is a critical step in the initiation of downstream signaling. This binding induces the formation of a receptor complex, leading to the phosphorylation of TGFBR2 and the subsequent phosphorylation of SMAD2/3, thereby initiating transcriptional programs that promote fibrosis and proliferation. Drug molecular docking analysis demonstrated that 4-HBA can competitively occupy the binding site of TGFBR2, even when TGF-β is already bound to the receptor (Fig. 4D). The binding energy was calculated to be -5.287 kcal/mol, indicating a stable interaction. Furthermore, Co-IP assays revealed that the addition of 4-HBA attenuated the interaction between TGF-β and TGFBR2, suggesting that 4-HBA can disrupt the formation of the TGF-β/TGFBR2 complex (Fig. 4E). Additionally, both in vivo and prior in vitro experiments demonstrated that live Ba, Ba supernatant, and 4-HBA inhibited the activation of the TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway, as evidenced by reduced levels of phosphorylated TGFBR2 (S225) and phosphorylated SMAD2 (Ser465/467)/SMAD3 (Ser423/425) (Fig. 4H-I). Collectively, these findings indicate that 4-HBA competitively binds to TGFBR2, thereby weakening TGF-β-induced receptor activation. This results in the suppression of SMAD2/3 phosphorylation and downstream pro-fibrotic and pro-proliferative pathways, ultimately counteracting the pro-carcinogenic effects associated with Hp infection.

Combining Hp eradication with Ba or 4-HBA offers greater therapeutic benefits for females

We next evaluated the independent effects of Ba or 4-HBA on HCC progression in the absence of Hp (Fig. 5A). In both female and male murine HCC models, Ba or 4-HBA alone mitigated tumor burden and hepatic fibrosis (Fig. 5B–D; Fig. S10 A, B). Mechanistically, both Ba and 4-HBA significantly suppressed TGF‑β/SMAD signaling activation, with decreases in p‑TGFBR2 and p‑SMAD2/3 (Fig. 5E), consistent with the mechanism described above. Given the more pronounced pathogenic impact of Hp in females, we compared Hp eradication therapy versus its combination with Ba/4-HBA in Hp-infected female HCC models (Fig. 5F). The rapid urease test was used to validate Hp colonization and Hp eradication (Fig. S10C). Triple therapy (Lansoprazole + Amoxicillin + Clarithromycin) alone achieved only partial suppression of hepatic fibrosis and HCC progression. However, adding Ba or 4-HBA on top of triple therapy produced greater therapeutic gains—fewer and smaller tumors and milder hepatic fibrosis (Fig. 5G–I; Fig. S10D, E). These findings suggest that, after removing the persistent pathogenic stimulus from Hp, supplementing Ba or 4-HBA further reinforces inhibition of TGF‑β/SMAD signaling and achieves deeper phenotypic relief. Therefore, while antibiotics in triple therapy help Hp eradication, they may transiently perturb gut microbial structure and function, delaying recovery of endogenous protective taxa and their metabolic activities. Exogenous Ba or 4-HBA can serve as a bridging strategy to accelerate restoration of anti‑fibrotic/anti‑tumor microecological pathways, thereby delivering superior therapeutic benefit on top of eradication.

Discussion

The global incidence of HCC is on the rise, positioning it among the foremost causes of cancer-related mortality. Although females exhibit lower overall incidence and mortality rates compared to males, this disparity is not a static, intrinsic characteristic but rather a dynamic result influenced by endocrine, immune, and gut microbial–metabolic networks. In this study, a retrospective clinical cohort demonstrated that female patients who tested positive for Hp exhibited a propensity for larger tumor volumes, more advanced disease stages and adverse serological profiles. Meanwhile, Hp infection induces a cascade of changes in the upper gastrointestinal tract, disrupting gut microecology and specifically depleting Ba—which is inherently enriched in the female intestine and closely linked to protective phenotypes—along with a significant reduction in its key metabolite, 4-HBA. Under normal physiological conditions, 4-HBA competitively binds to TGFBR2, attenuating TGF-β-induced SMAD2/3 phosphorylation. The Hp-mediated depletion of Ba and 4-HBA removes this inhibitory mechanism, thereby enhancing TGF-β/SMAD signaling, exacerbating hepatic fibrosis and increasing tumor burden. Collectively, these findings suggest that Hp-related gut-liver interactions may contribute to sex-specific susceptibility to HCC, highlighting the Ba–4-HBA–TGFBR2 pathway as a critical mechanism.

Previous research on the influence of Hp on HCC risk has considered several mechanisms, including direct hepatic colonization [45, 46], the delivery of bacterial products or outer membrane vesicles to the liver through the circulatory system, and systemic immune and metabolic reprogramming induced by gastric inflammation [33, 47–49]. In our study, we did not detect Hp in liver tissues, suggesting that direct intrahepatic colonization is not the predominant mechanism in our findings and supporting the hypothesis of an indirectly mediated pathway. The urease activity of Hp and the gastritis it induces can disrupt gastric acid secretion and pH homeostasis [28, 50], thereby altering the filtering and delivery functions of the upper digestive tract for microbes and metabolites. However, no study has yet addressed the sex-specific differences in this context. Notably, this study provides novel evidence that Hp infection diminishes the baseline sex divergence in gut microbiota. These observations support the notion that Hp undermines female‑biased resistance to HCC by reshaping the gut microbiome. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that Hp may also affect liver pathogenesis via CagA/VacA [51], outer membrane vesicles [52], or hit-and-run immune reprogramming [53]. Future studies using high‑sensitivity tracing tools are warranted to clarify the dynamic distribution of Hp and its bioactive products in the gut–liver axis.

Female mice exhibited significantly higher baseline Ba abundance than male mice, which was markedly reduced upon Hp infection. However, the upstream drivers of the female-enriched Ba phenotype, especially the role of sex hormones in this axis, remain to be elucidated. Estrogen signaling has been reported to enrich Bacteroides and maintain microbial stability, whereas estrogen deficiency leads to dysbiosis and reduced levels of beneficial commensals [54]. Bacteroides, including Ba, express β-glucuronidases that deconjugate estrogens to their active forms, sustaining a positive feedback loop [55]. This loop may contribute to the female-enriched Ba profile under physiological conditions, and its disruption by Hp could represent an early event in the loss of female-specific hepatic protection. Functionally, Ba is a commensal bacterium with a well‑tolerated physiological profile. It has been implicated in mucosal homeostasis, bile acid metabolism, energy regulation, and anti‑inflammatory responses [39, 56, 57]. Recent studies reported that Ba ameliorates alcohol‑related liver injury via bile acid remodeling and ammonia detoxification, and suppresses hepatic steatosis by regulating intestinal fructose metabolism [39, 58]. Here, we newly identified 4‑HBA as a functional metabolite of Ba that inhibits hepatic fibrosis and HCC cell proliferation. 4‑HBA is a naturally occurring phenolic metabolite derived from microbial metabolism or dietary components and has been safely used in food additives [59], which supports its favorable translational potential and clinical safety. Previous studies mainly focused on its antioxidant [60], anti‑inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties [61, 62], including attenuating acute pancreatitis via inhibiting p38/NF‑κB signaling [63] and protecting against ischemic brain injury [62]. This study is the first to demonstrate that 4‑HBA mitigates hepatic fibrosis and HCC progression by competitively targeting TGFBR2 and suppressing TGF‑β/SMAD signaling. This mechanism provides an organic link between sex differences, microecological metabolism, and the canonical fibrosis/proliferation pathway, offering an explanation for the disruption of female-specific protective effects against HCC under Hp exposure.

While guideline-based Hp eradication continues to be a priority in managing Hp infection, short-term antibiotic and acid-suppressive therapies may hinder the recovery of gut microecology and metabolic function, disadvantaging anaerobic commensals such as Ba. Our recent findings may inspire several translatable strategies: optimizing the antibiotic spectrum during eradication to minimize the collateral impact on anaerobic commensals; combining prebiotics to promote the re-establishment of the Ba niche; utilizing 4-HBA to mimic the effects of microecological recovery; and intensifying follow-up and safety monitoring in female patients. However, several limitations should be acknowledged before clinical translation. Due to the small size of our clinical sample set, our conclusions should be considered preliminary and multi‑center and larger‑scale clinical validation is required to investigate the potential therapeutic value of Ba and 4-HBA in hepatic fibrosis and HCC before clinical translation. Additionally, animal studies were conducted in mice with a homogeneous genetic background and controlled environment, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human gut microbiota and disease etiology. Further mechanistic and clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings and optimize sex‑specific therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

Hp infection accelerates hepatic fibrogenesis and the progression of HCC by altering the gut microbiota and depleting the female-enriched commensal bacterium Ba and its metabolite, 4-HBA. Mechanistically, 4-HBA competitively binds to TGFBR2, thereby attenuating the activation of SMAD2/3 and subsequently inhibiting HSCs activation and tumor cell proliferation. Supplementation with viable Ba or 4-HBA significantly mitigates fibrosis and tumor burden, and combination therapy based on Hp eradication is more effective than eradication alone. Collectively, these findings provide preclinical evidence supporting an association between Hp infection and enhanced HCC risk in females, and reveal a potentially promising exploratory strategy for intervention.
