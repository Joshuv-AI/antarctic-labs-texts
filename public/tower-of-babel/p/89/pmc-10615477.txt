Single-cell transcriptome analysis reveals the key genes associated with macrophage polarization in liver cancer
Authors: Zhenghua Ding, Zhongming Deng, Hengping Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:The aim of this study was to reveal the key genes associated with macrophage polarization in liver cancer.Methods:Data were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and the Cancer Genome Atlas databases (TCGA). R package Seurat 4.0 was used to preprocess the downloaded single-cell sequencing data, principal component analysis, and clustering. R package SingleR was used to annotate cell types and calculate macrophage polarization scores. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to obtain key genes highly correlated with macrophage polarization in liver cancer. The Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource algorithm was used to analyze the correlation between genes and the infiltration level of macrophages. Finally, the prognostic model was constructed based on 6 macrophage polarization-related genes by multivariate Cox regression analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves and receiver operating characteristic curves validated the prognostic value of the prognostic model.Results:Two thousand highly variable genes were obtained after the normalization of single-cell profiles. In all, 16 principal components and 15 cell clusters were obtained. Monocytes and macrophages were the main immune cells in the microenvironment of liver cancer tissues. Macrophage polarization scores showed that cluster 5 had the highest degree of polarization. Spearman analysis yielded that a total of 6 key genes associated with macrophage polarization (CD53, TGFBI, S100A4, pyruvate kinase M, LSP1, SPP1), and Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource analysis showed that 6 key genes were significantly positively correlated with macrophage infiltration levels. The model constructed by 6 key genes could effectively evaluate the prognosis of patients with liver cancer.Conclusions:The key genes associated with macrophage polarization, namely CD53, TGFBI, S100A4, pyruvate kinase M, LSP1, and SPP1, may be potential therapeutic targets for liver cancer.

INTRODUCTION

As the fifth most common cancer globally, primary liver cancer emerged as the second leading cause of cancer-related death. HCC is the most common type of primary liver cancer followed by intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. These 2 histological types add up to 95% of primary liver malignancies.1 Hepatitis, cirrhosis, smoking, obesity, diabetes, and various dietary patterns are well-established risk factors for liver cancer.2 Surgical resection, liver transplantation, radiotherapy, and local administration of chemotherapeutic drugs are the main options for liver cancer treatment.3 Due to the insidious onset of liver cancer and lack of specific biomarkers at the early stage, patients are frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage of liver cancer and missed the best time for surgery. Consequently, the overall survival time of patients with liver cancer is short, and the prognosis is poor.4 Currently, transcatheter arterial chemoembolization is an effective treatment for advanced liver cancer, but its therapeutic effect is not ideal due to the compensatory vascular proliferation of the hypoxic tumor tissue after surgery.5 Sorafenib, the mainstream drug used for targeted therapy of advanced liver cancer, has been proven to significantly prolong the median survival of patients in clinical observation. Nonetheless, the targeted drug sorafenib exhibits effectiveness in less than one-third of individuals. Also, 6 months after the start of treatment, patients’ tumors are prone to drug-resistant mutations, resulting in decreased therapeutic effects. Therefore, further studies on the pathogenesis of liver cancer are still needed to provide more therapeutic targets.6 Existing surgical therapies and chemotherapies cannot effectively improve outcomes in patients with liver cancer. Consequently, the identification and development of novel therapeutic targets are crucial to address this medical challenge.

As myeloid cells, macrophages are crucial in the innate immune response. Apart from phagocytosis and exogenous antigen presentation, macrophages are involved in immune regulation through cytokine and growth factor secretion.7 Macrophage polarization pertains to the activation state of macrophages in a specific environment. Polarization is not fixed and can be altered by multiple signals, such as those from microbes, damaged tissues, and the normal tissue environment.8 During wound healing in vivo, macrophages are polarized toward proinflammatory M1 phenotype during the early phase, which induces secretion of interleukins IL-12 and IL-23, and is capable of presenting antigens to inhibit cell proliferation and cause tissue damage. Macrophages then are polarized into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in the later healing phase, assisting inflammatory resolution and wound healing by producing mediators such as angiogenic TGF-β and VEGF.9 Tumor-associated macrophages have been found to participate in cancer cell latency, growth, and metastasis by secreting cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. Dan et al10 found that, in oral squamous cell carcinoma, RACK1 represses macrophage recruitment and alters the M1/M2 ratio to influence tumor development and metastasis. Also, M2 macrophage upregulation is associated with poor prognosis. Zhu et al11 showed that exogenous DLX6-AS1 from HCC induced M2 macrophage polarization, which promoted HCC migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Based on the proven association between macrophages and liver cancer development, it is of great significance to investigate how macrophage polarization-related key genes inhibit the development of liver cancer.

In the present work, hepatectomized liver cancer tissues were assessed using single-cell RNA-sequencing to identify cell types in liver cancer tissues. Key genes related to macrophage polarization were also identified to provide a theoretical basis for the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies for liver cancer.

METHODS

Data download

From the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database, a single-cell profile GSE146409 (annotation platform: GPL21697) of liver cancer microenvironment was downloaded. The profile was taken from liver cancer tissues excised from 6 patients including a total of 7947 cells, of which 4140 cells were from malignant sites and 3807 cells from nonmalignant sites. The nonmalignant sites did not show histological signs of fibrosis. The reference data set GSE135165 (annotation platform: GPL26999) was subsequently downloaded as the reference data for macrophage polarization. The data set contained data from 6 monocyte-polarized and 5 macrophage-polarized samples. mRNA expression data (normal: 50, tumor: 374) as well as clinical data for HCC were from the Cancer Genome Atlas database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/).

Single-cell cRNA-sequencing analysis

Using the R package Seurat (https://www.github.com/satijalab/seurat), single-cell sequencing counts were processed. The samples were filtered if gene content was greater than 50 (min. features=50) in a single cell and 3 cells contained the same gene (min. cells=3). Data were log-normalized according to default settings. Highly variable genes were identified using the function Find Variable Features of the Seurat package for principal component analysis. Based on the results of the JackStraw Plot and Elbow functions, the first 16 principal components were used for further downstream analysis. Clustering was performed using Seurat Find Clusters function (resolution=0.5). The t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) method was used to visualize the calculated clusters. The FindAllMarkers function was used to find differentially expressed genes in each cell type as its biological marker. Cells were annotated with the R package SingleR.12 Previously, calculated clusters were annotated for cell types based on alignments and known as cell markers.

Calculation of macrophage polarization fraction

Cells annotated as “Monocytes” and “Macrophages” cell types were selected for the calculation of macrophage polarization fraction. The selected single-cell transcriptome gene expression data were compared with cell line data of monocytes (THP-1) and macrophages (differentiated from THP-1) in the reference data set GSE135165 using the R package SingleR to obtain automatic annotation markers and polarization scores for each cell.

Screening macrophage polarization-related key genes

The marker genes of macrophage clusters with higher polarization scores were selected. Spearman correlation analysis of marker gene expression levels and polarization scores was manipulated to obtain key genes highly correlated with macrophage polarization in liver cancer (p<0.05, cor>0.3). At the same time, the heatmap of marker gene expression in normal and tumor tissues was drawn by the R package heatmap.

Correlation between key genes and macrophage infiltration

Spearman correlation analysis of the selected key genes and macrophage infiltration levels was performed through the Online Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource (TIMER) (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/).

Construction and validation of the prognostic model based on macrophage polarization-related key genes

The aforementioned selected key genes were used as candidate genes, and RNA-sequencing data of key genes in the Cancer Genome Atlas-liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) data set were employed to build a multiple regression model using the R package. Using this model, the risk score of individuals was calculated with the expression and risk coefficient of each gene on hand. We split samples into high-risk and low-risk groups by the median risk score. The survival curve was plotted according to the risk score of the samples. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the timeROC package to calculate the AUC values for the 1-, 3-, and 5-year ROC curves. The score and survival status distribution and feature gene heat maps were then plotted for the 2 risk groups.

Independent prognostic factor analysis

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses that combined clinical information and prognostic model risk scores were performed on the samples to draw the corresponding forest plots. The rms package was used to draw nomograms that predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates. Corresponding calibration curves were drawn to verify the predictive performance of nomograms and determine whether the model could serve as an independent prognostic factor or not. In addition, an ROC curve was drawn to evaluate the performance of the nomogram combined with clinicopathological features.

Based on the Cancer Genome Atlas database, the mRNA expression levels of 6 feature genes in normal and tumor tissues were analyzed, and the significance was calculated by the Wilcox test. The protein expression of 6 feature genes in normal and tumor tissues was downloaded from the HPA (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) database.

Tumor microenvironment analysis

We employed the ESTIMATE algorithm to assess differences in tumor microenvironment between the high-risk and low-risk groups, and the results were visualized using violin plots. Five algorithms (CIBERSORT, EPIC, MCP, TIMER, and xCell) in the IOBR R package were used to analyze the tumor immune microenvironment in the 2 risk groups, and the box plot was used to show the situation. The expression of majorhistocompatibility complex (MHC) and immune checkpoint molecules was presented using box plots. p<0.05 represented a significant difference between the 2 risk groups.

RESULTS

Cell fraction analysis of liver cancer tissue

We analyzed single-cell profiles from hepatectomized tissues of 6 patients with liver cancer. Two thousand highly variable genes were selected after the normalization of single-cell profiles for dimensionality-reduction principal component analysis. According to the results of JackStraw and Elbow (Figure 1A, B), we selected the top 16 principal components for cell classification. Fifteen cell clusters (Figure 1C) yielded after cell clustering were annotated. The results showed that there were 8 main immune cell types (monocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, epithelial cells, CD8+ T cells, adipocytes, and B cells) in the microenvironment of liver cancer tissues (Figure 1D). Among them, 1488 monocytes and 1291 macrophages, accounting for 18.72% and 16.25% of the total cells, respectively, emerged as the dominant immune cells in the microenvironment of liver cancer tissues. We also found that the 2 cell types are dominant in liver cancer tissues.

(C) t-SNE visualization of liver cancer tissue microenvironment. (D) Cell clusters annotation results. Abbreviations: PC, principal components; t-SNE, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding.

Calculation of macrophage polarization score

Next, we selected monocytes and macrophages for analysis and compared them to cell line data containing monocytes (THP-1) and macrophages (differentiated from THP-1) in the reference data set. Referring to macrophage polarization scores, we found that macrophages in cluster 5 were most polarized (Figure 2; Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A608), indicating that these cells may be highly polarized macrophage groups. Genes that determine macrophage polarization scores are presented in Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A609. Macrophages are associated with a variety of cancers, and the levels of M1/M2 are an important indicator of cancer prognosis.13 Therefore, modulating macrophage polarization levels may represent a target for liver cancer treatment.

Visualization of macrophage polarization score.

Identification of macrophage polarization-related key genes

As per the previous analysis, we used data mining of cluster 5 cells for key genes associated with macrophage polarization. Cluster 5 had a total of 588 cells and 227 marker genes were identified (Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A609). We analyzed the expression of these 227 marker genes in normal and liver cancer tissues, and the results showed that the expression levels of these genes were different in normal and cancer tissues (Figure 3A). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted of the expression of these marker genes and the corresponding macrophage polarization scores. A total of 6 key genes [CD53, TGFBI, S100A4, pyruvate kinase M (PKM), LSP1, SPP1] associated with macrophage polarization were obtained (Figure 3B). To verify the relationship between these 6 genes and macrophage immune infiltration, we performed Spearman correlation analysis using TIMER. The results showed that CD53, TGFBI, S100A4, PKM, LSP1, and SPP1 expression levels were all significantly positively correlated with the infiltration level of macrophage (Figure 4). Therefore, we speculated that CD53, TGFBI, S100A4, PKM, LSP1, and SPP1 could promote macrophage infiltration by affecting the polarization level of macrophages and then affect the progression of liver cancer.

Construction of prognostic models based on macrophage polarization-related genes in tumors

A prognostic model based on 6 macrophage polarization-related feature genes (CD53, TGFBI, S100A4, PKM, LSP1, SPP1) was constructed by multivariate Cox regression analysis (Figure 5A). The formula is as follows: risk score=−0.1051S100A4 + 0.1664PKM + 0.1144SPP1−0.1026TGFBI−0.0662LSP1−0.1013CD53 (Supplemental Table S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A610). Subsequently, survival curves were plotted for these 6 feature genes. Significant correlations were found between the expression levels of PKM and SPP1 and survival status (Figure 5B–G).

Construction of a prognostic model based on macrophage polarization-related genes in tumors. (A) Forest plot of multivariate regression analysis of 6 feature genes. (B–G) Survival analysis based on feature genes. Abbreviation: PKM, pyruvate kinase M.

Validation of the prognostic model

Based on the above prognostic model, ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were plotted with AUC values of 0.75, 0.7, and 0.68, respectively (Figure 6A). With the median risk score as the cutoff point, samples were assigned into high-risk and low-risk groups. Subsequently, survival analysis was performed to draw survival curves. It was found that the high-risk group had a lower survival rate (Figure 6B). In addition, the distribution of risk score and survival status (Figure 6C, D), as well as the heatmap of expression levels of the 6 feature genes, was plotted for the high-risk and low-risk groups (Figure 6E). We noticed that, as the risk score increased, the number of deaths of patients increased and the survival time declined. SPP1 and PKM expression levels were higher in high-risk patients.

Independent prognostic factor analysis

To verify whether risk score could be independently used as a prognostic marker, we used univariate Cox regression analysis, which included risk score and clinical information, and it showed that stage, T, and risk score were all crucial factors (p<0.001) (Figure 7A). Subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that only risk score was a significant factor (p=0.004) (Figure 7B), indicating that risk score could be a prognostic factor independent of clinical features. Referring to clinical information, the nomogram of the model was plotted to predict the patient’s survival (Figure 7C). The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year calibration curves indicated that the predicted survival of the model fitted well with the actual survival (Figure 7D–F). The ROC curve showed that the risk score had a higher AUC value (AUC=0.6463) compared with other clinical features except for stage, indicating that the risk score had a better prognostic performance. In addition, compared with risk score, the nomogram had the highest AUC value (AUC=0.7313), which indicated that our nomogram based on risk score and other clinical features had good diagnostic performance (Figure 7G). Based on the above results, the 6 feature gene risk prognostic model for liver cancer patients could be used as an effective independent prognostic factor.

We also analyzed the expression of these macrophage polarization-related feature genes in normal and tumor tissues of liver cancer. The results showed that except for S100A4 and LSP1, the expression of the other 4 feature genes in normal and tumor tissues was significantly different (p<0.05) (Figure 7H, I).

Analysis of immune microenvironment

In order to explore the differences in tumor microenvironment between the 2 risk groups, we first performed ESTIMATE analysis, with outcomes showing that patients in the low-risk group had a significantly higher immune score, stromal score, ESTIMATE score, and significantly lower tumor score (p<0.05) (Figure 8A). Next, the immune infiltration of the 2 groups of patients was analyzed using 5 algorithms (CIBERSORT, EPIC, MCP, TIMER, and xCell) in the IOBR R package. Most of the low-risk groups had a higher degree of immune cell infiltration than the high-risk groups (p<0.05) (Figure 8B–F). Finally, we explored the expression of MHC and immune checkpoint molecules, which showed that most low-risk groups had significantly higher expression levels of MHC class I (HLA-E, HLA-C, HLA-F) and MHC class II molecules (HLA-DQB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DRB5) (p<0.05) (Figure 8G). The expression levels of most immune checkpoints in the low-risk group were also significantly higher than those in the high-risk group (p<0.05) (Figure 8H). Interestingly, MHC class II molecules, key macrophage polarization markers of the M1 population (Figure 8G), and CD163, representing the M2 population (Figure 8H), were both expressed at higher levels in the low-risk group. In conclusion, the level of immune infiltration was relatively higher in the low-risk group.

DISCUSSION

Despite the advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for patients with liver cancer made by ongoing research into the pathogenesis, the clinical outcomes remain poor.14 The limited efficacy of current liver cancer treatment has made it a serious global health problem. Therefore, it is important to find biomarkers related to liver cancer for the diagnosis and treatment of patients with liver cancer.

We analyzed single-cell profiles from hepatectomized tissues of 6 patients with liver cancer and identified 8 cell types (monocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, epithelial cells, CD8+ T cells, adipocytes, B cells) in the microenvironment of liver cancer tissues. Monocytes and macrophages are the 2 predominant cell types. Zhang et al15 found by using single-cell RNA-sequencing that tumor-associated macrophages and dendritic cells were the 2 main immune cells in the tumor microenvironment of liver cancer. Another single-cell analysis of primary and early recurrent HCC samples showed that macrophages were more enriched in tumors compared to adjacent nontumor tissues.16 Similar to the above findings, liver macrophages are the largest population of innate immune cells in the liver. Previous literature suggested that tumor-associated macrophages can produce a variety of chemokines that attract regulatory T-cells to cancer sites and hinder cytotoxic T-cell activation, thereby inhibiting antitumor responses.17 From the above, macrophages are the predominant immune cells within the microenvironment of liver cancer and play an imperative role. In our study, MHC class II molecules, a key macrophage polarization marker of the M1 population, and CD163, a key macrophage polarization marker representing the M2 population, were simultaneously highly expressed in the low-risk group (p<0.05). This illustrated a higher level of macrophage polarization in the low-risk group of patients. However, the correlation between the specific degree of macrophage polarization and the risk for patients needs to be further explored.

Macrophage polarization is influenced by tumor stage and varies between tumors or regions within tumors. Tumor progression in liver cancer is thought to be related to macrophage polarization.18,19 Based on the previous analysis, we screened 6 key genes (CD53, TGFBI, S100A4, PKM, LSP1, SPP1) associated with macrophage polarization by Spearman correlation analysis of expression of marker genes from highly polarized macrophage groups and corresponding macrophage polarization scores. CD53 is a member of the tetraspanin superfamily expressed exclusively within immune compartments. CD53C plays a crucial role in immune processes as it is involved in regulating immune cell adhesion and migration.20 Reportedly, CD53 is a central gene associated with tumor immunity, and its high expression is concerned with a better prognosis in patients with liver cancer.21 TGFBI is a conserved connective tissue protein involved in extracellular matrix formation and features in tumor growth, metastasis, and immunosuppression.22 In the early stage of cancer, TGFBI, which can foster chemosensitivity, is considered a tumor suppressor. However, in the middle and advanced stages of cancer, TGFBI greatly promotes tumor progression. Elevated TGFBI is associated with poor clinical outcomes. As an important regulator of cancer progression, TGFBI levels may be biomarkers of chemoresistance and tumor progression.23 Mainly derived from macrophages, S100A4, also known as fibroblast-specific protein 1, is a member of the large S100 protein family. It is able to bind and regulate cytoskeletal components, thereby promoting metastasis and invasion of tumor cells.24 Sun et al25 found that S100A4 downregulation is significantly associated with a promising prognosis in patients with liver cancer. PKM, also known as pyruvate kinase M, is divided into 2 subtypes. PKM1 is expressed in adult tissues to facilitate oxidative phosphorylation, while PKM2 is commonly expressed in embryonic and tumor tissues, promoting aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells.26 Overexpression of PKM2 promotes the proliferation of liver cancer cells by enhancing aerobic glycolysis and bears some relation with a poorer prognosis.27 LSP1 is an F-actin binding protein expressed in lymphocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils that regulates fibrinogen adhesion, neutrophil motility, and transendothelial migration.28 Koral et al29 discovered from mice experiments that loss of LSP1 expression and function leads to hepatocyte proliferation and promoted the development and metastasis of liver cancer. SPP1 is a secreted multifunctional phosphoprotein that mainly participates in immune responses and is also involved in cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and chemotaxis.30 SPP1 is overexpressed in a variety of cancers and can be used to predict adverse outcomes. For example, Gao et al31 found that SPP1 is a high-risk cancer gene, and its high expression is linked with poor survival in liver cancer. In summary, the 6 macrophage polarization-related genes selected here had a tight relationship with liver cancer and may be potential therapeutic targets for liver cancer.

In conclusion, our study investigated immune cells in the microenvironment of liver cancer tissues by analyzing single-cell profiles from hepatectomized liver cancer tissues. 6 key genes associated with macrophage polarization in liver cancer were screened to construct a prognostic risk assessment model. Hopefully, this study could dawn on the development of novel therapeutic targets and strategies for liver cancer. However, our results obtained by bioinformatics analysis have some limitations that need to be validated by further experiments.
