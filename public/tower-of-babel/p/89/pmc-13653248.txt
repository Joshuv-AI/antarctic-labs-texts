Targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry profiling of arginine and histidine derivatives in chronic migraine: Discriminatory performance and changes after onabotulinumtoxinA therapy
Authors: Seyma Dumur, Kadidiatou Diarra, Joudy Sewidan, Julie Jalila Kalmoni, Demet Aygun, Dildar Konukoglu, Hafize Uzun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was conducted to characterize plasma arginine, histidine, 1‐methylhistidine, and 3‐methylhistidine in chronic migraine and to evaluate their discriminatory ability and association with early clinical response to onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT‐A).BackgroundAlterations in nitric oxide‐ and histamine‐related metabolic pathways may contribute to chronic migraine biology and may provide circulating biomarkers for disease stratification and treatment monitoring. Targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry profiling enables precise quantification of candidate amino acids and derivatives implicated in these pathways.MethodsThis retrospective secondary analysis was conducted as a case–control study with an embedded uncontrolled pre‐post analysis and included 30 adults with chronic migraine receiving BoNT‐A and 30 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls from Istanbul Atlas University Hospital, based on clinical and laboratory data collected between May 15, 2023, and December 15, 2024. Plasma arginine, histidine, 1‐methylhistidine, and 3‐methylhistidine were quantified by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. In the chronic migraine group, pain intensity (visual analog scale [VAS]) and monthly migraine attack frequency were assessed at baseline and 1 month after BoNT‐A. Between‐group comparisons and within‐subject pre‐post comparisons were performed, and exploratory analyses evaluated relationships between metabolite levels and clinical outcomes.ResultsAt baseline, compared with controls, patients with chronic migraine had higher plasma arginine (57.3 ± 13.8 vs. 35.9 ± 10.1 μmol/L; p < 0.001) and 3‐methylhistidine (1.0 ± 0.4 vs. 0.7 ± 0.4 μmol/L; p = 0.004), and lower histidine (51.7 ± 10.2 vs. 61.5 ± 6.8 μmol/L; p < 0.001); 1‐methylhistidine did not differ significantly (p = 0.217). After BoNT‐A, median VAS decreased from 8.0 (7.0–9.0) to 2.0 (0.0–3.0) (p < 0.001) and median monthly migraine attack frequency decreased from 11.0 (6.0–14.8) to 1.0 (0.0–1.8) (p < 0.001). Histidine increased from 54.10 μmol/L (45.27–59.81) to 60.23 μmol/L (57.87–64.45); p = 0.003) whereas 3‐methylhistidine decreased from 0.85 μmol/L (0.69–1.23) to 0.66 μmol/L (0.59–0.83); p < 0.001). 1‐Methylhistidine did not change significantly, with median values of 5.39 μmol/L (3.41–14.28) at baseline and 6.84 μmol/L (3.36–15.33) at month 1 (p = 0.658). Arginine also did not change significantly with median values of 56.74 μmol/L (46.56–65.95) at baseline and 52.83 μmol/L (44.22–66.30) at month 1 (p = 0.248). Change in histidine was not associated with ΔVAS, and exploratory regression analyses did not identify a robust independent association between baseline variables and pain reduction. In receiver operating characteristic analyses, histidine (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.79) and 3‐methylhistidine (AUC = 0.71) showed moderate discrimination, whereas arginine showed higher apparent discrimination (AUC = 0.91). An exploratory combined biomarker model incorporating arginine, histidine, and 3‐methylhistidine showed higher apparent discrimination (AUC = 0.94; leave‐one‐out cross‐validated AUC = 0.92).ConclusionChronic migraine is associated with an arginine‐histidine metabolic signature characterized by elevated arginine and 3‐methylhistidine and reduced histidine. Early response to BoNT‐A is accompanied by substantial clinical improvement and selective modulation of histidine‐related metabolites. Histidine and 3‐methylhistidine may have value as candidate biomarkers within a broader chronic migraine stratification framework, warranting validation in larger prospective cohorts. Keywords: 3‐methylhistidine, arginine, biomarkers, chronic migraine, Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, onabotulinumtoxinA

INTRODUCTION

Chronic migraine is a highly prevalent neurological disorder that substantially restricts quality of life and imposes a major socioeconomic burden through disability and loss of productivity. 1 Its prevalence is commonly reported at ~15%, with a marked female predominance affecting approximately one in four females and one in 10 males. 1 Clinically, migraine is characterized by recurrent attacks of predominantly unilateral, throbbing headache, frequently accompanied by nausea and/or vomiting and photo‐/phonophobia. Symptoms may worsen with routine physical activity. 2 , 3

The pathophysiology of migraine is multifactorial and is closely linked to interactions among the trigeminovascular system, meninges, and cranial vasculature. 4 Current evidence supports the combined contribution of genetic and environmental factors, neurotransmitter and cytokine signaling, cranial vascular dynamics, hypothalamic dysfunction, trigeminovascular abnormalities, and neurogenic inflammation. 4 Within contemporary models, cortical hyperexcitability and brainstem structures that facilitate attack initiation have gained particular emphasis, helping to explain both the heterogeneous clinical spectrum and the variability in treatment responses. 5 , 6

Although therapies targeting the calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP) pathway represent a major advance in migraine management, a substantial proportion of patients do not achieve adequate benefit and most available options are not disease‐modifying; relapse after treatment discontinuation remains a clinical challenge. 7 , 8 These limitations reinforce the need to further elucidate core biological mechanisms and to develop more effective and durable therapeutic strategies grounded in pathophysiology. 4 , 9 , 10

Botulinum neurotoxins, produced by Clostridium botulinum, comprise of multiple serotypes (A–G) and subtypes. 11 , 12 Among them, onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT‐A) is an established therapy for chronic migraine, with proposed mechanisms including modulation of neurotransmitter/neuropeptide release, alterations in receptor and cytokine surface expression, and enhancement of opioidergic transmission. 13 Experimental and clinical observations suggest that BoNT‐A may reversibly reduce exocytosis of mediators such as substance P, CGRP, and glutamate in peripheral sensory neurons, thereby suppressing peripheral sensitization and indirectly attenuating central sensitization. 14 , 15 However, the extent to which BoNT‐A is accompanied by systemic biochemical or metabolic changes and how these changes relate to clinical response remain insufficiently defined.

In this context, metabolomics offers a powerful framework to identify biochemical pathways implicated in migraine and to discover biomarkers that track a therapeutic response. Amino acids are particularly relevant as neurotransmitter precursors, components of energy metabolism, and modulators of vascular regulation, all of which are central to nervous system function. 16 Arginine is the substrate for nitric oxide (NO) synthesis; NO has been implicated as a mediator capable of triggering or sustaining migraine attacks, and it has been hypothesized that BoNT‐A may indirectly influence NO‐related pathways through effects on synaptic release processes. 17 Histidine is the precursor of histamine, which is also involved in migraine biology. Methylated histidine derivatives (1‐methylhistidine and 3‐methylhistidine) may reflect aspects of histamine metabolism and/or muscle protein breakdown and could act as indicators for systemic reflections accompanying peripheral/local BoNT‐A effects. 18 , 19 Moreover, links between arginine/histidine metabolism, NO bioavailability, and immune responses provide a biologically plausible interface with inflammatory processes associated with migraine. 20

In this study, we aimed to characterize plasma arginine, histidine, 1‐methylhistidine, and 3‐methylhistidine levels in patients with chronic migraine compared with healthy controls and to examine their changes 1 month after BoNT‐A treatment in relation to clinical outcomes. We also explored the discriminatory ability of selected metabolites and their association with early treatment response.

We hypothesized that plasma concentrations of arginine, histidine, 1‐methylhistidine, and 3‐methylhistidine would differ between patients with chronic migraine and healthy controls, and that selected metabolites would change in parallel with early clinical improvement following BoNT‐A treatment.

METHODS

Study design and ethics

This study was a retrospective secondary analysis of previously collected, ethics‐approved clinical records and plasma liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) data derived from a parent study. The underlying study design was a case–control study with an embedded uncontrolled pre‐post analysis, comprising a comparison of patients with chronic migraine and healthy controls together with a within‐subject assessment of changes after BoNT‐A treatment in the chronic migraine group. It does not represent the primary analysis of the parent data set. Rather, the present work focused on arginine, histidine, 1‐methylhistidine, and 3‐methylhistidine, which had been measured as part of the LC–MS/MS analyte panel in the parent study but were not reported in the previous publication from that project. 1 The previous report focused on other neurochemical markers and study questions, including substance P, GABA, 5‐HT, glutamate, and glutamine, whereas the present study was undertaken retrospectively to examine previously unreported amino acid and metabolite panel components in relation to a new research question. The analytic framework comprised a between‐group comparison of patients with chronic migraine and healthy controls, together with an embedded uncontrolled pre‐post analysis in the chronic migraine group following BoNT‐A treatment.

Ethical approval for this retrospective study was obtained from the Istanbul Atlas University Non‐Interventional Scientific Research Ethics Committee (approval no. E‐22686390‐050.99‐90,295; approved on January 26, 2026). The data analyzed in the present study originated from an ethics‐approved parent study for which written informed consent was obtained at the time of original participation and sample collection. The current work represents a retrospective secondary analysis of de‐identified repository data; no additional participant contact or intervention occurred, and renewed consent was not required for the present use of de‐identified data.

Study setting and data source

The data analyzed in this study were derived from the parent study conducted at Istanbul Atlas University Hospital, and covered the period from May 15, 2023, to December 15, 2024. LC–MS/MS data sets were obtained from the data repositories of the relevant academic units at Istanbul Atlas University and the collaborating laboratory data center. Clinical and laboratory records were matched using coded study identifiers derived from the parent data set, and all data were de‐identified prior to analysis in accordance with confidentiality principles. Demographic variables, including age and sex, were used solely for statistical purposes. Healthy controls were recruited from the same institutional setting as the chronic migraine group at Istanbul Atlas University Hospital. Control participants had no history of migraine, frequent headache, or known chronic disease and were enrolled on a voluntary basis, including during routine health evaluation. Matching to the chronic migraine group was performed at the group level (frequency matching) for age and sex, rather than by strict individual matching.

To improve characterization of the study groups, additional baseline variables available in the retrospective source data set were reviewed, including body mass index, serum urea, and serum creatinine. These variables were reviewed to provide additional metabolic and renal context for interpretation of circulating amino acid levels. However, fasting status, dietary protein intake, physical activity level, and detailed medication exposure were not available in a sufficiently standardized form in the retrospective data set and therefore could not be formally incorporated into the present analyses. This is particularly relevant for interpretation of 3‐methylhistidine, which may be influenced by dietary meat intake and muscle protein turnover.

Clinical outcome ascertainment

Clinical outcomes in the chronic migraine group were assessed retrospectively from the available follow‐up documentation within the parent clinical data set. Pain intensity (VAS) scores and monthly migraine attack frequency were extracted from routine clinical records, including clinician‐documented patient reports at follow‐up visits, headache diary‐based information where available, and corresponding database entries when applicable. These measures were not prospectively standardized specifically for the present metabolomic analysis.

Eligibility criteria

The present retrospective secondary analysis included only patients with chronic migraine and healthy controls derived from the parent study conducted at Istanbul Atlas University Hospital between May 15, 2023, and December 15, 2024. Chronic migraine diagnosis in the source cohort was established according to the ICHD‐3 criteria. For the current study, the migraine group was restricted to patients with chronic migraine who had received BoNT‐A treatment and had available LC–MS/MS data for arginine, histidine, 1‐methylhistidine, and 3‐methylhistidine. Healthy controls had no history of migraine or frequent headache.

Patients in the source cohort received BoNT‐A according to the PREEMPT protocol, consisting of a fixed dose of 155 U administered at 31 injection sites in the frontal, temporal, occipital, and cervical regions of the head and neck. Clinical reassessment was performed at 1 month after treatment.

Clinical exclusion criteria in the parent cohort included age <18 years, pregnancy, acute pain medication use within the preceding 24 h, current use of migraine prophylaxis medication, invasive procedures involving the skin of the head and face within 6 months, antiplatelet or anticoagulant use, acute or chronic sinusitis, cranial bone defects, relevant neurological or dermatological disease, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, use of a hearing aid or cochlear implant, and active ear infection. For the present secondary analysis, additional exclusion criteria included incomplete or technically inadequate LC–MS/MS raw data, inability to reliably detect the targeted metabolites, low signal‐to‐noise ratios, and records with clear technical artifacts likely to compromise analytical validity.

The parent data set comprised 31 chronic migraine cases and 30 healthy controls. For the present secondary analysis, the final analytic data set included 30 patients with chronic migraine and 30 healthy controls; one chronic migraine case was not included because complete LC–MS/MS data for the analytes of interest were not available.

LC–MS/MS analysis and raw data processing

LC–MS/MS quantification of plasma arginine, histidine, 1‐methylhistidine, and 3‐methylhistidine was performed using a Thermo Scientific LC–MS/MS platform with a commercial amino acid assay from RECIPE Chemicals + Instruments GmbH (München, Germany). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Accucore hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography analytical column (internal diameter 3 mm; Thermo Scientific). The mobile phase consisted of 90/10 acetonitrile/100 mM ammonium formate (pH 3). Before sample injection, the analytical system was equilibrated for 10 min at a mobile‐phase mixing ratio of 88:12 and a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min.

All target analytes were measured in positive electrospray ionization mode using analyte‐specific multiple reaction monitoring transitions. The quantifier transitions were m/z 156 → 110.2 for histidine, 175 → 70.3 for arginine, 170.1 → 126.2 for 1‐methylhistidine, and 170.1 → 124.1 for 3‐methylhistidine. Isotope‐labeled internal standards included arginine‐13C6, histidine‐13C6, 15N3, and lysine‐13C6,15N2. Internal‐standard assignment was as follows: arginine with arginine‐13C6, histidine with histidine‐13C6,15N3, and both methylhistidine analytes with lysine‐13C6,15N2. Reported retention times were 11.3 min for arginine, 14.3 min for histidine, 15.9 min for 1‐methylhistidine, and 16.1 min for 3‐methylhistidine.

Calibration was performed using ClinCal 6‐level plasma calibrators (levels 0–5), and internal quality assurance used ClinChek plasma controls (levels I and II). Internal standards were prepared using ClinMass Internal Standard IS. Calibration samples and controls were reconstituted and prepared in the same manner as patient plasma samples. The assay showed validated linearity ranges of 10–500 μmol/L for histidine, 5–600 μmol/L for arginine, 5–500 μmol/L for 1‐methylhistidine, and 1–5 μmol/L for 3‐methylhistidine. The limit of detection (LOD)/limit of quantification (LOQ) values were 3.40/9.8 μmol/L for histidine, 1.50/5.1 μmol/L for arginine, 1.40/5.3 μmol/L for 1‐methylhistidine, and 0.5/1.2 μmol/L for 3‐methylhistidine.

Analytical precision was reported as <6.2% intra‐assay coefficient of variation and <5.5% inter‐assay coefficient of variation for the studied parameters. Quality‐control procedures included at least three matrix‐blank injections at the start of each analytical series, repeated test injections until two consecutive chromatograms with comparable retention times and peak areas were obtained, use of freshly prepared controls in each run, and exclusion of analytically unreliable measurements on the basis of signal‐to‐noise and technical quality criteria. Method validation included assessment of linearity and recovery in spiked matrices; LOD was defined at a signal‐to‐noise ratio of 3, and LOQ at a signal‐to‐noise ratio of 5. Interlaboratory variation was assessed through participation in the European Research Network for Evaluation and Improvement of Screening, Diagnosis and Treatment of Inherited Disorders of Metabolism quantitative external quality‐control scheme.

Detailed analytical characteristics of the four target analytes, including multiple reaction monitoring transitions, internal standards, retention times, linearity ranges, and LOD/LOQ values, are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Exploratory robust linear regression using the Huber M‐estimator was performed in R using the Modern Applied Statistics with S package (version 7.3–58.1). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, including area under the curve (AUC) estimation and confidence interval calculation, were performed in R using the package for ROC curve analysis (version 1.18.0). Internal validation of the exploratory combined ROC model was performed using leave‐one‐out cross‐validation. All tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables were evaluated for distributional assumptions using the Shapiro–Wilk test, supported by inspection of histograms and quantile–quantile plots and by skewness–kurtosis indices. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non‐normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range). For paired pre–post comparisons, the choice between paired‐samples t‐test and Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was based on the distribution of the within‐subject differences. Given the distributional characteristics of the paired clinical and metabolite outcomes, these pre–post comparisons were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test.

Baseline demographic and metabolite differences between chronic migraine and healthy controls were assessed using independent samples t‐tests for normally distributed variables or the Mann–Whitney U‐test for nonparametric data. Within the migraine group, pretreatment and 1‐month post–BoNT‐A outcomes were compared using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 test or Fisher exact test when expected cell counts were small.

To facilitate clinical interpretation, change scores for clinical outcomes were defined so that improvements yielded positive values:

ΔVAS = VAS_baseline − VAS_1‐month, and

ΔVAS = VAS_baseline − VAS_1‐month, and

ΔAttack frequency = Attacks_baseline − Attacks_1‐month.

ΔAttack frequency = Attacks_baseline − Attacks_1‐month.

For biochemical variables, the direction of change was preserved:

ΔHistidine = Histidine_1‐month − Histidine_baseline, and

ΔHistidine = Histidine_1‐month − Histidine_baseline, and

Δ3‐Methylhistidine = 3‐methylhistidine_1‐month − 3‐methylhistidine_baseline.

Δ3‐Methylhistidine = 3‐methylhistidine_1‐month − 3‐methylhistidine_baseline.

Associations between metabolite levels (baseline and/or Δ) and clinical outcomes (VAS, attack frequency, ΔVAS, ΔAttack frequency) were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficients (ρ) due to the small sample size and the potential for non‐normal distributions.

The discriminatory ability of selected plasma metabolites (e.g., histidine and 3‐methylhistidine) to distinguish chronic migraine from healthy controls was assessed using ROC curve analysis. AUC values were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Optimal cut‐off values were determined using the Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity values were reported.

The multivariable regression models were exploratory and intended to assess associations rather than to develop or validate a prediction model. To explore associations between selected baseline variables and post‐treatment pain improvement (ΔVAS), multivariable robust linear regression was conducted using the Huber M‐estimator to reduce the influence of outliers and potential heteroscedasticity in a small sample. Candidate covariates were selected based on biological and/or clinical plausibility together with univariable exploratory associations. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor, with variance inflation factor <2.0 considered acceptable. Model assumptions were additionally evaluated using residual‐versus‐fitted plots to assess linearity and homoscedasticity and quantile–quantile plots of residuals to assess departures from normality.

No formal a priori statistical power calculation was conducted for the present study. Because this work represents a retrospective secondary analysis of an existing parent data set, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible chronic migraine cases and matched healthy controls with available clinical follow‐up data and LC–MS/MS measurements for the analytes of interest.

The present study used a complete‐case analysis approach. No missing data remained in the final analytic data set for the variables included in the analyses.

A total of 30 patients with chronic migraine (age, 38.9 ± 9.9 years; female, n = 23, 77%) and 30 healthy controls (age, 38.7 ± 10.8 years; female, n = 18, 60%) were included. There were no significant between‐group differences in age (p = 0.935) or sex distribution (p = 0.267). At baseline, compared with controls, patients with chronic migraine had lower plasma histidine (p < 0.001) and higher plasma arginine (p < 0.001) and 3‐methylhistidine (p = 0.004). Plasma 1‐methylhistidine did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1). At 1 month after BoNT‐A treatment, marked clinical improvement was observed. Median VAS decreased from 8.0 (7.0–9.0) at baseline to 2.0 (0.0–3.0) at month 1 (p < 0.001), and median monthly migraine attack frequency decreased from 11.0 (6.0–14.8) to 1.0 (0.0–1.8) (p < 0.001). DISCUSSION

In this targeted LC–MS/MS metabolomics study, chronic migraine was associated with a distinct circulating amino acid signature involving the arginine–histidine axis. Compared with age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls, patients with chronic migraine exhibited higher plasma arginine and 3‐methylhistidine and lower histidine, whereas 1‐methylhistidine did not differ. Following BoNT‐A treatment, patients demonstrated marked clinical improvement at 1 month, accompanied by an increase in histidine and a decrease in 3‐methylhistidine, whereas arginine did not change significantly. Together, these findings support the concept that chronic migraine is accompanied by measurable systemic metabolic differences and that effective preventive therapy may be associated with partial normalization of selected components of this signature.

Migraine is increasingly understood as a disorder of complex brain–peripheral network dysfunction involving trigeminovascular activation, neurogenic inflammation, and neuromodulator signaling. 4 , 5 , 6 Within this framework, the observation of higher arginine in chronic migraine may reflect altered arginine‐related metabolism. However, circulating arginine alone is insufficient to infer nitric oxide (NO) pathway activation, as NO biology is regulated by multiple interacting processes beyond substrate availability. Although NO has established relevance to migraine pathophysiology, including cranial vasodilation and trigeminovascular sensitization, the present data do not permit direct conclusions regarding NO production or bioavailability. 6 , 17 Elevated circulating arginine may instead reflect altered substrate availability, compensatory changes in arginine metabolism, or broader shifts in immune–metabolic regulation relevant to chronic migraine endotypes. 16 , 20 Future studies incorporating complementary markers such as asymmetric dimethylarginine, citrulline, and nitrite/nitrate would help clarify the relationship between arginine metabolism and NO‐related biology in migraine.

Conversely, the finding of lower histidine in chronic migraine, together with higher 3‐methylhistidine, points to dysregulation in histidine‐related pathways that intersect with inflammatory and neuromodulatory processes. Histidine is the precursor of histamine, and histaminergic mechanisms have long been considered relevant to migraine pathophysiology through effects on vascular tone, neuroinflammatory signaling, and nociceptive processing. 16 , 18 , 19 3‐Methylhistidine is widely interpreted as a marker of protein turnover; therefore, its elevation in chronic migraine may reflect increased catabolic or inflammatory burden associated with chronic pain states or repeated attacks. However, the specificity of this marker is limited, as circulating levels may also be influenced by dietary protein intake and broader systemic metabolic factors. 18 , 19 In addition, 1‐methylhistidine is known to be influenced by dietary intake, particularly meat consumption, 19 which limits its specificity as a marker of endogenous metabolic activity. Importantly, our findings support the concept of a coordinated metabolic pattern rather than reliance on a single analyte, which may be more consistent with the emerging view of chronic migraine as a complex systemic disorder with heterogeneous metabolic alterations. 4 , 5 , 6 , 21

A key finding of this study is that biochemical shifts in the histidine axis accompanied early clinical response to BoNT‐A. BoNT‐A is an established preventive therapy for chronic migraine, with proposed mechanisms including inhibition of peripheral release of nociceptive mediators (e.g., CGRP, substance P, and glutamate) and downstream attenuation of peripheral and central sensitization. 13 , 14 , 15 The rise in histidine and reduction in 3‐methylhistidine 1 month after BoNT‐A may reflect decreased neurogenic inflammatory drive and/or reduced systemic metabolic stress in parallel with symptom improvement. Notably, arginine did not change over this interval, suggesting that early benefit from BoNT‐A may be more closely coupled to histidine‐associated pathways (or their systemic correlates) than to circulating arginine availability itself, or NO‐related effects occur at local neurovascular sites not captured by plasma arginine concentration. 13 , 14 , 15 , 17

Exploratory regression analyses did not identify any variable with a robust independent association with pain reduction (ΔVAS) after treatment. Although baseline metabolic variation may still reflect biologically relevant heterogeneity in chronic migraine, the present data do not support firm multivariable conclusions regarding treatment responsiveness. Likewise, the absence of a significant association between ΔHistidine and ΔVAS suggests that short‐term biochemical change did not closely parallel clinical improvement in this cohort. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the limited sample size relative to the number of candidate covariates increased the risk of model instability and overfitting. Accordingly, the regression analyses should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than as providing stable inferential or predictive evidence.

In ROC analyses, histidine (apparent AUC of 0.79) and 3‐methylhistidine (apparent AUC of 0.71) showed moderate discriminatory ability between patients with chronic migraine and healthy controls. These findings indicate moderate discrimination and suggest that these metabolites may have value within a broader biomarker‐based stratification framework. An exploratory combined biomarker model including arginine, histidine, and 3‐methylhistidine showed higher discrimination than the single‐analyte models, with an apparent AUC of 0.94 and a leave‐one‐out cross‐validated AUC of 0.92. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the analysis compared patients with chronic migraine with healthy controls rather than with clinically relevant comparator groups, such as episodic migraine or other chronic headache disorders. This design likely inflates apparent discriminatory performance relative to real‐world diagnostic settings. Accordingly, the present ROC analyses do not establish clinical utility, and validation in larger prospective cohorts including diagnostically relevant comparator populations will be necessary before any clinical application can be inferred.

We also explored subgroup differences according to sex and smoking status. Males showed a greater reduction in VAS than females, whereas changes in attack frequency and histidine did not differ significantly by sex. Smoking status was not associated with clinical or biochemical outcomes. The sex‐stratified analyses were exploratory in nature and should be interpreted cautiously, as the limited number of male participants (n = 7) reduces the precision and stability of subgroup estimates and restricts the generalizability of the observed differences.

This study has several strengths, including targeted LC–MS/MS quantification, inclusion of an age‐ and sex‐matched control group, and a paired pre/post framework enabling within‐subject assessment after BoNT‐A. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study sample was relatively small, and the analysis was retrospective in nature, limiting both statistical precision and causal inference. Second, although the chronic migraine cohort underwent pre/post assessment, the study did not include a parallel comparator group for treatment response; therefore, the observed changes in clinical outcomes and circulating metabolites cannot be confidently distinguished from regression to the mean, natural disease fluctuation, contextual/placebo effects, or other nonspecific temporal influences. Third, although the source cohort was based on clinical diagnoses established according to ICHD‐3 criteria, the present study relied on existing clinical records and repository data, and any inaccuracy in the source classification could introduce misclassification bias, potentially affecting both the case–control and treatment–response analyses. Fourth, the follow‐up interval was limited to 1 month, capturing only a very early phase after BoNT‐A administration; thus, the observed biochemical shifts may reflect early pharmacological effects or transient biological fluctuations rather than durable treatment‐associated metabolic remodeling. In addition, although body mass index, serum urea, and serum creatinine were available and provided additional metabolic and renal context for the study groups, other potentially important determinants of amino acid metabolism, including fasting status, dietary protein/meat intake, physical activity, and detailed medication exposure, were not captured in a sufficiently standardized manner in the retrospective data set. This limitation is particularly relevant for 3‐methylhistidine, which may reflect not only disease‐related biology but also variation in dietary meat intake and muscle protein turnover. Finally, because the ROC analyses compared patients with chronic migraine with healthy controls rather than clinically relevant comparator headache populations, the observed discriminatory performance may be inflated and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical diagnostic utility.

In conclusion, chronic migraine was associated with an arginine–histidine metabolic signature characterized by elevated arginine and 3‐methylhistidine and reduced histidine, and early BoNT‐A response was accompanied by increases in histidine and decreases in 3‐methylhistidine alongside substantial clinical improvement. Exploratory regression analyses did not identify a robust independent association between baseline variables and pain improvement.

Future investigations should extend this work by evaluating multi‐metabolite biomarker panels, integrating arginine–histidine signatures with CGRP‐related markers and other biologically relevant readouts, and assessing metabolite dynamics over longer follow‐up periods after BoNT‐A treatment. Validation in larger prospective cohorts with clinically relevant comparator groups and standardized pre‐analytical controls will be necessary to determine whether these signals can contribute meaningfully to chronic migraine stratification and treatment monitoring.
