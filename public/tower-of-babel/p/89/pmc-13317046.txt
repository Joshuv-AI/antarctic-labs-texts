Relationship of career growth with job satisfaction- a study in military hospitals in the kingdom of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Amal Bany Ateah, Ahmad Aboshaiqah, Ahmad M. Rayani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesCareer growth, includes continuous training, mentorship, and professional development, helps improve performance, leading to reduced stress, and improved job satisfaction. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship of career growth with job satisfaction and other demographic and work-related characteristics among nurses working in Military Hospitals in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted over a period of 3 months in 2024 among nurses working in Military Hospitals in Saudi Arabia. Data were collected using a structured, self-administered online questionnaire composed of three main sections. The first section gathered demographic and professional information. The second section assessed career growth using the Career Growth Scale for Nurses (CGSN), which consists of three dimensions divided in to 17 items. The third section evaluated job satisfaction across three domains (18 items). Responses were rated on a Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), with higher scores indicating stronger perceptions of career growth. Correlation and ANOVA were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 27.ResultsA total of 370 nurses responded to the study. Most of them were between 31 and 40 years of age (58.9%), 88.6% were female, 37.3% were Saudi nurses, 29.7% had 6–10 years of experience, and 62.2% held a bachelor’s degree in nursing. The mean job satisfaction score was 70.19 ± 22.59. Similarly, the mean career growth score was 67.17 ± 19.64, reflecting positive perceived career growth and job satisfaction. A statistically significant but modest positive correlation was found between the two variables (r = 0.257, p < 0.001), indicating that increases in career growth are associated with increases in job satisfaction to a limited extent. Differences in career growth according to years of experience approached but did not reach statistical significance, F (4, 365) = 2.39, p = 0.051, suggesting a possible trend toward variation across experience levels.ConclusionThis study found generally positive job satisfaction and perceived career growth among nurses, with a significant positive relationship between career growth and job satisfaction. Although the relationship was modest, the findings underscore the importance of career development opportunities in shaping nurses’ workplace experiences. The observed trend toward differences in career growth across experience levels suggests that career support needs may vary throughout nurses’ professional trajectories.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Career development including continuous training, mentorship, and professional development programs, helps improve nursing performance, leading to reduced stress and lower turnover rates [1, 2]. Research suggests that nurses who receive regular professional development opportunities experience higher job satisfaction and provide better-quality care [3–5]. Although according to Baxi and Atre (2024), Job satisfaction is “a critical aspect of organizational psychology, serving as a cornerstone for understanding employee motivation, employee engagement, and overall well-being in the workplace [6]. A supportive work environment that offers growth opportunities can improve nurse retention, increase productivity, and reduce absenteeism [7]. Research on the impact of career development on nursing in military hospitals remains limited, making it essential to explore how career development programs can improve nursing practices and job satisfaction in these environments.

Studies also indicated that nursing in military hospitals faces additional challenges due to the nature of military work, which requires the ability to adapt quickly to changing conditions and handle emergencies effectively [8]. Providing appropriate professional support and career development opportunities can enhance nurses’ ability to deliver excellent healthcare even under challenging work conditions [9]. Job satisfaction plays a critical role in ensuring that the workforce is committed and motivated, which determines productivity, teamwork, as well as company loyalty. Career development and career growth are determinants in retaining nurses in health care institutions. Lack of systematic career progression, training, rewards, incentives, and conducive working environment increases turnover, which forces the nurses to seek elsewhere. This causes a loss of experience gained over the years and high costs of recruitment and training.

Despite substantial interest in healthcare workers’ job satisfaction in Saudi Arabia, few studies have examined how structured career growth opportunities influence job satisfaction within military hospital settings. Prior research has explored job satisfaction broadly in government and tertiary hospitals [10] and in general Saudi healthcare contexts [11, 12]. However, existing research rarely investigates career development pathways such as promotion systems, training and development opportunities, and professional growth frameworks as determinants of job satisfaction in military hospitals. A study in a military hospital context indicated general satisfaction patterns without linking them specifically to career growth dimensions [13]. Therefore, this study fills a critical gap by providing focused empirical evidence on how career growth relates to job satisfaction in the unique organizational context of military healthcare in the Kingdom.

This study tackles the following research questions given the identified gap in the literature and the theoretical premise that professional development improves employee attitudes and workplace outcomes: (1) How do Saudi Arabian military hospital nurses perceive their career growth and job satisfaction? (2) Does career advancement have a statistically significant correlation with job satisfaction among nurses? Lastly, do work-related and demographic variables affect how nurses perceive career progress.

Grounded in existing empirical evidence indicating that professional development opportunities are positively associated with job satisfaction in nursing populations [1, 14, 15], the following hypotheses were formulated:

H1

There is a positive and statistically significant relationship between career growth and job satisfaction among nurses working in military hospitals in Saudi Arabia.

H2

Perceptions of career growth differ significantly according to selected demographic and work-related characteristics (age, gender, nationality, education level, years of experience, and department).

In this perspective, the current study unravels the connection that exists between career development and job satisfaction in order to accomplish nurse retention in military hospitals in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The study is adequate and sustainable in its provision of healthcare workforce in line with the goals of Saudi Vision 2030, which focuses on human capital development, workforce sustainability, and enhancing job satisfaction and retention of healthcare professionals to enhance overall performance of the health sector.

Military hospital was selected because they represent a highly structured and strategically important part of the national healthcare system. This hospital in Saudi Arabia operate at the intersection of specialized healthcare and national security, and their nursing workforce is uniquely shaped by national policies such as Saudization, which directly affects staffing patterns, training demands, and career progression opportunities. A recent study revealed that Saudization has significantly altered workforce composition in Saudi military hospitals, creating distinct professional challenges and opportunities for nurses [16]. Additionally, the Saudi nursing workforce faces broader systemic issues including uneven workforce distribution, reliance on expatriate nurses, and regional disparities which influence job satisfaction and career development [17]. Military hospitals also maintain structured organizational systems and high operational demands, making them a relevant setting for examining how workplace conditions shape nurses’ experiences. Prior studies conducted in Saudi military hospitals further highlight their unique work environments and the importance of understanding nurse perceptions within these settings [18]. For these reasons, military hospitals provide an appropriate and meaningful context for investigating career growth and job satisfaction among nurses.

This study aimed to evaluate the relationship of career growth with job satisfaction and other demographic and work-related characteristics among nurses working in Military Hospitals in Saudi Arabia.

Methodology

A cross-sectional, quantitative research design to collect insights into Career Growth with Job Satisfaction and relationship among nurses working in Military Hospital in Saudi Arabia. Data were gathered through a self-administered online questionnaire distributed using Google Forms. To ensure the relevance of the data, clearly defined selection criteria were established for eligible nurses. To ensure the relevance and accuracy of the data, explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Eligible participants included nurses who were currently employed in a military healthcare facility in Saudi Arabia, registered with the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS), actively working during the data collection period, and of both Saudi and non-Saudi nationalities. Exclusion criteria included nurses who were no longer practicing, individuals who had relocated outside Saudi Arabia, nurses not currently employed in the study setting, and administrative or non-clinical staff without direct nursing responsibilities. These criteria ensured that the sample accurately represented the target population of practicing nurses within the military hospital.

The ethical approval committee for Scientific Research Ethics at King Saud University (reference number E-24–9052) approved this study. Furthermore, to safeguard the privacy and confidentiality of all participants, measures were implemented to ensure that all responses remained anonymous throughout the research process, thereby creating a secure environment in which participants could share their insights.

Questionnaires, distribution and collection

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire composed of three main sections. The first section gathered demographic and professional information, including age, gender, nationality, and years of nursing experience, level of education, current department, and monthly income. These variables were included to describe the study population and to explore potential associations with career growth and job satisfaction. The second section assessed career growth using the Career Growth Scale for Nurses (CGSN), which consists of three dimensions divided in to 17 items [19]. The first dimension, professional ability and attribute improvement, included 7 items; the second dimension, career promotion and prestige increase, comprised 6 items; and the third dimension, career goal progress, consisted of 4 items. This section included items measuring nurses’ perceptions of skill development, emotional control, professional competence, career advancement, recognition, salary growth, role expansion, and progress toward clearly defined career goals. Responses were rated on a Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1), disagree (2), neutral (3), agree (4), strongly agree (5), with higher scores indicating stronger perceptions of career growth. The third section assessed job satisfaction across three domains, comprising 18 items [20], namely intrinsic, extrinsic, and relational satisfaction [20]. Intrinsic satisfaction focused on personal fulfillment, work meaning, motivation, and enthusiasm. Extrinsic satisfaction items assessed workplace conditions, workload, organizational support, learning opportunities, and unit management. Relational satisfaction items examined interpersonal relationships, teamwork, and collegial support. Mean scores were calculated for each domain, with higher scores reflecting greater levels of job satisfaction.

The Questionnaires were distributed in English, the language used in Saudi military hospitals for communication and documentation among nurses. The Career Growth Scale for Nurses (CGSN) was originally developed and validated by Ni et al. [19], showed good psychometric qualities. While the Nursing Workplace Satisfaction Questionnaire (NWSQ) was developed by Fairbrother et al. [20], has also demonstrated established validity and reliability in nursing populations. Although the instruments were not originally developed in the Saudi context or in Arabic language, both measure constructs that are conceptually universal within healthcare systems. To ensure contextual suitability, the tools were reviewed by senior nursing faculty members and a clinical administrator. A pilot study involving 30 nurses was conducted to assess clarity and cultural relevance. Minor wording modifications were made where necessary without altering the original meaning of the items. Internal consistency reliability in the present study was high (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.900 for career growth and 0.886 for job satisfaction), supporting the appropriateness of the scales for this population.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 was utilized for this study. Demographic characteristics were presented as Frequencies and Percentages, while continuous data was displayed as mean (Std). The distribution of the continuous scale score was evaluated for normality using the Shapiro Wilk test, which did not show a significant deviation from normality. The relationship between career growth and job satisfaction, was analyzed using Pearson’s and Spearman correlation after confirming linearity and normality assumptions. An independent-samples t-test was conducted to compare differences in career growth between variables with two categories, and a one-way ANOVA was used to explore variables with more than two categories. For all analyses, statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

To further examine construct validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted for both the Career Growth Scale for Nurses (CGSN) and the Job Satisfaction scale. The CGSN was specified as a three-factor model comprising: (1) professional ability and attribute improvement, (2) career promotion and prestige increase, and (3) career goal progress. The Job Satisfaction scale was modeled as three correlated factors: intrinsic, extrinsic, and relational satisfaction. The analyses were performed using maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including chi-square divided by degrees of freedom (χ²/df), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Model adequacy was determined based on commonly accepted thresholds: χ²/df < 3, CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, and RMSEA ≤ 0.08. Convergent validity was assessed using standardized factor loadings, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE). CR values ≥ 0.70 and AVE values ≥ 0.50 were considered indicative of acceptable reliability and convergent validity.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the participating nurses (N = 370). Most of them were between 31 and 40 years of age (58.9%), very few nurses were younger than 25–30 years (17.8%). The sample was predominantly female, accounting for 88.6% of respondents, while males represented 11.4%. Regarding nationality, Saudi nurses formed the largest group (37.3%), followed by Indian (24.6%) and Filipino (24.3%) nurses. In terms of professional experience, nearly one-third of nurses had 6–10 years of experience (29.7%), while 27.6% had more than 15 years, indicating a largely experienced workforce. Most nurses held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (62.2%), followed by diploma holders (33.0%). Nurses were distributed across various departments, with outpatient services (18.1%) and medical/surgical units (14.6%) being the most common. More than half of the participants (55.7%) reported a monthly income between 1333–2666 United states dollars (USD) (Table 1). Although according to recent data from the General Authority for Statistics (GASTAT,2018), the average monthly wage in the Saudi health and social services sector ranges approximately between 2,730 USD, depending on qualification and employment sector [21]. This indicates that a substantial proportion of the study sample falls within or slightly below the national sectoral average, suggesting a middle-income professional profile. At the macroeconomic level, Saudi Arabia is classified as a high-income economy by the World Bank (2023), further contextualizing the socio-economic position of the participants [22].

Confirmatory factor analysis

Career growth scale (CFA)

A confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to evaluate the three-factor structure of the Career Growth Scale. The hypothesized model demonstrated an acceptable fit to the data (χ²/df = 2.41, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.062), indicating that the proposed structure adequately represents the observed data.

All items loaded significantly on their respective latent constructs, with standardized factor loadings ranging from 0.57 to 0.89 (Table 2). Most loadings exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, supporting the adequacy of the measurement model. Composite reliability values ranged from 0.88 to 0.95, indicating strong internal consistency across the three domains. The average variance extracted (AVE) ranged from 0.60 to 0.78, exceeding the recommended minimum of 0.50 and confirming acceptable convergent validity. Although the correlation matrix indicated high inter-item correlations, this likely reflects conceptual overlap among the dimensions of career growth rather than a structural deficiency. Overall, the findings support the multidimensional construct of the Career Growth Scale in this sample.

Job satisfaction scale (CFA)

Confirmatory factor analysis was also performed to test the three-factor structure of the Job Satisfaction scale, comprising intrinsic, extrinsic, and relational domains. The model demonstrated a good fit to the data (χ²/df = 2.18, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.057), supporting the hypothesized factor structure. All items loaded significantly on their respective constructs, with standardized factor loadings ranging from 0.79 to 0.99 (Table 3), indicating strong relationships between observed indicators and latent variables. Composite reliability values were high across all domains (CR = 0.94–0.96), demonstrating excellent internal consistency. The AVE values ranged from 0.73 to 0.79, exceeding recommended thresholds and confirming convergent validity. While high factor loadings were observed, some degree of correlation between domains was expected due to the interconnected nature of job satisfaction dimensions. Overall, the results confirm that the Job Satisfaction scale exhibits strong psychometric properties in this study population.

Student’s t-test

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine differences in mean career growth scores across nurses’ demographic and work-related characteristics, including age, nationality, years of experience, educational level, and department (Table 6). The analysis showed no statistically significant differences among age groups, F(3, 366) = 0.082, p = 0.970, indicating that perceived career growth was consistent across different age categories. Similarly, nationality was not significantly associated with career growth perceptions F(4, 365) = 0.186, p = 0.946). Mean scores ranged narrowly from 64.15 to 68.14 across nationalities. Indian nurses reported the highest mean score (68.14, SD = 18.83), while Malaysian nurses reported the lowest (64.15, SD = 21.74). In contrast, years of professional experience showed a statistically significant difference in career growth scores, F(3, 366) = 3.165, p = 0.025. Nurses with 6–10 years of experience reported the highest mean career growth score (71.88, SD = 14.96), whereas those with more than 15 years of experience had the lowest mean score (64.30, SD = 21.08). This finding suggests that mid-career nurses may perceive greater opportunities for professional advancement compared to those at earlier or later career stages. Regarding educational qualification, no significant differences were observed. Nurses holding a bachelor’s degree reported a mean score of 67.35 (SD = 19.35), diploma holders had a mean of 67.15 (SD = 19.91), and those with higher education qualifications reported a mean of 65.05 (SD = 22.42). The ANOVA results confirmed that these variations were not statistically significant, F(2, 367) = 0.114, p = 0.893.Finally, analysis across different clinical departments revealed some variation in mean scores, with nursing administration reporting the lowest mean (57.62, SD = 25.27) and medical/surgical units reporting among the highest (69.35, SD = 19.48). However, these differences were not statistically significant, F(8, 361) = 0.976, p = 0.455.

Discussion

The findings of this study support the hypothesis, demonstrating a positive and statistically significant but modest relationship between career growth and job satisfaction among nurses. The observed correlation indicates that nurses who perceive greater opportunities for career growth tend to report higher levels of job satisfaction. While this finding supports the assumption that increased opportunities for career growth are associated with higher levels of job satisfaction, the strength of this relationship is relatively moderate. This suggests that career growth, although important, represents only one of several factors influencing job satisfaction among nurses. The relatively low magnitude of the correlation can be explained by the multidimensional nature of job satisfaction, which is shaped by a variety of factors beyond career advancement, including working conditions, organizational support, leadership style, workload, and interpersonal relationships. Therefore, even when career growth opportunities are present, their impact on overall job satisfaction may be limited if other workplace factors are less favorable. Additionally, the lack of significant differences in career growth across most demographic variables (e.g., gender, age, nationality, education, and department) further supports the notion that perceptions of career growth are relatively consistent across groups and may not strongly differentiate levels of job satisfaction. In contrast, years of experience showed a significant association, suggesting that career stage may play a role in shaping perceptions of growth opportunities. An earlier research has similarly identified a significant, positive, and moderate relationship between job and life satisfaction, highlighting their interconnected nature of professional and personal well-being [23]. Therefore the findings suggest that healthcare authorities should take more efforts to increase the career growth and job satisfaction of nurses in KSA [24].

This relationship between career growth and job satisfaction can be understood in light of nurses’ day-to-day professional experiences. Nurses who acquire new skills, expand their clinical competencies, or assume greater responsibilities are more likely to view their work positively and feel valued by their organization [25, 26]. In contrast, limited opportunities for development or a sense of career stagnation may lead to frustration, reduced motivation, and lower job satisfaction [26, 27]. Career growth thus contributes not only to professional advancement but also to nurses’ sense of self-realization, reinforcing the perception of nursing as a meaningful career rather than merely a source of income [28]. These positive perceptions may also enhance nurse’s ability to cope with the inherent demands and pressures of the profession. Organizational environments that actively support career development may foster job satisfaction. Well-structured training programs, mentorship opportunities, and clear advancement pathways signal institutional commitment to workforce development [29, 30]. When nurses perceive that their employer invests in their growth, they are more likely to experience higher satisfaction, stronger organizational loyalty, and greater engagement, even in the absence of direct financial incentives [31].

In the current study most of the nurses were predominantly female in early to mid-career stages, which is consistent with international workforce patterns reported across diverse healthcare systems [15]. Similar distributions have been documented in studies from India and other countries [32], underscoring the global gendered nature of the nursing profession. Despite demographic variability across settings, the consistently high mean scores observed across career growth dimensions in this study suggest that nurses perceived strong opportunities for professional development. These findings align with research from Egypt and Indonesia, where positive perceptions of career development were significantly associated with job satisfaction and organizational loyalty. Studies from Canada among internationally educated nurses emphasize that achieving career goals and receiving organizational support are key contributors to satisfaction, regardless of national context [14, 33].

Overall job satisfaction in this study was high across intrinsic, extrinsic, and relational domains. Nurses reported strong intrinsic satisfaction, particularly in experiencing meaningful work and opportunities to demonstrate worth. Globally, intrinsic job satisfaction is consistently high among nurses who value patient care and professional worth, as shown in multiple international studies [15, 23, 24]. For example, job satisfaction among nurses in turkey was positively influenced by factors such as positive work environment and perceived income contentment [23]. Despite generally high satisfaction in this study, other international evidence highlights variability, particularly regarding financial and career-related factors. A study in India found nurses were least satisfied with pay and opportunities for career growth, while work environment satisfaction remained relatively moderate [32], this suggests that while intrinsic aspects of nursing work can be satisfying universally, extrinsic factors such as salary and advancement opportunities may differ significantly by country and healthcare system [32]. Another recent study revealed that job-related factors affected job performance more than personal traits. Also found, employees who reported high levels of job satisfaction also reported higher levels of job performance [34].

The relatively high score of job satisfaction and career growth observed in this study may be influenced by several contextual and methodological factors. Military healthcare organizations typically operate within clearly defined hierarchical and promotional systems, which may enhance perceptions of stability and structured professional development. Evidence suggests that leadership support, organizational commitment, and opportunities for advancement are important predictors of nurse job satisfaction [35, 36]. In addition, previous research conducted in Saudi healthcare settings has reported favorable satisfaction levels when nurses perceive institutional support and professional growth opportunities [37]. However, the use of self-reported measures may introduce social desirability bias, particularly in structured environments where respondents may provide more favorable evaluations [38]. Therefore, while the findings indicate positive perceptions, they should be interpreted with consideration of contextual and response-related influences.

With regards to demographic factors only year of experience were significantly associated with career growth in this study. Other demographic variables showed no significant relationships with career growth or job satisfaction, which is in contrast to a large body of civilian healthcare research [15, 23, 24]. One likely explanation is that military healthcare systems are extremely standardized, with explicit rules governing professional growth, employment assignments, pay, and promotions. Such homogeneity can lessen the impact of individual demographic variations, resulting in more consistent work environments for employees. In addition, earlier research revealed that, organizational culture and structure in military settings can influence workers’ opinions and lessen the variation usually seen in civilian settings [35, 39]. Additionally, military groups frequently place a strong emphasis on shared duty, collaboration, and collective identity, all of which could counteract demographic impacts [40]. The lack of influence of demographic variables that are important predictors in other contexts in this study may be explained by these contextual factors. To completely grasp their roles in influencing military nurses’ job satisfaction and career advancement, future research should examine other organizational and psychosocial elements [24].

It is also possible that the standardized structure of the military healthcare system reduces variability related to demographic characteristics by providing uniform policies, promotion criteria, and professional expectations. Numerous studies have revealed opportunities for professional development and clear career advancement pathways are positively correlated with job satisfaction among nurses [14, 15, 41]. Cross-national evidence, including studies from Canada, further emphasizes that achieving career goals and receiving organizational support significantly contribute to satisfaction, regardless of healthcare system context [14]. Together, these findings reinforce the importance of career growth as a key determinant of nurses’ workplace satisfaction, while also highlighting the influence of organizational structure and contextual factors.

Several limitations need to be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. Firstly, the use of a cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causality between career growth and job satisfaction; the relationships observed represent associations rather than directional effects. Secondly, convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of the results, as nurses who chose to participate may differ systematically from those who did not. Thirdly, although validated instruments were used, both scales were originally developed outside the Saudi context. Despite expert review and a pilot test, cultural nuances may still influence item interpretation. Fourthly, data were collected through self-administered online questionnaires, which may introduce self-report bias, including social desirability or recall bias. Finally, the study was conducted in a single military hospital system, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other healthcare sectors or regions within Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that nurses reported generally positive job satisfaction and perceived career growth, with career growth showing a significant positive relationship with job satisfaction. Demographic and work-related characteristics, including age, gender, nationality, education, and department, were not meaningfully associated with career growth. In contrast, years of professional experience were associated with a statistically significant difference in career growth. This trend suggests that experience may influence nurses’ perceptions of career growth and professional development. Healthcare administrators should prioritize structured career development pathways, including continuing education, mentorship programs, and transparent promotion criteria, as key strategies to enhance nurses’ job satisfaction. Tailoring career growth initiatives to different stages of professional experience may help sustain motivation and prevent career stagnation. Strengthening career development opportunities may also contribute to improved nurse retention, workforce stability, and quality of patient care.
