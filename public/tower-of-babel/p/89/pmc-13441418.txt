Virtual Sexual Health Education for Court-Involved & Court-Exposed Girls: A Pilot of the Adapted My Body, My Choice Intervention
Authors: Elizabeth Barnert, Carina F Rodrigues, Jaime La Charite, Sarah Godoy, Georgia Eleni Perris, Mikiko Thelwell, Eraka Bath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Court-involved non-incarcerated (CINI) girls and girls with prior carceral exposure often have heightened sexual and reproductive health (SRH) risks and unique needs but limited access to care. These risks are compounded by childhood trauma and lack of access to trauma-informed, developmentally-aligned SRH education. Despite this, few interventions are tailored to the needs of CINI girls or those with prior carceral exposure.Objective:We piloted the My Body, My Choice for CINI Girls (MBMC-CINI), a trauma-informed, community-adapted SRH education intervention, with CINI girls and girls with prior carceral exposure, assessing for changes in SRH knowledge, attitudes, and confidence.Methods:We conducted a pre-experimental, one-group pre-test–posttest study with three cohorts (N = 31). Participants identified as cis-girls, ages 12–18, who were either CINI or had prior carceral exposure. MBMC-CINI was delivered via Zoom and included 11 interactive modules facilitated by health educators, clinicians, and lived experience experts. Participants completed surveys at baseline, 1-month, and 3-months post-intervention. Measures included knowledge of birth control methods, sexual health concepts, confidence using contraception, and communication behaviors. We assessed changes over time using Exact McNemar and Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests.Results:Twenty-four participants completed baseline surveys, 21 completed 1-month follow-up, and 17 completed all three assessments. Delivery of MBMC-CINI was associated with significant improvements in familiarity with multiple contraceptive methods (e.g., birth control pills, condoms, injectable birth control) and confidence in correctly using condoms and contraception. Retention was high, with most participants attending the majority of sessions, despite substantial structural barriers; for example, 42% of participants reported homelessness in the prior year.Conclusions:The MBMC-CINI intervention was associated with improvements in SRH knowledge and confidence, along with strong intervention engagement, supporting further testing of this intervention at scale. Expanding access to tailored, trauma-informed SRH education has the potential to advance reproductive health outcomes and health equity for marginalized youth.Keywords: Sexual health education, juvenile legal system, adolescent girls, reproductive justice, court-involved youth, court-involved non-incarcerated girls, trauma-informed care, contraception

Girls impacted by the juvenile legal system experience disproportionately high sexual and reproductive health (SRH) risks, including early sexual initiation, elevated rates of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and unintended pregnancy (Berezin et al., 2022). These risks extend beyond youth currently confined to girls who are court-involved but non-incarcerated (CINI), those with prior juvenile legal system involvement, and those who are justice-exposed through family incarceration, reflecting shared structural vulnerabilities and barriers to care.

Moreover, these girls have unique SRH needs compared to their counterparts who are not system-impacted, yet they often face limited access to SRH care in community settings (Tolou-Shams et al., 2022). Multiple factors exacerbate their SRH risks, including high rates of childhood trauma, abuse, poverty, and family instability. These intersect with increased rates of substance use, psychiatric disorders, social stigma, and marginalization from health care services (Berezin et al., 2022). Additionally, STI rates among CINI girls have been reported to range from 3% to 45%, depending on demographic and contextual factors (Tolou-Shams et al., 2022). Rates of chlamydia and gonorrhea among girls in juvenile detention are 5–7 times higher than in their non-detained counterparts (McNeill-Johnson et al., 2025). Moreover, one in three girls in detention has experienced pregnancy (Saleeby et al., 2019; Suresh et al., 2020), compared to 13.1 per 1,000 U.S. adolescent girls aged 15–19 (Osterman et al., 2025).

While girls in detention often receive at least some SRH services, CINI girls and girls with prior personal carceral involvement living in the community share many of the same SRH risks but may receive none at all when in community settings (Tolou-Shams et al., 2022). Further, girls with indirect carceral exposure through family or household member incarceration are at heightened risk of carceral involvement and poor SRH outcomes (Barnert et al., 2021). These sexual health concerns, compounded by limited access to sexual healthcare, underscore the need for targeted, developmentally aligned, and trauma-informed SRH education that addresses the specific needs of CINI girls and girls with prior carceral exposure, including personal or household exposure.

Barriers to SRH Care

CINI girls and girls with carceral exposure often face significant barriers to quality SRH healthcare. Key barriers to accessing SRH healthcare include high rates of mental health and substance use disorder morbidity, confidentiality concerns, and feeling stigmatized or negatively judged by healthcare providers (Braverman & Murray, 2011; Tam et al., 2019; Tolou-Shams et al., 2022). Additionally, systemic and structural inequities, such as restrictive consent laws, lack of insurance, poverty, and other social determinants of health, further complicate this issue (Sickmund & Puzzanchera, 2014). The barriers faced by CINI girls and those with prior carceral exposure not only limit access to timely and adequate care, but contribute to unintended negative health consequences such as unintended pregnancies and STIs, further underscoring the importance of providing accessible, quality SRH education.

Sexual Health Education Needs

Despite this need, existing interventions to deliver SRH education have been designed for youth who have experienced trauma; however, these programs have not been tailored to meet the specific needs of CINI girls or girls with prior carceral exposure, despite their distinct risk profiles (Combs et al., 2019; Donenberg et al., 2018; Jemmott et al., 1998). For example, the “Making Proud Choices for Youth in Out-of-Home Care” intervention improved sexual health knowledge and attitudes and increased consistency of condom use but was predominantly developed and tested with Black adolescent boys (Combs et al., 2019; Jemmott et al., 2006). Although the intervention was trauma-informed, a key facet needed for carceral-system impacted youth, it did not integrate the perspectives of adolescent girls. While findings were promising, the intervention delivery was compressed into one day rather than over multiple sessions in the community, delivered mainly to boys, and did not assess knowledge and behavioral change over time. It also did not address substance use, an issue closely linked with sexual health risk for all youth, and especially for carceral-exposed girls (Tolou-Shams et al., 2022).

Several evidence-based sexual health interventions exist for trauma-exposed youth, including Making Proud Choices (Combs et al., 2019; Jemmott et al., 2006). Prior work by our team adapted Making Proud Choices into the My Body, My Choice (MBMC) curriculum for girls with histories of commercial sexual exploitation, demonstrating feasibility via a virtual format with wide accessibility and promising improvements in SRH outcomes (Bath et al., 2024). However, despite the overlap between youth with histories of sexual exploitation and carceral exposure, SRH education interventions that provide material through a virtual learning platform specifically tailored to youth with carceral exposure are lacking. The curriculum draws on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991; Muñoz-Silva et al., 2007) and cognitive-behavioral theories (Combs et al., 2019; Jemmott JB 3rd et al., 1998). TPB posits that behavior change is driven by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, which are directly targeted through MBMC-CINI’s modules addressing contraceptive attitudes, partner communication, and self-efficacy in condom and contraceptive use. Cognitive-behavioral principles further inform the intervention’s skill-building components, including condom use practice and negotiation skills (Crowley et al., 2021).

Therefore, building on this foundation, the current study further adapts MBMC for court-involved non-incarcerated girls and girls with prior carceral exposure (MBMC-CINI), a population with minimal access to tailored SRH education.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We piloted MBMC for CINI Girls (MBMC-CINI), a virtual sexual health education intervention for CINI girls and girls with carceral exposure, using a pre-experimental, one-group pretest-posttest design, comparing participants’ baseline responses (i.e., pre-intervention) with their scores at each follow-up. This community-engaged study was carried out in collaboration with the Los Angeles County Departments of Children and Family Services and Probation. Additionally, we partnered with community organizations and healthcare providers, including Saving Innocence, National Center for Youth Law, Children’s Law Center, and AltaMed. The University of California, Los Angeles Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures and permission for the study was also obtained from the Los Angeles County Superior Court, juvenile division.

Participants and Recruitment

To obtain an adequate sample to assess preliminary outcomes, we implemented MBMC-CINI with three cohorts, totaling 31 adolescent girls. Recruitment occurred through two pathways: (1) youth with current court involvement and (2) girls with prior personal or household legal system exposure, recruited through community organizations. For girls with current court involvement, we followed the Juvenile Court’s required multi-step process. The Los Angeles County Department of Probation provided a list of all court-involved non-incarcerated girls (N = 211) and their corresponding attorney information. Per court policy, attorneys had to provide permission before youth could be contacted. We sent outreach requests to attorneys and received approximately 20 responses. After submitting all required materials and receiving approval from both the court and the responding attorneys, we were granted permission to contact 12 potentially eligible girls. For girls with prior personal or household legal system involvement but not currently court-supervised, additional recruitment occurred through referrals provided by community partners such as Saving Innocence, adolescent group homes, and participant referrals. This pathway did not involve the court approval process.

Potential participants were contacted by phone, email, or text to assess interest and eligibility and to obtain verbal consent. Eligibility criteria included: (1) age 12–18 years, (2) self-identifying as a girl, (3) either current court involvement while living in the community (i.e., CINI) or prior personal/household legal system involvement, and (4) access to a computer or tablet.

Intervention

Adaptation Process

To meet the needs of CINI girls and girls with prior carceral exposure, we adapted the My Body, My Choice (MBMC) SRH education intervention, originally based on Making Proud Choices for Youth in Out-of-Home Care (Combs et al., 2019; Jemmott et al., 2006). This innovation extended from our prior work adapting the Making Proud Choices for Youth in Out-of-Home Care intervention to system-involved adolescent girls with histories of commercial sexual exploitation (Bath et al., 2024). To inform the adaptation process, we implemented a community-based participatory research approach, collaborating with lived experience experts and key multidisciplinary stakeholders (Wells & Jones, 2009). Following SAMHSA’s framework, trauma is defined as an event or circumstance that causes lasting adverse effects on functioning and wellbeing; trauma-informed approaches recognize the widespread impact of trauma and integrate this understanding into program design, language, and delivery to avoid re-traumatization (Substance Abuse & Mental Health Services Administration, 2014). Together, we identified developmentally aligned, trauma-informed language appropriate for CINI girls. This included revisions to enhance youth engagement with intervention materials in both the curriculum and assessments and to prevent unintentional re-traumatization. We also incorporated LGBTQ+ perspectives.

Additionally, the original curriculum, Making Proud Choices, was not LGBTQ+ affirming, echoing most research on specialized interventions for youth experiencing commercial sexual exploitation that almost exclusively focuses on impact and efficacy related to cisgender girls, despite the inclusion of youth who identify as non-binary or transgender in these programs (Ijadi-Maghsoodi et al., 2016; Jemmott et al., 2006). To address this gap, youth within the focus groups reported identification within the LGBTQ+ community and offered their perspectives on gender-based language throughout the curriculum, which prompted the inclusion of role plays that had gender-neutral names to simulate nonheteronormative relationships. We also conducted an extensive literature review and consulted with lived-experience experts to create a module discussing intersex identity, gender, sexuality, and anatomy with nuance and care. Lastly, we reviewed and implemented standards outlined by the ACLU ‘LGBTQ & Gender Inclusiveness Assessment Tool’ in developing our curriculum as well (American Civil Liberties Union n.d). We integrated substance use psychoeducation to address the interrelated risks of substance use and SRH behaviors and implemented a text message-based system to deliver sexual health information to participants as a supplement to the curriculum modules, based on findings from our prior work with the target population (Godoy et al., 2022).

Intervention Structure and Delivery

MBMC-CINI consisted of 11 modules, each approximately 60 to 90 minutes in length. Topics included: Introduction and Overview; Sexuality; Relationships and Healthy Sexual Decisions; Consent and Communication; Pregnancy and Your Options; Contraception and Condom Use; STI Prevention; HIV and AIDS; Judgment Impairment and Beliefs about HIV/AIDS; Practicing Affirmative Consent and Problem Solving; and Enhancing Advocacy and Communication Skills. Module topics were selected based on community-engaged youth input and empirical literature on SRH risk among this population; full details of module development are described elsewhere (Bath et al., 2024).

The MBMC-CINI intervention was delivered to three cohorts in September 2023 (Cohort 1), March 2024 (Cohort 2), and June 2024 (Cohort 3), respectively. Each round was held virtually via secure videoconferencing software (i.e., Zoom). The MBMC-CINI course was delivered with differing schedules for each round in response to youth feedback from prior research (Bath et al., 2024) and to accommodate facilitator availability. The September cohort met Monday through Friday, 3 to 6 PM, for a total of 15 hours. This session was led by five facilitators: two professional health educators from the federally-qualified health center AltaMed, one physician-resident, and two lived experience experts with prior histories of justice involvement and commercial sexual exploitation who completed training in MBMC-CINI and course facilitation. In response to feedback that three-hour sessions felt too lengthy, the March and June cohort schedules were modified to span two weeks, with sessions held Monday, Wednesday, and Friday from 4 to 6 PM, totaling 12 hours. To protect participants’ privacy and confidentiality, youth were instructed to remove their last names from their Zoom display names. Facilitators were compensated for their time, commensurate with standard wage payments in the community. Youth received up to $370 in e-gift cards for participating in MBMC-CINI sessions ($75), completing surveys ($240), and completing quizzes ($55).

Data Collection Procedures

Participants were asked to complete a survey at four time points: baseline (before the virtual intervention), immediately post-intervention, and at one- and three-month follow-up. The self-administered survey assessed sexual health knowledge, attitudes, risk behaviors, and substance use.

The post-intervention survey, administered immediately after the course, contained 25 items to evaluate the program’s acceptability and participants’ overall experience. No identifying information (e.g., names) was collected; instead, responses were linked using a unique ID code. Participants could skip any question without consequence. All surveys were administered through Qualtrics (Provo, UT, USA), a secure online platform, and the data were stored in a protected Qualtrics database.

Measures

The same survey was previously used to evaluate the MBMC-CINI intervention in other youth populations (Bath et al., 2024). A baseline demographic survey collected information on participants’ age, gender identity, race/ethnicity, education level, pregnancy history, and current living situation. These surveys assessed six key domains: (a) understanding of sexual health concepts, (b) knowledge of birth control methods, (c) attitudes and beliefs about sexual health topics, (d) communication about sexual activity, (e) access to birth control, and (f) personal sexual health history. To assess understanding of sexual health concepts, participants answered ten true/false questions covering knowledge of STIs, birth control methods, pregnancy, and healthy relationships. Knowledge of birth control was measured by asking participants to rate their familiarity with twelve birth control methods on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all familiar” to “very familiar.” Attitudes and beliefs about sexual health were assessed using ten Likert-scale items, with responses ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” (5-point Likert scale), covering topics such as condom use, consent, pregnancy, STIs, and safe sex practices. The same Likert agreement scale was applied to eight statements measuring communication with sexual partners and guardians about sexual activity and three items evaluating access to birth control. Finally, the personal sexual activity section inquired about participants’ history of pregnancy, birth control use, abortion procedures, and STIs.

For the primary analysis, all outcome measures were dichotomized to maintain consistency across items, enhance interpretability, and align with prior research using the same survey items (Bath et al., 2024). Knowledge scores were calculated by summing the number of correct responses and determining the percentage correct per individual at each time point. For birth control familiarity, responses of “Very familiar,” “Somewhat familiar,” or “In the middle” were collapsed and coded as “familiar.” Similarly, for attitudes, beliefs, and communication measures, “Strongly Agree” and “Agree” responses were combined into a single “agree” category.

Data Analysis

Baseline demographic characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages for the full sample, and for the 1-month and 3-month follow-up samples. The analytic sample size for each wave was used as the denominator regardless of item-level missingness.

To assess changes in the dichotomized dependent variables between baseline and follow-up, we used the Exact McNemar Test, which is appropriate for paired data in small samples. Comparisons were conducted between baseline and the 1-month follow-up and separately between baseline and the 3-month follow-up. Given the exploratory nature of this pilot study, we did not apply corrections for multiple testing. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of our findings to an alternative variable construction and analytical approach. For items with ordinal response options, we retained ordinal response categories and applied the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test, a non-parametric test for paired ordinal data. This approach increased statistical power by detecting changes in response distributions across the three time points.

Results

Twenty-four individuals completed the baseline survey. Of these, 21 participants completed the 1-month follow-up, and 17 completed all three assessments (baseline, 1-month, and 3-months). In the first cohort, 67% of participants attended at least seven sessions; in the second cohort, 75% attended six sessions. All participants who reported gender identity information identified as female. At baseline, 42% of respondents identified as Black or African American, 33% identified as Hispanic/Latinx/Latino, and 8% as multi-racial. Twenty-five percent had earned at least a high school diploma or GED. Additionally, 12% reported past involvement with the child welfare system, 25% with the juvenile justice system, and 4% with both. Forty-two percent reported experiencing homelessness within the past year. Additional descriptive statistics are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

Overall, findings suggest that MBMC-CINI is a promising sexual health education intervention for CINI girls and girls with prior personal or household carceral exposure that can be delivered virtually to overcome significant barriers to care. We observed sustained increases in familiarity with several forms of birth control, such as abstinence, birth control pills, internal condoms, and external condoms, with significant improvements at both the one-month and three-month follow-up surveys, compared to baseline. Participants also reported increased confidence in their ability to use condoms and other forms of birth control correctly. These findings suggest that a tailored, community-partnered sexual health education intervention like MBMC-CINI may help reduce sexual health risk among girls impacted by incarceration, while empowering them with skills to make healthy decisions. Improved contraceptive knowledge and self-efficacy are established precursors to consistent contraceptive use, which in turn is associated with reduced rates of unintended pregnancy and STIs (Ajzen, 1991). If sustained, the gains observed in MBMC-CINI participants may contribute to longer-term reproductive health benefits for this historically underserved population.

Participants in our sample identified as Black, Indigenous, or People of Color (BIPOC), and most had experienced significant structural marginalization. The study was conducted in Los Angeles County, a large, diverse metropolitan area with substantial SRH resources; yet despite this broader availability, CINI girls and those with carceral exposure often remain dis-connected from SRH services due to the aforementioned structural and systemic barriers (Braverman & Murray, 2011; Sickmund & Puzzanchera, 2014; Tam et al., 2019; Tolou-Shams et al., 2022). Notably, 42% reported experiencing homelessness in the year prior to the study. Despite these barriers, retention rates were high compared to similar studies involving youth in the juvenile legal system and unhoused youth (Abrams, 2010). This may reflect the strength of our community-engaged approach, including the involvement of lived experience experts and iterative adaptation based on youth feedback (Bath et al., 2024). The engagement and follow-up rates speak to participants’ “fierce autonomy” in navigating their sexual and reproductive health—countering harmful stereotypes portraying system-impacted girls as unreachable or disengaged (Godoy et al., 2020). Taken together with the broader sociopolitical climate of reproductive injustice, our findings highlight the value of community-tailored, trauma-informed, youth-centered interventions that address key SRH knowledge gaps.

These findings have several implications for programming. The virtual delivery format supports scalability and spreadability across geographic contexts where in-person programming may be logistically challenging, including integration into probation and community supervision settings. Compensating participants and lived experience facilitators supported strong engagement and retention and should be considered in program budgets. Finally, the community partnership model employed, including involving probation, child welfare, legal advocates, and community organizations, offers a replicable framework for delivering tailored SRH education to CINI girls in other jurisdictions.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the small sample size limited our power to detect statistically significant effects across all domains. A larger sample size may have identified additional significant associations (Etz & Arroyo, 2015; Tipton et al., 2017). For example, while we observed gains in knowledge and confidence, items related to healthy relationships did not reach significance. This may reflect sample size limitations or may suggest that MBMC-CINI’s content or virtual delivery was less effective for this topic area compared to skill-based content (e.g., condom use). Future iterations may benefit from additional emphasis on relational content or alternative formats (e.g., in-person, role play). Additionally, trauma histories common among CINI girls (Berezin et al., 2022), may complicate the practical application of communication and relationship skills, as exposure to adverse experiences can affect trust, emotional regulation, and interpersonal dynamics in ways that may not be easily addressed through brief psychoeducation alone. Second, our recruitment process was challenged by the multi-step requirements for court and attorney permission, which limited our ability to contact potential participants directly and may have introduced selection bias, as the possibility that responding attorneys represented girls with greater stability or resources cannot be ruled out. This barrier was partially mitigated through community-based referrals. Third, differential attrition was a concern. Additionally, the absence of a comparison group, inherent to the pre-experimental design, limits our ability to attribute observed changes solely to the intervention. Finally, the virtual format may have introduced participant fatigue or limited engagement—commonly referred to as “Zoom fatigue” (Deniz et al., 2022). However, the strong retention and observed impact suggest that remote delivery is both feasible and potentially scalable for systems-impacted youth.

Conclusion

MBMC-CINI shows promise as a sexual and reproductive health intervention for court-involved non-incarcerated (CINI) girls and girls with prior carceral system exposure. Findings suggest the value of policy and programmatic emphasis on SRH education, including when delivered through virtual formats to CINI girls and girls at high risk of carceral system involvement. The virtual format supports potential scalability, which should be further evaluated in future research. Expanding access to tailored SRH education for girls affected by incarceration could support reproductive justice and promote healthier life trajectories for carceral-exposed girls in the community, who often have limited access to care.
