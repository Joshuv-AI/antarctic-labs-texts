Administration of the Travoprost Intracameral Implant in an Office-Based Surgery Setting
Authors: Inder Paul Singh, George R Reiss, Brian E Flowers, L Jay Katz, Dale W Usner, David Applegate, Angela C Kothe, Tomas Navratil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe purpose of this analysis was to assess the safety outcomes of a subgroup of patients who received a travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgery setting from one of the phase 3 registration trials, GC-012, for the implant.MethodsAdult patients with open-angle glaucoma or ocular hypertension on 0 to 3 pre-study intraocular pressure (IOP)-lowering medications underwent a washout period (if applicable). Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Ophthalmic surgeries, particularly cataract surgery and glaucoma surgeries, are becoming more prevalent as the population ages.There is a growing interest in transitioning ophthalmic procedures from a facility-based surgery setting to a clinic-based setting or office-based surgery setting (often referred to by payers as non-facility settings) to reduce delays in patient care, improve accessibility for the patient and ophthalmologist, provide additional capacity as the volume of ophthalmic procedures increases, and decrease costs.This subgroup analysis evaluated the safety outcomes of administering the travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgery setting.What was learned from the study?Administration of the travoprost intracameral implant is compatible with being performed in an office-based surgery setting.Regardless of the administration setting, maintenance of aseptic technique and adherence to the same safety standards are vital for patient safety.

Introduction

As the U.S. population ages and the prevalence of age-related diseases such as neovascular macular degeneration, cataract, and glaucoma increases, the growing demand for eyecare and economic pressures are resulting in the need to transition ophthalmic procedures from facility-based surgery settings to a clinic-based setting or an office-based surgery setting. In the U.S., approximately 7 million intravitreal injections for treatment of neovascular age-related macular degeneration, diabetic macular edema, and retinal vein occlusion are performed annually [1]. Cataract extraction with implantation of an intraocular lens is the most commonly performed surgical procedure in the U.S., with approximately 5 million surgeries performed annually [2]. Furthermore, in recent years, treatment of glaucoma has evolved to include minimally invasive interventional therapies (microinvasive glaucoma surgery [MIGS], sustained-release drug delivery platforms) earlier in the treatment paradigm [3].

In the U.S., intravitreal injections may be performed at a facility-based surgery setting (hospital outpatient department [HOPD] or ambulatory surgical center [ASC]) or office-based surgery setting but are almost entirely performed in clinic-based settings due to their cost-effectiveness, accessibility and efficiency, and favorable patient experience [4, 5]. Patient safety is paramount; however, several large studies and meta-analyses have shown that endophthalmitis and perioperative complications (e.g., cataract formation, retinal detachment) occur very rarely following intravitreal injections, and that the rates of occurrence are independent of the administration setting [4, 5].

Cataract surgery successfully evolved from inpatient surgery to surgery in a HOPD in the 1980s due to advancements in surgical techniques. However, most cataract surgeries are now performed in an ASC versus HOPD due to greater accessibility for patients, and cost-effectiveness [6]. In 2024, Medicare paid an average facility fee of $1,183.00 if routine cataract surgery was performed in an ASC and $2,200.00 if performed in a HOPD [7]; however, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) continued to gather information on performing cataract surgery in non-facility-based surgical settings [8]. Stating that advancements in technology had significantly reduced cataract surgery procedure time, and improved the safety of the procedure and patient outcomes, the Department of Health and Human Services requested information on non-facility cataract surgery in a July 2015 Federal Register notification [9]. An analysis of more than 21,000 consecutive cataract surgeries, performed at three office-based surgery settings in Colorado between 2011 and 2014, demonstrated the safety and effectiveness of office-based cataract surgery [10]. A second study of 18,005 office-based cataract or refractive lens surgeries performed between August 2020 and May 2022 at 36 sites in the U.S. has been published subsequently, and also has demonstrated excellent safety outcomes [11].

As with cataract surgery, glaucoma surgeries transitioned from HOPDs to ASCs between 2001 and 2014 [12], and currently there is increasing interest in transitioning MIGS, particularly those that are standalone procedures, from a facility-based surgery setting to an office-based surgery setting [13]. The administration of sustained-release drug delivery products (i.e., procedural pharmaceuticals such as intracameral implants) also lend themselves to being performed in an office-based surgery setting [14]. As with other ophthalmic procedures performed in a clinic-based or an office-based surgery setting, this eliminates the challenge of delayed access and approval within a facility, thereby enabling earlier intervention, reducing stress for the patient, and affords patients the convenience of receiving their preoperative, operative, and post-operative care in one location.

Despite the implementation of robust guidelines to ensure that office-based surgical settings operate at the same safety standards as ASCs and HOPDs, and require accreditation by The Joint Commission, the Accreditation Association for Ambulatory Healthcare, or the American Association of Accreditation of Ambulatory Surgery Facilities (QUAD A), the risk of complications remains one of the greatest concerns of performing any intraocular procedure in an office-based surgery setting. The current study was conducted to assess the safety outcomes through a 12-month period following administration of the travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgery setting in a subgroup of patients from one of the 36-month phase 3 registration trials for the travoprost intracameral implant (GC-012). The travoprost intracameral implant consists of a 0.5-mm-wide by 1.2-mm-long titanium reservoir, which holds 75 mcg travoprost, a membrane that controls the release of travoprost, and a 0.6-mm titanium scleral anchor that immobilizes the implant at the iridocorneal angle. The implant is pre-loaded into a sterile single-dose inserter and is delivered via an ab interno approach into the anterior chamber through a small clear corneal incision. The implant is anchored through the trabecular meshwork into the sclera at the iridocorneal angle [15]. The travoprost intracameral implant 75 mcg (iDose® TR) was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in December 2023 for the reduction of intraocular pressure in patients with open-angle glaucoma (OAG) or ocular hypertension (OHT).

Methods

Study Design

The study (GC-012) was conducted according to a randomized, sham procedure/active-control parallel group design that compared two models of the travoprost intracameral implant (a fast-eluting and a slow-eluting) with timolol maleate (5 mg/ml) eyedrops [16].

Human subjects were included in this trial. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03868124) and was performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments, and with approval of the relevant Institutional Review Board (WCG Institutional Review Board, Puyallup, WA, USA [reference number 20181794]. All subjects provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Patients eligible for participation in the study included those 18 years of age or older with OAG (with or without pseudoexfoliation or pigment dispersion) or OHT, using zero to three intraocular pressure (IOP)-lowering medications at the screening visit. Only one eye was selected as the study eye for participation in the study; If both eyes qualified, the right eye was selected as the study eye. At the baseline visit, the study eye was required to have an unmedicated mean diurnal IOP (the average of the 8:00 AM, 10:00 AM, and 4:00 PM measurements) of 21 mmHg or greater, and an IOP of 36 mmHg or less at each of the three unmedicated IOP timepoints. The washout period ranged from 5 days (for miotics) to 56 days (for rho kinase inhibitors). Patients who were not on IOP-lowering medication at the screening visit were required to have an IOP of 21 mmHg or greater to 36 mmHg or less in the study eye at the screening visit, and to meet the same IOP eligibility criteria at the baseline visit as patients who underwent a washout period from IOP-lowering medication.

Patients with the following conditions in the study eye were excluded from participation: any form of glaucoma other than open-angle glaucoma; best-spectacle corrected visual acuity of worse than 20/80 Snellen; anterior chamber angle less than Schaffer grade 3 at the planned implantation site; presence of peripheral anterior synechia, rubeosis or abnormalities of the anterior chamber angle that could interfere with proper implant administration; inability to provide an adequate or interpretable result on perimetry or a visual field defect with mean deviation worse than − 12 dB; vertical cup/disc ratio greater than 0.8; visually significant cataract, anterior chamber intraocular lens, or cataract surgery in the previous 90 days; central corneal thickness of less than 440 µm or greater than 620 µm; prior incisional glaucoma surgery or argon laser trabeculoplasty; selective laser trabeculoplasty, micropulse laser trabeculoplasty or iridotomy in the previous 90 days; active corneal inflammation or history of intraocular inflammatory disease; corneal abnormalities preventing reliable applanation tonometry, or clinically significant corneal dystrophy; choroidal disorders; or degenerative retinal disorders. In addition, patients were excluded from participating if their fellow eye had a best-corrected visual acuity worse than 20/80; or was currently or had previously been enrolled in the trial.

Additional criteria which excluded patients from participation included: uncontrolled systemic disease or immunodeficiency disorder; use of systemic steroid or systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitor within the previous 30 days; contraindication or hypersensitivity precluding the safe administration of a topical ocular prostaglandin analog, beta-blocker, or eyedrops preserved with benzalkonium chloride; pregnancy or planned pregnancy; or participation in another clinical trial within the previous 30 days.

Study Procedures/Visits

There were two pre-treatment visits: a screening visit and a baseline visit. At the screening visit, patients provided written informed consent, demographic data and medical history were collected, ocular variables were measured (best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp examination, IOP using a Goldmann tonometer and a two-person technique, visual field, pachymetry, gonioscopy, dilated fundus examination, cup/disc ratio), and IOP-lowering medications were discontinued (if applicable). Patient eligibility was confirmed at the baseline visit based on the study eye having an unmedicated mean diurnal IOP of 21 mmHg or greater, and an IOP of 36 mmHg or less at each of 8:00 AM, 10:00 AM, and 4:00 PM. Baseline measures for several ocular variables (best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp examination, specular microscopy) were also collected and a pregnancy test was performed on women of childbearing potential. Eligible patients were scheduled for the surgery visit and dispensed a broad-spectrum antibiotic (fluoroquinolone or polymyxin B sulfate/trimethoprim) to be administered four times a day for at least 1 day prior to the procedure.

Implant Administration

An additional drop of topical antibiotic was administered to the study eye 30 min prior to the procedure. Ophthalmologists trained in angle-based surgical procedures and administration of procedural pharmaceuticals performed the procedures. After confirmation of the depth and anatomy of the anterior chamber angle, as well as the target implant location, patients were randomized in a 1:1:1 treatment allocation to the fast-eluting travoprost intracameral implant (with artificial tears for masking purposes), slow-eluting travoprost intracameral implant (with artificial tears for masking purposes), or sham procedure with timolol maleate (5 mg/ml) eyedrops.

The procedure was carried out under aseptic conditions (sterile gloves, sterile drape, disinfection of the eye and adnexa with povidone iodide). In the office-based surgery setting, a sterile environment for the procedure was facilitated by the use of a portable laminar air flow device, which directed the horizontal sterile laminar flow of air through a high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter over the surgical instruments and sterile field. With the patient supine and their head in a stabilized position under the operating microscope, topical anesthesia was administered to the eye and a small quantity of viscoelastic was placed on the cornea. Light touch gonioscopy was performed under the operating microscope to inspect the angle and angle structures at the nasal implant location. The tip of the sterile pre-loaded inserter was inspected under magnification to ensure the presence of the implant. A clear corneal incision of approximately 2.4 mm, which was designed to be self-sealing at the completion of the procedure, was created at the temporal limbus, a cohesive viscoelastic was added to the anterior chamber to improve visualization of the angle, and the inserter was entered into the anterior chamber and advanced to the nasal anterior chamber angle (2 o’clock position for the right eye; 10 o’clock position for the left eye) and trabecular meshwork while taking care to avoid contact with the cornea, iris and lens. The implant was administered directly through the trabecular meshwork by compressing the tissue until the implant anchor securely penetrated the sclera through the back wall of Schlemm’s canal, such that the base of the implant reservoir was firmly in contact with and compressing the trabecular meshwork. Once the implant was securely anchored, the inserter tip was opened to release the implant, and the inserter was withdrawn from the eye. The anterior chamber was irrigated and aspirated with balanced salt solution to remove all viscoelastic.

Following the procedure, patients were dispensed a topical ophthalmic nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug to be administered for one week in accordance with the medication’s prescribing information. Patients were also instructed to continue administering the broad-spectrum antibiotic four times daily for 1 week.

Follow-Up Visits/Safety Assessments

Post-administration, follow-up visits were conducted at days 1–2, day 10, week 4, week 6, month 3, month 6, month 9, and month 12.

At each of these follow-up visits, patients were assessed for treatment-emergent adverse events and a slit-lamp examination (including assessment of aqueous cells and flare, corneal abnormalities, pupillary irregularities, iris atrophy, lens opacities) was performed. In addition, gonioscopy was performed at day 10, week 4, week 6, month 3, month 6 and month 12; specular microscopy (in a subset of patients) at month 3 and month 12; vertical cup/disc ratio and perimetry at month 6 and month 12; and pachymetry and dilated fundus examination (including evaluation of the macula, vessels and peripheral fundus) at month 12. Visual acuity was assessed at all visits, including via pinhole at day 1–2 and day 10, with habitual correction at day 10, week 4, week 6, month 3, month 6 and month 9, and best-corrected following manifest refraction at month 12 or at any visit at which corrected visual acuity had decreased by ten letters or more from baseline). Clinically significant changes in any of these parameters were to be reported as adverse events.

IOP was assessed via Goldmann tonometry (using a two-person technique common to clinical trials) at a single timepoint (8:00 AM, 10:00 AM or 4:00 PM) on day 1–2, week 4, month 6 and month 9, and at all three timepoints (8:00 AM, 10:00 AM, and 4:00 PM) on day 10, week 6, month 3, and month 12.

Analysis

Safety and efficacy outcomes of the travoprost intracameral implant in the complete patient population from this study have been presented previously [16]. This subgroup analysis was not performed in accordance with a specified sample size; rather, the analysis of safety outcomes is based on the subgroup of patients from one of the phase 3 registration trials for the travoprost intracameral implant (GC-012) who were administered the travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgical setting. The outcome measure is the proportion of patients with adverse events, which included side effects spontaneously reported by the patient, those based on an analysis of changes in a patient’s health or medications, as well as clinically significant changes in findings based on slit-lamp examination, gonioscopy, pachymetry, dilated fundus examination, specular microscopy, perimetry, or best-corrected visual acuity.

Results

A subgroup of 37 patients at three clinical sites was administered the travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgery setting; their safety results are presented herein. The clinical sites were private ophthalmology practices or part of multispecialty ophthalmology practices, which had a procedure room outfitted with a portable laminar air flow device to provide a sterile environment for the intracameral procedure. The principal investigator performed the administration procedure on a consecutive series of participating patients from their clinical site, and the principal investigator or ophthalmologist sub-investigator examined the patients at follow-up visits.

Demographics and baseline ocular characteristics of the study group are presented in Table 1. Patients had a mean ± standard deviation (SD) age of 67.3 ± 12.5 years, 64.9% were female, and 81.1% were White. At the screening visit, 78.4% of patients were diagnosed with OAG, mean (SD) IOP was 18.9 ± 4.4 mmHg, and 75.7% of patients on IOP-lowering medications. All 37 patients (100%) completed 12 months of the trial.

All implant administration procedures were completed successfully, with the implant anchored at the intended site of administration, and no reports of perioperative or late ocular infection. A study eye ocular adverse event, none of which was serious, occurred in 32.4% (12/37) patients.

Adverse events that occurred in ≥ 3% of patients (i.e., two or more patients) included blepharitis (2/37, 5.4%), chemical eye injury (2/37, 5.4%), conjunctival hemorrhage (2/37, 5.4%), conjunctival/ocular hyperemia (4/37, 10.8%), floppy iris syndrome (2/37, 5.4%), and trichiasis (2/37, 5.4%). In addition, an adverse event of increased IOP was reported in 5.4% (2/37) of patients on the day following surgery; in both patients, the increased IOP was related to retained viscoelastic and resolved following a paracentesis. Ocular adverse events reported in the study eye of single patients (1/37, 2.7%) included allergic conjunctivitis, eye irritation, Meibomian gland dysfunction, keratitis, vitreous floaters, Meibomianitis, pterygium, and seasonal allergy. All adverse events were of mild or moderate intensity, with the exception of one of the events of increased IOP. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in four patients (4/37, 10.8%).

There were no adverse events based on clinically significant findings in gonioscopy, pachymetry, dilated fundus examination, specular microscopy, perimetry, or best-corrected visual acuity.

Least squares mean changes from baseline in IOP at visits across the 12 months ranged from − 5.0 (95% CI − 6.14, − 3.93) to − 8.3 (95% CI − 9.29, − 7.25) mmHg.

Discussion

Ophthalmic procedures/surgeries are transitioning to office-based surgery settings from facility-based surgery settings due to cost-effectiveness, eliminating the delay in access to a facility, and improving accessibility for the patient [10, 11, 17]. The risk of complications, particularly sight-threatening endophthalmitis, remains one of the greatest concerns of performing any intraocular procedure in an office-based setting. Regardless of the setting in which the ophthalmic procedure is performed, a low incidence of infection, as well as a robust method for early detection and prompt management, are vital, including the establishment of procedures to ensure that patients do not experience delays in accessing emergency eyecare [18]. In addition, patient safety, particularly for those with significant comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular disease, hypertension), is another critical concern and requires careful consideration of those selected for office-based procedures.

Surgical suites in office-based settings operate at the same safety standards as ASCs and HOPDs, and are accredited by The Joint Commission, the Accreditation Association for Ambulatory Healthcare, or the American Association of Accreditation of Ambulatory Surgery Facilities (QUAD A). In addition, they operate under the ophthalmologist’s license governed by their individual State Board of Medicine using either Class A (oral sedation) or Class B (monitored) anesthesia. Office-based procedure/surgery rooms minimally require a room of adequate size to accommodate the necessary equipment and surgical personnel, and constructed of the appropriate materials to allow for disinfection; appropriate environmental controls relative to temperature, relative humidity and air pressure; a power supply sufficient to power the necessary equipment and a backup source of power (i.e., generator); and ancillary storage space for sterile supplies. If appropriate safeguards are implemented to ensure aseptic conditions, the risk of serious adverse events such as endophthalmitis would be no different in an office-based surgery setting than in a facility-based surgery setting.

Data from two large-scale prospective studies totaling approximately 39,000 cataract or refractive lens surgeries have demonstrated the safety and effectiveness of performing these procedures in ophthalmologists’ offices [10, 11]. In addition, a retrospective analysis has been performed of 32,733 cases from patients undergoing office-based ophthalmic surgeries (cataract, refractive lens exchange, phakic intraocular lens, oculoplastics, glaucoma, retina and cornea) at 49 private practices in the U.S. This analysis of real-world cases, which included 19,989 cataract, 8744 refractive lens exchange, and 1629 phakic intraocular lens implantation, demonstrated that office-based surgery can be performed safely and with complication rates similar to or lower than that reported in the literature for facility-based surgery [19].

Administration of the travoprost intracameral implant is straightforward, with a small ab interno corneal incision that is designed to be self-sealing, administration of viscoelastic, anchoring of the implant through the trabecular meshwork into the sclera using the pre-loaded inserter, followed by irrigation and aspiration of the anterior chamber to remove the viscoelastic [15].

In our study, excellent safety outcomes were observed; all administration procedures were successful, there were no reports of early or late intraocular infection, or serious adverse events in the study eye. The proportion of study eyes with ocular adverse events in our study (32.4%) is similar to that reported previously (35.7%) for the travoprost intracameral implant over a 12-month period [16].

We acknowledge that our study is not without limitations. Most notably, the subgroup of patients administered the travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgery setting was small (i.e., only 37 patients). Another limitation may be that this study was conducted at three sites only and may not be generalizable to other ophthalmic practices.

In summary, as the U.S. population ages and more ophthalmic and non-ophthalmic surgeries vie for time in facility-based surgery settings, it is anticipated that economic pressures to contain costs and improved technology will force the transition of many ophthalmic procedures, including those of glaucoma procedures, including MIGS and interventional pharmaceuticals such as the travoprost intracameral implant, into office-based surgery settings.

Conclusions

In our subgroup analysis of 37 patients administered a travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgery setting of three ophthalmologists, the implant was administered successfully, with no reports of ocular infection, and an incidence of ocular adverse events similar to that reported previously in patients administered the implant in a facility-based setting.

This study has provided preliminary evidence of the feasibility of administering the travoprost intracameral implant in an office-based surgical setting utilizing appropriate aseptic technique and adhering to applicable safety standards.
