Spatial multi-omics analyses of the tumor immune microenvironment: advances and novel therapeutic avenues
Authors: Haoyu Wang, Yuze Zhu, Maoqian Fang, Xiaoli Zhang, Peng Su, Hongyuan Liang, Dan Dong, Lin Li, Kefeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is a spatially organized ecosystem in which the distribution and interaction of cellular and molecular components have a significant impact on disease progression and therapeutic response. Traditionally, bulk analyses have failed to capture the critical spatial context governing these dynamics. The latest breakthroughs in spatial multi-omics technologies, including spatial transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics, have completely transformed our ability to characterize the TIME at unprecedented resolution. These advanced platforms enable precise mapping of immune cell distribution, reconstruction of intercellular communication networks, and identification of pattern structures such as tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS). We reviewed the current experimental methods of spatial omics and highlights how their applications provide novel insights into tumor biology. We further discussed how insights from spatial profiling could inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies aimed at actively remodeling the TIME, provide information for prognosis prediction, and shape responses to treatments such as immunotherapy. Despite the challenges in standardization and clinical translation, spatial multi-omics provides an indispensable dimension for understanding tumor immunobiology, paving the way for more precise tumor diagnosis, prognosis and treatment methods.

Background

Tumors constitute a complex ecosystem, encompassing multiple cell types and intricate interactions among various components [1]. The various immune components that compose tumors are specifically defined as the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [2], including immune cells, non-immune cells, cytokines, chemokines, blood vessels, and extracellular matrix (ECM), which are interrelated, coexist and compete with each other [3]. Bidirectional crosstalk between tumor cells and surrounding stromal components in the TIME serves as a key determinant of disease progression and therapeutic response [4]. However, the biological characteristics of the TIME are not only determined by the abundance of cells, but also by precise organization and multi-level spatial relationships, which govern functional interdependence at different scales (Fig. 1) [5–7]. An in-depth understanding of the spatial architecture of the TIME is crucial for elucidating its functional mechanisms and differences in therapeutic responses. Neighborhoods are spatially distinct niches characterized by unique cellular compositions, molecular signatures, and functional states [8]. In the TIME of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the spatial neighborhoods with abundant or scarce immune cells significantly determine the treatment response. They either promote immune activation or form resistance barriers [9]. Proximity analysis refers to an analytical framework used to quantify the spatial co-localization and distance relationships between two or more biological entities, with the objective of deciphering their interaction patterns and phenotypic significance [10]. Proximity analysis in colorectal cancer (CRC) and gastric cancer (GC) has shown that metastatic subclones often originate from specific, deeper regions of the primary tumor, and the immune microenvironment is spatially remodeled during metastasis [11]. The cellular niche is a dynamic and functional concept, denoting a distinct spatial location and microenvironment that sustains specific cell types and governs their fate [12]. The cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) create a pro-tumor cellular niche by undergoing metabolic reprogramming to fuel tumor growth, and simultaneously secreting certain factors that establish an immunosuppressive and pro-inflammatory microenvironment. This dual effect drives a self-reinforcing cycle of tumor proliferation, immune evasion, and metastasis [13].

The latest breakthrough in spatial omics technology has completely revolutionized the characterization of the TIME at an unprecedented resolution. Compared with the early research that adopted traditional techniques, cutting-edge methods such as spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed fluorescence imaging can now enable high-throughput, multi-omics integration at single-cell resolution. These advanced platforms not only precisely map the spatial distribution patterns of immune subsets, but also reconstruct three-dimensional interaction networks, providing a novel dimension for deciphering the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune regulation within TIME. Therefore, exploring the cutting-edge technologies in spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics helps to understand their principles, advantages, and limitations in resolving TIME architecture.

This article focused on the latest advances in spatial omics and the emerging applications of novel research tools. These approaches have led to key discoveries, such as the compartmentalization of tumors and immune cells, and the organization and dynamic spatial remodeling of pattern structures like TLS during tumor progression and metastasis. We also discussed how the spatial features of the TIME influence clinical outcomes, including their roles in prognosis prediction and treatment response.

Current experimental approaches of spatial omics

Spatial multi-omics technologies can help tackle the key problems in TIME research, which are difficult to be addressed through either holistic or single-cell approaches. These issues include: How the spatial organization of cells in tissues impacts their functional states and interactions; How the heterogeneous molecular signatures in defined niches drive tissue functions and disease outcomes; And how spatial context regulates the evolution of the gene regulatory networks [14, 15]. Compared with traditional single-cell RNA-seq, which obliterates spatial relationships or holistic models that tend to treat biological networks as a completely mixed system, integrating spatial information brings a paradigm shift in multiple aspects.

It resolves the cellular heterogeneity within tissue context, uncovering gene expression gradients, cell type zonation, and specific niche states, which are often confused in bulk or single-cell analyses. It also decodes the spatial organization of the biochemical networks, demonstrating how the localization of enzymes or substrates can alter pathway kinetics, and enabling novel regulatory behaviors such as ultra-sensitivity and pattern formation. Moreover, spatial integration enhances inference and modeling accuracy by correcting inherent biases in temporal or dissociative analyses. This can be demonstrated by tools such as MUSE and spatial proteomics, which are capable of identifying disease biomarkers associated with specific tissue microdomains. Finally, it achieves multi-scale integration from subcellular compartments to tissue-level phenotypes, providing information for therapeutic targeting of niches in diseases such as glioma.

Overall, spatial multi-omics not only uniquely addresses TIME relevant questions, but also fundamentally shifts systems biology by preserving space as a foundational dimension, integrating molecular details with architectural context for both basic discoveries and translational innovations [10, 16]. Major technical methods in spatial multi-omics

Spatial transcriptomics

Spatial transcriptomics is a technique that resolves the characteristics of transcriptomics while preserving the spatial context of tissues. It can precisely locate gene expression within tissues, thereby revealing the spatial distribution of cell types, intercellular interactions, and regional expression profiles [38]. This approach is crucial for understanding the developmental process and disease mechanisms. This field emerged from the parallel advancements of genomics and microscopy. On the one hand, sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics (sST) utilizes DNA barcoding to locally capture RNA from tissue sections followed by next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based gene decoding. On the other hand, imaging-based spatial transcriptomics (IST) employs fluorescence in situ sequencing (ISS) or direct in situ hybridization (ISH) to detect mRNA transcripts within intact tissues [39, 40].

Although the two principal methods (sST and IST) differ in the number of detectable genes and the size of compatible tissues, they both generate spatially resolved gene expression matrices and exhibit convergent technical elements. The sST approach relies on the positional encoding of transcripts before NGS, while IST encompasses strategies of in situ amplification sequencing and sequential probe hybridization. Although their technical implementations are different, these complementary platforms provide synergistic gene expression measurements. Their standardized classification frameworks and validation metrics collectively jointly drive the development of this field (Fig. 2) [6, 41].

NGS-based spatial transcriptomics methods

Spatial transcriptomics technology was first developed in 2016 [42], integrating microarrays and NGS, and can enable spatially resolved gene expression analysis. This approach employs barcode glass slides containing an array of microwells or spots, each of which is coated with oligonucleotides with unique spatial barcodes and poly (dT) tails for mRNA capture. Tissue sections are placed on arrays, and RNA was released through osmosis, which then binds to poly (dT) primers. Through reverse transcription, cDNA is synthesized on the slide and bound to spatial barcodes. Subsequent sequencing allows the transcripts to be mapped back to their original tissue locations based on these barcodes. This method enables unbiased wide-space transcriptome analysis by utilizing spatial index surfaces (each position marked by DNA primers with unique barcodes), thereby promoting two-dimensional reconstruction of gene expression patterns within tissues.

Spatial transcriptomics has developed rapidly through the innovation of barcode strategies and the improvement of resolution. The Visium platform of 10x Genomics was first introduced in 2018. It is a representative platform for whole transcriptome capture, with spot diameters ranging from 55 to 100 micrometers, capable of covering approximately 3 to 10 cells, and suitable for delineating large compartments within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [42]. However, the mixed signals within each spot could obscure the proximity relationships between the cells. For instance, if a spot contains both CD8 + T cells and PD-L1 + tumor cells, it is impossible to determine whether they are in direct contact with each other. Subsequently, in 2019, Visium HD emerged, further reducing the feature sizes to 2 μm² [17]. Meanwhile, to achieve cellular or subcellular resolution, bead-based methods have emerged. Slide-seq employs 10-micrometer beads, which are large enough to distinguish individual cells, but its RNA capture efficiency is relatively low. In the earlier version, the detection rate of low-expression genes was lower than 30% of that of Visium [43]. In 2021, Slide-seqV2 significantly improved its sensitivity by optimizing the magnetic bead synthesis technology, achieving a tenfold improvement. However, fresh frozen tissue is still required, and it cannot be directly applied to the conventional archived clinical FFPE samples [18]. In 2022, Stereo-seq achieved the highest resolution using DNA nanoballs with a spacing of 220 nanometers, enabling the subcellular localization of RNA. However, the massive data volume and sparsity resulting from such extreme resolution pose significant challenges for bioinformatics analysis, and it has not yet been widely used for FFPE samples [6, 19].

IST technologies

IST techniques mainly include ISS and ISH. The ISS method achieves direct transcript sequencing within tissues by reverse transcribing mRNA into cDNA, amplifying it through rolling circle amplification (RCA), and decoding the sequence using barcoding strategies such as sequencing-by-ligation (SBL) or sequencing-by-hybridization (SBH) [44]. Early ISS approaches, such as gap-targeted and barcode-targeted sequencing, relied on padlock probes for gene-specific detection, but required prior knowledge of transcript sequences. Although ISS has achieved single-cell resolution, its multiplexing capacity is limited by optical crowding, and the detection efficiency remains moderate (5%-30%) [38]. Subsequent advancements, such as HybISS and SCRINSHOT, have enhanced the signal-to-noise ratio, bypassed reverse transcription to enhance efficiency [45].

For untargeted transcriptome-wide profiling, fluorescent ISS (FISSEQ) was developed, enabling de novo detection of approximately 8,000 mRNA species. although its detection efficiency is limited due to interference from rRNA [20]. Expansion sequencing (ExSeq) later combined FISSEQ with expansion microscopy, physically separating RNAs and achieving approximately 60% detection efficiency, while boloramis extended the detection to non-coding RNAs [21]. However, this improvement comes at a cost. The expansion process will alter the original spatial structure of the tissue, thereby making it difficult to accurately determine the membrane contact distance between immune cells and tumor cells. STARmap has further advanced 3D spatial analysis by integrating hydrogel-based tissue embedding and error-correcting SEDAL sequencing, enabling the detection of over 1,000 genes with an efficiency comparable to that of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) [22]. Despite the trade-offs between throughput and resolution, these methods collectively enhance spatial resolution transcriptomics, and emerging hybrids (ExSeq and NGS) blurring the lines between imaging and sequencing-based approaches.

Spatial transcriptomics technology originated from the ISH method, which uses labeled nucleic acid probes to determine the spatial distribution and expression levels of DNA and RNA in biological tissues and cells. Early techniques, such as single-molecule fluorescence ISH (smFISH), utilized fluorescent probes to hybridize with target RNA molecules for precise localization and quantification under a microscope [23, 46]. However, smFISH ‘s ability to simultaneously detect multiple genes is limited. To overcome this limitation, sequential FISH (SeqFISH) was developed [47], allowing for multi-gene detection through multiple hybridization rounds. However, SeqFISH is time-consuming and expensive. Zhuang’s lab was the first to introduce multiplexed error-robust FISH (MERFISH) in 2015 [48], which uses an N-bit binary coding system. It hybridizes encoding probes with specific readout sequences with mRNA targets, followed by multiple rounds of fluorescent readout probe hybridization and cleavage to decode the unique barcode of each transcript [24]. This method incorporates error-correction through a Modified Hamming Distance of 4 (MHD4) scheme, maintaining four constant bit-1 signals for each gene. When MERFISH is used in combination with an amplification microscope, it can detect up to 10,000 transcripts. However, it requires specialized equipment and, due to the wide use of probes, is challenged by the signal-to-background ratio (SBR).

Parallel developments include seqFISH and its enhanced version from the Cai lab, which utilize combinatorial color-coding through successive hybridization rounds with distinct fluorophores [49]. Hybridization chain reaction (HCR)-seqFISH improved detection efficiency by incorporating HCR amplification, while seqFISH+ significantly expanded multiplexing capacity by distributing 60 pseudocolors across three fluorescent channels, enabling 10,000-RNA detection through an elaborate four-barcode-round system. Split-FISH from the Chen lab introduced an innovative approach using split probes, which generates signals only when adjacent and significantly improves SBR by reducing non-specific binding [25]. Meanwhile, Nanostring’s spatial molecular imaging (SMI) technology employs a branch amplification system with photocleavable reporters, allowing the simultaneous detection of RNA and protein in clinical samples [50]. Although these methods vary in technical implementation, ranging from binary coding to colorimetric schemes, they all face common challenges, including probe complexity, equipment requirements, and signal optimization.

The “Xenium in situ analysis platform” developed by 10x Genomics is a hybrid technology that combines ISH with sequencing. Its core technical workflow is based on padlock probes with gene-specific barcodes. These probes must simultaneously hybridize to both ends of the target RNA sequence to achieve a stable connection, thereby ensuring high detection specificity. Subsequently, the signals of the successfully ligated probes were amplified through RCA. Then, multiple rounds of cyclic fluorescence probe hybridization, imaging, and chemical stripping were carried out to generate unique optical characteristics for each target gene. This design enables precise spatial localization and identification of up to 5,000 genes simultaneously with a 22.5 mm × 10.5 mm tissue area at the subcellular resolution. Since its launch in 2022, it has become a key tool in spatial transcriptomics research [26, 27].

Spatial proteomics

Spatial proteomics employs cutting-edge techniques to map and study the distribution and expression patterns of protein in cells and tissue structures, and can be classified into antibody-based spatial proteomics or mass spectrometry (MS)-based spatial proteomics (Fig. 3). The initially established antibody-based spatial proteomics enable the visualization of proteins by sequentially cycling the detection of fluorophore-tagged antibodies through staining, imaging, and fluorophore removal, with throughput ranging from tens to hundreds of proteins. For instance, the techniques include MACSima imaging cyclic staining (MICS) [51], tissue-based cyclic immunofluorescence (t-CycIF) [52], InSituPlex [53], and Multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC) [54]. However, the restricted channel capacity and the limited antigen detection per imaging cycle require repetitive and multistage experimental procedures. Alternatively, DNA-labeled antibody-based approaches such as Co-Detection by IndEXing (CODEX, commercialized as phenocycler) [55, 56], cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) [28], offer broader applicability and scalability for large-scale multiplexed spatial proteomics analysis.

MS-based spatial proteomics is a highly sensitive molecular imaging technique that can directly identify and spatially map proteins on tissues, single cells, or material surfaces, providing spatially resolved proteomic insights. The main advantages include label-free detection, high sensitivity, and the ability to image multiple molecules simultaneously [29]. Key methods include imaging mass cytometry (IMC) [57] and multiplex ion beam imaging (MIBI) [30], which utilize metal-tagged antibodies to detect proteins through isotopic mass signals. IMC combines laser ablation with CyTOF mass cytometry, while MIBI uses secondary ion MS (SIMS) to achieve higher spatial resolution. Both of these techniques can achieve single-cell proteomic profiling, advancing research on TIME and disease mechanisms.

In addition to metal-tagging, non-targeted MS methods maximize proteome coverage. Liquid chromatography-MS (LC-MS) is used to separates complex protein mixtures, but it has limitations in terms of automation and clinical scalability [31]. MS imaging (MSI), including Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption (MALDI)-MSI [32] and desorption electrospray ionization (DESI)-MSI [58], allows label-free spatial analysis of metabolites, lipids, and proteins. Although MALDI-MSI has a limited resolution (5–50 μm), it performs well in high-throughput biomarker detection. DESI-MSI can eliminate the matrix effect, but it will sacrifice the resolution. The mixed signals from multiple cells can obscure the fine structure of the boundaries of the TIME. SIMS has achieved extremely high resolution, but it has some issues with protein ionization efficiency, which primarily applicable for the detection of elements and small molecules [59]. To enhance accuracy, laser capture microdissection (LCM)-MS isolates specific tissue regions or single cells, minimizing contamination and preserving spatial data [60]. Despite advancements, challenges still exist in peptide identification, signal overlap, and quantitative accuracy due to sample heterogeneity.

Spatial metabolomics

Metabolomics methods represent a revolutionary approach to cell analysis, combining single-cell resolution with spatial imaging to investigate metabolic activities and intercellular interactions [61]. This technology leverages advanced techniques such as MSI, selective cell sampling (SCS), and three-dimensional spatially resolved metabolomic profiling (3D-SMF) to overcome challenges such as small sample size and high sensitivity requirements [62, 63]. In the MSI method, SIMS, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI), and DESI are pivotal. SIMS performs well in spatial resolution (down to 50 nm), but it faces limitations in metabolite identification due to ion fragmentation [64]. MALDI, with its matrix-assisted ionization, visualizes subcellular lipids and proteins, but is subject to interference in a low-mass range [33]. DESI is an ambient ionization technique that simplifies sample preparation and is an ideal choice for lipid profiling, despite its relatively low resolution (~ 50–200 μm). Innovative technologies such as nano-DESI and air flow-assisted DESI (AFADESI) enhance resolution and metabolite coverage [34].

Spatial metabolomics extends traditional metabolomics by preserving the spatial distribution of molecules, which is critical for studying cancer metabolism, drug distribution, and disease mechanisms. Unlike antibody-based imaging (MIBI/IMC), MSI techniques such as MALDI-MSI and DESI-MSI offer label-free, multiplexed detection of metabolites, lipids, and peptides. The resolution of MALDI-MSI reaches 10 μm and it is widely used in proteomics and lipidomics. The ambient analysis of DESI-MSI is suitable for clinical applications, such as tumor margin detection [35, 65, 66]. AFADESI-MSI further extends metabolite detection to over 1,000 compounds, filling the gap in DESI’s capabilities [67]. Although these methods have advantages, they face challenges in quantification, dynamic range, and sample preparation (FFPE tissue artifacts in MALDI). Emerging technologies such as 3D OrbiSIMS and SEAM improved 3D metabolite mapping and single-nuclei analysis, advancing research in TIME and cellular heterogeneity [68, 69].

Spatial epigenomics

Spatial epigenomics aims to elucidate the 3D structure of the genome within the nucleus and the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of gene expression. It focuses on the higher-order folding patterns of chromatin in the physical space, such as the formation of topological association domains [70]. These structures bring distant regulatory elements in close spatial proximity, thereby enabling precise spatiotemporal control of gene expression. This field not only investigates the spatial distribution of classical epigenetic markers, including DNA methylation and histone modifications, but also attempts to decipher how these modifications collectively shape and maintain the spatial architecture of chromatin, ultimately influencing critical cellular processes [71]. By integrating multi-dimensional information, spatial epigenomics ultimately strives to construct a comprehensive regulatory map that connects molecular modifications, 3D structures, and gene functions, offering a novel 3D perspective for understanding the mechanisms of development, differentiation, and disease occurrence.

The key spatial epigenomics methods include spatial-ATAC-RNA-seq, spatial-CUT&Tag-RNA-seq, and epigenomic MERFISH. The first two methods utilize the deterministic tissue barcoding through microfluidic chips to deliver two orthogonal oligonucleotide barcode sets onto the tissue section, thereby creating a two-dimensional pixel matrix and achieving a spatial resolution-based combined analysis of the epigenome-transcriptome within the entire genome range. The former employs Tn5 transposase for in situ chromatin accessibility labeling, while the latter uses antibodies targeting histone modifications, followed by Protein A–Tn5 for targeted tagmentation [37, 72, 73]. Both of these methods involve tissue fixation, permeabilization, and in situ reverse transcription, followed by streptavidin-based separation of cDNA and genomic DNA for library preparation. Using microfluidic chips with 50 or 100 channels, these approaches can achieve near-single-cell resolution (20 μm pixels), detecting 1,000–2,000 genes and 2,000–5,000 UMIs per pixel in brain tissues, along with 10,000–20,000 epigenomic fragments with ATAC-seq TSS enrichment of 15–25%. This platform is capable of conducting research on spatio-temporal epigenetic regulation in complex tissues [36]. The epigenomic MERFISH, when combined with the targeted cleavage and labeling CUT&Tag technology, can achieve single-cell epigenomic profiling at the cellular level in complex tissues. This approach can systematically map the profiles of hundreds of epigenetic loci, revealing their subnuclear distribution and providing high-resolution spatial localization of active promoters and putative enhancers [74].

Spatial omics data analysis

Spatial omics data analysis integrates methodologies from image analysis and traditional omics, particularly single-cell transcriptomics, to generate insights that surpass those obtained from separate analyses. The typical workflow primarily includes: (1) Identification of cells or other biologically relevant structures within the sample; (2) Molecular profiling and classification; (3) Identification of spatial patterns and interactions. Data analysis begins with rigorous experimental design and quality control, among which cell segmentation is a critical step [75]. This process utilizes nuclear staining agents (such as DAPI) or membrane/cytoplasmic markers, combined with traditional watershed algorithms or modern machine learning tools based on pre-trained models (such as Cellpose), to accurately delineate cell boundaries [76, 77]. The resulting matrices of RNA counts or antibody fluorescence intensity of each cell form the basis for subsequent analysis.

During the cell classification and clustering stage, spatial data can be processed using mature tools originally developed for scRNA-seq. The Seurat software developed in R language demonstrates excellent performance in batch effect correction and reference mapping using its “anchor” framework, making it suitable for studies with existing single-cell atlases. The latest version v5 has improved memory efficiency through a new layer structure and enhanced compatibility with large datasets [78]. The Scanpy software developed in Python prioritizes scalability, handling millions of cells via the anndata object and supporting trajectory inference through PAGA [79]. For spatially resolved data, Squidpy extends Scanpy by incorporating spatial graphs and image analysis capabilities, enabling tissue quantification and molecular-image integration [80]. In addition, Giotto provides an end-to-end pipeline with interactive visualization modules for describing tissue composition, spatial expression patterns, and cellular interactions [81].

To conduct a thorough exploration of spatial patterns, the analytical methods mainly involve identifying the differences between various spatial regions, analyzing the gradient changes relative to a specific reference point, and examining the interactions between cells. Tools such as Squidpy and Giotto provide fundamental functions for calculating spatial autocorrelation, constructing spatial network graphs, and analyzing cell co-localization. Meanwhile, specialized algorithms are constantly emerging. For instance, SPARK [82], SpatialDE [83], and CellTrek [84] are used to identify spatial domains, while tools such as CellPhoneDB [85], NicheNet [86], and CellChat [87] infer cell-cell communication by integrating curated receptor-ligand interaction databases. Spatial omics data enhance the biological validity of these inferences by providing precise proximity information. In the field of machine learning, graph convolutional networks such as SpaGCN integrate gene expression, spatial location, and histological features to identify spatial domains and discover spatially variable genes, achieve higher sensitivity for subtle spatial patterns, but require careful parameter tuning and are more computationally intensive [88].

To address the challenges in integrating multimodal data and conducting cross-sample analysis, recently developed algorithms and frameworks have provided targeted solutions. BANKSY integrates spatial and molecular features through a neighborhood-based embedding technique, achieving batch-corrected clustering. NicheCompass utilizes graph-based deep learning to simulate cell-cell interactions and learn the spatially-informed cell embeddings [89, 90]. SpatialGlue employs a graph neural network with a dual-attention mechanism to integrate multiple spatial omics modalities from the same tissue Sect. [91]. Meanwhile, SLAT aligns heterogeneous spatial datasets generated by different technologies or conditions through a graph adversarial matching method, achieving high accuracy and speed [92].

To ensure the reproducibility and standardization of analyses, a framework adhering to the FAIR principles has been established, such as SpatialData. These frameworks provide standardized data storage, manipulation, and integration, which can be used in conjunction with downstream tools [93]. Furthermore, the modular analysis pipelines represented by nf-core offers an automated and reproducible workflow from raw data to interpretable results through containerization and continuous integration testing [94]. Although significant progress has been made, spatial omics data analysis still faces numerous challenges, including data heterogeneity, batch effects, computational complexity, and the standardized integration of multi-modal data. Continuous algorithm innovation and workflow optimization are of vital importance for fully realizing its potential in fields such as precision medicine.

Integration of spatial multi-omics

Single-cell multi-omics can provide high resolution snapshots of molecular regulation, but they overlook the spatial organizational structure that determines how cells interact and function within tissues. Meanwhile, a series of continuously evolving spatial omics technologies are now capable of locating individual molecular layers across tissue sections. However, relying on computational alignment between different sections cannot solve the problem of the interconnections between different biological modules. Spatial multi-omics overcomes this limitation. It can simultaneously measure two or more omics layers, such as transcriptome and proteome, metabolome, or epigenome, from the identical tissue section.

Integrating transcriptome and proteome

Integrating spatial transcriptomics with spatial proteomics provides a powerful multi-dimensional strategy for dissecting the TIME, as each technique addresses distinct but interconnected biological regulatory layers. Although spatial transcriptomics provides a global map of gene expression across tissue sections, capable of capturing regional heterogeneity of cell states and immune activities, it cannot fully predict protein abundance, post-translational modifications, or functional dynamics, which all require direct measurement through spatial proteomics. By overlaying two datasets from the same tissue section, researchers can distinguish between transcriptional regulation and protein level control, identify regions where post-transcriptional inhibition or excessive activation of immune function occurs, and more accurately locate immune excluded or exhausted niches [95, 96].

At present, there are several emerging platforms that can achieve this kind of joint analysis, although there are varying degrees of differences in resolution, processing speed and multiple detection capabilities. For instance, Visium-based methods such as SPOTS and SM-Omics, can parallelly detect dozens of proteins as well as the entire transcriptome in fresh frozen or FFPE sections, although they typically operate at the multi-cellular resolution. NanoString GeoMx DSP supports high plex RNA and protein assays within the user-defined regions of interest, but single-cell resolution remains a challenge [97]. In contrast, chip-based approaches such as spatial CITE-seq achieve co-mapping of up to 200–300 proteins and the full poly(A) transcriptome at a pixel resolution of 10–20 μm, offering the highest protein throughput among spatial multi-omics techniques [98]. Meanwhile, imaging-based platforms such as CosMx, MERSCOPE and Xenium, which are increasingly commercialized, are now capable of simultaneously detecting hundreds of transcripts and dozens of proteins at single-cell resolution, making spatial co-analysis much more accessible [99, 100].

Integrating transcriptome and metabolomics

Integrating spatial transcriptomics with spatial metabolomics provides a complementary and powerful framework for characterizing the TIME. Transcriptomics can achieve nearly single-cell-level mapping of cellular identity, functional status, and intercellular communication, while metabolomics directly captures metabolic activities, availability of nutrients, and signaling metabolites that affect the behavior of immune cells. Applying these two methods to the same tissue section allows the direct association of gene expression programs with local metabolite distribution, revealing regulatory layers that cannot be discovered by either method alone [6, 101].

For instance, Vicari et al. [102] employed a spatial multimodal analysis workflow to link neurotransmitter associated gene modules with metabolite distributions in Parkinson’s disease. This approach can be extended to uncover immunosuppressive or pro-inflammatory metabolic circuits in the TIME. Similarly, Zhi et al. [103] combined these two spatial techniques to map the malignant progression from carcinoma in situ (CIS) to partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in oral submucous fibrosis derived oral squamous cell carcinoma. The discovery of abnormal polyamine metabolism is a key driving factor for tumorigenesis and immune escape. Zheng et al. [104] further applied this integrative approach to injured human brain tissue, revealing metabolic heterogeneity and complex transcriptional regulation. These findings are helpful for functional gene analysis. Therefore, spatial integration connects the pathways with metabolism, thereby enabling the discovery of biomarkers and targets for the TIME.

Integrating transcriptome and epigenome

To comprehensively describe the TIME, spatial transcriptomics and spatial epigenomics offer complementary insights, jointly linking the dynamic gene expression with the underlying regulatory logic. Transcriptomics reveals the abundance of global mRNAs and the activity of pathways, but it cannot explain why cells with the same genes exhibit different expression states. Spatial epigenomics addresses this issue by directly mapping DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility, and histone modifications within the tissue architecture. These factors determine which regulatory elements are active.

For instance, spatial ATAC&RNA-seq can simultaneously profile chromatin accessibility and mRNA expression on the same cryosection, linking the open chromatin landscape with transcriptional output [105]. Spatial CUT&Tag-RNA-seq further extends this technology by capturing specific histone modifications and poly(A) transcripts via microfluidic deterministic barcoding in tissues, generating two-dimensional pixels with spatial encoding markers [106]. These methods have been applied in the studies of developing and adult brain tissues, revealing how the epigenetic state shapes cell identities and undergoes dynamic changes. However, they are limited by the small size of the analyzable areas, inter pixel gaps, and the requirement for expertise in micro-manufacturing technology. Meanwhile, imaging-based methods, such as DNA-MERFISH and DNA-seqFISH+, can simultaneously visualize thousands of genomic loci, nascent transcripts, and nuclear structures at the subcellular level [107, 108]. These approaches reveal that active genes are usually located on the surface of nuclear body, and the global chromatin state persists independently of cell type. In clinical applications, laser capture microdissection combined with epigenomic transcriptome co-profiling has been employed in the study of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), uncovering the spatial epigenetic driving factors of metastasis and prognosis [109]. Therefore, their integration forms a multi-level framework for understanding how epigenetic programming coordinates the location, activation, and dysfunction of immune cells in native tissue niches.

Studies of tumor progression using spatial omics

Spatial imaging reveals compartmentalization of tumor and immune cells

Solid tumors exhibit complex heterogeneous architectures, composed of unevenly distributed tumor cells, immune cells, stromal components, and vascular networks. Through conventional histological analysis, distinct tumor cell-dense regions and stroma-rich zones can be observed. The current classification system mainly describes the distribution of immune cells based on the spatial localization of specific tumor tissue compartments, as these distinct spatial compartments critically determine their functional interactions with malignant cells, other immune populations, and various TME elements.

Spatial imaging has established compartmentalization as a key determinant of immune function. In various types of cancer, spatial omics studies had demonstrated that CD8 + T cells located in different compartments exhibit radically different functional patterns. In breast cancer (BC), Gruosso et al. [110] identified four distinct TIME subtypes in TNBC: immune desert (ID), margin-restricted (MR), stroma-restricted (SR), and fully inflamed (FI). It is crucial that only the last mode, where T cells can reach the epithelial cell compartment of the tumor, is associated with the signatures of type I interferon, cytotoxic activity, and a favorable prognosis. T cells that are confined to the stromal compartment do not have this benefit. This principle also applies to lung cancer. Using IMC, Enfield et al. [111] identified an immune excluded TME pattern in lung squamous cell carcinoma. The characteristic of this pattern is that the fibroblast barriers physically restrict CD8 + T cells to the peritumoral stroma, preventing them from entering the tumor cell compartments. Patients with this compartmentalization pattern, despite having a large number of T cells, still have a poor prognosis. This result strongly indicates that this compartment is a key determinant of function.

Spatial imaging has further identified the specific cellular architects that are responsible for constructing and maintaining compartment boundaries. The boundaries separating immune cells from tumor cells are not passive anatomical structures. On the contrary, spatial omics studies has revealed that these boundaries are actively constructed by specific non-immune cell populations. Using single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, Croizer et al. [112] discovered that FAP+ CAFs in BC exhibit plasticity in their spatial organization. Detox-iCAFs located in the perivascular compartment serve as a reservoir. When in contact with tumor cells, they convert into ECM-myCAFs, thereby remodeling the ECM and forming a physical barrier, effectively confining immune cells within the stromal compartment and excluding them from the tumor nests. Meanwhile, Enfield et al. [111] also used IMC to demonstrate that fibroblast barriers are the primary structures that maintain CD8 + T cells compartmentalization to the stroma in lung cancer. Extending these findings to CRC, Schurch et al. [113] used CODEX technique to show that tumors can also actively remodel cellular neighborhoods, that is, the local cellular compartments, concentrating immunosuppressive cells in specific niches, thereby interfering with the anti-tumor immune response.

Spatial omics has also revealed that this compartmentalization is also applicable to tumor cells themselves, manifesting at metabolic and epigenetic levels. Using MSI, Santos et al. [114] proved that in hepatocellular carcinoma, tumor cells located in different spatial compartments adopt distinct metabolic programs: the immune-activated compartment upregulates the urea cycle, the immune-deficient compartment enhances nucleotide synthesis, and the immune-suppressed compartment shifts to mitochondrial respiration. This spatial metabolic compartmentalization is not merely a related phenomenon; it is an immune evasion mechanism.

On a more fundamental level, spatial epigenomics has traced compartmentalization to chromatin regulation. Using the spatial ATAC-seq technique, researchers found that in glioblastoma, the core compartment of the tumor is mainly dominated by chromatin accessibility controlled by AP-1. This accessibility can promote mesenchymal differentiation and immunosuppressive effects. The peripheral compartment is rich in open chromatin related to neurons 115. Therefore, spatial omics studies has shown that the compartment to which the tumor cells belong can predict their epigenetic state, and in turn affect their interaction with the immune system.

Patterned structures of the TIME

Emerging spatial profiling technologies now enable molecular dissection of TIME patterns and macrostructures features, and have long been regarded as having diagnostic and prognostic value. For instance, TLS is the location where tumor antigens are presented to T cells and has been identified in several tumor types 116. Andersson et al. [117] identified the formation of TLS characterized by co-localization of B cells and T cells in HER2-positive BC using spatial transcriptomics analysis. Immunohistochemical validation revealed these regions exhibited typical architectural features of B-cell clusters surrounded by T-cell zones, while lacking well-developed germinal centers, suggesting an immature TLS phenotype. Researchers developed a sophisticated linear regression model based on gene expression patterns, identifying a robust 171 gene signature, including established TLS markers such as MS4A1 (CD20), B2M, and TRBC2. This predictive model demonstrated significant universality and effectively identified TLS regions across different technological platforms (visium) and cancer types (melanoma). It is worth noting that in the TCGA melanoma dataset, TLS signature recapitulated its established association with favorable clinical outcomes.

Meylan et al. [118] demonstrated that TLS in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) serves as a functional hub for B cell maturation, clonal selection, and PC generation, which can be proven by somatic hypermutation, isotype switching, and clonal expansion of B cells in TLS. Spatial transcriptomics and multiplex imaging revealed that PCs, particularly IgG-producing cells, disseminate along the CXCL12-positive fibroblastic tracks into the tumor beds. Their antibodies bind to tumor cells and are associated with caspase-3-mediated apoptosis and macrophage proximity, suggesting antibody-dependent cytotoxicity. Importantly, high levels of IgG-bound tumor cells were associated with improved response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and prolonged progression-free survival (PFS), highlighting that TLS-driven humoral immunity is a key determinant of anti-tumor immunity and therapeutic efficacy. These findings underscore the potential of targeting TLS and antibody responses to enhance cancer immunotherapy.

Other studies have shifted focus to the dynamics around tumors, complex regions characterized by heterogeneous cell populations, dynamic intercellular communications, and different phenotypic expressions. Gouin et al. [119] identified a unique epithelial subpopulation characterized by high expression of CDH12 in bladder cancer through single-nuclei RNA sequencing, which exhibited a distinctive diameter response to the main treatment methods. Due to chemotherapy resistance and fibroblast activation, CDH12-enriched tumors are associated with poor prognosis after surgery or neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). However, through the spatial co-localization of PD-L1/PD-L2 expression and CD49a interaction with exhausted CD8 + T cells, it shows a superior response to immune checkpoint therapy (ICT).

Pelka et al. [120] revealed distinct multicellular immune hubs in CRC through scRNA-seq and spatial profiling. Driven by chemokine-mediated neutrophil recruitment and fibroblast activation, a shared myeloid-rich inflammatory hub was identified at the luminal margin of mismatch repair-deficient (MMRd) and mismatch repair-proficient (MMRp) tumors. Additionally, MMRd-specific CXCR3-ligand+ immune hotspots were discovered, in which CXCL13 + T cells co-localize with IFNγ-responsive malignant cells and myeloid cells, forming a positive feedback loop to enhance T cells recruitment and anti-tumor immunity.

These findings emphasized spatially organized immune interactions, and also reinforced the biological value of those located at the edge of the tumor domain.

Spatial architecture of the TIME and tumor biology

The spatial architecture of the TIME plays a pivotal role in the initiation, expansion, invasion, and metastasis of tumors. In the early stage of tumor formation, the immune system recognizes the genetic alterations in precancerous cells and initiates targeted immune responses to eliminate these aberrant cells. However, in the case of immunosuppression, which is usually caused by persistent inflammatory conditions or viral infections, the spatial distribution is affected. The reduction of immune cells and IL-6-driven JAK/STAT3 signaling in monocytes enables abnormal cells to evade immune destruction, promoting the progression of CIS [121]. The TIME in CIS displays a unique distribution pattern of immune cells formed by variations in the composition of tumor cells, emphasizing its significance in the transition to malignant tumors. As the tumor expands, the distribution of immune cells changes, with some forming CNs that enhance anti-tumor immunity or promote immunosuppression, although their exact impact remains unclear [122]. During invasion, the EMT and immune interactions at the tumor margin are critical, with the invasive margin (IM) often exhibiting stronger immune activity than tumor core (TC), resembling ecological competitive dynamics [123].

Metastasis involves the formation of pre-metastatic niches, among which primary tumor-derived factors recruit immunosuppressive cells to prepare secondary sites for circulating tumor cells (CTCs) colonization [124]. After seeding, these niches evolve into metastatic niches, maintaining immunosuppressive features and influencing the plasticity of tumor cells [125, 126]. Although there is spatial heterogeneity between the primary and metastatic sites, certain immune patterns (T cells distribution) remain consistent, emphasizing the complexity of TIME dynamics [127]. Further research is needed to elucidate these spatiotemporal interactions and their clinical implications.

Spatial architecture of the TIME in clinical application

Deciphering the spatial architecture of the TIME has become essential for understanding treatment resistance and designing next-generation precision therapies。Recent advances in spatial multi-omics, have enabled researchers to map the three-dimensional organization of tumors with unprecedented resolution, revealing how the physical arrangement of immune, stromal, and malignant cells dictates therapeutic outcomes across multiple cancer types (Fig. 4).

Spatial architecture of the TIME in prognosis prediction

Recent progress has highlighted the critical role of spatial architecture in the TIME, which serves as a powerful predictor of clinical outcomes. Advanced high-resolution imaging and spatial profiling techniques have revealed the prognostic significance of the spatial heterogeneity of TIME in multiple cancer types. For instance, IMC revealed that in high-grade gliomas and brain metastases, long-term survival was associated with the presence of MPO+ macrophage-enriched niches. In brain metastases, the compact tumor tissue architecture and the distinct vascular interaction patterns with the tumors emerge as the dominant features [128]. Similarly, in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), spatial analyses identified B cells enriched neighborhoods and a close physical proximity between CD8 + T cells and tumor cells, which became a powerful prognostic indicators and was superior to traditional density-based indicators [129]. These findings underscore that the spatial organization of the TIME holds better prognostic value than mere cellular abundance.

The reason why these spatial insights are more persuasive is that they often outperform traditional clinical indicators. In LUAD, applying deep learning to a single 1 mm² tissue core can extract spatial features that accurately predict postoperative disease progression, and its effectiveness exceeds that of traditional clinicopathological factors [130]. In BC, a novel prognostic model (favorable combined immune signature, FCIS) was established based on the differential spatial distribution of CD8 + and CD163 + immune cells between TC and IM. The FCIS signature is defined by high CD8+/low CD163 + in TC combined with low CD8+/high CD163 + in IM, which is significantly superior to the traditional TNM staging in predicting disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival, particularly in patients with adverse clinicopathological features but with a favorable immune contexture [131]. Therefore, although the spatial features of different cancer types vary, the principle that spatial context can refine risk stratification seems to be universally applicable.

Furthermore, in ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), MIBI-TOF and machine learning suggested that the spatial dynamics of the myoepithelial-stromal interface, such as myoepithelial continuity and stromal remodeling, can predict the progression to invasive BC. DCIS patients with intact myoepithelium and retention of E-cadherin (for advanced patients) exhibited immunosuppressive TIME (with elevated PD-L1/IDO1 levels), while those with disrupted myoepithelial layers (non-advanced patients) displayed a pro-inflammatory stroma, which may inhibit invasion [55]. In this context, spatial parameters such as cellular neighborhood distributions improved the predictive accuracy of random forest models, highlighting that spatial architecture can capture biological states that cannot be obtained merely through quantity.

The regular structure of the above-mentioned TIME has significant predictive significance. In prostate cancer (PCa), studies elucidated the unique microenvironmental characteristics of the TLS and their pivotal roles in the spontaneous tumor regression. The organization and cellular composition of the TLS are closely related to the progression of the disease. For instance, in patients whose tumors have regressed, the TLS displays a marked enrichment of Tbet+ Th1 cells and CD8 + T cells. Meanwhile, the infiltration of Tregs decreased, the density of COX2 + cells reduced, tumor neovascularization decreased, and the expression of prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) was downregulated. These findings implicated that the immune activity status of the TLS, particularly the Th1/Treg balance and the infiltration of effector cells, are important biomarkers for the prognosis of PCa [132].

It is worth noting that the TLS enhances its anti-tumor efficacy by recruiting and activating immune cells, such as mature dendritic cells (DCs), T cells, and B cells, thereby promoting local adaptive immune responses. The key mechanism indicates that IgG + and IgA+ plasma cells (PCs) produced in the TLS spread through the CXCL12 + fibroblast network to the TC, and the locally produced IgG antibodies directly bind to the malignant cells and induce apoptosis. In clinical practice, patients with high levels of IgG tumor markers have a significantly better response to ICIs therapy (with objective response rates of 62% and 18% respectively), and their PFS is also significantly prolonged (16.3 months vs. 6.4 months). These findings not only establish the TLS as a predictive biomarker for the responsiveness of ICIs, but also highlight their potential as a therapeutic target for modulating anti-tumor immunity through microenvironmental engineering [116, 133].

Spatial architecture of the TIME in clinical treatment

Currently, multiple therapeutic strategies aim to remodel the spatial architecture of the TIME. For instance, the spatial distribution of immune cells in the TIME can be actively modulated, and there is evidence to support their recruitment and rejection from specific niches. Liu et al. [6] discoverd that PCSK9 suppresses the expression of MHC I on the cell surface by promoting its lysosomal degradation, thereby impairing tumor antigen presentation, reducing cytotoxic T-cell lymphocytes (CTL) infiltration into TC, and ultimately weakening anti-tumor immunity. Inhibition of PCSK9 through gene knockout or neutralizing antibodies can block this aberrant degradation, thus significantly upregulating MHC I expression on tumor cells. This enhances CTL infiltration into the TIME region with immune rejection, boosts CTL activity, and also increases T-cell receptor (TCR) diversity and clonal expansion. Importantly, this mechanism is independent of PCSK9’s role in cholesterol metabolism and does not involve LDLR.

Preclinical studies validated that PCSK9 inhibitors, when combined with PD-1 blockade, can overcome the resistance to immunotherapy. By Using myeloid-specific HIF-2α-knockout mice, researchers demonstrated that the absence of HIF-2α significantly impairs macrophage migration to inflammatory and tumor sites mediated by the downregulation of chemokine receptors such as M-CSFR and CXCR4. In the acute inflammation models, HIF-2α ablation reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines secretion (IL-1β, IL-12, and TNF-α), while elevating the level of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive milieu. In HCC and colitis-associated cancer models, the absence of HIF-2α led to a reduction of approximately 70% in the infiltration of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), while the proliferation of tumor cells was weakened and the malignant progression was delayed. These findings confirm that HIF-2α is a major regulatory factor that can fine-tune the chemotactic behavior of macrophages and the cytokine networks, thereby transporting immune cells to specific TIME niches [134]. Consequently, both PCSK9 and HIF-2α play a role as “molecular levers” that control which immune cells enter which spatial compartments, providing therapeutic opportunities for reshaping the architecture of the TIME.

In addition to the distribution of immune cells, the TIME is also influenced by the dynamic changes in angiogenesis and oxygenation, which collectively alter the metabolic and functional landscape of the TME. Anti-angiogenic therapy can transiently ameliorate the abnormal tumor vasculature and promote vascular normalization by targeting key pathways (such as VEGF and ANG2), thereby enhancing oxygenation and perfusion in the TME. This normalization not only alleviates hypoxia and acidosis, but also enhances the infiltration and function of immune effector cells, converting an immunosuppressive TME into an immunostimulatory state. The combined use of ICIs (PD-1/PD-L1 blockade) further counteracts the normalization of the immune escape mechanism (PD-L1 upregulation), and enhances the therapeutic effect through the synergistic regulation of the interaction between blood vessels and immune cells [135]. Chen et al. [134] uncovered that this fluorocarbon-chain-functionalized hollow mesoporous organosilica nanoparticle (FHMON) platform, which was loaded with sonosensitizer IR780 and oxygen, could deliver and release oxygen in response to ultrasound (US) irradiation, thereby effectively regulating tumor hypoxia. The FC chains can achieve stable oxygen binding and US-triggered release, significantly enhancing tumor oxygenation and overcoming hypoxia-induced resistance to sonodynamic therapy (SDT). It is worth noting that both vascular normalization and direct oxygenation strategies share a common goal, which is to alter the metabolic and spatial landscape of the TIME, thereby providing better conditions for the entry and function of immune effector cells.

Another complicating factor arises from the formation of TLS, whose structural organization and cellular composition may determine the outcome of immunogenicity or immunotolerance. TLS have shown significant clinical potential in immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Studies indicate that B cells enriched in TLS and their related molecular markers (CD20, CD21, and CXCR3) are closely associated with a favorable response of patients to ICB, particularly in melanoma and RCC. TLS enhances anti-tumor immunity by facilitating B cells clonal expansion, memory B cells differentiation, and antibody production, thereby amplifying the immune response in synergy with T cells [116]. In ICIs treatment, TLS may serve as a predictive biomarker for the therapeutic efficacy. Strategies for inducing TLS formation, such as the use of cytokines, chemokines, or engineered DCs, can potentiate lymphocytes infiltration and amplify the anti-tumor immune responses, especially in immunologically “cold” tumors. Furthermore, the synergistic effect of TLS with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and vaccine-based therapies has been proven to further augment the therapeutic outcome [136]. Hence, TLS is not a static biomarker, but rather a spatial structure with dynamic characteristics that can be actively modified to achieve therapeutic benefit.

Finally, the stromal architecture of the TIME is not merely a passive scaffold, but an active participant. The remodeling of matrix can affect the transportation, differentiation, and therapeutic response of immune cells. Najafi et al. [137] utilized a three-dimensional co-culture model to elucidate the synergistic regulatory mechanism of matrix stiffness and TAMs on EMT in LUAD. The findings implied that an increased in matrix stiffness (30–310 Pa) can significantly enhance the proliferation and invasion of A549 cells. The conditioned medium derived from M2c-polarized macrophages further upregulated EMT-related genes (CDH2, MYC, and TWIST1) under high stiffness conditions. Moreover, co-culture with tumor cells can induce M0 macrophages to transform into M2 phenotype. M2c macrophages, in conjunction with matrix stiffness, promote EMT by downregulating E-cadherin and upregulating vimentin and PD-L1 expression [138]. CAFs and M2 macrophages play central roles in ECM remodeling by promoting collagen cross-linking via TGF-β/LOX signaling pathway and matrix degradation through MMPs activation. Hypoxia further amplifies these processes, fostering a fibrotic and proteolytic niche that facilitates invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. This finding highlights a bidirectional relationship, the physical properties of the stroma influence the behavior of immune cells, while the immune cells in turn reshape the stroma.

Spatial architecture of the TIME in novel therapeutic approaches

In recent years, as we studied CAFs-immune crosstalk, the functional significance of spatial architecture become more evident. In GC, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and CRC, a recurring theme is that discrete CAFs subsets co-localize with macrophages, establishing immunosuppressive hubs. In GC, the CAFs expressing CCL2 physically interact with STAT3-activated macrophages. The disruption of this spatial co-localization by neutralizing CCL2 reverses CD8⁺ T cells exclusion and inhibits tumor growth [139]. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, spatial transcriptomics revealed that CAFs-derived POSTN was positively correlated with TAMs infiltration and PD-L1/PD-L2 expression, thereby defining a CAFs–POSTN–TAMs axis that drives resistance to ICIs. It is worth noting that using large-scale RNA-seq cannot observe this axis in the same tumor, which highlights the unique contribution of spatial approaches [140]. Applying this principle to targeted therapy, studies on RCC showed that a fatty acid-reprogrammed tumor subpopulation (FABP1) spatially co-localizes with PLAT+ endothelial cells via the PLG–PLAT ligand-receptor pair, thereby creating a pro-angiogenic niche. Using orlistat to target FABP1 and combining it with the tyrosine kinase inhibitor sunitinib in patient-derived organoids illustrated how spatial mapping directly guides rational combined treatment strategies [141].

Another emerging theme is that the physical exclusion or proximity at the tumor stroma interface carries prognostic and therapeutic implications. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, spatial transcriptomics revealed a stark reciprocal pattern. SASH1, a tumor suppressor, is downregulated in the malignant areas, whereas COL1A1 is highly enriched in the adjacent fibrotic stroma. This spatial segregation indicates that the absence of SASH1 not only promotes the intrinsic malignant characteristics, but may also orchestrate a desmoplastic environment that facilitates invasion and drug resistance [142]. In ulcerative colitis patients treated with the anti-integrin vedolizumab, spatial multi-omics showed that the degree of proximity between inflammatory mononuclear phagocytes and activated fibroblasts was reduced among the responders. The intact epithelial crypt base in pre-treatment biopsies predicts the treatment outcome. If the tissue architecture is not maintained, this discovery cannot be captured [143]. Furthermore, the formation of TLS represents a spatially organized immune response, which is closely related to the efficacy of immunotherapy. GC patients with intra-tumoral TLS enriched in CXCL13⁺ T cells and germinal center B cells consistently achieved the most significant responses to PD-1 blockade. Moreover, the CXCL13–CXCR5 axis was recognized as a driving factor for B-cell recruitment [144].

Spatial metabolomics has added a new dimension to our understanding of the heterogeneity of the TIME by mapping metabolic gradients and niches. GC can be classified into metabolic subtypes by MALDI-MSI, and each type has distinct prognosis and sensitivity to immunotherapy. For instance, the subtype characterized by enhanced carbohydrate metabolism and diffuse-type histological features has the poorest prognosis [139]. In RCC, those FABP1⁺ tumors that create a pro-angiogenic niche also exhibit high oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid catabolism, which directly links metabolic reprogramming to spatial vascular remodeling [141]. Encouragingly, some recent studies have taken a step beyond the discovery stage and have demonstrated their clinical applicability. The fibrotic microenvironment gene signature derived from the renal spatial transcriptomics can more accurately predict the progression to end-stage renal disease compared to traditional histopathology, particularly in patients with preserved organ function where traditional scoring fails [99]. The cell-to-gene pipeline established in lung cancer, from CODEX protein imaging to transcriptomic deconvolution and then to compact gene signatures, provides a template for the validation of regulatory-grade biomarkers [145]. Despite challenges such as cost, throughput, and the integration of multiple molecular layers from the same tissue section, emerging technologies and deep learning-based deconvolution tools are steadily reducing these barriers. Collectively, these findings indicate that the spatial architecture of the TIME is not only descriptive but also operable. Incorporating spatial multi-omics into clinical trial designs, from pre-treatment biopsies to post-treatment remodeling assessments, will accelerate the development of novel therapeutic approaches.

Discussion

The TIME is a complex abd spatially organized ecosystem, in which the distribution and interaction of immune cells, stromal cells, and tumor cells have a significant impact on disease progression and treatment response. The latest advancements in spatial omics, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, have provided unprecedented insights into the TIME architecture, revealing key features such as immune cell compartmentalization, TLS, and stromal immune crosstalk. These findings highlight the limitations of bulk analyses and underscore the need for spatially resolved approaches to fully understand tumor immunobiology.

Spatial profiling has identified distinct TIME subtypes with prognostic and therapeutic implications. For example, immune-excluded tumors exhibit stromal barriers that limit T cells infiltration, while TLS-rich tumors show enhanced B cells activity and improved responses to immunotherapy. Additionally, the tumor periphery often serves as a critical immunoregulatory zone, where the interaction of immune cells determines invasion and metastasis. These discoveries suggest that spatial biomarkers, including immune cell localization, TLS maturity, and stromal remodeling, could refine patient stratification and predict treatment outcomes more accurately than traditional metrics.

However, there are still key challenges in translating the results of spatial omics research into clinical practice. Standardizing the analysis pipelines, improving the resolution of single-cell and subcellular mapping, and integrating multi-omics datasets will be essential. Furthermore, the use of spatial insights to design novel therapies, including but not limited to TLS-inducing agents, stroma-targeting drugs, or spatially guided immunotherapies, could overcome drug resistance mechanisms and improve patient prognosis. Future research should also explore dynamic TIME remodeling during treatment, as real-time spatial monitoring may guide adaptive therapeutic strategies. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of TIME spatial biology will pave the way for precise oncology approaches that exploit the structural and functional complexity of the microenvironment.
