Sublytic pyroptosis blocks Schwann cell remyelination through caspase-1-mediated Tyro3 cleavage
Authors: Xianfu Lu, Yanxun Han, Dapeng Li, Shaojie Li, Zhenzi Li, Yue Li, Qing Liu, Yuchen Liu, Matthew H Morrison, Xiaojun Zha, Fang Wang, Ye Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammasomes canonically trigger pyroptotic cell death through GSDMD-mediated membrane rupture. This study investigates a distinct sublytic pyroptosis in Schwann cells following peripheral nerve injury, where inflammasome activation causes functional impairment without extensive cell death.MethodsUsing a mouse sciatic nerve transection-repair model combined with in vitro and in vivo analyses, we examined NLRP3 inflammasome activation and cell fate. Primary Schwann cell cultures and RSC96 cells were used for biochemical assays, while ultrastructural analyses were performed on sciatic nerve segments.ResultsDespite NLRP3 inflammasome activation, Schwann cells showed minimal GSDMD cleavage and maintained membrane integrity, resulting in low cell death rates (~ 8% in primary mouse Schwann cells and ~ 2–3% in RSC96 rat Schwann cells). However, these surviving cells failed to transition from repair to myelinating phenotype. Activated Caspase-1 directly cleaves Tyro3 receptor at Asp495, detaching the intracellular kinase domain and abolishing receptor function. This proteolytic inactivation disrupts the Gas6-Tyro3-Fyn signaling axis essential for Schwann cell myelination. Combined treatment with Caspase-1 inhibitor VX-765 and Gas6 prevented Tyro3 cleavage, restored downstream signaling, and promoted expression of myelin transcription factors Oct6 and Krox20 as well as myelin basic protein (MBP). In vivo application significantly enhanced myelin thickness, axon regeneration, and functional recovery assessed by sciatic function index and electrophysiology.ConclusionsSublytic pyroptosis represents a novel inflammasome-mediated proteolytic receptor inactivation as a previously unrecognized mechanism, distinct from classical pyroptosis. Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNI) have a global annual incidence of 13–23 per 100,000 person-years [1, 2], causing devastating sensory and motor deficits that persist despite advances in microsurgical repair. Recovery remains limited by slow axonal regeneration and incomplete remyelination—processes critically dependent on establishing a permissive regenerative microenvironment [3].

Schwann cells orchestrate this regenerative response [4, 5]. Following injury, mature Schwann cells rapidly transdifferentiate into "repair Schwann cells" that clear myelin debris, secrete neurotrophic factors, and form Bands of Büngner—longitudinal cellular columns that guide regenerating axons [4, 6]. This pro-regenerative phenotype is inherently unstable and frequently compromised by persistent inflammation [7]. How inflammation impairs Schwann cell function at the molecular level remains poorly understood, highlighting the need to define the inflammatory-regenerative interface.

The NLRP3 inflammasome serves as a pivotal innate immune sensor, recognizing damage-associated signals including myelin debris and extracellular ATP [7, 8]. Classical inflammasome activation triggers Caspase-1–mediated cleavage of Gasdermin D (GSDMD), leading to pyroptotic cell death characterized by membrane rupture and inflammatory content release [3]. However, emerging evidence reveals remarkable cell-type specificity in inflammasome responses, with certain cell types exhibiting sublytic inflammasome activation that preserves membrane integrity [9]. Whether Schwann cells follow the classical pyroptotic pathway or engage alternative mechanisms remains unresolved [7].

The Gas6–Tyro3 signaling axis is essential for Schwann cell–mediated myelination and regeneration. Loss of Tyro3 function reduces myelin thickness by ~ 30% and severely impairs regenerative capacity [10]. While proteolytic inactivation of TAM family receptors (Axl, Mer) has been documented in the central nervous system, whether Tyro3 undergoes similar processing in the peripheral nervous system remains unknown, and direct evidence for inflammatory protease–mediated Tyro3 cleavage has been lacking [11, 12].

Here we show that NLRP3 inflammasome activation in Schwann cells impairs cellular function through Caspase-1–mediated proteolytic inactivation of Tyro3 receptor, rather than inducing pyroptotic death. Using a mouse sciatic nerve transection–repair (SNTR) model combined with comprehensive in vitro and in vivo analyses, we demonstrate that Schwann cells exhibit sublytic inflammasome activation following nerve injury, characterized by minimal GSDMD cleavage and preserved membrane integrity. We reveal that activated Caspase-1 directly cleaves the intracellular domain of Tyro3 at Asp495, detaching the kinase domain and disrupting Gas6–Tyro3 signaling. Furthermore, pharmacological Caspase-1 inhibition with VX-765 [13, 14] and/or Gas6 supplementation effectively reverses these deficits, restoring Schwann cell myelination programs and significantly improving nerve regeneration and functional recovery [10, 15].

These findings reveal previously unrecognized crosstalk between inflammatory and regenerative signaling pathways, establishing protease-mediated receptor inactivation as a key mechanism by which inflammasomes impair nerve regeneration. We here introduce the term ‘sublytic pyroptosis’ to describe an inflammasome-activated state in Schwann cells that triggers pyroptotic signaling without causing cell rupture [16]. Our work provides both mechanistic insights and tractable therapeutic strategies for peripheral nerve repair, with promising translational potential given the established safety profiles of the therapeutic agents employed [17].

Methods

Animals and surgery

Adult female C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks; n = 8–10 per group) underwent sciatic nerve transection with end-to-end epineurial repair using 12–0 nylon under isoflurane anesthesia (4% induction, ~ 1.5% maintenance) and aseptic conditions on a thermostatic pad (~ 37 °C). Treatments included topical recombinant Gas6 (10 μg/mL, 20 μL evenly applied at the anastomosis) during surgery and intraperitoneal VX-765 (10 mg/kg) once daily for the first 7 days starting 24 h post-operation, then once weekly until week 12; controls received vehicle [15, 18]. All procedures conformed to IACUC guidelines (First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University; IACUC: #5,101,292).

Cell culture and DRG neurons

Primary Schwann cells were isolated from neonatal (P1–P3) C57BL/6 J or knockout mice (Tyro3−/−, Caspase-1−/−) by digestion with 0.05% collagenase IV + 0.25% trypsin (45 min, 37 °C), followed by trituration and culture in DMEM/F12 + 10% FBS, 2 μM forskolin, and 10 ng/mL neuregulin-1. Fibroblast depletion in mouse cultures used anti-Thy1.2 (CD90.2) plus complement as needed. RSC96 cells (ATCC CRL-2765) were maintained in DMEM + 10% FBS for PI/flow assays. DRG neurons (E13.5) were plated on poly-D-lysine/laminin (10 μg/mL each) at 5 × 10^4 cells/well (24-well) and maintained in Neurobasal + 2% B27, 1% GlutaMAX, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 50 ng/mL NGF (half-medium changes every 2–3 days) [19].

Inflammasome activation and cell death

NLRP3 was induced by LPS priming (1 μg/mL, 4 h) followed by ATP at condition matched to the readout: 5 mM for 30 min for cleavage/fragment Western blots, and 2 mM for 1 h for PI/LDH assays. Pyroptotic death was quantified by LDH release and PI uptake (1 μg/mL, 15 min, microscopy/flow). Mature IL-1β (p17) and caspase-1 (p20) confirmed activation by immunoblot.

Schwann cell–DRG co-culture

Schwann cells were primed with LPS (1 μg/mL, 4 h) and stimulated with ATP (2 mM, 1 h) in the presence of VX-765 (10 μM), Gas6 (100 ng/mL), IL-1Ra, or vehicle as indicated. Pre-activated Schwann cells were then washed twice to remove residual LPS, ATP, and treatment compounds, and subsequently co-cultured with freshly isolated DRG neurons (without prior culture) at 1 × 10^5 cells/well. This experimental design ensured that DRG neurons were never directly exposed to LPS, ATP, or pharmacological agents. Endpoints were assessed at 4 h (signaling) and 72 h (myelination; ascorbic acid 50 μg/mL was added at 24 h). Myelination and axons were assessed by MBP and NF200 staining, respectively.

Molecular analyses and genetic add-back strategy

Western blots probed Tyro3, myelination markers (Oct6, Krox20, MBP), phospho-Akt/Erk, and inflammasome proteins (NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, Gasdermin D); β-actin was loading control.

Genetic add-back

Full-length Tyro3-WT or cleavage-resistant Tyro3-D495A (GAT → GCT) were cloned in pCDH (EF1α) and packaged into third-generation lentivirus (psPAX2/pMD2.G). Tyro3 −/− Schwann cells were transduced (MOI ~ 5–10), and expression was verified by WB/IF [20]. For mapping caspase-1 cleavage, LPS + ATP was applied under the “cleavage” condition (ATP 5 mM, 30 min) and N-terminal Tyro3 blotting detected full-length (~ 130–140 kDa) and an N-terminal fragment (~ 50–60 kDa) in WT but not D495A. Protein interaction was assessed in HEK293T by FLAG-caspase-1/GFP-Tyro3 co-expression and anti-FLAG co-IP [21, 22].

Functional outcomes

Gait recovery was quantified using CatWalk XT (≥ 3 compliant runs/animal without pauses; SFI and gait metrics including print area, stance/swing time, stride length; analyses blinded) [23]. Electrophysiology measured motor NCV and CMAP with proximal (sciatic notch) and distal (ankle) stimulation and gastrocnemius recording, using square-wave pulses (0.05–0.1 ms, 1–2 Hz) delivered to maximal and then ~ 20% supramaximal intensity (typically up to 5–10 mA); sampling ≥ 10 kHz with band-pass filtering; animals maintained at ~ 37 °C. NCV was distance/latency difference; CMAP amplitude was peak-to-peak. Three to five traces/site were averaged [24, 25].

Histology and TEM

Cryosections (10 μm) were stained for S100β, MBP, NF200, Ki67, p-Tyro3, p-Akt, NLRP3, and IL-18 (confocal imaging). For ultrastructure, nerves were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed in 1% OsO4, embedded in epoxy; semi-thin Sects. (0.5–1 μm) were toluidine-blue-stained, and ultrathin sections (~ 70 nm) imaged at 80–120 kV. G-ratio (axon diameter/fiber diameter) and myelin thickness were quantified across multiple fields (ImageJ) [26].

Immunofluorescence quantification

Images were acquired with identical microscope settings across all conditions. Fluorescence was quantified in ImageJ following background subtraction.

For cell culture experiments, integrated fluorescence intensity was normalized to DAPI⁺ nuclei to yield mean fluorescence intensity per cell (MFI per DAPI⁺ cell, a.u.). This normalization was applied to p-Tyro3, total Tyro3, p-Fyn, total Fyn, NF200, and MBP. Phospho/total ratios were calculated by dividing per-cell phospho-protein MFI by the corresponding total protein MFI from parallel samples. For tissue section immunofluorescence, integrated fluorescence within manually delineated ROIs was normalized to ROI area (MFI per area, a.u.); this approach was used for p-Akt, MBP, S100β, Oct6, and Ki67.

Axon–myelin colocalization in co-cultures was assessed using the thresholded Manders' coefficient (tM1), calculated with the Coloc 2 plugin. NF200 was assigned as channel 1 and MBP as channel 2; thresholds were determined by the Costes automatic method. tM1 ranges from 0 to 1 and represents the fraction of MBP signal overlapping with above-threshold NF200 signal. To avoid pseudo-replication, ≥ 5 axonal segments per image were analyzed and averaged to generate a single image-level value. Line-scan intensity profiles along representative axons are provided for visualization only and were not included in statistical analyses.

Quantification was performed on 4–6 randomly selected fields per condition from 4 independent experiments (immunofluorescence) or 3 independent experiments (Western blot).

Statistics

Data are mean ± SD from ≥ 3 independent experiments or animals/group. Two-group comparisons used unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests; multiple groups used one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc. Longitudinal SFI was analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. P < 0.05 was significant (GraphPad Prism 8).

Results

Result 1: Schwann cells display differential susceptibility to inflammasome-mediated cell death with predominant sublytic activation

To examine how injury-related inflammation affects Schwann cells, we used a mouse sciatic nerve transection–repair model and assessed inflammasome pathway status and cell fate. Immunofluorescence showed colocalization of NLRP3 and IL-18 within S100β+ Schwann cells at the anastomosis site (Fig. 1A), consistent with inflammasome engagement in Schwann cells. Biochemical and functional assays (PI/LDH) were performed in primary Schwann cell cultures, whereas ultrastructural analyses were conducted on in vivo sciatic nerve segments. We next examined inflammasome effector production to gauge the extent of pathway activation in Schwann cells. Compared with macrophages, Schwann cells showed substantially lower effector levels. Quantitative immunoblotting showed that mature IL-1β (p17) in Schwann cells reached ~ 20% of macrophage levels (Fig. 1B), while GSDMD-N was only ~ 2–4% of macrophage levels (Fig. 1C). In the same preparations, cleaved caspase-1 (p20) in Schwann cells was ~ 10% of macrophage levels, with densitometry in Supplementary Fig. 1D–F. The significantly reduced GSDMD cleavage is consistent with a limited capacity for lytic, pore-driven rupture in most Schwann cells.

To determine whether this attenuated signaling translates to preserved viability, we performed cell-death assays. Following LPS + ATP stimulation, only a small fraction of Schwann cells became PI-positive (~ 8% in primary mouse cells; ~ 2–3% in RSC96), with LDH release remaining near baseline. In contrast, macrophages exhibited both robust PI uptake (Supplementary Fig. 1H–K) and substantial LDH release (Fig. 1D). These data indicate that most Schwann cells maintain membrane integrity despite inflammasome engagement, while a minority progresses to lytic death under the same conditions.

We then used electron microscopy to visualize the phenotypes underlying this heterogeneity. In the majority of examined fields, Schwann cells retained continuous plasma membranes and unfragmented nuclei with only mild cytoplasmic vacuolation—morphology compatible with sublytic membrane perturbation. By contrast, a subset exhibited pyroptotic morphology, including membrane ballooning and rupture, cellular swelling, swollen mitochondria, and peripheral chromatin condensation without nuclear fragmentation (Fig. 1E–F). Thus, Schwann cells are not uniformly resistant to lytic pyroptosis but display differential susceptibility, with sublytic activation predominating and a defined minority undergoing complete lysis.

Despite preserved viability in most cells, we asked whether sublytic inflammasome activation impairs Schwann cell function. Repair Schwann cells had aligned into Bands of Büngner, yet these scaffolds appeared sparse and discontinuous in injured regions (Fig. 1E). Myelin basic protein (MBP) expression was significantly reduced at the anastomosis site (AS) compared with the proximal (PN) and distal (DN) segments (P < 0.01; Fig. 1G–H), indicating compromised remyelination capacity. The proliferation marker Ki67 and Schwann cell marker S100β showed no significant regional differences (Supplementary Fig. 2).

These data support a threshold-like response in which sublytic inflammasome activation predominates in Schwann cells, whereas a defined minority crosses the lytic threshold to undergo pyroptotic death (only ~ 8% by PI staining in primary Schwann cells and ~ 2–3% in RSC96 cells).

Result 2: Caspase-1 inactivates Tyro3 receptor through proteolytic cleavage

Having established that Schwann cells undergo sublytic pyroptosis with preserved viability, we investigated how activated caspase-1 compromises function. Given Tyro3's essential role in Schwann cell regeneration, we hypothesized that caspase-1 directly cleaves Tyro3, thereby disrupting myelination programs.

Quantitative proteomics showed that Tyro3 protein was significantly higher in Caspase-1−/− sciatic nerves than in WT (Fig. 2A), consistent with caspase-1–dependent cleavage/turnover of Tyro3 in WT mice. Co-immunoprecipitation from nerve tissue showed that caspase-1 forms complexes with Tyro3 in vivo (Fig. 2B), and silver-stained bands in the precipitate were identified by mass spectrometry as Tyro3 and NLRP3 (Supplementary Fig. 3 A), supporting proximity of the inflammasome to the receptor.

We then examined cleavage after injury in vivo. In WT nerves, an ~ 60 kDa N-terminal Tyro3 fragment (Tyro3-N) appeared from day 1 and increased by days 3 and 5, with concomitant loss of full-length Tyro3; in Caspase-1−/− nerves, Tyro3 remained intact (Fig. 2C). The ~ 60 kDa band was detected by an N-terminal (ectodomain) antibody, consistent with cleavage in the intracellular juxtamembrane region that detaches the kinase domain.

In primary mouse Schwann cells, LPS priming (4 h) followed by ATP induced time-dependent Tyro3 cleavage: fragments emerged at 1 h and accumulated by 4 h (P < 0.001), with loss of the full-length band (Fig. 2D). Consistent with receptor inactivation, DAPI-normalized immunofluorescence quantification demonstrated decreased Tyro3 signal per cell in RSC96 cells following LPS + ATP treatment, which was partially rescued by VX-765 (Fig. 2H–I; additional representative fields in Supplementary Fig. 3B).

To establish direct cleavage, we co-transfected HEK-293 T cells with Flag–caspase-1 and GFP–Tyro3. Co-immunoprecipitation confirmed their interaction (Fig. 2E). Sequence analysis identified Asp495 in the juxtamembrane region as the putative caspase-1 site (Fig. 2F). Site-directed mutagenesis validated this finding: in Tyro3−/− Schwann cells reconstituted with WT or D495A Tyro3, only WT Tyro3 generated the ~ 60 kDa fragment upon LPS + ATP, whereas D495A showed > 95% resistance to cleavage (Fig. 2G). Additionally, the observed molecular weights reflect extensive N-glycosylation: full-length Tyro3 migrates at ~ 130–140 kDa (versus predicted ~ 97 kDa), while the N-terminal fragment appears at ~ 50–60 kDa, consistent with cleavage near the juxta-membrane region detaching the kinase domain.

Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition corroborated functional relevance. In RSC96 cells, the caspase-1–specific inhibitor VX-765 preserved Tyro3 levels after LPS + ATP (Fig. 2H–I). Flow cytometry showed that LPS + ATP reduced Tyro3+/CD271+ cells from ~ 85% to ~ 60%, an effect reversed by VX-765 pretreatment (P < 0.01; Fig. 2J). Consistent with the sublytic phenotype, only ~ 2–3% of RSC96 cells became PI-positive, and VX-765 further reduced this fraction (Supplementary Fig. 1H, J).

These findings identify protease-mediated receptor inactivation as an inflammasome effector mechanism in Schwann cells, explaining survival with functional failure upon inflammatory activation.

Result 3: inhibiting proteolytic receptor inactivation preserves the Schwann cell Gas6–Tyro3–Fyn axis and promotes myelination

Having identified caspase-1–mediated Tyro3 cleavage as a driver of Schwann cell dysfunction, we tested whether blocking this proteolytic inactivation restores regenerative signaling. Bulk RNA-seq from the anastomosis site underwent KEGG pathway enrichment. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Caspase-1−/− vs WT) were enriched for the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (Fig. 3A). Consistent with these pathway-level differences, quantitative proteomics showed that Tyro3 protein was significantly lower in WT than in Caspase-1−/− nerves (Fig. 2A).

We examined Gas6–Tyro3–Fyn signaling in RSC96 Schwann cells under control (vehicle) or inflammasome-activated (LPS + ATP) conditions, each with four treatment groups (Mock, Gas6, VX-765, Gas6 + VX-765). Under control conditions, Gas6 increased Tyro3 and Fyn phosphorylation (Supplementary Figs. 4E and 5E), whereas VX-765 alone had no effect. Upon LPS + ATP stimulation, Gas6-induced p-Tyro3 and p-Fyn were suppressed; co-treatment with VX-765 restored both signals to levels comparable to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 3B–E). Importantly, under inflammasome activation (LPS + ATP), Gas6 monotherapy was insufficient to restore the Tyro3–Fyn axis and its downstream effectors, as p-Tyro3/p-Fyn remained suppressed and Akt/Erk activation was not effectively rescued; efficient pathway recovery required concomitant caspase-1 inhibition with VX-765. The phosphorylated/total ratios for Tyro3 and Fyn (Fig. 3F–G; Supplementary Figs. 4H and 5H) showed the same pattern, indicating that the suppression of p-Tyro3 and p-Fyn was due to impaired signaling rather than solely reduced protein levels. Total Tyro3 was stable under vehicle conditions but significantly decreased under LPS + ATP (Supplementary Fig. 4F–G), consistent with caspase-1-mediated Tyro3 cleavage; VX-765 partially preserved Tyro3 levels. Total Fyn remained unchanged across conditions (Supplementary Figs. 5F–G). These results indicate that inflammasome activation suppresses Gas6–Tyro3–Fyn signaling through Tyro3 cleavage, and that caspase-1 inhibition prevents this degradation.

Subsequently, we assessed downstream signaling in primary mouse Schwann cells. Gas6 increased p-Akt and p-ERK and upregulated Oct6, Krox20, and MBP; LPS+ATP abrogated these responses, whereas VX-765 plus Gas6 largely reversed the suppression, as shown by Western blotting and densitometric quantification (Fig. 3H–J; Supplementary Fig. 6), supporting axis-wide protection of the myelination program.

Result 4: VX-765 + Gas6 restores Tyro3 activation and promotes myelination in Schwann cell–DRG neuron co-cultures

To determine whether preserving Gas6-Tyro3 signaling supports Schwann cell myelination under inflammatory stress, we established a primary co-culture system in which Schwann cells were pre-treated before contact with neurons, thereby excluding direct drug effects on the neuronal compartment. Schwann cells were primed with LPS (4 h) and stimulated with ATP (1 h) in the presence of designated treatments, washed, and then co-cultured with freshly prepared DRG neurons. At 4 h, Gas6 alone robustly elevated p-Tyro3 levels, whereas prior LPS + ATP exposure largely suppressed this response (Fig. 4A–G). IL-1Ra co-treatment partially restored p-Tyro3 phosphorylation (p = 0.017 vs. LPS + ATP + Gas6), indicating that extracellular IL-1 contributes to, but does not fully account for, the observed suppression. VX-765 + Gas6 produced a more complete recovery, significantly exceeding both LPS + ATP + Gas6 and IL-1Ra + Gas6 groups. MBP levels remained comparable across conditions at this timepoint (Fig. 4H), consistent with the delayed emergence of structural myelination markers.

By 72 h, functional differences became apparent. LPS + ATP-treated cultures showed reduced NF200 and MBP signals with fragmented myelin segments (Fig. 5A–F). Gas6 or IL-1Ra + Gas6 provided only marginal benefit, whereas VX-765 + Gas6 substantially enhanced MBP ensheathment along NF200⁺ axons and restored both markers (Fig. 5G–H). Thresholded Manders' coefficient (tM1), a quantitative index of axon–myelin coverage, confirmed selective rescue by VX-765 + Gas6 (Fig. 5I). These results demonstrate that downstream IL-1 blockade alone cannot compensate for caspase-1-mediated intracellular disruption; direct caspase-1 inhibition is required to preserve Gas6-Tyro3 signaling and its pro-myelinating function.

Result 5: combined treatment promotes peripheral nerve structural repair in vivo

To test in vivo efficacy, we administered VX-765 plus Gas6 in a mouse sciatic nerve transection–repair model. VX-765 was given intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg once daily starting 24 h after surgery, and Gas6 was locally applied intraoperatively at the anastomosis (10 µg/mL, 20 µL).

At 14 days post-surgery, control nerves exhibited extensive ultrastructural pathology—intracellular vacuolization, myelin debris accumulation, nuclear pyknosis, and swollen/degenerating mitochondria—consistent with severe Schwann cell dysfunction (Fig. 6A). By 28 days, necrotic nuclei, focal fibrosis, and inflammatory remnants persisted, indicating incomplete reconstruction of the regenerative microenvironment.

In contrast, VX-765 plus Gas6 enabled effective phenotypic progression. By 14 days, repair Schwann cells formed well-aligned Bands of Büngner (black dashed lines). By 28 days, newly formed myelin (green triangles) and mature myelinating Schwann cells (green asterisks) were abundant, reflecting an orderly transition from demyelination to repair to remyelination and restored axon–glia communication (Fig. 6B).

Quantitative analyses showed that combined treatment remodeled the regenerative microenvironment: myelin debris decreased (P < 0.0001; Fig. 6C), necrotic/swollen mitochondria decreased (P < 0.0001; Fig. 6D), and myelin thickness increased by 28 days (P < 0.0001; Fig. 6E). These improvements were associated with Schwann cell functional recovery and enhanced axonal repair.

At the molecular level, Western blot analysis of injured nerve lysates revealed that sciatic nerve transection induced accumulation of the Tyro3-N cleavage fragment (~ 60 kDa), providing direct evidence for caspase-1–mediated Tyro3 proteolysis in vivo (Fig. 6F, G). Treatment with VX-765 alone significantly reduced Tyro3-N levels, whereas Gas6 alone had minimal effect on Tyro3 cleavage. The combination of VX-765 and Gas6 not only suppressed Tyro3-N generation but also preserved full-length Tyro3 and markedly enhanced Akt phosphorylation (Fig. 6G). Notably, Gas6 alone produced a modest increase in p-Akt despite persistent Tyro3 cleavage, suggesting engagement of alternative signaling mechanisms (see Discussion). The p-Akt/Akt ratio confirmed that the combined treatment achieved the greatest degree of Akt pathway activation (Fig. 6H). Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated elevated p-Akt in Schwann cells at Day 3 and increased expression of the pro-myelinating transcription factor Oct6 and myelin marker MBP at Day 28 in the VX-765 + Gas6 group (Fig. 6I, J). These findings indicate that caspase-1 inhibition preserves Tyro3 receptor integrity and, when combined with its ligand Gas6, restores downstream pro-survival and pro-myelination signaling in vivo.

Result 6: targeting proteolytic receptor inactivation improves functional recovery after nerve injury

We validated the functional impact of the combined treatment using motor and sensory assessments. Representative paw morphology showed preserved toe extension in VX-765 plus Gas6–treated mice, in contrast to severe toe drop in controls (Fig. 7A). CatWalk gait analysis demonstrated that the Sciatic Functional Index (SFI) improved from ~ − 100 to ~ − 40 with VX-765 plus Gas6, significantly outperforming both the control group (~ − 60) and VX-765 monotherapy (~ − 50) across assessed time points (P < 0.05; Fig. 7B).

Electrophysiology further supported superior recovery. Nerve conduction velocity (NCV) was significantly higher in the combined-treatment group than in both control and monotherapy groups (P < 0.05), while compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude was likewise significantly higher than in both groups (P < 0.01; Fig. 7C–D). These changes are consistent with improved remyelination and axonal conduction. Target muscle preservation accompanied these gains, with gastrocnemius weight significantly greater in treated animals than in controls (P < 0.05; Fig. 7E).

Histology corroborated the functional improvements. Control nerves exhibited severe structural disruption (Fig. 7F–G), whereas VX-765 monotherapy yielded moderate improvement (Fig. 7H–I). The VX-765 plus Gas6 group showed restored myelin thickness and axonal architecture with abundant regenerating axons (Fig. 7J–K, arrows), in line with the behavioral and electrophysiological recovery.

These results demonstrate that protease-mediated receptor inactivation serves as an inflammasome effector mechanism in Schwann cells—where caspase-1 (p20) cleaves Tyro3, impairing regeneration without overt cell lysis—and indicate that caspase-1 inhibition plus Gas6 supplementation is a mechanistically grounded strategy with translational promise for peripheral nerve repair.

Discussion

We demonstrate that sublytic inflammasome activation in Schwann cells impairs nerve regeneration after peripheral nerve injury [27]. Schwann cells exhibit sublytic pyroptosis—minimal cell death (only ~ 8% of cells PI-positive in primary SC cultures, and ~ 2–3% in RSC96 cells) yet marked functional compromise. Quantitative proteomics showed Tyro3 enrichment in Caspase-1⁻/⁻ nerves, consistent with caspase-1-dependent downregulation in wild-type animals. Mechanistically, our data support caspase-1-dependent cleavage of Tyro3 at Asp495 under our conditions, disrupting the Gas6–Tyro3–Fyn axis and preventing transition to a myelinating phenotype. Combination therapy with VX-765 and Gas6 improved structural and functional outcomes; CatWalk analysis corroborated superior functional recovery alongside electrophysiological and myelin improvements, highlighting a tractable therapeutic avenue for peripheral nerve repair.

Sublytic inflammasome activation in Schwann cells

Schwann cells display a distinct inflammasome response with minimal GSDMD cleavage and rare PI positivity, consistent with a sublytic phenotype. This response aligns with emerging concepts of gasdermin independent inflammation [7, 28] and may drive pro-fibrotic reprogramming of Schwann cells via IL-1β–TGF-β/Smad signaling [29]. Increased extracellular matrix can constrain the endoneuria microenvironment, thereby hindering axonal regeneration [16, 30]. These microenvironmental changes complement the cell-intrinsic deficit described below—caspase-1-dependent Tyro3 cleavage—linking inflammation to impaired remyelination. The remarkably low GSDMD expression in Schwann cells (~ 2–4% of macrophage levels) is consistent with limiting irreversible cell loss during inflammatory stress while tolerating a reversible functional compromise to preserve tissue repair potential [24]. These observations support the concept that anti-inflammatory strategies may benefit from careful titration—over-suppression may delay clearance of danger signals, whereas under-suppression may perpetuate chronic dysfunction [31].

Operational definition of sublytic pyroptosis

We define "sublytic pyroptosis" as an inflammasome-activated state characterized by canonical upstream execution (caspase-1 activation, IL-1β/IL-18 maturation) in the absence of terminal membrane rupture. Operational criteria include: (i) limited membrane permeabilization (LDH release < 30% of lysed controls; minimal PI uptake); (ii) biochemical inflammasome activation (caspase-1 p20, mature IL-1β/IL-18); (iii) attenuated GSDMD processing insufficient to reach a lytic threshold; and (iv) preserved viability with functional impairment (disrupted Gas6–Tyro3–Fyn signaling; compromised myelination). This contrasts with classical lytic pyroptosis, defined by extensive gasdermin pore formation, osmotic lysis, and rapid cell death. Mechanistically, sublytic pyroptosis likely reflects equilibrium between limited pore formation and membrane repair, enabling sustained low-level DAMP release without overt Schwann-cell loss.

Notably, KEGG analysis of bulk RNA-seq from the anastomosis site identified enrichment of NF-κB signaling in Caspase-1⁻/⁻ versus WT nerves. While NF-κB is classically linked to inflammasome priming [32], pathway enrichment from bulk tissue does not necessarily indicate sustained canonical NF-κB activation within Schwann cells; rather, NF-κB transcripts may reflect shifts in cellular composition and reparative immune programs. NF-κB also has context- and time-dependent roles in nerve repair, including Schwann cell myelination [33] and neuronal axon growth [34]. Caspase-1 deletion disrupts inflammasome execution (e.g., IL-1 family cytokine maturation and pyroptotic outputs), thereby decoupling NF-κB transcriptional responses from Caspase-1 dependent effector activity. We therefore interpret NF-κB pathway enrichment in Caspase-1⁻/⁻ nerves as reflecting a remodeled injury microenvironment and/or reparative programs rather than increased inflammasome effector activity. Future cell type resolved approaches (e.g., scRNA-seq) and direct NF-κB activation assays will help delineate the relevant cellular sources and activation states.

Caspase-1-dependent Tyro3 proteolysis: from site mapping to functional consequences

Guided by caspase-1 substrate preferences, we screened candidate Asp residues in the mouse Tyro3 intracellular domain. Converging evidence identified Asp495 as the primary site: the D495A mutant showed > 95% resistance to cleavage after LPS + ATP in primary Schwann cells, and Caspase-1⁻/⁻ nerves lacked a detectable cleavage fragment, consistent with caspase-1 dependence under our conditions. Although the ATLD^495↓S motif differs from commonly referenced caspase-1-preferred motifs, its location in the juxtamembrane linker (approximately 45 residues from the transmembrane region) are consistent with protease accessibility and with cleavage that would detach the kinase domain [35]. Although ATLD^495↓S is non-canonical, its P4/P2/P1′ combination may recapitulate features described for caspase-1–preferred tetrapeptides (e.g., YVAD-like), and its flexible juxtamembrane context may provide a permissive geometry for recognition under our conditions [36–38].

Immunoblotting resolved full-length Tyro3 at 130–140 kDa—above the predicted ~ 97 kDa due to extensive N-glycosylation—and, upon inflammasome activation, a ~ 60-kDa N-terminal fragment (Tyro3-N) recognized by ectodomain antibody (consistent with a ~ 55-kDa backbone plus glycan mass). The corresponding C-terminal kinase fragment (Tyro3-C; ~ 42 kDa) was not assessed in this study, as our analysis focused on the N-terminal product; future work will determine the fate of the C-terminal kinase domain. This pattern—stable N-terminal fragment with loss of the kinase domain—uncouples ligand engagement from downstream signaling, functionally inactivating the receptor during inflammation [35].

Co-immunoprecipitation from injured nerves detected caspase-1–Tyro3 complexes alongside NLRP3, suggesting proximity-facilitated cleavage at the inflammasome; interaction was also observed in HEK293T under overexpression conditions [38]. The rapid kinetics support a putative enzyme–substrate relationship under our conditions; nevertheless, biochemical reconstitution with purified proteins is required for definitive proof.

Signaling network disruption

Tyro3 cleavage is associated with disruption of myelination programs [17]. Loss of Tyro3 signaling leads to decreased p-Fyn, p-Akt, and p-Erk [39, 40], followed by downregulation of Oct6, Krox20, and MBP [41, 42]. Our analyses revealed heterogeneity: the proportion of Tyro3⁺CD271⁺ Schwann cells decreased by ~ 25 percentage points, and the remaining cells showed reduced receptor density [43]. Early receptor recovery preceded later myelin protein increases, indicating temporal progression from receptor restoration to functional output. These data suggest that receptor integrity is a prerequisite for effective growth-factor responses, helping explain variable efficacy of neurotrophic therapies: without preserving receptor integrity first, exogenous growth factors cannot overcome threshold requirements for receptor density and activity [44]. Notably, caspase-1-mediated Tyro3 cleavage not only reduces the total receptor available for signaling but also sharply diminishes pathway activation. Our phospho/total ratio analyses confirmed that the suppression of Gas6–Tyro3 signaling reflects impaired receptor activation rather than merely reduced protein levels, underscoring the mechanistic link between Tyro3 cleavage and remyelination failure.

Therapeutic efficacy of combined treatment

A key mechanistic implication is that ligand supplementation alone is insufficient once the receptor has been proteolytically inactivated. Although Gas6 provides the essential pro-myelinating ligand input, effective signal transduction requires an intact Tyro3 intracellular kinase domain. Inflammasome activation functionally uncouples ligand engagement from downstream signaling by generating the Tyro3-N fragment and releasing the kinase domain from the membrane. Consequently, VX-765 is necessary to prevent caspase-1–mediated Tyro3 cleavage and restore receptor competence. This combined strategy therefore addresses both critical requirements—receptor integrity (VX-765) and ligand availability (Gas6)—to reinstate the Tyro3–Fyn–Akt/Erk pro-myelination signaling axis.

VX-765 and Gas6 improved outcomes through complementary mechanisms and may also attenuate broader inflammasome-driven injury responses [10, 11]. VX-765 preserved Tyro3 integrity by inhibiting caspase-1, while Gas6 activated retained receptors to promote myelination. The combination enhanced functional recovery and nerve physiology while reducing microenvironmental pathology, with elevated p-Akt, Oct6, and MBP in vivo compared with VX-765 alone and controls [45]. VX-765 also exerts pleiotropic effects through caspase-1 inhibition, attenuating IL-1β/IL-18 maturation and secretion, limiting GSDMD-dependent pyroptotic membrane rupture, and potentially mitigating secondary cell death including apoptosis [25, 46]. Gas6 provides pathway-specific support for the mechanistic relevance of Tyro3:: it restored downstream Tyro3 signaling and pro-myelinating programs, consistent with Tyro3–Fyn regulation of Schwann cell myelination [45] and findings that Gas6 within the injured nerve microenvironment promotes Schwann cell maturation and remyelination [17, 47]. Gas6/TAM signaling may also confer anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects [48–50]. Thus, VX-765’s pleiotropy precludes attributing the therapeutic benefit exclusively to Tyro3 preservation; nevertheless, these data support the Gas6–Tyro3 axis as a key mechanistic component of the observed recovery. Consistent with this interpretation, ultrastructural analysis revealed that VX-765 + Gas6 combination accelerated Bands of Büngner formation and remyelination by days 14–28 compared with VX-765 alone.

Integration with current understanding

Our findings extend the inflammasome functional repertoire beyond cytokine maturation and pyroptosis. Protease-mediated receptor inactivation suggests an additional effector mechanism that impairs cellular function without inducing death [48]. This mechanism may be particularly relevant in post-mitotic cells such as Schwann cells, where function can be modulated without loss of cell number during inflammation [49, 51]. The concept of sublytic pyroptosis aligns with reports of sublytic inflammasome activation, and our data add a receptor-level explanation—Tyro3 cleavage—for how inflammation limits the phenotypic transitions required for remyelination [49, 51]. More broadly, protease-mediated receptor inactivation may not be unique to peripheral nerve injury [52]; its relevance in other chronic inflammatory contexts—including the central nervous system and immune-mediated disorders—warrants systematic, context-specific investigation [39, 52, 53].

Potential relevance to CNS myelin repair

This framework may extend to CNS glia, as oligodendrocyte lineage cells express TAM receptors and respond to Gas6/TAM signaling, while CNS injuries often involve chronic inflammasome activation. Sublytic inflammasome activity—insufficient for lysis but sufficient to disable pro-repair receptors—may impair remyelination in CNS disorders with sterile inflammation (demyelinating disease, traumatic brain/spinal cord injury). Direct testing in oligodendrocytes and human glial systems is warranted.

Tyro3-N as a candidate biomarker

The ~ 60-kDa Tyro3-N fragment may report inflammasome-linked receptor inactivation. If detectable in serum, CSF, or nerve-biopsy lysates, Tyro3-N could (i) stratify patients by inflammasome activity, (ii) serve as a pharmacodynamic marker for caspase-1–targeted therapies, and (iii) identify candidates for combined inflammasome suppression and TAM pathway augmentation. Validation requires neo-epitope–based immunoassays and assessment of pre-analytical stability and clinical specificity.

Limitations and future directions

Our acute transection model may not fully recapitulate chronic neuropathies in which sustained low-grade inflammation could alter cleavage dynamics. We focused on NLRP3 and Tyro3; roles for AIM2/NLRC4 and Axl/Mer remain to be explored [17, 49, 54].

One unexpected finding was that Gas6 alone partially increased Akt phosphorylation despite incomplete Tyro3 preservation (Fig. 6G–H). Because Gas6 serves as a shared ligand for all TAM family receptors [17, 49, 54], it may engage Mer—which is expressed at lower levels in Schwann cells—to activate PI3K-Akt signaling when Tyro3 is compromised. Alternatively, the residual pool of uncleaved Tyro3 under inflammatory conditions may be preferentially activated by exogenous Gas6. The cellular heterogeneity of injured nerve tissue, which includes Schwann cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts with distinct TAM receptor profiles, likely contributes to the variability observed in vivo. Regardless of the precise mechanism, the combination of VX-765 and Gas6 consistently produced greater benefit than either agent alone, supporting a therapeutic strategy that preserves receptor integrity while providing ligand stimulation.

Our dosing schedule—intraoperative Gas6 plus postoperative VX-765—was guided by the pharmacokinetic properties of each agent: Gas6, a ~ 75–85 kDa glycoprotein with limited systemic bioavailability, requires local delivery, whereas VX-765 distributes broadly after systemic administration [55]. For clinical translation, sustained-release Gas6 formulations (e.g., mPEG-PLGA nanoparticles) warrant investigation [55]. Both agents are amenable to development: VX-765 has undergone Phase II clinical evaluation [56], and recombinant Gas6 is manufacturable. Optimal dosing, therapeutic window, long-term safety, and efficacy across injury severities require systematic study. Patient stratification based on local inflammation intensity or Tyro3 cleavage status may further refine therapeutic precision.

Although our genetic and in vivo data strongly support direct caspase-1–mediated Tyro3 cleavage at Asp495, in vitro reconstitution with purified proteins would provide definitive biochemical validation. Our current evidence includes: (i) near-complete resistance of the D495A mutant to inflammasome-induced processing, (ii) absence of Tyro3-N fragments in Caspase-1⁻/⁻ nerves, (iii) caspase-1–Tyro3 physical association by co-immunoprecipitation, and (iv) localization of the cleavage site to a juxtamembrane region compatible with caspase recognition. Future in vitro cleavage assays using purified recombinant caspase-1 and Tyro3 intracellular-domain constructs will define cleavage kinetics and stoichiometry.

Conclusion

Sublytic inflammasome activation in Schwann cells impairs regeneration through caspase-1-mediated Tyro3 cleavage. This protease-mediated receptor inactivation provides a mechanistic link between inflammation and failed nerve repair without extensive cell death. The efficacy of combined VX-765 and Gas6 treatment supports the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway, pending further validation.
