Tagitinin C‐Rich Extract From Tithonia diversifolia (hemsl.) A. Gray Exerts Activity Against Pathogenic Bacteria and Biofilms
Authors: Marianne Celestino Andrade, Rayane Andrade Santana Freitas, Helton Caio Santana Santos, Matheus Antoni da Silva Costa, Ricardo Luiz Cavalcanti de Albuquerque‐Junior, Lívia Maria do Amorim Costa Gaspar, Agenor Gomes dos Santos‐Neto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe increasing prevalence of persistent and difficult‐to‐treat infections has intensified interest in natural compounds able to act on both planktonic bacteria and biofilm structures. Tithonia diversifolia , a species rich in bioactive metabolites such as tagitinin C, has emerged as a promising source of antimicrobial agents. This study evaluated the antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of a tagitinin C‐rich leaf extract and potential mechanisms of action. The extract was obtained by supercritical CO2 extraction, chemically characterized by HPLC, and assessed against Staphylococcus aureus , Enterococcus faecalis , and Pseudomonas aeruginosa using disk diffusion, broth microdilution, and molecular docking assays. Antibiofilm activity was investigated against a biofilm‐forming S. aureus strain using coverslip disruption assays, optical microscopy, and scanning electron microscopy. The extract showed strong antimicrobial activity against S. aureus (MIC/MBC: 0.039 mg/mL), intermediate inhibition of E. faecalis (1.25 mg/mL), and moderate effects on P. aeruginosa (5 mg/mL). It also promoted significant biofilm disruption (66.2% ± 4.1%), with microscopy revealing reduced bacterial density and altered structural organization. Docking results demonstrated favorable binding affinities with MurA, MurE, and MurG, suggesting stable interactions with essential enzymes involved in peptidoglycan synthesis. Overall, the findings highlight the therapeutic potential of T. diversifolia extract against resistant microorganisms and biofilm‐associated infections.Keywords: antibacterial agents, Asteraceae, pharmacodynamics, plant extracts, sesquiterpene

Introduction

The global rise in bacterial infections represents one of the most critical contemporary threats to public health. This challenge is further intensified by the widespread dissemination of antimicrobial resistance, a phenomenon driven by multiple factors, including the indiscriminate and inappropriate use of antimicrobials, self‐medication, and failures in therapeutic regimens. Such practices undermine the efficacy of available drugs, contributing to rising mortality rates, prolonged hospital stays, and escalating healthcare costs [1, 2, 3].

Compounding this issue is the ability of numerous pathogenic bacteria to form biofilms, which substantially increases the complexity of infection management. Biofilms are structured microbial communities encased in an extracellular polymeric matrix that protects cells from antibiotic action and host immune defenses. This three‐dimensional architecture generates microenvironments with altered metabolic and physiological states, ultimately driving chronic, recalcitrant infections that are difficult to eradicate [4]. Consequently, developing new antimicrobial strategies effective against both planktonic cells and biofilms has become an urgent priority in medical microbiology and pharmaceutical biotechnology [5].

Within this context, there is growing interest in therapeutic alternatives that are simultaneously effective, safe, and environmentally sustainable [6]. Natural plant‐derived compounds have emerged as particularly promising sources of novel antimicrobial agents. Among these species, Tithonia diversifolia (Hemsl.) A. Gray, commonly known as Mexican sunflower, is notable for its broad spectrum of pharmacological properties and ethnomedicinal relevance. Traditionally used to treat wounds, fever, and diabetes, the species is widely recognized for its substantial medicinal and nutritional value [7].

Chemically, T. diversifolia is rich in secondary metabolites, including saponins, alkaloids, glycosides, phenolics, flavonoids, and especially sesquiterpene lactones, among which tagitinin C stands out due to its reactive α,β‐unsaturated structure and diverse biological activities [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Despite this therapeutic potential, obtaining chemically consistent extracts is challenging when using traditional solvent‐based methods, which rely on toxic and environmentally harmful organic solvents [12]. Supercritical CO2 extraction offers a superior alternative, as it is nontoxic, recyclable, and highly selective, producing solvent‐free, thermally preserved, and chemically reproducible extracts rich in tagitinin C [13, 14, 15, 16].

Given the strong therapeutic and biotechnological potential of T. diversifolia , investigating its antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities, as well as elucidating its possible mechanisms of action, represents an innovative and promising approach. Particular attention has been directed toward compounds able to interfere with peptidoglycan biosynthesis, an essential process for maintaining bacterial cell wall integrity [17]. In this context, tagitinin C shows potential to interact with key enzymes of the Mur pathway, such as MurA, MurE, and MurG, which catalyze critical steps in the formation of the UDP‐MurNAc‐pentapeptide precursor and are widely recognized as promising targets for new antimicrobials [18]. Inhibiting these enzymes can compromise essential stages of peptidoglycan biosynthesis, ultimately leading to structural weakening and bacterial cell death. Molecular docking studies can predict chemical interactions between molecules and these targets, enabling the analysis of potential multitarget mechanisms of action involving different points of the biosynthetic pathway. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities of the tagitinin C‐rich extract from T. diversifolia , as well as to predict its potential mechanism of action against clinically relevant microorganisms, to validate its biotechnological promise and contribute to the development of natural and sustainable antimicrobial alternatives.

Materials and Methods

Extraction and Chemical Characterization of the Tagitinin C‐Rich Tithonia diversifolia Leaf Extract (EscTD)

The extraction of EscTD and the identification of tagitinin C were previously performed by Santos‐Neto et al. [16], and the material was provided for the analyses conducted in this study. Briefly, T. diversifolia leaves were collected and taxonomically identified. After washing, drying, grinding, and sieving, the plant material was subjected to supercritical CO2 extraction (40°C, 200 bar, 180 min), yielding the extract designated as EscTD, which was stored under refrigeration. The identification of tagitinin C was carried out by fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a C18 column and a mobile phase consisting of water with 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile (45:55), with detection at 254 nm and confirmation by co‐injection with a purified standard.

Evaluation of Antimicrobial Activity

Agar Disk Diffusion Assay

The initial screening of the antimicrobial activity of EscTD was performed using the qualitative agar disk diffusion assay. Standard reference strains were used: Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212. After reactivation and isolation on Nutrient Agar (Kasvi) followed by incubation at 36°C ± 1°C for 24 h, microbial suspensions were prepared in sterile saline and adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to the 0.5 McFarland standard (≈108 CFU/mL). The standardized suspensions were then inoculated onto sterile Mueller Hinton agar (Kasvi) plates using a sterile swab and the spread‐plate technique. Sterile paper filter disks (6 mm diameter) were impregnated with EscTD (30 μg/disk). Trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (25 μg/disk) was adopted as the positive control, whereas 10 μL of the solvent used for extract solubilization (5% methanol) was adopted as the negative control. The plates were incubated at 36°C ± 1°C for 24 h, and antimicrobial activity was assessed by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zones formed around the disks, with the data expressed as mean values in millimeters. The assay procedures, as well as the extracts and control, were tested in triplicate, with results obtained from three independent repetitions and followed the recommendations of EUCAST [19], with the results interpreted according to the Breakpoint Tables for MIC and inhibition zone diameter interpretation [20].

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) Assay

The MIC and MBC assays were performed according to Saini et al. [21], with minor adaptations. Bacterial suspensions (≈104 CFU/mL) of the reference strains were inoculated into microplates containing Mueller‐Hinton broth (Kasvi) and exposed to serial dilutions of the EscTD, starting at 10 mg/mL. Gentamicin at 50 μg/mL was used as positive control, whereas 5% methanol served as the negative control (final concentration ≤ 2.5% in the well). The extracts and controls were tested in triplicate. After incubation at 36°C ± 1°C for 24 h, 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) was added for MIC determination and calculation of the inhibition percentage (Equation 1), based on absorbance readings at 530 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer. For MBC determination, aliquots from wells corresponding to concentrations ≥ MIC were plated on Mueller‐Hinton agar. MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial growth was observed. The results were obtained from three independent repetitions.

Analysis of Bacterial Biofilm Disruption

Polystyrene Microplate Adherence Assay

Biofilm quantification was performed according to the method described by Iorio et al. [22], using S. aureus ATCC 25923 (nonbiofilm producer) and ATCC 43300 (biofilm producer). Bacterial suspensions adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard were inoculated (20 μL) into 96‐well microplates containing 180 μL of BHI broth and incubated at 36°C ± 1°C for 24 h. After washing and drying, 2% crystal violet was added for 5 min, followed by washing and solubilization of the bound dye with 95% ethanol. Absorbance was measured at 492 nm. Biofilm production intensity was classified according to Stepanović et al. [23] as nonproducer, low, moderate, or strong, based on optical density (OD) values.

Coverslip‐Based Biofilm Disruption Assay

The evaluation of EscTD activity against bacterial biofilms was performed according to the methodology described by Nithya, Begum & Pandian [24], with modifications. Sterile coverslips (24 × 24 mm) were placed into six‐well plates containing 450 μL of Brain Heart Infusion broth (Kasvi) and 50 μL of the bacterial suspension previously adjusted to the turbidity corresponding to the 0.5 McFarland standard. After 24 h of incubation at 36°C ± 1°C, biofilm formation was allowed to occur on the surface of the coverslips, which were then treated with 1 mL of the extract (10 mg/mL) dissolved in methanol 5% (final concentration ≈2.5% in the well) for 48 h under the same incubation conditions. The negative controls were conducted using 2.5% methanol and untreated biofilms. All treatments were carried out in triplicate. Subsequently, the coverslips were gently washed with sterile saline to remove nonadherent cells, stained with 2% crystal violet, and examined under a light microscope (400×, Nikon Eclipse Ti 100).

Qualitative and Quantitative Analysis of Biofilm Disruption

Light microscopy analysis was performed in 20 random fields distributed across the entire surface of the coverslip, ensuring observation of central, marginal, and corner regions. The images obtained were processed and quantified using ImageJ software, and biofilm disruption was expressed as the percentage of biofilm‐free area relative to the total area analyzed. The morphology and integrity of the biofilms were examined by scanning electron microscopy using a Jeol JSM IT500‐HR microscope. Coverslips containing formed biofilms were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, washed in phosphate buffer, and dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series. After drying, the samples were mounted on aluminum stubs, sputter‐coated with gold, and analyzed under vacuum at 10 kV, with a 10 mm working distance and a secondary electron detector. Micrographs obtained from different regions of the coverslips allowed qualitative assessment of biofilm architecture, density, and structural disorganization following exposure to the extract.

Molecular Docking of Tagitinin C Against MurE, MurG, and MurA Enzymes Involved in Peptidoglycan Biosynthesis

For the molecular docking analysis, the three‐dimensional structures of the enzymes involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/): MurE from S. aureus (PDB: 4C12), MurG from P. aeruginosa (PDB: 3S2U), and MurA from E. faecalis (PDB: 8D84). The proteins were prepared by removing water molecules and nonessential ligands, correcting missing residues, and adding polar hydrogens to ensure the structural integrity required for docking. Docking was performed exclusively for the ligand tagitinin C using AutoDock Vina (v.1.2.7) [25], AutoDockTools (v.1.5.7), and BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (2025, v.25.1.0.24.284), maintaining standard search parameters and molecular flexibility. Interactions were evaluated based on binding energies (kcal/mol) and characterization of relevant intermolecular contacts, considering hydrogen bonds (≤ 2.5 Å) and hydrophobic interactions (≤ 5.0 Å), according to literature criteria for enzyme–ligand complex stability [26].

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard of the mean. Data showing normal distribution and homogeneous variances were analyzed using one‐way or two‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, version 5.0 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Chemical Characterization of EscTD

The extract used in this study was previously obtained and comprehensively characterized and published by our research group [16]. In summary, supercritical CO2 extraction resulted in a resinous extract, in which tagitinine C was identified as the major constituent by FTIR and reverse‐phase HPLC analyses. Quantification by HPLC demonstrated that tagitinine C corresponded to approximately 60% of the total extract content, supporting its use as a reliable chemical marker for T. diversifolia and reinforcing the phytochemical consistency of the material used in the present study.

Antibacterial Effect of EscTD

After extraction, an initial screening was conducted to assess the antimicrobial potential of the EscTD at high concentration using the disk diffusion assay. Overall, the EscTD demonstrated a selective antibacterial profile. No inhibitory effect was observed against E. coli , indicating a lack of activity toward this Gram‐negative species, in contrast with the susceptibility confirmed by the positive control. For P. aeruginosa , the extract exhibited moderate inhibition, whereas the control antibiotic had no effect, which is consistent with the intrinsic resistance of this species and suggests that the extract may act on strains less responsive to conventional drugs. Although P. aeruginosa produces a characteristic green pigment that diffuses throughout the medium, potentially masking visual assessment, clear zones of inhibition were observed in the assay. In these areas, confluent bacterial growth was interrupted, and isolated colonies near the inhibition zones exhibited a more brilliant appearance compared to regions closer to the disks. This indicates that, despite the uniform green coloration of the plate, the antimicrobial activity of the extract is effective against P. aeruginosa , highlighting the importance of careful visual interpretation when assessing pigmented bacterial cultures. In Gram‐positive bacteria, the EscTD showed strong inhibition of S. aureus and intermediate activity against E. faecalis , although to a lesser extent than the positive control, which may indicate a species‐dependent response pattern.

For a quantitative assessment of the antimicrobial potential of the EscTD, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were determined against the clinically relevant strains P. aeruginosa , S. aureus , and E. faecalis , which exhibited sensitivity during preliminary screening by disk diffusion, as detailed in Table 2. The results indicate that the extract displayed considerable antimicrobial activity against S. aureus , with MIC and MBC values of 0.039 mg/mL and intermediate activity against E. faecalis , with MIC and MBC values of 1.25 mg/mL. In contrast, P. aeruginosa showed relative resistance, presenting MIC and MBC values of 5.00 mg/mL.

For E. faecalis , the EscTD maintained inhibition levels above 80% down to 0.625 mg/mL, with mean values ranging from 103.5 ± 1.0% (10 mg/mL) a 81.1 ± 2.3% (0.625 mg/mL). At concentrations lower than this threshold (0.312 mg/mL), inhibition declined to < 80%, indicating a gradual reduction in antimicrobial efficacy at reduced doses. At higher concentrations, the extract showed performance similar to gentamicin, as shown in Figure 2B.

In contrast, P. aeruginosa showed sensitivity only at the highest extract concentrations (Figure 2C). Inhibition percentages > 80% were observed at 10 mg/mL (99.9% ± 1.6%) and 5 mg/mL (99.1% ± 1.6%), followed by a marked reduction at 2.5 mg/mL (58.9% ± 2.9%), with values remaining below 60% at the other concentrations tested.

Effect of EscTD on the Disruption of Biofilms Produced by S. aureus

The S. aureus ATCC 43300 isolate was classified as a moderate biofilm producer when compared with the negative control. In the assessment of biofilm formation capacity using a 96‐well polystyrene microplate, the mean optical density (OD) obtained for S. aureus was 0.2038 ± 0.079, whereas the negative control showed an OD of 0.0730 ± 0.003 (n = 6). These values indicate that the isolate forms significantly more biofilm than the control, characterizing it as a moderate producer and suitable for the biofilm disruption assay.

After confirming its biofilm‐forming ability, the S. aureus strain was induced to form a biofilm on a coverslip surface and subsequently treated with EscTD, with methanol‐treated and untreated groups maintained as controls. Treatment with EscTD promoted significantly greater disruption of the preformed biofilm matrix (66.19% ± 4.10%) compared with the methanol control (0.19% ± 0.18%) and the untreated control (1.83% ± 1.23%) (p < 0.0001). No significant difference was observed between the two control groups (p = 0.3151) (Figure 3A). These findings reinforce the antibiofilm potential of EscTD, acting primarily by disrupting the established biofilm matrix.

Microscopically, a similar pattern was observed on the biofilm surface in both the methanol‐treated and untreated groups (Figure 3B,C), characterized by a dense and homogeneous layer composed of bacterial cells embedded within the biofilm polymeric matrix, evident through intense violet staining. Bacterial cells appeared incorporated within the matrix, hindering clear visualization of individual cells, a typical feature of mature biofilms under light microscopy. In contrast, the biofilm treated with EscTD (Figure 3D) exhibited heterogeneous areas with regions without a biofilm matrix. In these areas, coccoid bacterial cells were observed adhered to the support surface, either clustered or dispersed, as well as zones completely free of cellular structures or biofilm, which were clearly distinct from the other groups.

Scanning electron microscopy highlighted the biofilm‐disrupting potential of EscTD (Figure 4A,B). The untreated area exhibited an irregular surface with numerous elevations and accumulated material characteristics of mature biofilms, in contrast to the area treated with EscTD, which displayed a visibly smoother surface. At higher magnification, the micrograph revealed that the treated region (Figure 4C) contained only thin residual layers of biofilm along with a low number of microorganisms, either isolated or in small clusters. Conversely, the untreated area showed multiple overlapping layers of extracellular matrix and abundant spherical bacterial cells embedded within the organic structure of the biofilm (Figure 4D).

Molecular Docking of Tagitinin C From EscTD Against the MurE, MurG, and MurA Enzymes

As shown in Table 3, tagitinin C was evaluated for its interaction potential with S. aureus MurE. According to the molecular docking predictions, the compound exhibited a binding affinity of −8.1 kcal/mol for MurE, indicating an energetically favorable interaction with the enzyme's active site. Tagitinin C was also assessed against other enzymes involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis. The predicted affinity for MurA from E. faecalis was −8.2 kcal/mol, while the most notable value was observed for MurG from P. aeruginosa , with −8.6 kcal/mol. These findings suggest that tagitinin C displays a consistent and potentially relevant interaction profile against multiple targets within the bacterial cell wall biosynthesis pathway.

Molecular docking analysis (Figure 5) revealed that tagitinin C exhibits high affinity for essential enzymes of the peptidoglycan biosynthesis pathway, binding stably to the active sites responsible for cell wall formation in S. aureus , E. faecalis , and P. aeruginosa . In all evaluated proteins, hydrogen‐bond interactions (both conventional and side‐chain‐mediated) were predominant, reflecting a consistent pattern of stereoelectronic complementarity between the ligand and the catalytic residues of the enzymes.

Three‐dimensional and two‐dimensional structures showing the predicted molecular docking interactions between tagitinin C from EscTD and the enzymes (A) MurE [chain A], (B) MurA [chain E], and (C) MurG [chain A].

For S. aureus MurE, tagitinin C established three relevant hydrogen‐bond interactions (Figure 5A). Residue ASN151 formed a hydrogen bond at 2.33 Å, while HIS353 exhibited a conventional interaction at 2.59 Å. Additionally, HIS205 engaged with the ligand at 3.68 Å, forming a carbon‐type hydrogen bond. The complementarity between the compound's oxygenated groups and polar enzyme residues favored its accommodation within the catalytic center, suggesting that tagitinin C may impair substrate recognition and positioning during MurE activity.

In E. faecalis MurA, two strong hydrogen‐bond interactions were identified (Figure 5B). HIS129 formed a short 1.82 Å bond, indicating strong electrostatic attraction, while VAL166 interacted at 2.17 Å, representing another conventional hydrogen bond. The orientation of tagitinin C within the catalytic site suggests direct competition with phosphoenolpyruvate, potentially interfering with the early steps of peptidoglycan synthesis.

The most complex anchoring pattern occurred in P. aeruginosa MurG, where six hydrogen bonds were observed (Figure 5C). These involved residues THR12 (2.75 Å), GLY79 (2.41 Å), SER13 (2.23 Å), ASN17 (2.02 Å), ARG93 (2.13 Å), and GLY14 (2.52 Å). The short distances, particularly with ASN17 and ARG93, indicate highly strong interactions. The distribution of these bonds throughout the active site demonstrates that the ligand anchors deeply within the catalytic cavity, engaging side chains as well as backbone atoms, a multivalent interaction pattern characteristic of effective inhibitors of the late stages of peptidoglycan biosynthesis.

Discussion

Supercritical CO2 extraction stands out as a highly efficient and advantageous method for obtaining the bioactive compounds of T. diversifolia . This technique offers greater selectivity, preserves heat‐sensitive structures, and eliminates toxic residues, outperforming conventional solvent‐based methods. In addition, it promotes phytochemical standardization and yields purer extracts, characteristics that are essential for pharmaceutical applications and for the development of products with greater reproducibility and quality [13, 14]. In this context, the ability to obtain an extract with a high content of chemical markers such as tagitinin C represents an important advance for translational development since chemical reproducibility is a prerequisite for future pharmaceutical application and industrial scalability.

According to the disk diffusion assays, the negative control exhibited no inhibition zones in any of the tests, and the positive controls showed the expected results for the tested strains according to the breakpoints established by EUCAST, supporting the reliability and reproducibility of the results. Additionally, the inhibition zones obtained for positive control were interpreted according to the clinical breakpoints established by EUCAST [20], allowing the experimental results to be correlated with internationally recognized susceptibility standards and thus contextualizing the observed antimicrobial efficacy in comparison with consolidated clinical data. Overall, the findings demonstrate that the supercritical extract of T. diversifolia rich in tagitinin C displays selective and significant antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram‐positive bacteria, with efficacy comparable to trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole. This activity is likely associated with tagitinin C, the major constituent of the EscTD, underscoring the biotechnological and pharmacological potential of the species as a natural source of antimicrobial agents active against resistant strains.

The antimicrobial activity observed for EscTD corroborates previous findings reporting the potential of T. diversifolia against pathogenic bacteria, especially Gram‐positive species [28, 29]. In the present study, S. aureus exhibited high sensitivity in both diffusion assays and MIC/MBC determinations, with efficacy comparable to gentamicin, indicating the strong ability of the extract to compromise the cellular integrity of this species. This behavior consists of the higher permeability of Gram‐positive bacterial cell walls to lipophilic compounds, such as tagitinin C [30]. Moreover, compounds with multitarget mechanisms tend to reduce the likelihood of rapid resistance development, making them particularly relevant in the face of the growing threat posed by multidrug‐resistant microorganisms [31].

In contrast, P. aeruginosa showed low susceptibility to the extract, with reduced inhibition zones and elevated MIC and MBC values. Such resistance is compatible with the biology of the species, which possesses a highly impermeable outer membrane, active efflux pumps, and intrinsic mechanisms that hinder the entry of lipophilic substances such as tagitinin C [32]. The discrepancy between the limited activity observed here and the positive results reported for polar extracts of T. diversifolia highlights the critical influence of phytochemical profiles [33, 34]. More hydrophilic extracts exhibit greater permeability in Gram‐negative bacteria, whereas lipophilic extracts obtained by supercritical CO2 encounter more rigid structural barriers, explaining differences among studies [30]. This contrast validates the need for future investigations employing permeabilization strategies or nanostructured encapsulation capable of enhancing the diffusion of lipophilic compounds across the Gram‐negative outer membrane.

For E. faecalis , antibacterial activity was observed, with the extract maintaining inhibition values above 80% at moderate concentrations, indicating a promising profile, although less pronounced than that observed for S. aureus . Given that E. faecalis is frequently associated with persistent infections and biofilms, even intermediate activity may have clinical relevance when combined with other antimicrobial agents or therapeutic strategies [35]. It is also important to note that initial in vitro assays, such as disk diffusion and broth microdilution, provide only preliminary susceptibility categorization, based on comparisons between controls and variations in inhibition zone diameters across species. Although such parameters were used here to classify sensitivity levels, the confirmation of true clinical relevance necessarily depends on pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic studies, as high MIC values or reduced inhibition zones may still reflect susceptibility.

The antibiofilm evaluation demonstrated that EscTD exerts a significant effect on disrupting S. aureus biofilms. Both light and electron microscopy revealed a substantial reduction in the extracellular matrix, loss of structural uniformity, and decreased bacterial density in treated areas. These findings suggest a multifactorial mechanism in which antimicrobial action is not limited to inhibiting growth but also affects complex three‐dimensional structures that are typically more resistant due to physical barriers, quorum‐sensing communication, and altered biofilm physiology [36, 37]. The convergence of antibacterial and antibiofilm activity reinforces the extract's potential as a therapeutic agent in chronic infections involving biofilms. Additionally, agents capable of acting against biofilm structures are particularly valuable, given that more than 70% of chronic human infections involve biofilm forms, which typically exhibit tolerance up to 1000‐fold higher than that of planktonic cells exposed to conventional antibiotics [38]. Assays such as Minimum Biofilm Eradication Concentration (MBEC) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), considered for the evaluation of biofilm eradication and cell viability, respectively, were not performed in this study. However, the inhibitory effects observed on bacterial growth indicate that the extract has potential activity against biofilm‐forming strains. Future studies including MBEC and CLSM assays are needed to confirm its efficacy against established biofilms and to evaluate its activity in complex microbial communities [39, 40].

The molecular docking analyses performed in this study provided important evidence regarding the suggestion of the action of tagitinin C, indicating that the compound may act as a multitarget inhibitor of the cytoplasmic pathway of peptidoglycan biosynthesis. The binding affinities observed for MurA, MurE, and MurG revealed energetically favorable interactions with essential catalytic residues of these enzymes, suggesting that tagitinin C can stably occupy functional sites responsible for the formation of the structural precursor of the bacterial cell wall. It is noteworthy that the docking simulations were conducted specifically at the catalytic active sites, that is, the regions where natural ligands (substrates and cofactors) bind during each step of the pathway [18].

The interaction of tagitinin C in these same regions suggests direct competition with natural ligands, which may hinder progression along the biosynthetic pathway. By interfering with multiple essential catalytic steps that are highly conserved among bacterial species and absent in human cells, tagitinin C stands out as a pharmacologically relevant molecule capable of directly affecting cell wall integrity and contributing to a broad‐spectrum mechanism of action.

Although MurG from P. aeruginosa displayed the highest theoretical affinity, this predicted potency was not reflected in the in vitro activity, likely due to the limited cellular permeability of this species [41]. This finding highlights a well‐known discrepancy in the literature between computational and biological efficacy, emphasizing that cellular access to the intracellular target is as determinant as molecular affinity [42]. These results underscore the importance of integrated approaches, combining in silico predictions, biochemical assays, and microbiological testing to better understand the potential of natural compounds as antimicrobial candidates.

In comparison, the strong agreement between docking predictions and the high sensitivity of S. aureus suggests that tagitinin C reaches its targets more efficiently in Gram‐positive bacteria, supporting the hypothesis of a mechanism directed toward inhibition of cell wall synthesis. This would explain both the bactericidal activity observed and the extract's ability to disrupt biofilms. This dual action, against planktonic cells and biofilms, is uncommon among natural compounds and reinforces the relevance of tagitinin C as a multitarget candidate.

MurA, MurE, and MurG constitute an essential cytoplasmic pathway for peptidoglycan biosynthesis, catalyzing sequential reactions that culminate in the formation of UDP‐N‐acetylmuramyl‐pentapeptide, which is indispensable for the maintenance of bacterial cell wall integrity. Because they participate in highly conserved processes that are absent in human cells and vital for bacterial survival, Mur enzymes are considered strategic targets in the development of new antimicrobials. MurA and MurG, for example, are already recognized as targets of natural and synthetic inhibitors, while MurE is responsible for incorporating critical amino acids into the peptide core, directly influencing peptidoglycan architecture [17, 18]. Therefore, the identification of favorable interactions between tagitinin C and MurA, MurE, and MurG strengthens the hypothesis of an effective cell‐wall‐targeted mechanism, which is particularly relevant considering the urgent need for new antimicrobial agents effective against resistant pathogens.

Although variations in MIC values and extract potency are reported in the literature for T. diversifolia , such differences can be attributed to intrinsic factors such as extraction method, chemical profile, edaphoclimatic conditions, and plant phenological stage [29, 43]. The standardization achieved in the present study, with a high content of tagitinin C, helps reduce such variability and strengthens the translational potential of EscTD. Nevertheless, in vivo studies, toxicological assessments, and pharmacokinetic analyses will be essential to confirm the safety, bioavailability, and systemic efficacy of the extract, enabling advances toward pharmaceutical formulation development, including nanoparticles or controlled‐release systems designed to optimize stability and increase penetration into target pathogens while overcoming pharmacodynamic barriers. Future studies assessing synergism between encapsulated conventional antibiotics and the extract may reveal therapeutic combinations capable of reducing dosages, minimizing adverse effects, and expanding the spectrum of antimicrobial activity.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that the supercritical extract of T. diversifolia leaves, rich in tagitinin C, exhibits notable antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity, particularly against Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecalis . The extract showed bactericidal effects at low concentrations, significant biofilm disruption capacity, and consistent interaction with key enzymes of the peptidoglycan biosynthesis pathway, suggesting a multitarget mechanism of action. Although its efficacy against Pseudomonas aeruginosa was lower, likely due to intrinsic structural barriers, the antimicrobial potential of the extract may be enhanced through optimization strategies. Overall, these findings support T. diversifolia as a promising source of bioactive compounds and provide a solid basis for future investigations, including in vivo studies, toxicological assessments, and the development of innovative formulations aimed at addressing antimicrobial resistance.
