Integrating image processing and deep learning framework for automatic supernumerary tooth detection on occlusal radiographs
Authors: Jui-An Fu, Chia-Lin Hsu, Tsung-Yi Chen, Hung-I Wu, Tsung-Yun Cheng, Wei-Chun Lu, Chiung-An Chen, Sze-Teng Liong, Kuo-Chen Li, Ya-Yun Huang, Wei-Chen Tu, Patricia Angela R Abu, Li-Chuan Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeSupernumerary tooth is the most common form of hyperdontia in children and is typically identified through radiographic examination. Although occlusal radiographs are recommended for assessing anterior abnormalities, anatomical superimposition and variability in pediatric imaging make early detection challenging, thereby increasing diagnostic inconsistency. Artificial intelligence has shown strong potential in dental imaging, but applications involving pediatric occlusal radiographs remain limited due to low clinical utilization and insufficient datasets. Therefore, a reliable automated tool is needed to assist clinicians in improving supernumerary tooth screening accuracy.Materials and methodsWe collected 600 pediatric occlusal radiographs from children aged 6–12 years. An image-processing algorithm was developed to automatically segment the tooth-bearing region and improve feature visibility. These processed images were used to train five convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for the classification of supernumerary tooth. A fuzzy control strategy was employed to integrate the outputs of the five models, utilizing majority voting and weighted confidence scoring to generate the final diagnostic decision and mitigate model-specific bias.ResultsAll five CNN models achieved accuracy above 90 %. EfficientNet performed best and reached 96.12 % accuracy and 96.06 % F1 score. Statistical comparison further showed that EfficientNet achieved the highest agreement with ground truth, achieving R2 with 95.73 % and the strongest agreement level, as indicated by Cohen’s κ value of 0.9598.ConclusionThe proposed hybrid image-processing and deep learning framework provides accurate and interpretable supernumerary tooth detection on occlusal radiographs, offering a reliable tool to support pediatric diagnosis and improve clinical decision-making.Keywords: Convolutional neural networks, Fuzzy control strategy, Supernumerary tooth, Occlusal radiographs

Introduction

Hyperdontia refers to the presence of additional teeth beyond the normal dentition, most commonly manifesting as supernumerary teeth located along the maxillary midline or the anterior region.1 Epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of supernumerary teeth among individuals aged 6–19 years in Taiwan is approximately 11.25 %.2 Hyperdontia is typically diagnosed through clinical radiographic examinations, as most supernumerary teeth are impacted and cannot be detected visually. Therefore, dental panoramic radiographs (DPR), cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and periapical radiographs (PA) are often used for initial assessment,3 while occlusal radiographs are strongly recommended for children presenting with anterior dental abnormalities.4 Following radiographic evaluation, clinicians determine the appropriate timing for extraction and whether orthodontic intervention is necessary, depending on the tooth position, morphology, and its impact on adjacent structures, to ensure normal dental and orofacial development. However, due to the inherent projection angle of occlusal radiograph, anatomical superimposition is unavoidable, which increases the risk of missing early supernumerary teeth in children.5 This superimposition can further complicate interpretation for less experienced clinicians, and delayed detection during the primary dentition may lead to impeded eruption of permanent teeth or even altered facial development.6

PA, CBCT and occlusal radiograph are commonly used to screen for supernumerary teeth and to evaluate its relationship with adjacent teeth.7,8 However, the interpretation of these images often relies heavily on clinicians’ subjective judgment, which may lead to variability in diagnostic outcomes.9 In recent years, the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has accelerated the development of data-driven analysis and computer-assisted diagnostic technologies in dental imaging, with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and image-processing methods demonstrating strong performance in visual diagnostic support.10,11 As a primary imaging modality for examining anterior teeth and associated oral conditions, occlusal radiograph has also increasingly been integrated with AI for applications such as caries lesion detection and occlusal type classification,12,13 supporting the clinical feasibility of AI-based analysis on occlusal radiograph images and its potential to streamline diagnostic workflows and enhance clinical decision-making.

However, most AI-assisted diagnostic studies have focused primarily on adult dental conditions, and research involving pediatric occlusal radiograph remains relatively limited. Several factors contribute to this gap, including the lower clinical utilization of occlusal radiograph compared with PA and CBCT,14 the substantial variability in pediatric occlusal radiograph imaging due to unstable head and film positioning, and the lack of sufficiently large, standardized occlusal radiograph datasets,15 all of which pose challenges to developing robust and generalizable AI models. Despite these limitations, occlusal radiograph remains an essential imaging modality for evaluating the anterior dentition and detecting supernumerary teeth in children,16 underscoring the need for reliable automated systems that can improve diagnostic accuracy and consistency. To address this clinical demand, this study integrates AI with image-processing to develop an accurate and dependable supernumerary teeth detection framework designed to assist clinicians in interpreting pediatric occlusal radiograph, enhance diagnostic consistency, and strengthen clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Occlusal radiograph dataset collection and ground truth annotations

The occlusal radiograph dataset was collected from 600 pediatric patients aged 6–12 years and was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the chang gung medical foundation (IRB number: 202400084B0), Chang gung memorial hospital, Taoyuan, Taiwan. All occlusal radiograph were de-identified and annotated by three oral medicine specialists, each with at least three years of clinical experience. The exposure time was incrementally adjusted from 0.12 to 0.16 according to clinical occlusal radiograph requirements. Size-4 occlusal radiographs (7.5 × 5.7 cm) were selected for imaging, and the digitized images had a resolution of 1662 × 907 pixels. The dataset consisted of 295 occlusal radiographs with supernumerary teeth and 305 without supernumerary teeth. A total of 120 occlusal radiograph were reserved as the test set, while the remaining data were divided into a training set (75 %) and a validation set (25 %). Moreover, the dataset was split at the patient level to prevent data leakage. All occlusal radiographs from the same patient were assigned exclusively to either the training, validation, or test set, ensuring that no patient appeared in more than one dataset. All research procedures were conducted under the supervision of senior AI experts and pediatric dentists to minimize operator-related bias.

Supernumerary teeth feature extraction algorithms by image processing techniques

The original occlusal radiograph contained substantial background information that could adversely affect the accuracy of supernumerary teeth classification. Thus, we developed an automatic segment algorithm based on image processing techniques. First, the original occlusal radiograph (Fig. 1A) was processed using adaptive thresholding. Based on the mean pixel intensity of the occlusal radiograph, pixels with values above the threshold were converted to 255, whereas those below the threshold were set to 0. This step enhanced the contrast between the dental structures and the background region (Fig. 1B). We computed the horizontal pixel accumulation profile by summing the pixel intensities of each image row to determine the valid segment region within the occlusal radiograph. As shown in Fig. 1C, the accumulation curve exhibits a sharp decline followed by a stable near-zero plateau, indicating the transition from the tooth-bearing region to background areas. The local minimum marked in Fig. 1C was identified as the segmenting point, representing the boundary beyond which the image contains no clinically relevant structures. Therefore, rows from 0 to the detected segmenting point were retained and overlap to original occlusal radiograph, while the remaining rows were removed, and the result is shown in Fig. 1D. Moreover, the segment images were normalized to 256 × 256 to ensure consistent spatial dimensions (Fig. 1E). Contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) and bilateral filtering were then applied to enhance the edge contrast of each tooth while suppressing artifacts caused by uneven illumination or dark regions (Fig. 1F). Finally, color mapping technique was used to convert the grayscale format into a color format, which improved the detectability of potential lesion regions (Fig. 1G).

Convolutional neural networks model training

CNNs have become essential tools in dental X-ray image analysis, effectively overcoming challenges such as low contrast, blurred anatomical boundaries, and noise. By leveraging hierarchical feature extraction.17 CNNs can learn meaningful structural and pathological representations from complex dental imagery. In this study, five CNN models were employed for supernumerary teeth classification, and a fuzzy control system was used to determine the final prediction output, mitigating model-specific bias. For example, Fig. 2 shows the EfficientNet-based architecture employed in this study (Santa Clara County, CA, USA, 2019). The network takes a normalized 256 × 256 occlusal radiographs as input. It begins with a standard 3 × 3 convolutional layers, followed by a sequence of mobile inverted bottleneck (MBConv) blocks with kernel sizes of 3 × 3 and 5 × 5. These MBConv blocks expand and project feature channels while maintaining high computational efficiency through depthwise separable convolutions and squeeze-and-excitation optimization. The deeper stages of the network incorporate multiple stacked MBConv 5 × 5 blocks, enabling the extraction of more abstract, high-level, and large-receptive-field features for supernumerary teeth features extraction and accurate classification.

EfficientNet architecture used for supernumerary teeth classification on occlusal radiograph. MBConv, mobile inverted bottleneck.

A fuzzy control strategy was employed to integrate the predictions from five CNN models and reduce the risk of misclassification.18 During the supernumerary teeth classification stage, each model generated its own prediction independently. When at least three models agreed on the same class, that class was directly adopted as the final diagnosis, ensuring the robustness provided by majority voting. In cases where no majority consensus was reached, a weighted fuzzy decision mechanism was applied to consolidate the outputs further.19 Each CNN model produced a confidence score, defined as the softmax probability of the predicted class, representing the certainty of its prediction. When model predictions differed, the softmax probability associated with the predicted class from each model was used as its confidence weight. The probabilities supporting the same class were summed across models, and the class with the highest aggregated probability was selected as the final decision.

Model assessment metrics

A comprehensive evaluation of the proposed model was conducted using several widely adopted assessment metrics, to quantify its diagnostic accuracy and stability, which is shown in (1)–(5). These evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score, were calculated using the confusion matrix.20 In this study, the true positive (TP) and true negative (TN) values were obtained from the confusion matrix, representing model predictions that correctly match the ground-truth labels. In contrast, the false positive (FP) and false negative (FN) values indicate incorrect predictions, where FP refers to instances incorrectly classified as positive, and FN denotes positive cases that were mistakenly classified as negative. Accuracy represents the proportion of samples that were correctly classified by the model. Precision reflects the model’s ability to correctly identify supernumerary teeth among all predicted positive cases, whereas sensitivity measures its capability to detect all true positive instances. F1 score defined as the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity, offers a balanced assessment of overall model performance. In addition to these evaluation metrics, the 95 % confidence interval (95 % CI) was calculated to represent the range of uncertainty in the model’s parameter estimates at a 95 % confidence level. It reflected the variability and reliability of the estimation derived from the sample data. Specifically, χ̄ denoted the mean of the dataset, s denoted the standard deviation, and n represented the sample size.

Moreover, because our dataset is relatively small, we employed large-scale statistical analysis techniques to enhance the reliability of the study and ensure that the data distribution was free from bias. Statistical analysis metrics are shown in (6)–(8). R2 is used as a regression metric to quantify how much variance in the dependent variable is explained by the model, with higher values indicating stronger predictive power. Cohen’s κ measures inter-rater agreement between two classifiers while accounting for chance agreement, where Po is the observed agreement and Pe is the expected agreement. In addition, the McNemar test (χ2) was employed to evaluate whether there was a statistically significant difference in the classification error patterns between two CNN models when applied to the same test dataset, where b and c represent the numbers of discordant samples between two classification models.

Results

We evaluated the supernumerary teeth classification performance using a test dataset of 120 occlusal radiographs, and the results are shown in Table 1. All five models achieved performance above 90 % across the evaluation metrics. EfficientNet obtained the highest average performance with 96.12 % accuracy (95 % CI: 95.84–96.33), 96.33 % precision (95 % CI: 95.97–96.37), 95.79 % sensitivity (95 % CI: 95.13–96.07), and 96.06 % F1 score (95 % CI: 95.18–96.23). In addition to average performance, class-specific evaluation revealed notable differences between the detection of supernumerary teeth and non-supernumerary teeth. EfficientNet achieved 95.88 % accuracy (95 % CI: 95.52–96.11), 95.67 % precision (95 % CI: 95.31–95.92), 95.21 % sensitivity (95 % CI: 94.83–95.56), and 95.40 % F1 score (95 % CI: 95.02–95.71) for supernumerary teeth. For non-supernumerary teeth predictions, EfficientNet achieved 96.79 % accuracy (95 % CI: 96.48–96.98), 97.01 % precision (95 % CI: 96.74–97.23), 96.39 % sensitivity (95 % CI: 96.05–96.61), and 96.71 % F1 score (95 % CI: 96.32–96.94). Similar trends were observed in other models, all of which demonstrated slightly higher performance in identifying non-supernumerary teeth compared to supernumerary teeth.

Discussion

Supernumerary teeth detection plays a critical role in pediatric dentistry, as early diagnosis of supernumerary teeth can prevent eruption delays, adjacent-tooth displacement, and more complex treatment requirements.21 In clinical practice, dentists routinely interpret occlusal radiographs to identify supernumerary teeth presence, location, and relation to adjacent teeth prior to treatment planning. Due to their low radiation dose, high accessibility, and high resolution, occlusal radiographs remain commonly used as an initial screening tool for supernumerary teeth.22 However, occlusal radiographs provide only two-dimensional information, and image quality variability or blurred structural boundaries may hinder planning and increase subjectivity. 23 Therefore, in most clinical environments, particularly within primary care settings or high-volume pediatric practices, there is a growing need for an objective, standardized, and automated supernumerary teeth detection system to enhance diagnostic consistency and screening efficiency.

In recent years, the AI-assisted detection of supernumerary teeth has become a significant clinical need in dentistry. Syed et al. utilized CT-combined with a CNN model, achieving a precision of 90.72 % and an F1-score of 95.13 %.24 However, CT delivers a substantially higher radiation dose compared with 2D imaging for initial screening in children. Therefore, Zheng et al. adopted Faster R–CNN on PA to automatically detect supernumerary teeth, achieving 95 % accuracy and 90 % precision.25 Nevertheless, the limited field of view in PA easily crops the tip of the supernumerary teeth, resulting in misclassification of the supernumerary teeth. Accurately placing the PA inside the mouth can be challenging for children who are prone to a gag reflex. Therefore, compared with previous studies, occlusal radiograph offers complete coverage of the premaxilla area and provides significant advantages for preliminary supernumerary teeth screening in pediatric patients.4

The goal of this research was to provide an automated framework for preliminary assessment of the supernumerary teeth on occlusal radiograph. The main contributions are as follows.

We proposed a feature extraction algorithm by image processing to enhance the supernumerary teeth feature and used EfficinetNet to reach 96.12 % accuracy (95 % CI: 95.84–96.33) and 96.06 % F1 score (95 % CI: 95.18–96.23) and demonstrating robust and reliable diagnostic performance.

We introduce a clinically interpretable decision mechanism that integrates Grad-CAM visualization with a fuzzy control strategy to consolidate multi-model predictions, providing transparent, visualization-guided detection of supernumerary teeth.

Nevertheless, this study had several limitations. First, the occlusal radiograph dataset was collected from a single institution, which may restrict the generalizability of the proposed AI-based supernumerary teeth detection framework to different clinical settings and various radiographic equipment. Second, although the models demonstrated strong performance, the overall sample size remains limited. While this accuracy aligns with top-tier dental AI benchmarks, the inherent error margin and the potential for class imbalance in rare dental anomalies may affect statistical stability and model robustness. Furthermore, the proposed method is intended solely as a clinical decision-support tool and should not be used as a definitive diagnostic or treatment standard. Ultimately, the final diagnoses must remain under the professional judgment of qualified dentists to ensure that critical pathological features are not overlooked. Future research should incorporate multi-center datasets, increase the diversity of pediatric populations, and integrate standardized imaging protocols to enhance the reliability of the model further. Additionally, expanding the system to detect other dental anomalies, improving model interpretability, and developing real-time clinical integration modules may enhance its applicability in routine dental practice.
