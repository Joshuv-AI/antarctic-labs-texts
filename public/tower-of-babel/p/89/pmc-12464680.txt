El Niño-Southern Oscillation and shifting malaria risks in the Amazon
Authors: Gabriel Zorello Laporta
Published: 2025

A sequence of prolonged droughts once destabilized a flourishing society. Settlements in regions with poor soils or limited water reserves declined first, while those in more buffered environments endured somewhat longer. As environmental stress converged with political fragmentation and resource pressure, widespread urban abandonment followed. This description refers to the collapse of the Classic Maya civilization,1 yet it also serves as a cautionary illustration of the vulnerabilities Amazonia faces under today's climatic and social pressures.2 In this context, a recent study by Arisco and colleagues in The Lancet Regional Health—Americas highlights the complex interplay between climate variability, including El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events, and malaria transmission in local Amazonian communities.3 The study emphasizes how socioeconomic, ecological, and climatic factors jointly shape disease risk and underscores the importance of developing localized, context-specific malaria control strategies that integrate climate forecasts. Its findings reinforce the need for adaptive public health planning as climate extremes become more frequent in vulnerable regions such as the Amazon.

Temperature and rainfall exhibit nonlinear effects on malaria transmission, with optimal ranges that maximize mosquito vector abundance and disease spread in each region and month. Moderate rainfall promotes higher vector density, whereas excessive rain can reduce mosquito populations by flooding breeding sites.4 Similarly, temperature shifts influence mosquito survival and parasite development. Transmission peaks within specific thermal windows, approximately 25–30 °C depending on the species, while higher or lower temperatures sharply reduce transmission, consistent with empirically derived nonlinear thermal responses.4,5 These climatic factors interact with seasonal and local extinction dynamics, determining whether malaria persists or temporarily disappears in vulnerable areas. Overall, the interplay of rainfall, temperature, and mosquito population dynamics governs the spatial and temporal variability of malaria risk.

The study by Arisco and colleagues provides results consistent with our 2015 study,6 which used climate data from 1970 to 2000 to predict that the dominant malaria vector in the Amazon, Anopheles (Nyssorhynchus) darlingi, would likely be displaced under drier future conditions. Arisco and colleagues analysed five million malaria cases recorded in the Brazilian Amazon between 2003 and 2022 and identified both expected and unexpected climate–disease relationships. As anticipated, temperatures of 25–30 °C and moderate rainfall in the preceding week increased malaria risk by 10% and 80%, respectively. Importantly, they also found that ENSO events (El Niño and La Niña) tended to create drier-than-average conditions in the Amazon, which were associated with reduced malaria incidence (approximately 37,000 fewer cases during El Niño and 2000 fewer during La Niña). These observations provide early empirical support for the 2015 prediction that An. darlingi would decline under drier conditions.

Although drier conditions may appear favourable by reducing malaria risk, the Maya catastrophe reminds us that environmental stress can produce cascading consequences. In the Amazon, two perils are particularly concerning. First, prolonged aridity could erode biodiversity, undermining ecological resilience that buffers disease emergence.7 Second, such changes may facilitate the invasion of novel vectors, notably Anopheles stephensi, which has expanded from South Asia into the Horn of Africa and beyond, thriving in urban and peri-urban habitats.8 Should this species reach Amazonia, it could establish in human-dense environments where local vectors are less adapted, reversing recent gains in malaria control. Health care policies and services must therefore remain vigilant, strengthening surveillance systems and preparedness strategies for both local transmission fluctuations and the potential incursion of highly competent invasive vectors.

An additional limitation of current models is the lack of explicit consideration of hydrography.9 Anopheline vectors rely on water bodies fed by rivers, and malaria suitability is strongly influenced by hydrological processes that determine the availability and stability of larval habitats. In the Amazon, deforestation not only alters local climate but also modifies drainage patterns, increasing ponds, puddles, floodplains, and small dams.10 These changes gradually transform flowing rivers into more stagnant environments, creating ideal breeding sites that can persist for decades and sustain malaria transmission even under drier conditions. Including hydrographic and land-use factors in predictive models is therefore essential, as it may reveal pathways for malaria persistence and resurgence that climate variables alone cannot fully capture.

Temperature, rainfall, hydrology, and vector dynamics drive Amazonian malaria, with transmission peaking at 25–30 °C. Deforestation alters habitats and may favor invasive species such as An. stephensi. The findings of Arisco and colleagues reinforce the urgent need for adaptive, evidence-based public health strategies that integrate climate, hydrology, and land use. Just as the Maya collapse illustrates cascading environmental impacts, Amazonia today demands proactive interventions to safeguard population health.
