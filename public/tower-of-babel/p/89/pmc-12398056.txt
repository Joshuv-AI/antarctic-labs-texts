E-health literacy and influencing factors of in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer patients: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Lidan Xu, Tingting Guo, Fangfang Chen, Ya Ma, Yuying Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundE-health literacy is critical to driving healthy behaviors in in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET) patients. However, the underlying subgroups of e-health literacy in IVF-ET patients and their influencing factors are unclear.MethodsFrom July 2023 to January 2024, 1200 participants were recruited from a women’s and children’s hospital, using a convenience sampling method. The participants completed the E-health Literacy Scale, the General Self-efficacy Scale and the General Demographic Information Questionnaire. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) with Mplus 8.0 identified potential e-health literacy categories in IVF-ET patients. Group differences were analyzed using chi-square tests or one-way ANOVA in SPSS 25.0. Multiple logistic regression (SPSS 25.0) explored factors associated with e-health literacy.ResultsA total of 1074 valid questionnaires were included. Three potential categories were revealed, namely “low e-health literacy group” (N = 455, 42.4%), “medium e-health literacy group” (N = 505, 47.0%), and “high e-health literacy group” (N = 114, 10.6%). Multivariate logistic regression showed that interest in electronic health information search (β = 1.882, P = 0.005, OR = 6.565, 95% CI: 1.741–24.748), attitude toward electronic health information (β = 3.088, P < 0.001, OR = 21.929, 95% CI: 4.599–104.555), lack of access to electronic health information (β = -3.376, P = 0.002, OR = 0.034, 95% CI: 0.004–0.298), and self-efficacy (β = 0.341, P < 0.001, OR = 1.406, 95% CI: 1.332–1.484) were significant predictors of higher e-health literacy in IVF-ET patients.ConclusionsThe e-health literacy level among IVF-ET patients requires further enhancement. This can be achieved by fostering patients’ interest and positive attitudes toward seeking e-health information and strengthening their self-efficacy. Such strategies not only elevate e-health literacy but also enhance patients’ capacity to utilize e-health services effectively, ultimately contributing to improved reproductive health outcomes.

Introduction

Statistical evidence [1] indicates that globally, approximately 48.5 million couples have encountered infertility. With the progress of assisted reproductive techniques, in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF - ET) has emerged as one of the primary approaches for treating infertility [2]. IVF - ET entails the process of fertilizing mature eggs and sperm ex vivo, resulting in the formation of a zygote. This zygote subsequently develops into an embryo, which is then implanted into the female uterus; it is colloquially referred to as “test - tube baby” [3, 4]. However, due to the long and complex treatment process, uncertain treatment outcomes, and high costs, IVF-ET patients still face several challenges, including a lack of reproductive health knowledge, poor psychological adaptation, and incorrect health behaviors [5, 6].

With the rapid development of modern technology, digital health services have gradually become an important form of support for IVF-ET patients throughout their treatment cycle, leading patients to rely heavily on digital services and technology to search for and understand professional knowledge and treatment methods for infertility, thereby maintaining and improving their health status [7].

E-health literacy refers to the multidimensional skill of users to access, apply, and evaluate health information through electronic devices, and use health information to guide health decisions. It includes the ability to use network health services, judge, and make decisions [8]. As an important indicator of patients’ digital service acquisition capabilities, e-health literacy has become a significant research hotspot in the field of health promotion [9, 10]. Research has revealed that e-health literacy, by empowering women and providing support, can help them cope with external risks and promote positive health behaviors [11].

In the field of reproductive health, over 40% of American women report using the Internet as a primary source of information and resources for infertility [12]. Hinton et al. [13] reported that the internet provides anonymity, emotional support, normalization, and comfort to infertile patients, facilitating the social process of sharing resources and support and changing the experience of infertility. Additionally, Sykes et al. [1] developed an e-health literacy framework for infertile patients, which combines the use of the internet and virtual communities, where functional, interactive, critical, and distributed health literacy play a role in the online environment, fully demonstrating the important value of the internet in providing information and support to infertile couples. Compared to other medical activities, the treatment of IVF-ET is more complex and time-consuming. Therefore, e-health literacy may affect the ability of IVF-ET patients to use internet platforms effectively to actively solve their health problems and become a key link in their reproductive health outcomes.

According to previous studies, sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and electronic resource-related factors [14, 15] can all affect an individual’s e-health literacy. Particularly, there is a close relationship between an individual’s self-efficacy and their e-health literacy and skills, a finding that has been verified in elderly patients [16], breast cancer patients [17], diabetes patients [18], college students [19], and other populations. Bandura’s social cognitive theory proposes that self-efficacy is the judgment people make about their ability to organize and execute the actions required to achieve a given behavioral goal, which can enhance the intrinsic motivation for learning and inspire lifelong learning [20]. People with high self-efficacy are more likely to seek health promotion opportunities by controlling their health. Further research has demonstrated that e-health literacy can be improved by developing self-efficacy, and higher self-efficacy can lead to continuous improvement in e-health literacy, creating a beneficial two-way cycle [21].

Self-efficacy models have become common conceptual models used in interventions to improve e-health literacy. In 2019, Nahm et al. [22] developed and tested a patient portal-based e-learning program (T-PeP) based on self-efficacy theory. They conducted a 4-month randomized controlled trial with 272 elderly patients with chronic diseases, and the results showed that the T-PeP was effective in improving their knowledge and use of patient portals, health decision-making behaviors, and communication with healthcare providers. Additionally, some studies have revealed that self-efficacy plays a mediating role between e-health literacy and health behavior habits or good self-management in patients with chronic diseases [21, 23]. However, the intrinsic relationship between self-efficacy and e-health literacy among Chinese IVF-ET patients remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the relationship between self-efficacy and e-health literacy in IVF-ET patients to improve their e-health literacy through their self-efficacy and thereby help them to correctly utilize e-health resources to analyze, judge, and make correct decisions, and ultimately improve their quality of life in fertility.

The Anderson Model, also known as the Behavioral Model of Health Services Use, divides the factors influencing the utilization of health services into three aspects: predisposing factors, enabling factors, and need-for-care factors [24]. Although the Anderson model was originally used to explain people’s use of health services, scholars have successfully applied it to the field of e-health [25, 26]. In the present study, we used the Anderson model as a theoretical framework to organize predictive variables of e-health literacy in IVF-ET patients, explore the impact of predisposing factors, enabling factors, and need-for-care factors on individual e-health literacy, and thereby summarize the findings in detail.

Furthermore, in previous research on e-health literacy, most studies have focused on the entire population [27, 28], to some extent neglecting information from the individual level. In this case, we are unable to accurately understand the e-health literacy of patients and identify corresponding characteristics, which hinders further research. In this study, LPA helped us identify personalized characteristics of e-health literacy in IVF-ET patients. This can provide effective support for researchers in developing targeted interventions for e-health literacy in IVF-ET patients, thereby playing a positive role in promoting reproductive health in the public health sector.

Based on the above discussion, this study aimed to identify potential subgroups of e-health literacy in IVF-ET patients under the guidance of the Anderson model and explore the differences in individual characteristics, disease characteristics, and self-efficacy among different subgroups of patients, thereby providing intervention targets for specifically improving the e-health literacy level of IVF-ET patients.

Guided by the study’s overarching aim, we sought to address the following questions:

Methods

Study design

This study is a single-center cross-sectional study adopting convenience sampling and participants’ self-report, and it adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE, Appendix S1) [29].

Study participants

This study was conducted in Zhejiang Province, China, from July 2023 to January 2024. All participants were recruited from a women’s and children’s hospital in Zhejiang Province. This hospital is one of the largest tertiary Class A maternity and gynecology hospitals in mainland China, with 1,520 beds and approximately 110,000 patients receiving diagnosis and treatment each year. This hospital bears important social responsibilities for safeguarding the health of women and children and has a significant influence in East China and even throughout China. The inclusion crteria for the study were as follows: participants who were more than 20 years of age (the legal marriage age in China is 22 years for males and 20 years for females), were married, were aware of their medical diagnosis (infertility, requiring IVF-ET treatment), possessed basic literacy in reading and communication skills (before the survey, the researcher will talk with each participant to assess their cognitive level of language and writing; e.g., participants could independently read and understand the written content of the questionnaire), and were capable of independently completing the electronic questionnaire. All participants provided informed consent for this study during the recruitment period. Patients with a history of severe physical or mental illness were excluded.

Prior to the formal investigation, a pre-survey involving 40 patients was conducted to optimize the questionnaire design. A total of 40 electronic questionnaires were distributed during this phase, with a 100% valid response rate. Based on feedback from participants, the questionnaire was systematically revised and adjusted. Notably, during the pre-survey, participants identified redundancy between the item “Do you believe networked electronic devices can help you make health decisions?” and the final item of the e-health literacy scale. Consequently, this redundant item was removed to enhance questionnaire clarity and avoid measurement overlap.

Measurement tools

Self-made questionnaires

The self-made questionnaires included demographic characteristics and e-health informations. The demographic characteristics of IVF-ET patients, including age, educational level, occupation, average monthly income per person in the family, duration of infertility, and method of IVF, were collected. Subsequently, we collected information related to e-health, including the frequency and interest in searching for e-health information, attitude of patients toward online health information, frequency of forwarding and sharing e-health information, willingness to use electronic devices to search for health information, and number of ways to obtain e-health information.

In the Anderson Model, predisposing factors refer to individual characteristics existing before illness, mainly demographic characteristics, social structure, health beliefs, and other variables. Enabling factors refer to an individual’s ability to obtain health services and the accessibility of health service resources, including personal resources, family resources, and community resources. Need-for-care factors refer to the perceived need and evaluated need for health services [24]. E-health literacy scale

This study used the E-health literacy scale to measure the level of e-health literacy in Chinese IVF-ET patients. The E-health scale was originally developed by Norman et al. [8] and was the first tool created to assess e-health literacy. Chinese scholars translated it into a Chinese version in 2013, after which e-health has been widely used by the Chinese population [30]. The scale includes three dimensions—the ability to apply network health information and services, ability to evaluate, and decision-making ability—and consists of eight items. The scale employs a 5-point Likert scale, with “strongly disagree” scoring 1 point and “strongly agree” scoring 5 points. The total score ranges from 8 to 40 points, with higher scores indicating a higher level of e-health literacy. The Chinese version of the e-health scale has a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.913, indicating good reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.950.

General self-efficacy scale

This study employed the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) to investigate the self-efficacy levels of Chinese IVF-ET patients. The GSES was developed by Professor Schwarzer et al. [31] In 2001, Chinese scholars completed the translation and revision of the GSES for the Chinese cultural context [32]. The Chinese version of the GSES consists of 10 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all correct” to “completely correct,” with scores ranging from 1 to 4 points. The total score ranges from 10 to 40 points, with higher scores indicating stronger self-efficacy. The Chinese version of the GSES has a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.87, a test-retest reliability of 0.83, and a split-half reliability of 0.90. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.949.

Data collection

The data were collected through an anonymous online survey platform (Wjx.cn; https://www.wjx.cn/) from July 26, 2023, to January 11, 2024. The purpose and significance of the study were explained to the patients, and they were sent a link to the questionnaire. Before the start of the study, the researcher provided each participant with an easy-to-understand electronic informed consent form. The informed consent form was written in plain language, elaborating on the research content in detail, and was explained face-to-face by the researcher before signing to ensure that the participants signed it voluntarily after fully understanding it.

In this study, only questionnaires that can truthfully, accurately, and completely reflect the actual situations and thoughts of the participants are regarded as valid questionnaires. We set the following rules to determine its validity, for example to avoid duplicate data collection, we set up the condition that each internet protocol address could only access the questionnaire once. At the same time, to improve the number of valid responses, we established the rule that the questionnaire could only be submitted successfully after all questions were answered and that unanswered questions were not allowed. Questionnaires with answering times less than 90 s or more than 25 min were excluded after double-checking. During the recruitment phase, we informed IVF-ET patients that if they participated in the survey, they could receive additional free online questions and guidance throughout their treatment, which significantly increased patient participation in the study. A total of 1200 eligible patients were approached for participation in the survey from July 26, 2023, to January 11, 2024. Statistical analysis

LPA was conducted using Mplus version 8.0 to identify distinct subgroups based on the measured variables; Logistic regression analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 to examine associations between demographic factors and subgroup membership. In this study, continuous variables adhered to a normal distribution and were characterized by the mean and standard deviation. LPA could categorizes groups into subgroups with different characteristics based on the pattern of responses to explicit variables, with individuals in each subgroup sharing similar characteristics, thereby identifying the heterogeneity among groups [33]. To identify the optimal number of profiles, potential profile models with a class range from 1 to 5 were compared. Model selection primarily relied on the following indicators: (1) The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample - corrected BIC (aBIC). Lower values of these criteria signify a better - fitting model. (2) An entropy value closer to 1 indicates more accurate model classification. (3) In the Lo - Mendell - Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR - LRT) and Bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), the null hypothesis posits that the k − 1 class model and the k class model exhibit the same level of fit. If the test yields a significant result (p < 0.05), the null hypothesis is rejected, suggesting that the k class model fits better than the k − 1 class model.

Next, chi-square tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for group comparisons to analyze whether there were differences in demographic data, e-health information-related issues, or self-efficacy between different categories of e-health literacy. Finally, multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted on IVF-ET patients with different categories of e-health literacy to explore factors influencing profile classification. The level of significance α was set at 0.05, with a two-tailed P < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. IRB-20230216-R), and all participants provided written informed consent before completing the survey.

Discussion

This study investigated the e-health literacy of 1074 IVF-ET patients in China using the Anderson model by dividing them into three subgroups and exploring the factors influencing different e-health literacy profiles. This is the first survey in mainland China to examine the distribution of e-health literacy subgroups in IVF-ET patients. This study provides valuable insights for targeted improvements in e-health literacy among IVF-ET patients.

The e-health literacy score of 1074 IVF-ET patients was (27.91 ± 6.03), which is relatively low, similar to the (27.27 ± 6.28) score reported by Brørs et al. in 2924 percutaneous coronary intervention patients [35] but lower than the (31.60 ± 5.40) score reported in patients with spinal cord injury (SCI) [36] and (30.34 ± 5.30) reported in patients with chronic diseases [37]. Moreover, the low e-health literacy group accounted for 42.4% of the total, which suggests that the e-health literacy level of IVF-ET patients needs further improvement. Cultural background, e-health literacy rates, and healthcare environments vary between countries and may be important factors influencing the e-health literacy of residents. Among the dimensions of e-health literacy, the dimension with the highest score was the ability to use online health services, while the dimension with the lowest score was decision-making capacity. This indicates that most IVF-ET patients have a certain ability to search for e-health information but need to strengthen their decision-making ability regarding such information. In mainland China, despite the development of various e-health devices or applications, most IVF-ET patients still prefer offline medical services. This may be linked to the immaturity of e-health services in healthcare institutions and the public’s insufficient ability to use e-health applications [38, 39].

The results of the multiple logistic regression analysis in the current study show that under the enabling factor dimension of the Anderson model, IVF-ET patients who are “interested in searching for e-health information” are more likely to be classified as having “high e-health literacy”. A study in Hong Kong, China, targeting primary care patients, also pointed out that patients with more active online health-seeking behaviors have greater e-health literacy [40]. Melholt et al. [38] created a professional website for related diseases that effectively encouraged patients with various diseases to actively search for health information online. To mobilize the interest of IVF-ET patients in searching for e-health information and ultimately improve their reproductive health literacy, support can be provided by medical institutions or developers, including offering trial products to help them experience the convenience and advantages of e-health devices and stimulating their motivation to search for e-health information. Second, “having no or few sources of accessing e-health information” is likely to be classified as “low e-health literacy”. In the present study, patients in this category accounted for as much as 47.77%, which, to some extent, indicates that the channels for accessing electronic health information and the resources of electronic health search platforms for IVF-ET patients in mainland China are relatively scarce. Many countries have launched internet-based fertility health services for infertile couples, with the common goal of providing information, support, and promotion of mental health. This suggests that mainland China can draw on these experiences, considering the local cultural background, to improve the application capabilities of IVF-ET patients for e-health services [41]. However, because of the economic development gap and unequal distribution of medical resources in China, these services remain unable to be fully implemented in remote or underdeveloped areas. In the future, improving access to mobile health and adjusting mobile health equipment to meet the needs of vulnerable groups will help reduce the gap and achieve health equity.

Our study also revealed that under the need-for-care factor dimension in Anderson’s model, IVF-ET patients who “hold a believing attitude toward e-health information” are more likely to be classified as having “high e-health literacy”. A national cross-sectional survey by Serrano et al. [42] also showed that the degree of trust in professional information is closely related to the willingness to participate in mobile health information exchange. Patients are less willing to exchange information that may be considered sensitive or complex through mobile devices, which is particularly true for IVF-ET patients. Although e-health services worldwide have developed rapidly during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, many false information disseminations have undermined the public’s trust in e-health information [43]. Consequently, relevant national departments need to further strengthen the supervision and regulation of the release and interaction process of health-related content on online platforms to promote the authority, professionalism, and credibility of e-health information platforms. Additionally, the results of our study indicate that IVF-ET patients with higher levels of self-efficacy are more likely to have “high e-health literacy”, consistent with many previous studies [17, 44, 45]. Self-efficacy is an important factor in determining patients’ e-health needs. Individuals with high self-efficacy have a more active attitude in seeking e-health resources and engage in two-way or multidirectional interactions and communication with healthcare providers, utilizing and sharing electronic information to achieve health goals [46, 47]. However, the self-efficacy score of the 1074 IVF-ET patients in our study was (25.11 ± 6.64), which was lower than that of the Swedish adult women trying to lose weight (31.2 ± 4.8), [48] indicating that the self-efficacy of IVF-ET patients must be strengthened. Bandura proposed four methods to improve self-efficacy: (1) master experience, (2) social modeling, (3) improving physical and emotional states, and (4) verbal supervision [49]. In previous studies, the self-efficacy conceptual framework has been widely applied in the intervention of public e-health literacy. Once participants’ self-efficacy in using e-health is enhanced, they are more likely to report positive outcomes in managing health-related issues [50]. Consequently, interventions to improve self-efficacy in IVF-ET patients may become a key measure to enhance their e-health literacy.

In contrast to previous studies, this study did not find some factors affecting the e-health literacy of IVF-ET patients, such as age, education level, occupation and IVF method. This may be associated with sample selection, as most participants were from the economically developed eastern regions of China, and the patients were mostly young and middle-aged women with a good education, possibly resulting in no significant differences in electronic device usage. In addition, most of the recruited participants had undergone IVF or ICSI techniques (accounting for 90.69% of the total), resulting in minimal differences in IVF-ET methods. However, the intrinsic impact of the demographic data of IVF-ET patients on e-health literacy must be further verified in the future.

Recently, the People’s Republic of China issued the “Action Plan for Promoting Reproductive Health (2023–2025)”, which pointed out that taking various approaches, especially e-health services, is crucial for improving the level of reproductive health services [51]. Additionally, the increasingly severe aging of the Chinese population has also increased demands on the national level of reproductive health [52]. In the coming period, researchers can plan and implement actions to improve the e-health literacy of IVF-ET patients. By developing specialized e-health information websites and actively establishing intervention projects that can be widely promoted, these patients can have the opportunity to participate in online interventions for e-health literacy and receive informational, systematic care, which is essential for promoting reproductive health equity.

Limitation of the study

This study has some limitations. First, we only conducted a convenience sample of IVF-ET patients in a province in eastern China, which restricts the scope of the applied population, which inherently introduces selection bias. Simple random sampling or stratified sampling in different regional environments could improve the generalizability of the research. Second, the single-center nature of this study represents another significant limitation. Conducting research at a single institution may introduce setting-specific biases related to patient demographics, healthcare practices, or local environmental factors. For example, patients at our center may have had more severe disease presentations or access to specialized resources compared to those in other regions. These differences could affect the generalizability of our findings to diverse patient populations. Multicenter studies involving multiple geographical locations and patient cohorts are recommended to reduce these biases and improve the external validity of the results. Moreover, our study relied on questionnaires to collect data, which inherently carries the risk of response bias. Participants may have inadvertently or deliberately provided inaccurate information due to social desirability or recall limitations. Combining self-report data with objective measures such as medical records in future research could enhance data accuracy and reliability. Finally, this study employed e-health literacy scale [35] as the tool for measuring e-health literacy. While this scale remains in use across numerous studies, the burgeoning development of electronic health information may necessitate its update in the near future.

Conclusions

This study, using the Anderson model as a theoretical framework, identified three potential profiles of e-health literacy among IVF-ET patients: the “low e-health literacy group”, “medium e-health literacy group”, and “high e-health literacy group”, thereby enhancing understanding of predictive variables related to e-health literacy in these patients. It was found that IVF-ET patients exhibited low e-health literacy and self-efficacy, along with individual heterogeneity. These findings are of great significance to healthcare personnel, enabling them to identify specific subgroups with poorer e-health literacy. They thus open new avenues for developing targeted interventions to enhance the e-health literacy of such subgroups. Moreover, this study advances the Anderson model in IVF-ET treatment, highlights the role of self-efficacy in e-health literacy, and offers valuable clinical practice experience and a reference for interventions to improve the e-health level of IVF-ET patients and achieve reproductive health.

Implications for practice and futer research

For clinical practice, the identification of “low,” “medium,” and “high” e-health literacy profiles among IVF-ET patients enables healthcare providers to deliver stratified interventions: developing simplified visual materials (e.g., illustrated guides or videos) for the low-literacy subgroup to improve understanding of digital fertility resources, offering advanced digital tools (e.g., treatment-tracking apps) to the medium/high-literacy subgroup to enhance self-management, and prioritizing literacy-sensitive communication strategies like “teach-back” methods to reduce information gaps. Additionally, integrating self-efficacy-building interventions (e.g., peer support groups) into clinical care is critical, as our findings link low self-efficacy to poorer e-health literacy, empowering patients to engage with digital health tools confidently.

For research, this study expands the Anderson model by validating its applicability in reproductive health and underscoring the importance of individual-level factors (e.g., self-efficacy) in e-health literacy research. Future studies could explore how social determinants (e.g., socioeconomic status, technology access) interact with these profiles. Prospective research is also needed to investigate whether targeted literacy interventions correlate with clinical outcomes (e.g., treatment adherence, pregnancy rates), while cross-cultural replications would assess the generalizability of these profiles and inform global reproductive health strategies. These insights strengthen both clinical practice and theoretical frameworks in e-health and reproductive care.
