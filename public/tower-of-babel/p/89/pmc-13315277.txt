Frequency and Impact of Thyroid Hormone Abnormalities in COVID-19: An Observational Study at the Peak of the Pandemic
Authors: Paweł Komarnicki, Alicja Rajewska, Jolanta Tomczak, Elżbieta Wrotkowska, Zbigniew Krasiński, Marek Ruchała
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:COVID-19, caused by SARS-CoV-2, affects multiple organ systems, including the thyroid gland. Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome (NTIS) is frequently observed in severe systemic disease. NTIS-like thyroid hormone alterations have previously been reported in COVID-19. However, the relationship between these changes and disease outcomes remains unclear.Objectives:We aimed to evaluate thyroid function and vitamin D concentrations in hospitalized COVID-19 patients, and to analyze the association between hormonal measurements and patient characteristics with COVID-19 mortality.Methods:We conducted a retrospective database search of 846 adult patients hospitalized in a dedicated COVID-19 hospital at the peak of the pandemic. Based on the availability of serum levels of TSH, FT3, FT4, and vitamin D, 137 patients were included in the analysis. Descriptive statistics, comparative analyses, and logistic regression were used to evaluate the associations of laboratory measurements, sex, and age with COVID-19 mortality.Results:Decreased thyroid hormone and vitamin D concentrations were more common than in the general population, although none differed between deceased and COVID-19 survivors. Among the cohort, 63.5% had decreased TSH, 21.2% had decreased FT3, 24.1% had decreased FT4, and 75.9% presented with vitamin D deficiency. Comparative analyses showed no significant differences in TSH (P = .82), FT3 (P = .40), FT4 (P = .81), or vitamin D (P = .78) levels between deceased and surviving patients. Deceased patients were older than survivors (median [IQR]: 75.5 [71.0-80.8] vs 71.0 [61.0-77.0] years; P = .002), with age emerging as the only predictor of mortality (OR = 1.07, 95%CI: 1.02-1.11; P = .001; all other variables P > .33).Conclusions:NTIS-like thyroid hormone alterations and vitamin D deficiency are highly prevalent among hospitalized COVID-19 patients but do not predict in-hospital mortality. Age remains the major risk factor for death in this population. Our findings confirm that COVID-19 frequently affects the endocrine system and highlight the need for further research on long-term thyroid outcomes.

Introduction

In 2019, a new single-stranded RNA coronavirus emerged in Wuhan, China. It was called Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and the disease was assigned a name Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19). COVID-19 rapidly spread globally, prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare a global pandemic on 11th March 2020.1,2 By November 2021, over 20 vaccines were approved around the world, including adenoviral vector vaccines, mRNA vaccines, subunit vaccines, inactivated SARS-CoV-2, and DNA-based platforms. Widespread vaccination programs had a significant impact on the reduction of COVID-19 incidence and severity. 3 According to official WHO data, approximately 779 million confirmed COVID-19 cases have been reported since the beginning of the pandemic. The burden of COVID-19 on healthcare systems is currently not as severe as during the peak of the pandemic. As of February 2026, more than 50 000 monthly COVID-19 cases are reported worldwide. However, this number must be interpreted in the context of the reduced virologic surveillance and the withdrawal of routine reporting compared to the pandemic peak. Currently, the majority of the cases are associated with hospital admissions, with the current rate of death per 1000 hospitalizations remaining around 10%. 4 However, its consequences remain a concern. Up to 69% of individuals who recover from SARS-CoV-2 infection experience persistent symptoms. Termed long COVID, they may include fatigue, cognitive dysfunction and multi-organ complications.5,6

Although primarily a respiratory illness, COVID-19 affects various organs and systems, including the thyroid gland, kidneys, liver, central nervous system, cardiovascular, and immune systems. 7 Patients with long COVID exhibit heightened antibody responses against non-SARS-CoV-2 pathogens, dysregulated immune mediator and hormone levels, particularly cortisol. The underlying mechanisms behind endocrine dysfunction in COVID-19 remain poorly understood. 7 One theory suggests that the endocrine system can be damaged directly by SARS-CoV-2 via Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors. ACE2 acts as a transmembrane protein (eg, in the respiratory system), allowing SARS-CoV-2 to invade host cells. These receptors are also found in the endocrine system, including thyroid gland, hypothalamus, and pituitary. 8 However, the results are conflicting on whether SARS-CoV-2 can invade follicular cells or if the infection of pericytes is the driver behind COVID-19-related thyroid dysfunction. Park et al suggest that ACE2 protein is expressed in the pericytes and not in the follicular cells, implying that the thyroid dysfunction is mediated indirectly. 9

Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome (NTIS), alternatively Low T3 syndrome or Euthyroid Sick Syndrome (ESS), is a phenomenon characterized by abnormal concentrations of thyroid-related hormones secondary to a non-thyroidal, systemic illness. NTIS is considered a general endocrine response, prevalent across different age groups and severe disease contexts. 10 The defining laboratory findings include low serum concentrations of triiodothyronine (T3), with normal or decreased concentrations of serum thyroxine (T4) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). 11 NTIS can be a result of chronic conditions such as liver insufficiency, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus and inflammatory diseases.12,13 In acute states such as infection or trauma, NTIS serves as an adaptive mechanism in order to lower cellular metabolic activity and support systemic disease response. 14 An inhibited 5’-deiodinase activity reduces peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 and results in decreased total T3 concentrations. A subsequent decline in T4 levels results from a decreased binding affinity and levels of thyroxine-binding globulin. Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α cause the malfunction of hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, inhibiting the secretion of thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus. 15 In 1 study, over 42% of septic patients presented with NTIS. 16 In COVID-19, NTIS has been identified as the most frequent thyroid disorder, found in up to 64% hospitalized COVID-19 patients. 17 However, throughout the pandemic, the results of NTIS research have been inconclusive across different research centers and in various stages of the pandemic. 18 In recent years, numerous studies have examined the relationship between thyroid function and COVID-19. However, the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and thyroid function changes is not yet fully understood. 19 An epidemiological study of more than 5 million patients by Qeadan et al found that antecedent COVID-19 increases the risk of both hypo- and hyperthyroidism. 20 In a recent review, Anbardar et al consider thyroid function assessment a significant aspect of COVID-19 management. 21 Vitamin D deficiency has been linked with poor COVID-19 outcomes since beginning of the pandemic. 22 It is not only integral to physiological immune regulation, decreased vitamin D concentrations have been linked with increased risk of developing Autoimmune Thyroid Diseases.23 -25 The prevalence of NTIS in COVID-19 reported in previous studies varies, among others due to methodological discrepancies, the emergence of viral variants, and differences in vaccination status over time.

The main aim of this study was to evaluate changes in thyroid function in a uniform cohort of patients with COVID-19. The secondary end points were to analyze the relationship between alterations of thyroid-related hormones and vitamin D concentrations, and the severity of COVID-19.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

We performed a retrospective analysis of patients hospitalized due to COVID-19 between January and April 2021 at the Regional Hospital in Słupca, Wielkopolskie, Poland. In this period, during the peak of the pandemic, the hospital was dedicated to treating COVID-19 patients.

Patient Selection

Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and laboratory results (serum levels of TSH, FT4, FT3, and vitamin D) were collected from hospital records. A total of 846 patients diagnosed with COVID-19, confirmed by PCR testing were initially considered for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included prior history of thyroid disease, unavailability of thyroid hormones laboratory measurements, pregnancy, incomplete patient data, and patients under 18 years of age. Furthermore, subjects transferred to external facilities after admission were also excluded from the analysis. After the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria and based on the availability of aforementioned laboratory measurements, 137 patients were included in the analysis. The primary outcome of interest was COVID-19 mortality, defined as death during the hospital stay. The detailed patient inclusion flow-chart is presented in Figure 1.

Patient selection flowchart.

Laboratory Measurements

On admission, venous blood samples were collected from patients to measure serum concentrations of TSH, FT4, FT3, and vitamin D. Acquired samples were processed according to standard operating procedures and stored at −80°C until analysis. All measurements were performed using ELISA kits from DRG Diagnostics GmbH (Marburg, Germany). The results were categorized as low, normal, and elevated according to the assay manufacturer’s reference ranges: DRG TSH ELISA (0.5-5.0 mIU/ml); DRG Free T4 ELISA (male: 0.68-1.88 ng/dl; female: 0.66-1.79 ng/dl); DRG Free T3 ELISA (male: 2.21 4.48 pg/ml; female: 1.99-5.58 pg/ml); DRG Vitamin D (total) ELISA (30-100 ng/ml). Vitamin D deficiency was defined as serum concentrations below 20 ng/ml, per Polish Guidelines. 15 If multiple laboratory measurements were performed, only the initial sample (acquired on admission) was considered.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the demographics and clinical characteristics of the study population. We performed comparative statistical tests and used univariate and multivariate logistic regression to evaluate the relationship between study variables and COVID-19 mortality. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and P-values were calculated for each variable. For group comparison we used the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess the normality of continuous variables. A P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis, data processing, modeling, and visualization were performed in Python 3.12. using the following packages: pandas 2.2.2, stats models 0.14.2, scikit-learn 1.4.2, numpy 2.0.0, and matplotlib 3.8.4. We conducted a post-hoc sample size calculation for age – a continuous variable with significantly different results between groups in our study. Assuming an alpha of .05 and a power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 98 patients. Our study recruited 137 patients, exceeding this requirement.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the analysis of archived and anonymized samples and the retrospective character of the research, Bioethical Committee agreement was deemed not necessary, according to bioethical regulations in Poland. A written confirmation statement signed by the head of the regional bioethical committee was obtained.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The final analysis included 137 patients. Mean age of study participants was 70.7 ± 12.1 years. Subjects were predominantly female (53.3%). There were 42 deaths during hospitalization in the group, resulting in the overall mortality rate of 30.7%. Detailed patient characteristics are displayed in Table 1. However, we observed no differences in TSH, FT3, FT4, and vitamin D levels between survivors and deceased subjects. Discussion

Our study reveals that the majority (63.5%) of patients hospitalized due to COVID-19 exhibited low TSH concentrations. Decreased FT3 (21.2%) and FT4 (24.1%) levels were the most common thyroid hormone alterations. However, despite these changes and widespread vitamin D deficiency, only age was the risk factor for COVID-19 mortality.

A 2021 update on NTIS by Fliers and Boelen, suggests that during severe illness, tissue thyroid hormone concentrations do not always mirror the decreased serum levels. Instead, tissue TH levels may vary depending on the specific organ and the nature of the illness, showing a decrease, remaining unchanged, or even increasing in certain contexts. 26 NTIS is driven in part by cytokines. Panesar et al suggest that the cytokine storm occurring in COVID-19 can be a shared pathomechanism with NTIS, resulting in indirect tissue damage caused by an uncontrolled systemic inflammatory response. 19 The main cytokines involved in the COVID-19 inflammatory response include IL-6 and IL-1b, both of which have been shown to influence the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis. Specifically, they decrease the sensitivity of TSH secretion to low circulating fT3 levels, which results in inappropriately low or normal serum TSH concentrations. 19 As suggested by Warner and Beckett, much of NTIS regulation is primarily linked to central hypothyroidism caused by reduced secretion of hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone, potentially triggered by low leptin from malnutrition and altered deiodinase activity. The early reduction in serum T3 and T4 levels observed during the acute phase of illness stems from diminished thyroid hormone-binding capacity in the plasma, rather than alterations in deiodinase enzyme activity. 27 Free thyroid hormone levels exhibit only a modest decline; however, impaired hormone uptake into peripheral tissues may occur due to altered transporter function, influenced by reduced intracellular ATP levels or the presence of plasma-derived inhibitors. Receptor expression demonstrates a differential pattern, increasing in chronic illnesses but decreasing during acute conditions such as sepsis or trauma. 27 NTIS pathomechanism cannot be simplified to a single cause decrease in FT3, and is rather a coordinated response involving central and peripheral hormonal regulations. Although direct COVID-19 thyroid infection is unlikely, the immune-mediated and tissue damage mechanisms can combine, with pericyte damage caused by SARS-CoV-2 invasion via ACE2 receptors. 9

Vitamin D deficiency was present in 75.9% of the cohort (defined by Polish Guidelines as below 20 ng/ml28,29). The high prevalence of thyroid dysfunction and vitamin D insufficiency in hospitalized COVID-19 patients is consistent with previous studies, although the impact of these alterations differs from previous reports. 30 In our cohort, no laboratory measurement was able to distinguish deceased patients from COVID-19 survivors and age was the only variable that was significantly higher in those who died from COVID-19.

Thyroid Hormones Alterations in SARS-CoV-2 Infection

Despite high incidence of thyroid dysfunction in our cohort, we found no association between levels of thyroid hormones and TSH, and COVID-19 outcomes. This contrasts with earlier studies suggesting that thyroid hormones may predict disease course in critically ill patients. Llamas et al meta-analysis reported lower FT3 levels in severe COVID-19. 31 In cross-sectional study conducted by Berkebile et al. researchers analyzed that patients who underwent thyroid function tests during the first wave of COVID-19 in 2020 had significantly lower TSH and FT3 levels and higher FT4 levels compared to patients tested in 2018 and 2019. 32 A 2024 study linked lower TSH, FT3, and FT4 levels to higher mortality (44.4%) in COVID-19 ICU patients, compared to 27.3% in a general ICU. 33 Other researchers linked presence of hypothyroidism or autoimmune thyroid diseases with higher COVID-19 mortality. 34 The discrepancy in our study could be assigned to a homogeneous study population in terms of disease severity. Due to healthcare system overload during the pandemic peak, only the most critical patients were hospitalized (eg, those requiring oxygen therapy), resulting in similar characteristics between survivors and non-survivors. Even then, nearly a fourth of the study participants exhibited decreased thyroid hormone levels. This contrasts sharply with the general population. For instance, 2 studies of elderly Polish citizens (4000 and 6000 patients examined) reported 8%-13.9% incidence of hypothyroidism, 1.6% to 3% of hyperthyroidism, and the prevalence of undiagnosed thyroid disease at just 3%.35,36 In our study, participants were expected to have normal thyroid function, as individuals with known thyroid disease were excluded. However, this was not the case, suggesting that these alterations are more likely to be a consequence of COVID-19 rather than pre-existing hypothyroidism. This interpretation is reinforced by the predominantly decreased TSH, more characteristic for NTIS rather than primary hypothyroidism.

Vitamin D and COVID-19

Similar to TSH and free-thyroid hormones, vitamin D deficiency was highly prevalent in our cohort (75.9%), yet its serum levels did not correlate with COVID-19 mortality. These findings are intriguing given vitamin D’s well-documented role in inflammation response and immune regulation, including a reduction in the risk and severity of infections - potentially by activation of human antimicrobial peptides such as cathelicidin.26,37 A correlation between vitamin D deficiency and increased risk and severity of SARS-CoV-2 infection has been a widely-covered topic since the early days of the pandemic. 22 In 2020, Carpagano et al reported that 81% prevalence of hypovitaminosis D in ICU-treated COVID-19 patients with acute respiratory failure. Within this cohort, severe deficiency was linked with higher mortality. 30 Other studies linked higher vitamin D concentrations with a diminished risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection. 38 A 22-study meta-analysis linked vitamin D deficiency with the increased risk of hospitalization and death from COVID-19, but not with an infection itself. 39 Furthermore, Bikle et al (2022) demonstrated that the correction of vitamin D deficiency reduced the risk of hospitalization, ICU admission, and death from SARS-CoV-2 infection. 40 In 2025 Monroy-Iglesias et al reported that low vitamin D levels were associated with an increased risk of hospitalization due to SARS-CoV-2 infection, but were only weakly linked to the likelihood of contracting the infection. 41 A near universal vitamin D deficiency in our cohort (75.9% deficient, 16.1% suboptimal levels) may limit the study’s statistical power to detect the same associations found in previous research. The prevalence of vitamin D deficiency is higher than in general Polish population (65%, according to 1 study by Płudowski et al 29 ). However, this should be cautiously interpreted, considering a single center design and the seasonal changes in vitamin D concentration. Our study was conducted in late winter and early spring in Poland, when vitamin D concentrations are typically lower due to limited sunlight exposure. There is growing evidence which suggests that vitamin D deficiency is associated with the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. A 2025 study by Klenczar-Kciuk et al analyzed vitamin D deficiency in the group of Polish children with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes mellitus. 35.5% of children presented 25(OH)D deficiency, 37% had suboptimal levels (20-30 ng/ml). Low vitamin D is linked to autoimmune thyroid diseases like Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT). Since vitamin D regulates Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells, its deficiency may disrupt immune balance and contribute to thyroid autoimmunity. Gierach and Junik study showed significantly lower vitamin D levels in hypothyroid patients, especially those with HT, compared to healthy controls. 42

Impact of Patient Demographics on COVID-19 Severity

Statistical analysis identified age as the sole predictor of mortality in hospitalized COVID-19 patients, with each year increasing the odds of death by 7% (OR = 1.07; 95%CI: 1.02-1.11; P = .001). The median age of deceased patients was 75.5 years, higher than 71.0 years in the survivors group (P = .002). Since the first reports about the pandemic emerged, age was highlighted as the risk factor for death due to COVID-1943,44 and is still considered one of the strongest risk factor of disease severity by CDC. 45 Moreover, patients 65 years or older account for nearly 90% of COVID-19 deaths, as per continuously updated WHO reports highlighting the vulnerability of elderly individuals to COVID-19. 4 The process of aging has been demonstrated to be associated with a deterioration in immune function, an increased prevalence of comorbidities, and a persistent state of subtle inflammatory response. 46 Contrary to some reports, we have found no difference in sex between deceased and survivors (chi-squared = 0.62; P = .43) and no significant association between sex and disease mortality (OR = 0.69; 95%CI: 0.33-1.45; P = .33). In prior publications, not only sex, but also a traditional male lifestyle was suggested as a predisposing factor for infections, with smoking playing a pivotal role among the contributing factors. 47 Low serum testosterone levels are suggested to be a contributing factor to the severity of coronavirus disease in aging men. Papadopoulos et al suggest that testis, and most likely the Leydig cells, could also be a putative target organ for the virus. 48

Limitations and Future Perspectives

While this study provides valuable insight into endocrine dysfunction in COVID-19, it is not without its limitations. This study was conducted during the peak of the pandemic, when healthcare systems were overwhelmed and prioritized critically ill patients, thus, patients with mild to moderate disease were more likely to be treated at home instead of being hospitalized. This could have homogenized disease severity in our cohort and potentially blur the lines between deceased and survivors. Furthermore, a single center design could limit the statistical power to detect these associations between laboratory parameters and mortality. Our study is limited to severely ill hospitalized patients at a specific timepoint (and likely a predominant COVID-19 variant at a time) and another analysis comparing various causes of NTIS or could prove practical. Nevertheless, a single-center, single-period study of single-trigger NTIS is the strength of this analysis. Future research should focus on larger, multicenter studies with a more comprehensive evaluation of endocrine markers and their dynamic changes during the course of the disease. The evaluation of the relationship between thyroid hormones or vitamin D and other laboratory variables (eg, blood morphology parameters) or different indicators of disease severity (eg, hospital duration stay) should also be considered.

Conclusions

COVID-19 remains a burden on healthcare systems worldwide, not only limited to a respiratory illness but impacting various organ systems. Our study identified frequent vitamin D deficiency and thyroid dysfunction – consistent with NTIS – in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. NTIS studies are difficult to execute due to the variety of triggering factors and the irregular, unpredictable patient recruitment. Serum concentrations of these hormones did not differ between deceased and survivors and could not predict patient survival in our cohort. Instead, age emerged as the only significant predictor of disease mortality, reinforcing the vulnerability of the elderly to COVID-19. Although the impact of COVID-19 is not as severe as during the peak of the pandemic, its long-term complications remain an issue. Constantly evolving COVID-19 variants differ in presentation and clinical course. A dominant variant at the time of the study might affect the endocrine system differently than its successor. Currently, the majority of infections are attributed Omicron variants of SARS-CoV-2. The XFG (Stratus) variant (55.36%) is predominant in Europe and the Americas, whereas the NB.1.8.1 (Nimbus) variant (19.31%) is more frequent in the Western Pacific region. 49 Future studies should focus on evaluating whether the changes incurred by COVID-19 during acute infection impact thyroid function long term as well as if and how long COVID affects the endocrine system. When treating COVID-19 patients, thyroid hormone alterations and NTIS should be considered.
