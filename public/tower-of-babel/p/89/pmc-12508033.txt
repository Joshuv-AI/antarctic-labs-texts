Displacement response of ancient marine sedimentary soils to boring construction of triaxial cement mixing piles adjacent to metro
Authors: Daoming Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In foundation pit projects adjacent to Wenzhou Metro Line S1, controlling the disturbance of ancient marine sedimentary soils caused by the boring construction of triaxial cement mixing piles is crucial to safeguarding the operational safety of rail transit. This study systematically investigates the influencing mechanisms of boring parameters on surrounding soil displacements via a combination of field monitoring, numerical simulation, and parametric optimization. Based on the typical geological conditions of the coastal alluvial-lacustrine plain in Longwan District, Wenzhou—characterized by a narrow separation between the foundation pit and the metro station, and by highly sensitive marine sedimentary soft soils—this research reveals the disturbance characteristics of boring construction through monitoring the horizontal displacements of retaining structures and surface settlement. The results show that shallow soil displacements are significantly affected by time-dependent effects, deep-soil displacements remain relatively stable, and surface settlement increases as the distance from the foundation pit decreases. A finite element model integrating the Mohr-Coulomb model and Biot’s consolidation theory was developed in COMSOL, where simulation results exhibited high degrees of consistency with field data (R2 = 0.92 and 0.96, respectively), thus verifying the model’s reliability. Orthogonal simulation experiments were conducted to analyze the coupled effects of boring depth and radius, indicating that increasing the boring radius significantly increases deep-soil horizontal displacements but reduces near-zone surface settlements; increasing the boring depth exerts a notable influence on upper-soil horizontal displacements, whereas bottom-soil displacements undergo only minor changes. To achieve differentiated protection objectives, a “Construction Drilling Safety Evaluation Coefficient” is proposed, which comprehensively accounts for the deformation control requirements of above-ground buildings, underground structures, and near- and far-zone environments. The optimal construction scheme is identified as one adopting a smaller boring depth and a moderate radius. This research provides theoretical support and engineering guidance for deformation control during the construction of triaxial cement mixing piles in marine sedimentary soils adjacent to metro lines within coastal cities.

Introduction

With the expansion and extension of urban rail transit networks1,2foundation pit projects adjacent to existing rail lines have emerged as typical scenarios in underground space development in coastal cities3,4. The core challenge of such projects is that while triaxial cement mixing piles adjacent to metro lines can form protective structures after construction5boring construction inevitably disturbs surrounding strata6. Ancient marine sedimentary soils, widely distributed in coastal areas7–9have undergone long-term marine dynamic processes10,11exhibiting high sensitivity12,13 and significant anisotropic permeability14,15—key characteristics of coastal soft soils—thus responding more intensely to construction disturbances. Once soil displacements induced by boring exceed the threshold, they may cause uneven settlement of metro structures or track gauge deviations6thereby directly threatening operational safety16,17. Therefore, revealing the disturbance mechanism of boring construction adjacent to metro lines on ancient marine sedimentary soils is a key scientific issue for ensuring rail transit safety.

There is a paucity of simplified calculation studies addressing the unique properties of ancient marine sedimentary soils. International research has a solid theoretical foundation for soft soil deformation, with Terzaghi’s consolidation theory and Biot’s coupled theory widely employed in soil displacement calculations18–21. However, the majority of these studies focus on unsaturated or layered soft soils under complex conditions. For instance, Chao et al.20 modified the Biot’s consolidation theory framework to account for gravitational effects, quantifying the impacts of gravity on pore water pressure dissipation and soil settlement, and comparing gravity effects in saturated and unsaturated soils; Jiang et al.21 incorporated Terzaghi’s consolidation theory to analyze displacement data acquired via InSAR, quantifying soil settlement attenuation characteristics. While some scholars have focused on ancient marine sedimentary soils, their work primarily examines long-term deformation under self-weight consolidation or external loading22–24. For example, Ma et al.23 utilized a nonlinear elastic constitutive model based on a double-logarithmic formulation to enhance the prediction accuracy of long-term consolidation settlement for layered soft soil foundations under multi-stage loading; Cui et al.24 developed a one-dimensional nonlinear rheological consolidation model that retains the influence of creep effects during the primary consolidation stage of soft clay on long-term settlement calculations. However, studies investigating short-term disturbances caused by boring construction remain scarce. Furthermore, most existing studies focus solely on monitoring triaxial cement mixing pile projects25; few integrate experimental and numerical simulation approaches26and simplified calculation models are rarely implemented.

There are significant research gaps regarding the dynamic disturbance mechanisms of construction activities adjacent to metro lines. Internationally, research on construction-induced disturbances near underground projects primarily focuses on passive deformation during excavation27,28with limited attention paid to dynamic disturbances from active boring operations and inadequate analysis of grout injection and mixing zones29. More critically, most existing studies treat pile-related parameters (e.g., depth, radius) as independent variables30,31. For example, Cao et al.30 employed finite element methods to simulate the ratio of clustered piles to the total area, providing guidance for deep cement mixing (DCM) cluster design and assessing deformation risks; Phutthananon et al.31 optimized pile cap diameter and matched pile strength by fixing pile length, thereby enhancing pile bearing capacity and improving failure behavior. However, all these studies overlook the coupled effects of these parameters, and there remains a lack of systematic understanding regarding how these two parameters synergistically influence the displacement field of ancient marine sedimentary soils.

There is a lack of quantitative methods for implementing differentiated protection strategies. Scholars worldwide have recognized the sensitivity of metro structures to deformation; however, existing evaluation indicators (e.g., settlement rate, horizontal displacement gradient) are primarily applicable to general buildings32–34with few accounting for the unique characteristics of different functional zones within engineering protection areas35. For example, Zhu et al.36 proposed differentiated protection measures for above-ground and underground structures when shield tunneling machines need to underpass or pass alongside existing buildings; however, this consideration has not been integrated into existing quantitative models. Furthermore, for ancient marine sedimentary soils, research on delineating construction influence zones (e.g., Liu et al.37 delineated the area outside the foundation pit into four influence zones based on tunnel displacement control criteria) and establishing differentiated boring parameter control standards requires further advancement.

This study is based on a foundation pit project adjacent to Wenzhou Metro Line S1. This site features typical ancient marine sedimentary soils and retaining structures constructed in close proximity to metro infrastructure, making it an ideal research platform. The research aims to address three core objectives: (1) Elucidate the deformation mechanism of ancient marine sedimentary soils during boring construction adjacent to metro lines—a topic rarely addressed in existing literature, which primarily focuses on long-term consolidation rather than short-term construction-induced disturbances; (2) Analyze the coupled effects of boring depth and radius on horizontal displacements of soils at different depths and surface settlements, overcoming the limitation of treating these parameters independently in prior research; (3) Develop a “Construction Drilling Safety Evaluation Coefficient” for the differentiated protection of above-ground buildings, underground structures, and near- and far-zone environments—a quantitative tool currently lacking in metro protection frameworks.

The theoretical value of this study lies in addressing the research gap regarding the response of ancient marine sedimentary soils to boring-induced disturbances, while its practical significance lies in providing quantifiable construction parameter optimization schemes for foundation pit projects adjacent to metro lines in coastal cities—thereby directly supporting the safety protection of metro-adjacent structures.

Field measurement study

Project overview

A foundation pit project associated with Wenzhou Metro Line S1 is situated in Yongxing Street, Longwan District, Wenzhou. Its eastern side lies a mere 18.88 m from the critically protected Wenzhou Metro Line S1 light rail station. The project site spans two east-west-oriented plots and is primarily planned for the construction of office buildings, residential buildings, commercial podiums, and auxiliary functional rooms—featuring an overall 1-story basement, with 2 stories in localized sections.

The excavation depth of the foundation pit varies from 4.70 to 9.65 m, and the length of the parallel segment between the left-plot foundation pit and the tunnel is approximately 137.95 m. The site is situated within a coastal alluvial-lacustrine plain, adjacent to Wenzhou Bay. The stable groundwater table lies at depths ranging from -1.9 m to -0.5 m; following site leveling, the groundwater table is proximate to the ground surface, and geological conditions at the site are complex. Given the extensive excavation area, significant depth, and proximity to existing roads and critically protected metro stations, strict control over the deformation of foundation pit support structures is therefore essential—aimed at minimizing adverse impacts on the surrounding environment.

Considering the aforementioned engineering conditions, bored piles—with a diameter of 800 mm, length of 24.75 m, and spacing of 1000 mm—have been selected as the retaining structure along the metro-adjacent side of the foundation pit’s eastern edge; triaxial cement mixing piles (diameter: 650 mm; length: 10.5 m; spacing: 450 mm) are employed as supporting waterproof curtains—designed to minimize disturbances to the metro-adjacent area during both support construction and foundation pit excavation. Analysis of monitoring results

To accurately characterize the deformation behavior of concrete bored piles and assess the safety of the foundation pit and its surrounding environment, inclinometer holes were installed around the retaining structure (see Fig. 1 for the detailed layout). Of these inclinometer holes, one monitoring hole is positioned at the midpoint of the foundation pit’s northern side—which is adjacent to the metro; triaxial cement mixing piles were constructed along the east-west axis of the foundation pit. Additionally, five surface settlement monitoring points were installed along a southwest-northeast transect, with sequential intervals of 2 m, 3 m, 4 m, and 5 m. Surface displacements were measured using a DSA320 automatic level paired with a high-precision invar leveling rod; deep horizontal displacements in the subsurface soil were periodically measured using an inclinometer (Fig. 2) installed either in the foundation pit’s retaining structure or directly within the soil mass. Detailed positions and layouts are provided in Figs. 2 and 3.

After plotting the distribution curves of horizontal displacement along the pile depth under various working conditions (Fig. 4) and the vertical settlement curves of the ground surface at distances from the foundation pit (Fig. 5), the following preliminary observations were derived:

(1) Construction of triaxial cement mixing piles and excavation boring induce disturbance in the upper 10.5 m of the stratum; specifically, significant deformation occurs 2 m below the ground surface, and notable displacement is observed at the bottom of the borehole;

(2) Construction of triaxial cement mixing piles and excavation boring induce surface settlement, with settlement magnitudes increasing as the distance from the foundation pit decreases;

(3) Over time, shallow horizontal displacement of the soil on the side of the borehole opposite the foundation pit gradually increases, whereas deep horizontal displacement undergoes only minor changes.

Across different time scales, in addition to the variations in surface settlement distribution reported in existing literature, the deformation behavior of soil outside the pit may also exhibit significant differences. The displacement of ancient marine sedimentary soil layers following excavation exhibits a time-dependent pattern of “initial increase, subsequent deceleration, and final stabilization.” This behavior arises from the high saturated clay content, low permeability, and pronounced creep characteristics of these soils, as well as the stage-specific stress-seepage-creep coupling induced by excavation: (1) Initial stage: Instantaneous plastic deformation—driven by a sudden drop in effective stress due to unrelieved pore water pressure—induces rapid displacement; (2) Mid-stage: Consolidation drainage (which restores shear strength) and decelerating creep together slow the rate of displacement growth; (3) Late-stage: Equilibrium of effective stress and cessation of creep lead to stable displacement.

Therefore, in practical engineering, investigating the distribution characteristics of soil deformation curves in the foundation pit under various scenarios, comparing the disturbance magnitudes of different drilling methods at the same depth, and subsequently selecting an appropriate triaxial cement mixing pile construction method (based on the surrounding environment) to induce favorable and controlled borehole deformation behavior, holds significant importance for: (1) Minimizing soil disturbance at targeted locations; (2) Controlling structural deformation within specified limits; (3) Safeguarding the surrounding environment and structural integrity.

Numerical analysis model and result verification

Introduction to calculation model and parameters

COMSOL Multiphysics finite element software was chosen for its exceptional capability to couple soil mechanics (Mohr-Coulomb model) and fluid-solid interaction (Biot’s consolidation theory)—a critical requirement for simulating saturated ancient marine sedimentary soils, where pore water pressure dynamics govern short-term displacements. Additionally, its flexible meshing and parametric scanning functionalities enable accurate modeling of small boreholes within a large computational domain and efficient execution of orthogonal experiments, respectively.

COMSOL Multiphysics was employed for numerical modeling and analysis. The model domain includes a soil region with dimensions of 155 m (length) × 77.5 m (width), while the borehole is defined with a diameter of 650 mm and length of 10.5 m. These dimensions ensure that boundary conditions do not influence foundation pit deformation—a key validation criterion for numerical models.

Three borehole radii were selected—0.3 m, 0.325 m (ZKD65-3 A drill bit radius), and 0.35 m—based on standard engineering practices: a 50 mm increment was adopted, covering the ± 5% error range of the 0.325 m baseline radius. Three drilling lengths (9.5 m, 10.5 m, 11.5 m) were determined based on engineering rationale: 10.5 m is the baseline mixing pile length (calculated using Lizheng Deep Foundation Pit Support Structure Design Software V7.038 for the 5.85 m-deep pit), while 9.5 m and 11.5 m improve fault tolerance. This is achieved by resolving trade-offs between strict 0.5 m depth increments and required drilling speeds39 (0.5–1.0 m/min for sinking, 1–2 m/min for lifting)—speeds that would otherwise compromise construction efficiency and quality. Notably, neither parameter set is dictated by stratum conditions.

The Mohr-Coulomb model was adopted to simulate soil behavior in the model, with physical and mechanical parameters selected to match those of typical silty clay layers in urban Wenzhou. To simplify computations, the effect of soil stratification was neglected, and a homogeneous soil layer was used for analysis. Soil material parameters were simplified via a thickness-weighted averaging method (calculated as: [sum of (each soil layer’s parameter × its thickness)] / total soil thickness).

As presented in Table 1, the ancient marine sedimentary soils in this study correspond to the ②-2 layer of Wenzhou’s coastal alluvial-depositional plain. This layer is characterized by shell fragments (a defining feature of marine sediments), gray hue, fluid-plastic consistency, high compressibility, high sensitivity, and a thickness of 15.8 m (distributed across the entire site). The ②-2 layer alone exhibits a layer-specific compression modulus (Es) of 1.96 MPa, whereas a thickness-weighted Es of 4.05 MPa was used for numerical simulations—calculated for the entire soil profile, this value avoids biases associated with the arithmetic average method. This 4.05 MPa lies between the Es values of upper layers (①-1: 3.42 MPa; ②-1: 3.52 MPa) and deeper layers (④-1: 4.33 MPa; ⑤-2: 4.78 MPa). Its high compressibility and sensitivity distinguish it from typical soils. Notably, the high compressibility of the ②-2 layer is linked to engineering settlement risks. Thus, the thickness-weighted Es provides accurate parameters for foundation treatment and settlement control—this value was also incorporated into the model to capture the layer’s “thick, highly compressible” characteristics—ensuring the model accurately reflects the actual mechanical behavior of the soil.

Based on laboratory geotechnical test results and weighted calculations (presented in Tables 1 and 2), the physical and mechanical parameters of the soil are as follows: unit weight of 18.38 kN/m3, internal friction angle of 11.3°, cohesion of 19.69 kPa, Young’s modulus of 20.27 MPa (set to five times the compression modulus40–43, horizontal hydraulic conductivity of 1.19 × 10− 6 cm/s, vertical hydraulic conductivity of 1.01 × 10− 6 cm/s, isotropic hydraulic conductivity of 1.1 × 10− 6 cm/s (calculated as the square root of the product of horizontal and vertical hydraulic conductivities44, porosity of 0.51, Poisson’s ratio of 0.3 (referenced from the Plaxis 2D Manual40–42 and COMSOL Documentation43, and Biot coefficient of 1. Furthermore, using the total (rather than effective) values of the soil’s internal friction angle and cohesion allows for a more conservative estimation of the soil deformation zone. These parameters were used as input values for the simulation. Mud parameters were determined via reference to relevant literature45–47: unit weight of 15 kN/m3, effective cohesion of 1 kPa, effective internal friction angle of 5°, Young’s modulus of 1 MPa, Poisson’s ratio of 0.499, isotropic hydraulic conductivity of 10− 5 cm/s, and Biot coefficient of 1.

Specifications of triaxial cement mixing piles and boundary load settings for construction equipment: Triaxial cement mixing piles have a diameter of 650 mm, spacing of 450 mm, and length of 10.5 m; the construction equipment used is a ZKD65-3 A triaxial mixer, with a total weight of 21,305 kg and a pressure plate area of 10 m2. The model’s basic analysis workflow is as follows: (1) Generate the initial Darcy pressure field; (2) Generate the initial geostress field (incorporating the Darcy pressure field as additional external stress); (3) Reset the initial displacement to zero; activate soil elements in the borehole region to simulate mud formation during support structure construction; and apply construction equipment loads to the borehole side; (4) Extract horizontal displacements along the depth direction and vertical displacements at the ground surface adjacent to the foundation pit—for validation against measured data; (5) Finally, adjust relevant research parameters for parametric scanning; generate the mesh (see Fig. 3); and repeat the aforementioned steps (element activation, load application, and displacement extraction).

Verification of calculation results

In this study, calculated and field-measured curves of surface settlement and horizontal displacement outside the pit are presented in Figs. 4 and 5, with calculated curves represented by solid lines and field-measured curves by short dashed lines. Herein, downward settlement is defined as negative, while horizontal displacement directed away from the foundation pit is defined as positive—a critical convention for interpreting the curves. As field-measured boring progresses, soil displacements in the upper depth range of -5.25–0 m eventually stabilize, exhibiting the following key characteristic: the time-dependent influence of effective stress on shallow soil displacements is significantly more pronounced than its influence on soils in the depth range of -10.5 m to -5.25 m (Fig. 6). Comparisons reveal that the model’s calculation results are in good agreement with field-measured results for both surface settlement and horizontal displacement, with R2 values of 0.92 and 0.96, respectively.

It is important to note that given that deep soil displacement monitoring is performed at 0.5 m intervals, it is not feasible to capture field-measured data for every potential point. Additionally, there is a positional offset of approximately 1 m between the monitoring hole and the actual measurement location, which leads to a slight reduction in data accuracy (rather than data integrity). Specifically, as time elapses, field-measured data stabilize after 40 days. The maximum deep soil displacement coordinate is (displacement magnitude: 1.01 mm; depth: -1.5 m); the maximum deep soil horizontal displacement coordinate measured after 30 days is (displacement magnitude: 1.02 mm; depth: -2 m). Additionally, the maximum surface soil displacement coordinate on Day 40 is (horizontal distance from the pit: 1 m; settlement: -3.5 mm). The error observed in deep soil horizontal displacement measurements is primarily attributed to limitations of manual measurement techniques48. Additional sources of such error may include the randomness of construction-induced disturbances and the simplification of boundary conditions in the model49.

In conclusion, the Biot consolidation-based settlement model—which simulates triaxial cement mixing pile construction via element activation and accounts for soil effective stress—effectively captures the deformation characteristics of both deep horizontal displacements of soil outside the pit and vertical displacements of surface soils; this also confirms that the soil parameters employed in the model are reasonable and reliable, and the model’s calculation results exhibit high credibility. This supports the model’s use for further investigations into the influence of triaxial cement mixing pile construction (for foundation pit support) on both deep soils outside the pit and the surface displacement field. Orthogonal simulation experiments were conducted, with three drilling depths (9.5 m, 10.5 m, 11.5 m) and three borehole radii (0.3 m, 0.325 m, 0.35 m) selected for simulation. For instance, the experiment labeled “9.5 m − 0.3 m” corresponds to a drilling depth of 9.5 m and a borehole radius of 0.3 m.

Influence of boring radius and depth on soil outside the foundation pit

Figure 7a depicts the influence of varying boring radii on deep horizontal displacements of soil outside the pit, under a constant boring depth: as the boring radius increases from 0.3 m to 0.35 m, deep horizontal displacements of soil—from the ground surface to the midpoint of the boring section—gradually increase. The topmost soil layer (-1.715 m depth) exhibits a maximum displacement of 0.930 mm when the radius is 0.35 m; from the midpoint of the boring section to the borehole bottom, displacements are more strongly influenced by radius; the bottom soil layer (-9.905 m depth) reaches a maximum displacement of 0.418 mm at a radius of 0.35 m. This phenomenon can be attributed to the shallowly buried topsoil, which exhibits lower resistance to boring-induced disturbance and thus is more susceptible to larger displacements (consistent with Su et al.45. Meanwhile, the more pronounced influence of radius on the bottom layer’s maximum displacement (similar to Gao et al.50 is hypothesized to stem from the expanded borehole bottom area and associated higher resistance demands when the radius increases (consistent with Nguyen et al.51.

Figure 7b presents the effect of varying boring radii on vertical displacements, under a constant boring depth: as the radius increases from 0.3 m to 0.35 m, surface vertical settlements at the same horizontal distance from the pit gradually decrease (Wang et al.52. Settlements at distances exceeding 10 m are less affected, whereas those between 1 m and 10 m show a more distinct decrease; For instance, at a radius of 0.3 m, the vertical displacements at 1 m and 10 m distances are -2.061 mm and -0.773 mm, respectively (negative values indicate settlement). This observation can be explained by significant stress release in the region adjacent to the borehole during construction, which disrupts the soil’s mechanical equilibrium (Soomro et al.53.

Figure 8a depicts the influence of varying boring depths on deep horizontal displacements, under a constant radius: as the boring depth increases from 9.5 m to 11.5 m, displacements from the ground surface to the midpoint of the boring section gradually increase. The topmost soil layer (-1.687 m depth) reaches a maximum displacement of 0.937 mm at a boring depth of 11.5 m; from the midpoint of the boring section to the pit bottom, displacements are strongly influenced and exhibit a longitudinally expanding trend; at depths of -8.93 m, -9.94 m, and -11.002 m, the bottom-layer displacements reach 0.382 mm, 0.390 mm, and 0.401 mm, respectively. This can be attributed to the topsoil’s lower resistance, which makes it more susceptible to displacement. In contrast, the deep subsoil at the borehole bottom—supported by underlying soil layers—exhibits greater resistance, rendering it less susceptible to increases in boring depth (consistent with Zhong et al.54.

Figure 8b presents the effect of varying boring depths on vertical displacements, under a constant radius: as the boring depth increases from 9.5 m to 11.5 m, surface vertical settlements at identical positions gradually decrease. Settlements at distances exceeding 10 m are less affected, whereas those between 1 m and 10 m show a more distinct decrease; For instance, at a boring depth of 9.5 m, the vertical displacements at 1 m and 10 m distances are -1.979 mm and -0.773 mm, respectively (negative values indicate settlement). This can be explained by significant stress release in the region adjacent to the borehole, which disrupts mechanical equilibrium. Specifically, a boring depth of 9.5 m requires the upper shallow soil to bear greater resistance, whereas a depth of 11.5 m enables the deep soil layer (9.5–11.5 m) to share the load—thereby reducing displacement variability (similar to Khodair & Abdel-Mohti55.

Influence of drilling radius and depth on soil displacement around piles during 11.5 m pile-forming drilling

During the 11.5 m pile-forming drilling process, as illustrated in Fig. 9a, soil horizontal displacements exhibit a gradual upward trend as drilling depth increases. Specifically, while the maximum horizontal displacement in the upper portion of the pile continues to increase, its growth rate slows significantly; in contrast, horizontal displacement in the lower portion increases steadily. Meanwhile, the number of major extreme points in horizontal displacement increases from one to two. Consistent with Li56two distinct horizontal displacement peaks also occur during the drilling process. This variation serves as a critical criterion for delineating drilling-affected zones.

Based on these two extreme points, zone delineation can be performed as follows: (1) Using the maximum depth of the shallower first major extreme point as a boundary, the drilling-affected zone is clearly divided into upper and lower portions; (2) Using the smaller horizontal displacement value of the second (shallower) major extreme point as a boundary and incorporating the counterclockwise division rule, four sub-zones (I, II, III, and IV) are further identified.

Further observation reveals that the depth of the first major extreme point exhibits a variation pattern of “initial decrease, subsequent increase, and final stabilization” as drilling depth increases. This pattern indicates that the influence of drilling depth on shallow soils is limited, and its effect on increasing shallow displacements in the later stages has become negligible. In contrast, the second major extreme point gradually emerges as drilling depth increases. Due to computational resource constraints, the zone where it is located is classified as an evolutionary region. Moreover, these extreme points are aligned approximately linearly, with horizontal displacement values remaining relatively consistent and increasing only marginally with depth.

Figure 9b reveals a distinct influence pattern: The drilling radius exerts a significantly weaker influence on the shallow sub-zones (I and II) than on the deep sub-zones (III and IV). Integrating the analysis results from Fig. 9a and b, it is concluded that drilling depth—rather than drilling radius—is the key factor governing soil horizontal displacements. Additionally, displacements of the shallow soil surrounding the formed borehole and at the borehole bottom govern the overall soil displacement.

During the 11.5 m pile-forming drilling process, as illustrated in Fig. 10a, soil vertical displacements exhibit a gradual downward trend as drilling depth increases, with variations in vertical displacement observed across different locations. Specifically, while the maximum vertical displacement in the vicinity of the pile continues to decrease, its rate of decrease slows significantly; on the side distal to the pile, vertical displacement increases steadily, with a slight reduction in uplift observed at the farthest location.

Additionally, soil vertical displacements exhibit a logarithmic relationship with distance from the pile57. Specifically, for distances exceeding 1 m from the pile, this function yields a relatively good fitting result; across the entire distance range, the overall fitting goodness-of-fit (R2 exceeds 0.97. This favorable fitting result indicates that vertical displacements at locations exceeding 1 m from the pile (induced by pile formation) can be estimated using the settlement coordinates of two points on the side distal to the pile.

Furthermore, given the poor fitting result for the maximum displacement of the pile within the range of approximately 1 m, to more accurately analyze the underlying patterns, the slope can be calculated using coordinates of the maximum vertical displacement point near the pile and the farthest point (45 m from the pile); a corresponding plot can then be generated (with fitting results shown in Fig. 10b). Figure 10b demonstrates that a strong correlation exists between the maximum vertical displacement of soil near the pile and its distance from the pile—this relationship also follows a logarithmic pattern—with the drilling radius exerting minimal influence on this correlation.

Essentially, drilling disturbance induces the reorganization of the soil stress field and energy transmission (e.g., piles bearing loads from yielding soils58, with the logarithmic attenuation of energy with distance being the dominant factor.

Optimal evaluation of boring structure parameters for different protection types

Figure 11 compares soil deformation characteristics under various combinations of boring radius and depth. During triaxial cement mixing pile construction, if settlement exceeds allowable limits due to construction operational errors (e.g., excessively large radius or excessive depth), ensuring the safety of adjacent critical structures becomes challenging. Therefore, this study proposes the “Construction Drilling Safety Evaluation Coefficient (Drilling Safety Score, DSS)” as an evaluation indicator, employing a comprehensive scoring approach59. This indicator comprehensively accounts for the coupled effects of boring radius and depth, enabling quantification of the extent of construction-induced disturbance to the surrounding environment. It is particularly suited for differentiated analysis of protection requirements for above-ground structures and underground metro tunnels.

Specifically, the region above the midpoint of the boring section is designated as the above-ground building influence zone, with the maximum deep horizontal displacement within this range denoted as u₁; the region below the midpoint of the boring section is designated as the underground structure influence zone, with the maximum deep horizontal displacement within this range denoted as u2; the surface vertical settlement 1 m from the foundation pit is defined as the maximum displacement of the near-zone environment, denoted as s1; the surface vertical settlement 10 m from the foundation pit is defined as the maximum displacement of the far-zone environment, denoted as s2. Given that the distance between the foundation pit and the metro line is 18.8 m, and the metro-centered protection zone is defined as 10 m60, 10 m was ultimately determined as the boundary following difference calculation and rounding. For soft soil regions, enhanced settlement control in the near zone is required, so a 1 m near-zone boundary was established61,62.

Weights were determined using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)63–65: Four first-level indicators were defined—u1 (above-ground buildings), u2 (underground structures), s1 (near-zone environment), and s2 (far-zone environment)—to construct a 4 × 4 pairwise comparison matrix. Ten experts anonymously rated the relative importance of each indicator pair using the 1 to 9 point AHP scale (Table 3). The geometric mean of the ratings was computed, and weights were derived following standard AHP protocols64,65 (Table 4). A Consistency Index (CI) < 0.1 for the matrix validated its reliability, yielding final weights of u1 = 0.30, u2 = 0.25, s1 = 0.25, and s2 = 0.20.

The indicator weights require dynamic adjustment based on the priority of protected objects: the weight for above-ground building protection (u1) is set to 30%, as shallow soil deformation exerts a more pronounced impact on building structures; the weight for underground structure protection (u2) is 25%, as deep deformation may compromise the safety of underground pipelines and foundations; the weight for near-zone environment protection (s1) is 25%, given that the 1 m range is an environmentally sensitive zone requiring strict settlement control; the weight for far-zone environment protection (s2) is 20%, as the 10 m range exhibits a broad influence but relatively low sensitivity. For the above indicators, following the principle that “smaller deformation corresponds to a higher score,” the 9 sets of test data were ranked and assigned scores from 1 to 9 (9 points for the smallest deformation, 1 point for the largest). The total score was then computed via weighted summation. The calculation formula is: Total Score = (u1 Score × 30%) + (u2 Score × 25%) + (s1 Score × 25%) + (s2 Score × 20%). A higher total score indicates better safety performance of the corresponding construction scheme.

Comprehensive analysis results (Table 5) indicate that Scheme No. 1 (boring depth: 9.5 m; boring radius: 0.3 m) is the optimal construction scheme, achieving the highest comprehensive score of 5.40. For the same radius, as boring depth increases from 9.5 m to 11.5 m, the comprehensive score decreases consistently—indicating that increased boring depth exacerbates adverse impacts on the surrounding environment. Therefore, adopting a smaller boring depth (9.5 m) and a moderate radius (0.3 m to 0.35 m) is more conducive to mitigating construction-induced disturbance to the surrounding environment. The Construction Drilling Safety Evaluation Coefficient proposed in this study can provide a quantitative reference based on differences in protected objects (e.g., above-ground or underground structures) and variations in construction parameters (radius, depth). In practical engineering applications, it requires dynamic adjustment in conjunction with specific engineering conditions, thereby providing a scientific decision-making basis for protecting surrounding structures in similar construction scenarios.

Conclusions

This study focuses on the construction of triaxial cement mixing piles in typical silty clay layers within urban Wenzhou. A numerical model was developed in COMSOL, and its reliability was validated through integration with field-measured data. Parametric analysis was conducted on the pile structural parameters, leading to the following conclusions:The calculation results of the developed finite element model (incorporating the Mohr-Coulomb model and Biot’s consolidation theory) exhibit good agreement with field-measured data (R2 = 0.92 and 0.96, respectively), thus validating the reliability of both the model and the selected parameters. Both boring radius and depth influence soil disturbance, albeit with distinct characteristics. When the boring depth is 10.5 m, increasing the radius from 0.3 m to 0.35 m increases deep horizontal displacements (a 15.5% increase at the borehole bottom) while reducing surface settlements in the 1–10 m range (an 11.5% reduction at 1 m), thus confirming boring radius as a key parameter for disturbance control. When the boring radius is 0.325 m, increasing the depth from 9.5 m to 11.5 m significantly increases upper horizontal displacements (a 5.76% increase at the top), with only a minor change (4.97%) in bottom displacements, and suppresses near-zone settlements (a 3.34% reduction at 1 m).During pile-forming drilling with a length of 11.5 m, drilling depth is the dominant factor governing soil horizontal displacements. As drilling depth increases, horizontal displacements increase, and the number of extreme points changes from one to two—facilitating the delineation of influence zones—whereas the influence of boring radius is relatively minor.During pile-forming drilling, soil vertical displacements decrease as drilling depth increases and exhibit a strong logarithmic relationship with distance from the pile. The influence of boring radius is minor, with the logarithmic attenuation of energy with distance acting as the dominant mechanism. Based on the proposed “Construction Drilling Safety Evaluation Coefficient,” the scheme with a boring depth of 9.5 m and radius of 0.3 m is comprehensively optimal (total score: 5.40). This indicates that parameters featuring a shorter boring length and moderate radius are more conducive to safeguarding environmental safety.
