Determinants of female genital mutilation among daughters in Ethiopia: a negative binomial regression approach
Authors: Tewodros Getachew Tsegaye, Gedion Asnake Azeze, Sintayehu Solomon Kena, Berhan Tsegaye Negash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFemale genital mutilation is a major issue that has considerable effect on millions of girls in Africa, including Ethiopia. Despite the state initiatives to eliminate the practice by making it illegal, many mothers continue think that it should be continued for their daughters. Therefore, we aimed determinants of the number undergoing female genital mutilation of daughters in Ethiopia.MethodsData for this study were obtained from the 2016 EDHS. The weighted sample included in this study was 3754 adolescent girls and young women aged 15–24 years. Descriptive data were summarized using descriptive statistics and tables. A negative binomial regression model was fitted. The strength of association was assessed using AIR with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and a p-value < 0.05 was reported. The deviance test was used to check the goodness of fit.ResultsOf the 3754 women, 1728 (46%) were aged from 25 to 34 years and 2503(66.7%) women were uneducated. Maternal age between 25 and 34 years (AIRR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.16–0.37), as well as between 35 and 44 years (AIRR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.44–0.739), living in urban areas (AIRR = 0.343, 95% CI: 0.222–0.532), having no employment (AIRR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.1,1.6), not listening to the radio at all (AIRR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.96–3.7) or listen to it less than once per weeks (AIRR = 2.39, 95% CI:2.391.68–3.38), and watching television less than once per week (AIRR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.24–3.6), along with having one to five household members(AIRR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.54,0.76), were significant predictors of daughter’s FGM.ConclusionIn this study, factors that help protect a daughter from female genital mutilation include older maternal age, residing in urban areas, and having a smaller household size. Conversely, maternal unemployment and limited exposure to TV and radio are associated with a higher risk of female genital mutilation. Address these issue, efforts should be made to empower mothers through employment opportunities, and addressing the female genital concerns in targeted media campaigns is crucial.

Background

Female genital mutilation (FGM) includes all procedures that involve the partial or complete removal of the external female genitalia or cause harm to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons [1]. The procedures differ considerably according to customs, religious beliefs, cultural practices, and traditional norms [2]. FGM procedures are often carried out at various stages of a woman’s life, including the first week after birth, infancy, before puberty, before the first childbirth, and at other times, depending on the region and the primary motivation for the practice [3, 4].

There are several reasons for conducting female genital mutilation in various societies. One of the main reason for the practice of female genital mutilation practice is the intention of controlling female sexuality [5, 6]. However, a previous study has shown that FGM and outcomes of sexual behavior were not correlated [7]. Furthermore, FGM is also performed to prepare girls for marriage and adulthood. In some societies, its cultural significance leads to the belief that it maintains girls’ chastity, preserves fertility, improves hygiene, and enhances sexual pleasure for men [3, 6, 8, 9].

On top of that, the fear of losing community protection and support if a girl does not undergo female genital mutilation has also been cited as a reason [3, 8–10]. Finally, some perform the procedure for anatomical/aesthetic reasons, prevention of child mortality, or hygiene purposes, although medical evidence does not support these claims [2, 3, 8].

Female genital mutilation is a severe form of gender-based discrimination against girls and women, which violates a girl’s right to health, the right to be free from inhuman or degrading treatment, and the right to life when the procedure results in death [4]. It reflects deep-rooted gender inequality and represents an extreme form of harmful practice that is deeply embedded in the social, political, economic, cultural, and religious fabric of communities [11]. This often leads to girls and women facing significant social pressure from their families, peers, and others in their social network to undergo FGM [4].

FGM has been associated with both immediate and long-term health complications. The immediate complications that may arise from FGM include severe pain, infection, urinary retention, excessive bleeding, genital tissue swelling, shock, and even death [12–15]. The health impacts of FGM pose a significant burden on health systems [15]. The cost of treatment is estimated to be US$1.4 billion per year, and this number is expected to rise unless urgent action is taken to eliminate the practice [4].

Female Genital Mutilation is a pressing issue that affects millions of girls and women globally. According to data from 30 countries where FGM is practiced in Western, Eastern, and North-Eastern Africa, as well as some countries in the Middle East and Asia, over 200 million girls and women alive today have been subjected to the practice, with an estimated 3 million girls at risk each year [4]. A meta-analysis of 30 studies indicates that the pooled prevalence estimate of FGM among women aged 15 to 49 years is 36.9%, the magnitude reach at 8.27% among girls aged 0 to 14 years [16]. The prevalence of FGM was even higher at 56.4% in Africa [17]. In Ethiopia, it is estimated that 25 million girls and women have been subjected to FGM, which is the highest number in Eastern and Southern Africa [18]. According to the EDHS 2016 report, 65% of women aged 15–49 have undergone FGM, with 49% of them being circumcised before the age of five and 24% before the age of ten [19].

The Government of Ethiopia has identified FGM as top priority. Hence, it has developed a national strategy to end FGM by 2025 [20]. Furthermore, the government has made it a criminal offense through its revised national criminal code, with Articles 565 and 566 of the Criminal Code of Ethiopia specifying that the practice of FGM is punishable by imprisonment ranging from three months to ten years [21].

Even though Ethiopia is making efforts to end FGM, there is still a concerning 18% of women who believe that the practice should continue [19]. Furthermore, to meet SDG target 5.3, the progress in eliminating female genital mutilation must be eight times faster than what has been observed in the past 15 years [18]. Although there is literature available on FGM in Ethiopia, studies are scarce using this specific model. Therefore, this explicit model overdispersion will produce more reliable standard errors and inference.

This study captures the count nature of the number of daughters of women who undergo FGM to avoid loss of information. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by utilizing a negative binomial regression model to investigate factors influencing a daughter’s FGM status.

Methods and materials

Study setting, design, data source and period

A cross-sectional study design using secondary data from the 2016 EDHS was conducted. The study is conducted in Ethiopia. According to the United Nations statistics, Ethiopia’s estimated population in 2024 is 132,059,767, representing 1.62% of the global population and ranking it as the world’s 10th most populous nation. The country covers 386,102 square miles, with 22.1% of its inhabitants residing in urban areas. The population’s median age is 18.9 years [22]. Ethiopia’s healthcare infrastructure included 17,750 health posts, 3,735 health centers, and 353 hospitals, with an additional 425 health posts, 96 health centers, and 107 hospitals under construction. Furthermore, specialized facilities such as warehouses, trauma centers, mini blood banks, and laboratory infrastructures were established to support the healthcare system [23]. The requested data was obtained from the Demographic Health Survey (DHS) program’s official database, www.dhsprogram.com, after providing an abstract and stating the justification of the study’s objectives via an online form. The research utilized data from the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), a nationally representative sample collected between January 18 and June 27, 2016.

Study population and sampling technique

The sampling frame comprised 84,915 Enumeration Areas (EAs) from the 2007 Ethiopian Population and Housing Census. A two-stage stratified cluster sampling method was employed. The first stage involved selecting 645 EAs (443 in rural areas), while the second stage chose an average of 28 households per cluster. The Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS 2016) used a stratified two-stage cluster sampling approach. Each enumeration area (EA) contained about 181 households. In the first stage, 645 EAs were selected and distributed proportionally according to the number of EAs across the country, including both urban and rural settings. Of these, 202 were drawn from urban areas and 443 from rural areas. In the second stage, a fixed sample of 28 households was chosen from each EA using systematic random sampling. This process yielded a total of 18,008 households, of which 17,067 were occupied by women of reproductive age. However, only 16,583 eligible women of reproductive age were identified in the selected households. Of these, 15,683 participants provided complete responses, resulting in a response rate of 95%. In this study, we have included women who have a daughter.

Data collection tool

Five questionnaires were utilized in the 2016 EDHS - the Household Questionnaire, the Woman’s Questionnaire, the Man’s Questionnaire, the Biomarker Questionnaire, and the Health Facility Questionnaire. All five questionnaires are presented as an appendix in the EDHS 2016 publication [24].

The questionnaires were developed in conjunction with the Demographic Health Survey (DHS), which is customized to an Ethiopian context. Since the DHS recodes the original data into different databases.

Study variables

Operational definitions

The outcome variable is the number of daughters in the household who have undergone female genital mutilation. FGM among adolescent girls aged 0–14 years and young women aged 15–24 years. Information on female genital mutilation (FGM) among girls aged 0–14 and adolescent girls aged 15–24 years was collected by asking women with daughters how many of their children had undergone FGM, categorized as minor nicking, partial removal, or sewing shut; the responses ranged from no daughters affected to all daughters within this age group.

We have tried to assess various independent variables, categorized into socio-demographic, health-seeking, and reproductive factors. Socio-demographic factors such as age, location of residence, education level, employment status, and wealth index. Furthermore, health-seeking variables include mobile phone ownership, frequency of radio listening, frequency of watching television, and frequency of reading newspapers. Finally, reproductive health factors comprised the following factors: Gender of the household head, household size, number of live children, and history of abortion. These variables were selected because previous studies have shown them to be associated with FGM [25–28].

Education was categorized into no education, primary, secondary, and tertiary. Age of the respondents was initially categorized primarily into seven 5-year groups, but, during analysis, it was re-categorized into four groups (15–24, 25–34, 35–34, 45–49).

The wealth index was created using data from household surveys, which included information on asset ownership, housing quality, and access to essential services. The Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) employed Principal Components Analysis (PCA) to calculate a household wealth score based on these assets. Each de jure household member was assigned a score derived from this index. Households were then ranked according to their scores, and the wealth index was divided into five quintiles: the lowest 20% were labeled “poorest,” the next 20% as “poorer,” the middle 20% as “middle,” the next 20% as “richer,” and the highest 20% as “richest“ [29].

Data analysis

Data were obtained from the EDHS 2016, and further coding and analysis were performed using STATA version 14. Key explanatory variables associated with the practice of female genital mutilation (FGM) among daughters were identified through an extensive literature review, then defined, categorized, transformed, and recorded. Frequencies and proportions were calculated using cross-tabulation, and descriptive data were presented in tables and summary statistics. To reduce bias and variance, several measures were implemented: first, individual women were weighted by dividing by 1,000,000 to estimate point values; second, a survey design file was created using three essential variables for complex survey analysis—primary sampling unit, sample strata, and weight; third, sampling with replacement was specified as the estimator assumption; finally, the survey design file was analyzed. To use Poisson regression, the assumption that the mean and variance are equal should be met. To handle overdispersion and excess zeros, we have considered negative binomial Poisson regression and zero-inflated Poisson models, respectively, to obtain accurate results. The presence of overdispersion and excess zeros in the data was assessed using the alpha test and the Vuong test, respectively. The Vuong test comparing the negative binomial and zero-inflated negative binomial models was not statistically significant (Z = 1.21, p = 0.23), indicating that the zero-inflated negative binomial model did not provide a significantly better fit than the negative binomial model.

The standard Poisson regression model was the first model considered while working with count data. It is a technique used to describe count data as a function of a set of independent variables, which assumes the observations should be independent over time and the mean and the variance of the dependent variable should be equal [30]. However, the assumption of the Poisson regression failed (the sample variance exceeds the sample mean), in the case of overdispersion. In such a case, the negative binomial regression model that includes an unobserved specific effect (random term or error term) for the parameter was the preferred one to handle the overdispersion. The negative binomial regression model is used when the Poisson regression is not an appropriate model because of overdispersion [31, 32]. A Likelihood Ratio(LR) test for the parameter α (p-value < 0.001) was used for the over-dispersion parameter, in the negative binomial specification against the Poisson model specification [33].

A negative binomial regression model was employed. Statistical significance of independent variables was defined by a P value less than 0.05. The adjusted incidence rate (AIRR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals was calculated. Among four fitted models, the better model was chosen using deviance statistics, since it measures model fitness. A model with a lower deviance value was considered a better-fitted model for this study. Multicollinearity among independent variables was checked using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Adjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence interval was used for measuring the strength of association between independent variables and female genital mutilation.

Ethical approval consent to participate

As a secondary analysis of the 2016 EDHS data, this research did not require ethical approval. To conduct our investigation, we obtained permission to access and download the necessary data files from the DHS online archive after registering and submitting a request. The EDHS 2016 report indicates that participants’ information was anonymized as part of the data collection process for the survey [19].

Multivariable negative binomial regression analysis

Maternal age, residence, working status, listening to the radio, watching television, and the number of houses hold members had a significant association with Female genital mutilation of daughters.

Compared to the age group of 15–24, the incidence rate of FGM among daughters of maternal age groups 25–34 and 35–44 years was decreased by 75% (AIRR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.16–0.37) and 43% (AIRR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.44–0.739), respectively. Daughters of mothers residing in urban areas had a 65% lower rate of FGM compared to their counterparts (AIRR = 0.343, 95% CI: 0.222–0.532). Daughters of unemployed mothers had a 1.4 higher incidence rate of FGM (AIRR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.1, 1.6) than daughters of employed women.

Daughter of women who did not listen to the radio at all or listened to it less than once per week had a 2.68 more higher rate (AIRR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.96–3.7) of getting FGM. For daughters of women whose mother listened to the radio once per week, the chance of being mutilated is raised by 2.39 (AIRR = 2.39, 95% CI: 2.391.68–3.38).

Daughters whose mother watches TV less than once per week have a 2.1 times higher rate of FGM (AIRR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.24–3.6) compared to daughters of women who watch TV at least once a week. The size of family (1–5) decreased the risk of FGM by 36% compared to their counterparts (AIRR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.54, 0.76) (Table 4).

Discussion

The number of daughters undergoing FGM is an indicator of fertility-related exposure and clearly shows the parental decision-making process. It also reflects family structure and possible cultural patterns.

The likelihood of daughters undergoing FGM decreases as the mother’s age increases. Mothers aged 25–34 and 35–44 are associated with a reduced risk of their daughters experiencing FGM compared to mothers aged 15–24. The finding is supported by findings from Ethiopia and Ghana, which indicate maternal age has a significant effect on daughters’ FGM status [34–36]. Evidence suggests that decisions regarding FGM are not solely based on age. Instead, they are significantly shaped by factors such as education, awareness, and location. Younger mothers, who often have less education and awareness, tend to support the practice more [37]. Conversely, older mothers, having more personal experience and understanding of FGM’s negative effects, may be less inclined to have their daughters undergo the procedure.

In this study, residing in urban areas was associated with a decreased risk of FGM for daughters. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia, Benin, and Ghana, which showed that the risk of FGM is higher among rural residents [34, 38–40]. This may be due to the fact that urban women tend to have better education, healthcare coverage, and awareness about sexual and reproductive health, which leads to a decrease in the risk of FGM for their daughters [41].

The risk of FGM among daughters was higher among mothers who did not work, compared to their counterparts. The finding aligns with studies from Ethiopia and Africa [38, 42, 43]. The possible reasons could be that most employed women are better educated and understand the consequences of FGM on their daughters, leading them to refuse the practice [44]. Furthermore, employed women possess the decision-making authority to prevent the continuation of FGM on their daughters [44, 45].

The current study revealed that mothers who had low exposure to radio and TV had a significantly higher incidence of their daughters being subjected to FGM. The finding is supported by several studies done in Ethiopia [34, 42, 46, 47]. The increased exposure to media among mothers may lead to greater awareness about the harmful effects of FGM, resulting in challenging traditional beliefs and practices that promote the practice, empowering mothers to resist societal pressures and motivate them to prevent their daughters from undergoing FGM [48, 49]. Moreover, Media exposure enables mothers to learn about legal frameworks and laws that prohibit FGM, ultimately safeguarding girls from this practice.

Having fewer than 5 household members is also associated with a decreased incidence rate of FGM compared to having more than 6 household members. Similarly, a study conducted in Ethiopia has shown that a higher number of living children is linked to an increased risk of female genital mutilation [34]. This can be due to several interrelated mechanisms. In large families, economic burden and financial strain may increase the emphasis on daughters’ marriageability as a means of reducing household responsibilities, with FGM often perceived as a mechanism to secure marriage [50]. Furthermore, women in larger families often have less decision-making power and are less likely to resist community pressure, making them less able to refuse FGM for their daughters [45]. Moreover, in such families, mothers are more likely to have undergone FGM themselves, increasing the risk of intergenerational transmission of the practice [51].

Limitation

This study utilized national data with a sufficient sample size, enhancing the validity of the findings. However, it did not account for important clinical factors due to the use of secondary data.

Conclusion

This study found that older maternal age, living in urban areas, and having fewer household members were factors that reduced the risk of female genital mutilation. Conversely, unemployment of the mother and infrequent television and radio exposure were identified as factors that increase the risk of girls’ genital mutilation. These findings suggest that it is important to promote maternal empowerment, mainly through the provision of employment opportunities. Moreover, campaigns in rural areas and using mass media like radio and television can play a significant role in raising awareness and promoting behaviors that contribute to reducing female genital cutting.
