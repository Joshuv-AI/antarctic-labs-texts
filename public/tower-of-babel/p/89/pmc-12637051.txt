u-LINNDA: A protocol for user-optimized lymphoma identification through neural network detection aid
Authors: Maximilian Fischer, Miriam Cindy Maurer, Robin Peretzke, Florian Max Hauptmann, Chiraz Ben-Salah, Martin Jakobs, Manuel Kaes, Kianush Karimian-Jazi, Moritz Robert Scherer, Martin Bendszus, Klaus Maier-Hein, Sandro Manuel Krieg, Jan-Oliver Neumann, Peter Neher, Paul Vincent Naser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlioblastoma (GBM) and primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) are aggressive brain tumors that require neurosurgical treatment. For targeted treatment, biopsy and histopathological identification of the tumor entity are necessary. Here, we present a protocol for diagnosing PCNSL using a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based algorithm. We describe steps for installing the u-LINNDA (user-optimized lymphoma identification through neural network detection aid) algorithm, data preparation and preprocessing, and predicting tumor entities using u-LINNDA. We then detail procedures for predicting tumor identity and inspecting the u-LINNDA report.

Before you begin

We recently developed a CNN-based workflow for diagnosing GBM and PCNSL.1 We largely neglected hardware constraints during our development phase, focusing instead on model performance. To make our algorithm accessible to clinical end-users with a reasonable technical background and limited technical resources, we ported the neural network to a CPU-based virtual environment running in Docker. This transition does not affect the validity of the predictions.2 Furthermore, we added a preprocessing pipeline for raw exported DICOM images. The protocol below describes all necessary steps, from setting up the u-LINNDA algorithm over data preprocessing to final application and prediction on DICOM files. The successful execution of this protocol will yield a PDF report detailing the predicted tumor entity.

Innovation

The u-LINNDA protocol advances differentiating glioblastoma (GBM) from primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) by combining deep learning with an accessible, end-to-end workflow tailored to clinical end-users. Our previously published convolutional neural network achieved non-inferiority compared to experienced neuroradiologists but required advanced programming skills and GPU infrastructure, limiting its applicability. The present protocol overcomes these barriers by refactoring the pipeline into a Dockerized CPU-based environment that can be executed on standard consumer hardware. This makes the workflow reproducible and deployable in settings without specialized computational resources. In addition, we provide a streamlined preprocessing pipeline that converts raw DICOM exports into the standardized, coregistered NIfTI format required for inference. The integration of preprocessing, inference, and reporting into a single reproducible pipeline reduces user error, ensures consistency, and enables robust application across diverse datasets. Finally, the protocol outputs a standardized PDF report with predicted tumor entity, optional skull-stripped images, and segmentation masks, thereby bridging the gap between algorithmic predictions and practical interpretation in the clinical workflow. By merging established image registration methods, lightweight containerization, and a validated neural network into a single package, u-LINNDA represents a significant innovation over prior approaches, lowering technical barriers, improving reproducibility, and providing clinicians with a ready-to-use decision-support tool for one of the most challenging differential diagnoses in neuro-oncology.

Hardware requirements

The minimum hardware requirements for executing this workflow are estimated to consist of 4 CPU cores, 8 GB of RAM, and 50 GB of storage in an x86-based machine. On ARM-based systems, 16 GB of RAM may be required. For optimal performance, it is recommended to use a system with an 8-core processor or greater, 16 GB of RAM, and at least 100 GB of storage.

Limitations

To enable the use of the u-LINNDA algorithm on a wide variety of devices (see above), we replaced the GPU-based prediction algorithm with a CPU-based one. However, this impacted the prediction speed, which is now several minutes, compared to seconds in the case of GPU-based prediction. Furthermore, u-LINNDA remains experimental and should not be used in clinical decision-making.

/home/run.sh line 4: 9 Bus error. Hd-bet -I “/inputdata” -o “skullstripped_mri” -device=cpu –disable_tta --verbose

Potential solution

We have encountered this error in cases of insufficient RAM. While for x86/x64 based systems, 8 GB of RAM are sufficient, systems running the newer Apple M chips seem to require at least 16 GB of RAM to successfully run u-LINNDA.

Problem 5

u-LINNDA is providing incorrect predictions on my datasets.

Potential solution

u-LINNDA is not infallible and can correctly diagnose PCNSL in approximately 80% of cases we have tested so far. In any case, you must not rely on u-LINNDA’s prediction for clinical decision making (see disclaimer). If the algorithm runs correctly and repetitively provides the incorrect diagnosis, you should identify two known sources of error.•Verify the accurate coregistration of your datasets before feeding them into the u-LINNDA workflow. While our provided simpleITK based coregistration works well if there is little movement by the patient between acquisition of the MRI sequences, more exact registration methods, e.g. FLIRT, may be necessary depending on your data quality.•Verify the correct naming of the imaging sequences.
