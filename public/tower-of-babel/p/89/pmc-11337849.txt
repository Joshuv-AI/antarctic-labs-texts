Knowledge, attitudes, and practices of government workers on zoonotic parasites in pet animals: a study in Central Gondar Zone, Ethiopia
Authors: Adanie Delie, Gashaw Getaneh Dagnaw, Abebe Tesfaye Gessese, Haileyesus Dejene
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParasitic zoonoses are infections transmitted between vertebrate animals and humans, posing serious public health risks and significant economic consequences. The study aimed at assessing government workers' knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding zoonotic parasites of pet animals in Ethiopia's central Gondar zone.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out in selected towns of Central Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia from January 2022 to August 2022. Four hundred randomly selected government employees participated in the study. Data were collected through semi-structured questionnaire administered by face-to-face interview. The chi-squared (χ2) was calculated for association and p-value less than 0.05 was considered as significant.ResultsA 57.25% of respondents were males and 42.75% were females. Most of the respondents, 67.5%, had completed college/university education. About 60% of the respondents had 'high knowledge', 57.25% had 'positive attitude', and 73% had 'good practice' regarding pet animals as a source of zoonotic parasites. The chi-square analysis revealed a significant discrepancy (p < 0.05) between knowledge with sex, educational level, and position in the house. The attitude level was significantly associated (p < 0.05) with educational level, religious view, and service year. Significant association was also observed (p < 0.05) between practice level of the respondents and educational level and marital status. The study also showed a significant association between knowledge and attitude (χ2 = 40.4, p ≤ 0.001), knowledge and practice (χ2 = 34.9, p ≤ 0.001), and attitude and practice (χ2 = 12.76, p = 0.013) of the respondents.ConclusionAlthough our results revealed that more than half of the participants had good knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding zoonotic parasites of pet animals, enhanced routine hygienic practices and regular awareness creation programs are recommended to bridge existing gaps.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19811-0.

Introduction

Zoonoses are infections that spread naturally between vertebrate animals and humans. It could be caused by bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungus, and protozoa [1]. In the absence of proper management of the relationship between animal and human populations, zoonotic diseases can pose a serious public health risk with significant economic consequences [2]. Zoonotic diseases endanger the health and productivity of animals; threaten people's livelihoods, and cause illness and death in consumers. Over the last decade, the direct cost of zoonotic diseases has been estimated to be more than $20 billion, with over $200 billion in indirect losses [2, 3]. Zoonoses have a significant impact on public health and livestock industries, accounting for 61% of all infectious organisms known to be pathogenic to humans [4].

Zoonotic diseases in developing countries have been associated with various factors such as farming practices, educational background, food habits, the presence or absence of a reservoir population, and awareness of disease control program [5]. Ethiopia ranked second and first from the continent by human population and livestock population, respectively [3]. Approximately 80% of Ethiopians are agriculturally dependent, maintaining direct contact with livestock or other domestic animals, creating a heightened risk of zoonotic disease transmission. Ethiopia experiences a substantial health burden from zoonotic diseases, given its large population of low-income livestock farmers [2, 6].

Diseases from food of animal origin are common health problems in Ethiopia. In study conducted in Northwest part of Ethiopia showed that, the health burden due to zoonotic diseases was estimated at 0.2, 0.1 and 1.3 disability adjusted life years (DALYs) per household per year. The overall health burden due to foodborne zoonotic diseases was estimated to be 89.9 DALYs per 100,000 populations per year [3].

Furthermore, pet dogs and cats are often considered loyal friends and close companion animals to humans. This human-animal bond can provide significant benefits to humans' emotional development, socialization, and psychological and physiological well-being [7]. However, it is important to be aware that dogs and cats can also act as reservoirs for a wide range of parasitic zoonoses, including toxoplasmosis [8], echinococcosis [9, 10], ancylostomiasis [11], toxocariasis [12], and leishmaniasis [13, 14]. Their roles in the transmission of human infections have been widely acknowledged [7, 15, 16].

Zoonotic parasites are found in a wide range of protozoa, cestodes, nematodes, trematodes, and arthropods and a number of zoonotic parasites play an important role. Furthermore, some parasitic zoonoses have emerged as agents of opportunistic infections in recent years [17, 18]. These diseases are transmitted to humans through the consumption of environmentally resilient transmissive stages (spores, cysts, oocysts, ova, larval, and encysted stages) or through the consumption of raw or undercooked 'meat' containing infective tissue stages. Moreover, they could be transmitted through the consumption of contaminated food or water particularly in rural areas [5, 19].

Besides poor hygiene and a lack of veterinary attention, lack of zoonotic parasitic disease awareness can exacerbate the disease transmission. Ethiopia faced tremendous public health threats originated from animals [20]. Ethiopia lacks established parasitic disease control program [17]. Despite the Ethiopians residing both in urban and rural areas have close contact with their animals as they shared same micro-environment, level of their knowledge, attitude and practices regarding the zoonotic diseases including zoonotic parasites from pets were lacking. Keeping a dog and cat in their houses for various reasons such as gardening and also biological control of rats are commonly practiced in rural areas and also in semi-urban areas. Having the level of community awareness about zoonotic diseases, attitudes and practices is important for the control and prevention strategies. Therefore, the current study was conducted with the aim to assess the knowledge, attitude, and practice of employees from various government organizations regarding pet animal parasites that infect humans. The data generated from this study will be used as a baseline for the implementation of diseases intervention program by policy makers.

Materials and methods

Study area

The research was carried out in four selected towns in the central Gondar zone of the Amhara regional state of Ethiopia, namely, Gondar, Makesgnit, Chuahit, and Kola Diba. Gondar is a town in Northwest Ethiopia, 727 km from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia's capital city. The town is located at latitude 12.5° N and longitude N 37° E and its population is 390,644. It is located 2133 m above sea level (m.a.s.l). The town has an average temperature of 20°C year-round and an annual rainfall of 2077mm. Maksegnit town is located 695 km from Addis Ababa. The area is located at latitude of 12.38° N, and longitude of 37.55° E, and 1923m.a.s.l. The average temperature in the area ranges from 15°C to 31°C. Chuahit is located 789 km Northwest of Addis Abeba at 12.33° N latitude and 37.226° E longitude. It has an annual average temperature of 28°C and elevation of 1837 m.a.s.l. Kola Diba is located 773km from Addis Ababa at latitude 12.42° N, longitude 37.325° E, and elevation 1891 m.a.s.l [21] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Map of the study areas

Study design and study period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January-2022 to August 2022 to assess government workers' knowledge, attitude, and practice level regarding zoonotic parasites of pet animals.

Study population

The study population included government employees of both sexes, adults (≥ 18 years), who had owened pet animals. Type of religions, years of service, marital status, and educational levels were collected. Individuals who had not owned a pet animal and were not government employees were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling strategy

Hence, there was no previous study in the study area, we used Thrusfield formula [22] for sample size calculatios which assumed that 50% of the respondents have knoledge, attitude and practices on zoonotic parasites of pet animals. Accordingly, 95% confidence interval (CI) and 5% absolute error were considered in the sample calculation. A total of 400 samples were included after considering 5% non-response rate. The rationale for using Thursfield's simple population proportion formula is that it aligns with the study's goal, helps determine the sample size needed to achieve statistically significant results with a specified margin of error and confidence level, is consistent with the cross-sectional survey design, and provides straightforward and effective sample size calculations for epidemiological surveys.

The study areas were selected purposively due to their unique geographical and demographic features, including population density for both humans and pets, as well as interactions with pet animals. These characteristics ensure that the districts are representative of broader regional conditions. Additionally, the presence of established government structures and institutions provides an organized framework for data collection among government workers. The absence of previous research in this area further underscores the importance of this study, presenting a valuable opportunity to address an existing gap in KAP. All participants who had owned pet animals (either dog or cat) from all governmental organizations were selected using a simple random sampling by computer method.

Method of data collection

Data collections

A semi-structured pre-tested questionnaire was used for data collection. The questionnaire, developed after a thorough literature review of KAP surveys on zoonotic diseases [1, 6]. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English by the principal investigator. It was then translated into the local language (Amharic) and subsequently translated back into English to ensure consistency. The questionnaire comprised four sections, primarily featuring close-ended questions. Two experts in epidemiology and public health reviewed the items for content validity. Based on their feedback, modifications were made to tailor the questions to the local population. The final English version of the questionnaire was used for data collection, which was conducted through face-to-face interviews of the respondents.

Measurement tools

Socio-demographic data

The socio-demographic data consisted of seven questions, which covers sexes, age, education level, religion, years of service, and respondents’ position in the household and marital status (Annex I).

Knowledge on zoonotic parasites of pet animals

Eleven questions were administered to assess the knowledge of government employees regarding zoonotic parasites transmitted through pet contact. Seven questions used a Likert scale, comprising six positive statements and one negative statement. Additionally, four open-ended questions allowed respondents to provide their opinions (Annex II). Each closed-ended question awarded three points for a correct answer, two points for an uncertain answer, and one point for a wrong answer. Scores ranged from 7 to 21 points and were categorized into three levels based on Bloom's cut-off point [23] of 60–80% as follows: High level (80–100%): Scores of 17–21, Moderate level (60–79%): Scores of 13–16, and Low level (less than 60%): Scores of 7–12.

Attitudes towards zoonotic parasites of pet animals

This section explores the attitudes of participants towards pet contact zoonotic parasites. Participants were asked five questions in total, comprising four positive statements and one negative statement, rated on a Likert scale from agree to disagree (Annex III). The attitude scale was utilized to assess the rating scale. Scores ranged from 5 to 15, with individual responses summed and averaged for calculation. Attitudes were categorized as follows: Positive Attitude for scores of 12–15 (80%-100%), Neutral Attitude for scores of 9–11 (60%-79%), and Negative Attitude for scores of 5–7 (Less than 60%).

Practices on zoonotic parasite of pet animals

This section comprised seven questions, six of which were close-ended with Likert scale responses, and one open-ended question (Annex IV). Responses were rated using the Likert scale as previously described. Scores measuring the practice of managing zoonotic parasites in pet animals ranged from 6 to 18, and were categorized into three levels based on Bloom's cut-off point of 60–80% [23]. The practice levels were as follows: Good (80–100%), scoring 14–18; Fair (60–79%), scoring 11–13; and Poor (Less than 60%), scoring 6–10.

Data management and analysis

The collected data was entered using Microsoft Excel® 2010, and then the data was classified, filtered, and coded. The STATA version 16 (Stata Corp. Texas, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants and open ended questions were summarized using descriptive statistics. Numerical data was expressed as mean, standard deviation, or percentage. Chi square (χ2) was used to determine the relationship between knowledge, attitude, and practice with the socio-demographic. When the p-value is less than 0.05, the relationship is considered significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of respondents

Among 400 respondents recruited, 57.25% were male, and 42.75% were female. More than half of the respondents (60.75%) were married. The average age of the respondents was 34.55 (± 8.21) years. More than two-third of the participants were belonging to the Christian Orthodox religions. About 67.5% of the respondents attended at college/university education while non-formal education was 3.5%. Almost half of the respondents (53%) had more than 6 years of services (Table 1). Respondents' knowledge on zoonotic parasites of pet animals

The respondents' mean knowledge score was 16.95 (SD = 2.375) out of a possible 21 points. The majority (60.5%) of the respondents had "high knowledge," while 144 (36%) had "moderate knowledge," and 14 (3.5%) had "low knowledge" about zoonotic parasites of pets. On the other hand, two hundred seventy (67.5%) of the respondents agreed that pet animals are a source of zoonotic parasites. The majority of the respondents (68.75%) agreed that eating raw meat increases the occurrence of zoonotic parasites. In addition, two hundred sixty-five (66.25%) of the respondents agreed that the cause of zoonotic parasites is parasite. Meanwhile, a third of the respondents, 120/400, disagreed that bacteria are the cause of zoonotic parasites. More than half of the respondents (53.25%) were certain that a lack of veterinary attention can exacerbate the spread of zoonotic parasites (Table 2). Association between socio-demographic variables and knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) on pet association parasitic zoonotic infections

The results showed a significant difference between the respondent sex and knowledge level regarding parasitic zoonosis related to pets (χ2 = 12.35, p = 0.002). The education status of the respondent showed a significant relationship (χ2 = 86.93, p ≤ 0.001) with the knowledge level. The difference observed between knowledge with the respondents' age group, marital status, religious affiliation, and years of services was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 5). On the other hand, there is no significant association (p > 0.05) observed between attitude level with sex, marital status, and position in the house of the respondents towards pet associated zoonotic parasites. The association of attitude level with educational attainment of the respondents was significant (χ2 = 29.42, p ≤ 0.001). The respondent's religious beliefs (χ2 = 17.3, p = 0.027), age group (χ2 = 32.9, p ≤ 0.001), and years of service (χ2 = 21, p ≤ 0.001) showed a significant association with attitude levels (Table 6). Additionally, as shown in Table 7, there was no significant association (p > 0.05) between the respondents' practice level with sex, age group, position in house, religious affiliation, and years of service. There was a significant association between the practice level with education level (χ2 = 50.1, p ≤ 0.001) and marital status (χ2 = 18.8, p = 0.004) of the respondents (Table 7). Discussion

Understanding the disease's mode of transmission and its characteristics is essential to the introduction and implementation of effective control measures. According to the KAP theory, people's knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding the disease have a general impact on how the disease is controlled in the community [24]. One of the most significant actions that can help control the zoonotic parasite is public education [24–26]. The study's findings showed that majority of the government employees are knowledgeable about the spread, prevention, and management of parasitic diseases.

It is well known that having pets improves one's health. However, there are numerous obstacles to the human-animal bond that heighten the risk of infection between animals and people [27]. In this study, majority of the participants reported that eating undercooked meat increases the risk of contracting zoonotic parasites, and also not seeking veterinary care for pets increases the risk of contracting zoonotic parasites by 53.25%. A similar finding was noted by Tesfaye et al. [28] in Jimma, who indicated that 82.3% of the respondents believe that the consumption of raw or undercooked meat is responsible for the transmission of taeniasis. In addition, a study conducted in Ada’a district by Abunna et al. [29] showed that majority of the respondents (82.7%) and (79.9%) believed that consumption of raw meat and milk, respectively responsible for transmission of zoonotic diseases. The variation may be a result of the respondents' difference in social background, educational level, and professional background. Approximately 42% of the respondents in the current study are certain that bacteria are the root cause of zoonotic parasite disease. This explains the lack of knowledge because during interviews, the word "parasite" is frequently used instead of the word "bacteria."

In the current study, 35.75% of the participants believe that having a pet outweighed any possible health risks. Similarly, 39.5% of the participants were not sure whether having a pet outweighed any possible health risks. However, they are not overly concerned about diseases that are spread by pets. Pets can spread various zoonotic infections to people, even though the advantages outweigh the risks. Cats, in particular, carry the toxoplasma parasite, which can enter the brain and result in toxoplasmosis [30]. A study by Stull et al. [31] in Ontario, Canada, and Tamiru et al. [32] in Western Ethiopia showed that 61% and 76.15% of the respondents, respectively, perceived that the benefits of owning pet animals outweigh any health risks associated with pet ownership. Their perception was higher than the current finding. The difference in perception between the studies might be due to the varying cultural and educational backgrounds of the participants in each study.

The recent rarity of pet contact associated zoonosis control programs in Ethiopia may help to explain the lack of knowledge about these illnesses and methods for prevention and treatment. Because understanding the risk of zoonotic diseases is a requirement for effective prevention, the public's limited knowledge of zoonotic diseases is a serious problem [32]. Participants in the current study (64.25%) are aware that zoonotic parasites on pets are controllable. This result is comparable to the one reported by Zewdie et al. [33] in the Yabello District of the Oromia Regional State of Ethiopia, which was 69.2%. According to the current study, 36% of the respondents' pets need to be dewormed. This agrees with the reports of Steele and Mor, [34] in Australia. This finding highly disagrees with a study by Gebremichael et al. [35] in Assayita, northeastern Ethiopia. However, it is lower than the earlier study conducted by Khan et al. [36] in Pakistan, which found that 68.5% of the respondents dewormed their pet. This discrepancy observed might be occurred from ignorance of zoonotic parasites.

Through some actions like good animal husbandry, hand hygiene, and modified animal-contact behaviors, many of the disease risks associated with pet contact can be eliminated or significantly reduced. It is necessary for people who come into contact with animals to be aware of these disease risks in order for infection prevention to be effective. Therefore, understanding the risk of zoonotic diseases is essential for successful disease prevention [30]. In this study, 99.25% of participants almost always wash their hands before eating. This finding is comparable to Khan et al. [36] in Pakistan, but it is higher than the report of Wumbiya et al. [37] in Greater Kapoeta South Sudan. The study found that approximately 39.5% of government employees had experience with disposing of pet animal wastes than had been previously reported by Wumbiya et al. [37] in Greater Kapoeta, South Sudan.

In the current study, 14.25% of the respondents said their pets do not receive treatment for parasitic infections. This indicates that there is a very high risk of zoonotic parasite transmission from intermediate to definitive hosts. In the current study 4.0% of the respondents never had the practice of treating infected person for parasitic disease. The current finding was higher when compared with the reports of Wumbiya et al. [37] in Greater Kapoeta, South Sudan. Also, 39.5% of the respondents are aware about the practice of taking pet animals to the veterinarian for a diagnosis. The current finding is comparable to the report of Tamiru et al. [32] in Western Ethiopia and Kiflu et al. [38] in Addis Abeba, Ethiopia. Companion animals are increasingly consuming raw food, which presents a significant zoonotic risk. According to a study by Larry et al. [39], some raw foods are become a source of contamination with pathogenic microorganisms. The current result indicates that 45.75% of respondents fed raw meat to their pets. This coincides with findings of Kiflu et al. [38] in Addis Abeba, Ethiopia. On contrary, lower habit in raw meat feeding was reported by Stull et al. [31] in Canada. In general, giving raw meat for dogs is crucial for completing the hydatidosis lifecycle.

In the current study, the participants who had completed secondary education, vocational education, or college or university had higher knowledge scores than those who had only completed primary or elementary school or had never attended any formal education. Thus, the association between education level and Knowledge score is attributed to the idea that a deeper knowledge base can positively influence their knowledge about zoonotic parasitic diseases. In addition, a significant variation (p < 0.05) observed between the knowledge level with the respondent sex. The difference might be due to difference in their educational level, responsibility in the household, and professional background between the study participants.

In this study, a higher positive attitude level was documented in people with two to five years of experience as compared to people with less than two years of experience. The discrepancies observed in the attitude level of the study participants between the various sociodemographic characteristics could be attributed to differences in their education level and experience. Furthermore, in the current study, people who attend college or university had a greater degree of excellent practice than people who had no formal education. In addition, married respondents had a greater degree of good practice when compared to unmarried and widowed participant. The variation in practice level observed among the different categories of socio demographic variables may be due to differences in the number of participants in each category, differences in their practices obtained through education. In addition, a married person becomes responsible and has a good practice of washing their hands to protect their children from various diseases. Additionally, a relationship between respondents' knowledge and practice, knowledge and attitude, and practice and attitudes were assessed and was statistically significant (p < 0.05). This suggested that the proportion of the respondents who has good practice level associated with the study participants' knowledge level.

Limitation of the study

The present study has had certain limitations such as the authors give attention for literate individuals to measure the perception of the people, the design shows the status of the workers at the point of data collection, the study only has focused on parasitic diseases and the authors did not try to see the status of the zoonotic parasites in pet animals.

Conclusion and recommendations

The findings indicate that there is considerable level of knowledge in the study participants. More than half of the study participants are aware of pet animals are source of human infection. Feeding raw meat can increases the occurrence of zoonotic parasites. Further, more than half of the respondents have had positive attitude towards the effect of zoonotic parasites related to pets. The majority of government workers have had good practices towards the zoonotic parasites of pet animals. In this assessment, the knowledge of respondents was significantly associated with sex and educational level. The respondents’ attitude was associated with educational level, religious and service years of the respondents. The practices habit of the study participants was influenced by educational level and marital status. The results show the necessitates of regular awareness creation which plays an important role in zoonoses parasite prevention, promoting routine hygiene practices, addressing the potential for parasite transmission through feeding of raw meat, and parasitic zoonosis prevention program establishment are recommended.
