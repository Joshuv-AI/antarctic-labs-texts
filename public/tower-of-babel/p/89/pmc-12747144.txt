Introduction

Indian society is strongly patriarchal and male-dominated. A vast majority of rural women have limited access to education, health care, and financial resources, and their contributions are frequently undervalued. Women from marginalized communities are even more downtrodden and struggle with oppressive cultural norms and a low social status. This is despite constitutional rights which guarantee equality and dignity to women.

About 75% of Indian rural households keep livestock. More than 80% of the livestock are reared by resource-poor farmers and landless laborers (Vijayalakshmy et al., 2023). There is high livestock mortality and sickness due to lack of awareness and scarce availability of preventive veterinary care (Kumar et al., 2021). Women play a major role in caring for livestock. Since the early 2000s, many agencies and projects across India have started to build the capacity of village women to provide preventive health services to small ruminants, poultry, and pigs. They came to be called pashu sakhis (women friends of livestock). They are trained community animal health workers who provide fee-based services such as vaccinations, deworming, ecto-parasite control, first-aid, guidance on feeding and breeding practices, and castration of male goat kids to be raised for meat production. The pashu sakhi model became a well-established and impactful approach to livestock extension services in India (Nimbalkar et al., 2025). This model has resulted in better livestock health and productivity and also improved the social and economic status of pashu sakhis (Leitch et al., 2020). There are possibly more than 20,000 pashu sakhis in many states of India, for example, over 6,000 pashu sakhis in Bihar state and another 6,000 in Jharkhand state. The Agriculture Skill Council of India offers certification for pashu sakhis, aiming to standardize community-based livestock service providers.

This paper discusses how pashu sakhis contribute to the community impact arising from improvements in goat productivity and how there is an opportunity to deploy community-based breeding programs (CBBP, Figure 1) to lift the genetic potential and performance of local goat populations.

Background

More than 85% of Bihar’s population of 105 million live in rural areas, and 34% of those live below the poverty line. About 14.8% of Rajasthan’s population of 69 million people live below the poverty line, and most of the poor live in the tribal southern Aravali hills region. Goats are valuable assets, and goat rearing is one of the main sources of income, along with agriculture and wage labor. Local goats are resilient to heat, disease, and nutritional stresses due to seasonal climatic variations. In Bihar, the highly prolific Black Bengal goats are reared, while in Rajasthan and Gujarat, mainly local goats and some Sirohi breed goats are reared. Recent community engagement revealed that goat rearers aspire to having more productive goats, but there is no planned, systematic breeding. Nor was there any identification of goats or measurement of performance, or recording of their data. In Bihar, goats would mostly be mated by one or two roaming bucks in each village, let loose as a customary ritual. In Rajasthan and Gujarat, breeding bucks were maintained, including Sirohi bucks introduced from outside, but there were several other bucks in the village, and due to flocks grazing together, there was mostly indiscriminate mating.

Introduction of Selection for Genetic Improvement in Two Projects

The Aga Khan Foundation has been implementing a community-based project called Project Mesha in Bihar since 2016 for women’s empowerment by improving goat production. A cader of trained pashu sakhis provides services to goat rearers. Typical pashu sakhis were 30 to 40 yr old, and each one catered to about 160 households owning about 300 goats in total. Over 80% of pashu sakhis working with Project Mesha belonged to households living on less than US$3.2 per person per day, and more than 30% were earning money for the first time.

During the last decade, the Centre for MicroFinance (CmF) and Collectives for Integrated Livelihood Initiatives (CInI) strengthened goat rearing in the Aravalis of Rajasthan and Gujarat, respectively, by promoting improved goat rearing practices through pashu sakhis.

Project Mesha and the project “Climate change mitigation initiative managed and led by women farmers” (CCMIM-LWF), implemented by CmF and CInI started in 2024, stand out for implementing scientific CBBP for genetic improvement of local goats through data recording done by pashu sakhis using the Dtreo cloud-based platform (https://dtreo.io). The authors of this paper have been associated with these projects as consultants for the goat CBBP. There were women’s farmer producer companies established and operating successfully in CCMIM-LWF project areas, while the Mesha women goat rearers’ producer company was registered in 2024. The concept of selecting from among women goat keepers’ locally adapted goats instead of bringing breeding bucks from outside without any knowledge of their or their progeny’s performance was introduced. Scientists and project workers gained an understanding of which traits of their goats the women expected to be improved and which improvements would increase their incomes. Weight/growth rate, milk yield, twinning (but not triplets or higher litter sizes in Bihar), and adaptation to local conditions or resilience were the preferred traits. Accordingly, the Dtreo platform was set up in English and Hindi languages (for Bihar and Rajasthan) and Gujarati (for Gujarat). Dtreo has an events-based recording structure, and some of the events were adding new goat data, kid and mature doe weighing and measurements, and assessing the health and body condition of a doe or a buck. In Project Mesha, Dtreo was programmed to provide a selection merit index score for each young male kid to guide the decision of which kids to select as potential breeding bucks. This index score was designed to identify fast-growing male kids from desirable healthy does with region-appropriate litter sizes and a history of satisfactory kid survival. The process of testing and setting up a selection merit score is going on in CCMIM-LWF.

The authors and their colleagues gave training to pashu sakhis on the CBBP rationale, the meaning of genetic improvement, and the need to record the performance of goats for their genetic evaluation. They were trained to put ear tags on goats, weigh and measure goats, and enter these data into the Dtreo mobile App. Most importantly, they were trained to castrate bucks not required for breeding using a “baby” Burdizzo castrator (see infographic).

Selected breeding bucks in Project Mesha were placed in villages at least 20 km away from the buck’s village of origin and were rotated among villages every year to avoid the risk of inbreeding.

Impact

In an impact study commissioned by Project Mesha in 2021, over 80% of the 139 pashu sakhis interviewed reported improved self-confidence and quality of life. Top improvements included higher income and savings (a 65% increase in earnings on average), better ability to cover household expenses such as food for the family, and higher status within their communities (being recognized everywhere for their work). Almost half were earning more because of working as a pashu sakhi, and a third said this occupation helped them cope with the COVID-19 pandemic. The authors have observed that pashu sakhis learn new skills such as ear tagging and weighing goats, and data entry into the mobile App quickly, and enjoy doing challenging work. They have also imbibed the principles of genetic improvement.

In Project Mesha, goat rearers and pashu sakhis were initially opposed to the idea of maintaining bucks for breeding, but that resistance was overcome when they found out that offering the services of the bucks to female goats on estrus was a good source of income. Placement of selected bucks increased gradually, and now more than 180 selected breeding bucks placed with buck keepers are offering breeding services to does in over 100 villages. Abeykoon et al. (2025) showed that progeny of selected breeding bucks in Project Mesha had a significantly higher average daily gain (13.6%) up to 100 days’ age compared to progeny of free-roaming bucks. Initially, bucks weighing more than 9 kg at 100 d were given the highest score in the calculation of the overall selection merit score. Over 7 yr from 2018 to 2024, there were about 300 bucks with the highest score out of about 1,200 bucks weighed. After 6 yr of use of selected bucks for breeding, the highest 100-d weight of bucks is now 12 kg.

Conclusions

Effective CBBPs can be developed by building the capacity of local, poorly educated women (over 250 pashu sakhis in Project Mesha) to collect and record data on goats using the Dtreo platform. CBBPs can be powerful means of improving goat productivity, raising incomes of goat keepers (about 50,000 households in Project Mesha in Bihar), and also conserving indigenous goat breeds such as the Black Bengal.

About the Authors

Chanda Nimbkar has a PhD in animal breeding and has led small ruminant research for the last 30 yr at the Nimbkar Agricultural Research Institute in Maharashtra state of India. Her focus is on developing, implementing, and improving practical strategies for genetic improvement of sheep and goats reared by smallholders. She has 20 peer-reviewed and 50 other publications to her credit. Under her leadership, NARI has developed the internationally famous prolific NARI Suwarna sheep breed that carries the FecB gene for prolificacy, yields about 50% higher profit, and has become popular among sheep rearers in South India. Chanda has worked on the boards and advisory committees of a range of national and international institutions, including the Indian Council of Agricultural Research and the International Livestock Research Institute. She is one of the 75 Indian women scientists honored by the Government of India in the book Vigyan vidushi published in 2023 on 75 yr of Indian independence.

Peter Amer is the Group Managing Director at AbacusBio Limited (Dunedin, New Zealand) with a strong interest in the economics and design of breeding programs. He has published extensively in the area of breeding objectives, and economic selection indexes developed under his leadership are widely used around the world. The challenge of achieving genetic progress, which will benefit the most disadvantaged global communities, is also of strong interest. He believes that collaboration and consultation are fundamental to the adoption and impact of both multi-trait selection indexes and community-based breeding programs.
