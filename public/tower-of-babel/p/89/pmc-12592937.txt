Firsekibart as a Prophylactic Treatment for Acute Gout Flare in Participants Initiating Urate‐Lowering Therapy: A Phase 2, Randomized, Open‐Label, Multicenter, Active‐Controlled Trial
Authors: Yiyun Yu, Yu Xue, Jiankang Hu, Ning Zhang, Rongbin Li, Hongtao Guo, Long Qian, Wei Gou, Jing Yang, Li Mao, Jing Yu, Shengyun Liu, Qian Xu, Tianhong Luo, Hejian Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of firsekibart versus colchicine for prophylaxis against acute gout flares in participants initiating urate‐lowering therapy (ULT).MethodsIn this randomized, multicenter, open‐label, active‐controlled, phase 2 trial, participants were randomized (1:1:1) to receive a single subcutaneous injection of firsekibart 100 mg or 200 mg or oral colchicine 0.5 mg/day for 12 weeks. ULT was initiated at baseline or within 1 week before screening. The primary endpoint was the mean number of acute gout flares per participant over 12 weeks.ResultsA total of 162 participants received firsekibart 100 mg (n = 55), 200 mg (n = 52), or colchicine (n = 55). Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups. No flares were observed in the firsekibart 200 mg group within 12 weeks. The adjusted mean number of acute gout flares per participant was 0.02 with firsekibart 100 mg and 0.34 with colchicine, with a rate ratio of 0.05 (95% confidence interval 0.01–0.43; P = 0.0060). Both treatment groups receiving firsekibart (at either dose) had a lower proportion of patients with at least one acute gout flare than the colchicine group. Firsekibart had a favorable safety profile, with no treatment‐emergent adverse events (AEs) leading to treatment discontinuation/study withdrawal, no treatment‐related serious AEs, and no deaths.ConclusionFirsekibart provided superior prophylaxis against acute gout flares compared with colchicine in patients initiating ULT, with a favorable tolerability profile, supporting its potential as a treatment option for patients with gout initiating ULT.

INTRODUCTION

Gout is a common inflammatory arthritis characterized by recurrent acute attacks of joint inflammation and pain interspersed with symptom‐free periods. 1 The prevalence of gout is rising globally, with an estimated 55.8 million people affected in 2020, and projections estimating an increase to 95.8 million by 2025. 2 In China, more than 16.7 million people have gout (2021), 3 another common metabolic disease after diabetes mellitus. 4 Gout is caused by hyperuricemia, defined as an elevated serum urate concentration of ≥6.8 mg/dL, leading to the formation of proinflammatory monosodium urate crystals in the joints. 5 , 6 Without proper treatment, gouty arthritis can progress to chronic tophaceous gout, resulting in joint damage and deformities. 2 , 5 , 6

Gout is associated with a substantial clinical burden, often linked to premature mortality and cardiometabolic‐renal comorbidities, including metabolic syndrome, obesity, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease, as well as reduced health‐related quality of life. 2 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 The standard of care for patients with gout includes treat‐to‐target urate‐lowering therapy (ULT) to maintain long‐term control of serum uric acid levels, alongside lifestyle changes. 10 , 11 However, ULT initiation can trigger recurrent acute gout flares, given that fluctuations in serum uric acid levels may cause the dissolution of urate crystals in joints and surrounding soft tissue. 12 , 13 Current Chinese guidelines recommend low‐dose colchicine or nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) as the treatment options for acute gout flare in patients initiating ULT. 14 If these treatments are ineffective or contraindicated, glucocorticoids are recommended, especially in patients with renal insufficiency. 14 Given that colchicine, NSAIDs, and glucocorticoids are all associated with a range of side effects, 14 there remains an unmet clinical need for alternative effective and well‐tolerated treatments as prophylaxis against acute gout flares in patients with gouty arthritis initiating ULT.

Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) is a proinflammatory cytokine that plays a key role in the pathogenesis of gout. 15 Inhibiting IL‐1β has been shown to reduce inflammation, pain, frequency of acute gout flares, and risk of recurrence in patients with gout. 16 Hence, international guidelines recommend IL‐1 inhibitors for the treatment of gout flares when standard therapies provide inadequate efficacy or are contraindicated. 14 , 17 , 18 Canakinumab is the first and only fully human monoclonal antibody targeting IL‐1β that has been approved by European Medicines Agency in 2013 and the US Food and Drug Administration in 2023 as a second‐line therapy for the treatment of acute flares of gouty arthritis. 19 , 20 However, canakinumab has not yet been approved in China.

Firsekibart, formerly known as genakumab, is similarly a high‐affinity, fully human anti–IL‐1β monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to IL‐1β, blocking the IL‐1β/IL‐1 receptor interaction. 21 Firsekibart is an IgG4/λ isotype, whereas canakinumab is IgG1/κ. IgG4 antibodies are generally associated with reduced antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity and complement‐dependent cytotoxicity compared with IgG1, which may potentially lead to fewer inflammatory adverse events (AEs). Both agents have shown similar half‐lives and low immunogenicity in clinical studies. Firsekibart was administered as a single dose of 100 mg or 200 mg in the current phase 2 study, whereas in phase 2 prophylaxis trials, canakinumab has been evaluated at doses ranging from 25 mg to 300 mg, administered subcutaneously. Preclinical models suggest firsekibart has a higher binding affinity to human IL‐1β and enhanced neutralization activity (unpublished data).

In a first‐in‐human phase 1 study (NCT04337437), favorable tolerability and safety profiles were established for firsekibart in healthy Chinese participants receiving single doses of up to 6.0 mg/kg (data on file). In the pivotal phase 3 GUARD‐1 study (NCT05983445), firsekibart provided noninferior short‐term pain relief and significantly superior long‐term prevention of gout flares compared with compound betamethasone in Chinese adults with gout for whom colchicine and/or NSAIDs were contraindicated, ineffective, or not tolerated. 21 , 22 , 23 Furthermore, in a recent phase 2 trial (NCT05936268), first‐line administration of firsekibart 200 mg achieved rapid and sustained pain relief and reduced the risk of gout flare recurrence compared with etoricoxib and was well tolerated in Chinese patients experiencing acute gout flare. 24 Here, we investigate the efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity of subcutaneous firsekibart injection compared with colchicine as prophylaxis against acute gout flares in patients with gout initiating ULT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and treatment

This randomized, open‐label, active‐controlled phase 2 trial (NCT05936281) was conducted at 41 centers in China. The study included a 1‐week screening period followed by a 12‐week treatment period. Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive a single subcutaneous injection of firsekibart 100 mg or 200 mg or oral colchicine 0.5 mg/day for 12 weeks. Randomization was performed using stratified block randomization based on serum uric acid levels (<480 or ≥480 μmol/L).

ULT was initiated at baseline or within 1 week before screening and was administered to all randomized participants for 12 weeks. The available ULT options included allopurinol 100 mg/day, febuxostat 40 mg/day, or benzbromarone 50 mg/day, with dose adjustments made at the investigator's discretion. Dose adjustment, addition, or switching was permitted based on investigator judgment in cases of poor efficacy, tolerability issues, or urate control. No ULT use restrictions were imposed during screening or treatment. If laboratory abnormalities emerged, including changes in liver or kidney function, investigators were instructed to adjust ULT, or prophylactic colchicine, based on their clinical judgment. Rescue therapy was permitted for participants experiencing unbearable pain during acute gout flares, with NSAIDs as the preferred option, for ≤7 days. If NSAIDs were contraindicated or intolerable or did not adequately relieve pain, alternative options included oral prednisone ≤30 mg/day or methylprednisolone ≤24 mg/day. Other medications for gout flare prevention or treatment were not allowed during the study. Tophus assessment could be based on current physical examination or prior or current computed tomography or ultrasound findings suggestive of tophus.

This study was conducted in accordance with international ethics guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki, and complied with Good Clinical Practice standards. The study protocol was approved by an independent ethics review board before initiation, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18–75 years who had a body mass index (BMI) ≤40 kg/m2, had a diagnosis of gouty arthritis as defined by the American College of Rheumatology 2015 gout classification criteria, 25 experienced at least two acute gout attacks within 1 year before screening, and were willing to initiate ULT or had initiated ULT within 1 week before screening. Participants were excluded from the study if they had a history of hypersensitivity to the study drugs or similar compounds or if they had contraindications or intolerance to colchicine. Other exclusion criteria included occurrence of an acute gout flare within 2 weeks of screening; active hemorrhagic disorder or serious bleeding tendencies, such as hemophilia; use of aspirin or heparin as anticoagulation therapy; secondary gout, excluding cases associated with impaired renal function; or a diagnosis or suspicion of rheumatoid arthritis or infection/septic arthritis. The full inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Exhibit S1.

Study endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint was the mean number of acute gout flares per participant over 12 weeks. An acute gout flare was defined as the occurrence of at least three of the following criteria: (1) patient‐reported flare, (2) patient‐reported swelling in any joint, (3) patient‐reported increased skin temperature in any joint, and/or (4) patient‐reported pain at rest score of 30.0 mm (0–100 mm visual analog scale [VAS]). 26 The joint exhibiting clinical symptoms such as pain, swelling, and increased skin temperature was considered the affected joint. During a gout flare, participants were required to identify the affected joint and perform a pain VAS assessment on it. Secondary efficacy endpoints included the proportion of participants experiencing at least one flare over 12 weeks, the time from randomization to first flare, and the mean duration of gout flares per participant in each treatment group.

Safety analysis included AEs, laboratory tests, electrocardiogram (EKG), vital signs, and physical examination. Treatment‐emergent AEs (TEAEs), treatment‐related AEs (TRAEs), serious AEs (SAEs), and AEs leading to treatment discontinuation, study withdrawal, or death were summarized by group. TEAEs and TRAEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary of Regulatory Activities version 26.0 by System Organ Class (SOC) and Preferred Term. Participants attended study visits at weeks 2, 4, 8, and 12 for efficacy and safety assessments.

Immunogenicity was assessed based on the incidence of antidrug antibodies (ADAs). For participants who tested positive for ADAs, further assessment for neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) was performed. Blood samples were collected before firsekibart injection and at weeks 4, 8, and 12 as well as at premature withdrawal, if applicable.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated based on the efficacy of firsekibart in reducing the mean number of acute gout flares over 12 weeks using PASS 2021 software. Assuming that treatment with colchicine would result in 0.55 acute gout flares per participant over 12 weeks and firsekibart would have a rate ratio of 0.28 compared with colchicine, with a one‐sided alpha of 0.025 and 80% power, the required sample size was calculated to be 50 participants per group, for a total of 150 participants across three groups, accounting for dropout rates.

Efficacy endpoints were assessed in the full analysis set (FAS), which included all randomized participants who received at least one dose of the study drug and had at least one postdose efficacy evaluation. Safety outcomes were assessed in the safety set (SS), including all participants who received at least one dose of the study drug and had a safety assessment. Immunogenicity was evaluated in the immunogenicity set, consisting of all participants who received firsekibart and had pre‐ and postdose immunogenicity data.

The primary endpoint was analyzed using a negative binomial regression model, with the randomization stratification factor as a covariate, to estimate the rate ratio and 95% confidence interval (CI) of firsekibart versus colchicine. A post hoc Wilcoxon rank sum test was conducted for the comparison with colchicine because no flares occurred in the firsekibart 200 mg group. The proportion of participants with at least one acute gout flare within 12 weeks was summarized using descriptive statistics, with the 95% CI for treatment difference (firsekibart vs colchicine) calculated using the Newcombe‐Wilson score method. Time from randomization to first acute gout flare in each treatment group was analyzed using the Kaplan‐Meier method, and a Cox proportional hazard model adjusted for stratification factor at randomization except for treatment groups was used to estimate the corresponding hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI. Safety and immunogenicity outcomes were summarized descriptively. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4 or later).

RESULTS

Participants

Between July 20, 2023, and January 19, 2024, a total of 223 participants were screened, and 165 were randomized. Of these, 162 participants received firsekibart 100 mg (n = 55), firsekibart 200 mg (n = 52), or colchicine 0.5 mg/day (n = 55). A total of 160 participants completed the study (Figure 1). All 162 participants who received treatment were included in the FAS and SS. Of these, 107 participants who had received firsekibart were included in the immunogenicity set.

Trial profile.

Baseline demographics and disease characteristics were comparable across the treatment groups (Table 1). All participants were male. In the firsekibart 100 mg and 200 mg groups, the mean ± standard deviation (SD) age was 39.0 ± 12.7 and 42.5 ± 12.7 years with a mean ± SD BMI of 27.4 ± 3.5 and 26.9 ± 3.7 kg/m2, respectively. In the colchicine group, the mean ± SD age was 41.0 ± 9.3 years, with a mean ± SD BMI of 27.3 ± 4.3 kg/m2. The duration of gouty arthritis was 62.0, 65.0, and 73.1 months in the firsekibart 100 mg, 200 mg, and colchicine groups, respectively. Across the treatment groups, most participants (≥80%) reported experiencing two to five acute gout flares during the preceding year.

The use of prior and concomitant medication was comparable across treatment groups. Overall, 29.0% (n = 47) of participants had previously received anti‐inflammatory or antirheumatic medications, and 10.5% (n = 17) had a history of antigout treatment. Regarding prior ULT, 55.6% (n = 90) of participants had previously used ULT, including febuxostat (47.5%, n = 77), benzbromarone (11.1%, n = 18), uric acid production inhibitors (1.2%, n = 2), and allopurinol (0.6%, n = 1). Concomitant medication was used by 50.0% (n = 81) of participants, with lipid‐lowering agents being the most common (17.9%, n = 29).

Efficacy

Of the 162 participants in the FAS, 1.8% (n = 1) of participants receiving firsekibart 100 mg experienced one acute gout flare, whereas no participants in the firsekibart 200 mg group had a flare within 12 weeks (Table 2). In contrast, 21.8% (n = 12) of participants receiving colchicine had at least one acute gout flare, of whom 14.5% (n = 8), 5.5% (n = 3), and 1.8% (n = 1) experienced one, two, and five flares, respectively.

Analysis of the primary endpoint showed that the adjusted mean number of acute gout flares per participant within 12 weeks was lower in the firsekibart groups (0 for the 200 mg group, and 0.02 for 100 mg group) compared with the colchicine group (0.34) (Table 2). Based on the negative binomial regression model, a single injection of firsekibart 100 mg significantly reduced the number of acute gout flares per participant over 12 weeks versus daily colchicine, with a rate ratio of 0.05 (95% CI 0.01–0.43; P = 0.0060) (Table 2). Because no acute gout flares were reported in participants receiving firsekibart 200 mg, the model could not provide a stable estimate of the SE or CI for the rate comparison with the colchicine group; the P value of the Wilcoxon rank sum test was 0.0004.

Additionally, the proportion of participants in the FAS experiencing at least one acute gout flare over 12 weeks and the duration of flare was numerically lower in both the firsekibart 100 mg and 200 mg groups compared with colchicine (Table 3). The difference in the proportion of participants with at least one acute gout flare over 12 weeks between firsekibart 100 mg and colchicine was −20.0% (95% CI −31.5% to −8.5%), and between firsekibart 200 mg and colchicine the difference was −21.8% (95% CI −32.7% to −10.9%).

The median time from randomization to first acute gout flare was not reached in any treatment group within 12 weeks. In the firsekibart 100 mg group, only one participant had an acute gout flare, which occurred 23 days postdose and lasted for 2 days, resulting in flare rates of 1.8% at weeks 4, 8, and 12. The affected joints were the right metacarpophalangeal joint (VAS 49.0 mm) and the left metacarpophalangeal joint (VAS 13.0 mm), both presenting with swelling and increased skin temperature. In comparison, 21.8% (n = 12) of participants receiving colchicine experienced at least one acute gout flare, with flare rates of 18.3% at weeks 4 and 8 and 22.1% at week 12, and a mean duration of 11.6 days.

Based on the Cox proportional hazards model, the HR for experiencing a gout flare with firsekibart 100 mg versus colchicine was 0.07 (95% CI 0.01–0.57), indicating a reduction in risk of acute gout flare. As no acute gout flares were observed in the firsekibart 200 mg group, the HR and 95% CI for firsekibart 200 mg versus colchicine could not be estimated.

Safety

Among the 162 participants in the SS, the incidence of TEAEs was 80.0% (n = 44), 76.9% (n = 40), and 76.4% (n = 42) in the firsekibart 100 mg, firsekibart 200 mg, and colchicine groups, respectively (Table 4). All TEAEs with an incidence of >5% are listed in Table S1. Most TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity (Common Terminology Criteria for AEs grade 1–2), and there was no evidence of a dose response for any AE. The proportion of participants experiencing grade ≥3 TEAEs was comparable between those receiving firsekibart and colchicine, with rates of 17.8% (n = 19) in the combined firsekibart groups and 16.4% (n = 9) in the colchicine group (Table 4). In the colchicine group, two participants (3.6%) experienced TEAEs that led to temporary suspension of the study drug because of diarrhea, which was deemed to be treatment‐related by the investigator (Table 4).

TRAEs occurred in 52.7% (n = 29), 46.2% (n = 24), and 41.8% (n = 23) of participants receiving firsekibart 100 mg, firsekibart 200 mg, and colchicine, respectively, with grade ≥3 TRAEs occurring in 12.7% (n = 7), 5.8% (n = 3), and 9.1% (n = 5) of participants in the respective groups (Table 4). The most common TRAEs (incidence ≥5% in any group) included hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase, upper respiratory tract infection, increased gamma‐glutamyl transferase, and increased blood creatinine (Table 4). In addition, injection site reactions were reported in one participant (0.9%) in the firsekibart group. No AEs led to treatment discontinuation or study withdrawal, and no deaths were reported during the study.

Overall, two participants (1.2%) experienced four SAEs. In the firsekibart 200 mg group, one participant (1.9%) experienced one SAE (a femoral fracture), which was unrelated to the study drug and resolved with recovery. In the colchicine group, one participant (1.8%) experienced three SAEs, including contusion and two fractures, all of which improved or resolved and were unrelated to treatment. Furthermore, laboratory tests, EKG assessments, vital signs tests, and physical examinations did not reveal any safety concerns.

Immunogenicity

Of the 55 participants receiving firsekibart 100 mg, 1.8% (n = 1) of individuals were positive for ADAs before treatment and remained positive following firsekibart injection; 10.9% (n = 6) of participants were positive for ADAs following treatment, with 1:640 being the highest titer reported. However, the participant in the firsekibart 100 mg group who experienced a gout flare tested negative for ADAs at all time points. In the firsekibart 200 mg group, 5.8% (n = 3) of participants were positive for ADAs before treatment and all were negative after treatment; 19.2% (n = 10) of participants were positive for ADAs after treatment, with 1:80 being the highest titer of ADAs recorded. Among the participants who tested positive for ADAs, all were negative for NAbs. Notably, no safety events were associated with ADA positivity.

DISCUSSION

Despite available treatment options, there remains an unmet need in the management of gout flares. A key strength of this randomized study was the inclusion of an active comparator group, allowing a direct evaluation of firsekibart against standard treatment. In the US, colchicine 0.6 mg is the standard formulation for gout, with the 0.5 mg dosage only recently approved in 2024 for nongout indications. Colchicine at doses of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/day is recommended for flare prophylaxis in patients initiating ULT by both the European League Against Rheumatism 17 and the British Society for Rheumatology, 27 as well as the 2019 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hyperuricemia and Gout. 28 The latter specifically advises a low dose (0.5–1.0 mg/day) for at least 3 to 6 months during the initial phase of ULT. 28 Similarly, a previous study evaluating canakinumab for flare prophylaxis in patients starting ULT used colchicine 0.5 mg/day for 16 weeks. 29

Based on clinical experience and existing evidence suggesting that lower doses are better tolerated, particularly by reducing the incidence of diarrhea, 30 our study selected a colchicine dose of 0.5 mg/day to optimize the balance between efficacy and safety. In addition, >90% of the participants in our study received febuxostat as ULT, which reflects the current clinical practice in China. 31 , 32 Notably, there is a high prevalence (10–20%) of the HLA‐B*58:01 allele in the Han Chinese population. This allele has been associated with an increased risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions induced by allopurinol, which may have contributed to the low use of allopurinol in our study. 28 The pivotal phase 3 studies that supported the regulatory approval of febuxostat (40, 80, and 120 mg) in the US and Europe include the FACT, 33 APEX, 34 and CONFIRMS 35 trials, which have all demonstrated the superior efficacy of febuxostat versus allopurinol in lowering serum urate levels in patients with gout.

In this study, a single dose of firsekibart significantly outperformed daily colchicine in preventing acute gout flares in participants initiating ULT while maintaining a favorable safety profile. Over the 12‐week study period, a single subcutaneous injection of firsekibart 100 mg or 200 mg significantly reduced the number of acute gout flares and shortened the duration of flares, demonstrating a marked advantage over daily colchicine. Notably, no gout flares occurred in the firsekibart 200 mg group throughout the study, highlighting its potential as an effective prophylactic agent for patients initiating ULT.

This study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of firsekibart for patients with gout, consistent with findings from previous clinical trials. Firsekibart has been shown to be effective in Chinese adults with acute gout flare in both the first 24 and second‐line settings. 21 , 22 , 23 Moreover, we observed that firsekibart exhibited a similar trend to canakinumab in reducing the mean number of flares when compared with standard treatment (both colchicine), despite differences in endpoint evaluation periods (12 weeks vs 16 weeks). This comparison is based on a previous 16‐week trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of canakinumab in a similar patient population in which canakinumab across different dose groups reduced the mean number of flares per participant (rate ratio: 0.28–0.38; P ≤ 0.0083) and proportion of participants experiencing at least one flare (15%–27% vs 44%; P < 0.05) compared with colchicine. 29 However, caution is warranted when making cross‐trial comparisons because of potential differences in patient populations and procedures.

In the present study, firsekibart was well tolerated, with no new safety signals reported, consistent with previous studies. 22 In general, the safety profile of firsekibart, including the incidence of TEAEs and TRAEs, was comparable to colchicine 0.5 mg/day. Given the critical role of IL‐1β in the immune system, concerns have been raised about the potential risk of infections or immune‐related AEs following IL‐1β inhibition. However, in this study, the incidence of TEAEs in the infections and infestations SOC among participants receiving firsekibart was relatively low (15.9%; n = 17), comparable to those receiving colchicine (16.4%, n = 9). Furthermore, the incidence of upper respiratory tract infection was comparable between the firsekibart (10.3%, n = 11) and colchicine groups (9.1%, n = 5), suggesting that firsekibart does not increase the risk of infections compared with colchicine, which is an important consideration for clinical use. The incidence of upper respiratory tract infections in our study was higher than in a previous trial of canakinumab, in which the reported rate was 3.7% in both the canakinumab and colchicine groups. 29 Notably, the colchicine group in our study also had a higher incidence of upper respiratory tract infections. However, within corresponding treatment groups across studies, the reported rates remained generally consistent. One possible explanation for these differences in respiratory infection rates is the timing of the studies. The canakinumab trial was conducted earlier, from 2008 to 2009, 29 whereas our study was conducted between 2023 and early 2024. Since the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, there has been an overall increase in respiratory infections in China, including influenza and COVID‐19, 36 , 37 which may have contributed to the higher upper respiratory tract infections rates observed in our study.

In all treatment groups, the TEAEs with the highest incidence were metabolic and nutritional disorders, including hypertriglyceridemia and hypercholesterolemia. The relatively high incidence of these AEs may be related to underlying lipid metabolism disorders in the study population. 38 , 39 The overall incidence of increased ALT was slightly higher in participants receiving firsekibart 100 mg or 200 mg (16.8%; n = 18) compared with colchicine (12.7%; n = 7). This may be associated with concomitant ULT therapy, as most participants in this study (approximately 90%) received febuxostat, a commonly used ULT known to be associated with liver enzyme elevation. 40 It is also notable that, in our study, ADAs were low‐titer and nonneutralizing, with no clinical impact on efficacy or safety. A low incidence of NAbs, which are considered to have minimal clinical consequences, has been observed with canakinumab. 29

Given that colchicine is advised to be used for 3 to 6 months in clinical practice for flare prophylaxis 28 and that generally sustained urate‐lowering and tophus mobilization typically require a similar timeframe, the relatively short follow‐up duration of this study (12 weeks) limits the assessment of long‐term safety profiles or sustained efficacy. Future trials should evaluate the efficacy of firsekibart in preventing acute gout flares during the early phase of ULT initiation over an extended observation period. In addition, as all participants enrolled in this study were Chinese, the generalizability of the findings to a global population remains uncertain. Further studies are needed to investigate the safety and efficacy of firsekibart in diverse populations, including female patients (recognizing that gout is more prevalent in men than in women), 41 and individuals with different genetic predispositions or comorbidities. Another limitation of the study is that, although potentially challenging because of the natures of the respective treatments (single‐dose injection vs daily oral colchicine), this was an open‐label trial and so blinding was not implemented. Furthermore, it could be beneficial for future studies to include more intensive ULT therapies and to monitor ongoing serum urate levels in participants while tracking specific reasons for any changes in ULT dose.

Gout is a highly prevalent disease with a rising global burden and is often associated with significant comorbidities. The findings in this study suggest that firsekibart may serve as a novel strategy for preventing acute gout flares in patients initiating ULT, potentially transforming the management paradigm for gout.
