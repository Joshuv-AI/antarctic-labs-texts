Throwers oblique rotator cuff (TORC) MRI view of the shoulder improves reader confidence for internal impingement abnormalities in overhead throwing athletes
Authors: Lea E McDaniel, Midhat Patel, Rafael A Buerba, Anup A Shah, Evan S Lederman, Pamela Lund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe thrower’s oblique rotator cuff (TORC) view was developed to more optimally visualize the commonly injured posterosuperior rotator cuff and capsulolabral structures in overhead athletes. The purpose is to evaluate whether the addition of the TORC view to standard MRI sequences improves the diagnostic evaluation of rotator cuff pathology and labral abnormalities in throwing athletes. Shoulder MRIs of MLB pitchers ordered by team physicians from 1/1/2019 to 12/31/2021 were retrospectively reviewed. The inclusion criteria were standard and TORC MRI sequences per individual player and players aged 18–35. The MRIs were read by a musculoskeletal radiologist and two orthopedic surgeons with and without the addition of the TORC plane. The TORC view resulted in minor redistribution of supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and labral classifications. Signal size measurements differed significantly between standard and TORC views at the individual reader level (p = 0.042, p = 0.003, and p < 0.001), with generally larger measurements on the TORC view; however, no significant difference was observed when measurements were pooled across readers (median 17.0 mm; p = 0.197). Inter-reader reliability for signal size improved with the TORC view (ICC 0.47 → 0.73). Overall diagnostic confidence increased significantly for two of three readers with the TORC view (median paired increase, 1.0 point; p < 0.001), while no significant change was observed for the third reader. The addition of the TORC plane was associated with improved reader confidence and inter-reader reliability for quantitative signal size assessment without introducing systematic changes in categorical tendon or labral classification. These findings support the use of the TORC sequence as an adjunct to standard shoulder MRI protocols to enhance interpretive consistency and confidence, particularly in elite overhead throwing athletes.Keywords: Professional baseball players, Overhead athletes, TORC MRI viewSubject terms: Anatomy, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Shoulder injuries are a significant concern among overhead throwing athletes, particularly in pitchers. Their ability to generate and maintain high performance throwing through adaptive changes relies on meticulous biomechanics, often stressing the normal glenohumeral joint. These adaptive changes can cause injuries, leading to loss of velocity and accuracy, durability, and long-term functional impairment.Many overhead throwing athletes demonstrate varying degrees of anterior glenohumeral laxity and eventual posterior capsular contracture to permit the hyper-external rotation required during throwing. Over time, this repetitive motion is believed to result in repetitive microtrauma forcing the humeral head to translate posterior and superior with respect to the glenoid. This new “humeral setpoint” is believed to contribute to increased shear forces across the posterior capsulolabral complex.

The "peel-back" phenomenon observed in the throwing shoulder is a well described adaptive mechanism, wherein the biceps anchor, labrum, and rotator cuff undergo torsional stresses to accommodate the hyper-external rotation1. The repetitive, continued increased external rotation is believed to be a cause of superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) lesions. In addition, the posterior capsular contracture resulting in glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD) and predisposes these athletes to internal impingement.

During annual spring training, baseline magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the throwing shoulder in professional baseball pitchers is performed. Multiple studies have described the utility of MRI as a mainstay for diagnosing shoulder pathology in throwing athletes2,3. and various studies have examined internal impingement on MRI specifically; however, the three traditional planes of a shoulder MRI may not optimally capture internal impingement abnormalities4–6 MR arthrogram and abduction external rotation (ABER) views lead to significant patient discomfort and increased scan time involved for repositioning and coil change7.

The authors have observed improvements in visualization of the posterior rotator cuff in internal impingement, increasing detail and confidence for diagnosing rotator cuff and labral abnormalities in shoulder MRI of elite throwing athletes utilizing a novel 45-degree oblique MRI plane orthogonal to the posterior rotator cuff, which we have termed the Throwers Oblique Rotator Cuff (TORC) plane.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate whether the addition of the thrower’s oblique rotator cuff (TORC) plane to standard magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences improves the diagnostic evaluation of rotator cuff pathology and labral abnormalities in throwing athletes. Our hypothesis is the addition of TORC plane MRI images enhances diagnostic confidence, inter-reader reliability, and consistency in measurements across multiple physicians compared to standard MRI sequences.

Methods

This is a retrospective review of shoulder MRIs of Major League Baseball (MLB) pitchers ordered by one team from 1/1/2019 to 12/31/2021. Inclusion criteria included MRIs ordered by two MLB teams, MRI sequence and TORC MRI planes available per individual player, and ages 18–35. Exclusion criteria included: MRIs ordered by teams not part of study, inadequate or absent TORC MRI plane, incomplete or non-diagnostic MRI study, MRIs from outside imaging centers, studies performed outside study range, and patients outside of age criteria. Only one MRI was reviewed per player. The MRIs were read by a musculoskeletal fellowship trained radiologist (over 30 years in practice) and one orthopedic sports trained surgeon (over 5 years in practice), and one dual sports and shoulder and elbow fellowship trained surgeon (over 15 years in practice) in a sequential fashion, first without and then with the addition of the TORC view with their confidence and impressions recorded. A calibration study with all readers was performed prior to the reads to standardize the grading. All MRI studies were performed on 3TGE (GE Healthcare 2024) or Siemens (Siemens Healthineers) equipment using dedicated shoulder coil 4 mm sliced thickness, no gap, 18–24 cm field of view, PDFS -TR 2800/TE 40 ms, 1-2NEX, 250-400 MATRIX size, select cases underwent AI enhancement with using SUBTLE MR TM post processing.

The TORC view was obtained during the standard thrower’s shoulder MRI protocol using 3 plane scout planner sequences and prescribing a 40–45-degree oblique proton density fat saturated (PDFS) sequence from posterosuperior to anteroinferior to obtain orthogonal images of the posterior rotator cuff and anterior-inferior labral capsular attachment approximating the 10–11:00 and 4–5:00 locations (right), respectively as seen in Fig. 1. No patient repositioning, coil change or repeat scout images are required, and the image acquisition time is approximately 2 min.

28-year-old pitcher for baseline participation MRI shows improved visualization posterior RTC tendinopathy with TORC plane. (A) Standard coronal PDFS MR view shows posterior rotator cuff tendinopathy vs. partial tear (green line), (B) sagittal 45 degree TORC planning angle (yellow lines) and (C) distinct intrasubstance tendinopathy infraspinatus tendon in TORC view (blue arrow).

A standardized reader form was completed by each reader for each MRI scan that included the assessment both standard (alone) and standard plus TORC view (together) read sequentially as seen in Fig. 2. The supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendon were evaluated for either tendinopathy or tear (T vs t) and level of confidence was recorded (0–5) for individual and overall findings. Criteria for partial and full thickness tear included a partial or full thickness fluid defect, intermediate to high signal fiber obliteration and contour abnormality, and tendon thinning greater than 50%. Signal size was noted for the supraspinatus and infraspinatus and the corresponding level of confidence was recorded. Signal size was determined based on maximum oblique transverse and oblique AP signal dimensions parallel or orthogonal to tendon fiber axis including one or more tendons on fluid sensitive proton dense fat suppression as shown in Fig. 3. The presence of a labral abnormality (Yes/No) was also recorded with the corresponding level of confidence. The overall confidence was also recorded (0–5). The same questionnaire was completed for the standard view and the standard + TORC plane.

Signal size was determined based on maximum oblique transverse and oblique AP signal dimensions parallel or orthogonal to tendon fiber axis including one or more tendons on fluid sensitive proton dense fat suppression.

Statistical analysis was performed using paired methods to account for within-reader comparisons between standard MRI sequences and standard plus TORC sequences. Overall diagnostic confidence was defined as the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes included signal size measurements, categorical tendon classification (tear, tendinopathy, normal), and labral abnormality detection and were analyzed as secondary, exploratory findings.

Paired continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical outcomes were analyzed descriptively. Inter-reader reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous variables and Fleiss’ kappa coefficient for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

There was no funding used for this manuscript. This was an institutional review board (IRB) approved study from University of Arizona Health Science (STUDY00001828) and was carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

When pooled across all readers, a greater number of rotator cuff tears were identified on the TORC view compared with the standard view (31 vs 23).

At the individual reader level, signal size measurements differed significantly between imaging protocols for Reader 1 (p = 0.042), Reader 2 (p = 0.003), and Reader 3 (p < 0.001), with most measurements demonstrating an increase in signal size on the TORC view. When signal size measurements were pooled across all readers, no statistically significant difference was observed between imaging protocols (p = 0.197).

Inter-reader reliability for signal size measurements improved with the addition of the TORC view, with the intraclass correlation coefficient increasing from 0.469 on the standard view to 0.726 on the TORC view.

For Reader 1, labral abnormalities were identified in 82.9% (29/35) of studies on the standard view and 80.0% (28/35) with the addition of the TORC view. For Reader 2, labral abnormalities were identified in 60.0% (21/35) of studies on the standard view and 65.7% (23/35) on the TORC view. For Reader 3, labral abnormalities were identified in 100% (35/35) of studies on the standard view and 97.1% (34/35) on the TORC view.

Across all readers, the addition of the TORC view resulted in only minor redistribution of labral abnormality classifications, without a consistent or dominant shift in detection.

Reader confidence scores were recorded on a 5-point scale and are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR).

On the standard view, median confidence levels were 4.0 (IQR, 3.5–4.5) for supraspinatus pathology, 4.0 (IQR, 3.0–4.5) for infraspinatus pathology, 5.0 (IQR, 4.0–5.0) for labral abnormalities, and 3.0 (IQR, 3.0–4.0) for signal size. With the addition of the TORC view, median confidence levels increased to 4.5 (IQR, 4.0–5.0) for supraspinatus pathology, 4.5 (IQR, 4.0–5.0) for infraspinatus pathology, 5.0 (IQR, 4.0–5.0) for labral abnormalities, and 3.5 (IQR, 3.0–4.0) for signal size.

Differences in confidence levels between imaging protocols were evaluated using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. For overall diagnostic confidence, Readers 2 and 3 demonstrated a statistically significant increase in confidence with the addition of the TORC view (median paired increase, 1.0 point; p < 0.001 for both readers), while Reader 1 demonstrated no statistically significant change (p = 0.414).

For individual assessments, Readers 2 and 3 demonstrated statistically significant increases in confidence for supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and labral pathology with the TORC view (all p < 0.01), while Reader 1 demonstrated no statistically significant change across structures.

Inter-reader reliability was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa coefficients for categorical variables and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for continuous measurements. For supraspinatus pathology, slight inter-reader agreement was observed on the standard view (κ = 0.13), with a similarly low level of agreement on the TORC view (κ = 0.14). For infraspinatus pathology, slight agreement was observed on the standard view (κ = 0.09), while agreement on the TORC view was negligible (κ = − 0.01). For labral abnormalities, agreement was slight on the standard view (κ = 0.13) and remained low on the TORC view (κ = 0.06).

Signal size measurements demonstrated moderate inter-reader reliability on the standard view (ICC = 0.47), which improved to good reliability with the addition of the TORC view (ICC = 0.73). ICC calculations were performed using studies in which all readers recorded a measurable signal size.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether the addition of the TORC plane improves diagnostic confidence, inter-reader reliability, and measurement consistency compared with standard shoulder MRI sequences. Across readers, the addition of the TORC view resulted in only minor redistribution of supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and labral classifications, without a consistent or dominant shift toward tear or tendinopathy designation. These findings suggest that the TORC sequence does not substantially alter categorical diagnostic assignments but may provide complementary information.

In contrast, diagnostic confidence and measurement reliability demonstrated more meaningful improvement with the addition of the TORC view. Overall diagnostic confidence increased significantly for two of three readers, with a median paired increase of one point on a five-point scale. Similarly, signal size measurements demonstrated improved inter-reader reliability, with the intraclass correlation coefficient increasing from moderate on standard imaging (ICC = 0.47) to good reliability with the TORC view (ICC = 0.73). These findings suggest that the TORC sequence enhances reader confidence and consistency in quantitative assessment, even in the absence of major changes in categorical classification.

Taken together, the addition of the TORC plane appears to improve confidence and measurement reliability without introducing systematic shifts in diagnostic categorization, supporting its potential role as an adjunct sequence in shoulder MRI interpretation.

The presence of rotator cuff abnormalities in overhead athletes has been previously studied. Miniaci et al. reported labral abnormalities in 79% of shoulders in 14 asymptomatic professional baseball players8. Connor et al.3 evaluated shoulder MRIs of asymptomatic elite overhead athletes over a five-year study. Rotator cuff tears, either partial or full-thickness, were present in 40% of the dominant shoulders but did not correlate with clinical symptoms.

Magnetic resonance arthrography (MRA) has been shown to provide additional value in detecting and classifying shoulder pathology compared to MRI, particularly when combined with ABER positioning. Studies have demonstrated that a 3 T-MRI can perform as well or better than MRA (Sheridan 2015). The addition of the ABER position enhances the detection and classification of labral lesions during MRA9. Shafiei et al.10 conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis concluding that combining MRA with ABER positioning improves accuracy for labral pathology compared to either technique alone.

Connor et al.3 highlighted false positives in 50 shoulder MRIs from 20 overhead athletes and 10 control subjects. They found a high incidence of rotator cuff abnormalities and Bennet’s lesions in dominant shoulders that remained asymptomatic at 5-years. They suggest that MRI alone is not sufficient to guide operative decisions. Jost et al.11 evaluated 50 MRIs of professional handball players (symptomatic and asymptomatic) and found that 93% of throwing shoulders had abnormal MRI findings, only 37% were symptomatic.

MRA offers high sensitivity and specificity for identifying both partial articular side rotator cuff tears and labral tears but is not always appropriate, particularly when assessing the asymptomatic athlete due to injection-related discomfort during active sports participation12. ABER positioning, routinely performed during MRA which was adapted from the apprehension test, improves visualization of the posterior RTC attachment and adjacent labrum. However, it may be poorly tolerated by overhead athletes due to discomfort associated with the prolonged arm positioning at > 90 degree of shoulder abduction and elbow hyperflexion required to accommodate the limited space of even wide bore MRI equipment7.

In contrast, we have observed improvements in anatomic depiction, increased number of MRI images and extended coverage along the areas of impingement, increased accuracy, and confidence for diagnosing internal impingement abnormalities in shoulder MRI of elite throwing athletes utilizing a novel 45-degree oblique MRI plane orthogonal to the posterior rotator cuff, which we have termed the Throwers Oblique Rotator Cuff (TORC) view.

Del Grande et al. used high resolution 3-T MRIs to study asymptomatic baseball pitcher draft picks, reporting rotator cuff tendinopathy in 68% and partial-thickness supraspinatus tears in 32% of participants13. Lesniak et al.2 analyzed preseason MRIs of 21 professional pitchers and their subsequent placement on the disabled list. They found rotator cuff tears in 11 players, SLAP tears in 10, and anterior or posterior labral tears in 13, but no significant relationship between MRI findings and time on the disabled list. Notably, pitchers with rotator cuff tears had a higher number of career innings pitched (1014 vs 729 innings).

This study evaluated the utility of adding the TORC view to baseline spring training imaging. Advantages include detailed diagnostic studies, greater reader confidence, avoidance of contrast (vs MRA) and uncomfortable positioning (vs ABER view). However, the study’s limitations include its retrospective nature, moderate sample size, limited to single MLB team, limited availability of TORC sequences, reliance on one musculoskeletal radiologist, and subjective confidence assessments.

Future considerations should include additional teams and players, direct comparisons of TORC plane, ABER positioning, and MRAs, as well inter- and intra-observer reliability assessments between radiologists and orthopedic surgeons.

The addition of the TORC plane to standard shoulder MRI in throwers improved diagnostic confidence and inter-reader reliability without patient repositioning or significant time penalty, and trended toward increase size of infraspinatus tears and larger rotator cuff abnormalities suggesting that TORC enhances confidence without compromising diagnostic consistency. The findings support incorporating the TORC plane into MRI protocols for evaluating rotator cuff and labral abnormalities, particularly in baseball players. Future studies should explore its impact on surgical outcomes and cost-effectiveness.
