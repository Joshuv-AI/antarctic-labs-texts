Upper extremity fractures and subsequent total knee arthroplasty incidence in knee osteoarthritis: a cross-continental propensity score-matched cohort study
Authors: Wen-Chi Chan, Yu-Chen Tsai, Wen-Tien Wu, Ru-Ping Lee, Ing-Ho Chen, Tzai-Chiu Yu, Ji-Ze Hsu, Jen-Hung Wang, Kuang-Ting Yeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) represents a major clinical and economic burden globally, and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains the definitive treatment for end-stage disease. Upper extremity fractures in older adults often indicate skeletal fragility and reduced physiological reserve, but their influence on subsequent TKA incidence remains unclear. We investigated whether upper extremity fractures influence TKA incidence among patients with KOA.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective propensity score-matched cohort study using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network (2005–2024) and performed external validation with the Asia-Pacific Research Network. Adults aged ≥ 50 years with KOA were identified; the exposure cohort included individuals who sustained upper extremity fractures after KOA diagnosis, while matched controls had no fractures. One-to-one propensity score matched more than 40 covariates, including demographics, comorbidities, and medications, with all standardized mean differences ≤ 0.02. The primary outcome was time to TKA at 1, 3, and 5 years, analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models with subgroup analyses by age and sex.ResultsAmong 210,808 matched U.S. participants (105,404 pairs), upper extremity fractures were associated with significantly lower TKA utilization at 1 year (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 0.25, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.23–0.26), 3 years (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.40–0.43), and 5 years (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.48–0.52). External validation in 13,018 Asia-Pacific participants (6,509 matched pairs) yielded comparable results (1-year HR 0.25, 95% CI 0.19–0.33; 3-year HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.32–0.49; 5-year HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.40–0.59). Cumulative incidence curves demonstrated persistent divergence between groups in both cohorts (log-rank test, p < 0.001).ConclusionsUpper extremity fractures are associated with a 50% reduction in subsequent TKA utilization among adults with KOA, a finding replicated across geographically and ethnically diverse populations. These findings suggest that upper extremity fractures may serve as clinically actionable markers of systemic frailty that influence surgical utilization patterns and may inform individualized surgical timing and multidisciplinary osteoarthritis management strategies.

Background

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a leading cause of pain and disability among older adults and is characterized by progressive degeneration of articular cartilage and subchondral bone [1]. The global burden of KOA continues to rise with population aging and increased life expectancy, resulting in substantial healthcare utilization and socioeconomic costs [2]. In cases of advanced disease refractory to conservative management, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains the most effective intervention and provides sustained pain relief and functional restoration [3, 4]. The demand for TKA has increased steadily worldwide, influenced by demographic trends, comorbidity burden, and evolving surgical eligibility criteria [5, 6].

Upper-extremity fractures—including distal radius, proximal humerus, and clavicle fractures—are among the most common fragility injuries in older adults, particularly postmenopausal women [7, 8]. These fractures often reflect systemic skeletal fragility rather than localized trauma and serve as an early marker of osteoporotic vulnerability [9]. Prior research, which has predominantly focused on lower-extremity or hip fractures, shows that fragility fractures predict subsequent musculoskeletal complications and functional decline, underscoring their role as clinical warning signs of broader skeletal deterioration [10, 11]. However, the potential influence of an upper-extremity fracture on the clinical course of coexisting degenerative joint disease, such as KOA, remains uncertain.

We conducted a large-scale retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated real-world data platform that integrates de-identified electronic health records from healthcare organizations in the United States and other countries, to address this knowledge gap [12]. We investigated whether upper-extremity fractures alter the clinical course of KOA by affecting the likelihood and timing of TKA and examined all-cause mortality as a competing outcome.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from both the U.S. Collaborative Network and the Asia–Pacific Network within the TriNetX platform (Cambridge, MA, USA). The U.S. Collaborative Network comprises more than 70 healthcare organizations and over 100 million patients, while the Asia–Pacific Network includes more than 30 healthcare organizations and over 10 million patients. All data within TriNetX are de-identified and derived from electronic health records contributed by participating institutions. All data collection, processing, and transmission procedures adhered to applicable data protection regulations governing participating institutions, including the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR; Regulation 2016/679) and the U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). TriNetX is ISO 27001:2013 certified and maintains a comprehensive information security framework to ensure the protection of personal and healthcare data [13]. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital (IRB112-133-C; approval date: June 9, 2023). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and the exclusive use of de-identified data, which posed minimal risk to participants.

We identified adults aged 50 years or older who had a diagnosis of KOA according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes between January 1, 2005, and December 31, 2020. The fracture cohort consisted of patients who sustained documented upper extremity fractures following their KOA diagnosis, with the index date defined as the date of first fracture occurrence. The control cohort comprised patients without any fracture records during the study period, with the index date defined as the date of first KOA diagnosis. We acknowledge that this index date definition introduces a temporal asymmetry between cohorts: patients in the fracture cohort must survive without TKA from the time of KOA diagnosis until fracture occurrence to enter the exposed group, which may introduce immortal time bias. This design reflects real-world clinical trajectories in which fractures occur as intercurrent events during the natural history of osteoarthritis, but it should be interpreted as capturing differences in surgical utilization patterns and clinical decision-making rather than direct biological modification of osteoarthritis disease progression. Alternative analytical approaches, such as time-dependent exposure modeling or landmark analyses, are discussed in the limitations section. Exclusion criteria: Patients were excluded if they had: (1) a prior history of TKA before the index date; (2) a history of other major joint replacement surgeries, such as total hip arthroplasty; (3) malignancy-related or pathological fractures; or (4) major trauma (ICD-10: T07) (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were assessed during the one year preceding the index date. We collected demographic information, including age, sex, and race. Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes, including essential hypertension (I10), diabetes mellitus (E08-E13), depressive episodes (F32), ischemic heart diseases (I20-I25), chronic kidney disease (N18), inflammatory polyarthropathies (M05-M14), cerebrovascular diseases (I60-I69), nicotine dependence (F17), inflammatory bowel disease (K50-K52), benign prostatic hyperplasia (N40), long-term steroid use (Z79.5), and Alzheimer’s disease (G30). Medication use was documented, including corticosteroids, opioids, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), calcium supplements, vitamin D, bisphosphonates, hyaluronate, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogues, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, denosumab, selective estrogen receptor modulators, parathyroid hormones, and romosozumab. Laboratory parameters included body mass index (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR < 30 and 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m²), triglycerides (≥ 200 mg/dL), and hemoglobin A1c (≥ 6.5%).

The primary outcome was the occurrence of TKA during follow-up periods of 1, 3, and 5 years after the index date. All-cause mortality was assessed as a secondary outcome to evaluate potential competing risk. Patients were followed until TKA occurrence, death, or the end of the study period, whichever occurred first. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed based on the predefined cohort criteria to minimize confounding and ensure balance between study groups. Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression models that included the covariates listed in Table 1. A nearest-neighbor matching algorithm without replacement was applied, with a caliper width of 0.1 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score [14]. PSM was conducted between the fracture and non-fracture groups. In addition, matching was repeated for each subgroup analysis to preserve comparability and ensure adequate control of confounding in both overall and subgroup analyses. Balance between matched cohorts was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate baseline balance [15]. Cumulative incidence of outcomes was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group differences were assessed with log-rank tests. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards assumption was verified by Schoenfeld residuals within the TriNetX platform. Subgroup analyses were stratified by age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years) and sex to evaluate potential effect modification. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using the TriNetX Analytics Network Platform and R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

We initially identified 3,043,272 adults aged ≥ 50 years with knee osteoarthritis who had at least four recorded encounters in the U.S. TriNetX database between January 2005 and December 2024 (Fig. 1). These patients were stratified into fracture (n = 174,846, 5.7%) and non-fracture (n = 2,784,018, 91.5%) groups based on subsequent upper extremity fracture occurrences. After excluding patients with prior total knee arthroplasty before the index date, knee osteoarthritis diagnosed before 2004, or fractures due to malignancy, pathological causes, or major trauma, 105,407 fracture patients and 2,470,255 non-fracture patients remained eligible. One-to-one propensity score matching using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 standard deviations created 105,404 matched pairs (210,808 participants), with 3 patients (0.003%) unable to be matched due to extreme propensity scores outside the common support region.

The propensity score matching achieved covariate balance across all variables, with all standardized mean differences ≤ 0.02 (Table 1). Both cohorts had a mean age of approximately 67 years and 71% female representation. Comorbidity prevalence included hypertension (68%), diabetes mellitus (28%), depressive episodes (23%), and chronic kidney disease (15%). Medication use patterns showed 18% using corticosteroids, 35% using opioids, and 52% using non-salicylate NSAIDs. Laboratory parameters including BMI categories, eGFR, and HbA1c levels were balanced between groups. For the Asia-Pacific validation cohort, an analogous patient selection and propensity score matching process was implemented using the TriNetX Asia-Pacific Research Network database. From 140,079 eligible patients, 6,509 matched pairs (13,018 participants) were created after applying identical exclusion criteria. The Asia-Pacific cohort achieved covariate balance with all standardized mean differences ≤ 0.05 (Table 2). The combined analytic cohorts comprised 223,826 participants across two continents.

Primary outcome: total knee arthroplasty incidence in the U.S. cohort

During a median follow-up of 4.2 years (interquartile range 2.1–6.8 years), 1,054 TKA events occurred in the fracture cohort versus 4,216 events in the non-fracture cohort at 1-year follow-up, yielding an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.25 (95% CI 0.23–0.26, p < 0.001) (Table 3). At 3 years, the adjusted hazard ratio was 0.41 (95% CI 0.40–0.43, p < 0.001), and at 5 years, it was 0.50 (95% CI 0.48–0.52, p < 0.001). The cumulative TKA incidence at 5 years was 4.8% in the fracture cohort compared to 9.5% in the non-fracture cohort, representing an absolute risk difference of 4.7% points. The cumulative incidence curves demonstrated divergence between the two groups throughout the 5-year follow-up period (log-rank test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The non-fracture cohort reached approximately 4.0% cumulative incidence at 1 year, 7.5% at 3 years, and 9.5% at 5 years. The fracture cohort reached 1.0% at 1 year, 3.0% at 3 years, and 4.8% at 5 years. The 95% confidence intervals remained non-overlapping throughout the entire follow-up period. Subgroup analyses stratified by age and sex demonstrated consistent associations across all subgroups (Table 3). Among adults aged 50–64 years, the 5-year adjusted hazard ratio was 0.57 (95% CI 0.55–0.60, p < 0.001). Among adults aged ≥ 65 years, the 5-year adjusted hazard ratio was 0.46 (95% CI 0.44–0.48, p < 0.001). When stratified by sex, males demonstrated a 5-year adjusted hazard ratio of 0.53 (95% CI 0.45–0.51, p < 0.001), while females demonstrated a 5-year adjusted hazard ratio of 0.52 (95% CI 0.50–0.54, p < 0.001) (Table 3). All subgroup analyses showed statistically significant associations between upper extremity fractures and reduced TKA incidence, with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals compared to the non-fracture cohorts.

External validation in the Asia-Pacific cohort

In the Asia-Pacific cohort, the 1-year adjusted hazard ratio was 0.25 (95% CI 0.19–0.33, p < 0.001), the 3-year hazard ratio was 0.40 (95% CI 0.32–0.49, p < 0.001), and the 5-year hazard ratio was 0.48 (95% CI 0.40–0.59, p < 0.001) (Table 4). At 1-year follow-up, 66 TKA events occurred in the fracture cohort versus 255 events in the non-fracture cohort. At 5-year follow-up, 159 events occurred in the fracture cohort versus 317 events in the non-fracture cohort. The cumulative incidence at 5 years was 2.4% in the fracture cohort compared to 4.9% in the non-fracture cohort. The cumulative incidence curves for the Asia-Pacific cohort showed divergence between groups throughout the follow-up period (log-rank test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The 95% confidence intervals remained non-overlapping throughout the follow-up period. Subgroup analyses in the Asia-Pacific cohort also demonstrated consistent associations (Table 4). Among adults aged 50–64 years, the 5-year adjusted hazard ratio was 0.56 (95% CI 0.39–0.80, p < 0.001). Among adults aged ≥ 65 years, the 5-year adjusted hazard ratio was 0.52 (95% CI 0.43–0.65, p < 0.001). When stratified by sex, males (n = 3,766, 28.9%) demonstrated a 5-year adjusted hazard ratio of 0.31 (95% CI 0.18–0.55, p < 0.001), while females (n = 9,252, 71.1%) demonstrated a 5-year adjusted hazard ratio of 0.49 (95% CI 0.40–0.61, p < 0.001). All subgroup analyses in the Asia-Pacific cohort showed statistically significant associations consistent with the U.S. findings.

Discussion

This propensity score-matched cohort study of 210,808 adults aged ≥ 50 years with KOA demonstrated that patients who sustained an upper extremity fracture had a markedly lower likelihood of undergoing TKA during follow-up compared to matched controls without fractures. The consistency of findings across multiple time points and demographic subgroups supports the robustness of these primary results. All analyses demonstrated statistically significant associations with narrow confidence intervals, strengthening the validity of our conclusions. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied based on Schoenfeld residual testing, supporting the validity of the Cox regression models. The visual representation of the overall survival curves demonstrates a sustained reduction in TKA utilization over the five-year follow-up period. These results suggest that an upper extremity fracture may mark a clinically meaningful turning point in the subsequent management trajectory of KOA, reflecting altered surgical utilization and clinical decision-making rather than direct biological modification of osteoarthritis progression. The mechanisms underlying this association appear multifactorial, involving behavioral, clinical, and healthcare system-related pathways that influence surgical candidacy and treatment decision-making rather than direct biological modification of osteoarthritis progression. The observed reduction in TKA incidence reflects changes in surgical utilization patterns shaped by patient frailty, functional limitations, and clinical prioritization rather than alterations in the underlying degenerative joint disease process. Following an upper extremity fracture, patients often experience a recovery period characterized by temporary reductions in ambulatory activity and increased caution in mobility—behavioral adjustments that can transiently reduce knee adduction moments and mechanical stress [16–18]. Beyond these behavioral and systemic factors, a direct and practical mechanical consideration warrants emphasis: patients recovering from upper extremity fractures often lack the functional capacity to safely use assistive devices—such as crutches, walkers, or canes—that are routinely employed during early TKA rehabilitation. Even with modern enhanced recovery protocols emphasizing immediate weight-bearing and accelerated mobilization, most patients still require upper extremity support for balance, confidence, and fall prevention during the first postoperative weeks. This mechanical limitation creates a tangible barrier to TKA candidacy during the fracture recovery period, particularly during the first 3–6 months when upper extremity function may remain compromised. The pronounced reduction in TKA incidence observed during the first year following fracture (HR 0.25) likely reflects, in part, this practical rehabilitation constraint, which necessitates postponement of elective surgery until adequate upper extremity function is restored. This represents a modifiable factor that could be addressed through preoperative upper extremity strengthening programs or alternative rehabilitation strategies in selected patients. Additionally, shifts in healthcare utilization—such as delayed elective surgery scheduling or fracture-focused follow-up that diverts attention from knee symptoms—may contribute to the early decline in arthroplasty rates. As recovery progresses, rehabilitation often expands to include whole-body and balance-oriented exercises aimed at restoring postural control and neuromuscular coordination [19, 20]. Such programs have been shown to enhance function and delay surgical intervention thresholds even without radiographic improvement [21, 22].

At a biological level, these findings contrast with the patterns observed after lower-limb fractures, which often increase arthroplasty demand due to direct joint trauma [23, 24]. Upper extremity fractures, being non-weight-bearing injuries, do not alter lower-limb alignment but may trigger behavioral adjustments—such as slower gait and cautious mobility—that collectively reduce mechanical stress on the knee joint [25]. Meanwhile, fracture healing initiates anabolic and anti-inflammatory cascades—including transforming growth factor-β and insulin-like growth factor-1—that promote osteoblast differentiation and subchondral bone remodeling [26, 27]. Such remodeling may stabilize the osteochondral interface and reduce nociceptive signaling [28, 29]. Furthermore, fracture management frequently leads to the initiation of bone-protective therapies such as bisphosphonates or vitamin D supplementation, which have been associated with improved bone quality and lower arthroplasty incidence in observational cohorts [30, 31]. These biological pathways may contribute to the sustained reduction in surgical utilization observed over five years, complementing the mechanical and behavioral adaptations that follow fracture. The management strategy for upper extremity fractures—operative fixation versus conservative treatment—may further modulate these relationships. Operative fixation typically enables earlier functional recovery, faster restoration of assistive device capability, and may shorten the period during which mechanical and physiological barriers delay TKA candidacy. However, the selection of patients for operative management may also reflect lower frailty and better surgical candidacy, creating complex interactions between treatment choice and subsequent surgical utilization. This hypothesis requires empirical testing in future studies with detailed procedural data. Fractures often occur in older adults with multiple comorbidities, sarcopenia, and diminished physiological reserve—all of which influence both surgical candidacy and recovery potential [32]. Previous population-based analyses have demonstrated that frailty predicts not only reduced arthroplasty utilization but also increased postoperative complications [33–35]. The observed reduction in TKA utilization following upper extremity fractures aligns with this framework, suggesting that such injuries serve as clinical markers that identify a subgroup with limited physiological resilience, higher frailty burden, and altered surgical candidacy rather than conferring any protective biological effect on osteoarthritis progression. This pattern highlights that upper extremity fracture history may indicate underlying frailty-related musculoskeletal decline, serving as a clinical marker of systemic vulnerability within the KOA population [36]. From a public health perspective, recognizing an upper extremity fracture as a sentinel event could facilitate early multidisciplinary intervention—linking orthopedic, geriatric, and rehabilitation services—to optimize musculoskeletal health and reduce subsequent functional decline [37]. Such integrative care models may ultimately balance the competing risks of surgery and immobility while improving long-term quality of life. The observed reduction in TKA incidence following upper extremity fractures conveys an important clinical message. Patients sustaining fractures often face not only musculoskeletal compromise but also systemic frailty, which may influence surgical decision-making. This pattern, characterized by lower elective surgery rates, suggests that upper extremity fractures identify a population with diminished operative candidacy and altered treatment priorities. Clinicians should interpret an upper extremity fracture in a KOA patient as a clinical signal that identifies altered surgical candidacy and systemic frailty, prompting reassessment of overall health, mobility, and surgical goals rather than as an isolated injury event. The observed reduction in TKA utilization reflects changes in clinical decision-making and patient selection rather than disease modification, and should inform individualized risk stratification and shared decision-making regarding surgical timing. From a practical surgical planning perspective, clinicians should assess upper extremity functional recovery and assistive device capability when considering TKA timing in patients with recent upper extremity fractures. Ensuring adequate upper extremity strength and coordination prior to TKA may optimize rehabilitation safety and outcomes. In selected cases, preoperative physical therapy focused on upper extremity strengthening or the use of alternative assistive devices (e.g., rolling walkers that require less upper extremity strength) may facilitate earlier surgical intervention when clinically appropriate. Integrating upper extremity fracture history into predictive algorithms could enhance preoperative risk assessment and inform shared decision-making regarding the timing and appropriateness of TKA. The observed association between upper extremity fractures and reduced TKA utilization in KOA patients reflects a complex interplay of clinical, behavioral, and systemic factors. This finding extends current understanding of KOA progression by demonstrating that an intercurrent upper extremity musculoskeletal injury can reshape subsequent treatment pathways. The upper extremity fracture serves not merely as a comorbid event but as a quantifiable clinical marker that identifies a frailer, lower-operability phenotype within the KOA population, influencing subsequent surgical utilization through altered candidacy assessment and clinical decision-making rather than through direct modification of osteoarthritis disease progression. Incorporating upper extremity fracture history into prognostic assessment and personalized rehabilitation strategies allows clinicians to better align surgical decision-making with patient resilience and long-term outcomes. This study provides population-level evidence that upper extremity fracture events have enduring implications for surgical utilization, thereby redefining how comorbid musculoskeletal injuries are conceptualized in osteoarthritis care. Future research should also examine whether operative fixation of upper extremity fractures, by enabling faster functional recovery, allows for earlier safe TKA compared to conservative management. If confirmed, this could inform shared decision-making regarding fracture treatment in patients with symptomatic KOA awaiting arthroplasty.

The robustness of our findings is strongly supported by external validation across geographically and ethnically distinct populations. When the identical analytical protocol—including inclusion criteria, propensity score matching specifications, and outcome definitions—was applied independently to both the TriNetX US Collaborative Network and the TriNetX Asia-Pacific Research Network, nearly identical effect estimates were observed. The adjusted 5-year hazard ratio for TKA following upper extremity fracture was 0.51 (95% CI 0.47–0.55) in the Asia-Pacific cohort, compared to 0.50 (95% CI 0.48–0.52) in the U.S. cohort, with no significant heterogeneity (p = 0.62) and overlapping confidence intervals. This consistency extended across all follow-up periods: 1-year HRs of 0.38 versus 0.25, and 3-year HRs of 0.48 versus 0.41, both with overlapping confidence intervals despite some variation in point estimates during early follow-up. Age- and sex-stratified analyses further demonstrated concordant results between cohorts, with all subgroup-specific HRs differing by less than 0.10 and maintaining overlapping confidence intervals. This cross-continental replication is particularly noteworthy given substantial differences in healthcare systems, surgical practice patterns, cultural attitudes toward joint replacement, and baseline fracture epidemiology between Western and Asian populations [38–40]. For instance, TKA utilization rates, thresholds for surgical intervention, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols vary considerably across these regions; however, the association between upper extremity fracture and reduced subsequent TKA utilization remained consistent, reflecting shared clinical selection mechanisms and frailty-related decision-making patterns rather than region-specific factors. The concordance of findings across these diverse settings suggests that the association reflects a generalizable clinical phenomenon—likely mediated by shared biological processes and clinical selection mechanisms—rather than region-specific confounding, coding practices, or variation in healthcare access. This external validation strengthens causal inference by demonstrating consistency, a key criterion in observational epidemiology established by Bradford Hill, and supports the broader applicability of upper extremity fracture as a clinically relevant marker of altered surgical trajectories in knee osteoarthritis management across global populations.

This study has some notable strengths. First, the large sample size (389,720 participants across two continents) provides robust statistical power and enables precise effect estimation. Second, rigorous propensity score matching achieved excellent covariate balance (all standardized mean differences ≤ 0.02) across more than 40 demographic, clinical, and pharmacological variables, thereby minimizing measured confounding. Third, external validation in geographically and ethnically diverse U.S. and Asia-Pacific populations—despite substantial differences in healthcare systems, surgical practices, and baseline fracture epidemiology—demonstrates remarkable consistency (5-year HR: 0.50 vs. 0.51) and strengthens generalizability. Fourth, the extended 5-year follow-up period allows assessment of both immediate and sustained effects on surgical decision-making. Finally, the consistency of findings across multiple time points, age strata, and sexes supports the robustness of the conclusions. However, several methodological limitations warrant consideration. First and most importantly, the differential index date definition between cohorts—fracture date for the exposed group versus KOA diagnosis date for controls—introduces immortal time bias, as individuals must survive and remain TKA-free from KOA diagnosis until sustaining a fracture to enter the exposed cohort [41, 42]. This temporal asymmetry means our findings primarily reflect differences in surgical utilization and clinical decision-making trajectories rather than biological modification of osteoarthritis disease progression. While this design reflects real-world clinical scenarios in which fractures occur as intercurrent events during osteoarthritis management, alternative analytical approaches—such as time-dependent exposure modeling, landmark analyses, or target trial emulation frameworks—would better address this temporal asymmetry and are recommended for future investigations. Second, despite rigorous propensity score matching, residual confounding from unmeasured variables—including dietary habits, alcohol consumption, physical activity levels, fracture severity and mechanism (traumatic vs. fragility), post-fracture rehabilitation participation, and patient preferences regarding surgery—cannot be ruled out. Third, surveillance bias is possible if patients with fractures have more frequent medical encounters, leading to differential monitoring and treatment intensity. Fourth, the current TriNetX platform does not support Fine–Gray competing risk models; implementing such methods would further refine effect estimates by accounting for mortality as a competing event [15, 43]. Fifth, upper extremity fractures represent a markedly heterogeneous group of injuries with substantial variation across multiple clinically important dimensions. Anatomical heterogeneity includes distal radius fractures (predominantly affecting postmenopausal women and signaling osteoporotic fragility), proximal humerus fractures (often indicating more severe frailty and functional impairment), clavicle fractures (which may result from different injury mechanisms), and other sites such as the olecranon and radial head. Fracture mechanisms vary from low-energy fragility fractures (reflecting systemic bone fragility) to higher-energy traumatic injuries (which may occur in more active individuals with better baseline function). Fracture severity ranges from simple, minimally displaced fractures to complex, comminuted patterns requiring surgical intervention. Management approaches differ between operative fixation with ORIF—which typically enables earlier functional recovery and shorter rehabilitation periods—and conservative treatment with prolonged immobilization, which may influence both the timeline and likelihood of subsequent TKA. Patients undergoing ORIF may restore upper extremity function more rapidly, thereby shortening the period during which mechanical limitations preclude safe use of postoperative assistive devices and potentially demonstrating TKA rates closer to non-fractured controls. Conversely, the decision to pursue operative versus conservative management may itself reflect underlying frailty, comorbidity burden, and surgical candidacy—factors that would independently influence TKA decision-making. These multiple sources of heterogeneity mean that different fracture subtypes may have varying associations with subsequent TKA utilization through distinct mechanisms. For example, fragility fractures may primarily signal systemic frailty and reduced surgical candidacy, while traumatic fractures in more active patients may have different implications. Similarly, fractures requiring prolonged immobilization may create longer-lasting mechanical barriers to TKA compared to those managed operatively with early mobilization. Unfortunately, the current TriNetX platform does not provide sufficient procedural or diagnostic granularity (e.g., detailed ICD-10-CM subcodes, CPT codes, or ICD-10-PCS codes) to distinguish among these fracture subtypes, mechanisms, severity grades, or management approaches, precluding subgroup analysis by fracture characteristics. This limitation means our findings represent an average effect across a heterogeneous exposure and may not apply uniformly to all upper extremity fracture types. Important clinical variables were unavailable, including radiographic KOA severity (Kellgren-Lawrence grade), specific fracture type and precise anatomical location, fracture mechanism (fragility vs. traumatic), fracture displacement and comminution, fracture management approach (operative vs. conservative), timing and type of surgical fixation, fracture union status and complications, functional status, and patient-reported outcomes. Finally, although cross-continental validation enhances generalizability, the findings may not extend to populations with markedly different healthcare access patterns or cultural attitudes toward surgery.

Future investigations should delineate the relative contributions of biological mechanisms (systemic bone metabolism, biomechanical unloading), behavioral factors (activity modification, healthcare utilization), and clinical selection processes (physician decision-making, patient preferences) using multimodal study designs. Incorporation of radiographic or MRI-based grading of KOA severity, side-specific joint involvement, and precise fracture classification (e.g., distal radius, proximal humerus, clavicle) would clarify whether the observed association varies by anatomical subtype or disease stage. A particularly important question for future investigation is whether operative fixation of upper extremity fractures (e.g., ORIF) influences the magnitude and duration of the observed association with TKA utilization. Patients undergoing ORIF may demonstrate faster functional recovery, earlier restoration of assistive device capability, and potentially higher subsequent TKA rates approaching those of non-fractured controls, as operative fixation shortens the period of mechanical limitation. Conversely, the selection of patients for operative fixation may itself reflect better overall health status, lower frailty burden, and higher surgical candidacy—factors that would confound the relationship. Prospective cohort studies or registry-based analyses with detailed procedural data (including CPT codes, operative reports, and fracture union assessment) are needed to test this hypothesis and determine whether fracture management strategy modifies the relationship between upper extremity fractures and subsequent TKA. Such findings could inform clinical decision-making regarding both fracture management and TKA timing, potentially identifying subgroups in whom earlier TKA remains safe and appropriate despite recent upper extremity injury. Detailed fracture classification is essential for future research. Studies should distinguish among anatomical subtypes (distal radius, proximal humerus, clavicle, olecranon), fracture mechanisms (fragility vs. traumatic), severity grades (simple vs. comminuted, displaced vs. non-displaced), and management strategies (operative fixation vs. conservative treatment). Such granular analyses would clarify whether the observed association varies by fracture characteristics and would identify which fracture subtypes most strongly predict altered TKA utilization. For example, fragility fractures may serve as stronger markers of systemic frailty and reduced surgical candidacy compared to traumatic fractures in more active individuals. Registry-based studies linking fracture registries with arthroplasty databases, or prospective cohorts with detailed fracture characterization, would enable these critical subgroup analyses. Longitudinal imaging of subchondral bone microarchitecture, quantitative gait analysis, and measurement of bone turnover biomarkers (e.g., CTX, P1NP) may help determine whether these relationships reflect structural stabilization, systemic metabolic changes, or behavioral adaptation. Assessment of patient-reported outcomes (pain, function, quality of life), shared decision-making processes, and functional trajectories after fracture could elucidate how recovery experiences and health prioritization influence long-term surgical decisions. Prospective cohort studies that integrate these measures, combined with qualitative research exploring patient and physician perspectives, would provide more definitive insights into causal pathways. Time-dependent Cox models, landmark analyses, and target trial emulation frameworks could address immortal-time bias more rigorously. Randomized trials of enhanced bone health interventions or multidisciplinary care pathways following upper extremity fractures in patients with KOA could test whether recognizing these fractures as sentinel events enables optimization of surgical timing and functional outcomes. Ultimately, these efforts will clarify whether upper extremity fractures represent modifiable indicators of systemic musculoskeletal vulnerability that are amenable to intervention or whether they function primarily as markers of underlying frailty and clinical selection that inform individualized risk stratification and shared decision-making in osteoarthritis management.

Conclusion

These findings challenge the traditional view of upper extremity fractures as isolated injuries and position them instead as clinically actionable markers of systemic frailty that influence surgical utilization trajectories in KOA rather than directly modifying osteoarthritis pathophysiology. Recognizing these fractures as sentinel events that signal altered surgical candidacy and physiological vulnerability enables more anticipatory, patient-centered management strategies that optimize surgical timing, bone health, and functional capacity through multidisciplinary care. This paradigm shift has direct implications for clinical practice guidelines and may inform risk stratification tools that better align osteoarthritis interventions with individual resilience and long-term functional goals.
