Inter- and Intrarater Reliability of a Novel Ultrasound Doppler Device (earlybird) for Quantitative Assessment of Peripheral Arterial Flow
Authors: Marie Tryland Høiberg, Stine Marie Pauschert Kvamme, Steffen Felle Bentsen, Karoline Løkken, Gudrun Rohde, Hans Torp, Arne Seternes, Erik Mulder Pettersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGrowing interest in quantitative Doppler assessment has underscored the need for reliable, operator-independent tools to evaluate peripheral arterial circulation. This study aimed to evaluate the reliability of a novel ultrasound Doppler device (earlybird) for measuring blood-flow velocity in lower-extremity arteries and to validate its consistency across raters. We further explored multiple hemodynamic parameters derived from the Doppler spectrogram to examine their reproducibility and potential clinical applicability.MethodsThirty-five participants, including 25 with varying degrees of peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and 10 healthy individuals, were recruited. Four raters performed independent measurements of the peripheral blood-flow velocity in each participant (280 measurements). Six blood-flow parameters were extracted from the Doppler spectrograms: peak systolic velocity (PSV), time-averaged velocity (TAV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), pulsatility index (PI), resistive index (RI), and acceleration time (AT). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess reliability, which was visualized in scatter plots and Bland–Altman plots with 95% limits of agreement.ResultsGood correlation and agreement among the different raters were observed, with inter-rater reliability ranging from an ICC of 0.755–0.835 (p < 0.001). Intra-rater reliability showed poor to excellent correlation, with ICCs ranging from 0.450 to 0.961, (p < 0.001). Bland–Altman plots revealed wide 95% limits of agreement for PSV, TAV, EDV, and PI, but narrower limits for RI (–9.8% to 9.8%) and AT (–17.3% to 17.1%).ConclusionEarlybird was shown to be a reliable tool for assessing peripheral arteries across raters, with good inter- and intra-rater reliability. The angle-independent variables, RI and AT, showed narrow 95% limits of agreement, which justifies further exploration.Keywords: peripheral arterial disease, ultrasonography doppler, blood flow velocity, inter- and intrarater reliability, innovations

Introduction

Accurate diagnostic tools are essential for evaluating peripheral blood circulation to improve diagnosis of and tailor treatment for peripheral arterial disease (PAD),1,2 which is primarily assessed by the ankle–brachial index (ABI) and toe pressure.2,3 However, media sclerosis can falsely elevate these parameters, most commonly in patients with diabetes mellitus, kidney failure, or in the elderly.4 Ultrasound Doppler spectrogram evaluation has re-emerged as a method not hampered by media sclerosis and without the need for external compression.5–9 Recent studies have highlighted the potential of Doppler-derived parameters, particularly acceleration time, to provide quantitative assessment of peripheral perfusion, especially in intraoperative monitoring during endovascular treatment.10–12 However, the usefulness of monitoring acceleration time during endovascular treatment (for treatment guidance) still needs to be established.

Various techniques have been explored for intraoperative monitoring, including near-infrared spectroscopy, implantable sensors, laser Doppler flowmetry, and catheter-based flow measurement systems.13–18 However, each modality has inherent limitations related to invasiveness, spatial resolution, cost, or standardization. Against this background, Doppler-derived acceleration time represents a non-invasive and widely accessible alternative,10 provided that measurement reliability and reproducibility can be ensured. This underscores the need for standardized Doppler tools capable of consistent hemodynamic quantification across raters and clinical settings.

Earlybird is a novel pulsed Doppler ultrasound device that incorporates a highly sensitive transducer with a large surface area,19 a scanner, and a customized user interface. The device’s lightweight noninvasive design enables easy positioning above the vascular structure of interest. Earlybird provides continuous and simultaneous measurements of the peripheral circulation across multiple depths within the scanned sector and produces Doppler spectrograms, enabling analysis of various blood-flow velocity parameters. These features enable measurement of arterial circulation before, during, and after surgery.

Previous studies have demonstrated earlybird’s ability to detect microvascular circulatory changes, showing good agreement with methods, such as laser Doppler flowmetry and pulsed-wave Doppler.20 The device can assess volume flow rate in the radiocephalic fistulas of patients undergoing hemodialysis21,22 and show changes in the blood-flow velocity in patients with intermittent claudication23 and during endovascular procedures involving balloon dilatation and stent placement.24 The latter emphasizes that earlybird potentially can guide clinical decision-making in endovascular treatment. However, earlybird’s ability to make consistent measurements of blood-flow velocity in PAD, regardless of the performer’s measurements, remains to be validated.

The study aim was to evaluate the reliability of earlybird as a tool for measuring blood-flow velocity in lower extremity arteries and to validate its consistency across different raters. In addition, we evaluated various measurement parameters derived from Doppler spectrograms.

Materials and Methods

Patients

Participants were recruited from an outpatient clinic from September to October 2023. A total of 35 participants were recruited for this study, comprising 25 patients with PAD and 10 healthy participants. The inclusion criteria required the participants to be able to remain in a supine position for 30 minutes, be >18 years old, and to provide informed consent. Patients with PAD presented with varying degrees of disease, ranging from intermittent claudication to critical limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI). Candidates who did not meet these criteria were excluded. We required the candidates with intermittent claudication to have a diagnosis of PAD, have stable symptoms for the last 6 months, and have PAD-related gait pain. Patients with intermittent claudication underwent a progressive treadmill test25 and were classified according to Fontaine classification.26 Candidates with CLTI had to meet one or more of the following criteria: resting and/or night pain, nonhealing wounds (lasting >14 days), and reduced peripheral circulation as the underlying cause. Healthy participants were required to have no PAD and no gait-related pain.

Clinical Setup and Measurements

All participants were not fasting, asked to wear comfortable clothes, and placed in a study room with stable comfortable temperature. The participants were instructed to hold a supine position for ≥20 min before the measurements were made. During the measurements, the participants were instructed to lie still and remain silent. The ABI was used to assess each participant to verify the diagnosis of PAD and ensure fulfilment of the inclusion criteria as well as to determine the placement of the earlybird probe. An ABI of <0.9 is regarded a diagnostic for PAD.4 Health information regarding comorbidity and medication was obtained through a questionnaire.

The earlybird probe was placed in a hydrogel stand-off, which secured a 60° insonation angle on the surface. The nominal frequency was 6.93 MHz and transmitted at a pulse-repetition frequency of 10 kHz. In-house software, developed in MATLAB (MathWorks R2021, Natick, Massachusetts), registered multi-gated Doppler signals in the spectrogram and were later analysed.

For each participant, the lower extremity with the lowest ABI was first selected, corresponding to the leg with the most severe symptoms in patients with intermittent claudication or the most affected foot in patients with CLTI. Within that leg, the foot artery with the highest pressure, either the dorsalis pedis artery (ADP) or the posterior tibial artery (PTA), was chosen for earlybird measurements. All raters performed measurements on the same artery for each participant to ensure consistency (Figure 1).

Each rater independently placed the probe on the vessel of interest, as determined by the ABI, and adjusted it to optimize the real-time Doppler spectrogram. A recording of 30 seconds was undertaken for later analysis of blood-flow velocity parameters. Raters were unaware of each other’s probe placement over the vessel of interest and any adjustments made to enhance the Doppler measurements. Each rater performed two measurements per participant following a fixed sequence: Rater 1, Rater 2, Rater 3, Rater 4, then repeated in the same order, with the probe removed between rounds to ensure repositioning and evaluate inter- and intra-rater reliability. The four raters who participated in the study were all experienced in conducted ABI measurements using a continuous-wave Doppler probe. Three of them were nurses, and one was a resident specializing in vascular surgery.

Data and Statistical Analyses

All Doppler flow curves were reviewed and adjusted for the optimal sample volume and gain to enhance signal tracing and quality, facilitating automated data extraction. This post processing of the recorded Doppler spectrograms was performed by one of the authors (EMP), a vascular surgeon with extensive experience in vascular ultrasound. All measurement parameters were extracted automatically by the device algorithm from a 30-second Doppler recording including peak systolic velocity (PSV), time-averaged velocity (TAV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV) and acceleration time (AT). The exported values represented the average of all detected cardiac cycles within the recording period. Pulsatility index (PI) was calculated as (PSV − EDV)/TAV, and resistive index (RI) as (PSV − EDV)/PSV. AT was defined as the time interval from the onset of systolic upstroke to peak systolic velocity. The velocity calculations assumed a fixed insonation angle. The data of interest were exported to a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft 365 MSO) (Figure 2).

M-mode images (top) and corresponding Doppler spectrograms (bottom) for (A) a healthy participant and (B) a patient with peripheral arterial disease. In the M-mode images, the y-axis represents depth under the probe (mm), and the green marker on the right indicates the size and position of the sample volume. In the Doppler spectrograms, the y-axis represents blood flow velocity (cm/s) and the x-axis represents time (s). The red curve shows the automatically detected spectral envelope. Acceleration time was automatically calculated as the interval between two algorithm-placed markers (green dots) at the onset of the systolic upstroke and at peak systolic velocity.

Normality was assessed by histograms, Q-Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to determine if there was a significant difference in the measured parameters between the raters. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with its 95% confidence interval was used to assess the inter- and intrarater reliability analyses for agreement on consistency between measurements among the different raters. ICC estimates were calculated on the basis of a total of eight measurements per participant (two measurements by each of the four raters). For the intrarater reliability analysis, the ICC was derived from each of the rater’s two repeated measurements using a two-way mixed-effects model assessing absolute agreement. For inter-rater reliability, ICC was calculated using a two-way random-effects model based on all single measurements, with each rater’s two repeats treated as separate observations to preserve full variance across raters.27,28 According to Koo and Li,28 an ICC < 0.50 was considered poor; 0.50–0.75, moderate; 0.75–0.90, good; and >0.90 excellent.

Bland–Altman plots and their 95% limits of agreement were generated to illustrate the variability of each measured parameter across raters and repeated measurements. For this, the relative difference in each measured parameter from the mean of all measurements per participant was calculated. The distribution of these differences was considered approximately normal. We acknowledge that this deviates from the classical two-rater Bland–Altman approach, and the resulting limits of agreement are not directly interpretable as absolute between-rater agreement.29 These plots and calculations were used to evaluate the variance of each measured parameter. A one-sample t-test was performed to calculate consistent bias, and linear regression analysis was used to assess the proportional bias (B), along with its standard error (SE). A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. IBM SPSS Statistics v.29.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used to perform all statistical analyses.

The participants were divided into two groups: participants with PAD (n = 25) and without PAD (n = 10). None of the participants exhibited non-compressible vasculature during the ABI measurement. Inter- and Intrarater Reliability Analyses

Reliability analyses between the four raters indicated overall good inter-rater correlations across the six measurement parameters, as illustrated in Figure 3 and summarized in Table 2. There were no differences in the measurement parameters among the raters (p > 0.50). Good inter-rater correlation was found for all measured parameters (Table 2). For PSV, ICC showed good intrarater reliability for all raters except Rater 3 who had moderate correlation. For the TAV, good intrarater reliability was found for all raters except Rater 1 for whom moderate correlation was found. For EDV, the ICC indicated good intrarater reliability for all raters except Rater 3, who showed moderate correlation. Interestingly, slightly better intrarater reliability was found for the angle-independent parameters. The PI demonstrated excellent intrarater reliability except for Rater 2 who showed moderate correlation. For the RI, Rater 4 showed excellent intrarater reliability, whereas Raters 1, 2, and 3 showed good correlation. For AT, greater variability in the intrarater reliability was found: Raters 1 and 2 showed good correlation, Rater 3 exhibited poor correlation, and Rater 4 excellent correlation, respectively (Figure 3 and Table 2).

Analysis of Variance (Bland–Altman Plots)

Analysis of variance using Bland–Altman plots revealed a wide spread in the 95% limits of agreement across the different measured variables (Figure 4). However, analysis of the insonation angle-independent parameters, RI, and AT indicated narrower 95% limits of agreement: −9.8% to 9.8% (B = −0.002, SE 3.047, p = 0.999) and −17.3% to 17.1% (B = 0.002, SE 0.019, p = 0.907), respectively. However, the 95% limit of agreement for the PI showed moderately broad variation, ranging from −36.7% to 36.8% (B = −0.002, SE 0.604, p = 0.997). Broader 95% limits of agreement were observed for the angle-dependent velocity parameters. The EDV exhibited a very broad range of −195.5% to 195.4% (B = −0.001, SE 3.516, p = 1.000). Moderately broad 95% limits of agreement were found for PSV and TAV, with ranges of −41.1% to 41.1% (B = 0.006, SE 0.149, p = 0.966) and 42.9% to 42.9% (B = 0.000, SE 0.445, p = 1.000), respectively.

No significant proportional bias was detected; similarly, no consistent bias was identified for the relative difference of the measured variables.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the inter- and intrarater reliability of earlybird, a novel proprietary ultrasound Doppler device, across different raters for various peripheral blood-flow measurement parameters. The findings demonstrated good inter-rater reliability across all measurement parameters, whereas intrarater reliability, although generally good, was variable, with correlations ranging from poor to excellent. Although relatively wide 95% limits of agreement were observed for several of the measured parameters, narrower 95% limits were found for the angle-independent parameters, particularly the RI and AT.

Reliable and reproducible measurement tools are essential for addressing clinical challenges in PAD, and rigorous inter- and intra-rater reliability studies provide the foundation for adequate reporting and interpretation of Doppler measurements.30 However, few reliability studies have been conducted in patients with established PAD.31–36 In a study by de Oliveira Guirro et al,31 the authors evaluated arterial blood-flow velocity by performing Doppler ultrasound of the brachial, radial, popliteal, and posterior tibial arteries in 50 healthy individuals, and two assessments were performed with a 1-week interval. They reported a moderate correlation, with ICC values ranging from 0.501 to 0.886. Tehan and Chuter32 explored the reliability of hand-held device Doppler ultrasound examinations performed by podiatrists and found generally poor levels of agreement in interpreting audio and visual waveforms. Corriveau et al33 examined inter-rater reliability and found almost perfect agreement (κ > 0.8) on a categorical scale. However, for continuous measurements of PSV, wide 95% limits of agreement (–25% to 43%) were found. Ubbink et al34 evaluated the inter-rater reliability for measuring the PSV and obtained an ICC value of 0.72 (95% CI, 0.63–0.79) for the aortoiliac tract and 0.85 (95% CI, 0.79–0.89) for the femoropopliteal tract.

The earlybird device is a novel system using unfocused pulsed Doppler ultrasound to assess blood-flow velocity. Our findings indicate good inter- and intra-rater reliability for Doppler-derived parameters, particularly for angle-independent variables such as RI and AT. Differences in study populations, anatomic localisation and measurement conditions make direct comparison with previous reports challenging, as this may influence reproducibility. Nevertheless, the present results provide an important methodological foundation for further evaluation of Doppler-derived parameters in clinical and intraoperative settings. By establishing reproducibility across raters, this study contributes to the necessary step of translating novel Doppler tools towards standardised clinical use and potential adoption in quantitative perfusion assessment.

Psychological and patient-related factors, such as conversation, thoughts, emotional distress, discomfort, and changes in respiration patterns, can affect the measured flow dynamics by causing vasoconstriction or dilation.20,37,38 These changes occur within a short timeframe, but the measurements are not made simultaneously, so these effects may influence the findings. However, each measurement with the earlybird device was conducted blindly and repeated, to reduce the influence of the above-mentioned factors. Measures were taken to maintain stable room and core temperatures to reduce the external environmental influence.

The intrarater reliability analyses showed significant variations. Although all raters were familiar with hand-held Doppler probe measurements, their experience with advanced ultrasound examinations was limited. The minimal training with the earlybird device received prior to data collection by the raters may have reduced the reliability. The level of training is associated with improved accuracy in data collection.39,40 Nevertheless, the overall intrarater ICC demonstrated good correlation, even with inexperienced raters, supporting earlybird as a user-friendly device.

The Bland–Altman plots indicated wide 95% limits of agreement for the insonation angle-dependent variables, such as PSV, TAV, and EDV. In contrast, our study found a narrower 95% limit of agreement for the insonation angle-independent variables, such as the RI (−9.8% to 9.8%) and AT (−17.3% to 17.1%) (Figure 4). Other studies that explored various Doppler ultrasound techniques considered a variation of ±30% as acceptable.41–44 The effect of variance in the insonation angle stands out as an important source of error. However, natural variance in blood flow due to temporal change, vessel anatomy, and degree of atherosclerosis, along with added error from the ultrasound device, may influence measurement accuracy.33,45 The extremely broad 95% limits of agreement for EDV reflect substantial variation in this parameter. Doppler spectrograms analyzed the forward-flow direction only, excluding the negative flow components. Because EDV values were close to zero, even small absolute changes can lead to relative large proportional differences in the measured EDV parameter. Despite the challenges with this variable, we chose not to exclude the EDV to maintain the completeness of the described flow parameters in this study.

The earlybird probe demonstrated high sensitivity;19 however, the calculation of the blood-flow velocity is highly dependent on the insonation angle. We assumed that the vessel is perfectly aligned parallel to the skin, but this may not always be the case, so it could lead to potential inaccuracies in the angle correction.22,46 Thus, one cannot fully rely on the absolute values of the PSV, TAV, and EDV because the earlybird probe is not angle independent.23 However, an enhanced dual-probe device would make the earlybird device angle independent for calculating flow parameters. Such a device was previously found feasible for calculating volume flow rate in radiocephalic arteriovenous fistulas of patients undergoing hemodialysis.21

In our study, the insonation angle-independent flow parameters seemed more useful in evaluating the peripheral arterial blood flow. The AT, measured in the common femoral or popliteal artery, is considered to be a simpler and reliable parameter for assessing lesions in PAD.8 The maximal systolic acceleration (ACCmax), defined as the line tangent to the maximal slope of the upstroke of the pulse stroke expressed in m/s2, is excellent for detecting PAD.7,47 However, ACCmax is also angle-dependent and reportedly highly operator dependent and poorly reliable when dampened pulse curves are present, as in severe arterial disease.6 The AT, also referred to as the systolic rise time, measured at the ADP or plantar artery, has re-emerged as a method for assessing the severity of lower-extremity PAD and been found to correlate well with ABI and toe pressure.5,6,8,9

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of this study were the inclusion of multiple raters and two measurements per participant, which provided a substantial number of measurements without dropouts or exclusions. The measurements were made within a short timeframe, which minimized external influences and enhanced the device’s reliability.38 With consistent measurements observed in both healthy and PAD-affected lower extremity arteries, these findings suggest that the earlybird could be used in the broader population where appropriate.48

This study does not allow for analysis of intra- and interrater reliability over time, since the data was acquired at a single session and not measured across multiple sessions. Further studies stratified by anatomic severity, with measurements obtained in different orders and compared over time under the same conditions with duplex ultrasound, are needed to support the potential clinical utility of the earlybird device.

The main study limitation was the lack of angle-independence in earlybird when evaluating the measured parameters, which may have caused angle-dependent variations in the results. Even though the sample size was sufficient for our study, a larger sample size would strengthen the applicability of our findings to a broader population.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that earlybird showed good inter- and intra-rater reliability for measuring characteristics of the peripheral arteries in the lower extremities, both in patients with PAD and healthy participants. Angle-independent variables, such as the RI and AT, showed narrow 95% limits of agreement. These findings provide a foundation for future evaluation of earlybird in clinical and intraoperative settings, and support further exploration of Doppler-derived parameters for quantitative assessment of peripheral perfusion.
