Fabrication and DFT assisted investigation of novel Ru (III) imine complex for nickel corrosion inhibition and energy storage applications
Authors: Hoda Abd El-Shafy Shilkamy, Mohamed Salaheldeen, Tarek A. Yousef, Rafat M. El-Khatib, Mona M. A. Alharas, Inam Omar, Khulood A. Abu Al-Ola, Hala M. Abo-Dief, Valentina Zhukova, Arcady Zhukov, Ahmed M. Abu-Dief
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nickel metal is widely used in various applications, including surgical instruments, but its susceptibility to corrosion in acidic environments limits its applications and necessitates effective corrosion inhibition strategies. Herein, a novel Ru(III) tetra-dentate anil complex was synthesized and characterized, demonstrating exceptional performance as a corrosion inhibitor for nickel in 0.5 M sulfuric acid. The corrosion inhibition performance was evaluated using Tafel plots and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, while the electronic properties and adsorption behavior were investigated using DFT calculations and Monte Carlo simulations. The complex also exhibits promising charge–discharge characteristics, suggesting its potential in energy storage applications. The RuL complex significantly reduced the corrosion current density, achieving a maximum inhibition efficiency of 99.4% at an optimal dosage of 1 × 10–3 M. This enhanced corrosion protection is attributed to the strong adsorption of the complex on the nickel surface, as confirmed by Monte Carlo simulations and electrochemical studies. Furthermore, the complex showed promising charge–discharge behavior, indicating its potential application in energy storage devices. These findings suggest that the novel Ru(III) complex offers a promising dual-functional material for nickel corrosion inhibition and energy storage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48014-3.

Introduction

Nickel and its alloys are among the metallic metals that have attracted the most attention because of their unparalleled properties, features include exceptional strength output, sound corrosion resistance, and temperature stability. All of these can be further investigated for use in architecture and technology1,2. The breakdown of metallic things is characterized by the process of corrosion, which arises from interaction between the metal’s exterior and its surroundings [3–5]. Ni and Ni alloys have a variety of industrial applications and are utilized in extremely harsh condition6,7.

Acidic solutions are used in a variety of manufacturing processes, such as pickling, plating, and electrowinning. Ni and Ni-base alloys have been used to make heat exchangers and other parts of water-pressurized reactors. This is because of their strong passive coating on the metal’s exterior, which provides exceptional corrosion resistance in a variety of challenging circumstances.8,9. Pitting corrosion occurs when specific hostile ions are present in situations where these alloys are polarized across specific electrode potentials, like chloride and sulfate. This compromises the passive covering’s integrity8. Owing to its anti-scaling qualities, sulphuric acid is widely utilized in various treatments environments to make metals and alloys cleaner10.

Nickel corrodes considerably in neutral or mineral acid environments, while being thought of as a corrosion-resistant metal in many unfavorable fluids11. The interaction between metal and inhibitor changes the properties of the metal’s exterior and enhances its resilience to a hostile setting. It is commonly known that organic compounds can prevent rusting. The inhibitory impact of nickel and its alloys in acidic conditions has been studied using a variety of organic and inorganic inhibitors.12–16. The strongest organic inhibitors are compounds containing heteroatoms including phosphorus, nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen17. Apart from their inhibitory action, these substances also adsorb on the outside of the metal. In inhibitory compounds, π -electrons, or heteroatoms with unbound electron pairs, were readily shared to create bonds and serve as nucleophile centers. Metal atoms can indeed act as electrophiles, particularly when they are positively charged or have vacant orbitals, and negative charge on a surface generally facilitates the adsorption process of positively charged molecules or ions, and vice versa. For example, it was discovered that compounds containing nitrogen (N) primarily adsorb on the cathodic sites of iron in an acidic solution, whereas compounds containing sulfur (S) primarily function by chemisorption on the anodic sites of iron18–20. Although several inhibitor compounds have shown promising corrosion prevention, most are toxic to public health and the environment. Worries over the safety and effects on the environment of the corroding inhibitors employed in a variety of sectors are persistent on a global scale. The use of environmentally safe and harmless anticorrosive materials is prompted by these undesirable or unpleasant effects. Complexes in particular provide adequate protection in acidic media. A major problem in many industries, such as manufacturing, transportation, and construction, corrosion causes substantial material loss and raises maintenance expenses. Schiff base complexes have become an effective class of corrosion inhibitors, which are essential for mitigating these problems. Schiff bases, which are created when primary amines react with carbonyl compounds, have special structural characteristics that improve their capacity to shield metals from corrosive conditions21–26.

The main way that these complexes’ function is by adhering to metal surfaces and forming a barrier that prevents corrosive substances from entering. Their capacity to establish enduring bonds with metal ions, which can aid in stabilizing the metal surface and lowering corrosion rates, is frequently cited as the reason for their efficacy. Furthermore, because of their adaptability, Schiff bases can have their chemical structures changed to provide customized solutions for certain corrosion problems. The exciting potential of Schiff base complexes in corrosion inhibition is explored in this introduction, which also looks at their benefits, modes of action, and uses in protecting materials from deterioration.

To keep nickel from corroding in an acidic solution, the investigated {3,4-Bis-[(5-bromo-2-hydroxy-benzylidene)-amino]-phenyl}-phenyl-methanone ligand (L) is added as a supplement to enhance crucial medical Ni equipment. Additionally, under the same circumstances and with the same dosages, RuL complex has been utilized as an additive. A Tafel plot and using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, the electrochemical process of Ni in 0.5 M H2SO4. A Tafel plot was used to examine the temperature effect. Different thermodynamic constants were determined by doing an adsorption isotherm. The mechanism of inhibition and possible zero charge were investigated. The layers of oxide which developed on the surface of Ni were examined using a scanning electron microscope after Tafel plot studies. Additionally, the galvanostatic charge discharge method has been used to study and consider charge discharge processes. For Ni in the test solution, several charge discharge characteristics have been identified at varying current densities.

Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) at a 0.5 M concentration is commonly employed in corrosion studies of nickel metal due to several key advantages. Firstly, this concentration provides a controlled corrosion rate it is sufficiently strong to induce measurable corrosion while not being overly aggressive, thereby allowing accurate observation and quantification of corrosion over practical timeframes. Secondly, it holds industrial relevance, as it closely simulates the acidic conditions encountered in real-world applications such as chemical processing and mining industries where nickel components are frequently used. Thirdly, the use of a standardized concentration like 0.5 M enhances the reproducibility and comparability of results across various studies and laboratories. Additionally, sulphuric acid at this concentration creates an appropriate electrolyte environment for electrochemical techniques such as polarization curves and impedance spectroscopy, which are essential for analyzing corrosion mechanisms. Finally, 0.5 M H₂SO₄ supports the study of hydrogen evolution reactions on nickel surfaces, offering insights into cathodic processes that occur during corrosion.

Infrared spectrum

In KBr pellets, the infrared spectra of L and its complex were observed between 400 and 4000 cm−1. It is possible to verify the synthesis of the ligand and the metal complex by closely comparing the IR spectra of the two compounds. Important details about the makeup of the functional groups attached to the metal atoms can be found in these bands. The distinctive infrared wavelengths of the L pro-ligand and its complex, together with their associated assignments (Fig. S3). The different bands that arise from –OH and –CH=N reveal information about the structure of the ligand and how it interacts with the metal. The vibration of extending of C=N is responsible for the band seen in the L ligand at 1631 cm−1. During complexation, this band moves to a lower frequency (1624). The shift in this band indicates unequivocally that azomethine’s nitrogen atoms play a role in the production of metal chelates44,45.

This is corroborated by the emergence of a band at 440 cm−1, which is the stretching vibration of the M–N bond. Bands at 539 cm−1 represent the stretching vibration of M–O46. The band at 3476 cm−1 in the ligand spectra is the result of free –OH stretching vibrations. This band has been shifted to 3434 cm−1, also known as the υ(OH) stretching vibration of crystallized water molecules, according to the elemental analysis data. In the produced complex, the band at 886 cm−1 (OH rocking) suggests the existence of coordinated water. The absorption band in the low-frequency region of the L imine ligand spectrum, located at 1335 cm−1, could perhaps be attributed to the stretching vibration of the phenolic group (C–O). The calculated frontier molecular orbital (FMO) parameters provide important insight into the electronic behavior of the free ligand and its Ru(III) complex. The ligand shows a HOMO energy of − 6.43 eV, while the Ru complex exhibits a higher HOMO level (− 5.12 eV), indicating that coordination to Ru increases the HOMO energy and enhances the electron-donating ability of the complex. In addition, the ligand possesses a lower LUMO energy (− 5.18 eV) compared to the Ru complex (− 3.98 eV), suggesting a relatively higher electron-accepting tendency for the ligand. Notably, the Ru complex displays a smaller HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE = 1.14 eV) than the free ligand (ΔE = 1.25 eV), reflecting higher polarizability, improved intramolecular charge transfer, and enhanced chemical reactivity upon complex formation. These electronic changes confirm the significant redistribution of electron density after coordination, as illustrated in Fig. 6. This concept is also reinforced by similar results associating corrosion rate with the energy gap (ΔE)52–54. The ionization energy for the ligand is higher (6.43 eV) than that of the Ru complex (5.12 eV), suggesting that the ligand is less stable and more difficult to ionize55. The electron affinity of the ligand (5.18 eV) is greater than that of the Ru complex (3.98 eV), indicating that the ligand is more capable of gaining electrons. The chemical hardness of the ligand (0.63) is slightly higher than that of the Ru complex (0.57), implying that the ligand is somewhat more resistant to charge transfer. The softness (σ) of the ligand (1.60) is less than that of the Ru complex (1.75), suggesting that the Ru complex is more reactive. This shows that the inhibitor with the least value of global hardness (hence the highest value of global softness) is the best and vice versa. This is because a soft molecule (inhibitor) is more reactive toward a metal surface than a hard molecule (inhibitor). The electronegativity of the ligand (5.81) is significantly higher than that of the Ru complex (4.55), indicating that the ligand has a stronger tendency to attract electrons. The global electrophilicity index for the ligand (26.96) is notably higher than that of the Ru complex (18.16), suggesting that the ligand is a better electrophile and may be more reactive in interactions with nucleophiles55. The back-donation energy is slightly more negative for the ligand (-0.16) compared to the Ru complex (− 0.14), indicating that the ligand may have a slightly stronger tendency for back-donation, which can influence its stability and reactivity.

The adsorption data clearly demonstrate that the Ru complex exhibits significantly stronger interaction with the Ni surface compared to the free ligand. This is evident from its highly negative total adsorption energy (− 3764.25 kJ/mol) versus the ligand (− 265.16 kJ/mol), indicating much greater adsorption stability. The more negative rigid adsorption energy (− 293.96 kJ/mol) and deformation energy (− 35.303 kJ/mol) of the complex suggest that it undergoes favorable structural reorganization upon adsorption, further enhancing surface interaction. Additionally, the adsorption energy per Ni atom (− 350.59 kJ/mol for the complex vs. − 30.21 kJ/mol for the ligand) confirms stronger local binding affinity. The complex also outperforms the ligand in competing with corrosive species such as H₂SO₄, as shown by its more negative dEad/dNi (− 3.89 vs. − 3.05), indicating a greater ability to displace aggressive ions and form a more stable protective film. Altogether, these parameters confirm that the Ru complex has superior adsorption strength, structural adaptability, and corrosion inhibition efficiency compared to the free ligand. (Fig. 7). The combined use of DFT calculations and MC simulations offers a detailed insight into the adsorption behavior of the Ru(III) complex on the Ni (110) surface. DFT analysis revealed that the nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), and bromine (Br) atoms in the complex serve as key reactive sites, as evidenced by molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) maps and frontier orbital distributions (HOMO–LUMO). These atoms possess high electron density, indicating their role in electron donation to the Ni surface. This theoretical finding is consistent with MC simulations, which demonstrated that the Ru complex adsorbs in a near-parallel orientation on the Ni surface, optimizing surface coverage and forming strong interactions via the N, O, and Br donor atoms. The much larger negative adsorption energy of the Ru complex (− 3764.25 kJ/mol) compared to the ligand alone (− 265.16 kJ/mol) further confirms that these heteroatoms enhance binding strength. Together, these computational methods establish that the adsorption of the Ru complex is primarily governed by interactions between Ni atoms and the N, O, and Br donor sites. This leads to the formation of a stable chemisorbed layer, which improves corrosion inhibition efficiency.

When structure size of corrosion inhibitor molecules increases, the inhibition efficiency typically increases for several important reasons such as enhanced surface coverage as larger molecules occupy more space on the metal surface, physically blocking more potential corrosion sites. This creates a more complete protective barrier between the metal and the corrosive medium. Also, stronger adsorption-larger inhibitor molecules generally contain more functional groups that can interact with the metal surface, creating stronger adsorption through multiple points of attachment. This leads to more stable and persistent protection. In addition, electron density effects as larger structures frequently possess greater electron density that can be donated to metal surfaces, enhancing the chemical bonding between inhibitor and metal surface. Plus, the physical bulk of larger molecules create steric hindrance that prevents corrosive species from reaching the metal surface, even at points where direct bonding may not occur. In some cases, larger molecules can form multilayer protective films through molecular stacking or cross-linking, providing additional barrier protection. The increased size reduces the diffusion rate of the inhibitor away from the metal surface, leading to more persistent protection over time. This relationship between molecular size and inhibition efficiency is one reason why many effective industrial corrosion inhibitors tend to be larger, complex organic molecules rather than simpler, smaller compounds.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

EIS is a powerful technique for studying the electrochemical properties of surfaces, interfaces, and materials. It is widely used in corrosion studies, battery performance analysis, and the characterization of adsorbate − inhibitor interactions on metallic surfaces, such as Ni (110). Verification of potentiodynamic polarization results was aided by examining EIS of nickel metal in 0.5 M H2SO4 with different concentrations of L and RuL. The complex plane of impedance plot at Ecorr is shown in Figs. 9 and 10. Both with and without varying inhibitor dosages, one capacitive loop can be seen in the impedance spectra acquired with Ni as the anode. The single semicircle shape of the spectrum showed that there was a corrosive reaction occurring at the interface between the electrolyte and the external surfaces. Figures 8 and 9 showed that the impedance response of Ni changed significantly when additives in different amounts were added to corrosive media. This can be explained by the fact that both the lowest and greatest inhibitor doses cause an increase in substrate impedance. Semicircle looks to deviate from ideal capacitive behaviour because of roughness considerations, external side heterogeneities, and SO42−ion adsorption61. However, as the quantity of L additive increases to 1 × 10−5 M charge transfer resistance increases, then decreases with increasing inhibitor dosage from (5 × 10−5 to 5 × 10−4 M). In the presence of high inhibitor dose 1 × 10−3 M, the charge transfer resistance increases again and be higher than that obtained at all other L doses. Increasing charge transfer resistance at higher inhibitor concentration (1 × 10−3 M) compared to lower concentrations is the expected trend in corrosion inhibition systems and occurs due to several fundamental mechanisms such as enhanced surface coverage—Higher inhibitor concentrations lead to more complete coverage of the metal surface, blocking a greater percentage of active corrosion sites. This directly increases the charge transfer resistance as fewer sites remain available for electrochemical reactions. Also, formation of more robust protective films—At higher concentrations, inhibitors can form more dense, continuous, and defect-free protective films on the metal surface. These films create a more effective barrier against charge transfer processes. In addition, multi-layer adsorption—Higher concentrations can enable the formation of multiple inhibitor layers, creating a thicker protective barrier that electrons must traverse, thereby increasing the resistance to charge transfer. So that, semicircle obtained in the presence of the high L concentration (1 × 10−3 M) is the best semicircle get at all dosages. This behaviour is also observed when RuL utilized62–65. Charge transfer resistance is in this order 1 × 10−3 > 1 × 10−5 > 5 × 10−5 > 1 × 10−4 > 5 × 10−4 > Blank. The double layer capacitance Cdl. also steadily drops in the aforementioned sequence when the additive dosage is reduced.

The superior corrodes inhibition properties of ruthenium complex compared to its constituent ligand can be attributed to several key factors. Enhanced electron donation capabilities as Ruthenium metal centers act as electron reservoirs that can donate electrons to the metal surface more effectively than ligands alone. The d-orbitals of Ru participate in back-bonding with the metal substrate, creating stronger metal-inhibitor interactions. This stronger electronic interaction results in more stable adsorption and greater resistance to desorption. Also, synergistic effects between Ru and ligands as the combination of Ru and ligands creates electron delocalization across the entire complex. This delocalization enhances the overall adsorption strength through multiple coordination points. Ligands can be positioned optimally around the metal center to maximize surface interactions. In addition, increased molecular size and planar structure as RuL complex have significantly larger molecular footprints than individual ligands. A single complex can block multiple corrosion active sites simultaneously. Also, the planar or pseudo-planar structure of many Ru complexes enables them to lie flat on the metal surface, maximizing coverage. Plus, multiple anchoring points as complexes can adsorb through multiple functional groups simultaneously. This "multi-point anchoring" creates more stable adsorption than single-point binding of individual ligands. If one attachment point is temporarily broken, others maintain adhesion to the surface. Additionally, altered electron density distribution because coordination to Ru significantly changes the electron density distribution in ligands. This redistribution often creates more polarized regions that interact more strongly with the metal surface. The metal center can tune the electronic properties of attached ligands for optimal interaction.

The corrosion inhibition process and the potential of zero charge (PZC)

The idea that these inhibitors adsorb on the surface of the corroding metal can be used to explain how the inhibitors under investigation reduce corrosion. Numerous parameters, including complex formation, molecular size, the mode of interaction with the metal surface, and the quantity and charge density of the molecule’s adsorption active sites, affect how effective the inhibitory chemicals are at inhibiting74.

The potential zero charge (PZC) may help determine the type of adsorption that occurs at the metal outer side75. Metal surface charge can be defined by the open circuit potential’s location in relation to the associated PZC. For figuring out the metal’s PZC, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) method was utilized. When the difference of Eɸ = Ecorr − EPZC was negative, while Eɸ is then Antropov ‘rational’ corrosion potential, Since the metal surface has a negative net charge, cation adsorption has been preferred. On the other hand, when Eɸ became positive, anion adsorption was preferred. The PZC of the Ni in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution with the addition of 1.0 mM L and RuL inhibitors was determined after 1 h and found to be − 0.135 V and − 0.098 for L and RuL, respectively. The Ecorr of the Ni in the same conditions was − 0.146 V and − 0.110 V for L and RuL, respectively. The difference, Eɸ = − 0.011 and − 0.012 V (SCE) for Land RuL, respectively, where Eɸ is negative, this suggests that following an hour of exposure, the electrode surface has a negative charge at the open circuit voltage. Since the corroding Ni exterior side is known to have a negative charge under tested conditions, L and RuL molecules exist as protonated by nitrogen and/or OH atoms and are in equilibrium with similar molecular forms76. Metal surfaces may adsorb the protonated inhibitor molecules77. Ultimately, the L and RuL molecules create a compacted adsorption layer that prevents Ni from corroding78,79.

Despite the comprehensive experimental and theoretical investigations presented, the study still has some limitations. The work is confined to laboratory-scale experiments, which may not fully represent the complexities and operational challenges of large-scale or industrial systems, such as flow conditions, impurities, and long-term exposure.
