Comprehensive investigation of long non-coding RNA HOTAIR polymorphisms and cancer risk: a current meta-analysis encompassing 96,458 participants
Authors: B Madhu Krishna, Pankaj Garg, Sravani Ramisetty, Ayalur Raghu Subbalakshmi, Prakash Kulkarni, Ravi Salgia, Sharad S Singhal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer ranks as the second leading cause of mortality worldwide, prompting extensive investigations into factors contributing to its development. Among these factors, genetic variations, known as genotypic polymorphisms, have been identified as significant influencers in the susceptibility to various types of cancer. Recent research has focused on exploring the connection between polymorphisms in the Long Non-coding RNA HOTAIR and cancer risk. However, the results from these studies have been inconsistent, leading to ambiguity and controversy. To address this uncertainty, we conducted a systematic analysis by gathering relevant studies from PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar. Specifically, we focused on three well-studied polymorphisms within the HOTAIR lncRNA (HOTAIR rs920778 C > T, HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T, HOTAIR rs4759314 A > G) and their association with cancer risk. Our meta-analysis included data from 48 case–control studies involving 42,321 cases and 54,137 controls. The results of our updated meta-analysis revealed a significant correlation between HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and HOTAIR rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms and overall cancer risk, particularly in the homozygous and recessive genetic models. Subgroup analysis further revealed that these associations were notably pronounced in the Asian population but not observed in the Iranian population. Furthermore, our findings underscore the potential of HOTAIR polymorphisms as diagnostic markers for overall cancer risk, particularly in gynecological cancers, precisely, HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T polymorphism in breast cancer. In conclusion, our systematic analysis provides compelling evidence that Long Non-coding RNA HOTAIR polymorphisms are linked to cancer risk, particularly in certain populations and cancer types, suggesting their potential clinical relevance as diagnostic indicators.Keywords: Long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), HOX transcript antisense intergenic RNA (HOTAIR), Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), Gynecological cancerSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Cancer is a prominent contributor to mortality rates in the United States, as indicated by recent data forecasting 2,001,140 new cases and 611,720 deaths from cancer in the US alone by 20241. The escalating number of cancer patients annually in both developed and developing nations underscores the perplexing nature of the molecular pathways leading to malignancies2–4. Various factors such as smoking, adiposity, infectious agents, and alcohol abuse have been linked to the susceptibility, onset, and fatality of cancer. Investigations have unveiled the oncogenic potential of diverse genetic variations, prompting heightened scrutiny as potential risk determinants5,6. Among these, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are prevalent genetic elements associated with multiple cancer forms7–9.

Long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) constitutes a category of RNA molecules exceeding 200 nucleotides in length and were previously disregarded due to their lack of protein-coding capability10–12. Recent breakthroughs, however, have elucidated the pivotal role of lncRNAs play in the modulation of gene expression13, establishing them as significant participants in epigenetic regulation across a spectrum of biological processes, encompassing the progression of cancer14–16. HOX transcript antisense intergenic RNA (HOTAIR) is one such lncRNA, which is located on chromosome 12q13.13 and transcribed from the antisense strand of homeobox C gene (HOXC) locus17. HOTAIR modulates chromatin remodeling by methylating both H3K27 and H3K4 via directing polycomb repressor complex 2 onto the specific target gene, especially tumor suppressor genes18,19. Recent studies have suggested that numerous human cancers, including those of the breast, gastric, colon, and liver, exhibit aberrant expression of HOTAIR in comparison with their counterparts20. Furthermore, HOTAIR expression was found to be associated with cancer susceptibility, cell cycle progression, tumor growth, metastasis, and drug resistance in various cancer types, which suggests that HOTAIR could be a valuable biomarker for tumorigenesis and progression21. It is evident that genetic variability could be causative for the abnormal expression of the HOTAIR lncRNA22. Thus, HOTAIR polymorphisms could possibly be associated with cancer risk and progression.

Several studies compared specific genetic variations called SNPs within the HOTAIR lncRNA and their potential link to cancer risk. Among these SNPs, some of the most studied ones are rs920778, rs4759314, rs1899663, rs7958904, and rs874945. However, the findings from these studies have been conflicting and inconclusive. For instance, Zhang et al. found a correlation between HOTAIR SNPs (rs920778, rs4759314, and rs1899663) and higher risk of Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) in the Chinese population23. On the other hand, Hu et al. in 2017 found no such association with pancreatic cancer24, while Jiang’s study observed a strong correlation with pancreatic cancer susceptibility25. Similar investigations have been conducted for various other cancer types like breast, ovarian, cervical, lung, and gastric cancers, but the results have been contradictory across different reports26–30. This inconsistency highlights the necessity for more comprehensive studies with larger sample sizes to draw reliable conclusions.

To address this, meta-analysis has been conducted by Liu et al.31, where they analyzed studies up until 2019 and concluded that certain HOTAIR SNPs (rs920778, rs1899663, rs4759314, rs12826786 and rs874945) are indeed associated with cancer risk. However, inclusion of populations, like Iranians and Turks, as Caucasians in these analyses is debatable, which adds complexity to interpreting the results accurately. Given the ongoing research, including 23 additional studies since 2019, we conducted our own meta-analysis using 48 articles with a total of 90 studies, focusing on the correlation between HOTAIR rs920778, rs1899663, and rs4759314 SNPs and increased cancer risk23–30,32–72. Specifically, we looked at gynecological cancers collectively (breast, ovarian, and cervical) and independently at breast cancer. Our aim is to provide clearer and more precise information regarding the association of HOTAIR SNPs with overall cancer risk, as well as their specific impact on gynecological and breast cancer.

Results

Characteristics of eligible studies included in meta-analysis

The initial search across PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar resulted in a total of 1046 findings. Among these, 525 articles were excluded as they were either reviews or abstracts. The remaining articles underwent a meticulous evaluation, with abstracts being read to identify potential candidates for the meta-analysis. Around 85 articles with full texts were retrieved and thoroughly examined. From these, 48 case–control studies focusing on three specific HOTAIR polymorphisms were deemed suitable for inclusion in the meta-analysis, encompassing a total of 42,321 cases and 54,137 controls. To break it down further, there were 28 studies addressing the HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism, 26 studies covering the HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T polymorphism, and 36 studies discussing the HOTAIR rs4759314 A > G polymorphism. Each eligible study provided data on genotype and allele frequencies, with detailed characteristics outlined in Table 1. The selection process of these studies is visually represented in the PRISMA flow chart, included as Fig. 1. Moreover, studies focusing solely on gynecological cancers and those specifically on breast cancer were segregated and analyzed separately. Additionally, studies were categorized based on the methodologies used for detecting HOTAIR polymorphisms (such as RFLP, TaqMan, and other techniques), with separate analyses conducted for each subgroup.

Heterogeneity and publication bias

Heterogeneity was noticed among the studies, particularly in the recessive genetic model of the HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism. However, there was an absence of heterogeneity in any of the allelic and genotypic models concerning the HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms. Interestingly, both the gynecological and breast cancer subgroups displayed uniformity across all allelic and genotypic models for all three polymorphisms, without any signs of heterogeneity. An intriguing observation arose from the method-based subgroup analysis, revealing heterogeneity in the recessive genotypic model (utilizing the RFLP method) and the dominant genotypic model (employing the TaqMan method) of the HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism. However, similar to the overall cancer analysis, there was no heterogeneity observed in any of the models for the HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms. To evaluate publication bias among the studies, Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s regression test were employed. Studies indicating significant publication bias had their results adjusted accordingly. Stratified statistical analysis

In this study, a comprehensive analysis encompassed 48 case–control studies, delineating 28 studies focused on HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism, 26 studies on HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T polymorphism, and 36 studies on HOTAIR rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms. These polymorphisms underwent scrutiny regarding their correlation with overall cancer risk. The cumulative analysis revealed significant associations in homozygous and recessive genotypic models for HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T (Homozygous model TT vs. GG: OR = 1.285, 95% CI = 1.126–1.466, p = 0.001; Recessive model TT vs. GG + GT: OR = 1.316, 95% CI = 1.162–1.491, p = 0.001) and rs4759314 A > G (Homozygous model GG vs. AA: OR = 1.321, 95% CI = 1.110–1.572, p = 0.002; Recessive model GG vs. AA + AG: OR = 1.258, 95% CI = 1.064–1.486, p = 0.007) polymorphisms, indicating a heightened susceptibility to overall cancer risk (Figs. 2 and 3). In contrast, the dominant genotypic model of HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism (Dominant model: CC + TC vs. TT: OR = 0.827, 95% CI = 0.693–0.986, p = 0.034) demonstrated an association with reduced risk of cancer development (Fig. 4).

The p value < 0.05 was considered as significant. The OR > 1 was considered as associated with cancer risk.

However, various genotypic models for each HOTAIR polymorphism did not reveal any statistically significant association with cancer risk: for HOTAIR rs920778 C > T, the overall allele model C vs. T showed an odds ratio (OR) of 0.902 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.804–1.011 (p = 0.077), the homozygous model CC vs. TT had an OR of 0.826 with a 95% CI of 0.639–1.067 (p = 0.144), the heterozygous model TC vs. TT had an OR of 0.859 with a 95% CI of 0.737–1.002 (p = 0.053), and the recessive model CC vs. TT + TC showed an OR of 0.907 with a 95% CI of 0.789–1.042 (p = 0.169). Similarly, for HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T, the overall allele model T vs. G had an OR of 1.051 with a 95% CI of 0.973–1.135 (p = 0.204), the heterozygous model GT vs. GG had an OR of 0.946 with a 95% CI of 0.838–1.068 (p = 0.372), and the dominant model TT + GT vs. GG had an OR of 0.997 with a 95% CI of 0.894–1.111 (p = 0.953). Moreover, the HOTAIR rs4759314 A > G polymorphism did not show any significant association with cancer risk across all allele and genotypic models (p > 0.05). Additionally, subgroup analyses based on the detection technique used for HOTAIR polymorphism revealed slightly significant associations with cancer resistance in specific genotypic models: the RFLP technique subgroup for HOTAIR rs920778 C > T showed significance in the recessive genotypic model (OR = 0.835, 95% CI = 0.697–1.000, p = 0.049). Conversely, no significant association was observed for HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T in all allelic and genotypic models within the RFLP subgroup, while the TaqMan genotypic technique subgroup exhibited strong associations in the homozygous (OR = 1.605, 95% CI = 1.112–2.318, p = 0.012) and recessive (OR = 1.894, 95% CI = 1.511–2.374, p = 0.001) genotypic models for HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T. However, HOTAIR rs4759314 A > G showed no significant association with cancer risk across all allele and genotypic models in both subgroup analyses. Subgroup analysis

The investigation focused on gynecological cancers (Breast, Cervical, and Ovarian cancers) and subcategorized them to analyze their connection with HOTAIR polymorphisms. Across all allelic and genotypic models, no link was found between HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism and the risk of gynecological cancers. In contrast, the homozygous and recessive genotypic models of HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T (Homozygous model TT vs. GG: OR = 1.307, 95% CI = 1.054–1.620, p = 0.015; Recessive model TT vs. GG + GT: OR = 1.264, 95% CI = 1.030–1.552, p = 0.025) and rs4759314 A > G (Homozygous model GG vs. AA: OR = 1.544, 95% CI = 1.089–2.190, p = 0.015; Recessive model GG vs. AA + AG: OR = 1.510, 95% CI = 1.066–2.140, p = 0.020) polymorphisms exhibited a robust association with the risk of gynecological cancers (Figs. 5 and 6). Additionally, a subgroup analysis focusing on the association of HOTAIR polymorphisms with breast cancer risk revealed that while HOTAIR rs920778 C > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms did not show a significant link, the homozygous and recessive genotypic models of HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T (Homozygous model TT vs. GG: OR = 1.335, 95% CI = 1.065–1.674, p = 0.012; Recessive model TT vs. GG + GT: OR = 1.287, 95% CI = 1.038–1.595, p = 0.022) were notably associated with an increased risk of breast cancer (Fig. 7) [Table 3].

Additionally, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on the ethnic background of the samples. Studies examining the correlation between HOTAIR polymorphisms and cancer risk in Asian and Iranian populations were sub-grouped and subjected to meta-analysis. Intriguingly, the HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism exhibited a significant association with resistance to cancer among the Asian population. Across all genetic and allele models of HOTAIR rs920778 C > T (HOTAIR rs920778 C > T, Overall allele model C vs. T: OR = 0.867, 95% CI = 0.769–0.978, p = 0.020, Homozygous model CC vs. TT: OR = 0.728, 95% CI = 0.548–0.967, p = 0.028, Heterozygous model TC vs.TT: OR = 0.847, 95% CI = 0.720–0.996, p = 0.045, Recessive model CC vs. TT + TC: OR = 0.842, 95% CI = 0.732–0.969, p = 0.016, Dominant CC + TC vs TT: OR = 0.786, 95% CI = 0.649–0.953, p = 0.014), strong resistance to cancer risk was observed within the Asian subgroup. Conversely, the homozygous and recessive genotypic models of HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T (Homozygous model TT vs. GG: OR = 1.348, 95% CI = 1.145–1.587, p = 0.001; Recessive model TT vs. GG + GT: OR = 1.322, 95% CI = 1.127–1.551, p = 0.001) and rs4759314 A > G (Homozygous model GG vs. AA: OR = 1.292, 95% CI = 1.079–1.547, p = 0.005; Recessive model GG vs. AA + AG: OR = 1.229, 95% CI = 1.034–1.460, p = 0.019) polymorphisms showed a strong association with cancer risk specifically in the Asian ethnic group. These findings echoed the trends observed in gynecological cancer data. Furthermore, the subgroup analysis revealed no significant association of these HOTAIR polymorphisms with cancer risk in the Iranian population. Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis to assess how each study affected the combined odds ratio was conducted. This involved iteratively excluding each relevant study and performing a meta-analysis. The results indicated that no single study significantly influenced the combined odds ratio, indicating the credibility and robustness of our meta-analysis.

Discussion

The unchecked cell division that characterizes cancer is a growing issue globally, affecting both developed and developing nations. This surge in cancer cases has become a significant public health challenge1. Early detection through effective screening is crucial for improving patient outcomes and reducing relapse rates73. Researchers have been exploring genetic mutations as potential biomarkers for early cancer detection, uncovering a series of mutations linked to cancer development74. Despite decades of research into cancer’s mechanisms, our understanding remains limited, highlighting the necessity for continued investigation to deepen our insights.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a diverse group of RNA molecules exceeding 200 nucleotides in length, distinguished by their lack of protein-coding capacity10–12. Recent research highlights the crucial role lncRNAs play in regulating transcription, cell differentiation, and maintaining cellular pluripotency75–77. Interestingly, abnormal lncRNA expression has been implicated in various diseases, particularly cancer. Among these lncRNAs, HOTAIR stands out as an oncogene strongly linked to cancer grade and poor prognosis across multiple cancer types like breast, gastric, and colon cancer14–16,20. HOTAIR’s involvement spans tumor growth, recurrence, migration, invasion, cell cycle progression, stem cell characteristics, metastasis, drug resistance, and cancer susceptibility21. Growing evidence also suggests that HOTAIR polymorphisms significantly increase the risk of cancer development. The groundbreaking work by Zhang et al. first revealed the notable association of HOTAIR rs920778 with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma risk in the Chinese population23. Subsequently, numerous researchers explored HOTAIR polymorphisms in various cancer types. However, these studies yielded conflicting and inconclusive results, prompting our meta-analysis.

Earlier several researchers performed meta-analysis to conclude the association of HOTAIR polymorphisms with increased risk of cancer. In 2016, researchers performed a meta-analysis and found no association of HOTAIR rs4759314 polymorphism with increased risk of cancer78. In the same year Tian et al. suggested that HOTAIR rs920778 polymorphism is associated with increased risk of cancer79. Furthermore, in 2017 Zhang et al., performed meta-analysis using 11 studies and found that HOTAIR polymorphisms rs920778, rs4759314, rs7928904, and rs874945 are susceptible to overall cancer risk, especially HOTAIR rs1899663 polymorphism was found to be highly associated with cancer risk in Asian ethnicity80. Similar results were obtained for HOTAIR polymorphisms except “rs1899663” by Liu et al. with 21 eligible case–control studies, however, these results were confined to Chinese population81. Furthermore, Chu et al. confirmed the association of HOTAIR rs920778 polymorphism with cancer susceptibility82. In 2018, Min et al. assessed the correlation between 6 HOTAIR polymorphisms with cancer susceptibility with 36 eligible studies included and found that, except HOTAIR rs920778, and rs12826786 no polymorphism was found to be significantly associated with cancer risk83. The most recent meta-analysis performed by Liu et al. in 2020 suggested that these polymorphisms indeed were in association with increased risk of cancer31. However, these studies either included limited number of studies or mis-represented ethnic population, inclusion of large datasets will provide clear picture of association of HOTAIR polymorphism with cancer risk.

This meta-analysis, drawing from 48 case–control studies with substantial sample sizes, aimed to definitively establish the association between HOTAIR polymorphisms rs920778 C > T, rs1899663 G > T, rs4759314 A > G, and overall cancer risk. HOTAIR rs920778 C > T, rs1899663 G > T, and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms are among the most extensively studied genetic variations, linked conclusively to disease development and progression, notably in cancer. In our study, we discovered a strong association between HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms and an increased risk of cancer, particularly in two specific genotypic models (homozygous and recessive) for each polymorphism. Interestingly, the genotypic model of rs920778 C > T polymorphism showed resistance to cancer development. Moreover, when we analyzed data from studies that used the RFLP method to detect rs920778 C > T polymorphism, we found consistent evidence of resistance in the recessive genotypic model. These results were contradicting to all meta-analysis ever performed before and we are the first group ever to report the association of rs920778 C > T polymorphism and resistance to cancer development. Furthermore, we observed a significant association between the homozygous and recessive models of HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms and susceptibility to gynecological cancers overall. Similar associations were found in studies focusing solely on breast cancer for the HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T polymorphism. Ethnicity-based subgroup analysis revealed a robust association of HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms with cancer risk in Asian populations. Conversely, HOTAIR rs920778 C > T polymorphism showed contrasting results, suggesting that these specific polymorphisms could be valuable biomarkers, particularly among Asian individuals. However, none of the HOTAIR polymorphisms showed a significant association with cancer risk in the Iranian population. Our study’s conclusions remained consistent across different genetic models for HOTAIR rs920778 C > T, rs1899663 G > T, and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms, adding to the study’s strength and reliability. Additionally, our stratified analysis highlighted a ~ 1.3-fold increased cancer risk in carriers of specific genotypes compared to others, reinforcing the significance of these polymorphisms in cancer susceptibility. Notably, our study demonstrated minimal publication bias, as indicated by Egger’s regression test results, except for the rs920778 C > T recessive genotypic model, further affirming the reliability and robustness of our findings. Sensitivity analysis also validated the credibility of our results.

While previous meta-analyses have explored the relationship between HOTAIR polymorphisms and cancer susceptibility, our study offers several distinct advantages. The most recent meta-analysis, conducted by Liu et al. in 2020, covered reports published until 201931. In contrast, our study encompasses 12 new case–control studies published up to September 2023, incorporating 14,522 new subjects. To our knowledge, all prior meta-analyses have evaluated the association of HOTAIR polymorphisms with overall cancer risk. However, our analysis delves into the correlation between HOTAIR polymorphisms and an elevated risk of overall gynecological cancer and breast cancer. Notably, our study is pioneering in its meta-analysis concerning the association of HOTAIR polymorphisms specifically with Asian and Iranian ethnic groups, as well as the cumulative effects on gynecological cancer types and breast cancer.

Conclusion

The current study reveals a strong correlation between HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms and the overall risk of cancer. These genetic variations are also linked to increased risks of gynecological cancers and breast cancers. This study hints at the potential of HOTAIR rs1899663 G > T and rs4759314 A > G polymorphisms as biomarkers for early cancer detection.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy and identification of relevant studies

An extensive and methodical approach to search for relevant studies in established online databases like PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar was employed. Utilizing a combination of specific keywords such as “Hox transcript antisense intergenic RNA polymorphism,” “HOTAIR polymorphism,” “rs920778,” “rs1899663,” “rs4759314,” and “Cancer,” three researchers independently identified suitable research articles. Additionally, we meticulously examined cross-references to uncover any potentially overlooked studies. Our study encompasses all relevant publications up to January 2024, including 48 case–control studies with 42,321 cases and 54,137 controls focused on exploring HOTAIR polymorphisms.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of studies

The current study encompassed research that scrutinized the correlation between HOTAIR polymorphisms and cancer risk, meeting stringent inclusion criteria: publication in English, inclusion of both case and control groups, presentation of original data featuring genotypic frequencies for both cases and controls or reporting odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Excluded from this study were articles not published in English, those exclusively focused on case samples, investigations linking HOTAIR polymorphisms to non-cancer diseases, studies lacking genetic distribution and allelic frequency data, abstracts, reviews, redundant studies, and duplicates.

Data extraction

The critical data required for meta-analysis was meticulously extracted through a comprehensive evaluation of each publication, a process conducted independently by three researchers. These researchers adhered to a standardized data extraction protocol, gathering essential information such as authors’ first names, publication year, country of origin, ethnic demographics, sample sizes for case–control studies, genotyping methodologies employed, and both genotypic and allelic frequencies. Any disparities encountered during data extraction were addressed through collaborative group discussions among the researchers, ensuring accuracy and reliability in the collected data.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted utilizing the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis Software (CMA version 4) from Biostat (located at 14 North Dean Street, Englewood, NJ 07631, USA). This analysis aimed to explore the correlation between HOTAIR polymorphisms and cancer risk by computing combined odds ratios (ORs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values. To evaluate the degree of heterogeneity across the included studies, the chi-squared based Cochran’s Q test was employed. A higher degree of heterogeneity, indicated by an I2 value exceeding 50 and a p-value below 0.05, was noted among certain reported research periodicals. For studies exhibiting heterogeneity, a random-effects model was utilized, whereas a fixed-effects model was applied to studies with homogeneity. The presence of publication bias and the symmetry of Begg’s funnel plots were assessed using Egger’s regression test, with the significance of intercept evaluated via a t-test. Additionally, the "Trim and Fill" method was implemented to rectify asymmetrical funnel plots and render them symmetrical.
