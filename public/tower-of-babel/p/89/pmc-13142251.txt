Pharmacokinetics of cefazolin in bariatric surgery patients: a preoperative perspective
Authors: Alexandra Porlier, Mélanie Nadeau, Jérôme Laflamme, Sylvie Pilote, Philippe Gervais, Benoit Drolet, André Tchernof, Laurent Biertho, Simon Marceau, Chantale Simard, François Julien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesOptimal cefazolin dosing for surgical prophylaxis in patients with severe obesity remains controversial, particularly regarding tissue exposure at the surgical site. Plasma concentrations may not reliably reflect pharmacologically active antibiotic levels in target tissues. This study aims to characterize total and calculated unbound concentrations of cefazolin in subcutaneous adipose tissue, dermis and plasma in patients with severe obesity undergoing bariatric surgery, and to assess pharmacodynamic target attainment across clinically relevant MIC thresholds.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, patients undergoing sleeve gastrectomy received cefazolin prophylaxis according to current guidelines (2 or 3 g intravenously). Plasma, dermis and adipose tissue samples were collected at incision (T0), 30 min (T30) and end of surgery (Tend). Total cefazolin concentrations were quantified using validated HPLC methods. Concentration–time profiles were analysed using linear mixed–effects models adjusted for age, BMI and creatinine clearance. Target attainment was evaluated at MIC thresholds of 2, 4 and 8 mg/L.ResultsDermis and plasma total cefazolin concentrations consistently exceeded all MIC thresholds in both dosing groups, whereas adipose tissue concentrations were more variable, with reduced attainment at higher MICs. Calculated unbound cefazolin concentrations demonstrated marked matrix–dependent differences: plasma unbound levels consistently exceeded all targets, dermal unbound concentrations increased significantly from T0 to Tend and adipose tissue unbound concentrations remained low across all time points. Increasing the dose from 2 to 3 g modestly improved total adipose tissue target attainment but did not substantially enhance unbound adipose tissue exposure. No surgical site infections occurred.ConclusionsIn patients with severe obesity, guideline–recommended cefazolin dosing provides adequate plasma and dermis exposure but fails to ensure reliable unbound adipose tissue concentrations. These findings highlight the limitations of plasma-based assessments and support tissue–informed, individualized prophylactic strategies in bariatric surgery.

Introduction

Cefazolin (Ancef®) is widely used as a first-line antibiotic for preoperative prophylaxis because of its favourable pharmacokinetic properties, affordability, safety profile and well-documented effectiveness against pathogens frequently implicated in surgical site infections (SSIs), such as methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), Streptococcus spp. and E. coli.1 In bariatric surgery, where the risk of SSIs is elevated, cefazolin is typically administered within 60 min prior to incision.1 However, evidence guiding optimal dosing in patients with obesity remains limited and sometimes inconsistent.2–6

The number of bariatric procedures continues to rise, with over 270 000 operations performed annually in the USA and Canada.7 In Canada specifically, 8000–9000 bariatric surgeries are performed each year. Although relatively infrequent (less than 1% in laparoscopic procedures), SSIs are associated with increased morbidity, prolonged hospitalization and higher healthcare costs.8–11 Ensuring adequate perioperative antimicrobial exposure in this population is therefore essential.

Current US guidelines recommend intravenous (IV) cefazolin doses 2 g for patients weighing 80–120 kg and 3 g for those above 120 kg.1,12,13 Nevertheless, available pharmacokinetic data remain conflicting. While several pharmacokinetic studies support the adequacy of 2 g dose in patients with obesity, others report suboptimal cefazolin concentrations in adipose tissue.14 Plasma concentrations alone are an imperfect surrogate for prophylactic efficacy, as tissue exposure at the surgical site is more clinically relevant.15 Microdialysis studies have demonstrated reduced cefazolin penetration into adipose tissue with increasing body weight, whereas other investigators have reported tissue concentrations consistently exceeding the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for MSSA.16,17 More recent pharmacokinetic modelling and clinical studies in patients with severe obesity suggest that guideline-recommended dosing may still provide adequate coverage when administered appropriately.15,18,19

Interpretation of these findings is further complicated by variability in pharmacodynamic target concentrations used across studies. Proposed cefazolin targets range from 2 mg/L for MSSA to 8 mg/L for certain Enterobacterales, depending on pathogen susceptibility profiles and underlying pharmacodynamic assumptions.20 Historically, an 8 mg/L threshold was frequently used, reflecting earlier Clinical Laboratory Standard Institute susceptibility criteria and conservative dosing strategies aiming to maintain four times the MIC throughout the surgery.21 However, most MSSA isolates have MIC values closer of 1–2 mg/L, and current European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing epidemiological cut-off values for Enterobacterales are approximately 4 mg/L.20

Recent evidence has increasingly emphasized the relevance of unbound (free) cefazolin concentrations and the timing of exposure at skin incision and wound closure, rather than reliance on total plasma concentrations alone. In a recent systematic review, Ryan et al. (2025) demonstrated substantial heterogeneity across surgical populations and showed that achieving unbound plasma or tissue concentrations > 2 mg/L at incision and/or closure was commonly used as a pragmatic pharmacodynamic benchmark, while highlighting ongoing uncertainty regarding optimal targets in patients with obesity.22 Accordingly, more recent investigations have proposed lower pharmacodynamic targets, particularly when MSSA is considered the primary pathogen. For example, Cinotti et al. evaluated cefazolin tissue exposure in bariatric surgery using a 4 mg/L target.23 In addition, a comprehensive review by Nothofer et al. highlighted that contemporary MIC distributions and pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics considerations increasingly support target concentrations in the 2–4 mg/L range and emphasized that higher thresholds such as 8 mg/L should be interpreted as historically derived, conservative reference values rather than contemporary clinical breakpoints.21

This study aimed to evaluate subcutaneous adipose tissue, dermis and plasma concentrations of total and unbound cefazolin administered according to current dosing guidelines and to determine the proportion of patients with severe obesity in whom these concentrations reach established pharmacodynamic targets for effective SSI prevention.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This prospective, analytically oriented cohort study was conducted to investigate plasma, adipose tissue and dermis concentrations of total and unbound cefazolin following administration of standard doses recommended by the US clinical practice guidelines.1

Eligible participants were male or female adults aged over 18 years with a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 who underwent sleeve gastrectomy between April 2019 and February 2022 at the Institut universitaire de cardiologie et de pneumologie de Québec - Université Laval (IUCPQ-ULaval) (n = 70; 60 received cefazolin and 10 received clindamycin).

The IUCPQ-ULaval Research Ethics Board reviewed and approved the study (approval numbers 2019-3053, 21626), and the protocol was listed on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07253805).24

Eligibility criteria and renal function assessment

Eligibility and exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had a known allergy to penicillin (except for 10 control patients who received clindamycin), were pregnant or breastfeeding, had hepatic cirrhosis and chronic renal insufficiency [creatinine clearance (CLcr) < 60 mL/min as estimated by the Cockcroft–Gault (CG) equation] or experienced intraoperative blood loss ≥ 1 L.25

Renal function estimation in patients with obesity

Because all participants had obesity, renal function was estimated using multiple equations to account for the known limitations of traditional creatinine-based formulas in this population. In addition to the standard CG equation, glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and CLcr were also calculated using Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD), CKD-EPI 2021 and the lean-body-weight-adjusted CG LBW2005 equations.26–29 Preoperative serum creatinine values were used for all calculations. Ideal, adjusted, lean and fat-free weights were derived using standard formulas and applied when required.28 These weight metrics and renal function estimates are presented in Table 1.

Study procedures

Participant recruitment and preoperative evaluation

Each potential participant was evaluated by a bariatric surgeon at the IUCPQ-ULaval during their preoperative consultation. If deemed eligible for the study, the patient was approached the same day by a research nurse, who explained the study protocol, answered any questions and obtained written informed consent from those who agreed to participate. On the same day, demographic data were collected, and routine preoperative blood samples were drawn. Data on renal and hepatic function were retrieved from the electronic medical records. Comorbid diseases were also systematically recorded as part of the preoperative assessment.

Ten patients with a documented penicillin allergy who were scheduled for sleeve gastrectomy were recruited during their preoperative consultation. Each received an alternative antibiotic prophylaxis using clindamycin. Tissue and plasma samples collected from these patients—confirmed to be free of cefazolin—were used to validate the HPLC assay and to prepare matrix-based standard curves.

Preoperative preparation and consent

All participants scheduled for laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy were admitted to IUCPQ-ULaval the day before surgery. They were met by the surgeon, who reviewed preoperative tests and bloodwork, confirmed the patient’s fitness for surgery, addressed any remaining questions, obtained written surgical consent and reconfirmed the patient’s willingness to participate in the research study.

Antibiotic administration protocol

On the day of surgery, participants received an IV bolus of cefazolin at induction, with the dose adjusted according to body weight: 2 g IV for patients under 120 kg and 3 g IV for those weighing 120 kg or more. Control patients received a 900 mg IV perfusion of clindamycin administered over 30–45 min. To standardize the timing between antibiotic administration and the initial incision, cefazolin or clindamycin was administered by the anaesthesiologist or respiratory therapist at the time of chlorhexidine skin preparation.

Tissue sampling and storage

Subcutaneous adipose tissue (∼1 g) and skin (∼0.6 cm2) were collected from the incision site at three time points: at the time of incision (T0), 30 min after the start (T30) and end of surgery (Tend). Samples were stored at −80°C until cefazolin concentrations were analysed using HPLC. All samples were analysed in batches and underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle prior to analysis.

Blood sampling and plasma storage

At predefined intervals (T0, T30, Tend), 2 mL blood specimens were collected in K3 EDTA tubes, kept cold and centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min), and plasma was preserved at −80 °C for later cefazolin measurement using HPLC. Samples were analysed in batches and were subjected to a single freeze–thaw cycle.

Detailed bioanalytical methods, including sample extraction procedures, chromatographic conditions, method validation and unbound concentration calculations, are provided in the Supplementary Information (available as Supplementary data at JAC Online) document. Unbound concentrations were estimated using an albumin-binding model, as detailed in the Supplementary Information. Although this approach relies on assumptions of equilibrium across matrices, it represents a pragmatic method to approximate pharmacologically active exposure in the absence of microdialysis.

Statistical analyses

Data were compiled in Microsoft Excel® and analysed using SAS® 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). Because each participant contributed only three pharmacokinetic measurements (T0, T30, Tend), nonlinear mixed-effects modelling was not feasible. We therefore used a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept to account for repeated measures within participants.

Concentration data were log transformed to improve normality and homogeneity of variance. Model assumptions were evaluated using standard diagnostics, including tests for residual normality and heteroscedasticity. Fixed effects included dose group, time point and the dose–time interaction, while the variable ‘participant’ was modelled as a random effect. Analyses were adjusted for prespecified covariates (age, BMI and creatinine clearance). Sensitivity analyses using untransformed concentration produced consistent results.

Categorical variables (sex, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia and sleep apnoea) were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Patient demographics characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and total and unbound cefazolin concentrations as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses. Figures were created using GraphPad Prism® (version 11.0.0; GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 70 patients (52 women, 18 men) scheduled for a sleeve gastrectomy were included in the study. Of these, 21 received a 2 g dose of cefazolin, 39 received a 3 g dose and 10 received a 900 mg dose of clindamycin, in accordance with current prophylactic guidelines.1 Clinical and demographic characteristics of the study population—excluding patients who received clindamycin—are summarized in Table 1. No postoperative SSIs occurred in any patient during the follow-up period.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the two cefazolin dose groups with respect to surgery duration or intraoperative blood loss. However, significant between-group differences were noted for several baseline variables, including sex distribution, age, height, total body weight (at recruitment and admission), BMI (at recruitment and admission) and creatinine clearance.

Given these baseline imbalances, particularly for variables known to influence cefazolin pharmacokinetics, all subsequent concentration analyses were adjusted for the prespecified covariates age, BMI and creatinine clearance using linear mixed-effects models. Time was included as a repeated measure, and a subject-specific random intercept was incorporated to account for within-patient correlation. Additional analyses evaluated the potential modifying effect of sex by testing sex-by-dose interactions and comparing models with and without sex as a covariate. These analyses confirmed that sex did not materially influence the results, and all adjusted models yielded conclusions consistent with the primary analyses.

Analytical method validation

Under the specified chromatographic conditions, total cefazolin was eluted at 8 min (Figure 1a) and cefoxitin at 10 min. The analytical method demonstrated excellent linearity across a calibration range of 0–3000 mg/L for each matrix (Figure 1b). The coefficient of determination (R2) ranged from 0.9998 to 1.0, exceeding the pre-established linearity criterion (R2 > 0.995).30 These results confirm the robustness and reliability of the HPLC method for quantifying total cefazolin in tissue samples and plasma.

Total cefazolin

As shown in Table 2, mean total cefazolin concentrations exceeded MIC thresholds of 2, 4 and 8 mg/L in adipose tissue, dermis and plasma for both dosing regimens at all time points. Between-dose comparisons at the same time point revealed no statistically significant differences in adipose tissue and plasma concentrations. In contrast, total dermis cefazolin concentrations were significantly higher in the 2 g group compared with 3 g group at T30 and Tend (P < 0.05). Temporal changes in total cefazolin concentrations are shown in Figure 2. Within each dosing group, plasma concentrations decreased, whereas dermis concentrations increased significantly from T0 to T30 and remained elevated at Tend (P < 0.05 for T0 versus T30 and T0 versus Tend), indicating adequate intraoperative exposure. Adipose tissue concentrations showed comparatively limited temporal variation.

Calculated unbound cefazolin

Calculated unbound cefazolin concentrations showed marked matrix–dependent differences (Table 2). In adipose tissue, unbound concentrations remained low at all time points (<1.34 mg/L across doses), indicating limited penetration into lipid-rich tissue. In contrast, unbound dermis concentrations increased progressively during surgery, reaching mean values of 4.53 mg/L in 2 g group and 3.72 mg/L in the 3 g group at Tend, with a significant within-group increase from T0 to Tend observed in both dosing groups. In plasma, unbound cefazolin levels were substantially higher than in tissues, ranging from 12.07 to 12.82 mg/L at Tend, and consistently exceeded MIC thresholds of 2, 4 and 8 mg/L in both dosing groups.

As illustrated in Figure 2, dermal unbound cefazolin concentrations increased over time, with a significant difference observed between T0 and Tend, whereas plasma unbound concentrations decreased and unbound adipose tissue concentrations showed minimal temporal variation. Temporal comparisons shown in Figure 2 represent within-group contrasts derived from adjusted linear mixed-effects models.

Total cefazolin

Total cefazolin concentrations achieved >90% attainment at the MIC of 2 mg/L in all matrices for both dosing regimens. In adipose tissue, target attainment decreased at MIC 4 mg/L and declined further at MIC 8 mg/L (38.1% for 2 g and 53.8% for 3 g). Plasma maintained 100% attainment at all MIC thresholds, while dermis attainment was also 100% at MIC 2 mg/L and 4 mg/L. At MIC 8 mg/L; however, dermis concentrations reached 100% only at T30 and Tend and were lower at T0.

Unbound cefazolin

Unbound cefazolin target attainment showed pronounced matrix-dependent variability. Plasma consistently achieved high target attainment across all MIC thresholds (81%–100%), reflecting adequate systemic exposure. Dermis unbound cefazolin exhibited variable attainment, with high target achievement at MIC of 2 mg/L (95.0% and 82.1% at Tend), moderate attainment at MIC 4 mg/L (60.9% and 38.5% at Tend), and minimal attainment at MIC 8 mg/L (5.0% and 0.0% at Tend), depending on dose and time point. In contrast, unbound cefazolin in adipose tissue rarely reached pharmacodynamic thresholds: attainment remained ≤17.9% at MIC 2 mg/L, declined further to ≤2.6% at MIC 4 mg/L and was 0% at MIC 8 mg/L across all doses and time points.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of total and unbound cefazolin exposure in plasma, dermis and subcutaneous adipose tissue in patients with severe obesity undergoing bariatric surgery. Overall, plasma and dermis total cefazolin concentrations consistently exceeded the pharmacodynamic targets across MIC thresholds of 2, 4 and 8 mg/L, whereas adipose tissue concentrations were substantially more variable. Although total adipose concentrations frequently remained above lower MIC thresholds, target attainment declined at 4 mg/L and decreased further at 8 mg/L, particularly in the 2 g group. These findings underscore the challenge of achieving reliable antibiotic exposure in lipid-rich compartments and highlight the limitations of extrapolating plasma concentrations to infer tissue-level prophylactic adequacy in patients with obesity.

Importantly, no postoperative SSIs occurred in this cohort. While this precludes outcome-based inference, it suggests that reduced adipose exposure may not directly translate into clinical failure, particularly when conservative pharmacodynamic thresholds such as 8 mg/L are applied. As emphasized in recent literature, including the systematic review by Ryan et al. (2025) and the comprehensive review by Nothofer et al., the 8 mg/L threshold reflects historical susceptibility criteria and conservative dosing paradigms, rather than a universally required target for contemporary surgical prophylaxis.21,22 In contrast, MSSA—the predominant pathogen in clean bariatric procedures—typically exhibits MIC values of 1–2 mg/L, supporting the clinical relevance of lower pharmacodynamic targets. This rationale emphasizes our decision to evaluate target attainment at 2 and 4 mg/L in addition to 8 mg/L (Table 3).

Assessment of unbound concentrations further accentuated the divergence between systemic and tissue exposure. While plasma unbound cefazolin consistently achieved all MIC thresholds across doses and time points, dermis unbound attainment declined at higher MIC values, and adipose unbound concentrations remained uniformly low. In adipose tissue, unbound attainment reached ≤17.9% at 2 mg/L, ≤2.6% at 4 mg/L and 0% at 8 mg/L. These findings demonstrate that total concentrations may substantially overestimate pharmacologically active exposure at the surgical site, particularly in lipid-rich tissues. Consequently, reliance on plasma or even dermis concentrations alone may provide a misleading impression of prophylactic adequacy in patients with obesity.

Our results are consistent with prior pharmacokinetic studies reporting reduced cefazolin distribution into adipose tissue. Lanoiselée et al. (2021) observed frequent underexposure in adipose tissue despite adequate plasma concentrations, while Brill et al. (2014) demonstrated reduced subcutaneous interstitial fluid (ISF) penetration in patients with obesity versus without.16,31 Coates et al. (2022) similarly reported heterogeneous adipose target attainment across studies, despite adequate plasma levels.14 In contrast, Ryan et al. (2022) showed that 2 g dose can achieve sufficient plasma and ISF concentrations for up to 4 h, but emphasized the poor correlation between plasma and tissue exposure, reinforcing the need of site-specific pharmacokinetic assessment.15

Methodological considerations are also relevant when interpreting tissue exposure. Unbound concentrations were derived from homogenized tissue samples, which incorporate intracellular and structural components and may therefore underestimate true ISF exposure. This distinction has been well described by Mouton et al. (2007) and Cars (1990), who highlighted that ISF represents only approximately 20% of total tissue volume and constitutes the pharmacologically active compartment for β-lactam antibiotics.32,33 While microdialysis would provide a more direct assessment of ISF concentrations, it was not feasible in the present study; nonetheless, homogenates remain a pragmatic approach widely used in surgical pharmacokinetic investigations.

From a clinical perspective, our findings support the need to further individualized prophylactic strategies in bariatric surgery. Although the 3 g dose modestly improved total adipose target attainment, dose escalation alone was insufficient to overcome the intrinsic limitations of cefazolin penetration into adipose tissue, particularly for unbound concentrations.34 This is consistent with cefazolin’s hydrophilic properties and prior studies demonstrating that higher doses primarily increase plasma exposure without proportionally improving adipose penetration.34 Alternative approaches may include weight-based dosing, intraoperative redosing, or the use of agents with more favourable tissue distribution profiles.

Additional pharmacokinetic strategies merit consideration. Ryu et al. (2022) reported that higher daily cefazolin doses (up to 9 g/day) were safe in patients with high body weight, although clinical benefit remains uncertain.17 Accelerated cefazolin clearance in obesity, as described by Chung et al. (2023), may further compromise tissue exposure and argues for pharmacokinetic informed dosing strategies.35 Co-administration of probenecid, which prolongs cefazolin half-life and increases systemic exposure, represents an inexpensive adjunct; however, its impact on adipose tissue penetration remains unknown and warrants further study.36

Finally, these findings underscore the importance of measuring tissue—not just plasma—concentrations when evaluating prophylactic adequacy. As obesity prevalence rises, optimizing surgical prophylaxis in this high-risk population is essential to reduce SSI risk and improve outcomes.

Limitations

This single-centre study involved a relatively small cohort undergoing short-duration laparoscopic procedures, which may limit generalizability to other surgical settings. Variability in surgery duration and the timing of the final sample complicate interpretation of pharmacokinetic trends, and additional postoperative sampling (e.g. at 2 and 4 h) would have provided a more complete profile of clearance dynamics.

The sampling design, limited to three intraoperative time points per patient, did not permit formal estimation of clearance, interindividual variability, or covariate effects, nor population pharmacokinetic modelling. Accordingly, the analyses focused on descriptive comparisons rather than model-based simulations. Furthermore, tissue concentrations were obtained from homogenized samples rather than ISF measured by microdialysis, which may underestimate pharmacologically active exposure. Finally, body composition was not directly assessed; incorporation of bioimpedance or imaging-based morphometrics in future studies could improve understanding of the relationship between adipose distribution and antibiotic exposure.

Despite these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant insights into cefazolin tissue exposure in bariatric surgery and highlights areas where current prophylactic strategies may require refinement.

Conclusion

In patients with severe obesity undergoing bariatric surgery, guideline-recommended cefazolin dosing achieved adequate plasma and dermis exposure but failed to ensure consistent unbound adipose tissue concentrations, particularly at higher pharmacodynamic targets. Increasing the dose from 2 to 3 g provided only limited improvement in adipose exposure. Although no SSIs were observed in this cohort, the relatively small sample size precludes definitive conclusions regarding clinical effectiveness. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the limitations of plasma-based assessments and support the need for tissue-informed, individualized prophylactic strategies to optimize SSI prevention in this high-risk population.
