Impact of socioeconomic factors and birth-related conditions on autism spectrum disorder, Down syndrome, and attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder
Authors: Thamir Al-khlaiwi, Manan Alhakbany, Manal Khalid Aldossary, Lama Fahad Binsalamh, Lubna Musa Altamimi, Norah Ibrahim Alyahya, Reema Wajeeh Aldekhail, Razan Adel Alobaid, Maha Owainan Aljarbaa, Muhammad Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveThis study examined the association between socioeconomic factors and maternal birth-related variables in children diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), Down Syndrome (DS), and Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using data from King Khalid University Hospital Riyadh between 2015 and 2024. The sample included 269 children: 185 with ASD, 51 with DS, and 33 with ADHD. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify significant predictors.ResultsMaternal use of medications during pregnancy (excluding dietary supplements) was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of ASD (OR = 4.253, p = 0.012). Child age was also significant, with each additional year increasing ASD odds by about 5% (OR = 1.054, p = 0.040). Child birth order played a role, as first- or second-born children were less likely to be diagnosed with ASD compared to later-born children (OR = 0.660, p = 0.002).For DS, higher socioeconomic status index (SESi) was linked to a reduced risk, with each unit increase in SESi score, DS odds decreased significantly (OR = 0.578, p = 0.018). Maternal employment during pregnancy was associated with increased DS odds (OR = 1.535, p = 0.024). In contrast, maternal vitamin D supplementation was associated with reduced odds of DS (OR = 0.452, p = 0.028).Regarding ADHD, assisted conception was the only factor significant predictor. Children conceived through assisted reproductive methods had more than twice the likelihood of ADHD compared to those conceived naturally (OR = 2.401, p = 0.040).ConclusionMaternal medication use during pregnancy was associated with increased ASD risk, while birth order and child age also played roles. For DS, higher socioeconomic status reduced risk, whereas maternal employment increased it and vitamin D supplementation was less associated with the disorder. Assisted conception was the only factor linked to increased ADHD risk. Overall, maternal, socioeconomic, and perinatal factors showed distinct associations across ASD, DS, and ADHD.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Down syndrome, Attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, Maternal factors, Socioeconomic factors

Introduction

There is no doubt that neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), Down syndrome (DS), and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) represent major challenges for healthcare providers [1]. Their impact extends beyond patients’ health to include their families, as well as the financial, educational and social aspects of society and governments [2]. Alarmingly, the incidence of these disorders has been rising, partly due to the identification of multiple factors affecting the anatomical structures of affected individuals. For example, ASD incidence is almost 1 in 31 (3.2%), while the incidence of DS is about “1 in 1,000” [3–6]. Due to the lack of official statistical surveys in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), a systematic review and meta-analysis reported ASD prevalence ranging from 0.01% in Oman to 6.50% in Iraq, with an overall prevalence of 0.14% in the MENA region [7]. In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of DS is approximately 1 in 554 live births [8].

There is general consensus among researchers that these disorders have both biological and neurological origins, and tremendous efforts have been made by healthcare providers to identify genetic, medical, and immune-related factors [9–12]. In addition, some studies have reported that environmental and parental factors, including parental age, comorbidities, supplements, and socioeconomic conditions, are associated with neurodevelopmental disorders [13, 14]. However, comprehensive studies evaluating the impact of birth-related and environmental conditions on these disorders remain limited. Therefore, unraveling the impact of maternal-related conditions is of great value. In this comprehensive analytical study, we aimed to examine the association between socioeconomic factors, maternal conception, and birth-related conditions in patients diagnosed with ASD, DS, and ADHD. This may help reveal hidden factors contributing to these health issues and, consequently, support the development of strategies to mitigate their impact.

Methods

This retrospective study, based on medical records and parental interviews, was conducted in the Department of Physiology, College of Medicine, King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

Data were retrieved from the electronic system of King Khalid University Hospital (KKUH), Paediatric Clinic from January 2015-December 2024. The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with ASD (F84.0), DS (Q90), and ADHD (F90.0) through (ADOS-2, CARS, karyotyping, Conners’ scales) by attending pediatrcians of outpatients paediatric clinic at KKUH, and were residing in Saudi Arabia. Exclusion criteria included patients without the aforementioned developmental disorders or those not residing in Saudi Arabia. After identifying all patients who met the inclusion criteria, their parents were contacted and interviewed using a questionnaire through a convenience sampling technique. We collected 269 patients data. The questionnaire consisted of: (1) sociodemographic characteristics of parents and children; 7 items, (2) maternal history and complications; 39 items, and (3) labour and breastfeeding status; 5 items. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, College of Medicine, King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, (No. E-24-8717). The study maintained all participants’ data privacy and confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Statistical analysis

The mean (M) and standard deviation (SD) were used to describe continuous measured variables. The frequencies and percentages were used for describing the categorically measured variables. The Kolmogrov-Smirnov statistical test of normality was used to assess the statistical normality assumption for metric variables and variance inflation index and Tolerance indices were used to assess metric variables for collinearity or multicollinearity. The Cronbach’s alpha test was used to assess the internal consistency of measured questionnaires. The Bivariate chi-squared test of association was used to assess the correlations between the categorically measured variables. The One-way ANOVA was used to assess the statistical mean difference significance for metric variables when compared across the children’s diagnoses levels with post-hoc pairwise comparisons where required, but also the Welch’s adjusted One-way ANOVA were used for the tests that showed statistically significant unequal variances. The multivariable Binary Logistic regression was applied to assess the statistical significance of predictors for children’s diagnoses. The association between the predictor independent variables with analysed outcome variables were expressed as a multivariable adjusted Odds Ratio (OR) with its associated 95% confidence intervals. The Nonlinear Categorical Factor Analysis as a dimension reduction technique was used to estimate household socioeconomic status index (SESi) and the resulted SESi index was used as a predictor in the multivariable analyses. A categorical principal components analysis (Non-linear Factor Analysis) was conducted as a dimension reduction technique to derive a latent index representing families’ overall socioeconomic state. The analysis included six observed indicators: maternal employment status during pregnancy, mother’s educational level, father’s educational level, household monthly income, region of residence, and nationality. A licensed copy of the IBM SPSS statistics Version 30 was used for the statistical data analysis. Alpha significance level was considered at 0.050 level.

Results

Associations between socioeconomic characteristics and ASD, DS, ADHD

The study included 269 children (the number who participated) diagnosed with ASD (n = 185), DS (n = 51), or ADHD (n = 33) vs. declined or could not be cantacted (n = 0). Table 1 presents bivariate comparisons of parents’ socioeconomic characteristics across child diagnosis. A statistically significant difference was observed in child sex, χ²(2) = 10.76, p = 0.005, with males being more prevalent in the ASD group (79.5%) compared to DS (56.9%) and ADHD (72.7%). Maternal and paternal ages also differed significantly by child diagnosis. Mothers of children with DS were significantly older (M = 36.63, SD = 5.50) than mothers of children with ASD (M = 30.70, SD = 6.40) and ADHD (M = 31.24, SD = 5.52), F(2, 266) = 18.91, p < 0.001. Similarly, fathers of children with DS were significantly older (M = 43.30, SD = 7.75) than those of children with ASD (M = 36.30, SD = 8.34) and ADHD (M = 37.94, SD = 7.30), F(2, 266) = 14.90, p < 0.001. No significant differences were observed for maternal education (χ²(6) = 8.55, p = 0.20) or paternal education (χ²(6) = 8.39, p = 0.211), although trends suggested that higher education levels were more common among parents of children with ASD. Similarly, maternal employment did not significantly differ across groups (χ²(8) = 11.30, p = 0.187), although educational occupations appeared more common among mothers of children with DS (39.2%).

Associations between maternal and pregnancy-related factors and ASD, DS, ADHD

Table 2 represents associations between maternal history, pregnancy-related exposures, and child diagnosis (ASD, DS and ADHD). There was a significant association between birth order and diagnosis, χ²(6) = 31.66, p < 0.001. First-born children were more common in the ASD (27.6%) and ADHD (27.3%) groups, whereas DS was more frequent among later-born children, particularly those ranked fourth or higher (72.5%). Maternal physical activity during pregnancy also significantly varied by diagnosis, χ²(6) = 23.99, p < 0.001. Lack of physical activity was more common in the ASD and DS groups (78.9% and 76.6%, respectively), while higher levels of weekly activity were more frequently reported in the ADHD group. Vitamin D intake was significantly lower among mothers of children with DS (35.3%) than those with ASD (53.5%) and ADHD (57.6%), χ²(2) = 6.10, p = 0.049. Folic acid supplementation was lowest among mothers of DS children (80%), 98.7% in ASD and 97% in ADHD, χ²(2) = 6.43, p = 0.040. A statistically significant difference was also noted in the use of medications other than supplements (antibiotics, anti-epileptics, anti-inflammatory) during pregnancy, χ²(2) = 8.19, p = 0.017. Such medication use was notably higher in the ASD group (16.2%) than in DS (3.9%) and ADHD (6.1%). Delivery complications were significantly associated with diagnosis, χ²(2) = 6.75, p = 0.034, occurring most frequently in ASD (18.4%), compared to DS (5.9%) and ADHD (9.1%) suggesting a possible interplay between perinatal stress and ASD risk.

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis

Multivariable Binary Logistic Regression Analysis was used to assess the significant and relevant predictors associated with the odds of an ASD within the sample (Table 3). Notably, the child’s age emerged as statistically significant factor. Specifically, for each additional year of age, the odds of an ASD diagnosis increased by approximately 5% (OR = 1.054, 95% CI [1.002, 1.109], p = 0.040), which may reflect increased diagnostic awareness or clinical referral patterns as children grow older. Birth order was another influential factor; children who were first- or second-born were significantly less likely to be diagnosed with ASD compared to those who were third-born or later (OR = 0.660, 95% CI [0.510, 0.855], p = 0.002). Maternal use of medications other than dietary supplements during pregnancy was associated with a markedly increased likelihood of an ASD diagnosis (OR = 4.253, 95% CI [1.369, 13.215], p = 0.012). Several other variables approached, but did not reach, conventional levels of statistical significance. For instance, prenatal iron supplementation (OR = 1.892, p = 0.057) and the presence of delivery complications (OR = 2.422, p = 0.082) both showed trends toward increased ASD odds, indicating potential areas for further investigation. Other predictor variables assessed in the study were excluded from the final model during the iterative model-building process. These factors were removed either due to lack of statistical significance (p > 0.40) or due to low frequency in the sample (e.g., exposure to radiation), which led to hyperinflated standard errors during modelling. As such, these variables were excluded from the reported final model.

Table 3 also represents the results of the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis examining the odds of children being diagnosed with DS. Notably, birth order emerged as a significant predictor. Children who were fourth-born or later had more than double the odds of being diagnosed with DS compared to those with a lower birth order (OR = 2.152, 95% CI [1.495, 3.099], p < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 2. Importantly, the SESi showed an inverse association with DS risk. For each unit increase in SESi score, the odds of DS diagnosis decreased significantly (OR = 0.578, 95% CI [0.367, 0.910], p = 0.018). Additionally, the mother’s employment status during pregnancy was associated with increased odds of a DS diagnosis (OR = 1.535, 95% CI [1.057, 2.229], p = 0.024). Conversely, maternal uptake of vitamin D supplements during pregnancy was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of having a child diagnosed with DS (OR = 0.452, 95% CI [0.222, 0.919], p = 0.028).

The multivariable logistic regression model examined various sociodemographic and maternal factors to identify predictors of ADHD diagnosis among children is also shown in Table 3. Among the predictors examined, assisted conception was the only factor significantly associated with an increased risk of ADHD. Children conceived through assisted reproductive methods had more than twice the odds of being diagnosed with ADHD compared to those conceived naturally (OR = 2.401, 95% CI [1.042, 5.537], p = 0.040).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive report to investigate the socioeconomic and birth-related parameters associated with ASD, DS, and ADHD in the Saudi population using a relatively large sample size. Evaluation of socioeconomic characteristics of children diagnosed with ASD, DS, and ADHD revealed that both paternal and maternal ages contributed significantly to the etiology of DS, followed by ASD and ADHD, respectively. Mothers of children with DS (36.6 years) were older compared to those of children with ASD and ADHD, respectively. This finding aligns with the existing literature, which shows that women aged 35 years or older at the time of delivery are associated significantly with giving birth to a child with DS [15, 16]. Increasing parental age also emerged as an associating factor to DS risk, particularly when combined with advanced maternal age. A previous study reported that when maternal age reaches 40 years, the combined contribution of parental age to DS may account for up to 50% of cases [17]. In our cohort, the average age of fathers of children with DS was significantly higher (43.30 years) than that of fathers of children with ASD (36.30) and ADHD (37.94). Regarding ASD and ADHD, a U-shaped association between parental age and risk has been previously documented, where both younger parents (< 20 years) and older parents (above 35 years) are associated with an increased risk of these disorders in offspring [18, 19]. Thus, advanced paternal and maternal ages are importantly correlated with DS, ASD and ADHD in children [20–22].

Birth order was associated with ASD in this sample. It has been hypothesized that birth canal stress and difficult labour may expose first-born children to subtle brain injuries [23–26], potentially contributing to ASD phenotypes. In our study, a comparison of birth order across these three neurodevelopmental disorders revealed that children who were first- or second-born were significantly less likely to be diagnosed with ASD compared to those who were third-born or later whereas DS was more commonly observed in later-born children. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a higher prevalence of ASD in first-born children [23, 24]. The relationship between birth order and the likelihood of ASD is a complex phenomenon. A large study conducted in Western Australia (n = 5,404) involving children aged 1–18 years diagnosed with ASD reported a decrease in adaptive functioning and intelligence with increasing birth order [25]. The same study also found that first-born children without siblings at the time of diagnosis exhibited lower cognitive functioning compared to first-born children with siblings. The study also suggested that birth canal stress and difficult labour may expose first-born children to subtle brain injuries, potentially contributing to ASD phenotypes [26]. In contrast, there is limited evidence supporting a clear association between birth order and the risk of DS or ADHD. Delivery complications represent a complex, multifactorial issue involving maternal health status, pregnancy conditions and the delivery process itself. In our study, delivery complications were significantly more common among mothers of children with ASD compared to those of children with DS or ADHD children.

In our study, lack of maternal physical activity during pregnancy was observed across all three disorders, ASD, and DS, and ADHD groups but was more strongly associated with the ASD (78.9%) and DS (76.6%) than with the ADHD (60.6%) group. Although there is no independent association between maternal physical activity and ASD, existing research suggests that higher levels of physical activity during pregnancy may have a positive effect on infant neurodevelopment [27]. Previous studies revealed that higher physical activity during pregnancy is associated with lower prevalence of ASD and sleep disturbances in infants. Both very short (< 6 h) or very long (> 10 h) sleep durations during pregnancy have been linked to higher ASD [28]. Higher physical activity has anti-inflammatory effect by reducing inflammatory cytokines during pregnancy. Furthermore, it contributes to lowering the risk of maternal metabolic disorders, such as obesity, dyslipidaemia, and diabetes mellitus [29, 30].

The use of certain medications during pregnancy may increase the risk of ASD, although other confounding factors, such as family history, may also contribute to this elevated risk [31, 32]. In our study, maternal medication use during pregnancy was significantly associated with ASD group compared to the ADHD and DS groups. Specifically, 16.2% of mothers in ASD group reported medication use during pregnancy, compared to 6.1% in ADHD group and only 3.9% in the DS group. These findings suggest a possible potential neurodevelopmental sensitivity to pharmacological exposures in children later diagnosed with ASD. Nutritional supplements such as folic acid and vitamin D are well-known in the literature to have protective effects against neurodevelopmental disorders including DS and ASD. Our study found that vitamin D and folic acid intake was lower among mothers of children with DS compared to those of children diagnosed with ASD or ADHD. This aligns with previous findings suggesting that maternal intake of folic acid and multivitamins during pregnancy is associated with a reduced risk of ASD compared to mothers who did not take such supplements [33]. Overall, these findings highlight the role of maternal and prenatal health behaviours, particularly supplement intake, physical activity, and medication use, in influencing neurodevelopmental outcomes. The results underscore the importance of optimizing maternal health during pregnancy as a potential strategy for mitigating the risk of developmental disorders.

We employed multivariable binary regression analysis to assess the independent and statistically significant associations between various variables and the likelihood of a diagnosis of ASD, DS or ADHD diagnosis. For ASD, the most significant predictors were the child’s age, female gender, birth order and maternal medication use during pregnancy. For DS, significant associations were observed with female gender, birth order, maternal occupation during pregnancy and maternal supplement intake during pregnancy. In ADHD, maternal fast food consumption, type of conception and maternal supplements intake were the significant factors. Notably, our results revealed that maternal exposure to medications, including anti-epileptic, antibiotics and anti-inflammatory drugs, excluding nutritional supplements, was significantly associated with an ASD risk in offspring (OR; 4.2, p = 0.012).

Multivariable analysis showed that higher socioeconomic status is negatively associated with the incidence of DS. This inverse relationship may be attributed to reduced financial stress among higher-income families, which can contribute to improved maternal well-being and, consequently, more favourable child health outcomes. Conversely, maternal employment during pregnancy was associated with the prevalence of DS (OR; 1.5, p = 0.024). This finding may reflect unmeasured stressors, occupational exposures, or lifestyle factors associated with maternal employment, though further investigation is warranted to clarify the causal pathway underlying this association. In addition, our results indicated that maternal uptake of vitamin D supplements during pregnancy was negatively associated with DS (OR; 0.452, p = 0.028), which might suggest a protective role [34]. An increased risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes is associated with low socioeconomic status (SES) [35]. In one form of DS occurrence, low maternal SES, particularly low household income, has been associated with chromosomal non-disjunction errors during maternal meiosis phase II [36].

Given the inconsistent findings in the literature regarding the association between assisted conception and the risk of ADHD in offspring [37], our study adds to this growing body of research by identifying a positive association between the two. Specifically, assisted conception was associated with a significantly higher incidence of ADHD (OR; 2.4, p = 0.040) [38]. This association may reflect underlying biological vulnerabilities or iatrogenic factors related to assisted conception procedures.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. It was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions. In addition, the disproportionate distribution of sample sizes is another limitation. The ADHD subgroup (n = 33) was underpowered for multivariable analysis, and results for this group should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies with larger samples are needed to confirm these findings.

This resulted from including of all available ASD, ADHD, and DS cases from the hospital’s medical records between 2015 and 2024. Children diagnosed with DS and ADHD are relatively less common than those with ASD, which is reflected in their smaller representation in the sample. The retrospective design precludes causal inference; therefore, all reported associations should be interpreted as correlational. In addition, multiple comparisons were not adjusted for, which may increase the risk of Type I error. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory. In addition, parental interviews may introduce recall bias.

Conclusions

The study identified significant associations between socioeconomic and birth-related factors and neurodevelopmental disorders. Our findings revealed that maternal use of medications (other than supplements) during pregnancy was associated with higher odds of ASD. Regarding DS, higher socioeconomic status was associated with lower odds of DS. Maternal employment during pregnancy was associated with increased odds of DS, while vitamin D supplementation was associated with reduced odds of DS. For ADHD, assisted conception was the only significant predictor.

Overall, maternal medication use, birth order, supplement intake, and socioeconomic status were key factors associated with these conditions, with specific variations across ASD, DS, and ADHD. Understanding these risk factors may aid in diagnosis, management, and potentially reduce the incidence of these neurodevelopmental disorders. Further studies with larger sample sizes are required to better understand these associations.

Variable Principal Normalization

The resulting factor structure demonstrated strong and interpretable loadings, with five out of six variables contributing meaningfully (i.e., factor loading > 0.30) to the extracted socioeconomic component. The strongest contributors were mother’s education (loading = 0.786) and father’s education (loading = 0.756), followed closely by household income (loading = 0.742), and maternal occupation during pregnancy (loading = 0.531), indicating that parental education and maternal labor force participation were the most salient dimensions of socioeconomic status in this sample. Residence region also contributed meaningfully (loading = 0.384), while nationality exhibited the weakest association (loading = 0.201), suggesting limited discriminatory value in the constructed index. The overall solution demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with a categorical Cronbach’s alpha of 0.70, supporting the reliability of the index in capturing the shared variance among these indicators. The derived socioeconomic index was standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) and subsequently used as a proxy for parental socioeconomic state in multivariable regression analyses. Descriptively, families with higher socioeconomic index scores tended to be Saudi nationals residing in central regions, with higher levels of parental education, greater household income, and maternal employment in professional sectors. In contrast, families with lower scores tended to include non-Saudi nationals, individuals residing in peripheral regions, and parents with lower educational attainment and income levels. The index thus provides a valid and interpretable composite measure of socioeconomic status within the sociocultural context of the sample.
