Thermal shift engagement assay for Class IIA histone deacetylase inhibitor screening
Authors: Lauren N Kotsull, Mary Kay H Pflum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHistone deacetylase (HDAC) proteins are the targets of several anti-cancer clinical drugs, which motivates future inhibitor development. Class IIA HDAC proteins, HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC7, and HDAC9, are pseudodeacetylases that lack a key catalytic amino acid residue resulting in low deacetylase activity. Yet enzymatic deacetylase activity assays are typically used to assess inhibitor potency. A ligand binding assay is needed for a more relevant assessment of inhibitor engagement by class IIA HDAC proteins. As an efficient method to directly assess inhibitor binding, thermal shift assays were developed for HDAC4, 5, and 7. Consistent with prior activity assays, thermal shift analysis reproduced the expected binding of various inhibitors to HDAC1, HDAC4, HDAC5, and HDAC7. Additionally, a higher throughput thermal shift format was developed using dot blot analysis. Thermal shift analysis offers a useful complement to enzyme activity assays for characterization of HDAC inhibitors.Keywords: Thermal shift, Histone deacetylase, HDAC, Dot blot, HDAC inhibitor

Introduction

Histones play an important role in epigenetic regulation and gene expression by packaging genomic DNA into nucleosomes. Specifically, the N-terminal tails of nucleosomal histones are rich in lysine residues, which regulate gene expression as a function of acetylation [1,2]. Generally, histone acetyl transferase (HAT) enzymes acetylate histones to activate transcription, whereas histone deacetylase (HDAC) enzymes deacetylate histones for transcriptional repression. The 18 HDAC isoforms are divided into five classes based on size, sequence similarities, and cellular localization [3]. Class I includes HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC8, while Class IV contains only HDAC11. Class II is divided into two subsections: class IIA with HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC7, and HDAC9, and class IIB with HDAC6 and HDAC10. Finally, class III contains the seven Sirtuins. HDAC proteins in classes I, II and IV are metal dependent, while class III Sirtuins are NAD+ dependent [3]. This work focuses on the metal dependent class IIA HDAC proteins, as discussed below.

In some disease states, HDAC proteins are unregulated, which causes uncontrolled deacetylation and transcription, leading to aberrant apoptosis, tumor suppression, and cell signaling [4–6]. Specific to the class IIA HDAC isoforms [7], HDAC4 overexpression is linked to lung cancer [8] and hepatocellular carcinoma [9,10], HDAC5 abnormalities are associated with medulloblastoma and colon cancer [11], HDAC7 is overexpressed in both pancreatic cancer and acute lymphoblastic leukemia [12], and HDAC9 irregularities are correlated with glioblastoma [13], medulloblastoma [14], and acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Additionally, class IIA HDAC proteins are linked to cardiovascular disease, specifically cardiac hypertrophy, along with inflammation, immune responses to viral infections, diabetes, and neurodegenerative diseases [15–21]. Due to the prevalence of HDAC proteins in various diseases, HDAC inhibitors have been studied and developed as therapeutics [22–24].

Several HDAC inhibitors are FDA-approved to treat cancers by reducing proliferation and metastasis, as well as promoting apoptosis [25–29]. As examples, vorinostat (SAHA, Fig. 1A) [30] and pracinostat (SB939, Fig. 1B) [31] are clinical HDAC inhibitor drugs treating T-cell lymphoma or acute myelocytic leukemia. Vorinostat and pracinostat are pan inhibitors that target most or all eleven metal-dependent HDAC proteins, which could be responsible for side effects observed in the clinic [32,33]. A variety of isoform selective HDAC inhibitors have also been developed, which could be more effective drugs and biomedical research tools. Examples of single isoform selective inhibitors include HDAC3-selective RGFP966 [34], HDAC6-selective tubastatin A [35], and HDAC8-selective PCI-34051 [36]. Inhibitors with dual selectivity have also been reported, including BRD-6929 with preference for HDAC1 and HDAC2 (Fig. 1D) [37]. Related to class IIA isoforms, TMP269 is class selective by inhibiting HDAC4, 5, 7, and 9 (Fig. 1C) [38]. Without selective inhibitors to each HDAC isoform, development of additional HDAC inhibitors is an active area.

Chemical structure of selected HDAC inhibitors studied here, including pan inhibitor SAHA (A), pan inhibitor SB939 (B), class IIA-selective TMP269 (C), and HDAC1/2 dual selective BRD-6929 (D).

To develop HDAC inhibitors, a variety of assays are available to determine inhibitor potency and selectivity. The most widely used assay is a fluorescent deacetylase activity assay where purified HDAC isoform, inhibitor, and fluorogenic acetyllysine-containing peptide substrate are used to determine both potency and selectivity [39,40]. However, compared to the other metal-dependent HDAC isoforms, HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC7, and HDAC9 are pseudodeacetylases due to the lack of a key catalytic amino acid; the catalytic tyrosine of active class I, IIB, and IV is altered to a histidine in class IIA, resulting in minimal enzymatic activity [41]. Although minimally active, HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC7, and HDAC9 are contributors to HDAC-related disease states [9–21], making them valuable drug targets. To overcome the low enzymatic activity of class IIA HDAC proteins in deacetylase assays, a more reactive trifluoroacetyllysine-containing peptide substrate is used to encourage unnatural deacetylation [41]. However, use of activity assays with the inactive class IIA pseudodeacetylases might fail to accurately assess physiologically-relevant inhibitor engagement. Additionally, the activity assay requires purified recombinant isoforms, which adds cost. Finally, use of recombinant proteins might limit physiological relevance given that many HDAC proteins display elevated enzymatic activity in cellular complexes [42,43]. The unnatural substrate needed to overcome low enzymatic activity and use of purified proteins highlight a need for an alternative binding assay for development of class IIA HDAC inhibitors.

An ideal assay to measure ligand-protein engagement is thermal sensitivity or thermal shift measurements [44]. Thermal shift relies on the fact that most proteins are heat sensitive and precipitate at a specific temperature relative to their thermal stability. As a result, heating a lysate or complex mixture of proteins across a temperature range, quantifying the level of soluble protein at each temperature, and comparing the relative protein remaining to the unheated control enables curve fitting to determine the aggregation temperature (Tagg) where 50 % of the target protein remains [44–46]. Important for inhibitor screening, the thermal stability of the target protein will increase when bound to a ligand. The expectation is that the protein-ligand complex will display a higher Tagg compared to the unbound protein, with the difference in Tagg (ΔTagg) providing a measure of ligand engagement. Thermal stability assays represent a powerful tool to rapidly assess ligand engagement.

Thermal shift has been applied to various proteins and small molecule therapeutics, including HDAC1 with several known inhibitors [46,47]. Pan HDAC inhibitor panobinostat stabilized endogenous HDAC1 from HEK293T cells with a ΔTagg of 6 °C (47 °C unbound to 53 °C bound) using western blot analysis [46]. Likewise, overexpressed HDAC1 in primary cardiac fibroblasts displayed ΔTagg values of 3 °C and 7 °C with apicidin and mocetinostat (54 °C unbound to 57 °C or 61 °C bound) using a nanoluciferase readout [47]. These values generally correlated with the known inhibitory potencies from deacetylase assays, with IC50 (inhibitory concentrations at 50 %) values of 3 nM for panobinostat, 35 nM for mocetinostat, and > 10 μM for apicidin. In addition, thermal proteome profiling with panobinostat in K562 cells using quantitative mass spectrometry analysis reported a ΔTagg of 14 °C for HDAC1 (49 °C unbound to 63 °C bound) [48]. In the only published example, class IIA HDAC4 and HDAC7 showed no stabilization by panobinostat using thermal proteome profiling (ΔTagg of 0 °C), despite having low nM potencies in deacetylase activity assays [49]. The limited thermal proteome profiling data suggests that the unnatural substrate used in deacetylase activity assays fails to accurately assess ligand engagement with the class IIA pseudodeacetylases. However, further effort is needed to establish thermal shift with class IIA HDAC proteins.

Here, thermal shift was established as an inhibitor engagement assay for class IIA pseudodeacetylases. Initially, the heat sensitivity of HDAC4, 5, and 7 were determined using gel electrophoresis methods. Next, various HDAC inhibitors were tested, and the stabilization observed by thermal shift coincided with known potencies with HDAC4, 5, and 7. Finally, dot blot analysis was used to enhance the throughput of the inhibitor screening. Given the low activity levels of class IIA pseudodeacetylases, thermal shift assays represent an enabling and appropriate ligand engagement assay to determine inhibitor efficacy.

Results

Class IIA proteins are heat sensitive

Previous data established the heat sensitivity of HDAC1 using gel methods [46,47] and HDAC4 and HDAC7 using mass spectrometry analysis [48]. Before assessing ligand binding, the heat sensitivities of class IIA HDAC proteins were confirmed using gel methods, with HDAC1 as a control. HeLa lysates were preincubated with DMSO inhibitor vehicle for 1 h, followed by heating across a 25 °C temperature range. Soluble proteins were collected after centrifugation, separated via SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membrane, and visualized using western blot analysis. HDAC1, HDAC4, HDAC5, and HDAC7 (Fig. 2A) showed heat sensitivities between 50 °C and 75 °C, consistent with prior data [46–48]. Unfortuantely, HDAC9 was weakly and inconsistently detected by western blot analysis, likely due to low abundance in HeLa lysates and poor quality antibodies. Several cell lysates, including HEK293, HepG2, and K562, and several commerical antibodies were tested without success. Due to inconsistent data, studies with HDAC9 were discontinued.

To determine the aggregation temperature (Tagg), protein band intensities at each temperature were quantified and compared to the HDAC protein levels at 25 °C to calculate a percentage HDAC protein level remaining. The calculated relative percentages were plotted against temperature and fit to a sigmoidal curve to determine the Tagg temperature where 50 % of the HDAC protein remained (Fig. 2B). The HDAC1 control displayed a Tagg of 61 °C, which is slightly elevated compared to prior work showing 47–54 °C [46–48]. Among the class IIA isoforms, HDAC4 was the most stable (59 ± 1 °C) and HDAC7 the least stable (55 ± 1 °C). Compared to the thermal stabilities of 52 °C and 51 °C observed using proteomics analysis with HDAC4 and HDAC7, respectively, a slightly elevated stability was observed here. Given the known differences in HDAC1 thermal stabilities in the various cell lines used previously [46–48], a small difference was reasonable. In conclusion, HDAC4, HDAC5, and HDAC7 displayed heat sensitivity appropriate for thermal shift assays.

Thermal shift assays with HDAC inhibitors

With heat sensitivities established, four known HDAC inhibitors were assessed for binding with HDAC4, 5, and 7. Two pan inhibitors, SAHA (Fig. 1A) and Pracinostat (SB939, Fig. 1B), were selected due to differences in potency towards class IIA HDAC proteins observed using deacetylase activity assays; SB939 potently inhibited class IIA HDAC isoforms, but SAHA did not [30,31]. Two selective inhibitors were also tested: class IIA selective inhibitor, TMP269 (Fig. 1C) [38], and HDAC1/HDAC2 selective inhibitor, BRD-6929 (Fig. 1D) [37]. The diversity of potencies and selectivites among these four inhibitors will ideally test the thermal shift assay.

For the thermal shift assay with inhibitors, lysates were preincubated for one hour with each inhibitor at a concentration ten times higher than the highest IC50 value among HDAC1, 4, 5, or 7. After heating across a 25 °C temperature range, soluble proteins were collected after centrifugation, separated via SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membrane, and visualized using western blot analysis. As expected, temperature-dependent protein loss was observed with HDAC4, 5 and 7 in the presence of each inhibitor (Figs. 3, S2–S5). Focusing on the gel images with HDAC7 (Fig. 3A), all inhibitors stabilized HDAC7, with HDAC7 protein bands observed at a higher temperature compared to the DMSO vehicle control. As an example, HDAC7 protein bands were observed at a high intensity up to 70 °C with SB939 (Fig. 3A, lane 5), in contrast to the low intensity HDAC7 band observed at 70 °C in the DMSO control (Fig. 3A, lane 6). In fact, the significant stabilization observed with SB939 and TMP269 required a higher temperature range (55 to 80 °C, Fig. 3A) compared to samples with DMSO, SAHA, and BRD-6929 (50 to 75 °C, Fig. 3A).

To calculate the Tagg values with each inhibitor, HDAC7 band intensities in each gel image were quantified, compared to the HDAC7 protein levels at 25° to calculate relative percentages, and plotted against temperature for each inhibitor (Fig. 3B). For HDAC7, the pan inhibitor SB939 was the best stabilizer, with a Tagg values of 67 ± 1 °C, which was 12 °C higher than the DMSO control at 55 ± 1 °C (ΔTagg, Table 1). Similarly, class IIA selective inhibitor TMP269 also significantly stabilized HDAC7, with a Tagg of 63 ± 1 °C and ΔTagg compared to DMSO of 8 °C (Table 1). HDAC7 was least stabilized by SAHA with a Tagg value of only 56 ± 1 °C and insignificant ΔTagg of 1 °C (Table 1), which is consistent with prior activity assay data [30]. Unexpectedly, the HDAC1/HDAC2 selective inhibitor BRD-6929 significantly stabilized HDAC7 with Tagg of 61 ± 1 °C or ΔTagg of 6 °C (Table 1), although with reduced stabilization compared to SB939 and TMP269. In total, HDAC7 was stabilized by various HDAC inhibitors, consistent with prior deacetylase activity assay data.

Like HDAC7, HDAC5 was best stabilized by SB939, with a Tagg value of 67 ± 1 °C compared to the 58 ± 1 °C DMSO control, or ΔTagg of 9 °C (Table 1). In addition, SAHA only modestly stabilized HDAC4 with a Tagg of 62 °C or ΔTagg of 3 °C (Table 1). Finally, HDAC1 was also studied as a control, given prior thermal shift data [46]. As expected, HDAC1 (Fig. 4C) was significantly stabilized by BRD-6929, SAHA, and SB939, with Tagg values of 75 ± 1 °C, 74 ± 1 °C, and 73 ± 1 °C compared to the 61 ± 1 °C DMSO control (ΔTagg of 12–14 °C, Table 1). In addition, HDAC1 was minimally stabilized by class IIA selective inhibitor TMP-269 with a Tagg value of only 64 ± 1 °C, with a ΔTagg of 3 °C (Table 1). In total, the thermal sensitivities of all HDAC isoforms tested were influenced by the various inhibitors, consistent with binding.

Thermal shift dot blot assays

Thermal shift assays with class IIA HDAC proteins using SDS-PAGE and western blot analysis were successful, but low throughput. To increase throughput without compromising quality, we directly dot blotted lysates onto a nitrocellulose membrane with rapid western blot analysis to avoid SDS-PAGE separation of proteins in the lysates. HDAC7 was tested here given the high quality of the antibody and notable variance in ΔTagg values via thermal shift SDS-PAGE analysis. For thermal shift dot blot assays, lysates were incubated for one hour with each inhibitor at a concentration ten times higher than the highest IC50 value with HDAC7, as described previously. After heating, the heated lysates were directly spotted on a nitrocellulose membrane and visualized with western blot analysis. Similar to the earlier thermal shift assays with SDS-PAGE analysis, temperature-dependent HDAC7 protein loss was observed using dot bot analysis (Fig. 5). However, unlike prior SDS-PAGE analysis, a lower temperature range was needed to observe the 50 % protein loss to calculate Tagg values. Whereas prior SDS-PAGE analysis used a 50–80 °C temperature range (Fig. 3), analysis by dot blot required a lower 40–70 °C range (Fig. 5). We speculate that the need for lower temperatures was due to the reduced lysates used in the dot blot analysis (50 μg) compared to the SDS-PAGE analysis (150 μg). Specifically, the minimal quantities of HDAC7 present in the small amount of dot blotted lysate required lower temperatures to promote consistent visualization of protein levels. Similar to SDS-PAGE analysis, a higher temperature range was needed with TMP-269 and SB939 due to increased thermal stabilization (Fig. 5).

To generate Tagg values, HDAC7 protein levels were quantified, compared to the HDAC7 protein levels at 25° to calculate relative percentages, and plotted against temperature (Fig. 5B). As expected due to the lower temperature range used with dot blot analysis, lower Tagg values were observed after quantification. Specifically, the DMSO control using dot blot analysis showed a Tagg value of 51 ± 1 °C compared to a Tagg value of 55 ± 1 °C with SDS-PAGE separation (Table 1). Considering inhibitor-dependent stabilization, TMP269 was the best stabilizer, with a Tagg of 60 ± 1 °C and ΔTagg of 9 °C (Table 1). In addition, SB939 significantly stabilized HDAC7, with a Tagg of 56 ± 1 °C and ΔTagg of 5 °C (Table 1). Again, SAHA minimally stabilized HDAC7, with a Tagg of only 54 ± 1 °C and ΔTagg of 3 °C (Table 1). Finally, BRD-6929 again showed an unexpectedly high level of HDAC7 stabilization, with Tagg of 57 ± 1 °C and ΔTagg of 6 °C (Table 1). Gratifyingly, thermal shift with dot blot analysis reproduced the data observed with SDS-PAGE separation, which provides a higher throughput method for inhibitor testing.

Discussion

Inhibitor engagement with class IIa HDAC pseudodeacetylases is typically assessed using a deacetylase activity assay, which is awkward given their inactivity. As an alternative, a simple thermal shift assay was developed here to directly measure inhibitor engagement with HDAC4, HDAC5, and HDAC7. Consistent with prior data [49], HDAC4, HDAC5, and HDAC7 were heat sensitive (Fig. 2), which was a prerequisite for thermal shift analysis. Importantly, HDAC inhibitors influenced the thermal stabilities of the HDAC isoforms, with stabilization reasonably consistent with the known potencies based on the deacetylase activity assays. Specifically, SB939 is a pan inhibitor with nanomolar potency for HDAC4, 5, and 7 in deacetylase assays (Table 1) [31]. As expected, all four HDAC isoforms were stabilized by SB939 in thermal shift assays, with ΔTagg values of 9 °C to 12 °C (Table 1). Where SAHA preferentially inhibits HDAC1 over class IIA isoforms in activity assays (Table 1) [30], SAHA similarly stabilized HDAC1 with a ΔTagg value of 13 °C compared to 3 °C or less with HDAC4, 5, and 7 (Table 1). Finally, in the case of BRD-6929, which is an HDAC1 and 2 selective inhibitor [37], HDAC1 was strongly stabilized with a ΔTagg value of 14 °C (Table 1), while HDAC4, HDAC5, and HDAC7 were less stabilized with ΔTagg values 6 °C of less (Table 1). The data with SB939, SAHA, and BRD-6929 show strong correlation between potency in activity assays and thermal stability in engagement assays.

In contrast to SB939, SAHA, and BRD-6929, TMP269 showed unexpected results in the thermal shift analysis. TMP269 is a class IIA selective HDAC inhibitor with nanomolar potency for HDAC4, 5, and 7, but not HDAC1 (Table 1) [38]. As expected,TMP269 minimally stabilized HDAC1 with a ΔTagg value of 3 °C and strongly stabilized HDAC7 with a ΔTagg value of 8 °C (Table 1). Unexpectedly, HDAC4 was not stabilized (ΔTagg value of 0, Table 1) and HDAC5 was only modestly stabilized (ΔTagg value of 4 °C, Table 1) by TMP269. The thermal shift analysis suggests that TMP269 displays greater selectivity for HDAC7 than previously observed using deacetylase activity assays.

Inconsistencies between thermal shift analysis and activity assay potencies could be a result of several factors. First, activity assays with the inactive class IIA pseudodeacetylases require unnatural fluorinated peptide substrates to allow monitoring of catalytic turnover, which could poorly reflect inhibitor binding. Second, recombinant HDAC proteins are used in enzymatic activity assays, which might poorly mimic the activities of cell-derived HDAC isoforms. Overall, while the thermal shift results aligned in most cases with reported potencies from activity assays, the variations with TMP269 suggest that widely used activity assays would benefit from complementary ligand engagement assays, such as thermal shift analysis.

To enhance the throughput and value of thermal shifts assays, dot blot analysis of thermal stabilities with HDAC7 was performed. Similar to analysis using full SDS-PAGE, SB939 and TMP269 significantly stabilized HDAC7 with ΔTagg values of 5 °C and 9 °C, respectively, while SAHA was the weakest stabilizer a ΔTagg value of 3 °C (Table 1). BRD-6929 also surprisingly stabilized HDAC7 with a ΔTagg value of 6 °C (Table 1), like the SDS-PAGE analysis. In addition to accurate analysis, dot blots offer many advantages, including fewer steps (2 vs 4), reagents, time (8 h vs 22 h), and cost. Given the advantages, dot blot-based thermal shift has several applications. First, dot blots would be helpful in preliminary studies to rapidly determine the thermal shift temperature range for each protein-inhibitor complex. Second, dot blots could quickly determine the most effective inhibitor binder, which would inform subsequent binding analysis. For example, dot blot distinguished strong binders, like SB939 and TMP269, from inhibitors with weak binding, such as SAHA. Dot blots analysis of thermal stability offers a useful and effective technique for inhibitor engagement testing.

While a useful tool to evaluate the binding engagement of inactive class IIA HDAC proteins, thermal shift analysis has drawbacks. Thermal shift analysis requires a cell lysate of interest with reasonable concentrations of HDAC4, 5, or 7, as well as a good quality antibody. In fact, HDAC9 was a challenge to probe by thermal shift due to low concentrations in lysates and weak antibodies. Thermal shift with dot blot analysis was particularly sensitive to HDAC protein levels and antibody quality. Fortunately, HDAC4, 5, and 7 were of sufficient abundance with antibodies appropriate for analysis. To overcome issues with low protein abundance or weak antibodies, overexpression of tagged proteins via transient transfection could be used, although adding additional time and cost. Thermal shift also offers only a qualitative assessment of inhibitor engagement and must be coupled with quantitative assays to determine dissociation constants (Kd). For example, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and polarimetry quantitatively measure binding affinity and thermodynamics [52]. Thermal shifts are also complemented by methods that assess ligand engagement in cells, such as radioligand competition assays and fluorescent ligand-based assays [53–55]. Microarrays also offer a high throughput option to measure protein-ligand binding [56]. Given these many complementary assays, quantitative assays are needed to further explore the unexpected selectivity observed here with TMP269 and HDAC7 using thermal shift assays. However, these complementary assays also have limitations, such as use of large quantities of protein (ITC, SPR, polarimetry), radioactive or other non-native labels, or specialized equipment (microarrays). Compared to other ligand engagement assays, thermal shift methods require minimal reagents, accessible equipment, and unpurified cellular proteins. In total, thermal shift analysis is a useful qualitative tool to quickly test inhibitor engagement.

Overall, thermal shift analysis offers a simple, quick, inexpensive, and qualitative technique to monitor inhibitor engagement with the inactive class IIA pseudodeacetylases, which complements existing assays. Coupled with dot blot analysis, thermal shift assays provided the increased throughput appropriate for inhibitor screening. Therefore, thermal shift assays provide an initial appraisal of binding, which can be incorporated into broader screening campaigns to guide inhibitor design and development. In total, thermal shift analysis is an effective technique for studying class IIA pseudodeacetylase binding to facilitate drug design and development of HDAC inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Four HDAC inhibitors were obtained commercially and used without purification: SAHA (Cayman Chemical, 10009929), TMP269 (Selleck Chem, S7324), SB939 (BPS, 27771–1), and BRD-6929 (Sigma, #SML2521). All inhibitors were dissolved initially in 100 % DMSO prior to addition and dilution into binding reactions.

Lysis.

Cells were obtained from Cell Culture Company. Pellets (1 L growth) were lysed in lysis buffer (4 mL, 50 mM Tris HCl buffer, 150 mM NaCl, 10 % (v/v) glycerol, 0.5 % (v/v) Triton X-100, pH = 8) containing protease inhibitors (42 μL, GenDEPOT, P3100–020). Lysate concentrations were determined using Quick Start™ Bradford 1× Dye Reagent (BioRad).

Thermal shift reactions with gel analysis

HeLa lysate (250 μg total protein) was added to strip PCR tubes (ThermoFisher) and diluted with enough deionized water to achieve a final lysis buffer concentration of 20 μM Tris HCl. Lysates were preincubated with each inhibitor for 1 h at 25 °C at a concentration ten times higher than the highest IC50 value among HDAC1, 4, 5, or 7, with final concentrations of DMSO (2 %), SAHA (400 μM in 0.6 % DMSO), TMP269 (400 μM in 4 % DMSO), SB939 (1.5 μM in <0.001 % DMSO) or BRD-6929 (300 μM in 2.4 % DMSO) in a total volume of 20 μL. Reactions were then heated in a QuantStudio5 qPCR (ThermoFisher) across a 25 °C temperature range, with each tube varying 5 °C (e.g. 5 °C increments from 50 to 75 °C). After heating for 3 min, reactions were held at 4 °C before centrifugation for 10 min at 17,000 g/min to remove aggregated proteins. Equal volumes of supernatant were removed from reaction (8 μL) and denatured by adding loading dye (2.7 μL of 4×, generating a 1× final concentration, BioRad,) by boiling at 95 °C for 2 min. Proteins were separated by 8 % SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membrane (Immobilon P, Fisher Scientific) and visualized via western blot analysis with HDAC1 (Sigma-Aldrich, H3284), HDAC4 (ThermoFisher, MA5–15580), HDAC5 (Protein Tech, 16166–1-AP), or HDAC7 (Cell Signaling, 10831) primary antibodies, HRP-conjugated mouse anti-rabbit (1:1000 dilution, Cell Signaling, 7074S) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:10,000 dilution, Abcam, ab97040) secondary antibodies, and an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (1 mL of 1:1 peroxide:luminol enhancer, SuperSignal™ 50 ELISA Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate, Thermo Scientific) using the Protein Simple FluorochemQ gel imager. Membranes were incubated with primary antibody for 16 h at 4 °C and secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature before detection.

Thermal shift reactions with dot blot analysis

Thermal shift reactions were performed as described above except using greater HeLa lysate (450 μg total protein) without dilution with a 20 μL total volume. Use of more concentrated lysates was necessary to increase HDAC protein levels in the small volume spotted on the membrane to ensure detection. The supernatant from each heated reaction (2 μL, approximately 50 μg of lysate) was spotted on dry nitrocellulose membrane (ThermoFisher, 88014) using a multichannel pipettor to ensure equal spacing of dots. The samples were allowed to dry on the membrane for at least 3 h at room temperature. Proteins were visualized via western blot analysis for HDAC7 as described above, except with only a 2 h incubation at room temperature with HDAC7 primary antibody.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Protein band quantification for SDS-PAGE analysis was performed using Multiplex Band Analysis in the FluorochemQ imaging instrument by using the same sized box around each HDAC protein band to measure signal intensity. For dot blot analysis, ImageJ (Version 1.53) software used the same sized circle to quantify each protein spot. Intensity values for each protein band or spot were divided by the intensity of the control lane at the lowest temperature within each trial. Percentage protein levels were calculated from the protein intensity ratio, with the control lane set to 100 %. KaleidaGraph (Version 5.01) was used to plot and curve fit the quantified percentage protein remaining data using the equation y=100/(1+(x/Tagg)y). All data are represented as mean ± standard error [57] from at least three independent trials (Tables S1–S6).
