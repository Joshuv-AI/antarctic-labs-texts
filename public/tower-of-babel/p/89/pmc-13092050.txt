Age-dependent induction of ER stress in retinal pigment epithelium impairs phagocytosis via ADAM17-dependent MERTK shedding
Authors: Hiroto Yasuda, Shinsuke Nakamura, Aimi Shirakawa, Yoshiki Kuse, Kazutoshi Mori, Masamitsu Shimazawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinal pigment epithelium (RPE) plays a crucial role in maintaining visual function by phagocytosing photoreceptor outer segments (POS). Age-related decline in RPE phagocytic activity has been linked to the development of degenerative retinal diseases, including age-related macular degeneration (AMD). However, the underlying mechanisms of RPE phagocytic dysfunction remain poorly understood. In this study, we examined age-related induction of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in RPE cells and its association with POS phagocytosis using tissues from middle-aged mice and cultured RPE cells. In the RPE-choroid complex of 12-month-old mice, ER stress marker proteins were significantly upregulated compared to younger mice. Notably, this increase was absent in the neural retina at the same age. In cultured RPE cells, pharmacological induction of ER stress by tunicamycin (Tm) significantly reduced both phagocytic activity and lysosomal function. Treatment with sodium 4-phenylbutyrate, a chemical chaperone, and transfection with chaperone protein-inducible plasmids alleviated the ER stress-induced phagocytic dysfunction in RPE cells. In the lysates of ER stress-induced RPE cells, the extracellular domain of Mer tyrosine kinase receptor (MERTK) and phosphorylation of focal adhesion kinase were significantly decreased. Mechanistically, ER stress promoted the maturation of a disintegrin and metalloprotease 17 (ADAM17) through Ca2+-dependent activation of the Furin protease, leading to MERTK shedding. Furthermore, ADAM17 knockdown attenuated the Tm-induced impairment of POS internalization. Collectively, our findings suggest that ER stress impairs RPE phagocytosis through an integrated mechanism and may contribute to the pathogenesis of AMD.Keywords: ADAM, aging, calcium intracellular release, endoplasmic reticulum stress (ER stress), eye, phagocytosis, retina

The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) plays a crucial role in maintaining retinal homeostasis through its interaction with photoreceptor cells. Photoreceptor cells receive light signals and perform phototransduction, which exposes them to continuous damage from various stresses (1). Simultaneously, photoreceptor cells synthesize new photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and shed older, distal portions (2, 3). RPE cells phagocytose these shed POS from photoreceptor cells and degrade their components to recycle them, supporting visual function (4, 5) Disruption of this interaction can lead to lipid accumulation and secondary inflammation, triggering the onset of age-related macular degeneration (AMD), which results in severe and irreversible vision loss (6, 7). Indeed, RPE cells from elderly individuals show lower phagocytic activity, which declines with age. Importantly, RPE cells from AMD patients exhibit defective phagocytic function compared to age-matched donors (8). These findings suggest that age-related changes in RPE phagocytic activity may contribute to the onset and progression of AMD. However, the mechanisms by which aging affects RPE phagocytic function remain unclear. Therefore, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of age-related dysfunction in POS phagocytosis is crucial for developing new strategies for preventing and managing of AMD.

The process of POS phagocytosis involves multiple steps (9, 10). Discarded POS are recognized by cell surface receptors on the RPE, such as CD36 and integrin adhesion receptor αvβ5 (11, 12, 13). This recognition triggers the engulfment process mediated by Mer tyrosine kinase receptor (MERTK) (14, 15). Activation of MERTK promotes the recruitment of adaptor and effector proteins that facilitate pseudopod extension (9, 16). MERTK signaling also interacts with integrin αⅴ via focal adhesion kinase (FAK), promoting POS recognition and signaling pathways that engulfs POS into the cell (17). Internalized POS are encapsulated in phagosomes, which mature through stepwise fusion with endosomes and lysosomes, ultimately leading to POS degradation (18). Through this coordinated process, unwanted POS are efficiently cleared without accumulating between photoreceptor cells and RPE, thereby preventing the formation of lipofuscin and drusen.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a large organelle that contributes to protein folding and post-translational modifications, enabling proteins to function properly (19). Damage to the ER and dysfunction in protein clearance lead to the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins in the ER, a condition known as ER stress (20). ER stress activates several adaptive cellular pathways collectively known as the unfolded protein response (UPR), which aims to restore ER homeostasis by activating specific transcription factors. Notably, the upregulation of chaperone proteins such as glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) and glucose-regulated protein 94 (GRP94), as well as proteins for ER-associated protein degradation (ERAD) helps reduce protein accumulation and alleviate ER stress (21, 22). However, chronic ER stress exerts cytotoxic effects and contributes to the development of diverse age-related diseases (23, 24). Previous reports have investigated the physiological and pathological roles of ER stress. ER in RPE cells from AMD donors exhibited irregular morphology, characteristic of stress-induced ER structural alterations (25). Induction of ER stress resulted in RPE apoptosis, alteration of tight junctions, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which were observed during the pathogenic phase of AMD (26, 27, 28, 29). In single-cell RNA-seq analysis of human normal RPE, ER stress-related transcripts were more enriched in macular RPE, the main site of AMD lesion, than in peripheral RPE (30). These findings suggest that ER stress plays a role in both maintaining RPE homeostasis and developing AMD pathology. Phagocytosis is a common cellular function that helps maintain homeostasis, and Ito et al. showed that induced ER stress reduced the activity of efferocytosis in macrophages (31). However, the role of ER stress in POS phagocytosis by RPE, which is also implicated in the early phase of AMD, remains unclear.

Thus, the aim of this study was to investigate the involvement of ER stress in POS phagocytosis. We analyzed the expression of ER stress-related proteins in the eyes of 12-month-old mice, corresponding to middle-aged mice, and examined the effects of ER stress on phagocytosis in cultured RPE cells. Interestingly, we found that early induction of ER stress occurred in the RPE-choroid complex but not in the neural retina during aging. Additionally, our investigation indicated a fundamental mechanism by which severe ER stress impairs RPE phagocytic activity, specifically through lysosomal dysfunction and MERTK shedding, mediated by Ca2+-dependent maturation of a disintegrin and metalloprotease 17 (ADAM17). These findings shed light on the underlying mechanism of age-related phagocytic dysfunction in RPE and suggest the potential for targeting ER stress as a strategy for regulating POS phagocytosis and preventing age-related retinal dysfunction.

Results

Expression of ER stress marker proteins in retina and RPE-choroid complex in 2- and 12-month-old mice

We assessed the expression levels of ER stress marker proteins (Fig. 1A) in the neural retina and RPE-choroid of 2- and 12-month-old mice. In the retina, the expression levels of ER stress markers—including nuclear form of activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6n), spliced X-box binding protein-1 (XBP1s), ATF4 and GRP94—remained unchanged between the two age groups, while phosphorylated eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (p-eIF2α) and C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) showed a significant increase or increasing trend (Fig. 1, B and C). In contrast, in the RPE-choroid complex of 12-month-old mice, all of the aforementioned proteins were significantly upregulated (Fig. 1, D and E). Therefore, in the aging eye, ER stress may be induced especially in the RPE-choroid, before it affects the neural retina.

The induction of ER stress attenuated POS phagocytosis in cultured RPE cells

To explore the impact of ER stress on POS phagocytosis by RPE cells, we used an established in vitro RPE phagocytosis system using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)- or pHrodo-labeled POS (Fig. 2A). FITC-POS inherently emits fluorescence, allowing tracking of its uptake into cells. The intracellular localization of FITC-POS was confirmed by cell membrane staining (Fig. 2B), and phagocytosed FITC-POS was colocalized with LC3B puncta, indicating association with the autophagic pathway (Fig. S1A). In addition, POS were labeled with pHrodo, a pH-sensitive fluorescent dye whose fluorescence intensity increases in acidic environments (pHrodo-POS). After treatment of RPE cells with pHrodo-POS, the fluorescence of pHrodo was merged with lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) positive puncta, reflecting POS degradation in lysosome (Fig. 2C). To evaluate RPE phagocytic activity, intracellular fluorescence intensities of FITC-POS and pHrodo-POS were quantified. The intracellular fluorescence intensity of FITC-POS peaked at 6 h after POS treatment, whereas pHrodo-POS fluorescence increased in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1, B and D). Detected fluorescence signals were significantly higher in fluorescent-labeled POS-treated RPE cells than in POS-untreated cells (Blank) which was regarded as background in phagocytosis assay (Fig. 1, C and E).

Following fluorescence-based phagocytosis assay, to investigate whether high ER stress conditions affect phagocytic activity in RPE cells, we evaluated the effect of pharmacological ER stress induction using tunicamycin (Tm), known as ER stress-inducible compound by inhibiting N-linked glycosylation (Fig. 2D). Tm treatment at 10 μg/ml for 6 h significantly attenuated the fluorescence intensity of FITC and pHrodo in ARPE-19, suggesting a downregulation of both intake and degradation of POS (Fig. 2E). In human primary RPE cell (hRPE), treatment with Tm at 10 μg/ml for 6 h significantly attenuated phagocytic activity (Fig. 2F). Treatment with thapsigargin (Tg), another ER stress inducer that inhibits sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase, similarly reduced the phagocytic activity of ARPE-19 at 100 nM for 6 h (Fig. 2G). Excessive ER stress can lead to cytotoxicity and cell death (32, 33). To confirm whether the observed phagocytic impairments were due to cell death by Tm or Tg, we evaluated the cell death rate after ER stress induction. Under the same protocols, no significant cell death or decline in cell viability was observed (Fig. 2H). To further evaluate the effect of long-term induction of ER stress on POS phagocytosis, we co-treated cells with Tm and pHrodo-POS for 6 h following a 2-days pretreatment with Tm at 0.01 to 1 μg/ml (total 54 h). Short-term treatments did not attenuate phagocytosis, but Tm treatment at 0.1 or 1 μg/ml for 54 h significantly reduced the phagocytic activity in both ARPE - 19 and hRPE (Fig. 2, I–K). To compare the degree of ER stress, we examined the protein expression of GRP78, a well-established ER stress marker that is upregulated upon ER stress induction, under both (6 h and 54 h) Tm treatment conditions. GRP78 expression was significantly increased in ARPE - 19 cells treated with Tm at 10 μg/ml for 6 h and at 0.1 or 1 μg/ml for 54 h (Fig. 2, A and B), consistent with the observed downregulation of phagocytic activity (Fig. 2, E and J). These data suggest that severe ER stress reduces the phagocytic activity of RPE cells and that the degree of ER stress is more important than the duration of its induction.

Tm attenuated lysosomal and mitochondrial activity in RPE cells

To assess the effect of ER stress on lysosomal and mitochondrial activity in RPE cells, we performed lysosomal staining and measured mitochondrial OCR. LysoTracker fluorescence intensity was not altered by brief (3 h) ER stress induction with Tm (Fig. 3, A and B), but decreased significantly after 6 h treatment with 10 μg/ml of Tm, suggesting lysosomal dysfunction (Fig. 3, C and D). Low concentrations of Tm, such as 0.1 or 1 μg/ml, also reduced LysoTracker fluorescence intensity of lysotracker after 54 h treatment (Fig. 3, E and F). Regarding mitochondrial function, OCR was already reduced 3 h after Tm treatment at 10 μg/ml (Fig. 3, H and I). These data suggest that ER stress impairs both lysosomal and mitochondrial activity in RPE cells, with mitochondrial impairment occurring particularly early in the process.

Effectiveness of ER stress inhibition on phagocytic dysfunction

Sodium 4-phenylbutyrate (4-PBA) is a chemical chaperone that is reported to suppress ER stress-related phenotypes (34, 35). To investigate the effectiveness of pharmacological mitigation of ER stress, we assessed the effect of 4-PBA on ER stress-induced phagocytic impairment (Fig. 4A). Treatment with 4-PBA attenuated Tm-induced downregulation of pHrodo and lysoTracker fluorescence intensity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4, B and C). In response to intracellular stress, ER stress is tightly controlled by chaperone proteins such as GRP78 and GRP94 to prevent excess protein accumulation, and their expression is regulated by ATF6- and XBP1-mediated UPR (36, 37, 38) (Fig. 1A). To evaluate the effect of chaperone induction on ER stress-induced phagocytic dysfunction, we transfected ARPE-19 cells with ATF6n- or XBP1s-expression plasmids. We confirmed the upregulation of ATF6n protein, XBP1s mRNA and their target genes including GRP78 and GRP94 (Fig. 4, D–F). Importantly, overexpression of ATF6n and XBP1s attenuated tunicamycin-induced phagocytic impairment (Fig. 4, H and I). Interestingly, a single treatment with XBP1s plasmid somehow promoted phagocytosis in ARPE-19 cells. These data suggest that the pharmacologically reducing ER stress and activation of the ER stress-suppressive system could help attenuate ER stress-induced phagocytic impairment in RPE cells.

ER stress induction attenuated phagocytosis-related signaling

To explore the underlying mechanisms of Tm-induced phagocytic impairment, we assessed changes in the expression of phagocytosis-related proteins such as MERTK and FAK, which are initially activated during phagocytosis in RPE (Fig. 5A) (17). After Tm treatment at 10 μg/ml for 6 h, ER stress marker proteins, including p-eIF2α, ATF4, and GRP78, were significantly increased. In contrast, p-MERTK and p-FAK were downregulated (Fig. 5, B and C). Interestingly, the expression MERTK at approximately 170 kDa, representing the full-length protein, showed a significant decrease in ARPE-19 (Fig. 5, B and C). In ARPE-19 cells under long-term ER stress induction, the same pattern of protein expression changes was observed (Fig. 2, C and D). Gene expression of GRP78 and MERTK mRNA was significantly increased after Tm treatment, which was the opposite of the MERTK protein expression (Fig. 5D). Since MERTK contains multiple N-linked glycosylation sites, it was possible that inhibition of N-linked glycosylation by Tm affect maturation of MERTK (39, 40). In lysates from Tm-treated hRPE cells (10 μg/ml, 6 h), the abundance of glycosylated MERTK (= Full length-MERTK) around at 170 kDa was markedly reduced, whereas the level of un-glycosylated MERTK around at 110 kDa remained unchanged. As a result, the ratio of glycosylated MERTK to un-glycosylated MERTK was significantly decreased (Fig. 5, E and F). To examine whether the reduction in full length-MERTK was due to its release into the extracellular compartment, we next examined the presence of MERTK in the culture medium. By ELISA analysis of the culture supernatant, the level of the extracellular domain of MERTK was increased after Tm treatment for 6 h in both types of RPE cells (Fig. 5G). In the culture medium of Tm-treated hRPE cells, increased levels of full length-MERTK (170 kDa) and soluble MERTK (120 kDa), consistent with the molecular weights reported previously, were detected using antibodies against the extracellular domain (Fig. 5, H and I). Additionally, a MERTK fragment around 65 kDa, representing un-glycosylated soluble MERTK, was also detected weakly which is consistent with the molecular weight estimated from the amino acid sequence. These data suggest that Tm-induced ER stress leads to shedding and release of MERTK to attenuate phagocytic signaling (see Discussion).

Tm induced MERTK shedding via Ca2+-dependent ADAM17 maturation

To investigate the mechanism of Tm-induced MERTK shedding, we focused on the function of ADAM17, protease known to cleave the extracellular domain of MERTK in macrophages (41). After Tm treatment, the mature form of ADAM17 (matADAM17), which is responsible for ectodomain shedding, significantly increased in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6A) Treatment with Tm at 1 μg/ml for 54 h also induced ADAM17 maturation (Fig. 6B). ADAM17 maturation is driven by the proprotein convertase Furin, which is localized in the trans-Golgi network. In ARPE-19 cells treated with Tm for 1 or 3 h, ADAM17 was significantly and highly expressed in regions marked by Golgin-97, a known trans-Golgi marker (Fig. 6C). To determine whether tunicamycin-induced ADAM17 maturation is mediated by Furin, we examined the effect of decanoyl-Arg-Val-Lys-Arg-chloromethylketone (CMK), a Furin inhibitor. Treatment with CMK (100 μM) suppressed Tm-induced ADAM17 maturation. Furin's protease activity is dependent on Ca2+ concentration. Additionally, high ER stress conditions induce the upregulation of intracellular Ca2+ through Ca2+ release from the ER via membrane-localizing Ca2+ channels such as inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3R). Tm-induced ADAM17 maturation was suppressed by the Ca2+ chelator, 1,2-bis(o-aminophenoxy) ethane-N,N,N',N'-tetraacetic acid, tetraacetoxymethyl ester (BAPTA-AM, 100 μM) and the IP3R inhibitor, 2-aminoethoxydiphenyl borate (2-APB, 100 μM) (Fig. 6, E and F). We hypothesized that ER stress-induced ADAM17 maturation contributes to MERTK shedding (Fig. 6G), and we generated ADAM17 knockdown ARPE-19 cells using ADAM17 siRNA (Fig. 6H). After Tm treatment, the expression level of the extracellular domain of MERTK was significantly decreased in siCont; however, ADAM17 siRNA attenuated this Tm-induced downregulation. These data suggest that ER stress triggers MERTK shedding via ADAM17 maturation, which is dependent on Ca2+ release from IP3R.

MERTK is a key molecule involved in recognizing POS and initiating their uptake. To investigate whether ER stress affects POS uptake, we evaluated POS phagocytosis in ARPE-19 cells using a FITC-labeled POS assay with chloroquine (Fig. 6J). Given that FITC fluorescence decreases under acidic conditions, the fluorescence intensity of internalized FITC-POS gradually declined, reflecting POS degradation within lysosomes (Fig. 3, A and B). Based on this characterization of FITC-POS, chloroquine was used to inhibit lysosomal acidification, thereby increasing lysosomal pH and allowing visualization of the internalized POS (42). Under chloroquine co-treatment conditions, while Tm treatment decreased internalized POS, ADAM17 siRNA treatment suppressed this downregulation (Fig. 6K). These data suggest that Tm-induced ER stress promotes Ca2+-dependent Furin activation and ADAM17 maturation via Ca2+ release from the ER, resulting in MERTK shedding and impaired phagocytosis.

Expression of ADAM17 in aged murine RPE-choroid

To determine whether ADAM17 activation is induced in RPE-choroid tissue in aging mice, the expression levels of ADAM17 were assessed by Western blot analysis. In RPE-choroid tissue from 12-month-old mice, both the precursor form of ADAM17 (preADAM17) and matADAM17 were significantly increased (Fig. 7, A and B).

Discussion

We showed that ER stress marker proteins are upregulated in middle-aged murine RPE-choroid tissue. In RPE cells, elevated ER stress conditions promoted MERTK shedding through ADAM17 maturation, leading to impaired POS uptake and induced organelle dysfunction. Our findings suggest a fundamental molecular mechanism underlying age-related phagocytic impairment in RPE cells.

With aging, the capacity for protein quality control declines, inducing ER stress. Several studies using aged rodents demonstrated that the upregulation of ER stress-related proteins occurs in several tissues and cells such as adipose tissue, kidney, neurons, osteocytes, and the retina (43, 44, 45, 46, 47). Age-related alterations in ER stress proteins are suggested to be tissue dependent due to differences in the tissue environment (48). The RPE is continuously exposed to metabolic stress through daily phagocytosis of shed POS, whose degradation products are reused in the retinoid cycle (3). Retinoid cycle–derived all-trans-retinal (atRAL) is cytotoxic and induces ER stress in RPE cells (49) and A2E, a major bis-retinoid fluorophore in RPE, has also been reported to impair RPE phagocytic function (50, 51). In this study, we analyzed ER stress marker expression in middle-aged mice to capture early aging-related changes. As a result, ER stress marker proteins were significantly upregulated in the RPE-choroid earlier than in the neural retina in middle-aged eyes. The triggers for age-related ER stress induction in the RPE-choroid remain unclear, but this expression pattern was consistent with our previous findings, where mitochondrial dynamics and lipid droplet-associated proteins changed in the RPE-choroid, but not the retina (52, 53). These results suggest that the RPE may be particularly sensitive to various stresses, including ER stress.

ER stress is characterized by the acculturation of proteins in the lumen of the ER (20). To investigate the impact of ER stress on phagocytosis by RPE cells, we mainly treated the cells with Tm in vitro experiments. Both short-term treatment with 10 μg/ml Tm and long-term treatment with lower concentrations significantly reduced the phagocytic activity of ARPE - 19 and primary hRPE cells. A previous study using macrophages demonstrated that a 6-h treatment with 10 μg/ml Tm reduced phagocytic activity (31). These data suggest that when the demand to respond to unfolded proteins in the ER exceeds the cellular capacity, ER stress may trigger phagocytic dysfunction. Additionally, based on RPE's tendency to accumulate ER stress (Fig. 1), it is hypothesized that ER stress may contribute to age-related phagocytic dysfunction. A detailed investigation is needed to reveal the relationship between ER stress upregulation and complex regulation of phagocytosis by RPE in vivo, however, our findings might suggest the possibility of resolving the underlying mechanism of phagocytic impairment not only in RPE but also in other phagocytic cells.

During POS phagocytosis in the RPE, discard POS are internalized into cell and degraded within lysosomes, a process that requires proper lysosomal acidification maintained by vacuolar-type H + -ATPase (V-ATPase) (9, 54, 55). In this study, ER stress induction caused lysosomal and mitochondrial dysfunction, with an early reduction in mitochondrial oxygen consumption following Tm treatment. Excessive Ca2+ transfer from the ER to mitochondria disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential and impairs ATP production (56, 57). Considering ATP is required for V-ATPase activity in the lysosome, ER stress-induced acute mitochondrial impairment could contribute to lysosomal dysfunction in RPE. Moreover, excessive ER stress disrupted lysosomal homeostasis by reducing the number of lysosomes in human trophoblasts (58). Our data indicated that ER stress might suppress direct and/or indirect lysosomal function, leading to impairment of POS phagocytosis. Organelle functions are tightly interconnected, and our previous studies demonstrated age-dependent alterations in mitochondrial dynamics and lipid homeostasis in murine RPE-choroid (52, 53). Together with the present findings, these results suggest that organelle dysfunction may contribute to impaired POS phagocytosis in the RPE. Further studies are needed to clarify how age-related organelle dysfunction influences AMD pathogenesis.

ER stress has been increasingly recognized as a therapeutic target for various diseases (59). Chemical chaperones, such as 4-PBA and tauroursodexycholic acid, are known to enhance the cell's activity to handle ER stress and have the potential to suppress the progression of ER stress-related disorders (33, 60). In our study, treatment with 4-PBA attenuated Tm-induced phagocytic impairment and lysosomal dysfunction. These data may indicate that controlling ER stress with chemical drugs could be an effective approach. Originally, protein unfolding reactions are typically processed intracellularly, with chaperone proteins such as GRP78 and GRP94 involved in protein folding. To demonstrate the effectiveness of chaperone protein induction in conferring stress tolerance to ER stress-induced phagocytic dysfunction, we used ATF6n- and XBP1s-expression plasmids to induce chaperone proteins, confirming that they can improve phagocytosis. Collectively, the inhibition of ER stress by directly targeting unfolded proteins using chemical chaperones and activating the ER stress-suppressive system might be a novel strategy to improve age-related dysfunction of POS phagocytosis.

MERTK is an essential factor in POS phagocytosis. MERTK plays a key role in the engulfment of discarded POS and functions directly in the RPE phagocytic process as a signaling molecule that triggers POS internalization in RPE (14). Indeed, RPE isolated from Mertk−/− mice have compromised phagocytic activity (61). In our study, under ER stress conditions, MERTK was cleaved, and the expression level of the extracellular domain of MERTK was significantly decreased. Additionally, Law et al. reported that soluble MERTK inhibits POS phagocytosis by acting as a decoy receptor (62). Considering that full-length MERTK on the plasma membrane could initiate POS uptake into the cell, the integrity of MERTK is essential to maintain the phagocytic activity of RPE. Our results indicated that ER stress could impair the protein integrity of MERTK, leading to phagocytic dysfunction. In addition, we confirmed the presence of full length-MERTK in medium sample and their expression level also increased by Tm treatment. Although it remains unclear whether full-length MERTK is released via extracellular vesicles, such as exosomes or plasma membrane-derived microvesicles, it would be interesting to investigate the detailed mechanisms by which full-length MERTK is secreted into the extracellular compartment. Because cells under high ER stress contain unfolded proteins that must be properly folded or degraded, ER stress-mediated MERTK shedding may serve as a protective mechanism to reduce cellular burden by limiting phagocytosis. In addition, we confirmed ER stress induction reduced the phosphorylation of FAK. In integrin αv–mediated phagocytosis, the activation of FAK is required to initiate POS internalization and to induce MERTK phosphorylation (17). Moreover, in endothelial cells, ER stress induction by Tm and Tg has been reported to downregulate FAK phosphorylation via tyrosine phosphatases and calcium signaling (22). These findings suggest that ER stress may broadly impair the overall POS internalization process.

In terms of the mechanism of MERTK shedding, we identified ADAM17 as a key regulator in this phenomenon. A previous report by Thorp et al. showed that MERTK shedding requires ADAM17 in macrophages (41). Rzymski et al. reported that ER stress induces ATF4 expression, leading to ADAM17 transcription and shedding of the tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 in HeLa cells (63). In our in vitro study using RPE cells, Tm-induced ER stress promoted ADAM17 maturation. Furthermore, our data suggested that ER stress induction promoted 1) excessive Ca2+ release from the ER via IP3R and 2) Ca2+-dependent ADAM17 maturation by Furin protease in the trans-Golgi (Fig. 4, C–F). These data indicated that ER stress triggered Ca2+ overload, followed by MERTK shedding mediated by matADAM17, which contributed to phagocytic dysfunction. In the trans-Golgi network, the secretory pathway Ca2+/Mn2+-ATPase (SPCA) plays a crucial role in Ca2+ uptake and influences the protease activity of Furin (64, 65). Although the detailed mechanism by which ER stress induces furin activation remains unclear, it may be interesting to focus on the involvement of SPCA in this sequential mechanism. In addition, the proposed mechanism in this study was mainly based on findings from in vitro experiments using Tm, an inhibitor of N-linked glycosylation, as an ER stress inducer. Since N-linked glycosylation originally involved protein modification such as proper protein folding and stability (66), it will be necessary to further elucidate the relationship between ER stress and age-related phagocytic dysfunction using multiple experimental approaches, including strategies distinct from inhibition of N-linked glycosylation.

In contrast to ER stress-induced cultured RPE cells, both preADAM17 and matADAM17 were elevated in the RPE-choroid tissue of middle-aged mice, although the maturation ratio remained unchanged. ER stress activates ADAM17 transcription via ATF4 in HeLa cells (63). In this study, severe ER stress induction by pharmaceutical treatment was required to induce phagocytic impairment in our in vitro experimental system, so we focused on the proteomic changes of ADAM17 under these conditions. The difference in the degree of ER stress between aged RPE-choroid tissue and pharmacologically induced ER-stressed RPE cells may account for the observed differences in ADAM17 expression. Considering that the matADAM17 possesses cleavage activity, the age-related upregulation of ADAM17 may contribute to the shedding of ectodomains from various substrates, including MERTK. It would be interesting to investigate whether increased ADAM17 expression in RPE is associated with age-related phagocytic dysfunction and could be a potential risk factor for the onset of AMD.

In summary, our findings indicate that ER stress induction in RPE reduces phagocytic activity through multiple integrated mechanisms, including lysosomal dysfunction and MERTK shedding mediated by Ca2+-dependent ADAM17 maturation. Furthermore, this phenomenon may play a role in the pathogenesis of age-related disorders such as AMD.

Experimental procedures

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 8 weeks were purchased from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan). The mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food (CE-2; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and water. All procedures followed the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved and monitored by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Gifu Pharmaceutical University.

Cell culture

The human-derived RPE cell line, ARPE-19, was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM)/Ham’s F12 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biosera, Kansas City, MO, USA). Human primary RPE cells (hRPE) were obtained from Lonza (Walkersville, MD, USA) and cultured in Epithelial Cell Medium containing 2% FBS following the manufacturer’s protocol. Both cell types were maintained in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37 °C. ARPE-19 and hRPE cells were passaged by trypsinization every 4 days; ARPE-19 from passages three to ten and hRPE cells from passages three to five were used for experiments.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed as described in a previous report with modifications (67). For the in vivo study, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the eyes were quickly removed. The RPE–choroid complexes were separated from the eyecups, and the samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. To extract protein, the RPE–choroid complex was homogenized using a Handy micro homogenizer (NS-310E3; Microtec Co., Ltd., Chiba, Japan) in cell lysis buffer. In the in vitro study, ARPE-19 or hRPE cells were collected with a cell lysis buffer. Cells were cultured in separate wells and lysed independently. Four or six biological replicates were analyzed for each condition. The lysates from both tissues and cells were centrifuged at 12,000g and 4 °C for 20 min, and protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA). The supernatants were boiled in sample buffer solution containing three-mercapto-1,2-propanediol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) for 5 min. Samples were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on a 5 to 20% gradient gel (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Immobilon-P; Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with Blocking One-P solution (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used for western-blotting are listed in Table 1. The primary antibodies were diluted using Can Get Signal Ⅰ (Nacalai Tesque). Membranes were washed with TBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (Bio-Rad) and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (RT). The following secondary antibodies were used: horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:2000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:2000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using ImmunoStar LD (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and images were captured using an Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Band intensities were quantified using Amersham Imager 680 Analysis software (GE Healthcare), and relative protein levels were normalized to β-actin or corresponded non-phosphorylated protein. The sample size (n) in Western blot analysis was defined as multiple lanes on the same blot, with details described in the corresponding figure legends.

In vitro phagocytosis assay

The isolation of POS from porcine eyes and subsequent labeling with pHrodo succinimidyl ester (pHrodo; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) isomer Ⅰ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed as described in Saito et al. (68).

ARPE-19 (15,000 cells/well) and hRPE (10,000 cells/well) cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated in DMEM/Ham’s F12 supplemented with 10% FBS (ARPE-19) or EpiCM supplemented with 2% FBS (hRPE) for four or 7 days. For the assessment of ADAM17 small interfering RNA (siRNA) or each plasmid in ARPE-19 cells, transfection was performed and started at Day two. After changing the culture medium to DMEM/Ham’s F12 with 1% FBS (ARPE-19) or EpiCM without FBS (hRPE), pharmacological ER stress inducer, tunicamycin (Tm; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) or thapsigargin (Tg; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and fluorescently labeled POS (pHrodo-labeled POS [pHrodo-POS] or FITC-labeled POS [FITC-POS]) at 1 × 105 POS/well were added to each well. To evaluate the effect of 4-PBA (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), 4-PBA was added at a final concentration ranging from 0.01 to 1 mM was added 1 h before Tm treatment. After cells were incubated for 6 h, the cells were washed with DMEM/Ham’s F12 containing 1% FBS or EpiCM without FBS to remove extracellular POS. Images were captured at the center of each well to cover most of the well area using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X710, Keyence, Osaka, Japan) and analyzed using ImageJ software, as reported previously (69).

To evaluate POS degradation, the medium of ADAM17 siRNA-treated ARPE-19 cells was replaced with DMEM/Ham’s F12 containing 1% FBS on Day four. Subsequently, the cells were co-treated with ADAM17 siRNA and the autophagy inhibitor, chloroquine (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and incubated for 1 h. Then, 1 × 105 FITC-labeled POS/well and Tm were added and incubated for 6 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) for 15 min at RT. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and internalized POS in ARPE-19 cells were photographed as mentioned above using a BZ-X700 all-in-one fluorescence microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

For plasma membrane staining, ARPE-19 cells (30,000 cells/well) were seeded into an eight-well chamber plate and treated with FITC-POS on day 4 post-seeding using the aforementioned method. After 6 h of incubation, cells were stained with PlasMem Bright Red (Dojindo Kagaku, Kumamoto, Japan) and Hoechst 33,342 for 30 min at 37 °C. The medium was then replaced with Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (+) (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and the cells were photographed using a confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell death assay

Cell death was assessed by co-staining with Hoechst 33,342 and propidium iodide (PI; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After Tm treatment for 6 h, the mixture of Hoechst 33,342 (final concentration: 8.1 μM) and PI (final concentration: 1.5 μM) was added to ARPE-19 cells, and the cells were incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. Images showing Hoechst 33,342-stained nuclei (all cells) and PI-stained nuclei (dead cells) were captured using a fluorescence microscope. The images were captured at the center of each well to cover most of the well area. The cell death rate was calculated as follows: (Number of PI-positive cells/Number of Hoechst33342-positive cells) × 100 (%).

Assessment of lysosomal activity

Lysosomal activity in Tm-treated ARPE-19 cells was evaluated using LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described previouly, with some modifications (70). Cells were incubated with 500 nM LysoTracker Red DND-99 and Hoechst 33,342 solution (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# H3570) for 30 min at 37 °C. Images were obtained at the center of each well to cover most of the well area using a fluorescence microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Seahorse flux analyzer

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using the Cell Mito Stress Test with a Seahorse XF HS Mini Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). ARPE-19 and hRPE cells were seeded at three × 104 or 2 × 104 cells/well into 6-well plates and incubated for 4 days. The culture medium was replaced to DMEM/Ham’s F12 containing 1% FBS, and Tm (10 μg/ml) was added. After a 6 h incubation, the cells were washed, and the medium was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM assay medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM pyruvate, and 10 mM glucose. The 6-well plates were set in the Seahorse XF HS Mini Analyzer, and OCRs were measured using the following subsequent treatment: 1.5 μM oligomycin, 1.0 μM FCCP, and 0.5 μM rotenone-antimycin A (Agilent Technologies). In assessment of mitochondrial respiration using Seahorse XF software (Agilent Technologies), we defined the basal and maximum respiratory OCR as follows: basal respiration was defined as the oxygen consumption rate measured prior to the addition of oligomycin, after subtraction of non-mitochondrial respiration. Maximal respiration was calculated as the FCCP-stimulated oxygen consumption rate, also corrected for non-mitochondrial respiration.

Plasmid transfection

pcDNA-ATF6α (1-373) [pATF6n], which induces ATF6n, and pcDNA-XBP1s [pXBP1s], which induces XBP1s, were prepared as previously described (38, 71). ARPE-19 cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/ml in 24 or 96 well plates and incubated for 2 days at 37 °C. The medium was replaced with DMEM/Ham’s F12 containing 10% FBS without antibiotics, and each plasmid was transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The final DNA concentration in each well was adjusted to 1 ng/μl pcDNA 3.1 (+) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a mock vector. Cells were used for experiments two days after plasmid treatment.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA from ARPE - 19 cells treated with Tm for 6 h was extracted using a NucleoSpin RNA Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan), and the cDNA library was synthesized using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To detect gene expression, PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The PCR primer sequences are listed in Table 2. Gene expressions were normalized to β-actin.

Detection of soluble MERTK in culture medium

ARPE-19 or hRPE cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 105 or 1.0 × 105 cells/ml and incubated for four or seven days. The culture medium was then replaced with FBS-free DMEM/Ham’s F12 or EpiCM following twice washing, and Tm (10 μg/ml) was added. After a 6 h incubation, the medium was collected and centrifuged at 2000g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove dead cells. The supernatant was then applied to centrifugal filter devices (Amicon Ultra-0.5; 3.0 kDa MW cut off; Merck Millipore, Burlington, VT, USA). The medium was centrifuged at 140,00g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was replaced in filter device and centrifuged additionally at 140,00g for 30 min at 4 °C. The final supernatant was collected, and the expression of released MERTK was determined by Western blotting using an antibody against the extracellular domain of MERTK (ab52968, abcam) and analyzed using Amersham Imager 680 Analysis software. To quantify the extracellular domain of MERTK, an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed using an anti-MERTK ELISA kit (ab300333, abcam) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Immunocytochemistry

ARPE-19 cells were seeded into an eight-well chamber plate at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/ml and incubated for 4 days. The culture medium was replaced with DMEM/Ham’s F12 containing 1% FBS, and the cells were treated with Tm for 3 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% PFA at RT for 15 min and washed three times with PBS. In validation of phagocytosis assay using FITC-POS and pHrodo-POS, the cells were fixed 6 h after FITC-POS treatment or 24 h after pHrodo-POS treatment. To permeabilize the cell membrane, the cells were incubated with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) for 15 min and blocked with Dako Protein Block (Agilent Technologies) for 30 min. For LC3B staining, 0.1% saponin (Nacalai Tesque) was added instead of Triton X-100 during permeabilization and also during antibody incubation. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies listed in Table 3. After primary antibody incubation, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 546 goat-anti mouse antibody (1:1000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 546 donkey-anti rabbit antibody (1:1000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey-anti mouse antibody (1:1000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Alexa Fluor 488 donkey-anti rabbit antibody (1:1000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at RT. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (1:1000 dilution) for 15 min at RT. After PBS wash, cells were mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images (seven or ten independent spots/well, three well/group) were captured using a confocal microscope. To quantify ADAM17 expression localized in the trans-Golgi network, fluorescence intensity of ADAM17 colocalized with Golgin-97 in each cell was quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA interference

ADAM17 knockdown using siRNA was performed as described previously, with minor modifications (72). ARPE-19 cells were cultured in 24-well or 96-well plates with DMEM/Ham’s F12 containing 10% FBS at 37 °C for 2 days. The medium was then replaced with DMEM/Ham’s F12 containing 10% FBS without antibiotics. The cells were transfected with 20 nM ADAM17 siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The siRNA sequence used was as follows:

Adam17-siRNA

Sense: 5′-CCAGGGAGGGAAAUAUGUCAUGUAU-3′

Antisense: 5′-AUACAUGACAUAUUUCCCUCCCUGG-3′.

For the control group, Stealth RNAi siRNA Negative Control Lo GC Duplex #2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was transfected using the same protocol. Cells were used for experiments two days after siRNA treatment.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical comparisons were made using Student’s t test, Welch’s t test, Dunnett’s test, Dunnett’s T3 test, Games-Howell test, Tukey’s test, and Kruskal–Wallis test with post hoc Bonferroni correction (SPSS Statistics software; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
