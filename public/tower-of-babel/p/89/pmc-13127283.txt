Introduction

Multiple Myeloma (MM) is characterized by the proliferation of clonal plasma cells that typically produce a monoclonal protein called M-component. Currently, it is an incurable disease, with successive responses and relapses after different lines of treatment 1 .

Bispecific antibodies are a new treatment option for relapsed/refractory MM (R/R MM). They are characterized by binding to both neoplastic cells, in this case MM plasma cells, and immune system cells, generating an immunological synapse and allowing the latter to produce selective destruction of neoplastic cells 2 . Elranatamab is a bispecific antibody that binds to both MM plasma cells expressing B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) and T lymphocytes expressing CD3. It has overall response rates of 61% in R/R MM, but not exempt from adverse reactions, the most common being hematological toxicity and cytokine release síndrome 3 .

Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) is a well-known complication of the treatment of certain hematologic diseases 4 , but given the low proliferation rate of plasma cells in MM, its frequency in this hematologic neoplasia is very low 5 . So far, there are no reports of TLS after application of elranatamab or other bispecific agents in R/R MM patients. Nevertheless, this complication should be considered with the use of these novel therapies, as their high efficacy may precipitate massive tumor cell lysis.

We report a case of R/R MM that presented clinical TLS after the first dose of elranatamab, which resolved with supportive measures and did not recur after subsequent applications.

Case Report

A 67-year-old male with a history of ischemic heart disease with placement of 2 coronary stents and arterial hypertension, medicated with nebivolol 5 mg every 12 hours and amlodipine 5 mg/day, was diagnosed 4 years ago with IgA Kappa MM, ISS I, R-ISS II due to high-risk cytogenetics (presence of translocation 4;14). At the time of diagnosis, he had anemia but no renal failure, hypercalcemia, or bone lesions. He underwent first-line treatment with bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone (VTD) followed by autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) and subsequently maintenance therapy with lenalidomide, achieving complete response (CR) with biochemical relapse after 12 months of ASCT. He received second-line therapy with daratumumab, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone, achieving a second CR with biochemical relapse after 18 cycles. At the time of relapse, he was switched to third-line therapy with carfilzomib, pomalidomide, and dexamethasone, achieving minimal response with progression after the 8th cycle of treatment. At the time of progression, he had 3% plasma cells in peripheral blood, without renal failure, bone involvement, or hypercalcemia, and the presence of 1q gain was detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). As he was a penta-refractory patient (refractory to 2 immunomodulators, 2 proteasome inhibitors, and 1 anti-CD38), fourth-line treatment with monotherapy elranatamab was indicated.

He was admitted for scheduled administration of the first two doses of elranatamab (12 mg on day 1 and 72 mg on day 4, both subcutaneous applications). Prior to the first application, he had normal vital signs, with a physical examination revealing no abnormalities. Laboratory tests before the first application showed white blood cells 4500 cells/mm3, hemoglobin 10 g/dl, and platelets 30,000 plt/uL. Creatinine was 1.09 mg/dl (normal range up to 1.20 mg/dl), LDH 256 U/L (normal range up to 250 U/L), potassium 4.6 mmol/L (normal range up to 5 mmol/L), sodium 144 mmol/L (normal range up to 147 mmol/L), ionized calcium 1.1 mMol/L (normal range up to 1-1.3 mMol/L), and normal liver function tests.

At 24 hours after the first elranatamab application, he developed a self-limited febrile episode without infectious focus or hemodynamic decompensation, classified as grade I cytokine release syndrome. Blood cultures were taken which subsequently did not yield isolation, and empirical piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated for 5 days. At 72-96 hours after the first application, he experienced palpitations, and ventricular extrasystoles were demonstrated on electrocardiogram. Laboratory tests showed acute renal failure, with creatinine 1.95 mg/dl, LDH 1645 U/L, uric acid 9.5 mg/dl (normal range up to 7 mg/dl), phosphorus 5.5 mg/dl (normal range up to 4.5 mg/dl), ionized calcium 0.90 mMol/L, and potassium 4.3 mg/dl. Urinary tract ultrasound was normal. Clinical TLS was diagnosed (according to Cairo and Bishop criteria4), and treatment with allopurinol 300 mg/day along with intravenous calcium replacement and hydration was initiated. At 24 hours after starting treatment, his ion levels were normal, and his creatinine level was 1.5 mg/dl.

Results

After 10 days of hospitalization, he was discharged with normal ion levels and creatinine. The patient did not experience tumor lysis syndrome again after subsequent elranatamab applications. At the end of the second cycle, the patient achieved complete response (CR) and is currently undergoing the 6th cycle with strict CR, with negative minimal residual disease by flow cytometry of bone marrow.

Discussion and Conclusion

TLS is characterized by the release of intracellular ions and metabolites into the bloodstream after the death of neoplastic cells, which can occur spontaneously or more frequently after oncospecific treatment 4 , 5 . The Cairo and Bishop classification divides TLS into two forms: a laboratory one, in which only biochemical abnormalities are found (hyperuricemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, and/or hyperkalemia), and a clinical one, in which, besides biochemical abnormalities, any of the following are present: acute renal failure, cardiac arrhythmias/sudden death, or seizures 4 . Our patient was diagnosed with clinical TLS since, in addition to ionic alterations, he presented acute renal failure and cardiac arrhythmia.

Given the low proliferation rate of MM plasma cells, TLS occurs in only 1-4% of MM patients and cases, both post-treatment and spontaneous, have been described 5 , 6 . In R/R MM patients receiving proteasome inhibitors, the frequency of TLS may increase up to 18% 7-12 .

In addition to treatment with proteasome inhibitors, other risk factors associated with TLS in MM include previous renal failure, hyperuricemia, high tumor burden (elevated LDH, multiple lytic bone lesions, and a high percentage of plasma cells in bone marrow), cytogenetic abnormalities, and hepatosplenomegaly 5 , 6 , 7 .

At the time of developing TLS, our patient presented two of risk factors described above: high tumor burden (elevated LDH and plasma cells in peripheral blood) along with cytogenetic abnormalities (translocation 4;14 and 1q gain).

The prognosis of TLS in multiple myeloma is highly variable; however, mortality is high in patients requiring renal replacement therapy 5 . Although the presence of TLS might be expected to be related to significant cellular destruction and therefore lead to more effective treatment responses, this is not the case in multiple myeloma. In fact, in some reports, it has been associated with lower overall survival 5 . In the presented case, renal replacement therapy was not necessary, and the patient is currently in complete response without experiencing TLS again after subsequent applications.

Bispecific antibodies, including elranatamab, are a new treatment option for R/R MM patients. They are characterized by binding to a target present on malignant plasma cells as well as cytotoxic immune effector cells, generating destruction of malignant plasma cells by activating immune cells 2 . They are characterized by overall response rates that can reach up to 80%. Among their most frequent adverse reactions are hematological toxicity and cytokine release syndrome 13 .

Despite not finding literature on TLS following bispecific antibodies in R/R MM patients, there are reports of TLS following therapy with BCMA-directed chimeric antigen receptor-T cells (CAR-T) in MM patients 14 , 15 . In one of these publications, TLS frequency was described as 17%, with a median time from CAR-T infusion to TLS onset of 8 days, with renal failure and cardiac arrhythmias occurring in 100% and 47% of cases, respectively 14 . In this publication, 100% of patients who developed TLS had cytokine release syndrome, and TLS was associated with lower response rates and lower progression-free survival 14 .

Although our patient did not receive CAR-T therapy but a bispecific antibody also directed against BCMA, similarities were observed with cases described by Zhang Q et al. 14 , such as the association with cytokine release syndrome, renal failure, and cardiac arrhythmias. The association of TLS and cytokine release syndrome may be due to both complications being associated with a high tumor burden prior to treatment 14 . Another possible cause of their association is that elevated cytokines in cytokine release syndrome could contribute to cell death and subsequent TLS 14 .

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first description of TLS associated with elranatamab, so we consider it important to report the case, as the high effectiveness of this drug will potentially increase its use, requiring knowledge of its possible adverse events for timely prevention and management.

In conclusion, the increasing use of these highly effective novel therapies will likely provide further insights into this complication, which has historically been rare in multiple myeloma.
