The intersections of climate change, gender, migration, and sexual and reproductive health in Sub-Saharan Africa: A scoping review
Authors: Tariku Shimels, Zewdneh Shewamene, Dagim Habteyesus, Abraham Fessehaye Sium, Getachew Teshome
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change drives socio-environmental shifts in Sub-Saharan Africa, disproportionately impacting marginalized groups. This scoping review aims to document the impacts of climate change, explore its effects on gender, migration, and sexual and reproductive health (SRH), while also mapping adaptation strategies.MethodsA systematic approach was employed to review peer-reviewed articles, gray literature, and relevant policy documents sourced from major academic databases and authoritative international organizations. The inclusion criteria focused on studies published between 2000 and 2025 that addressed climate change and its associated impacts in Sub-Saharan Africa and explored intersections with gender, migration, or SRH. Data were extracted using a standardized form and synthesized thematically to identify patterns, disparities, and interconnections in climate-related vulnerabilities across affected populations.ResultsFindings from the 22 included studies revealed that climate change disproportionately affects women, girls, and adolescents in Sub-Saharan Africa. These impacts are reflected in the deepening of existing gender inequalities, significant disruptions to SRH service availability and accessibility, and heightened vulnerability during migration and displacement events. Though limited in scale and depth of implementation, studies have also documented gender-responsive adaptation strategies, such as community-led resilience initiatives, local coping mechanisms, and targeted policy interventions, highlighting emerging models of localized adaptation and response.ConclusionClimate change presents a significant and multifaceted threat to adolescents, youth, and women, particularly in relation to SRH, gender inequality, and migration. Effectively addressing these challenges requires integrated, multidisciplinary approaches and inclusive policies that prioritize vulnerable populations.

Background

Sub-Saharan Africa faces unprecedented challenges due to climate change, which manifests as extreme weather events, resource scarcity, and environmental degradation [1]. Natural disasters caused by climate change have been reported to complicate internal displacement across countries in the region. For instance, Ethiopia, Somalia, and Kenya suffered severe floods in 2023, which were fueled by the onset of El Niño, and caused 2.9 million movements, nearly a third of the Fig. globally [2]. This phenomenon has been observed across all six eco-regions of Africa, leading to increased temperatures, rising sea levels, and a higher frequency of extreme events since the early twenty first century [3]. Most notably, climatic changes have been demonstrably linked to disproportionately impacting vulnerable populations, with women, girls, and adolescents bearing the brunt of its effects [4].

Gender inequality amplifies the adverse effects of climate change, especially in resource-constrained settings, by intensifying difficulties with resource access, healthcare quality, and decision-making authority [5]. Structural barriers, including unequal access to land, financial resources, and decision-making platforms, hinder women’s capacity to adapt to climate-induced stressors [6]. Women, while reported to contribute to over 60 % of the agricultural workforce, are particularly reliant on climate-sensitive sectors and natural resources for their livelihoods [7]. Climate-induced migration further exacerbates these vulnerabilities by disrupting social networks, intensifying gender-based violence (GBV), and limiting access to essential services such as healthcare and education.

The relationship between climate change and migration is extensively documented, with environmental disruptions increasingly recognized as drivers of population displacement [8]. In Bangladesh, climate-induced overwhelming migration has resulted in challenging urban consequences, that require attention to build resilient cities and ensure rural resilience [9]. These climate-induced movements often exacerbate existing vulnerabilities, particularly among women and girls [10]. This, in turn, may be closely linked to other dimensions, such as heightened risks of GBV, disrupted access to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, and the worsening of gender inequalities.

From a sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) perspective, climate change intensifies all forms of violence- physical, sexual, and emotional against women and girls [11]. Each 1°C increase in the annual mean temperature has been associated with a 4.5 % increase in the prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) [12]. Extreme weather also causes impediments to accessing SRH services, such as disrupting continuity of care in human immune deficiency virus (HIV) infected individuals [13], increased risky sexual behaviors that expose females to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [14,15], and overall negative maternal and newborn health outcomes [16]. Adolescents, particularly girls, face compounded risks, as climate impacts often force them into early marriages or limit their educational opportunities [17].

Despite the growing recognition of these issues, research and policy interventions addressing the nexus between climate change, gender, migration, and SRH remain fragmented. This review seeks to synthesize existing evidence to enhance understanding of these intersections, with a primary focus on mapping the impacts of climate change across these domains, and a secondary focus on adaptation strategies to inform resilience-building efforts in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted using a comprehensive methodological framework, grounded in the Arksey and O’Malley approach [18] and further enhanced in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines (PRISMA-ScR) [19]. The review protocol was prospectively registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) database [20].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they addressed the impacts of climate change and its adaptation strategies in Sub-Saharan Africa and explored intersections with gender, migration, or SRH. Eligible studies were published in English between 2000 and January 2025 and employed quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods research designs. In addition, gray literature sources were considered for inclusion. Non-English articles were considered if they included an English abstract. Studies were excluded if they did not focus on Sub-Saharan Africa, lacked relevance to gender, migration, or SRH, were purely environmental without human dimensions.

Search strategy and sources

A comprehensive search strategy was implemented to identify both published and unpublished literature relevant to the review objectives. Searches were conducted across multiple academic databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, and JSTOR. In addition, gray literature from reputable international organizations such as the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and the African Union (AU) was examined to capture relevant reports and policy documents. The search strategy incorporated a combination of keywords and subject headings related to ‘climate change,’ ‘gender,’ ‘migration,’ ‘sexual and reproductive health,’ and ‘Africa.’ Boolean operators (AND, OR), truncation, and controlled vocabularies such as MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) in PubMed and Emtree in Elsevier databases were used to tailor and refine search results for each platform. The detailed search strategies for each selected database are provided in the supplementary files (see Supplementary Material).

Study selection and screening

Two independent reviewers (TS and ZS with support by AFS) screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessments. Rayyan web-based software [21] was employed in the screening process. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (GT or DH). Data extraction

Data were extracted using a comprehensive and structured form aligned with review objectives. This form captured multiple dimensions of each study to enable in-depth synthesis and comparison. Each study was classified by study ID, design, and location, allowing for identification of methodological diversity and regional distribution across Sub-Saharan Africa. Population demographics were recorded to reflect the characteristics of study participants. Climate-related factors or changes that shaped the context of each study, such as prolonged droughts, floods, shifting rainfall patterns, and rising temperatures, were recorded. Reported outcomes reported were noted, including gender disparities, migration trends, disruptions in SRH services, and heightened exposure to GBV. Key findings were summarized to capture the core evidence and insights presented. Finally, adaptation and coping strategies, proposed interventions or conclusions detailing community and policy responses, gender-responsive climate actions, and recommendations were indicated (Supplementary Table 1).

Approach to framework analysis

A framework analysis approach, as recommended for use in multidisciplinary research [22], was used to structure and synthesize findings across the included studies. A structured coding framework was initially developed based on the domains; climate-related factors, gender dimensions, migration dynamics, SRH outcomes, and adaptation strategies. This framework provided a predefined structure for organizing and analyzing the extracted data.

Two reviewers (TS and DH) independently applied the coding framework to the extracted data, systematically mapping content under each domain. As the analysis progressed, the framework was iteratively refined to capture the depth and variation of findings within and across domains. A coding matrix facilitated cross-study comparison of key domains and emerging patterns.

Discrepancies in coding and interpretation were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers. Where necessary, additional members of the review team were consulted to ensure consistency and analytical coherence.

Charting of results

Data were synthesized and charted thematically to reflect the complex and intersecting impacts of climate change in Sub-Saharan Africa. The first theme focused on key climate change drivers in the region, including recurrent droughts, floods, temperature extremes, and shifting rainfall patterns, which have undermined agricultural productivity, strained water resources, and contributed to environmental degradation. The second explored the gender dimensions of climate change, highlighting how socio-cultural norms and systemic inequalities increase women’s and girls’ vulnerability to climate shocks, limit their adaptive capacity, and expose them to heightened risks such as food insecurity, unpaid care burdens, and GBV. The third examined migration dynamics, emphasizing how climatic stressors have led to both temporary and permanent displacements, rural-to-urban shifts, and cross-border movements, with risks for women, children, and other marginalized groups. Lastly, the synthesis addressed the nexus among climate change, gender, migration, and SRH, illustrating how disrupted health systems, reduced mobility, and social instability hinder access to SRH services for climate-affected and displaced populations. Across all themes, the synthesis captured the adaptation strategies and policy responses reported in the studies, including community-based resilience efforts and gender-sensitive interventions.

Quality appraisal and reporting

Consistent with PRISMA-ScR guidance and the scoping review methodology, no formal quality appraisal of included studies was undertaken. The purpose of this review was to map the breadth and nature of existing evidence rather than to evaluate the methodological rigor of individual studies. To ensure transparency and reliability, the reporting of methods, results, and key conclusions adheres to the PRISMA-ScR checklist [19], allowing readers to interpret the findings within the scope and intent of a scoping review.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

A total of 3,560 records were identified through database searches (n =3,542) and gray literature sources (n =18). From these, 259 records were excluded—220 duplicates and 39 records automatically identified as ineligible using the Rayyan web-based screening tool. The automated ineligibility filter was applied using predefined keywords and exclusion terms such as 'climate modeling only,' 'non-human studies,' 'outside Sub-Saharan Africa,' and 'non-health-related content,' to ensure relevance to the review's scope. The remaining 3,301 records were screened, of which 3,161 were excluded based on title and abstract for not meeting the inclusion criteria. A total of 140 reports were sought for full-text retrieval, and all were successfully retrieved. After full-text assessment, 118 reports were excluded: 55 for not involving the relevant population, 38 for being out of scope, and 25 due to differing contextual focus. Ultimately, 22 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review.

Characteristics and summary of sources of evidence

A total of 22 articles and documents were evaluated in this review. Reports encompassed a range of methodologies, including qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, systematic reviews, and expert-driven analyses, with a focus on the impacts of climate change on gender, migration, and SRH in Sub-Saharan Africa. Studies were conducted in various geographical settings, including Zambia [23], Ethiopia [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]], Kenya [30], and multi-country assessments covering Uganda, South Sudan, Sudan, Zimbabwe, and others [31]. There were two reviews [32,33] and continent-wide reports [34,35].Study populations ranged from smallholder farmers, women, and adolescents to healthcare providers, policymakers, and displaced persons. Climate-related factors analyzed included droughts, floods, erratic rainfall, rising temperatures, and extreme weather events, all of which contributed to gender inequality, migration patterns, and compromised SRH access [36,37].

The reviewed studies consistently reported women—particularly female-headed households and adolescent girls—faced increased vulnerabilities in the context of climate-induced migration. These included limited access to resources, heightened exposure to GBV, and socio-economic precarity in displacement settings [27,31]. Migration was frequently described as both a coping mechanism and a driver of additional risks, contributing to disrupted access to SRH services, increased adolescent pregnancy, and reduced healthcare continuity [38,39]. In terms of responses, the literature documented a range of adaptation strategies, including climate-smart agriculture, water conservation techniques, sustainable livelihood programs, community-based resilience interventions, and the implementation of gender-responsive policy measures aimed at reducing long-term vulnerability [30,32]. Detailed characteristics of the included studies—including study design, geographic focus, climate change dimensions addressed, key findings, and reported adaptation or coping strategies—are presented in the supplementary file (Supplementary Table 1).

Synthesis of key findings

Key climate change drivers in the continent

Sub-Saharan Africa faces a diverse range of climate change drivers, including extreme weather events like droughts, floods, heatwaves, and tropical cyclones [40,41]. These climatic stressors have caused significant disruptions, such as widespread drought conditions affecting over 29 million people in Ethiopia, Somalia, and Kenya, and severe flooding incidents like Tropical Cyclone Freddy, which resulted in substantial fatalities in Madagascar, Mozambique, and Zimbabwe [34,35]. Droughts and erratic rainfall patterns have been particularly severe in Ethiopia, exacerbating issues such as land degradation and rural-urban migration [42]. Rising temperatures and water scarcity are additional challenges, contributing to reduced agricultural productivity and food insecurity across the continent. Such extreme climatic conditions also drive forced migration, with notable examples of internal and transboundary movement in Ethiopia and Kenya due to crop failures and conflicts [43]. In recent years, major storms including Cyclone Freddy in 2023, Gombe in 2022 and Idai in 2019 have beaten Malawi, Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Madagascar causing heavy rains and winds that destroyed buildings and plants and caused significant mortality and morbidity [44]. The impact of these extreme weather conditions posed significant challenges, particularly for women and individuals from low socio-economic backgrounds. Many were forced to leave their homes as a coping mechanism, often facing heightened risks of GBV and severe disruptions to access to SRH services. The following sections delve into the critical dimensions of climate change's effects in the region.

The gender dimensions of climate change

Climate change exacerbates pre-existing gender inequalities across Sub-Saharan Africa, disproportionately affecting women and girls. Women, who produce 80 % of food and constitute over 60 % of the agricultural workforce, are particularly vulnerable due to their reliance on natural resources and climate-sensitive sectors for livelihoods [42]. Gendered vulnerabilities are amplified by limited access to resources, income disparities, and the burden of unpaid labor, which significantly reduce women's ability to adapt to climate change [36]. Female-headed households face heightened challenges, such as lack of access to agricultural technology and financial resources, making them more susceptible to climate-induced shocks like droughts and floods [26]. Furthermore, patriarchal structures impede women’s participation in decision-making processes and limit their adaptive capacities, deepening the feminization of poverty [36]. Gender-sensitive policies, including education and climate-smart agricultural initiatives, are critical to addressing these disparities and fostering equitable climate resilience.

Migration dynamics

Climate change is a significant driver of migration in Sub-Saharan Africa, with extreme weather events like droughts, floods, and crop failures forcing both internal and transboundary movements. For example, in Ethiopia and Kenya, climate-induced migration has been fueled by drought and resource scarcity, with rural populations seeking livelihoods in urban areas or migrating abroad [43]. Young people, particularly in harsh climate-insecure regions, show high intentions to migrate as a coping strategy, while those heavily affected by environmental changes often remain immobile due to a lack of resources [33]. Migration patterns are also gendered, with men migrating more frequently than women, leaving female-headed households with limited adaptive capacity [45]. However, migration is not always a viable solution; in some cases, it exacerbates vulnerabilities by straining resources in host regions. Policy recommendations include climate development finance, promoting sustainable livelihoods, and fostering education to support populations in both origin and destination areas, thereby making migration a managed adaptation strategy rather than a forced response [32].

Displacement and migration severely disrupt access to essential SRH services, including antenatal care, contraception, and safe delivery services. This disruption exacerbates challenges such as unplanned pregnancies, unsafe abortions, maternal mortality, and the spread of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [37]. The SRH needs of adolescents, especially girls, are often neglected in humanitarian and climate crisis responses, further compounding their vulnerability.

Additionally, women and girls often bear the brunt of environmental changes due to their gendered roles in natural resource collection, caregiving responsibilities, and limited access to economic and educational opportunities. Their reduced autonomy and minimal representation in disaster response and climate governance processes hinder the development of inclusive and equitable solutions [36]. The effect on climate change on maternal health has been reported as direct and indirect including increase in maternal death rates, with risks like preterm birth, stillbirth, and maternal hypertension exacerbated by climate change [38,46].

The intersectionality of identities including age, disability, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status amplifies the inequities faced by certain subgroups, such as adolescent girls with disabilities or women from pastoralist communities. Despite these risks, SRH is frequently excluded from climate resilience and disaster preparedness strategies, highlighting a major policy and programming gap.

This nexus indicates the urgent need for integrated, rights-based, and gender-transformative policies that simultaneously address the impacts of climate change, promote gender equity, and ensure comprehensive access to SRH services, especially for displaced and mobile populations. There is a call to broaden awareness, international collaboration and increased funding to climate adaptation [47]. Strengthening health systems resilience, investing in mobile and community-based SRH services, and including women and young people in climate adaptation planning are critical for ensuring the dignity, health, and well-being of affected communities.

Interventions and adaptation strategies

Effective intervention strategies are critical for mitigating the multifaceted impacts of climate change on vulnerable populations in Sub-Saharan Africa. Adaptation strategies must be gender-sensitive, emphasizing the inclusion of women in decision-making processes and access to resources. Promoting climate-smart agriculture, water harvesting, and soil conservation practices are vital for enhancing the resilience of rural and farming communities [26]. Community-based interventions, such as economic empowerment programs for women, education on adaptive agricultural practices, and dissemination of gender-sensitive climate information, have proven effective in improving adaptive capacity [30]. Additionally, enhancing access to SRH services, integrating SRH into climate adaptation policies, and addressing GBV are essential for protecting women and girls in climate-affected areas [37]. Policymakers are encouraged to adopt holistic approaches, including the promotion of sustainable livelihoods, capacity building, and innovative technologies, to address both immediate and long-term challenges posed by climate change [32].

Discussion

This scoping review highlights the complex, interlinked impacts of climate change on gender, migration, and SRH in Sub-Saharan Africa. The literature reports climate change disproportionately burdens women, girls, and other marginalized groups who already face structural inequalities [6,7]. These populations are heavily reliant on climate-sensitive sectors such as agriculture and natural resource collection which bear more responsibility to households. In addition, limited access to financial resources [48], education [49], healthcare [50], and political representation [51] further compounds vulnerability to climate changes. Gender-based disparities such as increased exposure to GBV, diminished autonomy, and exclusion from decision-making processes amplify the adverse outcomes associated with climate change, affecting not only economic security but also SRH and personal safety.

The studies explored documented migration may be associated with both a coping mechanism and a source of additional risk. While mobility can offer temporary protection against environmental shocks such as droughts and food insecurity [52], it also introduces new threats, especially for women and adolescents. Displacement often leads to disrupted SRH service access, exposure to unsafe living conditions, and heightened vulnerability to sexual exploitation, early marriage, and transactional sex. Many African migrants, both men and women, have lost their lives while undertaking dangerous cross-border and transcontinental journeys, often facilitated by human smuggling networks [53]. In addition, in humanitarian settings, adolescents, particularly girls, are uniquely at risk due to intersecting age and gender vulnerabilities [54].

Although several regional and continental policy frameworks exist, such as gender, SRHR, and climate-related migration, the literature notes gaps in implementation. Many national responses remain reactive and fragmented, failing to tackle root causes such as entrenched gender inequality, weak health systems, and limited livelihood opportunities. As such, the Sub-Saharan region still faces challenges due to lack of shared responsibilities and inaction of the Paris agreement [55]. Urban planning often lacks a holistic integration of development, migration, and climate adaptation, which undermines long-term resilience and weakens communities' capacity to sustainably adapt to climate-related stressors [56]. Linked to this, the role of media and advocacy efforts on the adaptation strategies of climate crises in the region are also limited [57,58]. Finally, policy gaps and weak integration of gender-sensitive climate adaptation measures remain a challenge, emphasizing the need for holistic and inclusive interventions to address the interlinked effects of climate change, migration, and SRH in Sub-Saharan Africa [33,43].

Despite these challenges, the review identifies promising practices in the form of community-based interventions and gender-sensitive climate adaptation strategies. Initiatives such as climate-smart agriculture, water conservation efforts, and integrated SRH services have demonstrated effectiveness in increasing local resilience and empowering vulnerable populations. However, these initiatives remain underfunded and unevenly scaled [42], [59]. Progress depends on sustained investment, inclusive governance, and multi-sectoral coordination involving local communities, national governments, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and international stakeholders.

A key strength of this review lies in its comprehensive scope, which draws on peer-reviewed studies, gray literature, and policy documents from diverse contexts across Sub-Saharan Africa. The thematic synthesis allows for a better understanding of the intersections among climate change, gender, migration, and SRH. However, the review also has limitations. Given the complexity and multidimensionality of the intersections between climate change, gender, migration, and SRH, this scoping review is limited in its ability to disentangle causal relationships or make definitive conclusions about the strength of association. Although extensive, our search was limited to articles indexed in selected databases, which may have excluded relevant literature published elsewhere. As this was a scoping review, no formal critical appraisal of study quality was conducted, in line with guidance for this review type. While this allowed for the inclusion of a broader range of evidence, it also means that the findings should be interpreted with caution, as the methodological rigor of the included studies may vary. In addition, some regions and population groups remain underrepresented in the current body of evidence, suggesting the need for further research to inform inclusive and context-specific interventions.

Conclusion

This review indicates the urgent need for integrated, multidimensional approaches to better understand and respond to the nexus between climate change, gender, migration, and SRH in Sub-Saharan Africa. The literature reviewed suggests that climate change disproportionately affects women, girls, and marginalized communities by amplifying existing inequalities and creating new vulnerabilities, particularly in the context of poverty. While migration often emerges as a coping strategy, the evidence indicates that it can also intensify risks by disrupting livelihoods, social networks, and access to essential services such as SRH. The studies included in this review point to the potential value of holistic strategies that promote gender equity, strengthen SRH systems, and support sustainable livelihoods. Reported adaptation efforts—such as investments in climate-resilient infrastructure, education, and community-based initiatives—appear critical to enhancing resilience among vulnerable populations. However, significant research gaps remain, particularly in understanding the intersectional impacts of climate change and in evaluating context-specific responses. Overall, the review revealed the importance of inclusive, coordinated, and forward-looking efforts that are responsive to the needs of the most affected populations.
