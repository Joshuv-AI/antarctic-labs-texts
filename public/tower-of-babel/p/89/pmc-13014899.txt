Geometric confound in head circumference percentile change during helmet therapy
Authors: Elias Oulasvirta
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with interest the article by Lindemann et al. reporting a significant decline in head circumference (HC) percentile during helmet molding therapy (HMT) for positional plagiocephaly (Lindemann et al., 2026). The authors observed a median percentile decrease from the 50th to the 25th (p < 0.001), with a z-score decline of 0.185 SD, and raised the possibility of suppressed cranial growth. However, there is a geometric confound that must be accounted for before any biological mechanism is considered.

HC is a perimeter measurement taken in a single axial plane. The isoperimetric inequality is relevant here: among all closed curves enclosing a given area, the circle has the smallest perimeter. A normal infant skull has a roughly elliptical axial cross-section, closer to a circle than the parallelogram-like contour that the authors themselves describe as characteristic of positional plagiocephaly. This means that a deformed cross-section has a greater perimeter, and thus a greater measured HC, than a normally shaped skull enclosing the same cross-sectional area.

Successful HMT restores the cranial cross-section toward a more regular ellipse. This necessarily reduces the perimeter-to-area ratio and thus the measured HC. In other words, a decline in HC percentile is not merely a possible side effect of shape correction but a mathematical certainty.

This is directly testable with the authors' existing data. Their Rodin4D surface scans allow computation of the isoperimetric quotient (Q = 4πA/P2) of each patient's axial cross-section before and after treatment. If the decline in HC percentile correlates with shape improvement (Q approaching 1), the geometric confound is confirmed. If not, a biological mechanism becomes more plausible. Notably, the authors derived ODDI and CPI values from these scans for diagnostic purposes but did not report them or correlate them with HC percentile change. A second test is also available: examining each patient's HC percentile trajectory from birth, through the development of plagiocephaly, and onward through helmet therapy. If the geometric confound is real, percentiles should rise as the deformity develops and fall as it corrects.

I urge the authors to correlate the degree of shape correction with HC percentile change to exclude the possibility that the observed decline is a geometric consequence of successful treatment rather than suppressed cranial growth.
