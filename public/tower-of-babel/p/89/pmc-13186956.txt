Erector spinae muscle characteristics predict 90-day survival in elderly pneumonia patients
Authors: Xiaoxue Wu, Jincheng Ma, Jianmei Huang, Zhendong Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPneumonia in elderly patients represent a critical clinical challenge with substantial mortality risks, where muscle deterioration has emerged as a potential prognostic biomarker. However, the specific morphological characteristics of the erector spinae muscle (ESM) in this vulnerable population and their predictive value for short-term outcomes remain poorly understood. This prospective observational cohort study enrolled 189 elderly patients (age 84.7 ± 4.1 years) diagnosed with pneumonia at Lishui Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital. A pre-specified, retrospective analysis of baseline chest CT images was conducted to quantify erector spinae muscle (ESM) parameters, including cross-sectional area (ESMCSA), thickness (ESMT), fat infiltration rate (FI), and CT attenuation values. Clinical variables, inflammatory markers, and severity scores were prospectively collected. LASSO regression was applied for feature selection, followed by univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses to determine factors associated with 90-day mortality. Among 48 non-survivors (25.4%), the death group exhibited significantly lower ESM CSA (adjusted for body surface area), ESMT, and albumin levels, alongside elevated FI, APACHE II/SOFA scores, CRP, and diabetes prevalence (all P < 0.05). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a 9.6-fold higher mortality risk (P < 0.001) in patients with lower ESMT/BSA. LASSO regression identified CRP, albumin, FI, ESMT, and diabetes as key predictors. Although univariable analysis associated lower ESMCSA/BSA, ESMT/BSA, and elevated FI with mortality (P < 0.05), multivariable Cox regression identified only higher albumin levels (HR = 0.882, 95% CI: 0.827–0.941) and ESMT/BSA (HR = 0.553, 95% CI: 0.449–0.683) as independent protective factors. Elevated CRP (HR = 1.098, 95% CI: 1.051–1.147) and FI (HR = 1.044, 95% CI: 1.006–1.084) independently predicted mortality. In patients with pneumonia, elevated CRP levels and the degree of erector spinae muscle fat infiltration were identified as independent risk factors for mortality, while higher albumin levels and the ratio of erector spinae muscle thickness to body surface area served as independent protective factors.Keywords: Erector spinae muscle, Pneumonia, Muscle wasting, Fat infiltration, MortalitySubject terms: Prognostic markers, Risk factors

Introduction

Pneumonia, a common and prevalent infectious disease affecting the lung parenchyma, exhibits age-dependent increases in both incidence and mortality rates1. The global burden of this condition is underscored by epidemiological data, which reveal that the incidence of pneumonia among individuals aged 65–79 years is approximately 63 per 10,000 population. This rate rises sharply to 164.3 per 10,000 population for those over 80 years of age2. Despite advancements in therapeutic strategies, including antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and supportive care, around 30% of elderly patients still succumb to this condition3. These statistics highlight the urgent need for early risk stratification to optimize clinical management and improve outcomes.

The aging process is associated with various physiological changes that increase susceptibility to pneumonia. Among these, muscle wasting stands out as a significant contributor. Muscle wasting is an age-related progressive skeletal muscle disorder characterized by diminished muscle mass and functional impairment4. However, it is increasingly recognized that beyond mere muscle mass, “muscle quality” plays a critical role in determining function and health outcomes. A key aspect of deteriorating muscle quality is the pathological infiltration of adipose tissue into the skeletal muscle, a condition known as myosteatosis or fat infiltration (FI). Emerging evidence has demonstrated the prognostic significance of muscle wasting in pulmonary infection outcomes. The mechanisms linking muscle wasting to worse clinical outcomes in pneumonia are multifaceted. First, muscle wasting impairs respiratory muscle function, reducing the ability of the diaphragm and accessory muscles to maintain adequate ventilation during acute illness. Muscle depletion affects swallowing mechanics and cough efficacy, increasing the risk of aspiration pneumonia and ventilator-associated lower respiratory infection5–8. Third, muscle wasting is associated with systemic inflammation, which may exacerbate pulmonary infection through the dysregulation of innate immune responses. Collectively, these findings suggest that muscle wasting-related parameters could serve as valuable biomarkers for both therapeutic decision-making and outcome prediction in elderly patients with pneumonia9,10.

The erector spinae muscle group plays a crucial role in respiratory mechanics due to its function in stabilizing the spine and contributing to diaphragmatic excursion during breathing. Additionally, this muscle group is accessible for assessment through imaging or physical examination, making it an attractive target for research in elderly patients with pneumonia11,12. Given the potential link between muscle wasting-related muscle loss and poor outcomes in this patient population, investigating the characteristics of the erector spinae could provide novel insights into the pathophysiology of age-dependent respiratory compromise.

The rationale for focusing on the erector spinae in this study is further supported by its unique physiological and clinical significance. While previous studies have explored muscle wasting in the context of pneumonia, there remains a gap in understanding the specific contributions of regional muscle groups like the erector spinae to respiratory function and mortality risk13.

Given that muscle wasting affects various muscle groups differentially, it is possible that the erector spinae may exhibit distinct patterns of atrophy and functional impairment in elderly patients with pneumonia. Such findings could help refine our understanding of the mechanisms underlying muscle wasting-related morbidity and mortality. In this study, we aim to investigate the relationship between erector spinae muscle measurements and short-term mortality risk in elderly patients with pneumonia. By employing advanced imaging techniques or physical assessments to evaluate erector spinae muscle characteristics, this study seeks to identify biomarkers that could improve clinical risk assessment protocols. Furthermore, these findings may guide the development of targeted interventions aimed at mitigating muscle wasting-related complications in this vulnerable population.

Patients and methods

Methodology

This study enrolled elderly patients with pneumonia treated at Lishui Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital between June 2022 and October 2024. Patients were divided into survival and mortality groups based on their 90-day clinical outcomes. Clinical data and parameters related to erector spinae muscle characteristics were collected, followed by Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to identify factors influencing the prognosis of elderly patients with pneumonia. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the hospital, and all patients and their families provided written informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged 65 years or older were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Presence of pulmonary infiltrates on chest imaging (X-ray, ultrasound, or CT) within 48 h prior to admission, and (2) Concurrent manifestation of at least one clinical or laboratory indicator of pulmonary infection13. Specifically, eligible patients were required to present with either (a) new or progressive cough (productive or non-productive), (b) fever (> 37.8 °C) or hypothermia (< 36 °C) measured orally/rectally, and/or (c) systemic inflammatory markers including leukocytosis (> 10 × 109/L or < 4 × 109/L), elevated C-reactive protein, or increased serum interleukin-6 levels. Exclusion encompassed patients who: (1) Did not undergo chest CT within 24 h post-admission; (2) Had pre-existing or acute neuromuscular disorders; (3) Carried a history of spinal surgery or vertebral fractures; (4) Exhibited immunocompromised states (hematologic malignancies, HIV/AIDS, neutropenia, hepatic insufficiency, or immune reconstitution disorders); (5) Were diagnosed with active lung/solid tumors; (6) Received immunosuppressants or chemotherapy within 3 months; (7) Demonstrated severe dysfunction of major organ systems (cardiac, hepatic, or renal); (8) Had a history of long-term immobility or bedridden status (e.g., due to stroke sequelae or advanced frailty) for more than 3 months prior to admission; (9) History of long-term or high-dose systemic glucocorticoid use within the past 3 months; (10) Diagnosed with COVID-19 (confirmed by PCR or antigen test).

Baseline characteristics and outcomes

Baseline data of patients were systematically recorded, encompassing general clinical variables, disease severity scores, interventions, infection categories, comorbidities, radiological findings, and laboratory parameters.

Patient outcomes were evaluated at 90 days post-admission through structured follow-up, including telephone interviews, electronic medical record reviews, and, when necessary, contact with family members or primary care physicians. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, categorized as either death or survival. Survival status was confirmed through hospital records or national death registries.

Features of erector spinae muscle

Image analysis

Quantitative assessment of erector spinae muscle (ESM) parameters was performed on non-contrast chest CT images at the mid-T12 vertebral level. Bilateral ROIs were manually delineated to encompass the spinalis, longissimus, and iliocostalis components using standardized boundaries: anterior limit at the posterior cortical margin of transverse processes/laminae, posterior limit at the deep thoracolumbar fascia, medial border ∼1 cm lateral to spinous processes (excluding multifidus), and lateral border at the fat plane separating iliocostalis from quadratus lumborum and latissimus dorsi. Adjacent structures (bony elements, neurovascular bundles, psoas major, subcutaneous fat) were systematically excluded. Semi-automated segmentation was subsequently performed using SliceOmatic V4.3 (Tomovision, Canada) with Hounsfield unit thresholds: -29 to 150 HU for muscle tissue and − 190 to -30 HU for adipose tissue. This generated three primary metrics: (1) ESM cross-sectional area (ESMCSA, mm2), (2) Muscle thickness (ESMT, mm), and (3) Fat infiltration (FI, %). Values were normalized to body surface area (ESMCSA/BSA and ESMT/BSA) using the DuBois formula.

Quality control

Two musculoskeletal radiologists (> 5 years experience) completed protocol training with 20 reference cases prior to measurements. All analyses were performed blinded to clinical outcomes. Interobserver reliability was assessed in 30 randomly selected patients (15% cohort), demonstrating excellent agreement for ESMCSA (ICC = 0.920, 95% CI: 0.873–0.956) and ESMT (ICC = 0.941, 95% CI: 0.911–0.986), with good agreement for FI (ICC = 0.893, 95% CI: 0.855–0.937). Discrepancies exceeding 5% in CSA or 7 HU in density triggered consensus review with a senior radiologist.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using R software (version 4.1.2; packages: ggplot2, survival and glmnet). Continuous variables underwent Shapiro-Wilk testing for normality assessment. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. For non-normal distributions or heteroscedasticity, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied with median and interquartile range reported. Categorical variables were described as frequency (%) and analyzed via χ2 tests for group comparisons.

Survival analysis was performed to estimate 90-day mortality among patients stratified by ESM characteristics, using Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Log-rank test for between-group differences) with median survival time and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. To identify potential predictors of 90-day outcomes from all candidate variables (including demographic characteristics, clinical scores, laboratory parameters, and muscle measurements), variable selection was first conducted via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. All candidate variables were entered simultaneously into the LASSO model, with the penalization parameter λ determined using 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal model that minimized prediction error. Variables with non-zero coefficients in the LASSO model (i.e., those retained by the algorithm) were then entered into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% CIs. This data-driven approach automatically shrinks coefficients of less influential predictors to zero, resulting in a parsimonious model without manually forcing any specific variables. To assess potential multicollinearity among the variables included in the final multivariable model, we calculated variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF > 10 considered indicative of problematic collinearity. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 281 elderly patients diagnosed with pneumonia were initially enrolled. After exclusions, 189 patients were included in the final cohort. All participants underwent a 90-day follow-up, during which 48 cases of all-cause mortality were recorded, including 45 deaths attributed to pulmonary causes, 2 to heart failure, and 1 to acute myocardial infarction. Consequently, 141 patients survived the follow-up period (Flowchart is detailed in Fig. 1).

Comparative analysis of ESM characteristics and survival outcomes

Comparative analysis of ESM characteristics between the survival and death groups revealed distinct patterns. The mean CT values of ESM showed no statistically significant difference between groups (51.03 ± 35.12 HU vs. 42.08 ± 28.09 HU, survival vs. death groups, respectively). However, the death group exhibited significantly higher fat infiltration index (FI) compared to the survival group (P < 0.05). Conversely, ESM cross-sectional area (ESM CSA), ESM Thickness (ESMT), and their body surface area-adjusted counterparts (ESM CSA/BSA and ESMT/BSA) were markedly lower in the death group relative to survivors (all P < 0.05). Variable selection via LASSO regression

LASSO regression analysis identified five variables significantly associated with mortality risk in patients with pulmonary infection: C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin (Alb), fat infiltration (FI), ESM Thickness (ESMT), and ESMT/BSA. These variables were retained in the final model following penalized coefficient shrinkage (Figs. 4 and 5).

Univariable and multivariable cox regression analyses

Univariable Cox regression revealed that elevated C-reactive protein (CRP; HR = 1.182, 95% CI: 1.131–1.236) and fat infiltration index (FI; HR = 1.084, 95% CI: 1.040–1.131) were significant risk factors for mortality, corresponding to a 18.2% increase in risk per unit increase in CRP or FI. Conversely, higher serum albumin (Alb; HR = 0.804, 95% CI: 0.760–0.852), increased erector spinae muscle thickness (ESMT; HR = 0.712, 95% CI: 0.646–0.785) and ESMT/BSA ratio (HR = 0.556, 95% CI: 0.471–0.656) demonstrated protective effects, with Alb reducing mortality by ~ 20% per unit increase and ESMT/BSA lowering risk by ~ 44.4% per unit increase.

However, in multivariable analyses, the inclusion of both ESMT and ESMT/BSA raised concerns regarding multicollinearity. Variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis revealed high collinearity between these two variables (VIF for ESMT: 5.910; VIF for ESMT/BSA: 5.780), indicating potential redundancy in the model. To address this, we performed an analysis to evaluate the impact of removing ESMT from the final multivariable model. The inclusion of ESMT/BSA alone remained significantly associated with mortality (HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.07–1.62, P = 0.013). Given the high correlation between these variables and their distinct clinical relevance—ESMT/BSA reflects muscle thickness normalized to body surface area, which may better account for variations in patient size compared to ESMT—we opted to retain ESMT/BSA as the primary indicator of ESM’s thickness.

The final multivariable Cox regression model, adjusted for potential confounding variables, included four independent predictors: CRP, Alb, FI, and ESMT/BSA. The model achieved an events per variable ratio (EPV) of 12, meeting the recommended minimum threshold for robust statistical inference. Bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) confirmed the stability of this model, with bias-corrected hazard ratios (HRs) consistently replicating across all iterations.

In this adjusted model (Concordance = 0.901, 95%CI: 0.872–0.930; Schoenfeld residuals test χ²=0.340, P = 0.560), CRP and FI emerged as independent risk factors for 90-day mortality, with each unit increase associated with a ~ 9.2% (HR = 1.098, 95% CI: 1.051–1.147) and ~ 4.4% (HR = 1.044, 95% CI: 1.006–1.084) increased risk, respectively. Conversely, albumin and ESMT/BSA demonstrated protective effects, with each unit increase linked to a ~ 11.8% (HR = 0.882, 95% CI: 0.827–0.941) and ~ 44.7% (HR = 0.553, 95% CI: 0.449–0.683) lower risk of mortality (P < 0.05 for all variables). These findings highlight the independent prognostic value of skeletal muscle wasting biomarkers in predicting outcomes among elderly pneumonia patients (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

To further evaluate the robustness of the identified prognostic factors, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially expanding the multivariable Cox model. Model 1 included only the original four predictors: C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin (Alb), fat infiltration (FI), and erector spinae muscle thickness to body surface area ratio (ESMT/BSA). Subsequent models (Model 2 and Model 3) incorporated additional clinical severity indices, namely APACHE-II score and SOFA score, respectively.

The results of the sensitivity analyses revealed that CRP, Alb, FI, and ESMT/BSA remained statistically significant predictors of 90-day mortality across all models. In Model 1, the hazard ratios (HRs) for these variables were consistent with those in the primary multivariable model. When APACHE-II was added in Model 2, the significance of these predictors was preserved, with no substantial changes in HRs or confidence intervals (Table 3). Similarly, the inclusion of SOFA score in Model 3 did not alter the independent prognostic value of CRP, Alb, FI, and ESMT/BSA, although the overall model performance showed slight variations in goodness-of-fit metrics (p for Schoenfeld residuals: p = 0.21 in Model 1 vs. p = 0.35 in Model 3).

Discussion

Pulmonary infection, an acute infectious condition affecting lung parenchyma, remains a leading cause of mortality in geriatric populations14,15. Emerging evidence indicates that elderly patients with pneumonia exhibit heightened susceptibility to skeletal muscle loss and secondary muscle wasting, factors likely contributing to adverse clinical outcomes16,17. While body mass index (BMI) has been conventionally employed as a diagnostic criterion for muscle wasting, its inherent limitations—stemming from the inclusion of visceral organ mass, extracellular fluid, and adipose tissue—significantly undermine its reliability in assessing lean muscle mass18,19. Recent advancements in computed tomography (CT) quantification have enabled precise measurement of total and regional skeletal muscle cross-sectional areas, offering an objective method for evaluating physical functional capacity20,21.

Our findings reveal that elevated CRP levels and ESM fat infiltration independently predict mortality risk in pulmonary infection patients, whereas serum albumin and the erector spinae muscle thickness-to-body surface area ratio (ESMT/BSA) demonstrate protective effects. Intriguingly, neither the mean CT attenuation nor the cross-sectional area (CSA) of ESM exhibited independent prognostic significance. These observations underscore the complex interplay between muscle morphology, metabolic adaptation, and systemic inflammation in determining critical illness outcomes.

The enhanced prognostic value of ESMT/BSA compared to raw ESMT measurements lies in its ability to standardize muscle thickness according to metabolic demands. Calculated using the DuBois formula (BSA = 0.007184 × weight0.425 × height0.725), BSA nonlinearly correlates with energy expenditure and serves as a physiological reference for scaling tissue mass22,23. Analogous to left ventricular mass index standardization in cardiology24,25, ESMT/BSA quantifies muscle reserve adequacy relative to systemic requirements. This equilibrium may critically influence outcomes during infection-induced hypercatabolism, where inadequate muscle mass exacerbates energy depletion and organ dysfunction.

While ESMT and CSA showed linear correlation (CSA = 60 × ESMT − 290), only ESMT emerged as an independent protective factor. This apparent paradox may originate from anatomical and methodological differences. Functionally, ESMT directly reflects the contractile capacity of deep paraspinal muscles, whereas CSA measurements are susceptible to confounding by intermuscular fat or fascicle orientation26. Clinically, ESMT demonstrates superior robustness by circumventing technical variability inherent in CSA quantification, such as segmentation errors or plane selection biases27. Future studies incorporating fat fraction analysis could help reconcile these morpho-functional discrepancies.

The clinical practicality of ESMT over CSA is further supported by its technical accessibility. As demonstrated by Shimoda et al.28, ultrasound-based ESMT measurement requires only single-plane visualization at standardized vertebral levels (typically L3/L4), contrasting with CSA quantification that necessitates meticulous cross-sectional tracing of multifidus and longissimus muscles across multiple fascial planes. This methodological simplicity proves particularly advantageous in resource-limited settings or serial monitoring scenarios requiring rapid assessment. Comparative studies report inter-rater variability of < 8% for ESMT versus 15–22% for CSA segmentation among trained operators29.

The independent protective roles of albumin and ESMT/BSA suggest synergistic yet distinct biological pathways. Albumin, a negative acute-phase reactant, serves as a dual marker of nutritional status and systemic inflammation30. Its depletion exacerbates capillary leakage, disrupts drug pharmacokinetics, and induces immunosuppression, perpetuating organ dysfunction31,32. Conversely, ESMT/BSA embodies metabolic-muscular coupling, where preserved muscle reserves may mitigate infection-related catabolism through amino acid mobilization, respiratory muscle maintenance, and myokine-mediated immunomodulation (e.g., IL-6’s tissue-repair functions)33. This duality aligns with the emerging “nutrition-immune-metabolic integration” (NIMI) paradigm advocating multifactorial interventions in critical care34.

The observed discordance between ESM fat infiltration severity and mean CT attenuation may arise from methodological, technical, and pathophysiological factors. First, assessment criteria differences exist: semi-quantitative grading systems emphasize focal adipose deposition, while mean CT values average signals across the entire muscle region of interest (ROI)35. Spatial fat heterogeneity—where severe focal infiltration coexists with preserved muscle—could reduce overall attenuation, masking group-level differences36. Similar phenomena occur in rotator cuff muscles, where focal fatty replacement minimally affects global CT metrics despite clinical significance37. Second, CT attenuation reflects composite signals from intramuscular components including collagen, fibrosis, and edema. Chronic fat infiltration often accompanies fibrotic remodeling (60–90 HU), which may counterbalance adipose tissue’s attenuation-lowering effects (<-30 HU)38. Third, technical limitations such as partial volume effects at clinical CT resolutions (0.5–1 mm) may obscure microscopic lipid droplets characteristic of early fat infiltration. Manual ROI placement often excludes overt fatty regions to avoid artifacts, systematically underestimating true fat content—a challenge paralleled in hepatic steatosis quantification where MRI outperforms CT in detecting early lipid accumulation39.

In this study, we proposed the ESMT/BSA ratio as a novel index. To our knowledge, the application of this specific composite index in predicting outcomes for elderly pneumonia patients has not been previously reported. The rationale for using BSA for indexing, rather than alternatives such as height squared or body weight alone, is based on physiological considerations. In critically ill patients with pneumonia, body weight can be highly unstable due to fluid resuscitation, edema, or cachexia, potentially confounding the interpretation of muscle measurements. BSA, calculated from both height and weight, provides a more stable estimate of the body’s metabolic demand and systemic inflammatory burden. By indexing ESMT to BSA, we aimed to better reflect the balance between skeletal muscle reserve (energy store) and the physiological stress imposed by the infection (metabolic demand), thereby offering a more accurate prognostic assessment than using muscle thickness alone.

Limitations and future directions

While this study elucidates prognostic muscle-immune-nutritional interactions, several limitations warrant consideration. First, single-slice CT measurements may inadequately represent three-dimensional muscle architecture; volumetric analysis or dual-energy CT could improve precision⁴⁰. Second, the observational design precludes causal inferences between muscle depletion and inflammation—longitudinal studies with repeated measurements tracking dynamic changes are needed. Third, mechanistic validation requires integration with functional assessments (e.g., grip strength, respiratory mechanics) and molecular profiling (e.g., myostatin, interleukin networks). Fourth, the study cohort comprised very elderly patients (mean age 85 years) from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to younger elderly populations (e.g., 65–75 years) and to other ethnic or regional groups with different body composition and muscle quality characteristics. These insights could guide personalized rehabilitation-nutrition protocols to reduce mortality risks in pneumonia. Although three deaths were from cardiovascular causes, the number was too small to perform formal competing-risk analysis, and their impact on the prognostic findings is likely negligible.

Conclusion

In elderly patients with pneumonia, elevated CRP levels and erector spinae muscle fat infiltration independently predict mortality risk, while higher serum albumin levels and ESMT/BSA ratio confer protection. Notably, neither mean CT attenuation nor erector spinae muscle cross-sectional area demonstrated independent prognostic significance in multivariate analysis. These findings underscore the critical roles of systemic inflammation, nutritional status, and muscle quality in determining clinical outcomes.
