Association Between Delayed Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery Completion and Postoperative Cardiometabolic Outcomes
Authors: Sophia Ngenge, Folefac Atem, Jackson M Francis, Luyu Xie, M Sunil Mathew, Jaime P Almandoz, Elisa Marroquin, Carrie J McAdams, Jeffrey N Schellinger, Sachin Kukreja, Benjamin E Schneider, Sarah E Messiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelays in completion of metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) remain common and may adversely affect postoperative cardiometabolic recovery. This study examined whether delayed MBS completion was associated with differences in postoperative cardiometabolic outcomes over 24 months.MethodsThis prospective longitudinal cohort study included 413 adults referred to MBS from academic and community-based programs in Texas. Of these, 172 participants completed MBS within the study period and comprised the analytic sample for the present analysis. Repeated measures of weight and cardiometabolic biomarkers were collected before MBS and 6, 12- and 24-months post-MBS. Generalized estimating equations were used to evaluate associations between cardiometabolic biomarkers (anthropometrics, HbA1c, lipids), timing of MBS completion (early completion: ≤3 months after referral; delayed completion: >3 to 15 months after referral), adjusting for covariates.ResultsAmong 172 adults who completed MBS, 66 completed surgery within 3 months of referral, and 106 completed surgery > 3 to 15 months post-referral. Among early completers, the median time from referral to surgery was 2.0 months (IQR 0.7–4.3), compared with 5.6 months (IQR 3.2–9.6) among delayed completers. Participants identified as 39.2% Non-Hispanic White, 39.0% Non-Hispanic Black, and 17.6% Hispanic. After multivariable adjustment, delayed MBS completers had higher total cholesterol (β = 14.52, 95% CI = 1.70 to 27.33, p = 0.026), higher non-HDL cholesterol (β = 16.96, 95% CI = 5.31 to 28.61, p = 0.004), higher cholesterol/HDL ratio (β = 0.43, 95% CI = 0.06 to 0.81, p = 0.024), and higher hemoglobin A1c levels (β = 0.36, 95% CI = 0.07 to 0.65, p = 0.016) compared to early completers post-MBS. Triglyceride levels were reduced significantly at 6 months (p < 0.001), 12 months (p = 0.002), and 24 months (p < 0.001). HDL levels increased significantly at 24 months (p < 0.001), whereas non-HDL cholesterol levels (p = 0.014) and cholesterol/HDL ratio (p = 0.001) reduced significantly at 24 months only. Weight, glucose, and HbA1c levels also decreased significantly over 24 months post-MBS (p < 0.05). A significant sex-specific effect was observed with women demonstrating higher levels of total cholesterol (p < 0.001), HDL (p < 0.001), LDL (p = 0.001), and non-HDL cholesterol (p = 0.001) compared to men.ConclusionDelayed completion of MBS was associated with less favorable postoperative cardiometabolic profiles, including higher total cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, cholesterol/HDL ratio, and HbA1c levels over 24 months following surgery. These findings support efforts to reduce delays in access to MBS and optimize timely surgical care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11695-026-08862-4.

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease that affects over 40% of adults in the United States and is strongly associated with cardiometabolic conditions, including type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [1–6]. Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) is the most effective long-term treatment for severe obesity and has been shown to produce sustained weight loss and substantial improvements in obesity-related medical problems [7–9]. Despite the benefits, many patients who are referred for MBS experience prolonged delays or fail to complete surgery altogether, limiting timely access to an effective intervention.

Prior research on MBS has largely focused on postoperative outcomes including weight loss, metabolic improvements, and long-term morbidity and mortality [10–14]. While this work has been critical in establishing the effectiveness of surgery, far less attention has been given to the care process leading up to surgery and the potential consequences of delayed surgical completion after referral. Delays in surgical completion may reflect a combination of patient-level clinical complexity, preoperative optimization requirements, healthcare system barriers, and differential engagement with care, yet these mechanisms remain poorly understood [15].

Although cardiometabolic disease burden may contribute to surgical complexity or preoperative optimization requirements, prolonged delays in accessing MBS may themselves adversely influence cardiometabolic recovery following surgery. Delays in treatment may prolong exposure to severe obesity and associated metabolic dysfunction, potentially limiting the degree of postoperative improvement in glycemic and lipid outcomes. However, few studies have evaluated whether delayed MBS completion is associated with less favorable postoperative metabolic trajectories, particularly in racially and ethnically diverse populations [16–19].

Understanding the impact of delayed MBS completion on postoperative cardiometabolic outcomes has important implications for improving access to care and reducing preventable delays. Identifying barriers associated with delayed completion may help streamline surgical pathways and promote more equitable delivery of bariatric care. Moreover, examining whether these associations differ by sex or race and ethnicity may help elucidate disparities in the surgical care continuum.

Accordingly, the objective of this study was to evaluate whether delayed completion of MBS following referral was associated with differences in longitudinal postoperative cardiometabolic outcomes over 24 months. We compared postoperative cardiometabolic trajectories between individuals who completed MBS early after referral and those who experienced delayed completion and evaluated whether associations varied by sex, race, ethnicity, or study time point. We hypothesized that delayed completion of MBS would be associated with less favorable postoperative glycemic and lipid outcomes over follow-up.

Methods

Study Procedures

The Bariatric Health Study is a prospective longitudinal cohort study of adults referred to MBS. Participants were recruited between August 2019 and October 2022 from academic MBS clinics and a community-based obesity management program in Texas, including UT Southwestern Medical Center–affiliated programs and partner surgical practices. Additional participants were recruited via social media during periods of COVID-19–related clinical disruption. Eligible participants aged ≥ 18 years, had been referred for MBS by a qualified medical provider, and provided informed consent. The study was approved by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston human subjects review board (IRB # HSC-SPH-18-0850).

Exposure: Timing of MBS Completion

The primary exposure was the timing of MBS completion, classified as early or delayed. Early completion was defined as undergoing MBS within 3 months of referral following attendance at an MBS educational seminar (attendance required to book surgery). Delayed completion was defined as undergoing MBS > 3 to 15 months after referral and educational seminar attendance. Time from referral to surgery was calculated using the study-defined referral/assessment date and confirmed surgery date. Median and interquartile ranges were calculated to describe the distribution of referral-to-surgery time within exposure groups.

Timing of surgery completion was self-reported and verified through electronic health or medical record review and confirmation by the clinical obesity medicine team. Exposure status was treated as a fixed, participant-level variable.

Outcomes: Cardiometabolic Biomarkers

The primary outcomes consisted of longitudinally measured cardiometabolic biomarkers collected at baseline (pre-MBS) and at approximately 6-, 12-, and 24-months post-MBS resulting in repeated measures over time. Outcomes included weight, fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides, non-HDL cholesterol, and the cholesterol-to-HDL ratio. Biomarker data were obtained from clinical laboratory records and supplemented by medical record abstraction. All outcomes were analyzed as continuous variables.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature and included age, sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, other), and body mass index (BMI) category (< 30, 30–34.9, 35–39.9, ≥ 40 kg/m²) [15–18]. Study time points (pre-MBS, 6, 12, and 24 months) were included to account for repeated measurements over follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics by timing of MBS completion. Means and standard deviations were reported for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. To evaluate whether cardiometabolic biomarker trajectories differed by timing of MBS completion, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an identity link and Gaussian distribution were used for continuous outcomes. Models included timing of MBS completion (early vs. delayed), study time point, and their interaction to assess whether biomarker levels differed over time by completion timing. An independent correlation structure was specified to account for correlation across repeated measures. All models were adjusted for age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI category, and study time point. Sex was analyzed as a binary variable (male or female with male as the reference variable); Age was analyzed as a categorical variable by grouping the age values into groups by years [18–29, 30–39], (40–49), (50–59), and (> 60). Study time point was analyzed as a categorical variable (pre-MBS as reference, 6, 12, and 24 months), and BMI was analyzed as a categorical variable grouped as (< 30), (30-34.9), (35-39.9), and (> 40). Race/ethnicity was categorized as non-Hispanic White (NHW; as reference), non-Hispanic Black (NHB), Hispanic, and others). Adjusted mean differences and 95% confidence intervals were estimated. Robust (Huber–White) standard errors were used to ensure valid inference under potential misspecification of the correlation structure. Model fit was evaluated using the quasi-likelihood under the independence model criterion (QIC).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using generalized linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts to assess the robustness of findings to alternative modeling approaches for correlated longitudinal data. Results were consistent with those obtained from GEE models and are presented in the Supplemental Tables.

Missing data for demographic variables were minimal (< 3%). Analyses were conducted using available data under the assumption of missing-at-random. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.5 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

A total of 413 adults were enrolled in the parent cohort study. Among these, 172 participants completed MBS during the study period and were included in the present longitudinal analysis, including 66 early completers and 106 delayed completers (Table 1). Participants were 39.2% NHW, 39.0% NHB, 17.6% Hispanic, and 4.2% other race/ethnicity. Mean age was (47.6 years). Median time from referral to surgery was 2.0 months (IQR 0.7–4.3) among early completers and 5.6 months (IQR 3.2–9.6) among delayed completers. Procedure type information was available for 150 of the 172 participants who completed MBS, and was unavailable or unknown for 22 participants. Among participants with available procedure data, 89 underwent sleeve gastrectomy, 50 underwent Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, 9 underwent duodenal switch, 1 underwent adjustable gastric banding, and 1 underwent SADI-S. The remaining participants had not yet undergone surgery or decided not to pursue surgery during follow-up. Follow-up sample sizes were 169 at 6 months, 154 at 12 months, and 137 at 24 months postoperatively (Fig. 1). Baseline cardiometabolic characteristics were generally similar between surgical completers and non-completers (Supplemental Table 1). The majority of participants were female (> 85%). Cardiometabolic risk factors were common at baseline, including elevated glucose, HbA1c and dyslipidemia (Table 1). Before MBS, mean BMI was 45.5 kg.m2, weight (278.0 lbs.), glucose (108.2 mg/dL), total cholesterol (174.6 mg/dL), HbA1c (6.2%), triglycerides (150.3 mg/dL), LDL cholesterol (97.1 mg/dL), HDL cholesterol (49.9 mg/dL), non-HDL cholesterol (125.4 mg/dL), cholesterol/HDL ratio (3.7), systolic blood pressure (130.1 mm/Hg), and diastolic blood pressure (77.8 mm/Hg) (Table 1). Importantly, there were no statistically significant baseline differences in cardiometabolic characteristics between early and delayed completers prior to surgery, suggesting that postoperative differences observed over follow-up were unlikely to reflect substantial preoperative metabolic differences between groups.

Several cardiometabolic markers were associated with delayed MBS completion after multivariable adjustment. Delayed completers had higher total cholesterol (β = 14.52, 95% CI = 1.70 to 27.33, p = 0.026), higher non-HDL cholesterol (β = 16.96, 95% CI = 5.31 to 28.61, p = 0.004), and higher cholesterol/HDL ratio (β = 0.43, 95% CI = 0.06 to 0.81, p = 0.024) (Table 4) than early completers. HDL levels increased significantly at 24 months (β = 8.61, 95% CI = 3.74 to 13.47, p < 0.001), whereas non-HDL cholesterol levels (β=-16.71, 95% CI=-29.99 to -3.44, p = 0.014) and the cholesterol/HDL ratio (β=-0.70, 95% CI=-1.11 to -0.30, p = 0.001) decreased significantly at 24 months only (Table 4).

Higher HbA1c levels were associated with delayed MBS completion (β = 0.36, 95% CI = 0.07 to 0.65, p = 0.016) and significant reduction of HbA1c levels were observed at 6 months (β=-0.80, 95% CI=-1.15 to -0.45, p < 0.001) 12 months (β=-0.84, 95% CI=-1.21 to -0.48, p < 0.001) and 24 months (β=-0.87, 95% CI=-1.28 to -0.46, p < 0.001) post-MBS (Table 5). Glucose levels also reduced significantly at 6 months (β=-13.81, 95% CI=-19.43 to -8.19, p < 0.001), 12 months (β=-14.54, 95% CI=-21.80 to -7.27, p < 0.001) and 24 months (β=-12.44, 95% CI=-21.28 to -3.61, p = 0.006) post-MBS. Also, compared to NHW participants, participants in the other race/ethnicity category had significantly lower total cholesterol levels (β=-45.07, 95% CI=-52.34 to -16.29, p < 0.001) and significantly lower HDL levels (β=-17.44, 95% CI=-22.80 to -12.08, p < 0.001) (Table 4). No significant associations with MBS completion timing were observed for triglycerides, HDL, LDL, or glucose levels after multivariable adjustment of age, sex, BMI and, race/ethnicity (Tables 3, 4 and 5). Generalized linear mixed model results also revealed similar results, which reflect the robustness of the approaches used in this analysis (Supplement Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Discussion

This study examined whether delayed completion of MBS was associated with differences in postoperative cardiometabolic outcomes over 24 months. Importantly, baseline cardiometabolic profiles did not significantly differ between early and delayed completers prior to surgery. However, participants who experienced delayed MBS completion demonstrated less favorable postoperative lipid and glycemic profiles during follow-up. Specifically, after adjustment for demographic and clinical factors, delayed completion was associated with higher levels of total cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, cholesterol/HDL ratio, and HbA1c, whereas weight, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and glucose were not associated with completion timing. A sex-specific association was observed for weight: women weighed less and had higher levels of cholesterol, HDL, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol. Additionally, there was significant weight, triglycerides, glucose, and HbA1c reduction across all study time points post MBS. This was accompanied by significantly increased HDL levels and significantly reduced non-HDL cholesterol and cholesterol/HDL ratio at 24 months post-MBS.

The associations observed between increased total cholesterol and cholesterol/HDL ratio with delayed MBS completion, may reflect increased medical complexity among patients with adverse lipid profiles. Individuals seeking obesity treatment frequently present with multiple obesity-related comorbidities, including dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risk factors, which often require optimization prior to surgery [19]. Elevated total cholesterol may be more difficult to improve through lifestyle modification alone, particularly when preoperative weight loss is required. This difficulty potentially prolongs the preoperative process and may delay surgical scheduling. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating high baseline cholesterol levels among patients undergoing bariatric surgery and improvements in lipid profiles following surgical intervention [20, 21].

High levels of non-HDL cholesterol were significantly associated with delayed MBS completion, independent of other lipid measures. Non-HDL cholesterol captures the total burden of atherogenic lipoproteins and has been shown to be a robust predictor of cardiovascular disease risk [22–24]. The findings instead suggest that delays in accessing MBS may be associated with reduced cardiometabolic recovery following surgery. Although limited literature has examined non-HDL cholesterol in relation to MBS completion, its association with dyslipidemia and cardiometabolic risk supports its relevance as a potential barrier to timely surgery [24].

Notably, a significant sex-specific effect was observed, with women having elevated total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol compared to men. This finding aligns with other research that shows elevated total cholesterol and LDL may be related to premenopausal or menopausal transition changes that may affect lipid metabolism, driven by the higher mean age of the women in this cohort [25–27]. This study also found that women had elevated HDL compared to men, which is supported by previous studies, and may be explained by the less atherogenic profile of women [26, 28].

In this study, several cardiometabolic factors improved post-MBS, including weight reduction and significant improvement of lipid profiles and indicators that included glucose and HbA1c levels. The improvement of these indicators may be explained by MBS completion, and the completion of all recommended follow-ups with medical providers post-MBS. These results are similar to previous studies that show MBS procedures result in significant weight loss and favorable improvement of cardiometabolic factors associated with obesity [10, 16, 29–32].

Elevated HbA1c was also associated with delayed MBS completion, consistent with the increased clinical complexity of patients with impaired glycemic control or type 2 diabetes [33–35]. Poor glycemic control may necessitate additional medical optimization, medication adjustments, and monitoring to reduce perioperative risk, thereby delaying surgical completion.

In contrast, weight and several commonly used lipid measures, including triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, and HDL cholesterol, were not associated with MBS completion timing after multivariable adjustment. Although prior studies have linked low HDL cholesterol, elevated LDL cholesterol, and unfavorable cholesterol ratios with obesity-related risk [36–38], these measures did not independently predict surgical delay in this cohort. This suggests that broader cardiometabolic burden, rather than individual lipid components or body size alone, may be more influential in determining the timing of MBS completion. Additionally, other racial groups in this sample, consisting of Asians, American Indians, and Native Hawaiians, showed a significant reduction of total cholesterol and HDL. However, this result should be interpreted carefully, as the current literature has found mixed results regarding this, as one study showed African Americans achieved better lipid profiles post-MBS [39], while another showed decreased total cholesterol in Asians only [40]. More research is needed to clarify the mechanism of racial differences in lipid profiles post-MBS.

The practical implications of these findings primarily relate to healthcare systems and access to care rather than individualized patient decision-making. Delays in MBS completion may arise from insurance authorization requirements, limited surgical capacity, prolonged preoperative pathways, financial barriers, or disruptions in continuity of care [15]. Because delayed completion was associated with less favorable postoperative metabolic outcomes, healthcare systems should prioritize strategies that reduce avoidable delays, including streamlined preoperative evaluation pathways, improved care coordination, expanded bariatric program capacity, and earlier referral for surgical assessment. Specific reasons for surgical delay were not systematically collected in this study and likely reflect a combination of patient-, provider-, and health-system-level factors. Future studies should directly evaluate modifiable barriers contributing to delayed MBS completion.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include its prospective design, racially and ethnically diverse cohort, and repeated assessment of cardiometabolic biomarkers over a 24-month follow-up period where lab results were obtained and verified from the electronic health record or provided by participant. However, several limitations should also be considered. MBS completion was self-reported, although validated through clinical records. Loss to follow-up occurred over time, particularly at later study visits, which is common in longitudinal bariatric cohorts and has been noted in prior studies [41]. Additionally, although the observed effect sizes were modest, they remain clinically meaningful at the population level. An important limitation is that detailed data regarding obesity-related comorbidities (including diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease) and medication use during the preoperative period were not systematically collected. Patients experiencing longer delays before surgery may have undergone additional medical optimization or medication intensification, which could independently influence postoperative glycemic and lipid outcomes. Consequently, residual confounding by clinical severity and medical treatment cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Delayed completion of MBS was associated with less favorable postoperative cardiometabolic outcomes over 24 months, particularly for cholesterol measures and glycemic control. Importantly, baseline cardiometabolic profiles did not significantly differ between early and delayed completers, suggesting that delays in accessing surgery may adversely influence postoperative metabolic recovery. These findings support health-system efforts aimed at reducing avoidable delays and improving timely access to MBS.
