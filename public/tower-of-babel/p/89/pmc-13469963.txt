Outcomes of revision reverse total shoulder arthroplasty after failed anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Ankur S Narain, Jordan H Larson, Joshua U Hancock, Kaitlyn N Christmas, Alexander J MacFarlane, Micah J Nieboer, Mark A Frankle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is the mainstay of treatment after failed anatomic TSA. The purpose of this study was to demonstrate patient-reported outcomes and re-revision rates after rTSA performed for failed anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty.MethodsBetween 2002 and 2022, 267 shoulders underwent revision rTSA after failed TSA in this single surgeon institutional database. From this cohort, 188 shoulders (70.41%) had minimum 1-year clinical follow-up and were included in outcomes analysis. Outcomes including American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, Simple Shoulder Test, stability score, visual analog scale pain, visual analog scale function, and patient satisfaction were collected both pre-operatively and post-operatively. Range of motion (ROM) measurements were recorded including forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation. Demographics and surgical factors including implant characteristics, use of bone graft, and indication for revision rTSA were included. Re-revisions were identified, and risk factors were analyzed. Statistical analysis including student's t-test, chi-square analysis, logistic regression, and multivariate stepwise regression was performed.ResultsThe average age at revision rTSA was 67.97 years, and average follow-up period was 61.72 months. Glenoid component loosening (40.82%) and rotator cuff failure (37.45%) were the most common indications for revision rTSA. There were improvements in American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores at 1-year follow-up (pre-operative 30.41 ± 16.64, post-operative 66.04 ± 24.93, P < .001). All ROM parameters improved from the pre-operative period to 1-year follow-up, with forward elevation improving from 70° to 129° (P < .001). Abduction improved from 64° to 119° (P < .001). External rotation improved from 27° to 38° (P = .009). Internal rotation, which was graded on a 0-8 scale, improved from 3.06 to 3.92 (P = .005). A total of 28 patients (10.49%) underwent re-revision at a mean time of 33.3 months. Baseplate failure was the most common re-revision indication (n = 14, 5.2%). Use of a larger, load sharing glenosphere was associated with a lower risk of baseplate failure requiring re-revision (2.4% vs. 6.2%, P < .001).ConclusionsRevision rTSA after failed TSA demonstrates significant improvements in patient-reported outcomes and ROM at an average follow-up period of greater than 5 years. Baseplate failure is the most common reason for re-revision. In this implant family, larger glenospheres were associated with lower baseplate-related re-revision in exploratory analysis. Use of larger glenosphere sizes (40–44 mm) is associated with lower rates of baseplate failure in this population within an implant system where the glenosphere is designed to contact the underlying glenoid bone. This finding should not be generalized to implant systems that do not share this design characteristic.Keywords: Revision shoulder arthroplasty, Anatomic shoulder arthroplasty, Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Glenoid loosening, Baseplate failure, Load sharing

Failure of anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) represents an emerging clinical problem, especially as patients age and the time from their initial arthroplasty increases.1,2 The etiology of failed aTSA can vary but can broadly be encompassed into categories including soft tissue deficiency, osseous deficiency, component wear, or any combination thereof.3,4 Specific reasons for aTSA failure have been investigated and can include glenoid or humeral component failure, rotator cuff tears, instability, infection, pain, or stiffness.6 Additionally, understanding the etiology of aTSA failure can help to guide subsequent treatment strategies.

Multiple different treatment strategies exist for failed aTSA. Clinical observation with symptomatic management is a viable strategy for some patients but is often not tolerated well and is reserved for those who cannot undergo additional surgical procedures due to significant comorbidity burden. Revision of failed glenoid components may provide short-term relief but can be wrought with significant clinical complications and poor outcomes due to often concomitant soft tissue deficiencies.1 Component resection without additional implantation is also an option but is associated with relatively poor functional limb ability. Revision to reverse TSA (rTSA) represents a viable option that can inherently deal with the combination of osseous and soft tissue deficiencies associated with failed aTSA.20 As such, rTSA has become the mainstay of operative treatment after failed aTSA.

Multiple previous investigations have demonstrated the efficacy of revision rTSA for the treatment of failed aTSA.2,5,9,11,13,16,17,20,21 A recent systematic review has shown that revision rTSA after failed shoulder arthroplasty generally improves pain, PROMs, and ROM, but complication and secondary surgery rates remain substantial, and the published literature remains dominated by retrospective, heterogeneous series. The single-surgeon design of the present study may eliminate operative variability as a confounder and allows for direct evaluation of specific intraoperative decisions, particularly glenosphere size selection, as risk factors for re-revision in the largest such cohort reported to date. The primary purpose of this study was to report patient-reported outcomes measures (PROMs) and re-revision rates after revision rTSA performed for failed aTSA. Secondary aims of this study included descriptions of patient-reported post-operative range of motion (ROM) and identifying potential demographic and operative factors associated with the risk of re-revision. We hypothesized that patients who do not require re-revision procedures will improve clinically. Additionally, there are identifiable risk factors that are associated with re-revision surgery.

Materials and methods

Patient population

Institutional review board approval was obtained for this study. This was a retrospective review of patients from a prospectively collected single surgeon shoulder arthroplasty registry at our institution. The registry was queried by Current Procedural Terminology codes to identify all patients who had undergone revision rTSA after a previous failed anatomic shoulder arthroplasty from 2002-2022 (23472 + 23,332 prior to 2014, and 23474 from 2014 through 2022). All operative procedures were performed by the senior author (M.A.F). Indications for requiring revision were determined from both review of operative notes and radiographic studies. The subset of patients with at least 1 year of clinical follow-up was also identified for inclusion in the analysis of PROMs.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed by and used the standard technique of the senior author (M.A.F). The procedures were performed through a standard deltopectoral approach and components were revised to either the Reverse Shoulder Prosthesis (RSP; Enovis Surgical, Austin, TX, USA), RSP Monoblock (Enovis Surgical), or AltiVate Reverse (Enovis Surgical). The RSP, RSP Monoblock, and AltiVate Reverse platforms primarily differ in their humeral-side design. The glenoid-side architecture, specifically the inlaid baseplate design and glenosphere sizing options, is identical across all 3 platforms. Glenosphere size selection was determined by intraoperative glenoid anatomy and was not systematically associated with a specific platform. Operative characteristics including implant sizes, use of humeral-sided lateralization, and use of adjunctive bone graft for glenoid defects were recorded.

Post-operative rehabilitation included sling immobilization for the first 6 weeks after surgery. From 6 weeks to 3 months, patients were instructed to perform active-assist shoulder ROM exercises daily. At 3 months after surgery, progressive strengthening was incorporated into the exercise program. Patients were not routinely referred to formal physical therapy.

Clinical outcomes

PROMs were routinely collected at pre-operative and at post-operative evaluations after 3 months. PROMs collected included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) Score, the Simple Shoulder Test, visual analog scale (VAS) Pain, VAS Function Score, shoulder stability score, and patient satisfaction rating on a 10-point Likert scale. ROM measurements were also collected as pre-operative and post-operative evaluations. ROM was collected through an online survey with subjects self-reporting ROM values.8,14 Collected motions included forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation.

Internal rotation was graded on a 0-8 scale with corresponding motions levels as follows: 0 for ipsilateral hip, 1 for ipsilateral back pocket, 2 for contralateral back pocket, 3 for L5-S1 level, 4 for T11-L1 level, 5 for T7-T10 level, 6 for T4-T6 level, 7 for T2-T3 level, and 8 for C8-T1 level. Patients requiring re-revision were also identified by Current Procedural Terminology search for revision shoulder arthroplasty (23472 + 23332 prior to 2014, 23473 or 23474 from 2014 through the end of the study interval) of our institutional arthroplasty database and records were confirmed via chart review. Re-revisions were defined as return to the operating room for modification or exchange of the humeral stem, glenosphere, or baseplate-screw construct. Return to the operating room for treatment of acute surgical site infections with irrigation and debridement was considered a reoperation but not a re-revision. Indications for revision were identified based on operative reports and radiographic studies. Risk factors for re-revision were identified from patient demographic factors and operative characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata SE 19 for Mac (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to quantify patient demographic and operative characteristics. Student's t-test and chi-square analysis were utilized to compare PROMs and ROM measurements from pre-operative to 1-year and final follow-up evaluation. As previous investigations have shown no significant difference between 1-year and 2-year patient-reported outcomes after shoulder arthroplasty procedures,18 1-year outcomes were reported as appropriate. Bivariate and stepwise multivariate Poisson regression analysis with robust error variance was utilized to determine risk factors for re-revision. This model produces incidence risk ratios, with an anchor value of 1. In this model, 95% confidence intervals that do not cross 1 are considered statistically significant. Significance was set at P < .05 except in the case of stepwise multivariate analysis during which the Bonferroni correction was used.

Results

Patient demographics

Two hundred sixty-seven patients were identified as having undergone revision rTSA after failed anatomic shoulder arthroplasty from 2002-2022. One hundred eighty-eight patients had at least 1-year clinical follow-up (70.4%) and were included in analysis of PROMs. Patient demographic characteristics are described in Table I. The mean age was 67.97 years. One hundred thirty-seven patients were male (51.31%), and average final follow-up was 61.72 months.

Clinical outcomes

All PROMs improved significantly from pre-operative evaluation to 1-year and final recorded follow-up. ASES scores improved from 30.41 ± 16.64 pre-operatively to 66.04 ± 24.93 at 1-year follow-up. Simple Shoulder Test scores improved from 2.33 ± 2.57 pre-operatively to 5.95 ± 3.50 at 1-year follow-up. VAS pain scores improved from 6.03 ± 2.71 pre-operatively to 2.83 ± 2.26 at 1-year follow-up. VAS function scores improved from 2.97 ± 2.56 pre-operatively to 6.37 ± 2.46 at 1-year follow-up. Shoulder stability ratings improved from 3.15 ± 2.96 pre-operatively to 6.93 ± 2.89 at 1-year follow-up. Finally, patient satisfaction ratings (1-10 scale) improved from 4.81 ± 3.41 pre-operatively to 7.79 ± 2.59 at 1-year follow-up. All improvements were statistically significant with P values <0.001 (Table III). Additionally, there were no significant differences between 1-year and 2-year follow-up for all PROMs tested (P > .05 for each).

Re-revisions

Table V represents data regarding re-revision rates. The re-revision rate was 10.49% (n = 28). The most common reasons for re-revision were baseplate failure (n = 14) and humeral loosening (n = 5). The average time to re-revision was 33.30 ± 37.98 months (Fig. 3). However, upon multivariate analysis no identifiable risk factors were found for re-revision for all indications (P > .05 for each).

Discussion

Failure of aTSA represents an emerging problem within shoulder surgery.19 While multiple treatment options exist, revision rTSA is the mainstay of operative management after failed aTSA.20 Understanding clinical outcomes after revision rTSA for failed aTSA will help practitioners counsel patients regarding appropriate post-operative expectations. As such, the purpose of this study was to utilize the largest to-date population of patients undergoing revision rTSA after failed aTSA to report trends in clinical outcomes and re-revision rates. Our hypotheses were that patients who did not require re-revision surgery would improve clinically and that risk factors for requiring re-revision could be identified. Both hypotheses were supported by the data.

Our investigation found that all tested PROMs and self-reported ROM improved after revision rTSA for failed aTSA. ROM improvements notably also included an increase in post-operative internal rotation. Otte et al, in a study of 75 patients undergoing revision rTSA after failed aTSA, also identified significant improvements in final ASES score (62) and shoulder forward elevation (128°). Active external rotation did not improve in these patients, and internal rotation values were not reported.17 In a study of 127 patients who underwent revision rTSA after failed aTSA with glenoid component loosening, Bartels et al2 demonstrated improvements in forward elevation (82.5°) and external rotation (27.4°), but not in internal rotation, at follow-up of 35 months. In a meta-analysis of 23 studies of revision rTSA after failed aTSA or hemiarthroplasty, Welch et al found that ASES score improved at final follow-up (61.8). Additionally, internal rotation was only reported in 10 of the included studies with no improvements in most investigations (9/10, 90%).21 Our results, combined with those of previous studies, continue to confirm the efficacy of revision rTSA as the primary operative management of failed aTSA. The expected improvements in PROMs and ROM are also important to know when setting expectations with patients during pre-operative counseling sessions prior to revision rTSA.

The rate of re-revision in our population was 10.49%. While the indications for re-revision did vary, the most common reason for re-revision in our cohort was baseplate loosening at 5.2%. The overall rate of re-revision in our study group is similar to other published reports, which have demonstrated re-revision rate for any indication between 6 and 20%.2,7,9,16,21 Regarding baseplate failure as an indication for re-revision, our rate of 5.2% is slightly lower than other published reports with baseplate failure rates of 6-13%.2,7,9,21 The etiology of baseplate failure as the most common indication for re-revision likely is a factor of compromised glenoid bone stock at the time of initial revision stemming from multiple factors including polyethylene wear, loosening, and bone loss during component removal. This is supported by Franke et al5, who demonstrated an increased odds ratio of requiring re-revision after rTSA for failed aTSA in patients who had a loose glenoid component at the initial revision. Recognizing scenarios where increased risk of glenoid-sided failure is present can aid surgeons in deciding when to utilize alternate baseplate fixation techniques and implant sizing strategies to mitigate such risk.

In this series, larger glenospheres were associated with fewer baseplate-failure re-revisions. This finding should be considered exploratory and implant-family specific. Prior biomechanical work suggests that larger glenospheres may improve glenoid-side stability when direct glenosphere undersurface contact with native glenoid bone is achieved15; however, the present study did not include radiographic confirmation of such contact and did not directly compare contact and noncontact constructs. Accordingly, our data do not prove a load-sharing mechanism and instead support only an association between larger glenosphere size and lower baseplate-related re-revision in this cohort. The etiology of this likely stems from increased biomechanical stability of the baseplate-bone construct. This is especially important in revision cases where compromised glenoid bone stock precludes the use of typical baseplate fixation strategies and implant sizes. Larger glenospheres may impart more inherent stability by allowing for undersurface contact of the glenosphere with the glenoid bone.10 This leads to an increased surface contact area of the baseplate-bone construct, which in turn leads to decreased micromotion and stress at the interface. In a previous study at our institution, Nigro et al tested these assumptions using finite-element analysis via a biomechanical computational model. In this analysis, micromotion and stress both decreased when baseplate and glenosphere bone contact area increased.15 Additionally, the largest glenosphere sizes were found to have the most significant decreases in construct micromotion at the baseplate-glenosphere-bone interface (Figure 5). These findings, along with findings of our study, support the use of larger glenosphere sizes in revision procedures both to improve construct stability and to mitigate the risk of baseplate failure requiring re-revision.

(b) Larger glenosphere demonstrates load-sharing via direct bone contact that dissipates forces seen at the implant bone-interface over a larger surface area.

This study is not without limitations. First, this study is limited by its retrospective design, which precludes causal inference and introduces the possibility of selection bias and incomplete data capture. Also, this investigation represents the results and experiences of a single-surgeon at a single institution which may limit overall generalizability. However, single-surgeon study design does provide uniformity in both surgical indications and intraoperative decision making. This consistency is especially important when complex intraoperative decisions need to be made such as when to use adjunctive fixation techniques, bone graft, and alternative glenosphere sizing. Second, a single implant type was utilized with a larger collection of glenosphere sizes and lateralization options compared to most others available on the market. Within the implant type utilized, there were 3 design iterations, which could constitute a potential confounder. The primary difference between these designs was localized to the humeral stem, while the glenoid-sided components did not change. As such, the results of this study may not be directly applicable to use of other implant systems. However, most other systems can create an analogous load sharing effect via such strategies as utilizing larger baseplates. Future study would be required to determine the effect of implant configuration. Lastly, the loss to follow-up rate was approximately 29.6%. As part of our standard practice, we attempt to maximize follow-up rates by contacting patients via both email and phone to ensure that they answer outcomes surveys and present to the office for their scheduled clinical follow-up. Additionally, those patients who do not follow-up are contacted by office staff multiple times at the 2-year, 5-year, and 10-year post-operative points to maximize clinical record completion. Furthermore, the follow-up rate in this study is similar to reported follow-up rates in other shoulder arthroplasty cohorts.12

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that revision rTSA after failed aTSA leads to improvements in clinical outcomes in those patients that did not require re-revision. Improvements in all PROMs as well as ROM of the operative extremity were seen at midterm follow-up. Surgeons can utilize this information to counsel patients appropriately regarding their expected clinical improvement after revision rTSA. The re-revision rate in this cohort was 10.49%, and the use of larger, load-sharing glenospheres was associated with a lower rate of re-revision for baseplate failure. Within the RSP implant system studied, larger glenospheres are designed to contact the underlying glenoid bone, which may improve baseplate construct stability by increasing contact surface area and reducing micromotion. Surgeons using implant systems where the glenosphere is designed to contact the glenoid bone should consider using larger glenosphere sizes in revision rTSA with the goal of improving baseplate-glenosphere-bone contact area, decreasing construct micromotion, and improving overall implant survival in revision rTSA.
