Effects of different planting patterns on potato rhizosphere soil microbial composition and yield
Authors: Xiaoting Wang, Qingqing Hou, Fan Li, Liming Wang, Xiaoyan Zhang, Ruping Yang, Muhammad Aqeel, Chunhui Tang, Guangrong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPotato is a crucial dual-purpose crop, serving both as a staple food and vegetable in Northwest China. However, the prevalence of continuous monocropping has increasingly exacerbated continuous cropping obstacles in the region. While numerous studies have explored effective strategies for mitigating these obstacles, the impact of different cropping systems on potato yield and rhizosphere microbial communities remain insufficiently understood.Methods16 S rRNA and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) MiSeq sequencing methods to elucidate the impact of different planting patterns, including continuous potato monocropping (PM), potato-soybean rotation (PS), intercropping with continuous cropping of potatoes and soybeans (IC), and intercropping with rotation of potatoes and soybeans (IR) on potato yield and rhizosphere soil microbial communities.ResultsPotato yield varied significantly among different cropping systems, with the PM system producing the lowest yields, whereas the IR and PS systems achieved the highest. Planting patterns exerted a pronounced influence on bacterial α‑diversity, with all indices (Shannon, the number of observed OTUs, Chao1, and ACE) reaching their minimum values under PM, while fungal α‑diversity remained largely unaffected. β-diversity analysis showed that the first two PCoA axes cumulatively explained 49.66% and 31.30% of the variation in bacterial and fungal communities, respectively. Conversely, bacterial community composition showed limited sensitivity to planting patterns, whereas fungal community composition varied significantly among patterns. Across all treatments, the dominant bacterial phyla included Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Gemmatimonadetes, Acidobacteria, Firmicutes, and Rhodobacteriota; whereas the dominant fungal phyla were Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Glomeromycota. The LEfSe analysis results further showed that the principal biomarkers of the bacterial community under the PS planting pattern belonged to the Bacteroidia, Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria. For fungal community, the biomarkers enriched under different planting patterns were affiliated with the phylum Basidiomycota. Different planting patterns also restructured rhizosphere microbial co-occurrence network. Bacterial co-occurrence network under PM cropping systems exhibited lower node number compared to other planting patterns, whereas fungal network under the IR planting displayed higher stability. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) further indicated that planting patterns, bacterial diversity, and fungal diversity promoted potato yield, whereas bacterial complexity exerted a suppressive effect. Among these factors, planting pattern emerged as the strongest determinants of potato yield.ConclusionThe increased yield under crop rotation and intercropping systems may be mediated by the modulation of soil microbial communities, specifically by increasing microbial diversity and reducing network complexity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04861-8.Keywords: Potato, rhizosphere soils, microbial communities, different planting patterns

Introduction

Modern intensive agriculture increasingly relies on simplified cropping systems and heavy inputs of inorganic fertilizers and pesticides to meet the growing global demand for agricultural products [1]. While effective in the short term, this paradigm has eroded farmland biodiversity and exerted adverse effects on environmental health and ecosystem functions, thereby posing grave risks to long-term food security and the sustainable development of agriculture [2]. Among globally important crops, soybean (Glycine max) and potato (Solanum tuberosum) play central roles in global agriculture. In northwest China particularly, potato has emerged as a traditional and dominant crop, where a comprehensive cultivation system has been established. Despite its agricultural significance, the large-scale expansion of potato production in this region has precipitated critical challenges, including the simplification of cropping systems, soil degradation due to continuous potato monoculture [3, 4], and difficulties in implementing effective crop rotation practices. These issues have severely compromised both potato yield and quality. Consequently, the adoption of diversified cropping systems has become a pivotal strategy to enhance potato productivity, improve crop quality, and mitigate the obstacles associated with continuous cropping [4–6]. Such measures are of paramount importance for ensuring long-term food security and promoting sustainable agricultural practices.

Legumes are commonly utilized in crop rotation or intercropping systems, with their primary advantage being the regulation of soil fertility [7, 8]. Legumes establish symbiotic relationship with rhizobia, enabling biological nitrogen fixation. This process enriches soil nitrogen availability, reduces dependence on chemical fertilizers, lower production costs, and mitigate environmental pollution [9]. For instance, highly adaptable varieties such as soybeans and faba beans (Vicia faba L.) are essential members of the legume family. Their integration into rotation or intercropping systems can enhance soil organic matter content, promote nutrient accumulation, optimize soil microbial communities, improve land-use efficiency, and deliver significant ecological benefits [10, 11]. For example, a faba bean-potato-oat-faba bean rotation has been shown to improve the soil microbial community structure and suppress potato black scurf [12]. Similarly, intercropping soybeans with potatoes can increase soil organic carbon and boost crop yields [13]. Previous studies have investigated the effects of different planting patterns on microbial community diversity and soil ecosystem multifunctionality [4, 7, 14]. However, studies on the diversity and composition of rhizosphere microbial communities under diversified cropping patterns remain comparatively scarce.

The rhizosphere, which serves as the primary gateway through which nutrients, water, and harmful substances enter the crop system, participating in the material cycling of the food chain, has become a major focus of recent research [15, 16]. Rhizosphere microorganisms play a crucial role in plant nutrient uptake, growth and development, and antagonism against pathogens [17, 18]. Studies have demonstrated that long-term monoculture deteriorate soil quality and alter rhizosphere microbial community characteristics through allelopathic effects and modification in root exudates [19–21]. Furthermore, even short-term continuous cropping can intensify the accumulation of soil-borne fungal pathogens and weaken the complexity and interactions of cross-kingdom networks between soil bacteria and fungi, ultimately reducing crop yields [1].

Emerging evidence shows that intercropping or rotation planting patterns alter rhizosphere microbial abundance in potatoes and alleviate the obstacles associated with continuous potato cropping [12, 22, 23]. For instance, Qin et al. [22] demonstrated that potato-maize intercropping increased the abundance of bacteria and fungi in the rhizosphere soil of both crops, thereby improving microbial functional diversity. Similarly, Qin et al. [12] reported that planting oats or faba beans after potato cultivation increased beneficial soil microorganisms, maintained ecosystem health, suppressed black scurf incidence, and improved yields. In another study, Wang et al. [23] reported that a cropping sequence including two years of green manure followed by one year of potato increased soil fertility, microbial abundance and potato yield. Collectively, it is essential to investigate how different planting patterns influence potato yield via modulation of the rhizosphere microbial community and the subsequent alleviation of continuous cropping obstacles.

This study investigates the effects of four distinct planting patterns on potato yield and employs 16 S rRNA and ITS high-throughput sequencing technologies to analyze their impact on the diversity and composition of the rhizosphere microbial community. The findings aim to establish a theoretical foundation for maintaining soil health and optimizing planting patterns in agricultural systems in Northwest China.

Materials and methods

Study site and treatments

The field experiment was conducted at the experimental station of the Gansu Academy of Agricultural Sciences, located in Huangyang Town, Liangzhou District, Wuwei City, Gansu Province (35°37′N, 105°13′E, 1594 m above sea level). The region is characterized as arid, with an annual sunshine duration ranging from 1664.6 to 2532.7 h, a mean annual temperature of 8 °C, a frost-free period of approximately 150 days, and a mean annual precipitation of 154.6 mm. The soil is classified as desert alluvial soil with moderate fertility. The experiment was initiated in 2017, with treatments arrangements across years detailed in Table 1 and Figure S1. Prior to sowing in the same year, the physiochemical properties of the topsoil (0–20 cm) were measured; including organic matter (10.21 g·kg⁻1), total nitrogen (0.67 g·kg⁻1), total phosphorus (0.79 g·kg⁻1), total potassium (11.07 g·kg⁻1), alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (39.82 mg·kg⁻1), available phosphorus (9.26 mg·kg⁻1), rapidly available potassium (99.89 mg·kg⁻1) and soil pH (8.01).

Experimental treatments across different years

Intercropping with continuous cropping of potatoes and soybeans; IR, Intercropping with rotation of potatoes and soybeans; PM, Continuous potato monocropping; PS, Potato-soybean rotation

Early-maturing potato (Atlantic) and soybean (Jidou 17), cultivated by the Cereal and Oil Crops Research Institute of Hebei Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences, were selected as the research crops. Four treatments were established: continuous potato monocropping (PM), potato-soybean rotation (PS), intercropping with continuous cropping of potato and soybean (IC), and intercropping with rotation of potato and soybean (IR). For the continuous potato monocropping (PM) treatment, the strip width was 220 cm with a potato width of 110 cm, where two rows of potatoes were planted on ridges with plastic mulch. For the intercropping with continuous cropping (IC) treatment, the strip width was also 220 cm, with a potato width of 110 cm where two rows of potatoes were planted on ridges with plastic mulch, and a soybean width of 110 cm where three rows were planted on flat ground. In the IC treatment, soybeans and potatoes were planted continuously within their respective strips each year. For the intercropping with rotation (IR) treatment, the planting pattern was the same as IC, but potatoes and soybeans were rotated annually between the strips. The potato-soybean rotation (PS) followed the same planting pattern as the PM pattern, involving the annual rotation of potato and soybean between years.

The experiment followed a randomized block setup, with planting oriented from south to north. Each treatment had three replicates, yielding twelve planting plots in total, each measuring 6.6 m by 10.5 m area. The planting densities for continuous potato monocropping, rotation, and intercropping were all set at 47,625 hills per hectare for potatoes, while soybean densities for rotation and intercropping were both set at 225,000 plants per hectare. Fertilizers were applied at 225 kg·ha⁻1 nitrogen (N), 90 kg·ha⁻1 phosphorus, and 75 kg·ha⁻1 potassium. Of this, 50% N, 70% potassium oxide (K2O), and 100% diphosphate pentoxide (P2O5) were used as base fertilizers; the remaining 50% N, and 30% K2O were applied as top-dressing during the flowering stage. The fertilization strategy was applied uniformly to all treatments in different years, following local best practice guidelines. Potatoes were irrigated three times during the growing season, with each application at 1700 m³·ha⁻1. Field management adhered to conventional high-yield cultivation practices, with strict pests and disease control throughout the growth period.

Soil sample collection

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected in mid-August 2024, corresponding to the 8th year of continuous planting, at crop maturity of both soybeans and potatoes. For potato plants, a five-point sampling method was applied: five plants with uniform growth were selected, and the entire root system was carefully excavated and pooled. The large soil aggregates adhering to the roots were gently shaken off (retaining the tightly adhering rhizosphere soil), and each plot was treated as one composite sample. Roots were then cut into approximately 2 cm segments and placed into 50 mL sterile, enzyme-free centrifuge tubes. An appropriate amount of sterile distilled water was added, and the tubes were shaken at 180 rpm for 30 min to detach the soil particles from root surface. Roots were subsequently removed from the centrifuge tubes, and the rhizosphere soil samples were obtained by centrifuging the tubes at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The experimental samples used for DNA extraction were stored at -80 °C.

Yield determination

At crop maturity (September 29 for soybean and September 10 for potato), total yield was measured by harvesting entire plots. Individual crop yields were calculated, aggregated, and converted to a per-hectare basis for uniform comparison among treatments.

Soil Microbial Community Analysis

Total DNA was extracted from 0.5 g rhizosphere soil using the Powersoil® DNA Isolation Kit (MO BIO Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the soil DNA were tested using a NanoDrop™ 2000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (OD 260/OD 280 ratio). The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primer pairs 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), and the fungal ITS region 1 was amplified using the primer set ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′) [24, 25]. PCRs were performed in 20 µl volumes containing 4 µl of 5× FastPfu Buffer, 2 µl of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µl of each primer, 10 ng of template DNA, and 0.4 µl of FastPfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech Co., China). Bacterial amplification conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, 30 cycles of 95 °C (30 s), 55 °C (30 s), 72 °C (45 s), and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Fungal amplification conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, 35 cycles of 94 °C (1 min), 51 °C (1 min), 72 °C (1 min), and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Each sample was amplified in triplicate to reduce bias, and amplicons were pooled, visualized on 2.0% agarose gels, and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, USA). Purified amplicons were pooled at equimolar concentrations and sequenced using pair-end (2 × 300 bp) chemistry on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, USA) at Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw sequences were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic (v0.36) and Pear (v0.9.6) to retain high-quality reads (average Phred score > 20 and minimum length = 50 bp) and paired-end reads were merged under strict criteria requiring ≥ 10 bp overlap and < 5% mismatches. After barcode-based demultiplexing, chimeric sequences were removed using the UCHIME algorithm [26], and the resulting FASTA sequences were further processed via the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME2 v.2018.6) pipeline [27]. Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity following singleton exclusion. Taxonomic classification of representative OTU sequences was performed using the SILVA database (v.138) [28] for bacteria and the UNITE database (v.10.0) [29] for fungi, yielding a total of 3,181 bacterial OTUs and 3,555 fungal OTUs, and all counts were transformed to relative abundancies.

Data analysis

Differences in potato yield under different planting patterns were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, while Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to determine significant differences in bacterial and fungal communities, followed by the Dunn’s test for post-hoc comparisons. The bacterial and fungal α-diversity indices, including Chao1, the number of observed OTUs, ACE, and Shannon were calculated. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix was performed to visualize the dissimilarity in bacterial and fungal community compositions, with ordination plots generated to reflect the spatial distribution of samples. Statistical significance of inter-group differences in community structure was tested through PERMANOVA with 10,000 permutations in the R package “vegan” using the adonis function. The “ggplot2” package in R was used to visualize differences in bacterial and fungal communities at the phylum level across different soil samples. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis was used to identify dominant microbial taxa across different planting patterns, with significant biomarkers screened by Kruskal-Wallis test P < 0.05 and LDA score ≥ 2.5 for bacteria and ≥ 2.0 for fungi.

A co-occurrence network was constructed based on Spearman’s correlations among the relative abundance of bacterial and fungal OTUs from all treatments. All possible Spearman’s correlations among OTUs were calculated, and only robust correlations with absolute values ≥ 0.75 and FDR-corrected P ≤ 0.01 were retained. Gephi v0.9.2 was used to visualize the microbial co-occurrence network. Subsequently, we extracted subnetworks for each treatment using the subgraph function in the R package “igraph” [30, 31]. Network properties, including the number of nodes and edges, average path length, average degree, connectivity, and average clustering coefficient were calculated. By averaging the standardized Z-scores of these six properties, we obtained the network complexity index. It should be noted that before conducting this calculation, the average path length was presented in the form of its reciprocal [32]. In addition, robustness was used to assess the network stability. For each subnetwork, robustness is defined as the proportion of remaining nodes after removing 50% of random nodes at 1000 iterations [30, 32].

To disentangle direct and indirect influences of soil microbial communities on potato yield under different planting patterns, structural equation models (SEMs) were employed. After data manipulation, we used a dataset to parameterize the model and tested the overall goodness of fit. The model was considered to be a good fit if the data included an insignificant (P > 0.05) chi-square test statistic, RMSEA < 0.05, P > 0.05, and both GFI and AGIF > 0.90 [33]. SEM analyses were performed using AMOS 21.0 (Amos Development Corporation, IBM SPSS, USA).

As shown in Table 2, potato yields varied significantly under different planting patterns. Among them, the PM planting pattern resulted in the lowest potato yield of 8291.66 kg·ha⁻1, while the highest potato yields were observed under the IR and PS patterns, reaching 11128.57 kg·ha⁻1 and 10553.57 kg·ha⁻1, respectively. Under the IC and IR planting patterns, there was no significant difference in soybean yield; when aggregating with potato yields, the total crop yield reached its highest levels at 11319.28 kg·ha⁻1 and 12650.00 kg·ha⁻1, respectively.

Effects of different planting patterns on potato rhizosphere soil microbial diversity and composition

Planting patterns significantly influenced bacterial community α-diversity indexes (Shannon, the number of observed OTUs, ACE and Chao 1) in potato rhizosphere (Fig. 1a-d), while fungal community diversity indexes were unaffected (Fig. 1e-h). For bacterial community, all α-diversity indices under the PM planting pattern were lower than those under the other patterns (Fig. 1a-d). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) showed that the first two axes i.e., PCoA1 and PCoA2 explained 32.23% and 17.43% of the total variation in the bacterial community composition, respectively. For the fungal community, PCoA1 and PCoA2 accounted for 17.56% and 13.74% of the community variation, respectively. PERMANOVA analysis indicated significant difference in the fungal community structure among planting patterns (R² = 30.2%, P = 0.031; Fig. 2b).

We classified the bacterial and fungal phyla with average relative abundance greater than 1% as the dominant phyla. It was found that the dominant microbial phyla in the rhizosphere soil of potato plants remained consistent under different planting patterns. The dominant bacterial phyla were Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Gemmatimonadetes, Acidobacteria, Firmicutes and Chloroflexi (Fig. 2c); while Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota and Mortierellomycota were the dominant fungal phyla (Fig. 2d).

Despite this general consistency, notable differences in their relative abundances were observed among planting patterns (Fig. S2, Fig. S3). Within bacterial phyla, Proteobacteria were the most abundant phylum under PM planting pattern and least under PS planting pattern; while Bacteroidetes peaked under PS planting pattern but was the lowest under IC and IR planting patterns; Firmicutes exhibited highest abundance under IC planting pattern and the lowest in PM planting pattern. For fungal phyla, Mortierellomycota showed the highest abundance in the IR and the lowest in the IC and PS planting patterns. The abundances of the other dominant phyla showed no significant differences among planting patterns.

LEfSe analysis further revealed the specific microbial taxa across all taxonomic levels that differed significantly under different planting patterns (Figs. 3 and 4). A total of 10 bacterial taxa (LDA score > 2.5) were identified, all with significantly higher relative abundances under the PS planting pattern compared to the other patterns (Fig. 3). In addition, 8 fungal taxa (LDA score > 2.0) were identified, with higher abundances of Vishniacozyma carnescens, Lenzites, Lenzites betulina, Archaeosporomycetes, and Archaeosporales in IR; Tremellodendropsidales and Phomatospora in PM; and Agaricaceae in PS (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Responses of potato yield to different planting patterns

Crop yield is shaped by multitude of determinants, including the natural environment, inherent genetic characteristics of crops, and diverse cultivation management practices [23, 34]. Our results clearly demonstrate that potato-soybean rotation and intercropping patterns significantly enhanced potato yield. Specifically, potato yield was lowest under the PM regime, whereas it reached the maximum levels under both IR and PS systems (Table 2). This phenomenon may be ascribed to the increased burden of soil-borne pathogens and pest pressure engendered by continuous cropping, which triggers continuous cropping obstacles. Such obstacles lead to stunted growth and dwarfism, ultimately culminating in yield reduction [12]. On the contrary, as a leguminous crop, soybean can significantly improve soil nutrient status and regulate soil fertility through its nitrogen fixation ability [9]. Therefore, crop rotation and intercropping with soybeans are likely to enhance potato productivity by improving soil physicochemical properties and enriching beneficial soil microorganisms [12, 22, 35].

Responses of the microbial communities in potato rhizosphere soil under different planting patterns

To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the microbial community structure in the rhizosphere of potato plants under different planting patterns, Illumina MiSeq method was employed [36]. We found discernible shifts in bacterial α-diversity, while fungal diversity showed no significant variation across different planting regimes (Fig. 1). Relative to the PM planting pattern, both intercropping and rotation increased bacterial α-diversity indices, which was aligned with previous studies [37, 38]. This divergence was mainly attributed to the higher sensitivity of soil bacterial community, relative to fungal community, to alterations induced by intercropping systems, while fungal abundance remained comparatively stable [39]. Notably, the lowest bacterial α-diversity was observed under the PM planting pattern, likely attributable to continuous monocropping, which can restructure bacterial community composition [40]. This finding is consistent with earlier research by Liu et al. [41], who reported that continuous cropping reduced bacterial diversity in the potato rhizosphere while exerting minimal influence on fungal diversity.

This study identified Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Gemmatimonadetes, Acidobacteria, Firmicutes, and Rhodobacteriota as the dominant bacterial phyla colonizing the potato rhizosphere under examined planting patterns (Fig. 2c). This finding aligns with results from other studies, which widely acknowledge Proteobacteria and Acidobacteria, as dominant microbial phyla in agricultural ecosystems, being widely distributed in the rhizosphere soils of various plants [42–44]. Notably, nitrogen-fixing bacterial assemblages in both rotation and monocropping soils are largely represented by the members of the Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria classes [45]. The LEfSe analysis results further showed that the principal biomarkers of the bacterial community under the PS planting regime belonged to the Bacteroidia, Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria (Fig. 3). Specifically, compared with other planting patterns, the PS significantly increased the relative abundances of Rhodobacterales, Rhodobacteraceae, Rubellimicrobium, Rhizobacter and Aurantisolimonas (Fig. 3). Among these, Rhizobacter enriched under the PS pattern belongs to the order Burkholderiales and is a well-known plant growth‑promoting rhizobacterium (PGPR). It can effectively colonize the crop rhizosphere, enhance nutrient uptake, and ultimately increase crop yield, thereby promoting plant growth and development [46, 47]. In parallel, Rhodobacterales, Rhodobacteraceae, and Rubellimicrobium classified within Alphaproteobacteria, are frequently associated with endosymbiotic nitrogen‑fixing associations in legume roots and play important roles in agriculture [48]. Therefore, we speculate that the alleviation of continuous cropping obstacles as reflected by increased potato yield under rotation systems, may be associated with the enrichment of beneficial bacteria such as plant growth‑promoting rhizobacteria.

Regarding fungal phyla, Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Glomeromycota emerged as the dominant phyla across different planting patterns, consistent with previous findings [49, 50]. For fungal community, the biomarkers enriched under different planting patterns were affiliated with the phylum Basidiomycota (Fig. 4). Notably, Vishniacozyma carnescens, which was selectively enriched in the IR planting patterns, belongs to the class Tremellomycetes. Its ecological function may involve modulating plant disease resistance. A previous study on wheat grains demonstrated that Vishniacozyma species abundances are closely associated with altered host resistance to both the hemibiotrophic Tilletia (causal agent of common bunt) and necrotrophic Fusarium (responsible for Fusarium head blight) pathogens [51].

Impact of different planting patterns on potato rhizosphere soil microbial co-occurrence networks

Interspecies interactions represent a key biological factor influencing the occurrence of soil-borne diseases and exert profound influence on community assembly that are strongly associated with key ecological processes and host health [52, 53]. To characterize these interaction patterns, microbial co-occurrence network analysis was conducted to investigate the organizational structure of potato rhizosphere microorganisms under different cropping systems.

The results indicated that the number of nodes in the bacterial subnetwork under the PM pattern was significantly lower than those observed in other patterns (Fig. 5a). This reduction can be attributed to the decline in bacterial abundance and diversity induced by continuous cropping [54, 55], which subsequently reduce inter-bacterial interactions and lead to the formation of less stable ecosystem. Such conditions may be further compounded by the specific enrichment of potentially harmful bacterial taxa induced by continuous cropping [56].

Within the fungal co-occurrence network, PM showed the lowest robustness, while IR showed the highest robustness (Fig. 6h), suggesting that IR may increase rhizosphere microbial diversity and enrich beneficial fungal taxa, thereby conferring greater network stability and resistance to environmental perturbations [57–59]. This may be attributed to the ability of intercropping to improve the soil microbial composition and enhance the functional diversity of microbial communities [60].

Responses of potato yield under different planting patterns to rhizosphere soil microbial communities

Crop productivity is strongly governed by cultivation management practices and soil microbial communities, as soil microorganisms play a significant role in promoting crop growth and development [61, 62]. In this study, structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to analyze the direct and indirect effects of planting patterns, rhizosphere microbial diversity, network complexity, and network robustness on potato yield (Fig. 7). The SEM results demonstrated that planting patterns as well as bacterial and fungal diversity exerted positive effects on potato yield, while increased bacterial complexity was associated with potato yield reduction. Among all evaluated factors, the most critical factor influencing crop yield is the planting patterns (Fig. 7b, d). Furthermore, both bacterial and fungal diversities exerted certain influences on potato yield (Fig. 7b, d). This may be attributed to the differences in rhizosphere microbial communities under different planting patterns, coupled with alterations in root-derived organic substances that modify soil physicochemical properties. These changes subsequently modulate nutrient transformation in the soil and enhance nutrient absorption and utilization of crops, ultimately impacting the growth performance and yield formation [22, 63].

Conclusions

Potato yields varied significantly under different planting patterns, with the PM pattern yielding the lowest output, whereas the IR and P-S patterns achieved the highest. Different planting patterns significantly reshaped the bacterial α-diversity in the potato rhizosphere, whereas fungal α-diversity remained unaffected. Different planting patterns also generated distinct co-occurrence networks within rhizosphere soil microorganisms. Under the PM planting pattern, the bacterial co-occurrence network exhibits a lower number of nodes; under the IR planting pattern, the fungal network displays greater robustness. SEM results further indicated that planting patterns, bacterial and fungal diversities positively influenced potato yield positively, while bacterial network complexity reduced potato yield. In summary, crop rotation and intercropping may increase yield by shaping soil microbial communities through enhanced diversity and reduced network complexity.
