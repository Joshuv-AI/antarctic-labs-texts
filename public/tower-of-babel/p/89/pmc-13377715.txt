Neural mesenchymal-like fibroblast-derived migrasomes promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration via tenascin-C transfer during peripheral nerve regeneration
Authors: Yongbin Ma, Qian Wang, Hang Yu, Xingwen Chen, Yue Liu, Shang Wang, Yunxia Fan, Dan Zhou, Juan Wang, Yu Feng, Liyang Dong, Tao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripheral nerve regeneration involves intricate intercellular communication between fibroblasts and Schwann cells (SCs). Migrasomes are recently identified extracellular vesicles generated in migrating cells that facilitate intercellular communication. In this study, we report that following peripheral nerve injury, neural mesenchymal-like fibroblasts (NMLFs) undergo extensive proliferation and expansion, with the concurrent production of migrasomes. These NMLF-derived migrasomes not only promote SC proliferation and migration but also act crucially as directional cues to guide SC migration. We demonstrate that mechanistically, NMLF migrasomes facilitate SC proliferation and migration through the transfer of tenascin-C (TNC). Collectively, our findings reveal a novel migrasome-mediated communication mechanism between NMLFs and Schwann cells, highlighting migrasomes as a promising therapeutic target for enhancing peripheral nerve regeneration.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02965-2.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries, with high clinical morbidity, can result in functional impairment and even lifelong disability; approximately 3% of all trauma patients are estimated to sustain peripheral nerve injuries [1]. Unlike the central nervous system, the peripheral nervous system exhibits an inherent regenerative capacity following traumatic injury [2, 3]. Peripheral nerve regeneration involves spatiotemporally ordered coordination and bidirectional crosstalk between nonneuronal cellular events and molecular signalling, which collectively establish a favourable microenvironment to promote axonal regrowth and functional reinnervation of target tissues [4, 5]. However, this regenerative capacity is relatively limited, and clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory [1, 6]. Therefore, determining the mechanisms underlying interactions between nonneuronal cells during peripheral nerve regeneration is crucial for overcoming the limitations of axonal regeneration efficiency and developing innovative therapies to enhance nerve regeneration.

In peripheral nerves, Schwann cells (SCs) and fibroblasts are the two major types of resident nonneuronal cells, accounting for 45% and 25% of the cellular composition, respectively [7]. SCs are highly plastic glial cells that undergo dedifferentiation after peripheral nerve injury to acquire a repair phenotype [8]. This repair phenotype provides spatial cues (e.g., Büngner bands) and biochemical signals (e.g., release of nerve growth-associated factors and extracellular matrix), which collectively promote axonal regeneration and reinnervation of target tissue [9, 10]. Given their key role in nerve injury repair, regulating SC phenotypic functions (including proliferation and migration) to enhance nerve regeneration has become a focus of research interest.

Like SCs, fibroblasts originate from the neural crest stem cell differentiation lineage [11]. Historically, their role in peripheral nerve regeneration was traditionally recognized as mere support cells whose collagen secretion and scar-forming properties were thought to impede axonal regeneration [12, 13]. However, recent studies have challenged this paradigm: accumulating evidence indicates that fibroblasts can construct a proregenerative microenvironment rather than an inhibitory one. For instance, fibroblasts facilitate SC migration and proliferation via paracrine secretion of bioactive factors [14–16], and their secreted exosomes significantly increase axonal regeneration and myelination [7]. These findings suggest that fibroblast–SC interactions during peripheral nerve regeneration rely on complex cellular communication networks. Notably, a subset of fibroblasts within peripheral nerves has been identified that expresses mesenchymal cell markers (e.g., PDGFR-α and CD90) and possesses multidirectional differentiation potential [17]. These neural mesenchymal-like fibroblasts (NMLFs) have demonstrated therapeutic potential in promoting fingertip regeneration and skin wound healing [17]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying their interaction with SCs and their specific contribution to peripheral nerve regeneration remain unclear.

Recently, large membrane-bound vesicle structures containing variable numbers of small vesicles have been reported and designated “migrasomes” because of their secretion by migrating cells [18]. During cell migration, migrasomes form along the retraction fibers of cell tails; as these fibers break under the tension of migration, migrasomes are released. They have emerged as a novel mode of intercellular communication, transferring intrinsic signalling to recipient cells and thereby regulating the functional phenotype of target cells [19, 20]. The repair process of peripheral nerve injury is accompanied by extensive cellular migration, in which fibroblasts act as primary migratory “pioneer cells” that establish pathways for the directed migration of SCs [21–24]. Importantly, these fibroblasts exhibit characteristics consistent with those of previously described NMLFs, including robust proliferative capacity and migratory initiative following nerve injury [17]. While fibroblasts are known to generate migrasomes [25], the function of fibroblast-derived migrasomes in nerve repair remains poorly understood. In the present study, we aimed to explore the role of NMLF-derived migrasomes in peripheral nerve regeneration. Specifically, we investigated their effect on the functional phenotype of SCs and the underlying mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) and neonatal mice (3–5 days old) were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu University (approval no. UJS-IACUC-AP-2024022816) and conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Sciatic nerve injury models

The mice were acclimatized for one week prior to the experiment. Under anaesthesia, the sciatic nerve was exposed using our previously published method [26]. Sciatic nerve injury models of varying severity were then generated: Model 1 (sciatic nerve crush model): the nerve was compressed with ultrafine haemostats for 10 s, which was repeated three times; Model 2 (sciatic nerve hemi-cut model): The sciatic nerve was transected with ophthalmic scissors to 50% of its diameter, leaving the remaining half intact; Model 3 (sciatic nerve bilateral hemi-cut model): A 50% transection was first performed on one side of the nerve, followed by a second 50% transection on the opposite side that was 4–5 mm distal to the first incision, ensuring that the intervening nerve segment remained intact; and Model 4 (sciatic nerve transection model): The sciatic nerve was completely transected with ophthalmic scissors. Finally, the muscle and skin layers were sutured.

Transmission electron microscopy

The nerve tissue was removed and placed in an EP tube, fixed for 2 h using a special electron microscope fixative solution (2.5% glutaraldehyde; G1102; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and stored/transported at 4 °C. The sample was prepared according to the standard operating procedures for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sample preparation and observed by a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan) to examine the morphology of the vesicles.

Bioinformatics analysis

We downloaded single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets of intact and injured sciatic nerves (GSE142541 [27], GSE147285 [28] and GSE120678 [17] datasets) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). scRNA-seq data were integrated using Seurat (v5.1.0), following the package’s standard workflow for cross-dataset normalization and batch effect correction.

Fluorescence immunostaining

The sciatic nerves of the mice were dissected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Fluorescent immunostaining was then performed according to the procedures described in our previous studies [26]. Primary antibodies against the following proteins were used for fluorescent immunostaining: PDFGR-α (AF1062, R&D, USA), Ki67 (GB111499, Servicebio), CD90 (GB113753, Servicebio), NF200 (GB12144, Servicebio), S100 (GB11359, Servicebio), TSPAN4 (A10253, ABclonal, China), PGCP (CPQ; GB115185, Servicebio), and PIGK (SC-398611, Santa Cruz, USA); the secondary antibodies used were as follows: CY3-labelled goat anti-rabbit IgG (GB21303, Servicebio), Alexa Fluor 488-labelled goat anti-rabbit IgG (GB25303, Servicebio), Alexa Fluor 488-labelled goat anti-mouse IgG (GB25301, Servicebio), and CY3-labelled donkey anti-goat IgG (GB21404, Servicebio). Additionally, for multiplex fluorescence immunostaining, the following secondary antibodies and corresponding TSA dyes were used: HRP-labelled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (GB23303, Servicebio), iF488-Tyramide (G1231, Servicebio), iF555-Tyramide (G1233, Servicebio), and iF647-Tyramide (G1232, Servicebio).

To assess tenascin-C (TNC) expression in NMLF-derived migrasomes, NMLFs were seeded into confocal dishes and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then stained with a primary antibody against TNC (YT5548, Immunoway, USA), followed by the secondary antibody CY3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG. After staining, 1 µg/mL wheat germ agglutinin 488 (WGA-488; W11261, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used for counterstaining, and the samples were visualized under a confocal microscope (LSM 900, Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective at a magnification of 630×.

Isolation and identification of NMLFs

The isolation procedure for NMLFs was based on a previous protocol [17]. Briefly, sciatic nerves were isolated from 3–5-day-old mice because of the increased survival and proliferation rates of isolated cells under culture conditions and the absence of large amounts of myelin debris during the isolation process. The isolated sciatic nerves were enzymatically digested using a mixture containing collagenase type XI and 0.05% trypsin-EDTA at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of 10% FBS to terminate enzyme activity, after which the nerves were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were cultured to the third passage for flow cytometric analysis of the marker PDGFR-α (135916, BioLegend) and fluorescent immunostaining identification of the markers CD90 (GB113753, Servicebio) and vimentin (GB11192, Servicebio). Additionally, the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation potentials of these cells were evaluated.

WGA staining

To observe the production of NMLF-migrasomes, as described previously [20], cells were seeded into confocal culture dishes for 24 h until they reached 40% confluence. The cells were then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 15 min, washed twice with PBS, and stained with 1 µg/mL WGA-488 and Texas Red-X (W21405, Invitrogen) for 15 min. The generation of retraction fibers and migrasomes was observed using a confocal microscope (LSM 900, Zeiss; STELLARISI5, Leica, Germany) equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective at a magnification of 630×.

Separation and identification of NMLF-derived migrasomes and extracellular vesicles

NMLFs were cultured in 100 mm culture dishes coated with 1 µg/mL fibronectin (F0895, Sigma, USA). When the cell density reached 40–50%, the cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin and collected in 50 mL centrifuge tubes. The samples were centrifuged at 1000× g for 10 min and then at 4000× g for 20 min to remove cells and large quantities of cellular debris. The supernatant was then collected and subjected to migrasome isolation according to our previously established protocol [29]. All subsequent operations were performed at 4 °C. Briefly, the collected supernatant was first filtered through a 0.45 μm filter and then reversed and squeezed with a medical syringe containing PBS. The filtrate was concentrated and purified by centrifugation at 2000× g for 5 min using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), and the resulting migrasomes were stored at -80 °C. The protein concentration of the migrasomes was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (P0012, Beyotime). The morphology of the migrasomes was observed by WGA-488 staining, and their shape and ultrastructure were observed by TEM (JEM-1200EX, Japan). The particle size distribution of the migrasomes was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Mastersizer 3000, UK).

To isolate extracellular vesicles (EVs), NMLFs were seeded into 100 mm culture dishes and cultured in serum-free medium for 24 h, after which the culture supernatant was collected. EVs were then isolated from the supernatant using an exoEasy Maxi Kit (76064, QIAGEN, Germany) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. The isolated EVs were subjected to Western blot analysis to examine the expression of their specific marker, TSG101.

NMLF-derived migrasome treatment

The mouse sciatic nerve crush injury model was established, and the mice were randomly divided into two groups: the PBS group (n = 5) and the migrasome group (n = 5). As previously described [30], we administered injections into the gastrocnemius muscle (in the proximal 2/10 and 3/10 of the calf length, where the intramuscular nerve endings of the gastrocnemius muscle are densely distributed) of 20 µL of PBS and 10 µg of migrasomes dissolved in 20 µL of PBS, respectively, at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days post-injury (dpi). The injection dose of migrasomes was selected from the previously reported dosage for EVs [31]. Sciatic nerve function index assessments were subsequently conducted on Days 7, 14, 21, and 28, as previously reported [22]. The mice were euthanized at 28 days, and neural tissue was collected for haematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and fluorescent immunostaining of NF200 (GB12144, Servicebio) and S100 (GB11359, Servicebio). The area of fluorescence-positive areas in the nerve tissue was measured using ImageJ (version 1.53a; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

A mouse model of sciatic nerve crush injury was established. Migrasomes labelled with the red fluorescent dye PKH26 (C3637S, Beyotime) were injected into the gastrocnemius muscle, with PBS serving as the control treatment. At 24 h post-injection, the IVIS® Lumina III in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to perform in vivo imaging on the injection site and the dissected injured nerve to observe the distribution of fluorescence. Afterwards, the injured nerve tissue was sectioned into frozen slices, and the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Red fluorescence within the nerve tissue was observed using a confocal microscope (Eclipse Ti, Nikon, Japan). On Day 28 post-injection, nerve tissues were dissected and collected for fluorescent immunostaining of S100 (GB11359, Servicebio) and Ki67 (GB111499, Servicebio) or myelin basic protein (MBP, GB12226, Servicebio). Moreover, the gastrocnemius muscles of the mice were dissected, and their wet weights and diameters were measured. The muscle tissues were then subjected to HE staining to evaluate the cross-sectional area of the muscle fibers. The fluorescence intensity of the nerve tissue and muscle area was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53a).

Cell culture

The rat Schwann cell line RSC96 was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Typical Culture Preservation Committee, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Beyotime) and maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

CCK-8 and EdU assays

Following the treatment of RSC96 cells with migrasomes, in vitro functional assays for RSC96 cell proliferation were performed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; BS350C, Biosharp, Hefei, China) and a 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) kit (C0078S, Beyotime).

For the CCK-8 assay, RSC96 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well), cultured overnight, and then incubated with different concentrations of migrasomes for 24 h. CCK-8 reagent was added, the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

For the EdU assay, RSC96 cells were plated in 24-well plates (5 × 10⁴ cells/well), treated with migrasomes for 24 h, and stained with EdU. The group not treated with migrasomes served as the control group. Images were captured using a CKX-53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated as follows: [number of EdU-stained cells (red fluorescence)/total number of DAPI-stained cells (blue fluorescence)] × 100%.

Transwell assays

The effect of migrasomes on RSC96 cell migration was evaluated using Transwell assays, with procedures performed as described in a previous study [32]. In brief, for the Transwell assay, RSC96 cells pretreated with migrasomes (1 × 105 cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium) were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (8 μm pore size; Corning, Cambridge, MA, USA), with the lower chamber containing 600 µL of complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation at 37 ℃, nonmigrated cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed. Cells adhering to the lower surface of the Transwell membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images were captured using an ICC50 HD microscope (Leica).

For chemotaxis assays, the procedure was analogous, but the lower chamber contained culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS and migrasomes, which acted as chemoattractants. After 24 h, nonmigrated cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed. Cells adhering to the lower surface of the Transwell membrane were fixed and stained as previously described, and images were captured using the same microscope.

NMLF-derived migrasome uptake

The migrasomes were labelled with CM-DiL (C1036, Beyotime) and then coincubated for 2 h with RSC96 cells that had been separately labelled with phalloidin (C2201S, Beyotime) and DIO (C1993S, Beyotime). Images were captured using a CKX-53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

WGA-stained migrasomes were added to RSC96 cells, and the uptake of migrasomes by the RSC96 cells was observed using a live-cell real-time imaging analysis system (EOS1; HSCORE, Beijing, China).

After the RSC96 cells were coincubated with CM-DiL-labelled migrasomes for 2 h and 24 h, the cells were harvested and evaluated using a Cytek® Aurora Full Spectrum Flow Cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, USA), and the results were analysed with FlowJo software.

WGA-stained migrasomes were added to a polylysine-coated 24-well plate and allowed to precipitate for 2 h. Then, RSC96 cells were added, and the effect of the migrasomes on the migratory behaviour of the RSC96 cells was observed using a live-cell real-time imaging analysis system (EOS1, HSCORE).

NMLFs were separately infected with the lentiviral vector HBLV-m-Tspan4-3xflag-ZsGreen-PURO and its corresponding control vector HBLV-ZsGreen-PURO (Hanbio, Shanghai, China). Stable cell lines were then selected using puromycin (HB-PU-500, Hanbio). RT–qPCR was performed to verify the TSPAN4 overexpression efficiency. TSPAN4-overexpressing NMLFs were subsequently seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well in fibronectin-coated confocal dishes (YA0571, Solarbio, Beijing, China). After overnight culture, the cells were counterstained with WGA-488 for 20 min and then were washed with PBS. Next, 2 × 103 CM-DiL-labelled RSC96 cells were added for coculture. Images were captured using a live-cell imaging workstation (ConfLive-Cell Microscope Incubation Systems, TOKAI HIT, Japan), and time-lapse videos as well as corresponding three-dimensional rendering were generated. For long-term time-lapse videos, images were acquired continuously for 40 h.

siRNA transfection

Targeted TNC and its control small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were designed and obtained from GenePharma. In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, the siRNA and its control were transfected into NMLFs. TNC expression in NMLFs was examined by Western blotting and RT‒qPCR. The activity and migrasome generation capacity of NMLFs were assessed using CCK-8 and WGA staining. TNC-si-migrasomes (TNC-si-Mig) and the control NC-si-migrasomes (NC-si-Mig) were isolated and purified as described above.

Western blotting

Protein extraction from medium (initial conditioned medium prior to migrasome isolation), migrasomes, EVs and cells was performed for Western blot analysis as previously described [29]. The primary antibodies used included antibodies against TSPAN4 (A10253, ABclonal), PIGK (ab201693, Abcam), EOGT (ab190693, Abcam), PGCP (CPQ; 16601-1-AP, Proteintech), NDST1 (26203-1-AP, Proteintech), TSG101 (ab125011, Abcam), TNC (YT5548, Immunoway), β-actin (4970T, Cell Signalling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) and GAPDH (WJ103, Epizyme, China). Goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (7074P2, Cell Signalling Technology) was used as the secondary antibody. Protein expression levels were quantified by ImageJ (version 1.53a).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) was performed to quantify TNC mRNA expression using a quantitative kit on the Quant Gene 9600 system (Bioer, Hangzhou, China). The reverse transcription kit, quantitative kit, and TNC primers were purchased from GenePharma. The gene expression levels were normalized to the GAPDH mRNA expression levels, and the data were analysed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

All the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data were analysed using Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with GraphPad Software (GraphPad Prism Version 5.0; La Jolla, California, USA). A P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Mesenchymal-like fibroblasts increase in number and produce “tadpole-like” membranous structures after peripheral nerve injury

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that neural mesenchymal-like fibroblasts (NMLFs) reside in peripheral nerves and proliferate after nerve injury [17]. To verify the existence of NMLFs and elucidate their spatiotemporal distribution patterns during nerve regeneration, we established three mouse models of sciatic nerve injury: a crush injury model, a hemi-cut injury model, and a bilateral hemi-cut injury model (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). Platelet-derived growth factor receptor-α (PDGFR-α) is a well-recognized specific marker for identifying multiple mesenchymal stem cell populations in vivo [33], whereas Ki67 is a widely used proliferation marker. To assess NMLF proliferation at the injury site, we harvested nerve tissues at 7 dpi and performed double-fluorescent immunostaining for PDGFR-α and Ki67 (Supplementary Fig. S1C). The results showed that the number of PDGFR-α/Ki67 double-positive cells significantly increased in the injury zones across all three injury models compared with that in intact nerve tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1D), indicating robust proliferation of NMLFs following nerve injury. CD90 (also known as Thy-1) is a surface glycoprotein widely used as a panfibroblast and mesenchymal stem cell marker [34]. We further performed quantitative CD90/PDGFR-α double immunofluorescence staining at 7 days after sciatic nerve crush injury and found a significantly greater number of CD90/PDGFR-α double-positive cells in the injured region than in intact nerve tissue (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. S2), confirming both the mesenchymal-like cellular phenotype of NMLFs and their marked proliferation at the injury site in response to nerve injury, which is in line with previous reports [17]. Notably, tadpole-like membranous structures positive for PDGFR-α (a membrane protein) displaying oval, strip-like, or bead-shaped structures were visualized in the interstitial spaces between NF200-labelled axons and S100-labelled SCs (white arrow, Fig. 1B). Intriguingly, PDGFR-α+ signals were detected within S100-positive SCs (yellow arrow), whereas this phenomenon was rarely observed in NF200-positive axons, suggesting that these structures may be associated with or internalized by SCs (Fig. 1B). To characterize the ultrastructural features of these structures, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on nerve tissues harvested from the injury sites. Distinct tadpole-like membranous structures (red arrow) containing varying numbers of small vesicles and mitochondria (yellow arrow) were identified in the interstitial space; such structures were rarely observed in uninjured nerves (Fig. 1C).

Given that nerve transection triggers the spontaneous formation of “nerve bridges” that connect the proximal and distal stumps, NMLFs have been proposed to represent the bridging/repairing fibroblast population [17, 24]. To elucidate the role of NMLFs in mediating neural bridging, we used sciatic nerve hemi-cut and bilateral hemi-cut models. In both models, PDGFR-α-expressing NMLFs formed connections between nerve stumps, assembling into distinct “bridge-like” structures, whereas S100-labelled SCs and NF200-labelled axons localized on either side of these bridges (Fig. 1D, G, white dashed boxes). This spatial distribution pattern was highly consistent with previous descriptions [17, 24], confirming that NMLFs proliferate and expand to cover and bridge the nerve injury site before SCs and axons. Morphological analysis of NMLF-derived bridges revealed abundant tadpole-like structures distributed throughout the bridge microenvironment (Fig. 1D, G, white arrow), whose morphological features were highly consistent with those observed in the crush injury model (Fig. 1B, white arrow). Quantitative analysis revealed that the average diameter of these membranous structures was 1.57 ± 0.88 μm in the hemi-cut model (Fig. 1E), whereas in the upper and lower compartments of the bilateral hemi-cut model, the average diameters were 1.40 ± 0.80 μm and 1.61 ± 0.75 μm, respectively (Fig. 1H). TEM revealed that these membranous structures exhibited elliptical or tadpole-like morphologies with intraluminal small vesicles; notably, mitochondria were clearly visible in some membranous structures, a feature rarely observed in the uninjured peripheral nerves (Fig. 1F, I).

Collectively, these findings indicate that NMLFs proliferate and expand to cover the nerve injury site and may generate tadpole-like membranous structures that are morphologically distinct from conventionally reported extracellular vesicles (EVs) [35]. Unlike typical EVs, which are generally smaller than 200 nm in diameter, these NMLF-derived vesicular structures are characterized by a significantly larger diameter and are enriched with variable numbers of intraluminal vesicles and mitochondria.

The “tadpole-like” membranous structures produced by NMLFs may represent migrasomes

Given that the NMLFs, the key cellular component involved in the formation of nerve bridges during nerve regeneration, produced tadpole-like membranous structures enriched with intraluminal small vesicles or mitochondria, we next aimed to determine the identity of these structures. Notably, migrasomes are a newly identified category of membranous structures defined by two core features: a diameter of 0.5–3 μm and the presence of small vesicles or mitochondria [18, 36–38]. These characteristics were highly consistent with the morphological and ultrastructural traits of the NMLF-derived structures observed in our study. We therefore hypothesized that the tadpole-like membranous structures derived from NMLFs might be migrasomes.

To test this hypothesis, we performed an integrated analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from mouse sciatic nerve samples, including GSE142541 and GSE147285, for detailed analysis. Specifically, the GSE142541 dataset provided baseline single-cell sequencing data from intact sciatic nerves [27], whereas the GSE147285 dataset contained single-cell sequencing data collected 3 days post-transection [28]. Together, these two datasets constituted a well-matched “pre-injury vs. post-injury” control system, offering critical data support for investigating the transcriptional characteristics of NMLFs following nerve injury. Initially, we utilized the Harmony algorithm to perform batch effect correction on the principal components and subsequently generated unified uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings and conducted graph-based clustering analysis to annotate cell types (Fig. 2A). A total of 9 major cell types were identified, including fibroblasts, Schwann cells (SCs), endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle (VSM) cells, macrophages, pericytes, NK cells, B cells, and T cells (Fig. 2B and C). To clarify the link between NMLFs and migrasome biogenesis, we extracted fibroblast clusters on the basis of the cell type annotation results, sorted PDGFR-α-expressing NMLFs from these clusters, and compared the transcriptomic expression levels of migrasome-related markers (TSPAN4, CPQ, PIGK, NDST1, and EOGT) [39, 40] in NMLFs between the uninjured and injured groups at 3 days post-injury. Unexpectedly, independent analyses revealed significant downregulation of the expression of migrasome-related markers in NMLFs at 3 dpi (Fig. 2D).

To verify this unexpected finding, we selected the three most significantly differentially expressed migrasome-related markers, namely, TSPAN4, CPQ, and PIGK, and performed double-fluorescence immunostaining by pairing each of these markers with PDGFR-α in the mouse sciatic nerve crush and hemi-cut injury models. As shown in Fig. 2E, all the tested migrasome markers (TSPAN4, CPQ, and PIGK) were detected in the tadpole-like or elliptical structures. Notably, TSPAN4 exhibited clear spatial overlap with PDGFR-α, whereas the CPQ and PIGK signals were detected in close proximity to PDGFR-α-positive regions, indicating partial colocalization without strict overlap. This finding was consistent with previous reports showing that different migrasome markers exhibit distinct localizations on migrasomes [39]. Furthermore, the fluorescence intensity of migrasome-related markers (TSPAN4, CPQ, and PIGK) was greater in both injury models than in the uninjured group (Fig. 2E). This finding suggests that despite reduced transcriptome levels, the protein expression of migrasome markers in NMLFs was significantly upregulated at 3 dpi, indicating potential posttranscriptional regulation. Interestingly, in the hemi-cut model, the migrasome marker fluorescence intensity was significantly greater in the proximal nerve stump than in the distal stump (Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these findings are consistent with our hypothesis that the tadpole-like membranous structures produced by NMLFs represent migrasomes.

Isolation and characterization of NMLF-derived migrasomes

We then focused on isolating and identifying NMLFs. Under a dissecting microscope, we isolated sciatic nerve tissue from 3–5-day-old neonatal mice and digested it into a single-cell suspension for primary cell culture (Fig. 3A). Primary culture cells (P0 generation) contained irregularly shaped fibroblasts and spindle-shaped SCs, whereas cells of the P3 generation displayed a homogeneous irregular morphology with cytoplasmic protrusions that grew radially (Fig. 3B). Flow cytometry analysis and fluorescence immunostaining revealed that these cells expressed mesenchymal and fibroblast markers, including PDGFR-α, CD90 and vimentin (Fig. 3C and D). Importantly, these cells exhibited osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation potential under the respective induction conditions (Fig. 3E), confirming their mesenchymal stem cell-like multipotency. These results indicate that we successfully and effectively isolated NMLFs, with the obtained cells having characteristics aligning with previously reported NMLF characteristics [17].

On the basis of the results of in vivo experiments suggesting that NMLFs may be the cells responsible for migrasome biogenesis, we cultured NMLFs in vitro for 24 h in fibronectin-coated confocal dishes, followed by staining with fluorophore-conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA-488 and Texas Red-X) to verify their migrasome-generating capacity. As expected, NMLFs formed retraction fiber networks, with migrasomes localized along the fibers, at branch points, and at the fiber termini (Fig. 3F, white arrow). Next, we isolated the migrasomes using a purification protocol established by our research group [29] (Fig. 3G). WGA staining revealed high-purity NMLF-derived migrasomes, which appeared as spherical or elliptical structures, occasionally with small tail-like appendages resembling “tadpoles” (Fig. 3H). TEM further revealed that the migrasomes were elliptical vesicles with wrinkled surfaces, often associated with small vesicles (resembling “tadpole tails”) and free-floating nanovesicles (Fig. 3I). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the peak size distribution of the purified NMLF-derived migrasomes was approximately 615 nm (Fig. 3J), which was consistent with previously reported migrasome dimensions [40]. Notably, the small vesicles observed in isolated samples may correlate with the formation of migrasome-derived nanoparticles described in our prior work [29]. Western blot analysis confirmed that NMLF-derived migrasomes expressed typical migrasome markers (TSPAN4, PIGK, EOGT, CPQ, and NDST1), while the characteristic EV marker TSG101 was undetectable (Fig. 3K, Supplementary Fig. S3), ruling out EV contamination (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Furthermore, to fully validate the enrichment efficiency of the migrasomes, we performed additional Western blot assays to compare the expression levels of migrasome-related markers between the initial conditioned medium prior to migrasome isolation and the purified migrasome fraction. The results demonstrated that migrasome-related marker proteins were barely detectable in the initial conditioned medium compared with those in the purified migrasome fraction (Supplementary Fig. S4B), indicating that migrasomes were highly enriched and concentrated during the purification process.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that NMLF-derived migrasomes were successfully isolated and purified with high efficiency.

NMLF-derived migrasomes promote nerve regeneration and motor function recovery after peripheral nerve injury in mice

Given the established role of NMLFs in facilitating the repair and regeneration of injured tissues [17], we next investigated whether NMLF-derived migrasomes similarly enhance nerve regeneration and functional recovery following peripheral nerve injury. To address this, we established a mouse model of sciatic nerve crush injury and designed experimental groups, injection protocols, functional assessments, and sample collection procedures, as outlined in Fig. 4A.

We evaluated motor function recovery in the injured sciatic nerve via mouse gait analysis. Representative walking footprints of mice in each group recorded at different time points are presented in Fig. 4B. Functional evaluations revealed that hindlimb paw spreading on the operated side was significantly improved in the migrasome-treated group compared with that in the PBS control group at multiple time points, a trend confirmed by quantitative analysis of the sciatic functional index (SFI) (Fig. 4F). Anatomical observations revealed that the nerve crush site remained visible in the PBS group at 28 days post-operation, whereas nerve morphology in the migrasome-treated group had largely normalized (Fig. 4C). To evaluate the regulatory role of migrasomes in nerve regeneration, we performed haematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining on longitudinal sections of the injured nerves. The results revealed that nerve fiber continuity was better in the migrasome-treated group than in the control group (Fig. 4D). Moreover, fluorescent immunostaining revealed larger areas positive for S100 and NF200 (Fig. 4E, G and H), as well as a significant increase in the number of S100 and Ki67 double-positive cells in the migrasome-treated group (Supplementary Fig. S5A), indicating significantly enhanced SC proliferation activation and axonal regeneration processes. Crucially, NF200-positive axons in the migrasome-treated group exhibited morphologically continuous and neatly aligned structures, with most showing coexpression with S100-positive SCs; this phenomenon indicates the successful formation of myelinated structures by SCs. Fluorescent immunostaining for MBP further validated this finding (Supplementary Fig. S5B), confirming that migrasome intervention significantly promotes axonal regeneration and myelin formation in injured sciatic nerves.

Additionally, considering our use of intramuscular injection into the gastrocnemius muscle to deliver the migrasomes, it was necessary to further clarify whether the migrasomes exert their neuroregenerative effects by directly regulating muscle function or by travelling via retrograde axonal transport pathways to the site of peripheral nerve injury. To address this, we labelled migrasomes using the red fluorescent dye PKH26 (Supplementary Fig. S6A) and administered labelled migrasomes to model mice via intramuscular injection in a mouse model of sciatic nerve crush injury. In vivo fluorescence imaging of the animals revealed that fluorescent signals were clearly detectable at both the intramuscular injection sites and in the dissected nerve tissues at 24 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. S6B and C). Subsequent cryosectioning and observation of nerve tissues revealed that distinct red fluorescent signals were visible in the nerve tissues of the migrasome-treated group compared with those of the PBS control group (Supplementary Fig. S6D). These results demonstrate that PKH26-labelled migrasomes could successfully reach nerve tissues following intramuscular injection of the gastrocnemius.

To investigate the potential effects of migrasome injection on muscle tissue, we further measured the wet weight ratio and diameter ratio of the gastrocnemius muscle in mice. The migrasome-treated group exhibited significant increases in both parameters compared with those in the PBS control group (Supplementary Fig. S6E–G). Furthermore, HE staining analysis revealed a significant increase in the cross-sectional area of the gastrocnemius muscle fibers in the migrasome-treated group compared with that in the PBS group (Supplementary Fig. S6H and I). These findings indicated that migrasome treatment significantly ameliorated muscle atrophy.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that NMLF-derived migrasomes administered via gastrocnemius intramuscular injection can reach injured nerve tissues and are associated with enhanced nerve regeneration and motor function recovery.

NMLF-derived migrasomes promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration

Previous studies have shown that migrasomes can regulate the functional phenotype of recipient cells through content transfer [41]. Given the critical role of fibroblast–SC crosstalk in peripheral nerve regeneration [4, 24], we further explored the regulatory effects of NMLF-derived migrasomes on SCs. To determine the optimal migrasome concentration, RSC96 cells were treated with varying concentrations, and cell viability was assessed using a CCK8 assay (Fig. 5A). The results revealed a concentration-dependent increase in RSC96 cell activity, with 10 µg/mL yielding the most significant effect (Fig. 5B); therefore, this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments.

To verify the internalization of migrasomes by SCs, we cocultured phalloidin- and DIO-stained RSC96 cells with CM-DiL-labelled migrasomes. The results revealed that migrasomes localized within RSC96 cells (Fig. 5C). Live-cell imaging analysis of WGA-labelled migrasomes clearly revealed the dynamic process of migrasome uptake by RSC96 cells (Supplementary Video S1). Moreover, quantitative analysis by flow cytometry revealed that the migrasome uptake level of RSC96 cells increased with increasing coincubation time (Fig. 5D), which further confirmed that SCs can efficiently internalize migrasomes.

Next, we examined the effect of the migrasomes on SC function. EdU incorporation assays demonstrated that migrasomes significantly increased RSC96 cell proliferation (Fig. 5E and F). Transwell assays revealed a marked increase in RSC96 cell migration upon migrasome treatment (Fig. 5G and H). Notably, when migrasomes were placed in the lower chamber of Transwell inserts (with RSC96 cells seeded in the upper chamber), no significant increase in migration was observed (Fig. 5I and J), which was inconsistent with the previously reported role of migrasomes as classical “signal localizers” [32].

To further clarify the effect of migrasomes on the migratory behaviour of Schwann cells, we seeded WGA-labelled migrasomes in fibronectin-coated 24-well plates, followed by the addition of RSC96 cells for real-time dynamic observation. The results revealed that during dynamic migration, the green fluorescence signal of RSC96 cells progressively intensified (Supplementary Video S2), suggesting that migrasomes may guide the migration trajectory of RSC96 cells. To exclude the randomness of the direction of RSC96 cell migration, we first verified the transfection efficiency of the TSPAN4-ZsGreen-overexpressing lentivirus in NMLFs via RT‒qPCR (Supplementary Fig. S7), followed by the seeding of successfully transfected NMLFs (NMLF-TSPAN4-ZsGreen) in confocal dishes and incubation for 24 h. Considering the potentially insufficient fluorescence intensity of ZsGreen, we further counterstained NMLF-TSPAN4-ZsGreen cells with WGA-488 and then cocultured them with CM-DiL-labelled RSC96 cells for 24 h. The imaging results demonstrated that RSC96 cells could actively internalize NMLF-derived migrasomes (Supplementary Video S3–S4). More importantly, long-term time-lapse imaging revealed that RSC96 cells exhibited directional migration rather than random movement. The cells actively reoriented during continuous migration, ultimately migrating along migrasome tracks and internalizing the migrasomes (Fig. 5K, Supplementary Video S5).

Collectively, these findings suggest that NMLF-derived migrasomes are internalized by SCs to promote their proliferation and, importantly, not only increase their migration capacity but also direct their migration trajectories.

NMLF-derived migrasomes promote Schwann cell proliferation and migration via TNC

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which NMLF-derived migrasomes regulate SC proliferation and migration, we analysed scRNA-seq datasets from the sciatic nerves of uninjured mice and those at 3 and 9 dpi [17, 28]. We isolated clusters of PDGFR-α-expressing fibroblasts (NMLFs) and SCs and then performed intersection analysis on genes with co-upregulated expression in both cell types at 3 and 9 dpi (Fig. 6A). Venn diagram analysis revealed 1482 shared upregulated genes (Fig. 6B). Using the DAVID bioinformatics tool, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses on these genes. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in biological processes such as the regulation of cell migration and the positive regulation of cell population proliferation (Fig. 6C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment in multiple signalling pathways (Fig. 6D), with focal adhesion and PI3K–Akt signalling ranking among the top 10 pathways, emerging as key candidates because of their known roles in regulating cell proliferation and migration [42].

To identify the core genes governing SC functional phenotypes, we used Metascape to construct a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network of genes enriched in these pathways. Tenascin-C (TNC), an extracellular matrix glycoprotein, was identified as a central node in this network (Fig. 6E). Previous studies have demonstrated that TNC regulates SC migration during peripheral nerve regeneration, is predominantly localized in fibroblasts within the injured nerve region and is rarely detected in other nonneuronal cells, including SCs [15]. ScRNA-seq dataset analysis revealed that TNC expression was upregulated in both fibroblasts and SCs (Fig. 6B), whereas our in vitro experiments revealed that treating RSC96 cells with NMLF-derived migrasomes did not alter TNC mRNA expression levels (Fig. 6N). TNC exerts its biological functions primarily through its protein from and is highly evolutionarily conserved at the protein level across species (Supplementary Fig. S8), and migrasomes are well-documented to mediate the active transport of intracellular proteins [41]. Fluorescence immunostaining confirmed the localization of TNC within migrasomes (Fig. 6F). We hypothesized that NMLF-derived migrasomes might regulate SC functional phenotypes by transferring the TNC protein.

To validate TNC as a key mediator, we first knocked down TNC expression in NMLFs. Four TNC-targeting siRNAs (si1–si4, corresponding to siRNA331, 1302, 1746, and 1953) were designed to minimize off-target effects. Quantitative real-time PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that si3 (siRNA1746) achieved the most efficient knockdown (Fig. 6G, Supplementary Fig. S9A) and was selected for subsequent experiments. Notably, TNC depletion in NMLFs did not affect cell viability or migrasome biogenesis (Fig. 6H and I), indicating the generation of TNC-knockdown migrasomes (TNC-si-Mig) from NMLFs (Fig. 6J and J1, Supplementary Fig. S9B). Next, we compared the effects of control migrasomes (NC-si-Mig) and TNC-si-Mig on SCs. EdU incorporation and Transwell assays revealed that TNC knockdown significantly attenuated the migrasome-mediated promotion of RSC96 cell proliferation and migration (Fig. 6K, L and L1). Importantly, treatment with NMLF-derived migrasomes increased TNC protein levels in RSC96 cells without altering TNC mRNA expression (Fig. 6M and N, Supplementary Fig. S9C), indicating that SCs acquire TNC via migrasome-mediated protein transfer rather than mRNA transcription. Furthermore, to confirm that the elevated TNC protein levels in RSC96 cells were indeed derived from internalized migrasomes, we treated RSC96 cells with TNC-si-Mig or migrasomes. As shown in Supplementary Figure S10, the increase in TNC protein in the TNC-si-Mig-treated group was significantly attenuated compared with that in the migrasome-treated group; moreover, no significant difference was observed between this group and the untreated control group.

Overall, these results indicate that the TNC protein is required for the NMLF-derived migrasome-mediated promotion of SC proliferation and migration.

Discussion

Repair of peripheral nerve injury is a complex process involving interactions between multiple types of nonneuronal cells, including SCs and fibroblasts [4]. Historically, research has focused on dedifferentiated SCs, which proliferate and migrate in an orderly manner to form Büngner bands, guiding axonal growth across nerve bridges and restoring functional connectivity [43]. However, the role of fibroblasts, key components of the injury microenvironment, has long been overlooked, and these cells have been dismissed as mere supportive cells. In this study, we highlight the active functional role of fibroblasts, specifically PDGFR-α-expressing NMLFs, in peripheral nerve regeneration. To our knowledge, our findings provide the first evidence that NMLF-derived migrasomes regulate SC proliferation and migration. These findings not only expand our understanding of fibroblast function in nerve repair but also provide a new framework for analysing cellular crosstalk during tissue regeneration.

Following peripheral nerve injury, fibroblasts accumulate at the injury site to form nerve bridges, which facilitate the coordinated collective migration of SCs [24], a process that redefines fibroblasts as active participants in repair rather than passive bystanders. Subsequent studies have confirmed that these repair-competent fibroblasts are mesenchymal-like cells characterized by the expression of mesenchymal markers [17]. Building on this characterization, our in vivo observations in a sciatic nerve crush model revealed abundant NMLFs (coexpressing PDGFR-α and CD90) at the injury site. Notably, across sciatic nerve injury models of varying severity, we observed that NMLF-formed nerve bridges contained numerous large vesicles (> 0.5 μm in diameter) with distinct morphological features, including variable numbers of internal small vesicles and encapsulated mitochondria. Given this spatial association with NMLFs, we hypothesized that these vesicles originated from NMLFs. Further observations revealed their association with SCs, suggesting possible internalization and raising a critical question: Could these vesicles serve as key mediators of fibroblast–SC communication during nerve regeneration?

Migrasomes, recently identified as vesicular structures secreted by migrating cells at the termini or bifurcations of retraction fibers [18], align well with this description. Defined as single-membrane structures (0.5–3 μm in diameter) enriched with small vesicles and mitochondria [18, 36–38], migrasomes match the morphological features of the vesicles observed in our models. Because fibroblasts act as pioneer cells leading to migration during peripheral nerve regeneration [44] and our data confirmed that NMLFs express migrasome-specific markers, we propose that the abundant vesicles in nerve bridges represent migrasomes released by migrating NMLFs. However, it is worth noting that an apparent discrepancy exists between the single-cell sequencing data, which revealed reduced mRNA levels of migrasome-related markers (TSPAN4, CPQ, and PIGK) in NMLFs, and the immunofluorescence staining, which revealed increased protein levels of these markers following nerve injury. We attribute this discrepancy to the well-documented moderate correlation between mRNA and protein abundance [45, 46], particularly in dynamic biological processes where posttranscriptional regulation plays a significant role [47]. Specifically, following nerve injury, the translational efficiency of the remaining TSPAN4, CPQ, and PIGK transcripts may be increased and/or the half-lives of these proteins may be prolonged, leading to protein accumulation despite reduced transcript levels. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that protein levels are determined not only by transcriptional output but also by translational efficiency and protein stability [45, 48]. We acknowledge that this hypothesis requires direct experimental validation (e.g., Ribo-seq or polysome profiling for translational efficiency and cycloheximide chase assays for protein stability) and represents a key direction for our future studies. Additionally, we observed that NMLFs in the proximal stump presented significantly elevated protein levels of migrasome-related markers (TSPAN4, CPQ, and PIGK) in a mouse hemi-cut injury model. Previous studies have demonstrated that fibroblasts proliferate and migrate from the proximal to distal stump much faster than other cell types involved in nerve repair during nerve regeneration [1]. We therefore speculate that the elevated protein levels of migrasome-related markers in proximal NMLFs reflect increased migrasome production capacity, a phenomenon that may be associated with their migratory behaviour. Nevertheless, this speculation awaits future validation, such as dynamic observation of NMLF migration behaviour using Ki67 tracking.

Emerging evidence indicates that migrasomes contribute to pathological processes [32, 40, 49], yet their functions in physiological tissue repair remain largely unexplored. Our study highlights the emerging role of migrasomes in peripheral nerve regeneration, in which in vivo experiments demonstrated that NMLF-derived migrasomes significantly promoted nerve regeneration and functional recovery, underscoring their important contribution to peripheral nerve repair. In vitro assays further confirmed that these migrasomes were internalized by SCs, increasing SC proliferation and migration, which was consistent with their role as intercellular communication mediators [19]. Notably, the SCs migrated along migrasome release trajectories while they internalized vesicles, a mechanism distinct from the previously reported roles of migrasomes as active “signalling effectors” that mediate cell positioning [32]. Instead, our findings suggest that these trajectories serve as physical scaffolds to passively guide SC migration. This finding resembles recent observations in the tumour microenvironment, in which migrasome trajectories from tumour-associated fibroblasts guided subsequent tumour cell migration [50], suggesting a common migrasome-mediated guidance principle across tissue contexts. Collectively, our findings support a model in which migrating NMLFs release migrasomes to form trajectories, thereby providing physical cues for directional SC migration and optimizing guidance during nerve regeneration.

We encountered a seemingly paradoxical question: why can SCs still migrate across the Transwell membrane without migrasome trajectories? This can be explained by two distinct modes of migrasome action on SCs, as suggested by our experimental results. The first is a “trail-dependent guidance” mode, in which SCs migrate along migrasome trails via spatial physical guidance (Fig. 5K, Supplementary Video S5). The second is a “contact-dependent priming” mode, in which migrasomes increase the intrinsic migratory capacity of SCs through direct interaction or proximity, without the need for preformed trials. The ability of SCs to migrate across the Transwell membrane in the absence of migrasome trajectories (Fig. 5G–H, upper chamber coculture) is achieved through this second mechanism, whereas the lack of migration towards migrasomes in the lower chamber (Fig. 5I–J) indicates that this priming effect requires proximal contact and does not operate via long-range chemotaxis. Thus, these two mechanisms are not contradictory but represent complementary modes of action under different microenvironmental contexts: the contact-dependent priming mode functions in settings allowing direct migrasome–SC interactions (e.g., Transwell upper chamber coculture), whereas the trail-dependent guidance mode is predominant in substrates permitting migrasome deposition and trail formation (e.g., fibronectin-coated dishes).

Tenascin-C (TNC), a highly conserved extracellular matrix glycoprotein with diverse roles in tumour metastasis [51] and axonal growth [52], has emerged as a key mediator in this process. Previous studies have shown that fibroblast-derived TNC promotes SC migration via integrin β1 in a paracrine manner [15], suggesting that TNC is a critical molecular target for fibroblast–SC crosstalk. Our group previously demonstrated that intracellular proteins can be actively transported to migrasomes [41]; in this study, we extended this finding by showing that TNC is abundant in NMLF-derived migrasomes. Importantly, TNC knockdown did not affect NMLF viability or migrasome biogenesis, indicating that migrasome formation is TNC independent. However, TNC depletion significantly attenuated the migrasome-mediated promotion of SC proliferation and migration. Furthermore, migrasome treatment increased TNC protein levels (but not mRNA levels) in SCs, confirming that NMLF-derived migrasomes regulate SC function through TNC protein transfer.

Despite these insights, several limitations warrant consideration. First, our focus on TNC overlooks the complexity of migrasomes, which are enriched with DNA [53], miRNAs [29], mitochondria [36–38], and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) [54, 55]. The roles of these uncharacterized molecules in regulating SC function require further validation. Second, while we used a standardized migrasome dosage and administration regimen in animal models, the optimal therapeutic dose and frequency for promoting nerve regeneration remain undefined. Third, despite the observed accumulation of NMLF-derived migrasomes in the injured peripheral nerve following intramuscular injection into the gastrocnemius muscle, the molecular mechanism underlying this targeted transport remains to be elucidated. Finally, our studies in a mouse sciatic nerve crush model do not address long-distance nerve defects, which is a major clinical challenge. Integrating migrasomes with tissue engineering strategies (e.g., biodegradable scaffolds and conductive polymers) to construct functionalized nerve guidance conduits may offer a solution, but their efficacy and translational potential require exploration.

Conclusion

In summary, our study revealed that NMLF-derived migrasomes promote peripheral nerve regeneration by increasing SC proliferation and migration, a process mediated in part by TNC protein delivery (Fig. 7). These findings illuminate a mechanism of migrasome-mediated crosstalk between NMLFs and SCs in the context of peripheral nerve injury and highlight the therapeutic potential of migrasomes for nerve repair.
