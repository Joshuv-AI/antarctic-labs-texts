Testicular Biopsies in Adolescent and Adult Andrological Patients: The EAA Clinical Guidelines
Authors: Lise Aksglaede, George Kanakis, Ewa Rajpert‐De Meyts, Davor Jezek, Ferran Algaba, Gert Dohle, Jolanta Slowikowska‐Hilczer, Sabine Kliesch, Hermann M Behre, Csilla Krausz, Dimitrios G Goulis, Niels Jørgensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHistological evaluation of testicular tissue is central to the assessment of infertile men, particularly those at an increased risk of testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT). Traditionally, testicular biopsies have been used primarily for diagnostic purposes, such as the detection of germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS). With advances in assisted reproductive technologies, testicular biopsies may also serve a therapeutic role, including testicular sperm extraction (TESE or microsurgical TESE) in men with nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA).ObjectiveTo provide clinical practice guidelines on the indications for testicular biopsies for diagnostic and combined therapeutic–diagnostic purposes, including surgical aspects, tissue preparation and staining, and interpretation of histological findings.MethodsEvidence‐based recommendations were developed following a systematic literature search of English‐language publications and graded using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system. The suggestions and recommendations were thoroughly discussed by the guidelines’ authors, who are experienced in testicular biopsies in a clinical andrology setting. Subsequently, the guidelines were approved by the Directors of 29 andrological centers accredited by the European Academy of Andrology (EAA).ResultsA total of 34 recommendations were formulated.ConclusionTesticular biopsies remain an essential tool for both the diagnostic evaluation of men at risk of TGCT and therapeutic sperm retrieval in NOA. Careful patient selection, appropriate preoperative evaluation, and follow‐up of reproductive hormones are crucial to optimize outcomes and minimize risks.

Introduction

Histological evaluation of testicular tissue has been performed since the early 20th century. With today's advanced immunohistochemical (IHC) techniques, it still has a strong place in clinical practice in the evaluation of infertile men with a predisposition to testicular germ cell tumor (TGCT) [1].

Testicular biopsies have mainly been performed for diagnostic purposes (diagnostic biopsies) to describe spermatogenesis and Leydig cells and to identify germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS), formerly known as carcinoma in situ (CIS) or testicular intraepithelial neoplasia (TIN) of the testes. With the advent of assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs), testicular biopsies are now also used therapeutically to retrieve spermatozoa with the techniques of testicular sperm extraction (TESE) or microsurgical TESE (mTESE) for sperm cryopreservation and use in intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). In the case of TESE/mTESE, the procedure is combined with diagnostic biopsies [2, 3].

In this guideline, we will discuss indications for testis biopsies for pure diagnostic (preventive) purposes and for combined therapeutic and diagnostic (TESE) purposes, including technical aspects of surgery, tissue preparation and staining, and the interpretation of the findings. This guideline does not include the evaluation of patients with undetermined intratesticular masses revealed by ultrasonography and a suspected TGCT.

Methodology

Data Search

The European Academy of Andrology (EAA) guidelines committee commissioned an expert task force of Academicians to provide a European perspective on the performance and interpretation of testicular biopsy in adolescent and adult patients.

Following scrutiny and discussion of the best evidence from published literature available in electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, CENTRAL, Embase), the authors generated a series of consensus recommendations according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system [4].

Levels of Evidence and Grades of Recommendation

According to GRADE, “1” denotes a strong recommendation described with the phrase “we recommend.” In contrast, “2” indicates a weaker recommendation and is described with the phrase “we suggest.” The quality of evidence is expressed through graphical descriptions: ØOOO denotes “very low‐quality evidence,” ØØOO “low quality,” ØØØO “moderate quality,” and ØØØØ “high quality.” Recommendations based on good clinical practice (GCP) represent consensus‐based guidance intended to support clinical decision‐making when high‐quality evidence is unavailable.

Each section of the current guideline is divided into four parts: “Recommendations” presents the list of guidelines for the corresponding section, formatted according to the GRADE system; “Evidence” briefly summarizes the best evidence that justifies the grading of each recommendation; “Values” refers to priorities set by the writing team; and finally, “Remarks” discusses technical issues and identifies areas of uncertainty.

Evidence (Diagnostic Biopsies for Preventive Purposes)

Testicular Germ Cell Tumors and GCNIS

TGCTs account for only 1% of all male cancers but are the most common cancer in young men of European ancestry [5]. The incidence of TGCTs increases from late adolescence, peaks in young adulthood typically during the third decade of life and then declines to very low rates by around age 60 [6, 7]. There are large ethnic differences in the incidence of TGCT, with one‐third of cases occurring in Europe and the highest age‐standardized incidence rates of more than 10:100,000 in Norway, Slovenia, Germany, and Denmark [8] and as high as 17:100,000 in the Faroe Islands [9]. Seminomas and nonseminomas account for approximately 90% of TGCT, with GCNIS as the common precursor [10, 11, 12, 13]. Patients with unilateral TGCT are at increased risk of developing a metachronous contralateral TGCT with an incidence of approximately 12–18 times higher compared with general population rates of testicular cancer corresponding to a 20‐year cumulative incidence between 2% and 5% [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Morphologically, GCNIS cells are large gonocyte‐like cells expressing embryonic pluripotency factors such as OCT4/POU5F1, NANOG, and AP2‐gamma/TFAP2C. Fetal gonocytes are precursors that asynchronously differentiate to spermatogonia during late fetal life and early postnatal life [13, 20]. GCNIS may develop during late childhood or puberty as a result of the failure of gonocyte differentiation into spermatogonia, and subsequently, the “delayed” gonocytes undergo genomic changes and further transform and proliferate. Therefore, reliable detection of GCNIS may not be possible before adulthood, and a biopsy during childhood or adolescence is not considered representative for excluding GCNIS [21].

GCNIS has been identified in 4%–7% of patients with TGCT in the contralateral testicle, and it is expected that it will progress to TGCT in all patients unless treated [22, 23, 24]. Infertility, a history of cryptorchidism, small testicular volume and young age are associated with a higher risk of the presence of GCNIS in the contralateral testis in men with TGCT [22, 25, 26].

As GCNIS is treatable, identifying it offers the chance of preventing invasive testicular cancer [11, 27]. Treatment depends on the extent of malignancy. Localized low‐dose irradiation is recommended in patients with bilateral neoplasia—either orchiectomized due to unilateral TGCT with contralateral GCNIS or bilateral GCNIS. In patients having two testicles but unilateral GCNIS, the treatment of choice is orchiectomy of the affected testicle [28]. Active surveillance can be considered in men with unfulfilled wishes for a child or being under fertility treatment with stable residual sperm production. Cryopreservation of sperm should be considered, as spermatogenesis may decline over time.

Risk of Testicular Germ Cell Malignancy

Published estimates of TGCT risk among patients referred to andrology clinics for fertility problems vary considerably, reflecting differences in study design, cohort size, patient selection, and biopsy methodology (including sample size, number of biopsies, and use of IHC markers; Table 1). Most studies agree that there is an increased risk of TGCT in men with reduced spermatogenesis, small testes, TM, inhomogeneous testicular parenchyma, and a history of undescended testis or familial TGCT (Table 2). In addition, anogenital distance (AGD) is on average shorter in patients with TGCT [29, 30], and some studies [31, 32] but not all [33, 34] detected an association between hypospadias and risk of TGCT. A testicular volume less than 6 mL (determined by palpation using an orchidometer) should be considered subnormal [35]. All the disorders mentioned above are associated with each other and linked to disturbed fetal development of the testis, which is the basis of the concept of testicular dysgenesis syndrome (TDS) [36]. However, there is general agreement among the authors of this guideline that a patient should have at least three of the risk factors listed in Table 2 to be considered at increased risk of GCNIS, with the inhomogeneity of testicular tissue on ultrasound carrying the greatest weight [37].

One of the first meta‐analyses that examined the published data on the prevalence of GCNIS in diagnostic biopsies performed in infertile men (nine studies, 9065 men) calculated the overall prevalence as 0.74% (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.6–0.9) [58]. An undescended testis in adulthood increased the prevalence of GCNIS to 2.9% (95% CI, 1.9–4.1) [58]. A Danish study of 453 consecutive patients biopsied because of infertility detected GCNIS in 2.2% (95% CI, 1.1–4.0), which was compared to the estimated 0.45% TGCT risk in the age‐ and birth‐cohort‐matched general Danish population [51]. In addition, a more recent Danish study found no cases of GCNIS in 58 biopsied azoospermic men, but in the total cohort of 1213 infertile men included in that study, 26 (2.1%) patients had been diagnosed with TGCT, including seven cases of TGCT and/or GCNIS diagnosed during the work‐up for infertility [60]. Accordingly, in a large Australian cohort of 350,835 men, cryptorchidism and the use of ARTs more than doubled the risk of TGCT (HR: 2.43; 95% CI, 1.65–3.58 and RR: 2.26; 95% CI, 1.58–3.25, respectively) [34]. It is important to underline that the detection of GCNIS strongly depends on the selection of the patients in a given study (see Table 1) and the number of tissue samples; unilateral versus bilateral testis biopsy, and single‐site versus multiple biopsies [22, 24].

Testicular Microlithiasis

The risk of TGCT in association with TM has been discussed for decades, and the controversy has been partly caused by differences in the definition of TM and the detection method. Testicular microliths are classified by testicular ultrasonography as hyperechogenic lesions between 1 and 3 mm in diameter without shadowing [61]. The definition of TM varies between studies. In some, TM is defined as the presence of one or more microliths [62], three or more microliths [63], or five or more microliths per view (visual field) [49, 64, 65, 66]. Some use a subclassification into classic or limited TM according to the presence of more or less than five microliths per view, respectively [67]. In contrast, others have subclassified classic TM into three categories (5–10, 10–20 and > 20 microliths per view) [68]. In these studies, the gradings were not associated with the risk of coexisting TGCT. Therefore, Van Casteren et al. suggested that all hyperechogenic foci on testicular ultrasound smaller than 3 mm without shadowing should be classified as TM, independent of the number per view or in the total testis [61]. However, more recently, The European Society of Urogenital Radiology recommended the definition of TM as five or more microliths per field of view based on the assumption that clustering of the microliths is an indication of an “unstable” area in the testis with increased risk of GCNIS [35].

Population‐based studies including 1504 American and 2179 Turkish army recruits and 480 Danish male conscripts have estimated the prevalence of TM to be 5.6% [69], 2.4% [70], and 1.1% [71], respectively. TM is associated with infertility [72]. In reviews focusing on infertile men, the mean prevalence of TM reported varied between 2.4% among 5899 men [61], 8.3% among 5228 men [73]. Several studies have assessed the association between TM and the risk of GCNIS [49, 62, 65, 66, 74] and the risk of TGCT [63, 64, 67, 75] in infertile men. One meta‐analysis of 11 studies estimated the relative risk of TGCT as close to 13 (RR: 12.7; 95% CI, 8.18–19.71; p < 0.001) [76]. Another more recent meta‐analysis of data from eight studies, including 180 infertile men with TM and 5088 men without TM, detected an OR of TGCT of 18.1 (95% CI, 8.09–40.55; p < 0.0001) [77]. The commonly accepted recommendation is that TM is only an indication for performing regular scrotal ultrasound or diagnostic biopsies if at least one additional risk factor for TGCT is present [35, 61, 78]. A more recent study, however, showed that only an atrophic testicle ipsilateral to TM or the coexistence of subfertility with bilateral TM was associated with GCNIS/TGCT [79].

Differences of Sex Development

Patients with disorders/differences of sex development (DSD) and a Y chromosome, or a part encompassing the SRY and other genes (for instance TSPY) within the so‐called gonadoblastoma (GBY) locus, have a higher risk of TGCT. Excluded from this are patients with Klinefelter syndrome who are at increased risk of mediastinal germ cell tumors, but not TGCT [80, 81, 82]. The risk estimate varies greatly from study to study, and solid estimates are difficult to provide. However, at risk are especially those with poor testes differentiation (risk: XY gonadal dysgenesis 30%–50% or mosaicism, e.g., 45, X/46, XY 15%–20%), androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS; risk: complete AIS 1%–3%, partial AIS 15%–20%), and androgen biosynthesis defects (risk: 17βHSD 17%, other unknown) [82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87]. In a considerable number of these patients, gonadectomy is performed in early childhood for the prevention of future malignancy. However, in patients with relatively good testicular development, preserved hormonal function and lack of GCNIS in a biopsy taken prepubertally, gonads are preserved. In conclusion, biopsies should be recommended in young adults with DSD conditions mentioned above and Y chromosome material [82, 84, 87]. The so‐called gr/gr deletion of the Y chromosome has been reported as genetic risk factor for TGCT by two large, multicenter studies [88, 89]. The most recent study, including 497 TGCT patients and 2030 controls from the Mediterranean area, reported a threefold increased risk of TGCT being associated with the variant (OR: 3.1; 95% CI, 1.4–7.0) [89]. However, the large meta‐analysis of international datasets comprising 2231 patients with TGCT and 196,075 controls did not detect an increased risk of gr/gr deletion, suggesting that this variant alone is not predictive of TGCT risk [90].

Evidence—TESE and mTESE

In patients with NOA, TESE is accepted as the standard method for sperm retrieval if no causal treatment of azoospermia is available or possible (see also contraindications). In contrast, TESA may be considered a less invasive procedure in patients with OA. Harvesting motile sperm from the epididymis may be considered in the hands of experienced clinicians if reconstructive surgery is available or intended. mTESE may be considered for sperm retrieval in patients with a history of bilateral cryptorchidism, testicular volume below 6 mL, Klinefelter syndrome, in cases carrying a pathogenic variant in genes associated with reduced spermatogenesis as well as prior TESE failure [91, 92, 93, 94, 95, 96].

Previously, percutaneous testicular fine needle aspiration (TEFNA) or TESA was applied in NOA [97, 98]. However, in controlled animal models, it was shown that the procedure had deleterious effects in the seminiferous tubules with chronic inflammation and necrosis after multiple punctures—and low outcome of sperm retrieval (14%) compared to TESE (50%) [99, 100, 101]. The inferiority of these procedures has been shown in men with azoospermia and in men with cryptozoospermia [102]. Hence, TESA is not recommended for sperm retrieval in men with NOA [3].

A meta‐analysis including men with NOA showed comparable sperm retrieval rates (SRRs) with 47% after TESE and mTESE, respectively. A subgroup analysis revealed an advantage for the microsurgical approach in patients with Klinefelter syndrome and men with reduced testis volume (including testes with Sertoli cell‐only [SCO]) or tubular atrophy confirmed by histological analysis [103, 104]. Pregnancy rates from 42 trials with a cumulative pregnancy rate of 29% per ICSI cycle, resulting in 569 live births (cumulative live birth rate of 24% per ICSI cycle), with an abortion rate of 19%, were comparable in both groups. However, only one study was a randomized controlled trial; thus, the quality of the studies included in the meta‐analysis has strong limitations [103].

Although azoospermia is the typical finding in men with Klinefelter syndrome, spermatogenesis—when present—is confined to isolated foci [105] rather than the randomly distributed seminiferous tubules seen in men with NOA. From a theoretical point of view, it therefore seems most reasonable to perform mTESE as the primary procedure in patients with Klinefelter syndrome. A meta‐analysis from 2017 compared SRR, pregnancy rates and live birth rates in 37 studies, including 1248 patients with Klinefelter syndrome, and did not detect a difference when comparing SRR of TESE (43%) and mTESE (45%) [106]. A more recent systematic review confirmed this finding [107]. In that review, eight studies (n = 441 TESE procedures) were compared to 15 studies (n = 1007 mTESE procedures) showing SRR of 37.2% and 34.3% in the two groups, respectively. However, SRR varies greatly between the included studies, that is, 14%–48% in studies of TESE and 20%–69% in mTESE. This discrepancy between studies was pointed out by Boeri et al. in their “real‐life” study of 103 patients with Klinefelter syndrome undergoing TESE or mTESE with an overall SRR of 21.4%, raising the question of the reliability of the results of the meta‐analysis [108]. Most studies are retrospective, and only a few prospective studies have been performed, which may influence the results and induce a risk of selection bias. Thus, until further clarification appears, we will favor mTESE for patients with Klinefelter syndrome.

After TESE‐failure the so‐called salvage mTESE has successfully retrieved sperm in approximately 40%–50% of cases [108, 109, 110, 111, 112]. In one study, patients who underwent salvage mTESE presented with histological diagnosis of hypospermatogenesis, maturational arrest or SCO, and spermatozoa were found in 75%, 57.1%, and 25% of these categories, respectively [112].

Patients with NOA have an increased risk for TGCT and GCNIS. Therefore, when performing TESE/mTESE, the procedure should be combined with diagnostic biopsies to rule out the presence of GCNIS. However, the exception is patients with Klinefelter syndrome, where diagnostic biopsies are unnecessary unless the patient also has a history of cryptorchidism. The diagnostic biopsy gives evidence of the underlying spermatogenic failure (and finally gives rise to an understanding of NOA) and the chance for early detection of an underlying disease, such as GCNIS. Jezek et al. were the first group to describe this combined approach of TESE, cryopreservation, and histological analysis [113].

For cancer patients with azoospermia, cryptozoospermia, or ejaculation failure, where cryopreservation of ejaculated sperm prior to gonadotoxic treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy) is not possible, oncoTESE may be considered [114]. Among men with TGCT, 2.9%–6.6% present with azoospermia before orchiectomy [115, 116, 117, 118]. In these cases, oncoTESE is particularly feasible, as it can be performed simultaneously with orchiectomy on the tumor‐bearing testis. OncoTESE in the contralateral testicle may be considered on an individual evaluation, taking hCG and testis size into account. For other cancers, such as hematological malignancies, oncoTESE is often not an option due to medical issues or the need for urgent treatment. In such cases, TESE or mTESE may be performed after remission if azoospermia persists [119, 120]. Although evidence remain limited, reported SRR range from 42.9% to 57.7% for oncoTESE and 32.4%–75.5% for postgonadotoxic TESE, depending on the type of malignant disease [120].

Patients with low sperm counts and a high percentage of spermatozoa with DNA fragmentation are at increased risk of ICSI failures. So far, only two studies with low‐quality evidence (one retrospective and one uncontrolled comparative study) give rise to the assumption that testicular spermatozoa are of better quality compared to ejaculated spermatozoa and finally result in a better ICSI outcome [121, 122]. The option of TESE and the use of testicular spermatozoa for ICSI may be considered and discussed with the couple, who should be informed that this approach is based on low‐quality evidence and is considered experimental [2, 3].

In the case of severe cryptozoospermia, a shortcoming of viable cells for ICSI treatment and cryopreservation for fertility protection can be observed. Thus, therapeutic–diagnostic biopsies will add to the pool of viable spermatozoa stored for later use in TESE–ICSI treatment [123].

Evidence (Diagnostic Biopsy and TESE/mTESE)

An elective testis biopsy is not performed if overt TGCT is diagnosed or suspected, for example, in palpable or by ultrasound visible masses or in case of elevated tumor markers. With scrotal biopsy taking, there is a risk of local spread of tumor cells and alterations of the lymphatic spread of tumor cells. However, using proper surgical techniques, for example, inguinal approach to explore the testis, fresh‐frozen biopsies are routinely taken from the tumor for immediate examination to differentiate between malignant and benign lesions. Usually, an immediate orchiectomy will be carried out if malignancy is confirmed. In such cases, oncoTESE as an option for fertility preservation should be discussed with the patient in case of presurgical azoospermia [78, 124].

Ongoing infection in the scrotum is also a risk factor for performing a scrotal incision for testis biopsy. Thus, biopsy taking must be postponed until the inflammation has been successfully treated.

Evidence (TESE/mTESE)

Postvasectomy Patients

Reconstructive microsurgical vasovasostomy (VV) offers a chance of up to 85%–90% for successful refertilization with patency and a consecutive chance for spontaneous pregnancy induction of 40%–50%. Success rates depend on the duration of the interval between vasectomy and VV and microsurgical experience [125, 126]. If no spermatozoa are found in the distal vas deferens due to epididymal blockage, a vasoepididymostomy can be performed. Cumulative pregnancy rates after VV and TESE–ICSI in postvasectomy patients are similar; however, time to pregnancy is longer after VV [127, 128].

Patients with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism due to pituitary or hypothalamic disorders, either congenital or acquired: TESE before gonadotropin treatment will most likely be unsuccessful and is contraindicated. Endocrine treatment with either pulsatile gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH) or gonadotropins will induce spermatogenesis in almost 90% of cases. In the case of postpubertal onset of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, reinduction of spermatogenesis may be achieved after a treatment period of 6–12 months, whereas this period may need to be extended in up to 2 years for congenital (and prepubertal) hypogonadotropic hypogonadism [129, 130, 131, 132, 133]. If azoospermia persists after these treatment efforts, TESE/mTESE should be considered before medication is reduced to testosterone substitution only.

Patients on testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) typically experience suppression of spermatogenesis, making the success of TESE/mTESE unlikely in most cases during TRT. Consequently, TRT should not be started in patients prior to TESE/mTESE. Furthermore, discontinuation of TRT is imperative in such patients if already on testosterone substitution. The time to sperm recovery varies and is shorter after transdermal testosterone application and takes up to 12–15 months after long‐term injectables [134]. Nevertheless, in cases of primary Leydig cell insufficiency, such patients will probably concurrently experience primary spermatogenic failure. Consequently, azoospermia or oligozoospermia will probably persist after TRT discontinuation. The level of evidence concerning this patient cohort is low. Most medications offered are off‐label and used with either hCG, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) or aromatase inhibitors [135, 136, 137]. A meta‐analysis of 22 studies including 1706 patients showed no effect of such therapy on surgical sperm retrieval in patients with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism and NOA [138]. In contrast, the evidence for improvement in eugonadal patients with NOA was weak, so routine use of hormonal treatment was not recommended [138].

Most patients diagnosed with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, typically will not achieve an adequate level of endogenous gonadotropin stimulation after discontinuing TRT. Consequently, this cohort is likely to require treatment with gonadotropins, as mentioned earlier.

Azoospermia factor (AZF) deletions of the Y chromosome are associated with spermatogenic failure and testing for such deletions and relevant counseling is therefore mandatory before TESE/mTESE in patients with azoospermia. Patients exhibiting AZFc deletions may present with either oligozoospermia or azoospermia. In instances of complete AZFc deletion leading to azoospermia, the likelihood of successful sperm retrieval through TESE stands at 50% (ranging from 15% to 71%) [139]. Conversely, a complete deletion of AZFa is linked to SCO. In contrast, complete AZFb deletions (absence of sY1192) and AZFbc deletions are associated with either SCO (50%) or spermatogenic arrest [139, 140]. However, partial AZFb deletions with preserved sY1192 distal marker may be compatible with residual spermatogenesis [139].

Patients with various degrees of reduced semen parameters and a need for fertility preservation before gonadotoxic treatment or orchiectomy who have a significant number of viable spermatozoa sufficient for cryopreservation should be offered cryopreservation of ejaculated spermatozoa. In these patients, TESE is not indicated.

Evidence (Diagnostic Biopsies)

TESE procedures could potentially induce structural damage to the testicles, resulting in Leydig cell dysfunction. This in turn, could lead to temporary or possibly permanent testosterone deficiency. Certain patients may already have a reduced testicular function before TESE and are therefore at increased risk of having postoperative Leydig cell insufficiency. Hormonal evaluation before surgery will allow for detecting such patients at increased risk. Testosterone deficiency due to TESE typically resolves spontaneously. Therefore, adopting a watchful waiting strategy for approximately 12 months following surgery is advisable. In instances where TRT is deemed necessary, temporarily halting treatment might unveil the anticipated restoration of testosterone secretion, prompting a reassessment of the need for long‐term therapy. Notably, patients with Klinefelter syndrome often present with more profound testosterone deficiency which remains after TESE, compared with other NOA causes [141]. In cases with permanent testosterone deficiency TRT should be discussed with the patient before surgery as it will become a necessary treatment thereafter.

Evidence (Diagnostic Biopsies)

The technical aspects of tissue retrieval are essential because neglect at any stage will impair the quality of the final specimen and might render the histopathological evaluation difficult, and clinical diagnosis is not certain. The surgical technique is very important, with three key rules; the incision should avoid large vessels, the tissue should be taken carefully to prevent mechanical squeezing or fragmentation, and the sample should be sufficiently large [145]. A representative open biopsy should have an average size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm, including approximately 100 seminiferous tubules in a cross‐section [146]. In cross‐section, 25–30 tubules are the minimum necessary for diagnostic biopsies.

In infertile patients, bilateral biopsies are recommended because of frequent discrepancies between the histology of the two testicles [147]. Multiple biopsies have been shown to reflect the testicular tissue's heterogeneity better and increase the diagnostic sensitivity. In one study, which included 295 patients, triple biopsies were superior in detecting GCNIS compared to single biopsies [24]. In another study of 2318 patients with TGCT, the diagnostic sensitivity was increased by 18% by two‐site contralateral biopsies compared to one‐site [22]. The segmental architecture of the testis may exhibit areas with total atrophy or SCO neighboring areas with at least qualitatively intact spermatogenesis, depending on the stem cell pool. The segments are separated areas, and the quality of focal spermatogenesis may vary tremendously between different segments. Also, reliable detection of GCNIS depends on multiple biopsies. It leads to the respective recommendations in actual guidelines for at least two biopsies from various areas (e.g., upper and lower part of the testis) [24, 145, 148, 149, 150].

Therefore, both testes are exteriorized through one 2–3 cm incision in the scrotal midline, especially for multiple biopsies. After opening the tunica vaginalis several small incisions on the tunica albuginea can be made, for example, on the upper, middle and lower position and the tissue is gently taken from the superficial areas. The deeper the excisions are, the higher the bleeding risk, which is more difficult to control.

Bleeding, Infections, Fibrosis, and Atrophy

Scrotal bleeding and infections are rare events after a single testicular biopsy. In a comparative study between conventional TESE and mTESE, postoperative bleeding was 3/119 (2.5%) after TESE procedures. No scrotal hematomas were found after mTESE in 107 patients [153]. Intratesticular hematomas, detected on ultrasound 3 months postoperatively after TESE/mTESE, are reported in 0%–58% [91, 154, 155]. Most of these hematomas will resolve spontaneously within 6 months. Testicular ischemia may result from larger hematomas and increasing intratesticular pressure, but these are rare. Segmental devascularization, detected by testicular Doppler ultrasound 3 months after TESE and mTESE, was found in 5.0% and 3.3%, respectively. However, 6 months postoperatively, none of the patients exhibited signs of segmental devascularization [91].

Scar tissue in the testis 3–6 months after TESE and mTESE occurred on ultrasound investigation in 30% and 3.3%, respectively [91].

Change in Semen Quality/Spermatogenesis

Men will remain azoospermic, of course. Re‐TESE results are published and often show testicular sperms if the first TESE procedure was successful. In case of a negative TESE, a mTESE procedure is advised with different SRRs, depending on testis volume, the histology of the testicular tissue and how well the first TESE was performed, and tissue was processed [156].

Leydig Cell Function—Testosterone Deficiency

Several studies have reported a temporary decrease in serum testosterone after TESE/mTESE. In a recent meta‐analysis, subanalyses including patients with Klinefelter syndrome or NOA separately showed a mean decrease in serum testosterone 6 months postoperatively of 4.1 nmol/L and 2.7 nmol/L in Klinefelter patients and other NOA patients, respectively [157]. They further showed that testosterone levels had returned to preoperative concentrations after 18–26 months. Data on different surgical methods (TESA, TESE, mTESE, and Trucut needle) were not separated in that study. LH is often elevated after TESE, indicating a compensated Leydig cell failure, although these changes may be transient [158].

After retrieval, the tissue specimen must be fixed immediately, preferably in a solution preserving well the structure of soft testis parenchyma and integrity of the cell nuclei, such as Bouin's or Stieve's fixative. These fixatives used to be less suitable for immunochemistry, but more recently developed antigen retrieval protocols (e.g., in standardized microwave ovens or pressure cookers) have eliminated this problem. Testicular biopsies can be evaluated using traditional light microscopy, but scanning (digital) microscopy has been increasingly advised. Digital microscopy simplifies the appreciation of histological details while focusing on the overall picture and facilitates communication between evaluators in discordant evaluation.

The H&E and IHC‐stained sections (described above) should preferably be evaluated by two evaluators experienced in testicular histology. The evaluation must be detailed, and general architecture, spermatogenesis, and somatic cell types must be assessed. Regarding germ cells, it is important to identify late spermatids, even if only a few can be seen. Heterogeneous patterns should be noted with a semiquantitative account of all stages of germ cell maturation. The previously used Johnsen score is not recommended because it does not consider heterogeneous patterns [1]. A more recent modification should be used, for example, the Kliesch–Bergmann score [163]. It is important to look for any other pathological changes, such as the presence of any neoplastic cells, inflammatory cell infiltration, or TM.

The presence or absence of GCNIS or microinvasive tumor cells should always be noted, and the supportive mandatory IHC markers, including the required positive and negative controls should be evaluated.

Evidence (Diagnostic Biopsies)

Internal controls of tissue preparation quality and routine histological stains must be implemented in each laboratory. For IHC recognition of cell types, especially GCNIS/malignant germ cells, antibodies with established specificity (by the manufacturer and confirmed in published scientific literature) and robust performance in paraffin‐embedded tissue should be used. For correct interpretation and to avoid false‐negative and false‐positive results, proper positive and negative controls should always be included in IHC staining [166].

The positive control includes a tissue section known to express the protein of interest and should be mounted on all slides next to the investigated biopsy, or at least on one slide in each series (Figure 1). The control tissue should be marked, for example, with a circle. A positive control for PLAP, OCT4, and D2‐40 should be either seminoma or testicular tissue containing GCNIS. The placenta is also suitable as a positive control for PLAP. A positive control must include testicular tissue with complete spermatogenesis for MAGE‐A4 and FAAH (or other used antibodies).

Two types of negative control exist. One is a serial section of the studied tissue processed and stained in parallel using the same protocol but an isotype IgG that will not react with the specific antigen of interest. The negative control can be performed with a dilution buffer alone, and the primary antibody can be omitted as an alternative to isotype IgG. The other type of negative control includes tissue known to be negative for the protein of interest and stained with the specific antibody of interest. Optimally, both types of negative control should be included in every staining, but the availability of relevant control tissue may be a limiting factor. In a clinical setting, if several biopsies are subjected to IHC in the same staining experiment, those without pathological cells will serve as negative controls to rare biopsies with malignancy.

Given the frequent discrepancy between evaluators of heterogeneous testis histology in infertile men [147], and observers’ errors, especially concerning quantitative assessment [167], it is recommended that two independent evaluators assess each testicular biopsy, and jointly discuss the results in discrepant cases. Digital microscopy equipment is advantageous for obtaining consensus.

In the first case, the cells are used for ICSI immediately, whereas in the second case, the isolated cells are cryopreserved using the same protocol as for cryopreserving ejaculated sperm. The third method, including cryopreservation of multiple biopsies, offers simultaneous TESE and additional immediate adjacent biopsies for histological evaluation. The advantage of such a procedure is that the most appropriate, cryopreserved biopsies can be selected for sperm retrieval based on a histological evaluation of spermatogenesis in the adjacent tissues [2, 113, 148, 163, 168].

After the excision of testicular tissue during a biopsy procedure, there is a common laboratory practice to immerse the tissue in a transport medium suitable for the purpose at a temperature of 30–37°C before the cryopreservation of the tissue specimen takes place. The purpose of the transport medium is to preserve the structure of the seminiferous epithelium (most importantly, late spermatids and spermatozoa) from desquamation and other structural damage and to maintain functionality. The seminiferous epithelium (after the biopsy excision) is prone to spontaneous and rapid sloughing, which can also create problems in interpreting testis histology [159]. Since the testis parenchyma is abundantly vascularized, immersion in the transport medium partially rinses the biopsy from the extravasated erythrocytes. The choice of the transport medium depends on the biopsy technique applied. For example, the open testis biopsy technique, where one gets a relatively large piece of the tissue (minimum 3 mm in diameter), would require immediate immersion of the biopsy in the transport medium [113, 169, 170].

Values and Remarks (TESE/mTESE)

We place a high value on adequately handling testicular biopsies upon the excision. Squeezing of the biopsy and its transportation without an adequate transport medium should be avoided.

Evidence (TESE/mTESE)

The purpose of testicular biopsy cryopreservation is to effectively conserve tissue and cell morphology and functionality, particularly spermatozoa. Thus, in cases of cryopreservation of tissue for later isolation of sperm for ICSI, the cryopreservation medium/cryoprotecting agent should have a good penetration rate and prevent the formation of ice crystals that could form during the process of tissue freezing, and the procedure should be quick to prevent the degradation of the seminiferous epithelium, especially spermatozoa [171, 172]. Yet, some laboratories prefer to subject the tissue to enzymatic digestion before cryopreservation to facilitate the isolation of individual testicular cells [173, 174, 175]. Most cryomedia work at room temperature and should be preconditioned to that temperature before freezing.

In general, the cryomedium used for the clinical routine should be reliable, prevalidated, registered, properly shipped (cold chain), properly stored, traceable, and accompanied by the proper documentation related to each respective batch (European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines & HealthCare [EDQM]: Guide to the Quality and Safety of Tissues and Cells for Human Application, 2022) [176]. For testicular biopsy cryopreservation, the most frequently used are glycerol‐based cryoprotectant solutions.

The protocols for testicular biopsy freezing usually imply slow step‐wise decreases in the cryomedium temperature with the help of specific equipment and programmable cooling curves [177, 178]. However, concerns about the safety and quality of cryopreserved testicular biopsies and isolated spermatozoa are raised regarding cell damage (membrane rupture, acrosome rupture, damage of mitochondria, nucleus, DNA, etc.), toxic effects of cryoprotectants, osmotic damage, and incomplete dehydration, all influencing the cell viability [179, 180].

Based on the data presented above, one can conclude that there is no universal cryomedium for testicular biopsy in widespread use. However, cryomedia with glycerol or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as cryoprotecting agents predominate. Before cryopreservation, some laboratories prefer enzymatic digestion of the testis parenchyma to freeze individual cells more effectively. Programmed slow freezing is the most common way to freeze testicular biopsy or individual cells. Although promising, vitrification should be considered as an experimental method.

Evidence (TESE/mTESE)

There is no scientific evidence that testicular biopsies or spermatozoa, if kept under appropriate storage conditions, deteriorate after a certain time of storage [181]. The use of frozen spermatozoa through assisted reproductive techniques has led to the birth of healthy offspring more than 20 years after initial storage [181]. The same goes for spermatozoa from the TESE procedure. A standardized ICSI procedure using frozen–thawed testicular tissue elucidates ICSI outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, and neonatal outcomes that appear not to be affected by the duration of the period of cryostorage [182].

The optimal storage temperature is based on the tissue type, cryoprotectant, and freezing method. A temperature less than −136°C for testicular biopsies or spermatozoa is appropriate. Temperature above −130°C is detrimental to the survival and quality of the material frozen. Even though storage in liquid nitrogen or liquid nitrogen vapors is common practice, it is important to maintain the minimum temperature when handling the stored samples (≤140°C) to prevent microbiological transmission.

Evidence (TESE/mTESE)

To perform TESE, testicular biopsy is minced with sterile needles and centrifugation [113, 169]. In the case of NOA, where only a few spermatozoa are expected or histological analysis pointed out intense hyalinization of lamina propria of seminiferous tubules, mixed atrophy with tubular fibrosis, interstitial fibrosis and other changes of testicular parenchyma indicating reduced spermatogenesis that can seriously impede TESE, the biopsy may be subjected to enzymatic digestion [183, 184]. Enzymatic digestion is performed using DNAse and collagenase type IV, and TESE was positive in 57% of NOA patients [183]. Moreover, the combination of enzymatic digestion, density gradient centrifugation, and stimulation of motility (where indicated) can yield even better results than enzymatic digestion alone [184].

Which Cell Type Should Be Used for ICSI in the Absence of Spermatozoa?

In wet preparations, under the Hoffman modulation contrast system, four categories of spermatid can be distinguished according to the shape, the amount of cytoplasm and the size of the tail: round spermatids, elongating spermatids, elongated spermatids, and mature spermatids, just before their release from the Sertoli cells. In the absence of spermatozoa, only mature spermatids should be considered for ICSI in men who have already proven their capacity to produce some spermatozoa.

Values and Remarks (TESE/mTESE)

We advise caution when using mature (elongated) spermatids for ICSI in the absence of spermatozoa and place a high value on their adequate recognition.

Conclusions

Historically, testicular biopsies have mainly been performed for diagnostic purposes, but with the development of more advanced assisted reproductive techniques biopsies may also serve therapeutic purposes (TESE or mTESE). Diagnostically, biopsies are used to identify GCNIS in patients with at least three risk factors for the development of TGCT but are contraindicated in patients with a suspicion of TGCT. Therapeutically, TESE or mTESE may be performed in patients with NOA to retrieve spermatozoa for use in ICSI. Therapeutic biopsies are often performed with a diagnostic biopsy to describe spermatogenesis and rule out GCNIS. Before performing TESE or mTESE the patient should undergo a complete andrological evaluation including karyotyping, Y chromosome microdeletion analysis and evaluation of reproductive hormones. Importantly, in patients with reversible azoospermia (e.g., hypogonadotropic hypogonadism or patients on TRT), TESE or mTESE should not be performed until the underlying condition has been corrected. Testicular biopsies may result in a transient decline in serum testosterone that is usually resolved spontaneously within 12 months. Therefore, re‐evaluation of reproductive hormones 12 months postsurgery is recommended in patients at increased risk of developing testosterone deficiency.
