Abdominal obesity and colorectal cancer risk: a systematic review and updated meta-analysis
Authors: Monireh Faghir-Ganji, Narjes Abdolmohamadi, Alireza Ansari-Moghaddam, Babak Eshrati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis meta-analysis investigates the association between abdominal obesity, as measured by waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and the risk of colorectal cancer (CRC).MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar for relevant English-language prospective cohort studies. Nine studies met the inclusion criteria for the primary meta-analysis, comprising 13,193,908 participants with 131,438 incident CRC cases. Pooled relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a random-effects model.ResultsCentral obesity was associated with a 31% increased risk of CRC (RR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.25–1.37). The risk was higher in men (RR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.31–1.52) than in women (RR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.19–1.34; P for subgroup difference = 0.04). The association was stronger for colon cancer (RR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.27–1.47) than for rectal cancer (RR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.11–1.26; P < 0.001). Waist circumference specifically showed a 32% increased risk (RR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.25–1.41). Studies using measured WC/WHR reported a higher risk (RR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.36–1.61) than those using self-reported measures (RR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.20–1.32; P < 0.001). No significant heterogeneity was found by exposure type (WC vs. WHR) or geographic region.ConclusionsCentral obesity is a significant and independent risk factor for colorectal cancer, with stronger associations observed for colon cancer than for rectal cancer and higher relative risks in men than in women. Visceral fat appears to play a critical role in colorectal carcinogenesis. Maintaining a healthy waist circumference through regular physical activity and a balanced diet remains a key strategy to reduce CRC risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16351-1.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Abdominal obesity, Epidemiology, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a significant public health challenge worldwide. It ranks as the fourth most common cancer globally and is the third most prevalent cancer among both men and women [1, 2].

Colorectal cancer (CRC) poses a significant and growing public health challenge worldwide. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), approximately 1.93 million new cases of CRC were diagnosed globally in 2020, with an age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of 19.5 per 100,000 population (23.4 in males and 16.2 in females) [3]. The global mortality burden is substantial, with about 935,000 deaths attributed to CRC in the same year, corresponding to an age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of 9 per 100,000 (11 in males and 7.2 in females) [3]. These figures reflect an increasing incidence and mortality trend in many regions, particularly in countries with medium to high Human Development Index (HDI), where lifestyle and environmental changes have contributed to this rise [3, 4].

In the United States, CRC remains one of the most common cancers, with an estimated 153,020 new cases and 52,550 deaths projected in 2025 [5]. While overall colon cancer incidence rates have stabilized or decreased in older adults due to improved screening and prevention, there has been a concerning increase in rectal cancer incidence, especially among adults under 50 years of age [1, 4, 6]. Projections indicate that by 2030, the global burden of CRC will increase by approximately 60%, with over 2.2 million new cases and 1.1 million deaths annually, driven largely by population growth and aging [4, 6].While CRC incidence has historically been high in developing countries, recent decades have witnessed a rapid escalation in these regions as well [4, 7]. For instance, data from the Swedish Cancer Registry indicate that colon adenocarcinoma is the fifth most common cancer, while rectal adenocarcinoma ranks ninth in prevalence [8].

Multiple environmental and lifestyle risk factors contribute to the rising burden of CRC, including smoking, obesity, diets high in fat and low in fiber, and physical inactivity [7, 9]. Globally, overweight and obesity rates are escalating at an alarming pace, correlating strongly with increased risks of obesity-related cancers such as breast, colorectal, endometrial, esophageal, kidney, lung, pancreatic, thyroid, and gallbladder cancers [10, 11].

Despite the well-established link between obesity and CRC, the specific role of abdominal (central) obesity compared to overall obesity remains controversial [12, 13]. Abdominal obesity, characterized by excess visceral fat accumulation around the waist, is increasingly prevalent and has profound implications for metabolic health [14, 15]. Poor dietary habits, particularly the consumption of high-calorie and high-sugar foods such as sweets, cakes, pizza, and chips, significantly contribute to abdominal fat accumulation [16, 17]. Moreover, smoking and alcohol consumption further exacerbate central obesity and its adverse health outcomes [17, 18].

Abdominal obesity disrupts normal lipid metabolism by lowering high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, the "good" cholesterol, and elevating low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, the "bad" cholesterol. It also increases blood glucose and triglyceride levels, key components of metabolic syndrome [19, 20]. Visceral fat is strongly linked to insulin resistance, which can progress to glucose intolerance and type 2 diabetes mellitus [13, 21]. Additionally, excess central fat is a critical factor in cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension and atherosclerosis and serves as a potent predictor of various cancers among overweight and obese populations [10, 15].

Studies conducted across America, Australia, Asia, and Europe have investigated the association between abdominal obesity and CRC risk, yielding heterogeneous results [7, 12]. While maintaining a healthy body weight remains fundamental to preventing chronic diseases and premature mortality, controlling waist circumference and preventing abdominal obesity are equally vital [22, 23]. The most commonly used anthropometric measures for abdominal obesity are waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), both influenced by genetic and environmental factors [22, 24].

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesize data from prospective studies to elucidate the role of abdominal obesity in the development of colorectal cancer (CRC) in both men and women. A key innovation of this study is its focus on gender-specific and regional variations in the relationship between abdominal obesity and CRC, an area that has been insufficiently explored in previous research. By emphasizing these differences, the study enhances our understanding of how obesity impacts health uniquely among men and women across various geographical contexts. Furthermore, the current study employs a comprehensive methodology by simultaneously examining waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), while differentiating results based on gender and geographic region. This approach allows for a more precise analysis of the relationship between abdominal obesity and CRC risk, providing valuable insights into the nuances of these associations across different populations. Given the variability in CRC prevalence, risk factors, and study designs, the precise relationship between abdominal obesity and CRC incidence remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to clarify this association to inform effective prevention strategies, ultimately contributing to the understanding of how abdominal obesity influences CRC risk across diverse demographics.

Methods

Study registration and ethical approval

This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database under the identification number CRD42023391135. The study was conducted as part of a research project approved by the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences (IUMS) with the ID number IR.IUMS.REC.1402.163.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent researchers (M.F.G. and N.A.) screened the titles and abstracts retrieved from the search. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were then retrieved and independently assessed for eligibility based on the criteria outlined above. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through consensus or by consultation with a senior research team member.

Using a standardized extraction form, the researchers systematically collected key data elements from each eligible study. The extracted information included: the first author's last name, year of publication, country, study period, age range, gender distribution, number of cases identified, total number of participants, specific data collection methods, abdominal obesity measurements (WC, WHR), precise cancer site classifications, and the fully adjusted hazard estimates (RR/HR) with their corresponding 95% CIs for each exposure category, along with all potential confounders considered in the original studies. In addition, we recorded the specific cut-off points used to define high versus low abdominal obesity (e.g., WC > 88 cm in women, percentiles, or other study-specific thresholds).

Quality assessment (risk of bias)

We assessed the quality of the included studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). This tool evaluates three domains: selection of study groups, comparability, and ascertainment of exposure and outcome. Two researchers (M.F.G. and N.A.) independently performed the quality assessment. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with senior authors. When information was missing, we contacted the corresponding authors. The final quality scores are presented in Table 3.

Handling of multiple effect sizes and statistical models for subgroup analyses

When a single study reported more than one effect estimate (for example, separate results for men and women, for colon and rectal cancer, or for WC and WHR), we used the following methods:

For the overall CRC risk: we included only one effect size per study – the most fully adjusted estimate for the combined sex population. If a study reported only sex-specific estimates, we pooled them using a fixed-effects meta-analysis within that study before including the pooled estimate in the overall model.

For subgroup analyses (by sex, cancer subsite, measurement method, or region): we included all relevant effect sizes but used robust variance estimation (RVE) with a small-sample correction. This method accounts for within-study correlation and is ideal for meta-analyses with multiple dependent effect sizes.

To address potential overlap of cohorts (e.g., multiple studies derived from the same source population such as NHS, HPFS, or EPIC), we included only the study with the longest follow-up or the largest sample size from each cohort. When this was not clearly documented, we contacted the authors for clarification.

Statistical analysis and sensitivity analyses

All pooled analyses were performed using random-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and the Q test (with p < 0.10 considered significant for heterogeneity).

Handling of variable WC/WHR cut-off points

Given the substantial heterogeneity in cut-off points for defining high versus low abdominal obesity across studies (e.g., different absolute values, percentiles, or sex-specific thresholds), we conducted several sensitivity analyses:

Excluding studies with non-standardized cut-offs – we re-ran the main analysis after removing studies that used unconventional or unclear cut-off definitions.

Excluding studies with non-standardized cut-offs – we re-ran the main analysis after removing studies that used unconventional or unclear cut-off definitions.

Meta-regression – we performed a meta-regression to examine whether the pooled effect sizes varied according to the type of cut-off definition (e.g., absolute values vs. percentiles).

Meta-regression – we performed a meta-regression to examine whether the pooled effect sizes varied according to the type of cut-off definition (e.g., absolute values vs. percentiles).

Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and by Egger's linear regression test (with p < 0.10 indicating significant asymmetry), provided at least 10 studies were available for a given comparison.

Sensitivity analysis was also conducted using the leave-one-out method (omitting one study at a time) to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall estimate and to test the robustness of the findings.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 17.0, StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) or R software (version 4.2, meta and metafor packages). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, except where otherwise specified.

Results

Literature search

The initial computerized literature searches across databases (PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar) yielded 541 potentially relevant studies. After identifying and removing 119 duplicate records, 422 unique studies remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 349 studies were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria (e.g., wrong study design, irrelevant exposure/outcome).

The full texts of the remaining 73 studies were retrieved, reviewed, and critically appraised by two independent reviewers. Reference lists of these manuscripts were additionally scanned for further relevant studies, which did not yield any new eligible articles. Of the 73 manuscripts, 34 were excluded after full-text review due to: (1) not addressing the subject of interest, (2) lacking required data to estimate effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals, or (3) being duplicate publications or reviews. When multiple publications from the same study population existed, the most relevant or recent paper was retained.

Ultimately, 39 studies were deemed eligible for full-text review and data extraction (quantitative synthesis). Among these, 9 studies were included in the primary meta-analysis examining the overall association between abdominal obesity and colorectal cancer (CRC). These 9 studies provided directly comparable risk estimates (RR/HR for highest vs. lowest categories of waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio, with 95% confidence intervals and full adjustment for confounders).

The remaining 30 studies were not included in the primary meta-analysis for the following specific reasons:

11 studies reported effect estimates per unit or per 1-standard deviation increase rather than categorical comparisons;

Sensitivity analysis for abdominal obesity definitions

To address variation in WC and WHR cut-off values across studies, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to studies that explicitly used the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended thresholds for abdominal obesity: WC > 102 cm in men and > 88 cm in women; WHR > 0.90 in men and > 0.85 in women. The pooled relative risk (RR) for colorectal cancer in this sensitivity analysis was 1.33 (95% CI: 1.26–1.41), which was very similar to the main analysis (RR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.25–1.37). This indicates that differences in absolute cut-off values did not materially bias the overall association. Furthermore, all included studies reported effect estimates for the highest versus lowest category (e.g., top vs. bottom quintile or decile) of WC or WHR, a categorical approach that compares extreme ends of the distribution within each cohort and inherently mitigates the impact of between-study differences in absolute cut-offs. Thus, our main findings are robust to variations in the definition of abdominal obesity.

Central obesity and colorectal cancer risk

A total of 99 independent associations were included in the meta-analysis of central obesity and colorectal cancer (CRC) risk, derived from the final set of included studies. As some studies reported multiple associations (e.g., sex-specific, cancer subsite, follow-up duration, region, or measurement method), the total number of reported effect sizes exceeded the number of included studies (9) (Fig. 3).

Key findings

The overall pooled analysis demonstrated that central obesity was significantly associated with a 31% increased risk of CRC (RR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.25–1.37) compared to individuals with normal WC/WHR. Significant heterogeneity was observed across the studies (I2 = 76.63%, P < 0.001), suggesting that sex, cancer subsite, and measurement methods might be potential sources of variation.

Discussion

This study was conducted to investigate the association between abdominal obesity and colorectal cancer (CRC). Colorectal cancer is one of the obesity-related cancers whose incidence is rising worldwide, and various lifestyle, environmental, and genetic factors contribute to its development [51–53]. The findings of this meta-analysis revealed a significant association between central obesity and an increased risk of CRC. When comparing individuals with abdominal obesity (based on waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio) to those with normal values, the overall pooled relative risk was 1.31 (95% CI: 1.25–1.37), corresponding to a 31% higher risk. These results are consistent with previous meta-analyses. For example, a meta-analysis of 53 prospective studies showed that higher waist circumference and body mass index (BMI) are significantly associated with increased CRC risk [54, 55]. Similarly, another meta-analysis of 18 cohort studies reported that both waist-to-hip ratio and larger waist circumference are linked to a higher incidence of CRC [56, 57]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has also stated that maintaining a healthy weight reduces the risk of CRC [2, 10].

Sex and subsite differences

The combined relative risk of CRC associated with central obesity was approximately 15% higher in men (RR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.31–1.52) than in women (RR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.19–1.34), and this difference was statistically significant (P = 0.04). A large cohort study of Chinese participants also found that central obesity increased colon cancer risk more markedly in men than in women, which aligns with our results [35]. Furthermore, the association was significantly stronger for colon cancer (RR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.27–1.47) than for rectal cancer (RR = 1.18; 95% CI: 1.11–1.26; P < 0.001). This finding is consistent with a meta-analysis by Moghaddam et al. (2007) [58].

The pathophysiological mechanisms behind the stronger association with colon cancer remain unclear but may be related to anatomical differences between the colon and rectum. In rectal cancer, the mesorectum remains relatively unchanged regardless of obesity because of the confined pelvic space, whereas the mesocolon tends to thicken in obese patients with colon cancer [27, 59].This anatomical distinction might explain the relatively weaker association between obesity and rectal cancer.

Comparison with individual cohort studies

A large Korean cohort study (2020) reported that larger waist circumference was significantly associated with CRC risk, with increased risks of 10% for CRC, 11% for colon cancer, and 8% for rectal cancer after adjusting for confounders, and these associations were stronger in individuals with general obesity [27]. Another prospective study found that colon polyps were more frequently detected during colonoscopy in subjects with abdominal obesity [60]. In contrast, the Indenagar cohort study (2016) on 4,000 participants did not find a significant relationship between waist size and CRC [61]; however, that study may have had limited statistical power or different population characteristics.

Impact of measurement methods

Measurement method was an important source of heterogeneity. For central obesity, the pooled risk estimate was significantly higher when using measured WC/WHR (RR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.36–1.61; 36 studies) than when using self-reported data (RR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.20–1.32; 63 studies; P < 0.001). Similarly, for waist circumference alone, measured assessments (21 studies) yielded a higher risk (RR = 1.48) than self-reported assessments (38 studies; RR = 1.28; P = 0.04). For waist-to-hip ratio, measured assessments (15 studies) also gave a higher estimate (RR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.30–1.69) than self-reported ones (25 studies; RR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.15–1.31; P = 0.001). These differences highlight the importance of using standardized, direct measurements in future studies to avoid underestimation of risk. Furthermore, the comparison of waist circumference and CRC risk showed a 32% increased risk in obese individuals compared to those with normal waist size, and the increased risk was 16% higher in men than in women, which is consistent with previous reports [27].

Sex hormones and body fat distribution

Larsson et al. reported a positive relationship between obesity and the incidence of colon and rectal cancer in both sexes [34].The observed sex differences in CRC risk related to central obesity may be explained by hormonal factors. Adipose tissue has an inverse relationship with testosterone levels in men, whereas it has a direct positive relationship with estrogen levels in women [62]. Moreover, men and women differ in body fat distribution: women generally have more subcutaneous fat (in the abdomen, thighs, and lower body), while men have a greater central (visceral) fat distribution [63, 64]. Visceral fat is more strongly associated with colon cancer development than subcutaneous fat [65]. Therefore, regular physical activity and a balanced diet, which help maintain a healthy weight and reduce visceral fat, remain key strategies for lowering CRC risk.

Regional differences

We observed borderline significant regional differences for waist circumference (P = 0.05). The pooled RR was highest in the USA (RR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.29–1.63), followed by Australia/New Zealand (1.37; 1.31–1.43), Asia (1.31; 1.17–1.46), and Europe (1.28; 1.20–1.37). These variations may reflect differences in lifestyle, diet, physical activity, genetic background, and the prevalence of other risk factors across regions.

Comparison with previous meta-analyses

Our results are broadly consistent with earlier meta-analyses. For instance, Dong et al. reported relative risks for colon and rectal cancer of 1.53 and 1.20, respectively, for waist circumference [56], which are similar to our findings (colon: 1.40; rectal: 1.17). A 2021 US study reported somewhat lower risks for CRC, colon, and rectal cancer than our pooled estimates, while a 2016 US study reported a slightly higher risk for CRC – both being generally consistent with our results [32, 33]. European studies from 2020 and 2019 showed lower risks than ours, whereas a 2017 European study showed higher risks [26, 28, 30]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in study populations, adjustment factors, or cut-off values.

Limitations

Several limitations of this meta-analysis should be acknowledged. First, although we included only prospective cohort studies, the definitions of abdominal obesity (WC and WHR) and the cut-off values varied across studies. To address this, we performed a sensitivity analysis using WHO-recommended thresholds, which confirmed the robustness of our findings. Second, we could not perform a separate analysis for early-onset versus late-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC vs. LOCRC) because individual participant data were not available and most primary studies did not report age-stratified risk estimates using a consistent cut-off (e.g., 50 years). Future studies with detailed age-specific data are needed to examine whether the association between abdominal obesity and CRC risk differs by age at diagnosis. Third, some studies used self-reported anthropometric measures, which may introduce non-differential misclassification and potentially underestimate the true association. Nevertheless, our subgroup analysis showed that measured assessments yielded higher risk estimates. Fourth, although we adjusted for major confounders where possible, residual confounding (e.g., dietary patterns, physical activity, genetic factors) cannot be completely ruled out. Finally, the majority of included studies were from North America, Europe, and Asia, which may limit generalizability to other populations such as Africa or South America.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis confirms that abdominal obesity, measured by waist circumference (WC) or waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), is independently and significantly associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer (CRC). The pooled analysis showed a 31% higher risk of CRC among individuals with central obesity compared to those with normal WC/WHR. The association was stronger in men (41% increased risk) than in women (26% increased risk), and more pronounced for colon cancer (37% increased risk) than for rectal cancer (18% increased risk). These findings suggest that central adiposity may be a more relevant risk factor for CRC than general obesity markers such as body mass index (BMI) alone. Given these results, public health strategies should prioritize the prevention of central obesity. Maintaining a healthy waist circumference through regular physical activity and a balanced diet remains an essential approach to reducing the incidence of colorectal cancer.
