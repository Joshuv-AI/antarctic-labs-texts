Maternal empowerment and child mortality in the Democratic Republic of the Congo: evidence from multilevel modelling of DHS data
Authors: Kabeya Clement Mulamba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study applied a spatial multilevel modelling approach to the latest Demographic and Health Survey data from the Democratic Republic of the Congo to examine the association between maternal empowerment and infant and under-five mortality. To improve temporal alignment between women’s empowerment and child mortality outcomes, the analysis was restricted to births occurring within the five years preceding the survey. The econometric modelling accounted for the hierarchical structure of the data, with children nested within clusters and clusters within provinces, and decomposed maternal empowerment into within-cluster and contextual (cluster-level) components. The results indicate that, after controlling for child-, maternal-, household-, cluster-, and province-level factors, neither the within-cluster component nor the cluster-level mean of maternal empowerment shows a robust average association with infant or under-five mortality. In contrast, substantial heterogeneity in child mortality persists at both the cluster and province levels, with particularly strong variation at the cluster level. These findings suggest that child mortality in the Democratic Republic of the Congo is more strongly shaped by local contextual conditions and demographic factors than by a uniform average effect of maternal empowerment, underscoring the importance of multilevel policy responses that address both household-level disadvantage and place-based inequalities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28504-9.

Introduction

The objective of this study was to examine the relationship between maternal empowerment and child mortality in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), using the 2024 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data restricted to births that occurred within the five years preceding the survey [1]. As highlighted by [2, 3], child mortality is a key indicator of a country’s overall level of development and remains a major global public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This challenge is especially acute in fragile contexts such as the DRC, which has experienced prolonged political instability, recurrent armed conflicts, and widespread poverty over several decades. These have weakened the health system and aggravated social inequalities. In such settings, women often face persistent social and economic marginalization that may constrain their ability to make decisions related to their own health and that of their children.

Given that child health and survival are closely linked to mothers’ roles as primary caregivers, it is important to assess whether—and to what extent—maternal empowerment is related to child mortality in the DRC. In this study, maternal empowerment is understood as women’s bargaining power within the household, encompassing access to resources and participation in decision-making processes that are directly linked to investments in child health. This conceptualization is grounded in the theoretical work of [4], who developed a household bargaining model predicting a negative relationship between maternal empowerment and child mortality.

A growing body of empirical literature has examined the relationship between maternal empowerment and child health outcomes more broadly [5–12]. Several important observations emerge from this literature. First, there is limited consensus regarding the empirical evidence on the nature and strength of the relationship between maternal empowerment and child health outcomes. Second, to the best of the author’s knowledge, no study has explicitly examined the association between maternal empowerment and child mortality in the DRC. Third, from a methodological perspective, most studies using DHS data have not simultaneously accounted for both geographical (spatial) dependence and spatial heterogeneity in their empirical analyses. In addition, maternal empowerment has often been modelled as a single individual-level predictor, whereas fewer studies have explicitly distinguished between within-cluster individual effects and contextual cluster-level effects. The present study addresses this issue by decomposing maternal empowerment into a within-cluster component and a cluster-level mean, thereby allowing a clear distinction between individual -level and contextual associations.

This study contributes to the literature by the relationship between maternal empowerment and both infant and under-five mortality using a spatial multilevel framework applied to recent DHS microdata from the DRC. The work of [3] is relevant in that it highlights the importance of geographical variation in child mortality in the country and incorporates a spatial dimension into the analysis. However, the two studies differ in both their substantive focus and analytical scale. Whereas [3] examined geographical location as a determinant of under-five mortality using aggregated provincial-level data from the 2007 DHS, the present study focuses on maternal empowerment as the main explanatory variable and uses child-level DHS data. Accordingly, this analysis is designed to assess both individual- and contextual-level associations within a multilevel framework, while [3] is more informative about the broader contextual and structural patterns. In addition, by using more recent DHS data collected after the 2015 administrative reform, which increased the number of provinces from 11 to 26, this study captures a finer and more policy-relevant spatial structure of child mortality in the DRC.

Moreover, this study lies at the intersection of child health, gender equality, and inclusive development—dimensions central to both the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [13] and the African Union’s Agenda 2063 [14]. Specifically, it aligns with SDG 3 (Good Health and Well‑Being) through its focus on reducing child mortality, and with SDG 5 (Gender Equality) by examining the role of maternal empowerment in shaping child survival outcomes. It also resonates with Agenda 2063 Aspiration 1, which envisions healthy and well‑nourished citizens, and Aspiration 6, which emphasizes the empowerment of women as key drivers of Africa’s development. Beyond bridging a critical empirical gap—discussed in previous sections—the findings aim to provide policy‑relevant evidence to support integrated strategies that advance child health (SDG 3) through gender‑responsive social and economic interventions (SDG 5), consistent with Africa’s vision of inclusive and people‑driven development under Agenda 2063.

In this context, the DRC, as a member of both the United Nations and the African Union, has committed to advancing these shared development objectives and aspirations. Improving child health outcomes—particularly by reducing preventable infant and under-five mortality—remains a central development priority for the country, with the potential to yield substantial long-term socio-economic benefits. Investments in child survival contribute to healthier and more productive populations, thereby strengthening human capital formation and enhancing the prospects of harnessing demographic dividends over the life course.

DHS data have [1] a hierarchical and geographical structure design, with individual nested within households and survey clusters (PSU), which in turn nested within provinces. This structure makes a multilevel modelling approach particularly appropriate for the analysis of child mortality. In the present study, births are modelled within a three-level framework, with children nested within clusters and clusters nested within provinces. By incorporating random effects at both the cluster and province levels, the empirical strategy captures unobserved contextual influences and residual heterogeneity operating at local and regional scales, thereby yielding more reliable parameter estimates.

Analyzing spatially structured micro-level data without aggregating observations to higher territorial levels is essential for preserving individual-level variation in child mortality and maternal characteristics. At the same time, child health outcomes are shaped not only by individual and household factors, but also by the broader contexts in which children live. In DHS data, these contextual influences operate at multiple levels. Survey clusters (i.e. primary survey units or PSU) represent local community environments that may capture shared conditions such as access to health services, infrastructure, sanitation, and local social norms, while provinces reflect broader administrative, institutional, and social contexts. It is therefore important to account for both cluster- and province-level heterogeneity while retaining the granularity of individual-level information. The multilevel modelling framework adopted in this study makes it possible to achieve this balance by simultaneously modelling individual-level associations and higher-level contextual influences on child mortality.

In applied research, the fixed effects models (FEM) are often used for unobserved heterogeneity associated with higher-level units [15–17]. However, although such models can absorb unit-specific influences, they do not explicitly model the multilevel structure of the data and therefore do not provide separate estimates of variance at different hierarchical levels. In the present study, this limitation is important because child mortality may be shaped simultaneously by individual-, cluster-, and province-level factors. By contrast, the multilevel modelling strategy adopted here explicitly estimates residual heterogeneity at both the cluster and province levels and, in the spatial specification, additionally accommodates dependence among neighboring provinces. This makes it better suited to the hierarchical and spatially structured nature of the DHS data used in this study.

In addition, the methodological approach adopted in this study is grounded in two complementary theoretical perspectives. The first draws on Tobler’s First Law of Geography [18], which holds that geographically proximate places tend to be more similar then distant ones. In the context of this study, the above principle provides a basis for modelling spatial dependence across clusters and provinces, recognizing that child mortality risks in neighboring clusters and neighboring provinces may be correlated. The second perspective is informed by sociological theory, particularly proposition that “social phenomena can only be fully understood within their broader social context” [19]. Applied to the present study, this suggests that mortality risks are shaped not only by individual and household characteristics, but also by the local community and regional environments in which children live.

Accordingly, the modelling framework used in this study allows for both contextual heterogeneity and spatial dependence. At the local level, DHS clusters (PSUs) represent community settings in which children may share exposure to similar services, infrastructure, environmental conditions, and social norms. At the broader level, provinces reflect wider institutional, administrative, and cultural contexts that may also influence child survival The multilevel structure therefore captures residual heterogeneity at both the cluster and province levels, while the spatial specification additionally allows mortality risks to be correlated across neighboring provinces.

Methodology and data

Data source

The analysis utilized two sets of data. First, the study drew on the recently published 2023/2024 DHS data [1], specifically the individual women (“CDIR81DT”) and the all births (“CDBR81DT”) files. To improve temporal alignment between the maternal empowerment measures (discussed in the next section) and child mortality outcomes, the “all births” file data were restricted to births occurring within the five years preceding the survey before the two files were merged. Relevant variables were then extracted and merged to construct a new dataset, in which each married woman—or woman living in union with a husband or partner— was linked to births occurring within the five years preceding the survey. The final analytical sample consisted of 20,947 observations across 778 clusters and 26 provinces in the DRC.

Second, two spatial datasets were used. The first was the DHS cluster shapefile (“CDGE81F”L), which contains centroid coordinates of survey clusters. The second was a provincial shapefile representing the polygons of the DRC’s 26 provinces.1 These spatial datasets were utilized to construct the spatial weight matrices based on inverse distances between clusters and between provinces for the spatial multilevel logistic modelling discussed in subsequent sections.

In the final analytical sample, the number of observations per cluster ranged from 4 to 53, with a mean of 26.9 (SD = 10.0), indicating moderate variation in cluster size without severe sparsity. At the province level, the number of observations ranged from 440 to 1,186, with a mean of 805.7. The number of clusters per province varied from 23 to 36, with a mean of 29.9 (SD = 3.7). Overall, these distributions support the inclusion of both cluster (PSU) and province levels in the multilevel specifications.

Measurement of variables

Maternal empowerment

As outlined in the introduction, this study adopted a spatial multilevel logistic modelling approach to examine whether maternal empowerment is related to child mortality in the DRC. Before presenting the model specification, it is important to clarify how maternal empowerment was operationalized for the purposes of the analysis.

There is broad agreement in the literature that maternal empowerment is a multidimensional concept; however, there is no consensus regarding its direct measurement [10]. As a result, existing studies have employed a wide range of indicators to capture different dimensions of empowerment, often by constructing composite indices from raw data—particularly using DHS or other large-scale household survey data. These indices are then introduced into empirical models as predictors of child health outcomes.

This study selected nine variables from the DHS, covering five dimensions of maternal empowerment, which were simultaneously entered into a multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) to construct a maternal empowerment index. The dimensions include economic participation, control over resources, asset ownership, household decision-making, and financial inclusion, as summarized in Table 1. MCA was particularly appropriate in this context because the empowerment indicators are categorical and are assumed to reflect a latent, multidimensional construct.

Due to space constraints, the MCA results are not discussed in detail here. However, it is important to note that the first MCA dimension—accounting for the largest share of total inertia (16.8%)—was retained as a continuous measure of maternal empowerment. This dimension reflects a general empowerment gradient characterized by women’s participation in economic activities, greater autonomy in household decision-making, ownership of productive assets, and access to formal and digital financial services. Higher values of the index therefore represent the accumulation of empowerment attributes across multiple domains, rather than being driven by any single indicator.

To distinguish individual- and contextual-level effects, the continuous maternal empowerment index was decomposed using within-cluster centering. Specifically, each woman’s empowerment score was expressed as the deviation from the mean empowerment level of her DHS cluster (PSU), yielding the variable empowerment_within, which captures the individual-level effect relative to the local cluster context. In addition, the cluster-level mean of the empowerment index, empowerment_cluster_mean, was included to capture the between-cluster effect of women’s empowerment. This decomposition makes it possible to assess whether child mortality is associated with a woman’s own level of empowerment relative to other women in her cluster, with the broader empowerment environment of the cluster, or with both. Accordingly, the maternal empowerment index forms the basis of the two key explanatory variables used in the regression analysis.

Some limitations in the construction of the maternal empowerment index deserve recognition as the discussion closes. The first MCA dimension in this study accounted for 16.8% of total inertia. Although this seems low compared to the criteria for variance explained in principal component analysis, when compared to principal component analysis, it is not unusual in MCA, as categorical variables usually do not contain information concentrated within a single dimension but rather across several dimensions. Even so, this indicates that significant variation exists in the original empowerment indicators across dimensions that were not included in the index.

Given the limitations, the measurement of women’s empowerment in this index is clearly multidimensional. Women’s empowerment encompasses a number of interlinked economic, social, and empowerment-related processes that cannot be expressed along a single dimension. The retained MCA dimension, therefore, was a partial but indicative measurement of women’s empowerment. The dimensions, especially, included in this study reflected women’s economic participation, women’s ownership of economic assets, women’s financial inclusion, and women’s decision-making. While these dimensions, along which women’s empowerment is measured, are important, they do not reflect dimensions of empowerment that are closely related to women’s and children’s survival, such as women and children’s health care and health-seeking behavior, as well as care processes and practices. Therefore, the lack of a strong average relationship between the empowerment index and the child mortality rate can be explained by the measurement of the empowerment index.

Control variables

The selection of control variables was guided by the existing literature relevant to this study, as well as by the availability and completeness of information in the DHS datasets referenced in earlier Sects [9, 10, 20–23]. Due to space constraints, a detailed justification for each variable is not provided here; interested readers are referred to the aforementioned studies for further discussion. Broadly, the control variables capture the socioeconomic characteristics of mothers and their partners, together with key household-level attributes, as summarized in Table 2.

Parental socioeconomic characteristics are captured by the mother’s age group, the mother’s education expressed in years of schooling, and the husband’s level of education. The references for the categorical variables are adolescent mothers (15 to 19 years) for age group and no formal education for husbands’ educational attainment. More than 65% of mothers were aged between 20 and 34 years. In addition, child-level variables are included, notably the gender of the child and birth order. The reference category for child gender is male, while the reference category for birth order is first-born. In the sample, 51% of children under five were male, and approximately 16% were first-born.

Variables related to household characteristics include the number of under-five children in the household, the place of residence, and household wealth. The reference category for the number of under-five children is one child, while urban is the reference category for place of residence. Notably, more than two-thirds of mothers in the sample reported having two or more children under the age of five, underscoring the relevance of birth spacing in the DRC context. Finally, Table 2 indicates that more than 70% of women in the sample resided in rural areas, highlighting the predominantly rural nature of the study population.

For analytical purposes, maternal education and household wealth were each decomposed to distinguish individual-level variation from socioeconomic variation operating at higher levels of the data hierarchy. This was done to allow more explicit adjustment for socioeconomic composition at the cluster and province levels and to reduce the risk that the estimated empowerment effects were confounded by unmeasured higher-level socioeconomic differences. A within–between decomposition, similar to that applied to maternal empowerment, was used. For each variable, three components were created: a within-cluster component, a cluster-level component, and a province-level mean. This approach aligns with the multilevel modelling literature, which emphasizes the importance of separating individual-level and higher-level variation when estimating effects in hierarchical data structures [24]. Maternal education and household wealth were selected for this decomposition because, unlike many other control variables, they are meaningful both as individual or household characteristics and as indicators of broader socioeconomic conditions within clusters and provinces. Thus, their decomposition allows the model to account not only for the socioeconomic position of individual mothers or households, but also for the socioeconomic environments in which they are located.

For maternal education, Mother’s education_within was computed as the difference between each woman’s completed years of schooling and the mean years of schooling in her DHS cluster. This variable captures the extent to which a woman is more or less educated than others in the same cluster, with positive values indicating above-average education and negative values indicating below-average education within the cluster. Mother’s education_cluster_context was obtained by subtracting the province-level mean years of schooling from the cluster-level mean. It therefore captures whether a cluster has a higher or lower average level of maternal education relative to other clusters within the same province, reflecting between-cluster differences nested within provinces. Finally, Mother’s education_province_mean represents the average years of schooling among women in a province and captures broader province-level variation in educational attainment.

The same decomposition was applied to household wealth. Specifically, Wealth_within measures the deviation of a household’s standardized wealth score from the mean standardized wealth score of its DHS cluster. Wealth_cluster_context captures differences in average household wealth between clusters within the same province, while Wealth_province_mean represents the mean standardized household wealth score within each province. Together, these variables allow the analysis to separate household- or individual-level socioeconomic position from contextual socioeconomic conditions operating at the cluster and province levels.

Given the relationship between socioeconomic status, maternal empowerment, and child health, additional tests were conducted to assess whether the empowerment measure significantly overlapped with the maternal education and household wealth variables. Maternal education and household wealth were not part of the empowerment index, but some association was expected, as the empowerment-related indicators are likely to reflect socioeconomic contexts. Therefore, the association between each item of the empowerment index and the education and wealth variables was examined. Correlation diagnostics for empowerment_within and the separate education and wealth variables, at both the individual and cluster levels, were also conducted. The results showed no strong correlations or significant overlap. Thus, maternal empowerment, education, and household wealth are related but capture different empirical constructs. Consequently, there were no qualms about putting them together2.

Econometric models

Setting the scene

As already noted, the empirical strategy adopted in this study involved estimating spatial multilevel logistic models to address the research objective. Before presenting the model specification, it is useful to clarify the data structure. The child-level observations used in the analysis are hierarchically nested within clusters (PSU) and, which are in turn, within provinces. The analysis is therefore specified as a three-level model, allowing child mortality to be examined within its local and regional contexts.

Model estimation

Model estimation in this study followed a two-step procedure. First, an unconditional (null) three-level logistic model was estimated to assess the extent of residual variation in child mortality at both the cluster and province levels. Second, multilevel logistic models were estimated to examine the relationship between maternal empowerment and child mortality while controlling for child, maternal, household, and contextual covariates. In the substantive models, maternal empowerment was entered through its within-cluster and cluster-level mean components in order to distinguish individual-level and contextual effect, as pointed out in the preceding sections.

Step 1: Unconditional multilevel logistic model

An unconditional three-level logistic model (hereafter referred to as the multilevel null model) was first estimated to assess the extent of residual clustering in child mortality outcomes—specifically infant and under-five mortality—at both the cluster and province levels. This step was essential for determining whether a multilevel modelling approach was more appropriate than a single-level model and for quantifying the extent to which child mortality risks vary across higher-level units prior to the inclusion of explanatory variables. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{ijk}$$\end{document} denotes the mortality outcome for child i in cluster j and in province k;\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{0}$$\end{document} is the overall intercept; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{jk}$$\end{document}is the random intercept for cluster j in province k, assumed to follow:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{jk}\sim N\left(0,\sigma\;_u^2\right)$$\end{document}; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{k}$$\end{document}is the random intercept for province k, assumed to follow: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{k}\sim N\left(0,\sigma\;_v^2\right)$$\end{document}.

Two complementary measures were used to assess the extent of clustering: the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and the median odds ratio (MOR). In the three-level logistic model, separate ICCs were computed for the cluster and province levels. These are given in Eqs. (2) and (3):2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{ICC}_{cluster}=\frac{{\sigma\:}_{u}^{2}}{{\sigma\:}_{u}^{2}+{\sigma\:}_{v}^{2}+\raisebox{1ex}{${\pi\:}^{2}$}\!\left/\:\!\raisebox{-1ex}{$3$}\right.}$$\end{document}3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{ICC}_{province}\frac{{\sigma\:}_{v}^{2}}{{\sigma\:}_{u}^{2}+{\sigma\:}_{v}^{2}+\raisebox{1ex}{${\pi\:}^{2}$}\!\left/\:\!\raisebox{-1ex}{$3$}\right.}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\raisebox{1ex}{${\pi\:}^{2}$}\!\left/\:\!\raisebox{-1ex}{$3$}\right.\right)\approx\:3.29$$\end{document} represents the Level 1 variance component of the standard logistic distribution. The cluster ICC indicates the proportion of total unexplained variance attributable to differences between clusters, while the province ICC indicates the proportion attributable to differences between provinces. Although there is no universally accepted threshold for ICC interpretation, values above 0.02 are often taken to indicate meaningful clustering in health research [25, 26]. In the present study, ICC values exceeding this threshold were interpreted as evidence that a multilevel specification was warranted. In addition to the level-specific ICCs, a combined higher-level ICC was calculated as the sum of the cluster- and province-level ICCs. This measure represents the proportion of total unexplained variance attributable to higher-level contextual structure overall, with larger values indicating stronger higher-level clustering.

For additional interpretation on the odds-ratio scale, the MOR was also computed for both levels. The MOR represents the median increase in the odds of child mortality when comparing two otherwise identical children from two randomly selected higher-level units, moving from the lower-risk unit to the higher-risk unit. The cluster- and province-level MORs are given in Eqs. (4) and (5):4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{MOR}_{cluster}=exp\left(0.6745\times\:\sqrt{2{\sigma\:}_{u}^{2}}\right)$$\end{document}5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{MOR}_{province}=\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\left(0.6745\times\:\sqrt{2{\sigma\:}_{v}^{2}}\right)$$\end{document}

An MOR greater than 1 indicates non-negligible higher-level heterogeneity. In practice, ICC values of at least 0.02 combined with MOR values greater than 1 provide evidence of meaningful clustering and support proceeding with multilevel regression. Finally, it is important to note that the unconditional model specified in Eq. (1) is non-spatial, since both the cluster- and province-level random effects are assumed to be independently and identically distributed. Spatial dependence across provinces is introduced only in the subsequent spatial specifications.

Step 2: Multilevel logistic models

Following the assessment outlined in Step 1, substantive multilevel logistic models were estimated for each dependent variable—infant mortality and under-five mortality—to examine the relationship between maternal empowerment and child mortality after adjusting for child-, maternal-, household-, and contextual-level covariates. As noted earlier, maternal empowerment was not entered as a single undifferentiated predictor. Instead, it was decomposed into two components: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ddot{E}}_{ijk\:}$$\end{document}, representing the woman’s deviation from the mean empowerment level of her cluster, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{-}{E}}_{jk}$$\end{document}, representing the average level of empowerment within the cluster. This specification allows for a clear distinction between individual- and between-cluster-level associations.

The general form of the substantive three-level IID model is given in Eq. (6):6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:logit\left\{P\left({Y}_{ijk}=1\right)\right\}\;&=\;{\alpha\:}_{0}+{\beta\:}_{1}{\ddot{E}}_{ijk}+{\beta\:}_{2}{\stackrel{-}{E}}_{jk}+\sum\:{\beta\:}_{r}{X}_{ijk};\\&+\;{u}_{0jk}+{u}_{1jk}{\ddot{E}}_{ijk}+{v}_{k}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{ijk}$$\end{document} denotes the mortality outcome for child i in cluster j and province k;\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{0}$$\end{document} is the overall intercept, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ddot{E}}_{ijk}$$\end{document} is the within-cluster component of maternal empowerment; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{-}{E}}_{jk}$$\end{document} is the cluster-level mean of maternal empowerment; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{ijk}$$\end{document} is a vector of additional child-, maternal-, and household-level covariates3.

There are two random effects at cluster level in Eq. (6), namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{0jk}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{1jk}$$\end{document}. The first is referred to as a cluster-level random intercept that allows the baseline log-odds of child mortality to vary across cluster, thereby capturing latent local contextual influences shared by children within the same clusters. The latter term is a cluster-level random slope for the within-cluster empowerment component (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\ddot{E}}_{ijk}$$\end{document}). It allows the effect of maternal empowerment to differ across clusters, considering that the nexus between maternal empowerment and child mortality may vary across local settings. In addition to these cluster-level random effects, Eq. (6) also incorporates a province-level random intercept \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({v}_{k}\right)$$\end{document}. This term captures broader contextual influences and residual heterogeneity across provinces in the DRC after accounting for the observed covariates and the cluster-level random effects. It is essential to note that all the random-effects terms in Eq. (6) are assumed to follow an independent and identically distributed (IID) distribution; hence, the specification is referred to as an IID multilevel model (IID model, in short).

However, the assumption of independence is relaxed for both cluster- and province-level random intercepts by introducing spatial structure through the intrinsic conditional autoregressive (ICAR) framework, which is well established in the literature [27, 28]. Under this alternative specification, the cluster-level random intercept is modeled as a spatially structured cluster effect, allowing geographically proximate clusters to share similar baseline child mortality risks. In contrast, the cluster-level random slope associated with the within-cluster maternal empowerment component remains unstructured. At the province level, the random intercept is likewise specified as spatially structured, permitting neighboring provinces to exhibit similar shifts in baseline mortality risk. Substantively, this formulation captures residual spatial dependence in child mortality at both the local (cluster) and broader regional (province) levels, after adjusting for observed covariates. The resulting ICAR multilevel model is formalized in Eq. (7):7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:logit\left\{P\left({Y}_{ijk}=1\right)\right\}\;&=\;{\alpha\:}_{0}+{\beta\:}_{1}{\ddot{E}}_{ijk}+{\beta\:}_{2}{\stackrel{-}{E}}_{jk}+\sum\:{\beta\:}_{r}{X}_{ijk}\\&+\;{u}_{1jk}{\ddot{E}}_{ijk}+{s}_{jk}+{w}_{k}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where everything else is defined as in Eq. (6), except that:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s_{jk}\vert s_{-jk}\sim N\left(\frac1{{\eta\:}_{jk}}\sum_{j'\sim j}\:_{}s_{j'\:k},\:\frac{{\sigma\:}_s^2}{{\eta\:}_{jk}}\right)$$\end{document}

and9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:w_k\vert w_{-k}\sim N\left(\frac1{{\eta\:}_k}\sum_{k'\:\sim k}\:_{}w_{k'\:},\frac{{\sigma\:}_w^2}{{\eta\:}_k}\right)$$\end{document}

Here, the cluster-level random intercepts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{jk}$$\end{document} and the province-level random intercepts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{k}$$\end{document} are modelled to explicitly capture the spatial dependence among clusters and provinces, respectively. Specifically, these terms represent residual variation in child mortality across clusters and provinces under the assumption that geographically proximate areas tend to exhibit similar outcomes, consistent with Tobler’s First Law of Geography. At the same time, they capture spatially patterned heterogeneity across these geographical units. As shown in Eqs. (8) and (9), the ICAR priors imply that the random intercept for cluster j in province k, as well as the random intercept for province k, is conditionally centered on the average of its neighboring clusters or provinces, as defined by the corresponding spatial adjacency structure. Moreover, the cluster- and province-level random intercepts in Eq. (7) account for unobserved contextual influences that are geographically patterned—such as local health system characteristics, service accessibility, cultural practices, or broader institutional conditions—thereby allowing for spatial dependence in child mortality outcomes at multiple levels of the hierarchical model.

Following model estimation, selection between the IID and ICAR specifications was based on the deviance information criterion (DIC), the Watanabe–Akaike information criterion (WAIC), and the marginal log-likelihood. Lower values of DIC and WAIC indicate superior model fit, whereas a higher (i.e., less negative) marginal log-likelihood provides stronger evidence in favor of the model. Consistent with common interpretive guidelines, differences in DIC or WAIC of less than 2 were regarded as indicating negligible differences in model fit. Accordingly, the subsequent discussion focuses on the specification that achieved the best overall balance between goodness of fit and parsimony for each mortality outcome.

Results and discussion

Table 3 presents summary statistics for the null models described in the methodology section for the two dependent variables. Accordingly, separate unconditional models were built for each outcome, that is, infant mortality and under five mortality. The estimated ICCs show the absence of explained, and the presence of unexplained, variation in child mortality at the cluster and province levels. For instance, in the case of infant mortality, the reported ICCs of 0.063 and 0.018 for clusters and provinces, respectively. At the same time, the analysis of under-five mortality, the reported ICCs are 0.061 and 0.019 for clusters and provinces. Therefore, it can be stated that approximately 6% of unexplained variation in both outcomes is attributed to clusters, and about 2% to provinces. The combined higher-level ICCs were 0.082 for infant mortality and 0.081 for under five mortality, suggesting that the unexplained child mortality variation, in terms of contextual structure, is about 8%. Although the ICCs show variation in higher-level child mortality, the numbers suggest a moderate degree of variation, but certainly large enough to justify the multilevel model, and therefore higher-level contextual child mortality is of greater relevance.

The main empowerment variables reported in Tables 5 and 6 do not show statistically credible associations with either mortality outcome. For infant mortality (Table 5), Empowerment_within has a small positive coefficient (β = 0.008; 95% CrI:−0.067,0.084), while Empowerment_cluster_mean is also positive but not statistically credible (β = 0.121; 95% CrI: −0.041,0.281). A similar pattern is observed for under-five mortality (Table 6), where Empowerment_within (β = 0.017; 95% CrI:−0.048, 0.082) and Empowerment_cluster_mean (β = 0.089; 95% CrI: −0.053, 0.231) both have credible intervals that include zero. These results suggest no robust average association between maternal empowerment and either infant or under-five mortality in the IID models. Taken together, these findings show that once the empowerment index is decomposed into its within-cluster and between-cluster components, neither component exhibits a robust average association with either infant or under-five mortality.

It is useful to situate these findings within the broader empirical literature before proceeding further. The findings on the relationship between maternal empowerment and child mortality in the present study contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting that this relationship is neither straightforward nor uniform across settings. Specifically, in this study, women’s relative empowerment within clusters (Empowerment_within) or between-cluster differences in average empowerment (Empowerment_cluster_mean) do not exhibit a clear or statistically credible association with either infant or under-five mortality. This pattern is consistent with recent evidence from Ghana, where women’s autonomy has not consistently been associated with reductions in child mortality and, in some cases, has even been linked to elevated mortality risks. Such findings underscore the importance of family and community dynamics—particularly in strongly patriarchal contexts such as the DRC—in shaping whether and how empowerment translates into child survival outcomes [29].

Evidence from cross-national research further indicates that some forms of empowerment, such as women’s political empowerment, may have limited or no direct association with child mortality, even when they are more clearly associated with intermediate child health outcomes such as nutrition or immunization coverage [30]. More broadly, this body of evidence suggests that the impact of women’s empowerment on child mortality depends on the specific dimensions of empowerment considered, as discussed earlier, as well as on the broader social, economic, and institutional context [31]. Consequently, the absence of a strong association in the present study should not be interpreted as evidence that empowerment is unimportant, but rather as an indication that its influence on child survival is likely context-dependent and mediated by wider structural and institutional forces. Further research is therefore needed to better understand the specific pathways through which different dimensions of maternal empowerment may affect child survival in the DRC.

Further insight emerges from the random-effects component of the model, particularly for infant mortality. In spite of the inconclusive statistical credible relationships between the within-cluster and contextual maternal empowerment, it must be noted that the estimated variance of the cluster-level random slope of mother’s empowerment_within is non-negligible in the analysis of infant mortality. This finding shows that the association between mother’s empowerment_within and infant mortality is not constant across clusters. Instead, this relationship varies across clusters, indicating that local context matters. On the contrary, in the case of under-five mortality analysis, the estimated variance of the random slope of mother’s empowerment_within is close to zero, indicating limited evidence of meaningful variation in the above-mentioned relationship across clusters. Broadly speaking, the above-mentioned results confirm that, in the case of the DRC, maternal empowerment is more context-sensitive in respect to infant survival than under-five mortality. One possibility for such occurrence is that infant survival is more immediately influenced by local caregiving practices, community support systems, and access to early-life health interventions. It is important to note that this interpretation emphasizes the perspective that the effect of women’s empowerment on child survival is often mediated by contextual conditions, including family structures, local norms, and institutional environment, rather than operating as uniform effect across all settings in the DRC.

After examining the estimates for the key predictor of interest, attention turns to the fixed-effects estimates for the control variables capturing child-level characteristics. Several points are worth noting. First, some control variable estimates are broadly consistent in direction and statistical credibility across both the infant and under-five mortality models. The coefficient for female children is negative and statistically credible in both models, with a posterior mean of − 0.306 in the infant mortality model (95% CrI: −0.433, − 0.178) and − 0.306 in the under-five mortality model (95% CrI: −0.433, − 0.178). This indicates that female children have lower mortality risk than male children, holding other factors constant.

Second, the estimates for birth order indicate a positive and increasing association with child mortality in both the infant and under-five mortality models. Relative to first-born children, the coefficient for birth order 2–3 is positive in both models, but its credible interval includes zero, indicating that this association is not statistically credible. By contrast, higher birth-order categories show clear and statistically credible associations with mortality. In both models, children of birth order 4–5 have higher log-odds of mortality than first-born children (β = 0.332; 95% CrI: 0.091, 0.573), while children of birth order greater than 5 face an even higher risk (β = 0.771; 95% CrI: 0.520, 1.023). These results suggest that high birth order is an important risk factor for both infant and under-five mortality in the DRC, consistent with mechanisms such as maternal depletion, intensified caregiving demands, and household resource constraints at higher parity levels.

Third, the estimates for the number of children under five in the household show a statistically credible negative association with both infant and under-five mortality in Tables 5 and 6. Relative to children living in households with 0–1 under-five children, those living in households with two under-five children have substantially lower log-odds of mortality (β=−1.897; 95% CrI:−2.053, − 1.741). The association is even stronger for children living in households with more than two under-five children (β=−3.234; 95% CrI:−3.721, − 2.748). These estimates suggest markedly lower observed mortality among children in households classified as having multiple under-five children at the time of the survey. However, this result should be interpreted with caution. Because the measure is cross-sectional and defined at the time of interview, the inverse association may partly reflect survivorship and fertility-stopping dynamics rather than a protective effect of having more young children in the household.

Overall, the child-level control variables reveal several clear patterns in mortality risk in the DRC. Female children have significantly lower odds of mortality than male children, while mortality risk increases at higher birth orders, particularly for children of birth order 4–5 and greater than 5. By contrast, the negative association between the number of under-five children in the household and mortality should be interpreted cautiously, as this pattern may reflect the composition of surviving children at the time of the survey rather than a direct protective household-size effect.

Turning to mother-level characteristics, maternal age is strongly associated with both infant and under-five mortality. Relative to mothers younger than 20 years, children born to mothers aged 20–34 years have lower log-odds of mortality (β=−0.475;95% CrI:−0.687,−0.263). The protective association is even stronger among mothers older than 34 years (β=−0.993;95% CrI:−1.277,−0.710). These estimates correspond approximately to a 38% reduction in the odds of mortality for children of mothers aged 20–34 years and a 63% reduction for children of mothers older than 34 years, relative to children of teenage mothers. This pattern is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that children of older mothers may benefit from greater maternal experience, maturity, and caregiving capacity [31].

Moreover, the findings suggest that contextual educational conditions matter more than individual differences in educational attainment. While neither a mother’s education relative to other women in her cluster (education_within) nor the average level of education at the provincial level (education_province_mean) is significantly associated with infant or under-five mortality, the cluster-level educational context (education_cluster_context) is negatively associated with both outcomes. This implies that children residing in communities with higher average levels of maternal education face lower mortality risks than those living in less educated communities within the same province, even after controlling for individual socioeconomic characteristics and other covariates. One possible explanation is that more educated communities facilitate the diffusion of health knowledge, awareness of available health services, and health-promoting norms. From a policy perspective, these findings suggest that improvements in child survival may require not only investments in formal education but also complementary Social and Behavioral Change Communication (SBCC) interventions aimed at strengthening health-seeking behaviors, improving maternal and child health knowledge, and promoting the effective utilization of available health services. Finally, the remaining control variables do not exhibit statistically significant associations with infant or under-five mortality after accounting for the other covariates included in the models. Accordingly, the present analysis does not provide sufficient evidence to conclude that these factors are independently associated with child mortality in the DRC.

Concluding remarks

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between maternal empowerment and child mortality in the DRC. It used the recently published DHS in which information was strictly restricted to birth that occurred withing five years preceding the survey and only children born to married mothers were considered. Given that the DHS data is hierarchical in the sense that children are nested within clusters and clusters nested within provinces, it became clear that a multilevel modelling framework was appropriate for such sample. In addition, in line with the practice in multilevel modelling, to avoid the conflation of effects, in particular when the predictor of interest is not centered, this study applied the within group centering that consisted of decomposition the raw maternal empowerment that was created using MCA into two components. The first component was obtained by subtracting each empowerment index to its cluster-level mean, whereas the second component was the cluster-level mean, which basically became a Level 2 predictor. These resulted in two predictors of interest, namely the within-cluster and contextual cluster maternal empowerment indices, respectively. These two components were incorporated into the model to assess the link that its has with infant and under-five mortality in the DRC.

Moreover, in addition to being hierarchical, DHS data is a good candidate of spatially structured data in these sense that clusters and provinces are geographical unit, of which child mortality outcomes in proximate clusters and provinces are susceptible of being influenced in similar fashion because of region-wise unobserved factors. Therefore, a spatial analysis had to be considered. This resulted in the estimation of IID and ICAR models to analyze infant and under-five mortality. After applying a rigorous diagnostic procedure, IID models were selected in both cases.

It emerged that the relationship between within-cluster or contextual-level component and infant and under-five mortality was weaker and more context-dependent, after accounting for control variables, as well as cluster- and province-level factors. It is worth pointing out that, the absence of statistically credible relationships between neither of maternal empowerment and child mortality in the DRC does not necessarily translate into a non-importance of women empowerment, but rather as an indication that its influence on child mortality survival may operate through more complex and heterogenous pathways that are not fully captured a single average coefficient. Further research, beyond the scope of the present study, is thus required to shed light on this issue.

The study also revealed the existence of a substantial residual heterogeneity in child mortality at both the cluster and province levels, with cluster level accounting for the larger share if unexplained variation. The importance of local contextual conditions having more influence in shaping mortality more than regional heterogeneity in the DRC is put to light in this study. Consequently, it is safe to conclude that child mortality in the DRC is, among others, influenced by place-based inequalities operating at multiple levels of the social and spatial hierarchy.

Taken together, the findings in the present study point to the need for policy responses that go beyond a narrow focus on individual maternal or household characteristics. Interventions or public actions to reduce child mortality, as envisaged by SDG target 3.2 and the AU’s 2063 Agenda, in the DRC should continue to support women’s empowerment as an important developmental goal, while also addressing local and regional disparities in health services, infrastructure, and social conditions. In particular, the substantive provincial heterogeneity observed in this study indicates the importance of more decentralized and context-sensitive health systems capable of addressing local socio-cultural barriers that may constrain maternal agency and child survival. More broadly, the present study highlights the value of multilevel approaches that explicitly account for contextual heterogeneity and spatial dependence when investigating the determinants of child survival using spatially structured micro-data in settings such as the DRC.
