A cross-sectional study underlying the sex-stratified associations between muscle strength and bone mineral density among children and adolescents
Authors: Stefany Carolaine B. Silva, Bruna T. C. Saraiva, Eduardo D. Lima Mesquita, Débora T. Furuta, Ewerton P. Antunes, William. R. Tebar, Gerson Ferrari, Eduardo R. de Victo, Diego G. D. Christofaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone mineral density (BMD) is crucial for evaluating bone health, particularly in the pediatric population. Physical parameters such as lower body strength (assessed by jump tests) and handgrip strength (a marker of musculoskeletal health) have been linked to BMD. This study aims to investigate the association between upper and lower limb strength and BMD of the forearm and femur in children and adolescents stratified by sex.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 57 participants (aged 9 ± 2 years) from a philanthropic institution in Brazil. Participants were assessed for anthropometric measurements, somatic maturation, and BMD and lean soft tissue using Dual X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA). Handgrip strength was measured using a dynamometer, and horizontal and vertical jump performances were also assessed. Statistical analyses included Quantile Regression to examine the relationships between strength measures and BMD, adjusted for somatic maturation and lean soft tissue.ResultsSignificant positive associations were found between handgrip strength and femoral BMD, especially in boys (Femoral neck: β = 12.65; 95%CI = 2.39; 22.92,; Trochanter: β = 17.37; 95%CI = 5.04; 29.70; Total femur: β = 11.13 95%CI = 0.98; 21.27).ConclusionUpper and lower limb muscle strength, particularly handgrip and vertical jump performance, were positively associated with BMD of the forearm and femur in children and adolescents with low socioeconomic status, with associations observed in boys.

Introduction

Bone mineral density (BMD) is a fundamental factor for assessing bone health, especially in the pediatric population, as it is related to the risk of osteoporosis and fractures later in life [4, 10]. One of the main aspects of BMD is that it serves as a marker of skeletal tissue, reflecting the strength and integrity of bones [18, 29]. Since the first two decades of life represent a critical period for bone mass accumulation [5], it is important to identify the physical parameters that can ensure a healthy skeleton in the long term [15]. From a biological perspective, bone tissue adapts to mechanical loads, as proposed by the mechanostat theory [8]. According to this concept, bone structure and mineralization are regulated by mechanical stresses imposed primarily by muscle contractions, increasing bone strength in response to habitual loads.

In addition to BMD, lower body strength, which can be easily assessed through jump tests such as vertical and horizontal jumps, serves as a valuable indicator of physical power and muscle strength [19, 22]. Vertical jump height is strongly associated with greater muscular power, which is essential for athletic performance and overall mobility [2, 19]. Similarly, horizontal jump performance, often used as a measure of explosive strength, reflects the ability to generate force and sustain dynamic movements [6], further contributing to physical capabilities and health outcomes.

Handgrip strength (HGS) has been widely used in epidemiological and clinical research as a practical, reliable, and low-cost indicator of overall muscular strength and musculoskeletal health [7, 28]. Although HGS is typically associated with upper limb muscles [28], recent studies have shown correlations with lower limb strength as well [27]. This may be explained by the broader relationship between muscle strength and bone health: as muscle mass increases, mechanical load on bones also increases, stimulating bone formation and, consequently, increasing bone density [9, 10]. Therefore, despite not representing all dimensions of muscular strength, HGS may serve as a useful proxy for general musculoskeletal function in population-based studies. In this context, HGS serves as a valuable indicator not only of upper limb strength but also as a proxy for musculoskeletal health in lower limbs. Investigating its potential as a proxy for lower limb strength is particularly relevant.

Moreover, the physical parameters mentioned previously (BMD, jump performance, and HGS) offer a comprehensive view of musculoskeletal health in children and adolescents. Therefore, this study aims to advance the investigation of the relationship between upper and lower limb strength and BMD, while stratifying the analyses by sex to determine whether this relationship is influenced by biological differences between girls and boys, and considering the state of biological maturation and lean soft tissue as control variables. Understanding how different types of muscular strength act in two distinct anatomical regions is essential to promoting a physically active adolescence and preventing musculoskeletal disorders in adulthood and old age.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to analyze the associations between indicators of upper and lower limb strength stratified by sex (handgrip strength, vertical jump and horizontal jump) and bone mineral density of the forearm and femur in children and adolescents, considering somatic maturation and lean soft tissue as covariates. Our hypothesis was that indicators of upper and lower limb strength would be positively associated with region-specific bone mineral density, independent of somatic maturation and lean soft tissue.

Methods

Sample

The sample was collected in partnership with a philanthropic institution, which serves around 200 children and adolescents and aims to provide adequate care for young people of both sexes, especially basic protection through social support services, in the city of Presidente Prudente, Brazil. More information about the Institution is presented in the study of Saraiva et al. [26]. All children and adolescents from this institution were invited to participate, and those who declined were excluded from the study. This cross-sectional study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Sciences and Technology, São Paulo State University (UNESP), under CAAE number 26702414.0.0000.5402 and approval number 549.549. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal guardians, and assent was obtained from the children prior to participation.

Data collection was conducted by qualified researchers. The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The calculation was based on a meta-analysis [17] investigating the relationship between changes in bone mineral density and muscle parameters in pediatric populations, which reported a pooled correlation coefficient of r = 0.37 (95% CI: 0.23–0.49) between bone and muscle parameters. Adopting a statistical power of 80% and an alpha error of 5%, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 56 participants for the total sample. Consequently, the final sample included 57 children and adolescents aged 5 to 15 years (9 ± 2 years), comprising 37 boys and 20 girls. The inclusion criteria were being between 5 and 15 years old, being enrolled in the philanthropic institution, and not having any physical limitations that would prevent the tests from being performed. The exclusion criteria were pregnancy and not completing the HGS, horizontal jump and vertical jump tests.

Anthropometric measurements

Body mass was assessed using a digital scale with a precision of 0.1 kg (kg). Height was measured using a fixed stadiometer (Sanny, American Medical do Brasil Ltda., Brazil) with an accuracy of 1 mm, following the technical standards described in the literature [11]. The equipment was calibrated in advance in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations prior to the evaluations.

Somatic maturation

Somatic maturation was estimated based on the time to reach Peak Height Velocity (PHV), using the predictive equation developed by [21]. This model utilizes anthropometric measures such as body mass and height to estimate how many years remain until the adolescent reaches the Age at Peak Height Velocity (APHV). Due to the broad age range of the sample, only the time remaining until PHV was used in the analysis.

Bone mineral density and body composition

The Dual X-Ray Absorptiometry (DXA, Lunar DPX-NT; General Electric Healthcare, Little Chalfont, Buckinghamshire, United Kingdom) was used to measure the following variables: bone mineral density (BMD; g/cm2) and lean soft tissue.

The examination consisted of total body scans, which were segmented into specific body regions (femur and forearm) using the equipment’s proprietary software (GE Medical System Lunar, version 4.7). Scanner quality was assessed daily by a trained researcher prior to the first examination, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. All scans were performed in the University’s laboratory, in a temperature-controlled room, by two trained researchers and to ensure measurement consistency, the same researcher was responsible for defining the regions of interest (ROIs), minimizing inter-observer variability. The precision of the equipment, assessed through the coefficient of variation, was 0.66% based on BMD measurements from 30 subjects not included in the study sample.

Lean soft tissue was analyzed as a percentage to mitigate differences in body composition associated with variations in age and maturation stages [20], ensuring individual normalization of the data. Accordingly, the variable percentage of lean soft tissue was used.

Each specific body segment of the femur and forearm was subsequently identified post-scan by the same researcher. Handgrip strength

Handgrip strength (HGS) was measured using an electronic dynamometer (CAMRY Dynamometer, model EH101, Guangdong, China). The values from the device were expressed in kilograms. The following measurement procedures were applied to all participants: i) they remained seated during the assessment; ii) with the elbow flexed at 90 degrees; iii) the test always started with the right hand, followed by the left hand; iv) participants were required to press and hold the dynamometer (for 3 to 5 s) twice with each hand as hard as possible, with a 1-min interval between measurements; v) the evaluators provided encouragement during all trials. The dominant hand was identified based on the hand with which the participant wrote and performed most of their daily activities. The dynamometer was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions prior to testing. All measurements were performed by the same trained evaluator to ensure consistency. The mean value of the two trials (for both hands) was calculated, according to Saraiva et al. [26].

Horizontal jump

For the horizontal jump, a measuring tape was placed on the floor parallel to the jumping direction. Initially, the participant stood with their feet parallel and hip-width apart, positioning the front of their feet on the starting line. The participants performed the jump with a countermovement, using their arms for assistance, followed by a simultaneous push from a knee flexion position to reach the furthest point possible. The measurement was taken from the starting point to the heel of the foot closest to the starting line, with an accuracy of 0.5 cm. Two attempts were made and the highest value was used. The tests were administered by the same trained evaluator following standardized procedures to minimize measurement variability.

Vertical jump

To conduct the vertical jump test, a three-meter measuring tape was attached to a smooth wall. The participant positioned themselves laterally to the measuring surface, with their feet fully planted on the ground and one arm fully extended above their head, where the highest point reached by the middle finger was marked. To facilitate marking, chalk powder was used on the fingertips. From the standing position, the participant was instructed to bend their knees and perform a countermovement jump, using their arms for assistance, and touch the highest possible point on the wall. The measurement was made with a precision of 0.5 cm. The final value was calculated as the difference between the highest point reached (during the jump) and the standing height, with the result expressed in centimeters. Two attempts were made and the highest value was used. The same evaluator conducted all measurements using standardized instructions to ensure consistency across participants.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed to assess normality. The sample characterization variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). To compare sample characteristics according to sex (male and female), the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used.

Associations between strength variables (dominant hand HGS, horizontal jump, vertical jump) and bone outcomes (femur BMD, forearm BMD) were analyzed using quantile regression at the 50th quantile (median). This approach was selected because: (i) it does not assume normality of residuals, (ii) it is robust to outliers, and (iii) the median provides a more reliable estimate of central tendency than the mean in small-to-moderate samples with potential asymmetry, as is common in pediatric development data.

All models were adjusted for PHV (peak height velocity) and lean soft tissue mass. Separate models were fitted for (a) total sample, (b) boys, and (c) girls. Coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values are reported. To control for multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni’s test was applied.

Statistical significance was set at 5% (p < 0.05), and the confidence interval was set at 95%. The statistical package used was IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.

Considering horizontal jump strength, the total sample showed correlations with almost all variables, except Ward’s BMD, Radius UD BMD, and Radius Total BMD. In females, no associations were observed between any BMD variable and horizontal jump strength. In males, associations were observed only for Trochanter BMD, Ulna 33% BMD, and Ulna Total BMD.

Regarding vertical jump strength, correlations were observed in the total sample with Ulna UD BMD, Radius 33% BMD, Ulna 33% BMD, Both 33% BMD, Ulna Total BMD, and Both Total BMD. In females, only Radius 33% BMD was correlated with vertical jump strength. Finally, in males, vertical jump strength was correlated with Neck BMD, Trochanter BMD, Diaphysis BMD, Total BMD, Radius 33% BMD, Ulna 33% BMD, Both 33% BMD, and Ulna Total BMD.

Discussion

The present study aimed to analyze the association between HGS, vertical and horizontal jump, and femur and forearm BMD in children and adolescents. As the main finding, this study observed a positive association between upper and lower limb strength and femur and forearm BMD in children and adolescents. Among the findings, significant associations were observed between HGS and BMD variables of the forearm and femur.

Similar findings were observed in the study by Huerta et al. [13], in which male participants showed moderate to high correlations between HGS and all BMD measurements across various age groups, including children and adolescents. However, the authors did not identify any relationship between HGS and bone variables in females, which differs from the present study, in which significant positive associations were identified in the ulna (Ulna UD and Ulna 33%) and femur (Neck BMD and Diaphysis BMD), as well as a negative association in the radius region (Radius 33% BMD). One possible explanation for these findings is that muscle strength may be associated with higher-intensity physical activities, which may consequently be associated with better bone health [26]. This mechanism may be more pronounced in boys, since males are generally more physically active than females [12]. Furthermore, this result suggests that the relationship between HGS and BMD may be more complex in girls, possibly due to hormonal factors or other physiological conditions that influence this association [24]. Furthermore, the results regarding sex differences should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively limited sample size and the imbalance between groups, with a higher number of boys compared to girls.

The results regarding the relationship between horizontal jump and BMD of the femur and forearm segments indicated no significant associations for either sex, suggesting that horizontal jump may not have a substantial impact on BMD in these regions in children and adolescents. One possible explanation for these findings may be that, although horizontal jumping reflects explosive strength, it may involve different muscle groups or generate less mechanical load when compared to vertical jumps, which could influence bone formation [23]. Another hypothesis is that jump tests involving arm movement, as used in the present study, are more influenced by motor coordination, which may reduce the test's ability to reflect exclusively lower limb strength or power. Thus, performance may be less dependent on strength and more dependent on the ability to synchronize arm and leg movements, which may confound more direct associations with musculoskeletal parameters such as BMD [16].

In contrast, significant relationships were observed between femoral BMD in boys (Diaphysis BMD and Total Femur BMD) and forearm BMD in both sexes (Ulna 33% BMD, Both 33% BMD, Ulna Total BMD, and Both Total BMD) with vertical jump. These findings suggest that vertical jump may have a similar positive impact on BMD in boys and girls, particularly in distal forearm regions. These findings may be related to muscle power, which acts as an important indicator of bone strength and is generally greater in boys than in girls [14]. In the adjusted models, PHV and lean soft tissue were included as covariates. Almeida-Neto et al. [1] reported that lean mass is a predictor of upper limb strength and muscular power in both upper and lower limbs among adolescent athletes, which may help explain the persistent associations observed between vertical jump and BMD of the femur and forearm, even after controlling for maturational status and body composition.

This study advances the literature by investigating different types of strength, including not only HGS, but also horizontal and vertical jump performance, providing a broader view of how these measures relate to BMD. In addition, the identification of positive relationships between muscle strength, particularly vertical jump and HGS, and femur and forearm BMD provides insights for future studies investigating strength-related BMD gains, especially in children and adolescents.

Despite the relevance of investigating different types of strength in relation to forearm and femur BMD, the present study has some limitations. The first is that causality cannot be established due to the cross-sectional design. Another limitation is the potential measurement bias in handgrip and jump tests, which may not fully reflect overall muscle strength. The vertical jump has the limitation of using chalk. In addition, nutritional factors (such as calcium and vitamin D intake) and habitual physical activity were not assessed, limiting a more comprehensive understanding of their influence on bone health. Although the Mirwald equation is widely used to estimate somatic maturation in pediatric populations, it has limited accuracy in younger children and in individuals at the extremes of the maturation spectrum. Furthermore, because it is a predictive model derived from a specific population, it may not fully capture interindividual variability; therefore, its estimates should be interpreted with caution. Finally, given the exploratory nature of this study and moderate sample size, we did not apply corrections for multiple comparisons to avoid inflating type II error [3, 25]. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, requiring replication in larger samples.

Conclusion

The present exploratory study suggested that there is a positive association between upper and lower limb muscle strength and forearm and femur BMD, with low socioeconomic status. Despite the interesting findings of this study, we did not perform corrections for multiple comparisons to avoid the possibility of type II error, considering the moderate sample size. Therefore, in future studies, we suggest, in addition to a longitudinal design, a larger sample size to confirm these findings.
