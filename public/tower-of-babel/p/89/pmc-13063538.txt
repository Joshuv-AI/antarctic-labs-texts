A comparative study on surgical management of long-course symptomatic cervical OPLL between anterior en bloc resection and posterior laminectomy with instrumented fusion
Authors: Kefu Chen, Yiwei Lu, Shu Liu, Lianshun Jia, Xingcheng Dong, Tianwen Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal surgical strategy for patients with long-course symptomatic cervical ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) remains controversial.MethodsFrom January 2012 to August 2021, a single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted to compare anterior cervical OPLL en bloc resection (ACOE) versus posterior laminectomy with instrumented fusion (PLF) in patients with long-course symptomatic cervical OPLL. All patients were followed for 48 months postoperatively. The primary endpoint was postoperative Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score, and the secondary endpoints were the JOA recovery rate (JOA-RR), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) of neck pain, and complications. Analyses used ANCOVA adjusted for preoperative JOA with overlap (ATO) weighting. Prespecified subgroups were defined by the canal occupying ratio (COR), K-line, OPLL index (OP-index), OPLL morphology, and operated levels. Sensitivity analyses included IPTW (ATE) and restriction sets.ResultsA total of 112 patients were analyzed (64 in ACOE, 48 in PLF). Operative metrics favored ACOE with fewer fused levels, shorter operative time and less blood loss. Primary weighted analysis showed that postoperative JOA in ACOE group was higher than that in PLF group, with an adjusted mean difference of 1.68 calculated as ACOE minus PLF (95% CI, 0.15–3.22; P = 0.033). There was no significant difference in JOA-RR and VAS between the two groups. Overall complication rates were low and comparable between the two groups. Cerebrospinal fluid leak occurred more frequently after ACOE, with wide confidence intervals. Treatment effects were directionally consistent across prespecified subgroups without significant interaction, and sensitivity analysis supported the primary findings.ConclusionsIn long-course symptomatic cervical OPLL, ACOE provided a modest but clinically meaningful improvement in neurological recovery at 48 months compared with PLF, with low and broadly similar complication rates. ACOE may be more preferrable when maximizing neurological recovery is the priority, particularly in patients with high COR or K-line negativity, while posterior strategies remain reasonable for extensive continuous multilevel OPLL. Prospective studies are warranted to verify these findings and conclusions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09661-9.Keywords: Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, Anterior en bloc resection, Posterior laminectomy with instrumented fusion, Japanese Orthopedic Association score, Long-course symptom

Introduction

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) of the cervical spine is a heterotopic ossified degenerative disease which may cause compression of the spinal cord and/or nerves, leading to neurological dysfunction [1–4]. Epidemiological studies have shown a generally higher prevalence of cervical OPLL in Asians than that in Caucasians [1, 5, 6]. Most patients with early cervical OPLL have no specific symptoms [7, 8]. But cervical OPLL is a progressive pathological disorder and often leads to a variety of symptoms in late stages [3]. Surgical treatment can improve neurological functions remarkably for cervical OPLL patients with myelopathic symptoms [6].

Anterior cervical decompression and fusion and posterior laminectomy with instrumented fusion (PLF) are two most common operative modalities for OPLL [9, 10]. Cervical OPLL with short-course symptoms tends to have better surgical outcomes [11]. Although some previous studies have compared the surgical outcomes of anterior and posterior procedures for cervical OPLL [7, 9], they rarely discussed the choice of surgical methods for OPLL patients with long-course symptoms (≥ 24 months from the first OPLL related symptoms to surgery).

Despite advances in surgical techniques, it remains uncertain which operative approach can yield superior neurological recovery in patients with long-course symptomatic cervical OPLL. In this cohort, all anterior cases underwent anterior cervical OPLL en bloc resection (ACOE). Both ACOE and anterior cervical corpectomy with fusion (ACCF) involved discectomy and partial vertebral body removal with anterior reconstruction; the defining difference was the mode of OPLL excision [12]. In ACOE, the posterior vertebral wall–OPLL complex was removed en bloc after thinning the four margins with a high-speed burr. By contrast, ACCF removed the OPLL piecemeal with rongeurs after corpectomy. ACCF was not used in this cohort and described here only to delineate ACOE. We conducted a retrospective cohort comparison of ACOE versus PLF, designating postoperative Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) within a prespecified 48-month follow-up window as the primary endpoint and JOA recovery rate (JOA-RR), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) of neck pain as the secondary endpoints. Baseline JOA was adjusted by using ANCOVA with overlap (ATO) weighting before performing prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses by the canal-occupying ratio (COR), K-line status, OPLL index (OP-index), OPLL morphology, and the operated levels.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzheng Hospital (Shanghai, China). Initially included in this study were 161 patients with long-course symptomatic OPLL who underwent surgical treatment with ACOE or PLF in our center between January 2012 and August 2021.

Demographic information including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), symptom duration, comorbidities, OPLL morphology, operated levels, COR, K-line status, OP-index classes, operative time, blood loss, JOA score and VAS score of neck pain were analyzed.

Radiological measures

Radiological data including K-line, OP-index and the canal occupying ratio (COR) were measured. The K-line is defined as the line connecting the midpoints of the C2 and C7 spinal canal on a neutral x-ray lateral view of the cervical spine. The K-line (-) is OPLL exceeding this line, and the K-line (+) is OPLL not exceeding this line [13]. The total number of vertebrae and disc levels involved in OPLL were quantitated by OP-index [14]. The presence of OPLL in the posterior part of each disc or vertebral body was recorded as 1; the higher the OP-index, the more widespread the ossification. According to the cervical OP-index [15], the patients were divided into three subgroups: Grade 1 (cervical OP-index ≤ 5), Grade 2 (cervical OP-index 6–9), and Grade 3 (cervical OP-index ≥ 10). COR was defined as the percentage of the maximum thickness of OPLL divided by the anteroposterior diameter of the bony cervical spinal canal at the same level [16].

Function evaluation

Neurological function was evaluated by the JOA score as the primary index. The secondary indicators were JOA-RR and VAS score of neck pain. JOA-RR was calculated using the following formula: JOA-RR (%) = (Post JOA – Pre JOA)/ (17 – Pre JOA) ×100% [17]. JOA-RR ≥ 75% was defined as excellent; 50–74% as good; 25–49% as fair; <25% as poor [18]. The VAS score was measured on a 0–10 scale, with 0 indicating no pain, and 10 indicating the worst pain.

Surgical procedures

The patients in ACOE group were treated with anterior cervical OPLL en bloc resection [9]. The patient was laid in a supine position. The anterior medial approach to the cervical spine was done. The four margins of the ossified mass were thinned using a high-speed burr until they reached the soft tissue. The mass was then gently elevated using an Allis or Kocher clamp and peeled from the dura using a thin nerve dissector. This allowed the posterior vertebral wall–OPLL complex to be removed en bloc. The resected specimen of OPLL is shown in eFigure S3. This technique avoids repeated manipulation of the compressed cord and helps reduce operative time and blood loss. Reconstruction after ACOE used standard anterior mesh and plate-screw fixation.

The patients in PLF group received laminectomy with internal fixation and fusion [9]. After laying the patient in a prone position, a median incision was made in the posterior aspect of the neck to reveal the spinous processes, vertebral plates and articular synovial joints posteriorly. Total laminectomy of C3-6 was routinely performed, and the lower half of C2 lamina and/or the upper half of C7 lamina were removed in some patients. Side block screws were applied to C3 to C6. If fixation of C2 and C7 was required, pedicle screw fixation was applied without compression forces applied across the screws/rods.

Statistical analysis

PLF served as the reference. Continuous outcomes were summarized as adjusted mean differences (AMD = ACOE − PLF); binary outcomes as RR and risk differences (RD, percentage points) with two-sided α = 0.05. Baseline characteristics were reported as mean [SD] or n(%), with SMDs only (no hypothesis testing); treatment-determined variables (e.g., the number of operated levels, operative time, blood loss, length of hospital stay, and segment of surgery) were descriptive only and excluded from confounding control. To mitigate selection bias, propensity scores were estimated via Generalized Linear Models (preferentially with a cauchit link; Firth-penalized logistic regression used if separation occurred), and ATO overlap weighting was applied (with PS trimming when needed), targeting |SMD| < 0.10. The primary endpoint (postoperative JOA) was analyzed using weighted ANCOVA (model: post_joa ~ group + pre_joa; PLF as reference) with robust (sandwich) variance for CIs; analyses reverted to unweighted ANCOVA when overlap was inadequate. Complications were first summarized raw with a/n(%), RR, RD (95% CI) and Fisher’s exact P; Haldane–Anscombe continuity correction was used for zero cells. When stable under weighting, robust Poisson (log link) was used for RR and identity-link models for RD; otherwise unweighted estimates were retained. Prespecified subgroups/interactions (COR, K-line, OPindex, morphology, operated levels) were examined within the ANCOVA framework as exploratory analyses without multiplicity adjustment. Sensitivity analyses included 1:1 PS matching (nearest neighbor, caliper 0.2 SD), IPTW (ATE) with stabilized weights, and restriction (e.g., operated levels ≤ 3, non-mixed morphology). The primary analysis set required completeness on PS covariates and postoperative JOA; other outcomes were analyzed as available. All analyses were conducted in R (key packages: WeightIt, cobalt, survey, sandwich, MatchIt, logistf).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 161 patients assessed for eligibility, 46 were excluded due to incomplete clinical data (38) and thoracic cord compression beyond the cervical segment (8). The remaining 115 patients (66 in ACOE, 49 in PLF) were included for analysis. Primary analysis further excluded three patients (2 in ACOE, 1 in PLF) from the study due to missing postoperative JOA (Fig. 1). All the 112 finally remaining patients met the prespecified 48-month outpatient follow-up window, and their clinical outcomes were ascertained from the outpatient follow-up records.

Radiological evaluation

Regarding anatomic indices, ACOE had a lower COR (48.3 ± 13.8% vs. 53.9 ± 14.7%; SMD 0.39), while K-line positivity was identical (68.8% in both groups; SMD 0.00). OP-index was markedly higher in PLF (2.2 ± 1.5 vs. 5.4 ± 3.1; SMD 1.33), with a right-shifted distribution in Fig. 2B. Overall morphology of OPLL also differed between the two groups (global SMD 0.85): focal/segmental patterns were more frequent in ACOE group (42.2%/28.1%), while mixed morphology predominated in PLF group (43.8%) with more continuous lesions (31.2%) (Table 1). A representative photograph of the resected OPLL specimen is provided in Supplementary Material I.

Function evaluation

In the primary ANCOVA (adjusting for preoperative JOA) with ATO overlap weighting (logistf-ATO with PS trimming when needed), ACOE was found to be associated with a higher postoperative JOA than PLF, with an adjusted mean difference of 1.68 points for ACOE minus PLF (95% CI 0.15–3.22; P = 0.033), and the effective number used was 15 for ACOE and 17 for PLF (Table 3). For secondary outcomes, the JOA recovery rate showed an ATO-weighted difference of 21.27% points for ACOE minus PLF (95% CI, -6.78-49.32; P = 0.132), and postoperative VAS showed − 0.13 points (95% CI, − 1.06 to 0.80; P = 0.773) (Table 3). The forest plot (Fig. 4) illustrated an overall effect favoring ACOE with directionally consistent patterns across K-line and COR strata. Secondary and follow-up outcomes at approximately 12 and 48 months are summarized in Table 4; point estimates were directionally consistent with the primary analysis, although CIs were wide.

Complications

The perioperative complication rate was low and broadly comparable between the two groups. For CSF leak, ACOE had 13/64 (20.3%) versus 3/48 (6.2%) in PLF, yielding a risk ratio of 3.25 with a 95% CI from 0.98 to 10.77 and a risk difference of 14.1% points with a 95% CI from 2.1 to 26.1; the Fisher exact P value was 0.054 (Table 2; eTable S2). Hematoma and infection did not occur in either group; continuity-corrected estimates yielded a risk ratio of 0.75 and a risk difference of 0.0% points, with a Fisher exact P value of 1.000 (Table 2; eTable S2). Instrumentation failure was marked as not available in this dataset and was not analyzed (eTable S2). In supplementary analyses, survey-weighted models for risk ratio and risk difference were attempted but proved unstable due to limited overlap and zero cells; therefore, weighted results were not reported and the table is flagged as unweighted only (eTable S2).

Additional analyses and diagnostics (post-hoc placement after the four required sections)

Propensity score diagnostics and covariate balance

After ATO overlap weighting (PS model with a cauchit link, segment of surgery excluded per protocol), covariate balance improved substantially; the Love plot showed that all baseline covariates achieved absolute SMDs < 0.10 (Fig. 3; detailed pre-/post-weighted means and SMDs are provided in eTable S1). As illustrated in eFigure S1, the unweighted propensity scores clustered near 0 and 1, whereas the ATO-weighted pseudo-population concentrated within the common-support region, indicating markedly enhanced overlap (caption: “PS model: cauchit; segmental_of_surgery excluded”).

Subgroup analyses across radiographic and surgical strata

Using ANCOVA adjusted for preoperative JOA and reporting effects as adjusted mean differences, Fig. 4 shows that the benefit of ACOE on postoperative JOA was directionally consistent across the prespecified strata of COR (≥ 50% vs. < 50%) and K-line (positive vs. negative); point estimates and 95% CIs aligned with the overall effect, with no qualitative treatment-by-subgroup reversal. In the extended analysis (eFigure S2), stratification by OPLL morphology (continuous, focal, segmental, mixed) and operated levels preserved the overall pattern favoring ACOE. Effective numbers are shown alongside each stratum—for example, continuous 13/15, focal 27/5, segmental 18/7, mixed 6/21; in the ≥ 4 levels stratum (3/48), CIs widened substantially due to extreme imbalance, indicating limited precision (captioned “ANCOVA adjusted for baseline JOA”). Overall, no statistically significant treatment-by-subgroup interaction was evident (directionally consistent; variability largely reflects sample-size distribution) (Fig. 4; eFigure S2). Sensitivity analysis

Three prespecified sensitivity strategies were performed and effects are reported as adjusted mean differences. First, 1:1 PS matching (nearest neighbor, caliper 0.2 SD) failed due to inadequate common support and no balanced matched set could be constructed. Second, IPTW (ATE) with stabilized weights yielded results consistent with the primary analysis for postoperative JOA (approximately 1.36 points, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.90; P < 0.001). Third, in restriction analysis, the operated levels ≤ 3 subset left the PLF stratum empty, precluding estimation, whereas restricting to non-mixed morphology preserved the advantage of ACOE (approximately 1.55 points, 95% CI 0.82 to 2.29; P < 0.001). Directions were stable across strategies, with CIs widening in line with reduced sample sizes (eTable S3).

Discussion

It was found in this retrospective cohort of 112 patients with long-course symptomatic cervical OPLL (≥ 24 months from symptom onset to surgery) and 48-month follow-up that ACOE yielded higher postoperative neurological function than PLF after adjustment for baseline JOA with overlap-weighted ANCOVA. Operative metrics favored ACOE with fewer fused levels, shorter operative time and less blood loss. The overall complication rate was low and broadly comparable between the two groups, although CSF leak was more frequent after ACOE with wide CIs. The treatment effect was directionally consistent across prespecified subgroups defined by COR, K-line, OP-index classes, morphology, and operated levels (no significant treatment-by-subgroup interaction), and the sensitivity analyses (IPTW and restriction) supported the primary findings. Collectively, these data suggest that, among patients with prolonged disease duration, ACOE confers a modest but clinically meaningful improvement in neurological recovery relative to PLF.

The progression of cervical OPLL may exacerbate invasive damage to the spinal cord and/or nerve roots, causing a variety of symptoms [3]. Surgical treatment can improve nerve damage in cervical OPLL patients with progressive myelopathy [10], but there are still controversies over choice of the anterior or posterior surgical approach, knowing that both have respective advantages and disadvantages [6, 19]. It is generally believed that the anterior approach is preferable for neurological recovery because it allows for direct decompression of the spinal cord and can theoretically prevent further progression of the ossification [5]. The posterior approach is relatively less technically demanding, but postoperative neurological recovery is not as good as that with anterior cervical surgery because the ossified material is not removed [9, 20]. In addition, long-term follow-up observations in some studies demonstrated that patients undergoing posterior surgery had reduced neurological function and may even require second-stage anterior surgery [21]. Similarly, a prospective comparative study from our center also demonstrated that anterior surgery was superior to posterior surgery for neurological recovery, especially in OPLL patients with high COR [9]. Compared to patients with a shorter course of disease, patients with a longer course of disease tend to have poorer outcomes [22, 23]. However, no study has discussed which surgical approach is more appropriate for cervical OPLL with a course longer than 24 months. At our center, we usually choose ACOE when direct ventral decompression is necessary and technically achievable, typically in cases of focal or segmental OPLL with a lower OP-index or short-segment and high canal occupancy, especially when the K-line is negative, while PLF is favored for extensive continuous/mixed multilevel disease with higher OP-index or when complete anterior resection is unlikely to be safe or effective. Unlike ACCF, ACOE lifts and removes the posterior vertebral wall–OPLL complex en bloc after thinning its margins, thus avoiding repeated Kerrison-rongeur bites and reducing manipulation of the compressed cord; as a result, the operative time is shortened, blood loss is reduced, and fewer levels need to be fused.

ACOE yielded higher final JOA and JOA-RR than PLF in long-course cervical OPLL, with more patients achieving excellent/good recovery. In prespecified strata with COR ≥ 50% or K-line negativity, ACOE remained superior, consistent with the advantage of direct ventral decompression and with prior reports that posterior surgery can risk suboptimal decompression in high-COR disease [24]. Clinically, ACOE suits localized/segmental lesions with lower OP-index, while posterior approaches often favor extensive continuous multilevel OPLL [18]. It is critical to carefully consider the advantages and disadvantages of each surgical procedure for the sake of making an appropriate choice that can better benefit OPLL patients. We frame the statement that posterior approaches may be more appropriate for extensive, continuous multilevel OPLL as a contextual clinical consideration rather a result-based claim. Our study was neither prespecified nor powered to test superiority within the long segment/continuous subgroup, and subgroup analyses were exploratory with limited overlap. Accordingly, this point reflects the expertise practice and the prior literature—e.g., scenarios in which safe, effective en-bloc anterior resection is unlikely—rather than evidence of comparative superiority in our data.

In contrast to Yoshii et al. [10], who reported longer operative time with anterior surgery, our long-course cohort showed that ACOE offered significantly shorter operative time and less blood loss than PLF, which is consistent with the randomized trial by Cao et al. [9]. In our practice, we usually select ACOE when direct ventral decompression is feasible and safe: ≤3 contiguous levels, anterior column reconstruction uncompromised, any canal stenosis (particularly COR > 60%), loss of lordosis or fixed kyphosis, and focal residual ventral compression after prior posterior surgery; PLF is favored for extensive continuous/mixed multilevel disease (≥ 4 levels or high OP-index) where reliable posterior drift is expected. ACOE is less favorable in cases where K-line is negative across long segments, fixed kyphosis is present, or dural ossification suggests high CSF-leak risk. In addition to the patient factors such as bone quality/osteoporosis, airway or swallowing risk, comorbidities and anticipated construct length also inform the choice. This pragmatic framework explains baseline differences between groups in our series and supports individualized decision-making rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

Previous studies have shown that anterior cervical surgery for cervical OPLL is associated with a higher complication rate as compared with posterior cervical surgery [10]. Our study demonstrated that the incidence of postoperative complications was similar between the anterior and posterior procedures for long-course symptomatic cervical OPLL, though the overall complication rate for ACOE is slightly higher than that of anterior surgery reported in the literature (28.13% vs. 21.8%) [25]. Our study demonstrated that CSF leak was the most common complication in ACOE group, accounting for about 20.31%, which is similar with that reported in the literature for anterior cervical surgery (6.5%-30.8%) [19, 20, 26], and is slightly higher than 8.33% in PLF group. This is considered mainly due to adhesion of about 13–15% of OPLL to the dural sac and dural sac lesions during intraoperative removal of the ossified mass [27, 28]. All cases of CSF leak recovered after application of an artificial dura and postoperative compression bandage, and no intraspinal infection, meningitis, respiratory obstruction, cutaneous CSF leak [29] or secondary surgery occurred in any case. Dysphagia and airway problems are common in ACOE. Dysphagia is mainly attributable to edema of the retropharyngeal tissues caused by constriction of the esophagus or protrusion of the titanium plate during ACOE. Risk factors for persistent dysphagia and airway problems have been reported to include age, prolonged surgery time, multi-segmental surgery and smoking [30, 31]. Treatment for dysphagia requires extra medical costs. In our study, the incidence of dysphagia in ACOE group was not significantly higher than that in PLF group, which may be because we avoided compressing the prevertebral fascia under the titanium plate when placing the plate, and that we did not overstretch the esophagus intraoperatively. However, PLF surgery is recommended for patients who already have dysphagia. Airway obstruction is a life-threatening complication which sometimes requires reintubation. It is therefore important to minimize these complications during anterior surgery. In elderly patients and other high-risk patients with smoking or respiratory comorbidities, PLF should be considered to avoid these complications [10]. Internal fixation dislodgement or subsidence occurred in two cases of ACOE group, but as neither patient developed neurological symptoms, they were followed up closely after discharge, and no re-operation was required for them. The two anterior hardware-loosening events occurred in longer constructs with reduced bone quality, suggesting multifactorial causes (construct length/endplate integrity/osteopenia) rather than device failure; we mitigated the risk by preserving the endplates, applying multi-screw locking, avoiding over-distraction, and optimizing bone health. Using the prespecified fusion criteria (CT bridging or ≤ 2°/≤2 mm motion without lucency), fusion rates were similar between ACOE and PLF at ~ 12 and 48 months, though estimates were imprecise. Collar use and rehabilitation protocols were standardized across groups, likely minimizing performance bias, but adherence was not captured. Finally, the retrospective design, residual confounding despite overlap weighting, reduced effective sample size under weighting, and sparse event counts constrain causal inference; prospective studies with standardized CT-based fusion adjudication and long-term hardware surveillance are warranted.

The study by Yoshii et al. [7]. showed that postoperative pain was significantly higher with posterior surgery than that with ACOE. Postoperative axial neck pain is mainly due to paraspinal muscle injury after posterior surgery. With remarkable advances in surgical techniques, axial symptoms associated with this type of muscle damage has been reduced markedly [32]. However, posterior internal fixation placement requires widespread separation of the paravertebral muscles, particularly the posterior staple-rod system. In addition, the scope of posterior surgical fixation is relatively large, and C2-7 Cobb angle change may also lead to a lesser reduction in cervical axial pain after posterior surgery as compared with ACOE [30]. The results of this study also demonstrated that ACOE offered a relatively lower incidence of axial pain after long-course cervical OPLL than PLF.

This retrospective observational comparison is subject to selection bias in surgical choice; overlap weighting reduces but cannot eliminate confounding. Although multicenter prospective randomized trials would best address this, randomizing surgical approaches in spine surgery is ethically challenging [7]. The study was not designed or powered for equivalence/non-inferiority, and approach-specific complications were uncommon, yielding wide CIs; overlap weighting also reduced the effective sample size, further limiting precision. Accordingly, we report no statistically significant differences rather than claim similarity, and defer definitive safety conclusions to larger prospective cohorts.

Conclusions

ACOE offered better neurological recovery than PLF at 48 months in patients with long-course symptomatic cervical OPLL, while overall perioperative complication rates were low in both groups, showing no statistically significant difference. Shorter operative time and less blood loss also favored ACOE. The benefit was directionally consistent across prespecified radiographic strata such as high COR and K-line negative whereas extensive continuous multilevel OPLL remains a scenario where posterior decompression may be appropriate. Taken together, ACOE may be preferred when maximizing neurological recovery is the priority, with the final approach individualized to morphology and disease extent; prospective studies are warranted to verify these findings and conclusions.
