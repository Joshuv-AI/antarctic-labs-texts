Comparison of Two Strategies of Analysis of Urinary Protein Composition for the Diagnosis and Follow‐Up of Renal Diseases
Authors: Joris Guyon, Jeanne Malaubier, Hong‐Van Pham, Lila Rami Arab, Laurence Pieroni, Marie‐Laure Curutchet‐Burtin, Rémi Segues, Sylvie Caspar‐Bauguil, Annie M Bérard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProteinuria analysis is necessary to detect the early stages of kidney disease before the estimated glomerular filtration rate deteriorates and to monitor the progression of treated kidney disease. Electrophoresis is often the first orientation test, although this test is time‐consuming and its interpretation may be subjective. Two types of electrophoresis gel for urinary proteins are available: (1) high‐resolution (HR) agarose gel and (2) agarose gel combined with immunological detection of specific urinary proteins after their electrophoretic migration (UP). As the former is known to provide the best results for the quantification of monoclonal protein and the latter for its characterization, we only investigated methods for determining the type of kidney damage in our study. Therefore, the aim of our study was to compare two strategies for proteinuria typing: UP gel to HR gel with quantification of specific proteins (albumin, eventually transferrin, α1‐microglobulin, eventually β2‐microglobulin, immunoglobulin G (IgG), and α2‐macroglobulin if blood is present), and HR gel allowing the visualization of RBP, β2‐microglobulin, and transferrin. The two methods were comparable in detecting abnormal excretion of tubular markers (α1‐microglobulin and β2‐microglobulin), nonselective glomerular markers such as IgG, and post‐renal marker (α2‐macroglobulin). The results differed for albumin, whose limit of detection was 25 times lower than the limit of quantification by immunoassay, leading to false positives and no differentiation between low and high excretion of albuminuria. In conclusion on UP gel, when proteinuria typing is prescribed without investigating monoclonal protein, we recommend carrying out immunoassays for specific proteins (e.g., albumin, α1‐microglobulin, eventually β2‐microglobulin, and IgG). In a context of associated monoclonal protein investigation, tubular and glomerular damage markers (excluding albumin) can be interpreted on a UP gel. If blood is present, α2‐macroglobulin must be measured using immunoassays to determine the post‐renal origin of proteinuria using the ratio α2‐macroglobulin/albumin.Keywords: albuminuria, chronic kidney disease, electrophoresis, proteinuria typing, quantitative immunoassays

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a public health priority [1]. It is defined by the presence of markers of kidney damage for more than 3 months, such as a high protein or albumin/creatinine ratio, or a decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [2]. Before the GFR deteriorates, kidney damage may be reflected by the onset of proteinuria, the presence of significant amounts of protein in the urine. Diagnosis and monitoring of proteinuria remain essential to prevent the underlying complications of this condition and to monitor the effectiveness of treatment [2]. Guidance for the selection of additional tests for evaluation of cause includes laboratory tests such as urine protein electrophoresis/immunofixation leading to characterize proteinuria and monoclonal protein [3]. There are several types of pathological proteinuria: overload, glomerular, tubular, mixed, and post‐renal proteinuria, which are characterized by different patterns of urinary protein excretion. Overloaded proteinuria occurs when the production of plasma proteins exceeds the renal capacity for their tubular reabsorption, such as free light chains in multiple myeloma or AL amyloidosis, or the hepatic capacity for their degradation, such as hemoglobin in intravascular hemolysis. Glomerular proteinuria is defined as damage to the glomeruli, which can no longer retain high molecular weight proteins, such as albumin (67 kDa) ± transferrin (80 kDa) in selective glomerular proteinuria, plus immunoglobulin (Ig) G (160 kDa) in nonselective glomerular proteinuria with severe structural defect of the glomerular basal membrane seen in various pathologies (e.g., diabetic nephropathy, lupus nephritis, and transplant glomerulopathy). Tubular proteinuria results from renal tubular dysfunction with impaired reabsorption of low molecular weight proteins such as α1‐microglobulin (33 kDa) (Fanconi or Sjögren syndrome, multiple myeloma). It should be noted that tubular damage may be associated with proteinuria of normal or subnormal concentration. Severe, acute changes in tubular reabsorption (drug nephrotoxicity) but also irreversible structural tubular defects (progressive renal diseases) cause a disturbed tubular reabsorption of numerous smaller proteins (retinol‐binding protein [RBP], 21 kDa; β2‐microglobulin, and 12 kDa). This is associated with chronic tubular damage, interstitial nephritis, and acute and chronic interstitial transplant rejection [4]. Finally, mixed proteinuria is characterized by the presence of both tubular and glomerular proteins in advanced kidney disease. Tubular interstitial involvement, often asymptomatic and therefore unrecognized, has been shown to be critical to the outcome of kidney disease. Thus, elevations in tubular interstitial protein markers reflect these changes and may serve as prognostic indicators [5]. Ultimately, the detection of blood with the reactive strip shows either hematuria with glomerular or excretory origin, hemoglobinuria (intravascular hemolysis), myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis), or contamination. In the former case, the protein profile may mimic nonselective glomerular proteinuria. Then renal hematuria can be differentiated from post‐renal hematuria by measuring the α2‐macroglobulin/albumin ratio [6].

There are several approaches used in medical laboratories to investigate the nature of proteinuria, including quantitative immunoassays for specific proteins or agarose gel electrophoresis techniques. The first strategy is inconsistent in detecting monoclonal proteins except in the case of the discrepancy between proteinuria and albuminuria creatinine ratio [7]. Another limitation is to be unable to quantify and monitor a monoclonal abnormality. The second method does not accurately identify the type of kidney damage. A third method combines immunological detection of proteins with agarose gel electrophoresis to type proteinuria and identify monoclonal proteins.

The aim of this study is to compare two strategies:

One combining immunoassays of specific proteins (albumin, α1‐microglobulin, IgG, β2‐microglobulin and transferrin if necessary, and α2‐macroglobulin in the presence of hematuria) with high‐resolution (HR) agarose gel electrophoresis, which allows the typing of proteinuria (according to supplier specifications) and the quantification of monoclonal proteins (according to international guidelines) [8];

One combining immunoassays of specific proteins (albumin, α1‐microglobulin, IgG, β2‐microglobulin and transferrin if necessary, and α2‐macroglobulin in the presence of hematuria) with high‐resolution (HR) agarose gel electrophoresis, which allows the typing of proteinuria (according to supplier specifications) and the quantification of monoclonal proteins (according to international guidelines) [8];

The other using immunological detection of specific urinary proteins after their electrophoretic migration on agarose gel to identify the different types of renal damage and to characterize monoclonal abnormalities (according to the supplier).

The other using immunological detection of specific urinary proteins after their electrophoretic migration on agarose gel to identify the different types of renal damage and to characterize monoclonal abnormalities (according to the supplier).

In both cases, the electrophoretic separation of proteins is based on their total charge in a given pH buffer. They can then be either directly stained (acid violet) and then analyzed densitometrically for relative quantification of each protein fraction, possibly monoclonal protein (Hydragel HR3 [HR gel], Sebia), or immunofixed by precipitation with specific antibodies against the different proteins, where the precipitated antigen–antibody complex is captured in the gel matrix and stained (Hydragel Urine Profile [UP gel], SEBIA).

By comparing the two methods, we expected to assist the biologist in making an informed choice of method to best serve the clinician for optimal patient management.

Materials and Methods

Human Samples

Our monocentric study (Medical Biochemistry Laboratory, University Hospital of Bordeaux) concerns the analysis of urine from patients with different profiles of proteinuria (physiological, glomerular, tubular, mixed, and post‐renal), performed by agarose electrophoretic gel coupled to immunoassays of specific proteins. All samples were collected on the basis of information from the Laboratory Information Management System software: GLIMS (Clinisys—MIPS) (n = 85). None of them had a visible monoclonal protein on electrophoresis or a known monoclonal IgG. Clinical and paraclinical data were extracted from medical records using a dedicated health record system (DxCare, DeDalus France). All samples were transported and stored at +5°C overnight prior to laboratory analysis and then stored at −80°C prior to comparison of the two study strategies. Prior to this study, eight urine samples were used to check the stability of the proteins after storage at −80°C, which showed no significant difference (data not shown). Nevertheless, all quantitative and electrophoretic analyses were performed on receipt of the samples and after storage at −80°C. Urine pH was adjusted to 7.0–9.0 if <6.0, as β2‐microglobulin is not stable in acidic urine. Some of the urine samples were diluted in order to determine the limit of detection (LOD) after electrophoretic migration, the LOD being the lowest concentration of an analyte in a test sample that can be easily distinguished from zero.

This work complies with the framework for the protection of personal data and privacy provided for in Article 65‐2 of the amended Data Protection Act and the General Regulation on the Protection of Personal Data. It was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (CHU Bordeaux, France).

Quantification of Urinary Proteins

Quantitative determination of total protein and albumin was performed on Architect (Abbott, France) using turbidimetric detection after benzethonium chloride precipitation, an enzymatic method, and an immunoturbidimetric assay, respectively. α1‐microglobulin, β2‐microglobulin, α2‐macroglobulin, and IgG concentrations were determined by immunonephelemetry on a BNII analyzer (Siemens, France). Limit of quantification (LOQ), being the lowest concentration of an analyte in a test sample that can be determined with acceptable accuracy and repeatability, for total protein, albumin, α1‐microglobulin, β2‐microglobulin, IgG, and α2‐macroglobulin as expressed in g/L or mg/L were below 0.07 g/L, 5.0, 5.6, 0.02, 4.1, and 3.0 mg/L, respectively. Reference values expressed per mmol of creatinine were for total protein <15 [9], albumin <3 [2], α1‐microglobulin, and β2‐microglobulin as reliable markers of impaired tubular reabsorption of microproteins <1.58 and 0.034, respectively, and IgG as a nonselective marker of glomerular injury <1.13 [10, 11]; α2‐macroglobulin as a marker of post‐renal cause of proteinuria must be undetectable.

Electrophoretic Methods

According to the manufacturer's recommendations, urine electrophoresis was performed on unconcentrated samples using the Hydragel 15 HR kit (ref: 4122; Sebia, France) and the Hydragel Urine Profile kit (ref: 4332; Sebia) on the Hydrasys 2 system (Sebia), a semiautomated multiparameter instrument. This was linked to the Phoresis software, which enabled the review, interpretation, and validation process. For the second type of gels (Figure 1), the proteins separated by electrophoresis were immunofixed with antisera against tubular (α1‐microglobulin, RBP, and β2‐microglobulin), glomerular (albumin and IgG), and post‐renal proteins (α2‐macroglobulin), as well as heavy and light chains of Ig (G, A, M, D, E, kappa, and lambda) and free light chains (kappa and lambda). For each series of analyzed patients, the two gels were run within 24 h of thawing. Interpretation was visual, and profiles were categorized as normal, glomerular (selective or not), tubular, mixed, or post‐renal proteinuria. Two persons, including a medical biologist, visually analyzed all gels on the same day they were run. They compared the results of the UP gels with those of the HR gel + quantitative immunoassays combination. They reanalyzed all results at the end of the study.

Example of UP gel electrophoresis. The first lane “ELP” corresponds to all migrated urinary proteins, which were revealed by only staining. The revealed proteins on the other lanes correspond to colored antigen–antibodies complex: Tub, tubular proteinuria revealed with using α1‐microglobulin, RBP, and β2‐microglobulin antibodies; Alb/A2M, albumin/α2‐macroglobulin; GAM, IgG, IgA, IgM; DE, IgD, IgE; Kl/f, bound/free kappa; Ll/f, bound/free lambda.

To determine false positive and negative results got by UP gels, we compare the detection of specific proteins on UP gels to interpretation of immunoassay tests in mg/mmol of creatinine. A detection of a specific protein on UP gels associated with a result expressed as mg/mmol of creatinine below reference value was considered false positive. On the contrary, there was no detection on UP gels, whereas the result in mg/mmol of creatinine above reference value was considered false negative. Therefore, true positive/negative results, respectively, corresponded to a detection/no detection of a specific protein on UP gels when this one was above/below reference value expressed in mg/mmol of creatinine.

Results and Discussion

Table 1 describes the range of values for specific proteins in mg/L and in mg/mmol of creatinine used in our study. It is generally accepted that a 24‐h urine collection is the gold standard for typing proteinuria. However, it is time‐consuming and difficult to control. After analyzing the data in the international literature, the Haute Autorité Santé in France concluded that it is not necessary to collect 24‐h urine samples for the screening of kidney disease [12]. As a result, the determination of urinary protein or albumin excretion from a single urine sample can be performed at any time of day, and the result expressed as the ratio of proteinuria/creatininuria or albuminuria/creatininuria in units of the International System of Units (mg/mmol). Therefore, all specific protein results were expressed in mg/mmol creatinine. The wide range of values used in this study makes it possible to cover all types of nephron damage to varying degrees of severity.

α1‐Microglobulin

α1‐Microglobulin was not detected on the UP gel at a concentration ≤5.82 mg/L close to the LOD on a nephelometer analyzer (n = 21, all true negatives <1.58 mg/mmol creatinine), whereas the protein was 100% visualized at a concentration ≥11.5 mg/L (n = 54). Of these latter samples, five did not reach significance when α1‐microglobulin was expressed as mg/mmol creatinine, resulting in five false positives on UP gels. Between these two values (n = 10) and compared to α1‐microglobulin expressed as mg/mmol creatinine, three samples were negative on UP gels (one true negative, one false negative, and one true positive with positive β2‐microglobulin and RBP), and seven were positive (seven true positives). In total, the results described five false positives and one false negative for α1‐microglobulin on UP gels with values for α1‐microglobulin ranging from 0.52 to 1.28 and being 5.05 mg/mmol creatinine, respectively. The only case of false negative results on UP gels was in a 6‐year‐old patient hospitalized for 12 days to monitor his hemolytic uremic syndrome, with no need for extrarenal dialysis (GFR at 80 mL/min) but transfusion of globule pellet because of significant anemia. Ultrasonography showed no morphologic or vascular abnormalities in either kidney. Laboratory analysis showed mixed proteinuria: total proteinuria at 130, albuminuria at 74.5, α1‐microglobulin at 5.05, and IgG at 2.31 mg/mmol creatinine. The appearance of the tubular marker protein α1‐microglobulin combined with glomerular proteinuria is a frequent finding in hypertensive or diabetic microangiopathy or advanced forms of renal disease. Involvement of the tubular interstitium, often without symptoms and thus unrecognized, is frequently critical for the final outcome of kidney disease. α1‐Microglobulin is a biomarker of tubular dysfunction able to improve characterization and prognostication for patients at risk for or with established CKD [13].

β2‐Microglobulin

β2‐Microglobulin was not detected on the UP gel at a concentration ≤0.28 mg/L (n = 18, all true negatives <0.034 mg/mmol creatinine), whereas the protein was 100% visualized at a concentration ≥0.80 mg/L (n = 29). Of these latter samples, all reached significance when β2‐microglobulin was expressed as mg/mmol creatinine (all true positives), and all of them got α1‐microglobulin above the usual levels. Between these two values (n = 2) and compared to β2‐microglobulin expressed as mg/mmol creatinine, one was a true positive result, and the other one was negative on UP gels (one false negative) and got a β2‐microglobulin level at 0.069 mg/mmol creatinine (N < 0.034) by immunonephelometry analysis. However, the corresponding concentration of β2‐microglobulin was 0.4 mg/L close to the LOD on UP gels. It should be noted that severe and acute changes in tubular reabsorption (drug nephrotoxicity) but also irreversible structural tubular defects (progressive renal disease) cause impaired tubular reabsorption of numerous smaller proteins such as RBP (21 kD) and β2‐microglobulin (12 kD). The resulting “complete tubular proteinuria” is associated with chronic tubular damage, interstitial nephritis, and acute and chronic interstitial graft rejection [4].

Albumin

Albumin was not detected on the UP gel at a concentration ≤0.2 mg/L (n = 3 or 3.5% of samples, all true negatives <3 mg/mmol creatinine), whereas the protein was 100% visualized at a concentration >0.2 mg/L (n = 82). When albumin was expressed as mg/mmol creatinine, 30 samples were false positive on UP gels (35.3% of total samples), and 52 samples were true positives (61.2%) but without distinguishing categories of albuminuria. There are actually three categories of albuminuria [2], which led to the term “microalbuminuria” no longer being used: A1: normal albuminuria: <3 mg/mmol of creatinin; A2: moderately increased albuminuria: between 3 and 30 mg/mmol; A3: enforced albuminuria: >30 mg/mmol. In our study, 20 cases got albuminuria below 30 mg/mmol of creatinine and 32 above 30 mg/mmol of creatinine. It is worth noting that, on the UP gels, when we compared albumin detected by antibodies (lane Alb/A2M) with albumin and transferrin detected on the first lane by colorimetry (lane ELP), we were able to better adjust the interpretation as shown in Figure 2. As a consequence, of the 30 false positives analyzed from the Alb/αM lane, 19 samples could now be considered to have normal albumin excretion, increasing the true negative percentage from 3.5 to 22.4. Nevertheless, almost 13% of total samples (n = 11) were false positive as the LOD for albumin was very low, 25 times lower than the LOQ. If no obvious cause is found, this could lead to control on a new urinary sample exploring albumin by immunoassay in order to determine the rate of albumin excretion. For the other 63 samples considered positive after analyzing the Alb/αM lane, taking into account the transferrin on ELP lane made it easier to interpret the presence of albumin. It was present in 31.8% of samples (n = 27), leading to glomerular proteinuria. Then proteinuria is said to be selective when it consists mainly of albumin (high‐flow albuminuria) and proteins of similar molecular weight, such as transferrin, and nonselective when its composition is similar to that of plasma proteins with proteins of higher molecular weight, such as IgG. For 42.4% of total samples (n = 36), transferrin on the ELP lane was not shown. In these cases, it was not possible to distinguish between normal or low/high albuminuria excretion. Currently, low‐flow albuminuria (albuminuria/creatininuria ratio of 3–30 mg/mmol) is considered a risk marker for CKD in Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes and as an independent risk marker for cardiovascular risk in hypertensive patients [14]. In the presence of low‐flow albuminuria, there are specific recommendations for the choice of treatment aimed at renal protection and/or hypertension control [2]. On the other hand, low albumin excretion with an albumin/proteinuria ratio <30% may also be associated with low molecular weight protein excretion in tubulopathies without glomerular disease. Finally, high‐flow albuminuria (albuminuria/creatininuria ratio above 30 mg/mmol) without transferrin must be identified and differentiated from low‐flow albuminuria, as it defines selective glomerular proteinuria. If the cause is unknown, a kidney biopsy is required.

Immunoglobulin G

IgG was not detected in the UP gel at a concentration ≤4.30 mg/L (n = 39, all true negatives <1.13 mg/mmol creatinine), whereas they were 100% visualized at a concentration ≥16.90 mg/L (n = 32, all true positives). All samples reached significance when IgG was expressed as mg/mmol creatinine. Between these two values (n = 14) and compared to IgG expressed as mg/mmol creatinine, eight samples were negative on UP gels (seven true negatives and one uninterpretable due to hematuria) and six were positive (three true positives and three false positives). Among these three false positive IgG on UP gels, IgG levels obtained by immunonephelometric analysis ranged from 0.55 to 0.86 mg/mmol creatinine (N < 1.13). On the basis of the routine analysis, two patients had very moderately elevated proteinuria (15 and 17.6 mg/mmol creatinine) and albuminuria (5.3 and 8.0 mg/mmol creatinine) without tubular or other glomerular markers. The first patient, aged 37 years, was being monitored for Type 2 diabetes. At the time of urinalysis, creatinine was 73 µmol/L, corresponding to a calculated GFR of 91 mL/min/1.73 m2. There was no major ion disturbance. The second patient, 18 years old, had chronic renal failure of unknown etiology that was discovered 3 years prior to his kidney transplantation. Since then, GFR > 90 mL/min/1.73 m2, good graft function, and no significant complications were observed at the time of the study. In these two cases, there was no evidence of nonselective glomerular damage. The third case presented with proteinuria at 84.2 mg/mmol creatinine and forced albuminuria (53.1 mg/mmol creatinine), but no glomerular markers other than transferrin. The proteinuria was considered a selective glomerular injury. This is a 48‐year‐old patient who had been followed for 8 years because of proteinuria of 80 mg/mmol with 70% albumin and microscopic hematuria without renal failure (GFR > 90 mL/min/1.73 m2). On light microscopy, the renal parenchyma has no visible abnormality and no glomerular deposits on immunofluorescence (data not shown).

α2‐Macroglobulin

α2‐Macroglobulin was also examined when hematuria has been demonstrated with the test strip. There was no detection on the UP gel at a concentration ≤2.40 mg/L (n = 29, all true negatives), whereas it was 100% visualized at a concentration ≥4.28 mg/L (n = 12, all true positives). All of these last 12 samples reached significance when α2‐macroglobulin was expressed as mg/mmol creatinine. But visualization of α2‐macroglobulin on gels did not allow differentiation between glomerular and post‐renal bleeding. Immunoassays actually would have made the difference by calculating the α2‐macroglobulin/albumin × 100 ratio. Between 2.40 and 4.28 mg/L (n = 4) and compared to α2‐macroglobulin expressed as mg/mmol creatinine, three samples were at the LOD, and for the fourth one with an albumin level <100 mg/L, a test would have required the morphological study of red blood cells (not done) [15].

Concluding Remarks

Regardless of the GFR, the persistence for more than 3 months of biological markers of renal involvement and/or morphological anomalies indicates a kidney disease that requires a diagnostic etiologic and morphological monitoring. It should be noted that a reference range GFR does not exclude renal disease [16]. KDOQI consequently recommends proteinuria analysis for detecting the early stages and GFR estimations for assessing the progression of kidney disease [17].

Our study evaluated the interpretation of proteinuria when urinary proteins were analyzed by either combining immunoassays of specific proteins with HR agarose gel electrophoresis (HR3 gels) or using immunological detection of specific urinary proteins after their electrophoretic migration on agarose gel (UP gels).

For the first time, we demonstrated that the two methods were comparable in the detection of abnormal excretion of tubular markers (α1‐microglobulin and β2‐microglobulin), nonselective glomerular markers such as IgG, and α2‐macroglobulin. This is related to the LOD on UP gels, which is very close to the LOQ of these proteins by immunoassay. It is worth noting that, in the presence of α1‐microglobulin alone on UP gels, it may be necessary to add a comment regarding the interpretation of tubular function, such as “to be monitored,” the best being a quantitative assessment of α1‐microglobulin. However, the presence of the three markers of tubular damage on UP gels led to the conclusion of “complete tubular proteinuria” without the necessary confirmation by immunoassays. Finally, even though we did not get any false negatives or positives for α2‐macroglobulin, it is recommended to use an α2‐macroglobulin/albumin ratio × 100 > 2 to define post‐renal proteinuria. On the other hand, the conclusion was different for albumin, which had an LOD 25 times lower than the LOQ by immunoassay leading to false positive and no differentiation between low and high albuminuria excretion. In any case, its dosage is recommended for chronic renal disease diagnosis and follow‐up [2, 18, 19, 20], and it has to be done by immunoassay test, which is widely available and easy to use.

Whatever the approach for the proteinuria typing used by a medical laboratory, albumin and α2‐macroglobulin have to be analyzed by immunoassays.
