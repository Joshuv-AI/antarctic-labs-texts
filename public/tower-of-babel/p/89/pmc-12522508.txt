Clinical implications of ctDNA-based minimal residual disease detection in newly diagnosed peripheral T-cell lymphoma: a single-center cohort study
Authors: Jin-Hua Liang, Wei Hua, Hua Yin, Yue Li, Xin-Yi Zhang, Jun-Heng Liang, Liu-Qing Zhu, Rui Gao, Chen-Xuan Wang, Yang Shao, Bi-Hui Pan, Xin-Yu Zhang, Jia-Zhu Wu, Qiu-Xiang Ou, Jian-Yong Li, Hao-Rui Shen, Li Wang, Wei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has been recognized as a promising tumor-specific biomarker, but its molecular features in peripheral T cell lymphoma (PTCL) have not been well explored. We investigated the translational significance of liquid biopsy in a uniformly treated PTCL cohort (N=64). Our study found that pretreatment ctDNA burden was strongly associated with clinical risk factors and identified as a superior predictor of progression-free survival and overall survival. Although 46.9% of patients achieved complete response at the end of therapy (EOT), only 25.9% achieved negative minimal residual disease (MRDend-) and demonstrated superior prognosis. These findings suggests that MRD status at EOT may be a critical factor in disease progression and recurrence for PTCL patients. Additionally, the most frequently altered genes were identified as TET2 (6.7%), DNMT3A (48.5%), RHOA (27.3%), and TP53 (15.2%), which were not cleared by first-line CHOP-like regimens. Most importantly, clonal evolution was displayed during induction therapy and follow-up across all histological subtypes of PTCL patients.These findings support that EOT MRD status could serve as an important prognostic marker for PTCL patients and clear the direction of exploration and selection the new drugs particularly targeted to TET2/DNMT3A/RHOA mutation integrating with conventional treatments for PTCL patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02438-y.

Introduction

Peripheral T cell lymphoma (PTCL) is a subtype of T cell lymphoma that originates from mature T lymphocytes in peripheral locations (e.g., lymph nodes, spleen, gastrointestinal tract, and skin). PTCL is known for its strong invasiveness and extremely complex heterogeneity, accounting for approximately 10% of all cases of non-Hodgkin lymphomas. Anthracycline-based chemotherapies, such as CHOP, are commonly used as first-line therapy, with autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) recommended as a consolidation treatment for PTCL patients. However, under the current strategies, a high incidence of relapse, disease progression, and death is observed in these patients[1, 2]. The main reasons for the poor prognosis in PTCL patients include: (1) the low incidence of complete response (CR) after first-line chemotherapy; (2) relapse in patients who achieve CR, possibly due to the presence of residual disease that is undetectable using imaging methods; (3) the emergence of aggressive treatment-resistance clones, which result in refractoriness to subsequent salvage therapies. Therefore, it is vital to identify patients at high risk of treatment failure, predict early clinical outcomes during diagnosis or treatment, monitor minimal residual disease (MRD) in patients who achieve CR or partial response (PR) after first-line therapy, and optimize patient selection for targeted therapy according to genetic landscape of new clones for effective PTCL patient management.

Liquid biopsy has emerged as an innovative and valuable approach in the field of non-invasive or minimally invasive diagnosis[3]. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in the blood, the most important biomarker of liquid biopsy, has been recognized as a promising tumor-specific biomarker in various cancers, including lymphomas[4–8]. However, its clinical utility remains underexplored in PTCL patients. Potential applications of ctDNA in these patients include early diagnosis, prognosis assessment, treatment monitoring, resistance evaluation, MRD detection, and guiding treatment strategies.

This study aimed to investigate the translational significance of plasma ctDNA in a newly diagnosed PTCL cohort who received CHOP/CHOP-like regimens as first-line treatment. First, we identified mutational features in pretreatment tumor and plasma ctDNA to explore potential prognostic factors. Second, we assessed ctDNA at the end-of-therapy (EOT) to determine its correlation with treatment response. Next, we explored transcriptional signatures, including tumor immune microenvironment, associated with ctDNA status at EOT. Lastly, we investigated dynamic clonal evolution over time in these patients.

Methods and materials

Patients and study design

A total of 64 newly diagnosed PTCL patients were consecutively enrolled at our center between August 2021 and September 2023. Their diagnosed subtypes included angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL), PTCL not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS), anaplastic lymphoma kinase positive and negative systemic anaplastic large cell lymphomas (ALK+ ALCL and ALK- ALCL), and enteropathy associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL). ALCL patients received the BV-CHP (brentuximab vedotin, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone) regimen as first-line treatment, while other PTCL patients received the CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) regimen. Tumor samples were collected at pretreatment (i.e., before fist-line treatment) for targeted DNA and bulk RNA sequencing. Oral swab (buccal) samples were also collected at pretreatment as controls for germline mutations. Blood samples were collected at the pretreatment, EOT, and at relapse for targeted DNA sequencing. A graphic outline of the study design and sample collection at different time points is illustrated in Figure 1A. Baseline clinical characteristics, including patient demographics, sex, age, Ann Arbor stage, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), bone marrow aspiration and biopsy, international prognostic index (IPI), computed tomography (CT) scans, and position emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) scans, were collected from patient records. Treatment response including complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD) was assessed in all patients using PET/CT according to the Lugano 2014 criteria[9]. The last follow up deadline is June 2024. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Broad of Jiangsu Province Hospital (No. 2023-SR-190). DNA extraction, library preparation, and NGS

Tumor genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples or fresh tissues using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Peripheral blood (8-10 ml) was collected at specified intervals in Streck cfDNA Blood Collection Tubes and processed within 48 hours to isolate cfDNA using the Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen). Oral swab DNA was prepared by QIAamp DNA Mini Kit(Qiagen) as control for germline mutations. DNA quantification was performed using the dsDNA HS Assay Kit on a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed at Nanjing Geneseeq Technology Inc., a CLIA- and CAP-accredited lab Sequencing libraries were constructed using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (KAPA Biosystems), and used a bi-barcoding known as Automated Triple Groom Sequencing (ATG-Seq) to enhance the purification of the cfDNAsequencing background[10]. Target enrichment was performed using a panel that covers 475 genes related to hematopoietic and lymphoid neoplasms (HemasalusTM, Nanjing Geneseeq Inc., Table S1). The input amount for cfDNA is 50 ng, whereas for tissue gDNA, it is 1000 ng. For oral swab (buccal) samples, the input is 500 ng. In this study, the average deduplicated depth was 781X for the tissue gDNA, and 3176X for the plasma cfDNA, with oral epithelial cell samples sequenced to a depth of 200X.

Sequence alignment and mutation calling

Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) and processed with Picard and GATK. Germline mutations were excluded by comparing matched oral swab DNA, and somatic mutations were filtered to remove common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, and 1000gEAS. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (INDELs) were identified with VarScan2 and annotated with ANNOVAR. Structural variants(SVs) and gene fusions were detected with icallSV and annotated with iAnnotateSV. Copy number variations (CNVs) were identified using ADTex, with amplification and deletion defined by fold changes >1.6 and <0.6, respectively. In both gDNA and cfDNA samples, SNVs , INDELs and SVs with a VAF of at least 0.3% and supported by at least three unique high-quality reads on different strands were retained[4]. Candidate mutations were manually reviewed with IGV to exclude anomalies and recover reliable sites with VAF below the limit of detection (LoD) but supported by evidence from multiple samples. To exclude Clonal Hematopoiesis of Indeterminate Potential (CHIP)-related variants, mutations were cross-referenced with the Chinese CHIP database[11, 12], and longitudinal ctDNA monitoring was conducted when feasible. Quality control data for sequencing and mutation information can be found in Tables S2 and S3.

ctDNA assessment

The ctDNA burden for each sample (hGE/mL) was calculated using the following formula: multiply the mean value of variant allele frequency (VAF) by the cell-free DNA concentration (pg/mL plasma) and then divide by the haploid genomic equivalent weight of 3.3 pg. The results were expressed as base-10 logarithms (log hGE/mL). ctDNA status at EOT was reported as MRD negative (MRDend-) or positive (MRDend+). The status of MRD was determined as follows: (1) if tissue samples were available, MRDend+ indicated the detection of any mutations present in the tissue; (2) if tissue samples were not available, MRDend+ was defined by the detection of at least one driver mutation[13] (Table S4) in plasma ctDNA corresponding to the baseline plasma. MRDend- was defined as un-detection of any mutations present in the plasma samples.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples was extracted using a RNeasy FFPE kit (QIAGEN). Libraries were prepared using a KAPA Stranded RNA-seq Kit with RiboErase (HMR) (KAPA Biosystems), followed by ribosomal RNA depletion using Rnase H. Libraries were then sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq NGS platforms (Illumina). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the “Limma” package in R software. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)[14] and Gene ontology (GO)[15] enrichment analyses were performed using the “ClusterProfiler” package. To comprehensively assess the degree of immunological infiltrate, various bioinformatic algorithms were utilized, including Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER)[16], Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subpopulations of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT)[17], QUANTISEQ[18], Microenvironment Cell Populations-counter (MCPcounter)[19], Xcell[20], and Estimation of Proportion of Immune and Cancer cells (EPIC)[21]. Each algorithm employed unique strategies and gene expression signatures to estimate the abundance of different immune cell subpopulations. In addition, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed to obtain per-sample pathway/immune-signature enrichment scores using the ssGSEA implementation in the GSVA R package; the immune and pathway gene sets were derived from the resource[22]. The ImmunophenoScore (IPS) was employed to assess the immune state of the tumor samples. This tool quantifies and visualizes four key immunophenotypes (antigen presentation, effector cell activity, suppressor cell activity, and checkpoint marker expression) using specific markers of immune response and tolerance[23].

Clonal evolution analysis

The clonal evolutionary trajectory was reconstructed through an integrative computational framework. Initially, PyClone (v0.13.1) was utilized to infer cellular prevalence (CP) values across multiple plasma samples of each patient, followed by Bayesian clustering of mutations sharing congruent CP dynamics. Phylogenetic tree reconstruction was subsequently performed using SCHISM (v1.1.2). Finally, spatiotemporal visualization of clonal architecture was generated via Timescape (v3.4.2), implementing geometric vectorization to produce fishplot schematics that integrate clone proportion trajectories with phylogenetic relationships.

Statistical analysis

Differences in ctDNA burden between two groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare three or more groups. Frequency differences were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Correlations between two continuous variables were assessed using Pearson analysis. Progression-free survival (PFS) was measured from the date of diagnosis to the date of disease progression, death from any cause, or the last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was measured from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from any cause or censored at the date of the last follow-up. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to identify factors related to survival outcomes, which were visualized using forest plots. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was applied to systematically assess the predictive contribution of baseline genetic variants to MRD status of patients. Area under receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUCs) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to evaluate the predictive capacity for EOT MRD status of a model. A P-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.3.1), SPSS software (version 20.0), or GraphPad Prism (version 10.0).

Results

Characteristics of the study cohort

The median age of these 64 patients was 64 years (range, 12 to 84 years). Four subtypes were identified in these patients, including 25 AITL, 17 PTCL-NOS, 7 ALK+ ALCL, 11 ALK- ALCL, and 4 EATL patients. At the EOT, 30 patients achieved CR (46.9%), 10 patients achieved PR (15.6%), 14 patients had PD (21.9%). Additionally, 10 patients died during first-line treatment. Pretreatment ctDNA level reveals different outcomes behind conventional risk groups

The top five most frequently altered genes were identified as TET2, DNMT3A, RHOA, IDH2, and TP53 in both tissue and plasma (Figure 1B and Figure S1A, respectively). The majority (N=130) of the mutations were identified in both plasma ctDNA and tissue gDNA, with comparable frequencies observed among the top 20 altered genes in tissue (Figure S1B). Analysis of gene mutations revealed that TET2 mutations predominantly co-occur with DNMT3A, RHOA, and IDH2, while exhibiting mutual exclusivity with ALK mutations (Figure S1C and Table S5). The pretreatment ctDNA burden was significantly higher in patients with advanced stage, elevated LDH, more than one extranodal site, bone marrow involvement, and a high-risk IPI score (P<0.05, Figure 1C). Among the five PTCL subtype groups, the highest ctDNA burden was observed in AITL and PTCL-NOS patients (Figure S1D).

Next, we want to explore the new risk factors such as pretreatment ctDNA level and top gene mutations except the conventional IPI risk factors to see whether they can provide additional prognostic value. The median follow-up time for our cohort was 20.0 months (range: 8.0–34.0 months). The median PFS was 16.0 months, while the median OS was not reached (Figure S1E). Using a cutoff value of 2.594 log hGE/mL, as determined with the X-tile software[24], we categorized our cohort into high and low pre-ctDNA burden groups. High pretreatment ctDNA burden was significantly associated with worse PFS (P=0.003, Figure 1D) and OS (P=0.001, Figure 1E). Next, we evaluated genetic variations with frequencies above 10% in relation to patient survival, considering both tissue and plasma variations. We found that patients with TP53 mutation (TP53mut) and STAT3 mutation (STAT3mut) in tissue and/or plasma had worse OS (Figure 1F&G), as well as PFS (Figure S2A&B), while no significant associations were found with other variations (Figure S2C&D).

Univariate Cox analysis showed that a high pre-ctDNA burden, as well as TP53mut, and STAT3mut, were significantly associated with worse PFS and OS (Figure S2C&D). Additionally, age>60, ECOG PS≥2, and high IPI risk were also associated with poorer OS (Figure S2D). Multivariate analysis identified high pre-treatment ctDNA burden and mutations in TP53 and STAT3 as independent factors associated with patient survival (Figure 1H&I). Subsequent grouping of patients based on the presence of these three independent risk factors yielded three cohorts: Group 1 (N=24) included patients with none of the risk factors, Group 2 (N=24) included those with one risk factor, and Group 3 (N=7) included those with more than one risk factor (Figure 1J). Survival analysis revealed significant differences in PFS (P<0.001, Figure 1K) and OS (P<0.001, Figure 1L) among the three groups. Notably, our three-group stratification demonstrated superior prognostic ability for PFS (P<0.001) compared to the IPI risk group (P=0.063) (Figure S2E&F). Together, these findings suggest the clinical significance of pretreatment ctDNA burden, which provide prognostic insight beyond established clinical risk groups.

Temporal analysis of ctDNA uncovers clinically meaningful molecular responses

To explore molecular features associated with treatment response at EOT, we first evaluated and compared baseline mutation frequencies between CR/PR and PD/dead groups. Patients in the PD/dead group showed significantly higher frequencies of TP53 variations in both tissue (P=0.048, Figure 2A) and plasma (P=0.004, Figure 2B) compared to those in the CR/PR group. Conversely, a significantly higher frequency of ALK alterations was observed in the plasma of CR/PR patients compared to the PD/dead group (P=0.036, Figure 2B). Pre-ctDNA burden was significantly higher in EOT non-CR group compared to EOT CR group (P=0.002, Figure S3A) and also significantly associated with EOT MRD status (P<0.001, Figure S3B). At EOT response evaluation, ctDNA was detected in 53.57% (15 of 28, MRDend+) of the EOT CR patients and 90% (18/20, MRDend+) of the EOT non-CR patients (Figure S3C). Furthermore, the dynamic fold change in ctDNA burden was significantly associated with EOT MRD status (P=0.004, Figure S3D). Plasma ctDNA burden at EOT was significantly associated with EOT response (Figure S3E&F); however, it is noteworthy that a marked proportion of CR/PR patient still had detectable ctDNA burdens at EOT (Figure S3F). Changes in the ctDNA burden before and after first-line treatment, stratified by different EOT responses, are illustrated in Figure 2C. No significant differences in ctDNA burden changes were observed between the CR and non-CR groups (Figure S3G), nor between the CR/PR and PD/dead groups (Figure S3H). Additionally, changes in ctDNA burden were not different among histological subtypes (Figure S3I) or between patients with low and high pre-ctDNA burdens (Figure S3J). Dynamic changes in plasma ctDNA burden during therapy and follow-up are shown in Figure 2D. At the end of therapy response evaluation, ctDNA was detected in 74.1% of patients (43 of 58, MRDend+). Although 46.9% patients achieved CR and 62.5% patients achieved overall response (OR), only 25.9% patients achieved ctDNA clearance at EOT (i.e., MRDend-). Additionally, among patients who achieved CR/PR at EOT, the proportion of those who progressed to PD at the first follow-up was higher in MRDend+ patients compared to the MRDend- patients (9/23 vs. 2/14, Figure 2E). As expected, MRDend+ patients had poorer PFS and OS compared to MRDend- patients (Figure 2F&G, respectively). Among the 30 patients who were EOT-PET negative, 9 (30.0%) experienced disease progression during follow-up, and 1 (3.3%) patient died (Figure S3K). Genetic factors and transcriptional signatures associated with MRD status

Given that only 25.9% of patients achieved MRD clearance at EOT and EOT MRD clearance was important for PTCL patients, we next investigated clinical and genetic factors that might predict EOT MRD status. Our analysis first revealed a marked difference in EOT MRD status among histological subtypes (Figure 3A). Specifically, ALK+ ALCL patients had a high incidence of MRDend- (80%), while the incidence of MRDend- was much lower in AITL (5%) and PTCL, NOS (20%) patients (Figure 3A). Mutational profiling of EOT ctDNA samples identified as MRDend+ (N=33, Figure 3B) showed that the top four frequently mutant genes that persisted after induction therapy were TET2 (66.7%), DNMT3A (48.5%), RHOA (27.3%), and TP53 (15.2%), which were the same top four altered genes found in pre-treatment plasma ctDNA (Figure S1A). Clinical factor analysis showed that only age>60 years was significantly associated with a higher proportion of MRDend+ (P=0.034, Figure 3C). Among the 33 MRDend+ patients, 72.7% (24/33) had at least one of the TET2mut, DNTM3Amut, or RHOAmut in their EOT ctDNA samples (Figure 3D). Furthermore, a significant correlation was observed between pre-treatment and EOT ctDNA burdens (R=0.58, P<0.001, Figure 3E). As regard to gene mutations, we found that only TET2mut and DNMT3Amut were positively associated with EOT MRD positivity while ALK rearrangement (ALKsv ) was negatively associated with EOT MRD positivity (Figure 3F). After taking all univariably significant factors into multivariable cox analysis, two risk factors including TET2mut and high pre-ctDNA burden were identified as independent predictors for MRDend+ (Figure 3G and Figure S4A&B). Model-1 was developed using the actual odds ratio value of the two individual risk factors. To facilitate its clinical application, we converted the two factors into binary variables to build Model-2 (Figure 3G). ROC curves showed no difference between Model-1 and Model-2, and both showed significantly better performance compared to IPI in EOT MRD prediction for PTCL patients (Figure 3H).

Pre-treatment tumor samples were subjected to bulk RNA sequencing to explore transcriptional signatures and immune microenvironment. Distinct gene expression patterns were observed between the MRDend+ and MRDend- groups among the five histological subtypes. KEGG pathway analysis revealed distinct signaling pathways enriched in each histologic subtype (Figure S5A). Next, we investigated the correlation between MRD status and immune cell infiltration across distinct histologic subtypes. Our analysis indicated that MRDend+ patients had a higher level of immune microenvironment disorders and MRDend- patients showed minimal immune cell infiltration, which may explain their better prognostic outcomes (Figure S5B and Table S6). Additionally, we examined the expression of immune checkpoint genes. Among ALK- ALCL patients, we found higher expression levels of CD47 in the MRDend+ group (P=0.025, Figure S5C), which correlated with poorer prognosis. Furthermore, the immunophenoscore (IPS) was evaluated in these patients, revealing that MRDend+ patients typically had higher MHC and effector cell (EC) scores (Figure S5D&E).

To further elucidate the immune characteristics associated with MRD status in each histologic subtype, we calculated the enrichment scores of immune cells using ssGSEA. PD-L1 and PD-1 checkpoint pathway is enriched in MRDend+ patients, along with increased proportions of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells (Figures S5F-H). For patients with ALK- ALCL and PTCL, NOS, the MRDend+ group had higher levels of cell proliferation, signaling by VEGF, Tfh, Th1, Th2 and Treg cells compared to the MRDend- group, suggesting a disordered immune microenvironment in lymphoma (Figures S5I-P).

Clonal evolution reveals possible explanation for disease progression and recurrence for PTCL

Since limited data was shown about to the patterns of clone evolution of PTCL patients treated with CHOP-like regimens, we evaluated the data of 33 MRDend+ patients who had sufficient material at multiple time points (i.e., diagnosis, EOT, and follow-up). Analyzing the variant allele frequences (VAFs) of mutations at these stages allowed us to track clonal evolution during treatment and distinguish two main evolutionary patterns at relapse: clonal persistence and branched evaluation. Clonal persistence was characterized by the continuous presence and dominance of specific mutational clusters and was the predominant pattern[25]. Branched evaluation characterized by both gain and loss of mutational clusters[26]. In patients diagnosed with AITL, nearly all patients showed persistence of the TET2mut or DNMT3Amut clones at relapse, suggesting that TET2mut or DNMT3Amut clones were either insufficiently inhibited by existing CHOP-like treatment regimen or that resistance mechanisms by passing TET2mut or DNMT3Amut inhibition contributed to treatment failure. However, in a minority of cases, loss of TET2mut or DNMT3Amut clones indicated alternative resistance mechanisms (Figures 4A-C, Figures S6 and Figure S7A). Similar results were observed across other histological subtypes (Figures S7B-I). Together, our analysis revealed that most mutations, particularly TET2 and DNMT3A mutations, which reappeared at relapse, were already as master clone mutations at diagnosis. Discussion

Our findings highlight the pivotal role of ctDNA in mutation profiling, risk stratification, prognosis assessment, treatment monitoring and MRD determination for PTCL patients, which were consistent with previous reports on the clinical utility of ctDNA in PTCL[27–31]. More importantly, our studies provided new insights into why a high incidence of relapse and poor prognosis occurred in PTCL patients, despite nearly 50% achieving CR and 60%–70% achieving OR after first-line CHOP-like regimen.

In addition to high IPI and pretreatment ctDNA burdens, we found that TP53 mutations, present in 20.0% of PTCL patients, and STAT3 mutations, present in 12.7% of PTCL patients, were also associated with worse survival outcomes. TP53 and STAT3 mutations have been reported as the biomarker for survival prognosis in previous study for PTCL patients[32–35]. A risk model combing pretreatment ctDNA burden, STAT3 mutation, and TP53 mutation status was developed for risk stratification, providing improved prognostic value for disease progression. However, this model requires validation in a larger cohort of PTCL patients in the era of ctDNA.

The second noteworthy finding is the extremely low incidence of EOT MRD negativity in PTCL patients (approximately 26%) after first-line CHOP-like induction therapy. Although nearly 50% of PTCL patients achieved CR at EOT, consistent with most previous studies[36], EOT ctDNA burdens were cleared in only 25.9% (15 of 58) of the PTCL patients, a rate significantly lower than that observed in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma[5]. Among the EOT CR patients, only 44.8% were EOT MRD negative. Furthermore, the ratio of the first PD were markedly higher in MRDend+ patients compared to MRDend- patients among the patients who achieved EOT CR/PR (39.1% vs 14.2%). Unsurprisingly, EOT MRD positivity was associated with poor outcomes in both PFS and OS. These results may explain the reason why high incidence of recurrence in PTCL patient following first-line CHOP regimens.

Given the extremely low incidence of EOT MRD negativity, the first question we sought to address was which gene mutations persist after first-line CHOP-like treatment for patients who achieved CR/PR. Intriguingly, the top four frequently altered genes detected in EOT ctDNA (i.e., TET2, DNMT3A, RHOA, and TP53) were consistent with those observed in pretreatment plasma, suggesting that these gene variations were resistant to clearance by first-line treatment. A similar mutation profile was found in the plasma of 13 patients who experienced disease progression during the first-line CHOP-like regimen. However, multivariable risk factor analysis showed that only high pre-ctDNA and TET2 mutation (the symbol of AITL and a part of PTCL-NOS) were the independent risk factors to predict EOT MRD status which was consistent with the EOT MRD status analysis according to histologic subtype.

We associated transcriptional signature and tumor immune environment analysis with EOT MRD status, which could be further validated in translational research. Our enrichment analysis revealed that MRDend+ PTCL patients predominantly featured pathways related to diverse cellular processes, environmental information processing, and organismal systems. Strikingly, our findings highlighted a strong association between the MRDend+ status and critical biological processes such as DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and proliferation. Through a comprehensive analysis of the tumor immune environment, we uncovered that the MRDend+ subgroup displayed heightened activity in pathways promoting tumor cell proliferation and immune evasion. This observation underscores the intricate interplay between tumor cell dynamics and immune modulation in MRDend+ PTCL, suggesting potential avenues for therapeutic intervention for disrupting these pathways.

Another interesting finding is that almost no clone evolution was found in all histologic subtypes of PTCL patients during the first-line CHOP-like regimen and follow-up, which was different from what is typically seen in B cell lymphomas (e.g., diffuse large B-cell lymphoma)[37, 38]. The mutation profile of PD ctDNA samples was nearly identical to the pretreatment ctDNA mutation profile. This finding provides important molecular information for selecting both first-line and salvage treatments for those patients. To improve long-term survival, particularly for AITL and PTCL-NOS patients, it is essential to incorporate new drugs that target and clear the TET2, DNMT3A, RHOA, and TP53 mutations in plasma. EOT MRD status could serve as an important marker for PTCL patients and may be a valuable endpoint for future clinical trial designs.

This study's limitations include a small, single-center sample size, requiring further research to verify findings. Due to PTCL's complexity and varied treatment responses, more extensive studies are needed to link ctDNA dynamics to specific genetic changes, especially those related to clonal resistance. Additionally, while our 475-gene panel revealed no major clonal shifts, we cannot exclude evolution driven by non-panel genes, subclonal CNVs, or epigenetic mechanisms, as discussed above. Critically, our median follow-up of 20 months may not capture late relapses (>24 months), which are well-documented in PTCL subtypes; thus, long-term survival outcomes warrant validation in extended cohorts. Despite efforts to differentiate tumor mutations from CHIP variants, some low-VAF mutations might still be CHIP-derived. The loss of mutations during dynamic monitoring might arguably reflect stochastic dropout at low VAF around or below 0.3% due to the limitation imposed by LoD, especially among the patients with reappearing mutations. Future research should use additional methods, such as whole-genome sequencing, epigenetic assays, single-cell analysis of sorted myeloid cells, and extended longitudinal monitoring, to better determine mutation origins, clonal dynamics beyond two years, and strengthen results.

In summary, our findings support the clinical utility of ctDNA for dynamic risk assessment in PTCL patients and clear the direction of exploration and selection the new drugs particularly targeted to TET2/DNMT3A/RHOA mutation integrating with conventional first-line and salvage treatments for PTCL patients.
