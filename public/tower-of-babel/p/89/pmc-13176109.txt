Metabolic and biochemical profiling reveals phenotypic heterogeneity in Zucker diabetic fatty rats
Authors: Marek Lepáček, Pavol Boďo, Barbora Kaločayová, Marta Šoltésová Prnová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZucker diabetic fatty (ZDF) rats exhibit significant phenotypic variability despite genetic uniformity, yet a comprehensive characterization of these divergent phenotypes remains limited.MethodsMale ZDF (fa/fa) rats (4 months, n = 22) and ZDF (fa/+) lean controls (n = 10) were maintained on standard chow for 6 months. Based on metabolic trajectory, ZDF (fa/fa) rats were stratified into three phenotypes: obese normoglycemic (O, n = 8), diabetic with cachexia (D, n = 8), and diabetic without cachexia (D‐C, n = 6). Comprehensive assessments included body weight, glycemic control, hepatic function, oxidative stress markers, and inflammatory cytokines.ResultsPrincipal coordinate analysis confirmed significant metabolic separation between phenotypes (p < 0.01). Diabetic cachectic (D) rats exhibited severe hyperglycemia (>25 mmol/L), insulin depletion, elevated hepatic enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] increased 2.5‐fold, p < 0.01), elevated interleukin‐6, and delayed pain response. Diabetic rats without cachexia (D‐C) exhibited intermediate hyperglycemia (≈13 mmol/L, p < 0.001 vs. control) with preserved insulin levels, and elevated fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and soluble receptor for advanced glycation end products (sRAGE). Obese normoglycemic (O) rats maintained normoglycemia with hyperinsulinemia and elevated triglycerides.ConclusionUnder identical genetic and environmental conditions, ZDF (fa/fa) rats develop distinct metabolic phenotypes encompassing euglycemic obesity, pre‐cachectic diabetes, and advanced cachectic diabetes. Phenotypic stratification is essential for accurate interpretation of ZDF experimental data and underscores the importance of characterizing individual metabolic trajectories when diabetic complications and therapeutic interventions are assessed in this model.

INTRODUCTION

The Zucker diabetic fatty (ZDF) rat is a well‐characterized animal model of type 2 diabetes. ZDF rats develop hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia by week 8 and overt diabetes by week 12, mimicking obesity‐related insulin resistance and inflammation observed in humans. 1 , 2 They exhibit hypertension, obesity, and increased superoxide anion production in the aorta and adipose tissue. 3

An inbred line of ZDF rats was established in 1985, with genetic standardization completed in 1991. 4 The ZDF rat model develops diabetes due to an autosomal recessive fa gene on chromosome 5, which causes leptin receptor deficiency. This leads to hypothalamic defects in leptin and insulin signaling, resulting in hyperphagia, early‐onset obesity, glucose intolerance, hyperinsulinemia, hyperlipidemia, and moderate hypertension. 5

ZDF rats are extensively used to investigate type 2 diabetes complications, including diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular complications. 6 Research demonstrates that ZDF rats exhibit impaired outward remodeling of resistance arteries, reduced endothelium‐mediated dilation, and endothelial dysfunction in coronary arteries. 7 , 8 The leptin receptor deficiency in these rats produces severe phenotypes, including disrupted islet architecture, β‐cell degranulation, and increased β‐cell death. 9 , 10 , 11

Insulin‐resistant ZDF rats exhibit increased PP2A (Akt‐inactivating protein phosphatase 2A) activity in liver and muscle tissue, which inhibits Akt activation and downstream targets. Insulin resistance develops progressively in the absence of mitochondrial dysfunction, with heightened PP2A activity involving increased regulatory subunits. 12 , 13 , 14 Male ZDF rats exhibit glucose intolerance, significant insulin resistance, and hyperlipidemia, becoming overtly diabetic after 8 weeks on a 6.5% fat diet. 15 , 16

Despite genetic uniformity, ZDF rats develop distinct metabolic phenotypes that emerge over time. Understanding these phenotypic differences is crucial for accurately interpreting experimental data and for developing precision medicine approaches to type 2 diabetes. This study aims to comprehensively characterize metabolic, biochemical, and physiological differences among ZDF (fa/fa) rat phenotypes and identify biomarkers determining disease trajectory.

METHODS

Animals and experimental design

Four‐month‐old male ZDF (fa/fa) obese rats (n = 22) and their lean littermate controls (fa/+) (n = 10) were obtained from CEM, SAS (Dobra Voda) and maintained on a standard chow diet ad libitum for 6 months of continuous longitudinal monitoring through 10 months of age. Longitudinal metabolic monitoring was performed at baseline (month 4) and at monthly intervals (months 5–10), yielding six time points of observation across the 6‐month study period. During the course of longitudinal metabolic monitoring, ZDF (fa/fa) rats exhibited divergent trajectories in glycemic control, body weight dynamics, and biochemical markers. At the conclusion of the study (6 months), animals were stratified into three distinct phenotypic groups based on their individual metabolic profiles: obese normoglycemic (O, n = 8), diabetic with cachexia (D, n = 8), and diabetic without cachexia (D‐C, n = 6). This stratification was based on an integrated assessment of glucose levels, HbA1c, body weight trajectory, and food/water consumption patterns maintained throughout the monitoring period. All animals received a standard chow diet ad libitum (19.2% protein, 65.1% carbohydrates, 4.0% fat, 4.0% fiber, 7.7% ash) and were housed under a 12 h light–dark cycle with free access to food and water. Weekly measurements of body weight, food consumption, and water intake were recorded. At the end of the experiment, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture in heparinized tubes, and organ samples were obtained for analysis. All tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Experimental Pharmacology and Toxicology, CEM, SAS, and conducted in accordance with the EU Directive 2010/63 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, as transposed into Slovak law (Act no. 437/2010 Coll. on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes), and NIH guidelines (NIH Publication no. 85‐23, revised 1985).

Biochemical plasma analysis

Frozen plasma samples were analyzed for glucose using enzymatic colorimetric assay (Glucose GOD 1500), insulin using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA, Mercodia), and HbA1c using the Crystal Chem Inc. rat HbA1c kit. Routine biochemical parameters (ALT, AST, ALP, GLDH, G‐GT, bilirubin, urea, creatinine, CK, fructosamine, triglycerides, electrolytes, bile acids, total protein) were assayed at an external laboratory (LABOKLIN, Bratislava). Cytokine and growth factor levels were determined using ELISA: MMP‐9, IL‐10, VEGF, β‐NGF, BDNF, and NGAL (R&D Systems), FGF21, IL‐6, and sRAGE (Proteintech). Real‐time glucose monitoring during the experiment was performed using the FreeStyleOptium Glucose Monitoring System immediately after tail blood collection.

Sorbitol assay

Red blood cells (RBC) were washed thrice with phosphate‐buffered saline (pH 7.4) and treated with ice‐cold 9% HClO4 to precipitate proteins, neutralized with K2CO3, and centrifuged. Similarly, frozen nerve tissues were powdered under liquid nitrogen and processed identically. Sorbitol levels were determined using modified enzymatic analysis with sorbitol dehydrogenase and diaphorase‐mediated fluorescent detection (excitation 544 nm/emission 590 nm), quantified against sorbitol standards. 17

Tissue processing and protein analysis

Spinal cords and cerebellum were homogenized using RIPA lysis buffer (200 μL/20‐mg tissue), incubated on ice, and centrifuged at 14 000 g for 5 min at 4°C. Protein concentration was determined using the Geiger–Bessman method with bovine serum albumin standards (2500–15.625 μg/mL).

Glutathione determination

Glutathione levels in RBCs, medulla oblongata, and sciatic nerves were quantified by fluorescence (excitation 350 nm/emission 420 nm) using calibration curves (0.21–32.55 nmol/mL) in 96‐well plates with o‐phthalaldehyde and Potassium Phosphate buffer.

Behavioral assessment

Rats were tested at the 2‐ and 4‐month time points following standardized neuropathy phenotyping criteria. 18 For hot water immersion test, water temperature was maintained at 50 ± 0.5°C; one third of tail was immersed, and tail‐flick reflex time was recorded. 19 For hot‐plate test, temperature was set to 55 ± 0.5°C; time until hind paw licking or brisk stamping was recorded. 18

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between control and experimental groups were made using one‐way or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test as appropriate, with p ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed to assess metabolic variation among groups using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the statistical significance of group separation.

RESULTS

Metabolic parameters and body composition

Control lean animals (C) increased weight from 280 to 480 g over 6 months. Diabetic cachexic animals (D) exhibited weight stagnation after 2 months and decline thereafter, creating a 243‐g difference from obese animals (O) by the end of the study (34% difference, p = 0.0221). Noncachexic diabetic animals (D‐C) maintained a steady weight gain, with a final 182‐g difference from D animals (p = 0.0252). Food consumption decreased significantly in D versus O and D‐C groups (final differences: D vs. O ~ 21 g/rat/day, D vs. D‐C ~ 19 g/rat/day, p < 0.0001). Water intake in D animals was significantly elevated (~145 mL/rat/day vs. ~32 mL/rat/day in D‐C and ~26 mL/rat/day in O, p < 0.0001) (Figure 1).

Glycemic control

Initial glycaemia showed no differences, but significant divergence emerged after 1 month. After 6 months, D animals exhibited glucose levels 21 mmol/L higher than O (p < 0.0001) and 13 mmol/L higher than D‐C (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2A–B). HbA1c followed similar patterns (Figure 2C), with all D subgroups differing significantly from controls and each other (p < 0.0001). Insulin levels (Figure 2D) did not differ between C and D groups, although highest hyperinsulinemia occurred in D‐C and O animals.

BDNF (Figure 6A) in cerebellum was 36% higher in D versus control animals (p < 0.0001), with lowest levels in O group (p = 0.0016). β‐NGF (Figure 6B) was significantly reduced in D animals (p = 0.0002) and further reduced in D‐C versus D animals (p = 0.0346). DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that despite a uniform leptin receptor mutation, ZDF (fa/fa) rats exhibit significant phenotypic heterogeneity reflecting the complex pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). 20 , 21 , 22 This heterogeneity has been linked to differential progression of β‐cell failure, insulin resistance, and metabolic stress, with possible contributions from epigenetic modifications and mitochondrial dysfunction. 23 , 24 Our findings parallel human T2DM, where clinical subtyping has identified distinct phenotypes—severe insulin‐deficient diabetes (SIDD), severe insulin‐resistant diabetes (SIRD), and obesity‐related diabetes—each with differential complication risks and progression patterns. 25 , 26

PCoA (Figure 8) clearly separated the three experimental groups, indicating global metabolic divergence (C vs. D, D‐C, O: p < 0.0001; D vs. D‐C: p < 0.01; O vs. D‐C: p < 0.05), consistent with distinct glucose‐metabolism and body‐composition patterns reported for ZDF cohorts. 22 , 27 This stratification underscores the value of phenotypic characterization even in genetically homogeneous strains. The observed variability likely reflects modifying factors, including metabolic adaptation capacity, hormonal sensitivity along the leptin/LepR axis, 28 , 29 and redox balance. 30

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance was performed to evaluate differences between experimental groups of rats. The analysis included standardized values of glucose, insulin, HbA1c, and body weight. The experimental groups comprised control lean (C, n = 10), Diabetic with cachexia (D, n = 8), Diabetic without cachexia (D‐C, n = 6), and Obese (O, n = 8). C vs. D, p < 0.0001; C vs. O, p < 0.0001; C vs. D‐C, p < 0.0001; D vs. O, p < 0.0001; D vs. D‐C, p < 0.01; O vs. D‐C, p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using PERMANOVA.

A limitation of this study is the use of male animals only. Female ZDF rats exhibit distinct metabolic trajectories and diabetes progression, and their inclusion would introduce sex as a major confounding variable. Future studies including both sexes with appropriate stratified analyses are needed to address sex‐specific mechanisms and improve translational relevance to type 2 diabetes.

Our findings demonstrate striking parallels with the classification of human type 2 diabetes subtypes proposed by Ahlqvist et al., 31 which identified five clinically distinct T2DM clusters based on autoantibody status, β‐cell function (HOMA2‐B), and insulin resistance (HOMA2‐IR). Our D phenotype exhibited characteristics consistent with SIDD: low insulin levels (Figure 2D), low body weight reflecting cachexia (Figure 1A), and severe hyperglycemia (Figure 2B), accompanied by significant progression of peripheral neuropathy—a finding consistent with SIDD's high risk of microvascular complications in humans. Our D‐C phenotype demonstrated a compensated SIRD phenotype: elevated hyperglycemia (Figure 2B) offset by high insulin levels (Figure 2D) indicating insulin resistance, preserved body weight (Figure 1A), and particularly protection from the acute neuropathic manifestations observed in SIDD, suggesting that β‐cell compensatory capacity may mitigate neuropathic complications despite insulin resistance. The O phenotype mirrored mild obesity‐related diabetes, characterized by robust β‐cell compensation maintaining normoglycemia (Figure 2B) despite high insulin levels (Figure 2D) and elevated body weight (Figure 2A), with minimal metabolic derangements. This naturalistic stratification within a genetically uniform ZDF population provides compelling evidence that phenotypic diversity in type 2 diabetes results not only from genetic heterogeneity but also from differential metabolic adaptation and β‐cell compensatory capacity, suggesting that ZDF rats serve as a valuable preclinical model for dissecting the mechanisms underlying distinct human T2DM subtype biology and may facilitate the development of precision medicine approaches targeting specific disease subtypes.

Metabolic trajectories and β‐cell compensation

We identified three distinct phenotypes: obese normoglycemic (O), diabetic with cachexia (D), and diabetic without cachexia (D‐C). The D group exhibited weight stagnation at ~6 months of life followed by a decline, reflecting the catabolic state associated with progressive β‐cell failure. 24 According to Finegood et al., 32 inadequate β‐cell mass expansion (only twofold compared to fourfold in nondiabetic Zucker rats) leads to decompensation. The D‐C group maintained steady weight gain paralleling obese animals, although diet likely contributed to phenotype divergence from previous studies: Purina 5008 reliably drives diabetes, 24 , 33 , 34 whereas standard chow often yields obese normoglycemic ZDF (fa/fa) subgroups. 35 Consistent with diabetic progression, polydipsia and polyphagia were most evident in D animals and attenuated in D‐C/O groups. 22 , 35 , 36

Plasma glucose levels confirmed the decompensated state in D rats (>25 mmol/L from month 5 onward), supported by elevated HbA1c and fructosamine. Insulin levels were significantly lower in D rats (indicating β‐cell exhausting) compared with D‐C but elevated in O and D‐C rats (compensatory hyperinsulinemia). 24 Shimabukuro et al. 37 demonstrated that β‐cell apoptosis rates increase more than sevenfold in diabetic ZDF islets compared to controls, mechanistically explaining phenotype divergence. These results are consistent with previous findings demonstrating early hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and progressive β‐cell failure. 34 , 36

This heterogeneity mirrors human T2DM: nonobese individuals exhibit predominantly secretory defects with impaired glucose sensitivity, whereas obese individuals exhibit significant insulin resistance with a relatively preserved β‐cell function. 38 , 39 , 40 Crucially, diabetes severity is determined not only by genetic predisposition but also by the balance between β‐cell compensation capacity and insulin resistance burden. 38 , 39 , 40 Our D‐C phenotype—although exhibiting intermediate disease markers—maintains compensatory mechanisms that preserve near‐normal glycemia, demonstrating that diabetes is not inevitable even with genetic susceptibility. 40

Hormonal dysregulation and polyol pathway activation

Corticosterone was significantly elevated in D and O rats, reflecting a catabolic state with enhanced gluconeogenesis and energy mobilization. This elevation mediates diabetes‐induced impairments of hippocampal synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis, 41 although promoting insulin resistance and hepatic glucose output. 42 In contrast, D‐C rats maintained corticosterone levels comparable to controls, suggesting better metabolic compensation. These findings parallel human studies demonstrating that flattening of the diurnal cortisol curve is associated with worse glycemic control (higher HbA1c) in individuals with diabetes 43 and that subclinical hypercortisolism promotes visceral adiposity, hepatic gluconeogenesis, and insulin resistance. 43 , 44

Sorbitol accumulation—indicative of polyol pathway flux—was significantly elevated in D rats' sciatic nerves (up to 10‐fold above controls), lens tissue, and erythrocytes, consistent with previous reports. 45 , 46 , 47 Particularly, lens sorbitol was paradoxically higher in D‐C than in D rats, suggesting advanced damage of D rats' lens and cell rupture leading to sorbitol excretion. Mechanistically, elevated corticosterone exacerbates hyperglycemia by stimulating hepatic glucose output, whereas sorbitol accumulation promotes osmotic stress and oxidative damage, driving diabetic neuropathy and ocular complications. 48 , 49 In humans, the polyol pathway contributes to 30% of glucose metabolism under hyperglycemic conditions, 50 with sorbitol accumulation documented in peripheral nerves, erythrocytes, and lenses of diabetic patients. 51 The osmotic stress from sorbitol (unable to cross cell membranes) depletes nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and impairs antioxidant defenses, promoting oxidative injury driving neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy. 51 , 52

Hepatic and renal dysfunction

Concurrent hepatic dysfunction in D rats was indicated by significantly elevated ALT, AST, ALP, G‐GT, GLDH, urea, and bile acids, reflecting metabolic overload and dyslipidemia, consistent with previous reports documenting hepatic impairment in diabetic ZDF rats. 53 In contrast, O rats exhibited significantly lower levels of these markers and preserved liver function despite obesity and progressive insulin resistance, suggesting a protective metabolic adaptation preventing hepatic injury. The D‐C group exhibited intermediate liver dysfunction, with biochemical markers substantially reduced relative to D animals, indicating emerging but not advanced hepatic impairment.

Renal function exhibited significant urea elevation in D rats (p < 0.05), indicating hyperglycemia‐linked impairment. NGAL levels were elevated in D rats, suggesting early tubular injury—NGAL being a sensitive early biomarker of kidney damage in both experimental models and human diabetic nephropathy, 54 , 55 , 56 Both D‐C and O groups maintained renal markers comparable to healthy controls, confirming that persistent hyperglycemia and metabolic derangement drive renal dysfunction. These findings align with clinical data demonstrating that NGAL elevations correlate with worsening glycemic control and can detect kidney injury before traditional markers become abnormal. 56

Oxidative stress and inflammatory signatures

D rats exhibited pronounced oxidative stress characterized by elevated pro‐inflammatory IL‐6, reduced anti‐inflammatory IL‐10, depleted sciatic nerve GSH and medulla oblongata GSH, increased G‐GT, and elevated FGF21—a biochemical signature of cachexia and advanced diabetes. The D‐C group exhibited a distinct profile: lacking elevated IL‐6 or diminished IL‐10 but increased MMP‐9, elevated sRAGE, and elevated FGF21—markers indicative of extracellular matrix remodeling and adaptive stress responses. 10

Particularly, GSH levels in erythrocytes were significantly higher in D‐C rats, whereas sciatic nerve GSH depletion persisted in obese O rats despite euglycemia and preserved sensory function. This dissociation suggests that obesity‐associated oxidative stress in peripheral nerves does not inevitably cause clinically evident neuropathy without sustained hyperglycemia and accompanying metabolic derangements. The D‐C group, exhibiting intermediate GSH depletion, maintained relatively preserved sensory function over 1 year, highlighting the protective role of compensatory metabolic mechanisms and preserved insulin secretion in mitigating neuropathic progression.

Recent findings highlight that significant brown adipose tissue (BAT)‐to‐ white adipose tissue (WAT) transition in diabetic ZDF rats reflects diminished uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1)‐mediated thermogenesis and dysregulated cardiac connexin 43 expression correlating with elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines, with FGF21 elevations coinciding with adipose alterations and reinforcing its role as a systemic metabolic stress marker. 57

Neuropathic phenotyping

Thermal testing of peripheral neuropathy confirmed that D rats exhibited significantly prolonged response latency in both tail‐flick and hot‐plate tests (p < 0.001), indicating decreased thermal sensitivity characteristic of diabetic peripheral neuropathy. 13 , 58 The O and D‐C groups exhibited significantly faster responses (p < 0.05), suggesting reduced neuropathic impairment. These findings indicate that neuropathy development is not driven only by oxidative stress markers but also by integrated metabolic dysfunction comprising hyperglycemia, insulin depletion, and prolonged inflammation—the defining features of the cachexic D phenotype.

CONCLUSION

Our study reveals that although ZDF (fa/fa) rats share an identical genotype, longitudinal monitoring on a standard chow diet (without Purina 5008) allowed us to identify three clearly separated phenotypic groups. The first group developed diabetes with cachexia within the first 10 months of life. The second phenotype manifested persistent hyperglycemia but did not progress to cachexia. The third group consisted of animals that were obese but did not exhibit either hyperglycemia or cachexia throughout the study duration.

This natural stratification occurred despite uniform genetic background and standardized experimental conditions, demonstrating that ZDF rats exhibit significant spontaneous metabolic heterogeneity. This mirrors what is observed in humans: even within genetically predisposed populations, diabetes evolution and complication burden show significant diversity, reflecting the interplay of genetic, metabolic, and environmental factors. Our results indicate that such heterogeneity is inherent in the ZDF model and should be carefully considered in experimental design, phenotype selection, and interpretation of results.

Although our 6‐month longitudinal monitoring period successfully identified distinct phenotypic trajectories and early‐stage complications (evidenced by elevated NGAL as a marker of early tubular injury and early neuropathy phenotyping), assessment of advanced complications such as overt nephropathy, severe retinopathy, and cardiac remodeling would require extended monitoring periods exceeding 12 months. Future studies with extended duration are needed to provide insights into the progression of these advanced complications across the three identified phenotypes.

Importantly, all phenotypic groups emerged under standard chow feeding, not the diabetogenic Purina 5008 diet, confirming that spontaneous metabolic divergence does not require an extreme nutritional trigger. The ZDF strain's natural variability makes it a robust model for studying the diversity of diabetes progression and complications.

Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms driving these divergent trajectories, including β‐cell resilience, insulin sensitivity, and compensatory capacity. Our findings strongly encourage stratified experimental approaches and highlight the necessity of precise phenotypic characterization for meaningful translation to human diabetes research.
