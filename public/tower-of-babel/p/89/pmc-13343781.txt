High output heart failure from an aortocaval fistula
Authors: Laura Wubker Diaz, Shanice Spence-Miller, Rhea Sancassani, Arash Bornak, Rosario Colombo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a 50-year-old male who sustained a right abdominal stab wound 20 years ago requiring laparotomy and extensive repair, now presenting with worsening dyspnea on exertion and palpitations. Physical examination was remarkable for jugular venous distention, bilateral lower extremity edema, and a right abdominal pulsatile mass with audible bruit. Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 30%–35% and a severely dilated inferior vena cava (IVC) with turbulent flow. Computed tomography venography of the abdomen revealed a fistula between the IVC and right renal artery, with aneurysm of the IVC measuring 8 cm. Cardiac catheterization confirmed a high cardiac output of 12 l/min. The patient underwent endovascular repair of the fistula with the use of an Amplatzer septal occluder, and subsequent improvement in symptoms and LVEF. This case highlights a unique etiology of high output heart failure, treated successfully by novel endovascular means.Keywords: heart failure, arteriovenous fistula, endovascular repair, case report

Introduction

Aortocaval fistulas are an exceedingly rare complication of traumatic abdominal injury and can lead to high output heart failure (HOHF) as blood is diverted from the arterial to venous circulation. Current research reveals that arteriovenous fistulas (AVF) account for about 20% of the total cases of HOHF, with even fewer being post-traumatic [1]. This case illustrates the presentation and management challenges.

Case report

A 50-year-old male with a history of right abdominal stab wound and resultant laparotomy over 20 years prior presented with one week of palpitations, shortness of breath, and lower extremity swelling. Vital signs were remarkable for hypertension (blood pressure 147/115 mmHg) and tachycardia (pulse 124 beats/minute). Physical examination revealed an irregular rhythm, bounding pulses, 2+ pitting edema of the lower extremities, jugular venous distention to the level of the earlobe, and rales in the bilateral lung fields. A large pulsatile mass in the right abdomen with a palpable thrill and audible bruit was noted. Laboratory studies were notable for NT-proBNP of 15 900 pg/ml, troponin-I with a peak value of 0.079 ng/ml, creatinine of 1.6 mg/dl and BUN of 28 mg/dl. A 12-lead electrocardiogram revealed rapid atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction. Computed tomography (CT) angiography of the chest showed pulmonary congestion without evidence of pulmonary embolism. Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 30%–35%, moderate global left ventricular hypokinesis, severely dilated atria, and a severely dilated inferior vena cava (IVC) with turbulent flow (Fig. 1).

Abdominal CT venography showed an 8 cm IVC aneurysm, a caval fistula at the level of the mid-right renal vessels, and a large and tortuous right renal vein with an atrophic right kidney (Fig. 1). Hemodynamic studies demonstrated a cardiac output (CO) of 12 l/min, cardiac index (CI) of 7.02 l/min/m2, elevated oxygen saturations in the right atrium (84.8%) and pulmonary artery (85.9%), and reduced systemic vascular resistance (SVR) (588 dynes*sec/cm5) (Table 1). A coronary angiogram showed no significant obstructive coronary artery disease.

Upon admission, the patient’s atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response was managed with esmolol and heparin drips, followed by transesophageal echocardiogram-guided synchronized cardioversion, restoring sinus rhythm. He was started on medical management with loop diuretics and beta blockers. His course was complicated by worsening renal function, suspected secondary to a nonfunctioning right kidney as a result of the fistula, limiting further guideline-directed medical therapy.

Given imaging and cardiac catheterization findings, he was evaluated by vascular surgery. Diagnostic angiography confirmed a fistula at the level of the right renal artery origin, communicating with the IVC, as well as a right iliac artery-to-vein fistula. Management options were discussed extensively between the cardiology team and at vascular surgery conference. Due to prior abdominal surgery, extensive venous collateralization, and the high risk of intraoperative bleeding and associated mortality of an open repair, an endovascular approach was favored. This approach was technically challenging due to the markedly dilated IVC and high flow, limiting the feasibility of covered stent placement or coil embolization. After detailed risk–benefit discussion, the patient underwent successful endovascular repair by deploying a 20 mm Amplatzer septal occluder (Abbott, Plymouth, MN) with one disc of the device in the aorta and the other disc into the right renal artery at its origin, excluding flow into the IVC and effectively closing the fistula. This was followed by deployment of a stent-graft (Gore Medical, Flagstaff, AZ) in the right iliac artery, covering the iliac arteriovenous fistula (Fig. 2).

The patient tolerated the procedure well, and postoperative TTE demonstrated an improved LVEF of 55%–60%. He was discharged with prompt follow-up with vascular surgery and cardiology. One year later, CT of the abdomen demonstrated stable post-procedural changes with appropriate device positioning (Fig. 3). Unfortunately, he was subsequently lost to follow-up due to social barriers.

Discussion

HOHF is an uncommon form of heart failure in which the heart is unable to meet systemic demands despite elevated CO. It is defined as a resting CO greater than 8 l/min or CI greater than 4 l/min/m2 [2, 3]. The central pathophysiologic feature is excessive systemic vasodilation with consequent reduction in arterial afterload, increasing venous flow and contributing to the high-output state. Additionally, increased metabolic demand and elevated resting oxygen consumption may further drive the need for augmented tissue perfusion, resulting in persistently increased CO [1]. Reduced SVR triggers neurohormonal activation, including the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), antidiuretic hormone release, and sympathetic nervous system activation, leading to water and sodium retention and intravascular volume expansion [3]. Over time, sustained high-flow arteriovenous shunting may lead to left ventricular remodeling, systolic dysfunction, and clinical heart failure [4]. In our patient with a long-standing aortocaval fistula, persistent low-SVR physiology led to progressive volume overload, subendocardial ischemia, and myocardial remodeling. Superimposed rapid atrial flutter likely further reduced LVEF despite persistently elevated CO from high flow through the fistula [5].

The management of HOHF is challenging as standard heart failure therapies such as vasodilators may be harmful, and no formal treatment recommendations currently exist [1]. Diuresis is often beneficial to combat the neurohormonal-mediated fluid retention. In our patient, difficulties were met in using RAAS inhibiting agents due to renal dysfunction from the fistula. Therefore, the only viable long-term treatment option was closure of the fistula.

Intervention for an AVF can be with either an open or endovascular approach. In our case, options were limited due to the large size, high flow, and friable vessels. Use of an atrial septal occluder device for AVF closure has been reported sparingly in literature and is even rarer for large fistulas resulting in HOHF [6]. Though off label, the usage of septal occluder devices could prove to be a viable alternative approach for complex cases involving large, high flow fistulas [7].

Conclusion

HOHF should be considered in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with new-onset decompensated heart failure. Aortocaval fistulas are a rare complication of abdominal injury, and can ultimately lead to HOHF, though few cases have been reported in the literature. Further research is needed to determine optimal medical management and expand intervention and repair strategies.
