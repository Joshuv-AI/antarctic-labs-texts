Investigating the causal associations between occupational noise exposure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: A Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Zhouxiao Lu, Xuzhou Yu, Mei Wang, XiaoYu Wu, Jun Song, Sheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Occupational noise exposure (ONE) occurs frequently, but the health risk of this exposure to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has not been evaluated. We aimed to explore the causal role of ONE in the risk of COPD. Instrumental variables of 3 levels of ONE were included: workplace very noisy: rarely/never (cases: 51,689; control: 39,088); workplace very noisy: sometimes (cases: 69,515; control: 21,382); workplace very noisy: often (cases: 17,469; control: 73,184). A univariate Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed to determine the genetic effect of ONE on the risk of COPD and lung function based on an “Inverse variance weighted (IVW)” approach. A multivariate MR was used to adjust the interfering factors. The heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy and Steiger directionality test were conducted to assess the robustness and reliability of MR. 12, 14, and 18 single nucleotide polymorphisms were identified as instrumental variables for 3 conditions: workplace very noisy: rarely/never, workplace very noisy: sometimes, and workplace very noisy: often, respectively. The IVW results showed frequent ONE and a higher liability of COPD in both the training set (odds ratio [OR] = 1.020, 95% confidence interval [CI] [1.008–1.032], P = .001) and the validation set (OR = 3.259, 95% CI [1.821–5.832], P = .012). Moreover, frequent ONE decreased the forced lung capacity (training set: OR = 0.879, 95% CI [0.782–0.987], P = .029; validation set: OR = 0.882, 95% CI [0.785–0.991], P = .035) and forced expiratory volume in 1-second (training set: OR = 0.828, 95% CI [0.728–0.943], P = .004; validation set: OR = 0.870, 95% CI [0.791–0.957], P = .004). The multivariate MR found the estimated role of frequent ONE on COPD was comparable to the univariate IVW estimate (OR = 1.021, 95% CI [1.008–1.032], P = .021). The study illustrated the causal association between ONE and the risk of COPD.

1. Introduction

This time has arrived ahead of schedule, despite the World Health Organization’s prediction that chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the most common chronic lung disease, will rank third globally in terms of causes of death by 2030.[1,2] According to reports, more than 200 million instances of COPD were diagnosed globally in 2019, and more than 3.3 million people lost their lives to the disease. Chronic airway inflammation and obstruction are hallmarks of COPD, which causes a progressive and permanent deterioration in lung function.[3] A few risk factors have been connected to the development of COPD, including air pollution, occupational dust and chemicals, and smoking.[3] Further investigation into the unique pathophysiology and pathogenic variables is still necessary, as this could enhance COPD treatment options and improve the disease’s prognosis.

In the industrial era, occupational noise exposure (ONE) frequently occurred, but the health risks associated with this exposure have not yet been assessed.[4] As is well known, exposure to occupational noise can cause various health hazards, including but not limited to the possibility of hearing loss.[5] In addition, a systematic study found a significant correlation between exposure to noise at work and elevated blood pressure. However, there is not enough data to establish a link with other cardiovascular diseases.[6] A long-term cohort study involving 5753 men found that exposure to high noise levels at work is associated with a higher risk of coronary heart disease but not with stroke.[7,8]

A cross-sectional study demonstrated the influence of road traffic noise on the prevalence of COPD.[9] A retrospective study based on the United States general population revealed that self-reported ONE improves the risk of COPD.[10] Nevertheless, recall bias may exist in the self-reported study, and stratified analysis was not executed to illustrate the role of different noise levels on COPD. Many studies on this topic are cross-sectional and do not fully consider the residual confounding of other factors related to noise in the work environment. Therefore, it is necessary to exclude relevant intervention factors to study the impact of occupational noise on health.

To infer causal correlations between exposure and outcome in epidemiology, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, a potent statistical technique for deducing causal correlation between exposure and outcome in epidemiology, is based on whole-genome association analysis and utilizes single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) as instrumental variation to represent exposure and explore the causal link of exposure to outcome.[11] The method is less influenced by reverse causality and confounding variables due to this being an organic randomized controlled experiment.

In the study, we performed a univariate MR analysis to determine the causal role of ONE on the risk of COPD. Then, we evaluated the genetic effect of ONE on the decline of lung function. Finally, we applied a multivariate MR to adjust the interfering factors, such as age, gender, smoking, air pollution, and occupational dust and chemicals, to confirm whether ONE was an independent pathogenic factor of COPD.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Overall study design

A univariate MR analysis was conducted to investigate the association of ONE with the genetically predicted risk of COPD as well as the decrease in lung function. Three basic assumptions to follow in an MR study: genetic variation has a solid association with exposure; genetic variation should be independent of potential confounders; these genetic tools should only affect outcomes via exposure. Furthermore, a multivariable MR was carried out to simultaneously estimate the direct effect of ONE on COPD conditioned on other influence factors, considering that COPD may be related to other influence factors. 2.2. Data sources for exposure and outcome

All data used in the study were obtained from a genome-wide association study (GWAS; https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), and all participants were from Europe. The GWAS summary data for ONE included 3 levels: workplace very noisy: rarely/never (GWAS ID: ukb-d-22606_0); workplace very noisy: sometimes (GWAS ID: ukb-d-22606_1); workplace very noisy: often (GWAS ID: ukb-d-22606_2). Two independent datasets (GWAS ID: ukb-b-16751; GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90018807) were used to determine and validate the role of ONE on the risk of COPD. The GWAS summary data for forced lung capacity (FVC) in both the training and validation sets were gathered from ukb-b-14713 and ieu-b-105, respectively; for forced expiratory volume in 1-second (FEV1), they were from ukb-b-19657 and ukb-b-11141, respectively. The detailed information on all datasets used in the study was presented in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1.

2.3. Genetic instrumental variable (IV) selection

In the univariable MR, we screened IVs according to the following conditions: because only one SNP was identified with strict screening criteria (P < 5e × 10−8), we applied a loose filter condition of SNPs (P < 5e × 10−6) to select genetic IVs; IVs were clumped for independence using linkage disequilibrium R2 < 0.001 within ± 10 000 kb distance in the 1000 Genomes Project as a reference panel; the F-statistic was calculated with β divided by the square of the standard error, and IVs with F-statistics > 10 would be filtered; to eliminate other factors influencing the development of COPD, we used PhenoScanner V2 (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) to remove SNPs that may have violated the second and third critical hypotheses and have potential confounders and bypassing (e.g., age, gender, smoking, occupational particulate exposure, other disease).[12]

2.4. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.1 (https://www.r-project.org).

2.5. Univariate MR analysis

Packages: “TwoSampleMR,” and “Variant Annotation,” were applied to conduct MR analysis. Five methods were proposed: “Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW),” “Weighted Median,” “Simple Mode,” “MR-Egger,” and “Weighted Mode.” The primary means of estimating the causal impact of exposure on outcome was the fixed effects IVW method, as it produces more reliable estimates in a broader range of scenarios.[13] The random-effect model would take the place of the fixed-effect model if null heterogeneity were rejected. The assessment of heterogeneity used Cochran Q statistic. The evaluation of horizontal pleiotropy was using the MR-Egger intercept test and the outlier method (MR-pleiotropy residual sum and outlier [MR-PRESSO]).[14,15] P < .05 was considered as statistical difference. If there is horizontal pleiotropy, the MR-PRESSO method would eliminate outliers and compare the significance of differences before and after adjusting data. Omission analysis (Leave-one-out analysis) was used to explore whether a single SNP influenced the results.[16] The Steiger directionality test was used to determine whether the directionality of a single SNP was correct.

2.6. Multivariate MR analysis

Numerous factors affected the occurrence of COPD, and they were interrelated. Considering these relationships, we carried out a multivariable MR analysis to simultaneously estimate whether ONE was an independent pathogenic factor of COPD by adjusting interfering factors such as age, gender, smoking, air pollution, and occupational dust and chemicals. The multivariable MR analysis was implemented using the R package: “MendelianRandomization.” The IVs clumping for linkage disequilibrium were set as R2 < 0.001 within ± 10 000 kb distance. The multivariable MR results were presented with the IVW method.

3. Results

After harmonizing, 12, 14, and 18 SNPs were identified as IVs for 3 conditions: workplace very noisy: rarely/never, workplace very noisy: sometimes, and workplace very noisy: often, respectively (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2). The F-statistics ranged from 20.8 to 29.5, indicating a low risk of bias caused by weak IVs (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2). The Steiger directionality test showed the directionality of all was correct (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2).

3.1. Causal estimates between ONE and COPD

We first performed a univariate MR analysis to investigate the causal role of ONE on the risk of COPD in the training set (ukb-b-16751). Notably, the fixed-effect IVW method illustrated a strong causal association between frequent ONE and a higher liability of COPD (odds ratio [OR] = 1.020, 95% confidence interval [CI] [1.008–1.032], P = .001, Fig. 2 and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 3). A similar result was observed in the Weighted Median model (OR = 1.022, 95% CI [1.005–1.039], P = .012). In addition, few or occasional ONE did not increase the risk of COPD. No heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy were observed (Table S3 and S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4). Then, we validated the results with the validation set (ebi-a-GCST90018807). In line with previous findings, the IVW and Weighted Median model showed frequent ONE were associated with genetically predicted COPD (IVW: OR = 3.259, 95% CI [1.821–5.832], P = .012; Weighted Median: OR = 3.405, 95% CI [1.429–8.117], P = .006, Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content 5 and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 3). The heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy results were similar to those in the training set (Table S3 and S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4). No particular SNP could drive a causal estimate in leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 6)

3.2. Causal estimates between ONE and FEV1

Next, we explored the causal effect of ONE on lung function in the training set (ukb-b-19657). The random-effect IVW method evidenced that frequent ONE would decrease FEV1 (OR = 0.828, 95% CI [0.728–0.943], P = .004, Fig. 3 and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 3). However, Cochran Q statistic demonstrated apparent heterogeneity (IVW: P = .011; MR-Egger: P = .015, Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 7). Although no horizontal pleiotropy was observed in the MR-Egger intercept test (P = .269, Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 4), the MR-PRESSO method showed significant horizontal pleiotropy (P = .019, Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 4). After correcting the horizontal pleiotropy by removing SNP rs12189679, no heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy were detected (Table S3 and S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4). After adjusting for horizontal pleiotropy, the causal association between frequent ONE and FEV1 was also robust (OR = 0.870, 95% CI [0.791–0.957], P = .004). We verified the relation between ONE and FEV1 using new outcome data (ukb-b-11141). A similar negative trend between frequent ONE and FEV1 was observed in the random-effect IVW model (OR = 0.810, 95% CI [0.692–0.948], P = .009, Fig. S3, Supplemental Digital Content 8 and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 3), with obvious heterogeneity (IVW: P = .001; MR-Egger: P = .001, Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 7) and horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept test: P = .034; MR-PRESSO P = .001). Then, SNP rs12189679 was discarded to adjust for horizontal pleiotropy, and analogous results were discovered in the fixed-effect IVW method (OR = 0.881, 95% CI [0.782–0.993], P = .037). No single SNP seriously violated the overall impact on COPD in the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 6).

The causal effect of ONE on FEV1 analyzed with ukb-b-19657 data. CI = confidence interval, FEV1 = forced expiratory volume in 1-second, MR = Mendelian randomization, ONE = occupational noise exposure, OR = odds ratio.

3.3. Causal estimates between ONE and FVC

We assessed the causal role of ONE on FVC at 3 levels in the training set (ukb-b-14713) and revealed that rare/never ONE increased the FVC (fixed-effect IVW method: OR = 1.140, 95% CI [1.006–1.293], P = .040, Fig. 4 and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 3), whereas frequent ONE decreased the FVC (random-effect IVW model: OR = 0.808, 95% CI [0.692–0.943], P = .007, Fig. 4). Nevertheless, Cochran Q test indicated the presence of heterogeneity (workplace very noisy sometimes: IVW: P = .003; MR-Egger: P = .037; workplace very noisy often: IVW: P < .001; MR-Egger: P < .001, Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 7), and the MR-PRESSO method and the MR-Egger intercept test detected the presence of horizontal pleiotropy (workplace very noisy sometimes: MR-Egger intercept test: P = .048, MR-PRESSO: P = .002; workplace very noisy often: MR-Egger intercept test: P = .298, MR-PRESSO: P = .002, Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 4) in estimating the association of occasional and frequent ONE with FVC. Thus, we eliminated outliers to correct the horizontal pleiotropy. No obvious heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy were observed after adjusting. The fixed-effect IVW method also suggested a strong causal association between frequent ONE and FVC (OR = 0.879, 95% CI [0.782–0.987], P = .029). The results in the validation set (ieu-b-105) were consistent with those in the training set (Fig. S4, Supplemental Digital Content 9 and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 3). Furthermore, no SNP significantly violated the overall impact of ONE on COPD.

The causal effect of ONE on FVC analyzed with ukb-b-14713 data. CI = confidence interval, FVC = forced lung capacity, MR = Mendelian randomization, ONE = occupational noise exposure, OR = odds ratio.

3.4. Multivariable MR analysis

The univariate MR analysis illustrated that frequent ONE increased the genetic risk of COPD. Considering that multiple factors affect the development of COPD and may influence the role of ONE on COPD, we performed additional multivariable analysis to investigate whether frequent ONE was an independent pathogenic factor of COPD. In model 1, we adjusted the effects of air pollution, smoking, dusty workplace, and chemical or other fumes at the workplace on COPD. We found the estimated role of frequent ONE on COPD was comparable to the univariable IVW estimate in the training set (OR = 1.021, 95% CI [1.008–1.032], P = .021, Table 1). Then, we adjusted more interfering factors in model 2, and the results were the same as those in model 1 (OR = 1.017, 95% CI [1.007–1.029], P = .041, Table 1). The MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO did not provide conclusive evidence for horizontal pleiotropy in models 1 and 2 (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 4). The Cochran Q test did not show the presence of heterogeneity. Finally, we verified this result in the validation set and obtained a similar conclusion (model 1: OR = 1.605, 95% CI [1.272–3.831], P = .026; model 2: OR = 1.115, 95% CI [1.047–2.792], P = .047, Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 10).

4. Discussion

COPD is one of the most common respiratory diseases in clinical practice and has become the third most common cause of life loss worldwide, just behind ischemic heart disease, lung cancer, and stroke.[17] Studies have revealed that environmental factors, such as air pollution, significantly influence COPD.3 A study also examined the lung function of 28 women who had worked in the textile industry for over 10 years and demonstrated “small airways aggression by noise,” although residual confounding cannot be ruled out.[18,19] Additionally, a study has reported a higher risk of COPD mortality caused by traffic noise in Madrid.18 However, few studies reported on the impact of ONE on COPD, except for a cross-sectional survey, which demonstrated the close relationship between self-reported ONE and COPD risk but was not related to objective noise levels.[10] However, a cross-sectional study cannot clarify the causal relationship between ONE and COPD. Therefore, the possibility that it is spurious cannot be entirely excluded. Moreover, the study did not include some risk factors for COPD, like smoke, fumes, and dust, which may bias the results.

According to its rule of “random allocation of parental alleles to offspring,” it is considered a natural randomized controlled trial. This study illustrated a robust causal association between frequent ONE and genetically predicted COPD. In contrast, no apparent relation between a few or occasional ONE and the risk of COPD was observed. Furthermore, the experimental data indicated that frequent ONE would decrease lung function (FVC and FEV1). Some risk factors are related to the development of COPD, including air pollution, occupational dust and chemicals, and smoking. The results of multivariable MR analysis were comparable to the univariable MR estimate, indicating ONE was an independent risk factor for COPD. Our study provided genetic evidence for frequent ONE increasing the risk of COPD, which was in line with previous findings.

Noise, as a social acoustic stressor, can disrupt various neurochemical processes and consequently affect immune response functions. Prolonged exposure to noise leads to an elevation in stress hormones, catecholamines, and cortisol levels. This increase in stress hormones may result in a decrease in the count and activity of lymphocytes and natural killer cells, making the respiratory system more vulnerable to infections. Additionally, long-term noise exposure can contribute to sleep disorders, which in turn hinder lymphocyte division and migration, impair antiviral immune responses, and promote systemic inflammation.[20] Furthermore, noise exposure can promote the production of reactive oxygen species and inflammatory factors to exacerbate lung inflammation and contribute to extracellular matrix degradation and connective tissue fibrosis.[21,22]

1)All data used in the study were collected from the GWAS catalog, and some clinical information was missing, so we cannot rule out all potential pleiotropy and heterogeneity.

Furthermore, special populations, such as industrial individuals and high-risk smoking communities, have not been further analyzed. This may lead to potential collider bias and limited generalizability of this conclusion. Thus, it is necessary to verify this conclusion in other populations.

5. Conclusion

Altogether, we determined the causal link between ONE and the risk of COPD. However, because all cases were from Europe, further cohort studies and randomized controlled trials involving a larger population, such as Asian and Middle-Eastern populations and high-risk smoking communities, are necessary to validate the results. In addition, basic experiments to explore the potential mechanisms of ONE on COPD development are necessary.
