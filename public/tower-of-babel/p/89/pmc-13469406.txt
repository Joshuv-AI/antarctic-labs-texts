Enhanced weathering of lizardite-rich sand in seawater: effects of pH and CO₂ pressure on carbonate formation for climate change mitigation
Authors: Arshad Ali, Usman Taura, Sami Al-Khamisi, Zulfiqar Ahmad Rehan, Amrou Al-Alawi, Anas Hamood Al-Rashdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhanced weathering (EW) of serpentinized peridotites is being investigated as a potential approach for ex situ CO₂ sequestration in both coastal and open-ocean environments. Previous studies on serpentine minerals have provided important insights into their geochemical reactivity, highlighting their preferential dissolution behavior and enhanced weathering potential under natural conditions. This study examines the CO₂ sequestration potential of lizardite-rich sand and evaluates the formation of hydrated and carbonated mineral phases under varying CO₂ conditions. Mineralogical analyses indicate the presence of aragonite, magnesite, and other secondary minerals associated with CO₂ uptake, suggesting successful carbonation. Changes in pH and dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) point to differing geochemical dynamics under ambient and elevated CO₂ conditions, with acidification observed in both scenarios. While the findings support the feasibility of using lizardite-rich materials for EW-based carbon sequestration, the associated pH decline raises concern about potential ocean acidification. However, incorporating lime derived from industrial marble waste into sand presents a promising approach to mitigate both climate change and ocean acidification. This mixed sand offers dual environmental benefits by facilitating carbon mineralization and promoting the sustainable utilization of industrial by-products, thereby contributing to climate resilience and environmental sustainability. The results underscore the need for careful selection of rock types and operational conditions to ensure both carbon removal efficacy and environmental compatibility. Ongoing work aims to identify optimal materials for sustainable deployment in marine environments.Keywords: Enhanced weathering, CO₂ sequestration, Lizardite, Carbonation, PH and dissolved inorganic carbon, Ocean acidification

Introduction

Serpentinites are hydrous ultramafic rocks predominantly composed of serpentine-group minerals—chrysotile, lizardite, and antigorite—formed through the low-temperature hydration (serpentinization) of olivine- and orthopyroxene-rich peridotites (Boudier et al. 2010; Streit et al. 2012; Boskadi et al. 2020; Corre et al. 2023). These minerals, with a general composition of Mg₃Si₂O₅(OH)₄, incorporate substantial structurally bound water (~ 10–15 wt%), making serpentinites important reservoirs of water and fluid-mobile elements in the upper mantle and forearc environments (Scambelluri et al. 2019; Guillot and Hattori 2013; Deschamps et al. 2013). As a result, serpentinite-bearing systems play a key role in geodynamic processes such as slab hydration, mantle wedge melting, and the mechanical weakening of plate boundaries, while also influencing fluid-mediated geochemical fluxes.

Serpentinite exposures are widespread in ophiolitic complexes, forearc settings, and orogenic belts, ranging from large massifs such as the Semail Ophiolite in Oman (~ 20,000 km2) to smaller fault-controlled bodies (Leask et al. 2021). In addition to their tectonic importance, serpentinites contribute to economically and environmentally significant processes. For instance, tropical weathering of serpentinites produces nickel–laterite deposits, while natural and engineered carbonation reactions form stable carbonate minerals capable of long-term CO₂ sequestration. Furthermore, low-temperature serpentinization reactions generate hydrogen and reduced carbon species that sustain chemosynthetic microbial ecosystems (Butt and Cluzel 2013; Mervine et al. 2014).

These attributes have led to increasing interest in ultramafic rocks as feedstocks for carbon dioxide removal (CDR) via enhanced weathering (EW) and mineral carbonation. Field-scale and modeling studies indicate that the application of crushed silicate materials can result in measurable CO₂ drawdown, although the magnitude and durability of this effect depend on factors such as mineralogy, reaction kinetics, particle size, climatic conditions, and lifecycle emissions (Beerling et al. 2020; Knapp et al. 2023). In marine settings, additional complexities arise from seawater carbonate chemistry, buffering capacity, and ecological sensitivity, necessitating careful experimental validation under realistic conditions (Beerling et al. 2024; 2025).

The reactivity of lizardite, a common serpentine polymorph, has been explored in both natural and engineered systems, where its dissolution releases Mg that can subsequently form stable carbonate phases such as magnesite and hydromagnesite (Hansen et al. 2005; Wilson et al. 2009). Similarly, the formation of carbonate minerals including aragonite during serpentine–seawater interaction, particularly under elevated CO₂ conditions, is well documented (Kelemen and Matter 2008; Hövelmann et al. 2012; Lacinska et al. 2017). While these studies establish the fundamental pathways of mineral carbonation, they do not fully address how such processes are influenced by site-specific materials, natural seawater chemistry, and alkalinity management strategies under controlled marine conditions.

In this context, the present study does not aim to establish carbonate formation as a new finding, but rather to investigate how these well-established processes operate within a distinct and application-relevant experimental framework. Specifically, this work integrates (i) a lizardite-rich, readily available, and low-cost material derived from the Oman Ophiolite; (ii) the use of natural coastal seawater; (iii) alkalinity enhancement via lime produced from locally sourced marble waste; and (iv) systematic pH manipulation to simulate realistic marine environments. This combined approach enables a more comprehensive assessment of enhanced weathering in coastal systems, where both carbon sequestration efficiency and environmental impacts must be considered.

The primary objective of this study is therefore to elucidate the coupled geochemical and mineralogical evolution of lizardite–seawater systems under controlled yet environmentally relevant conditions. Particular emphasis is placed on carbonate formation, pH dynamics, and the development of secondary mineral phases. By doing so, this study aims to provide new insights into how regionally available materials and alkalinity amendments can be effectively combined to optimize ocean-based enhanced weathering strategies, while minimizing unintended consequences such as localized acidification and secondary phase formation (Beerling et al. 2020; Renforth and Kruger 2013).

Materials and methods

Materials

The F-1 rock sample (23°31ʹ40.8ʺ N, 58°00ʹ08.7ʺ E) was collected from the Fanja area of the Oman Ophiolite. Throughout the manuscript, the F-1 sample is referred to interchangeably as lizardite, LIZ, and F-1. The sample was later pulverized to a particle size below 280 μm for subsequent mineralogical characterization and analytical measurements. Lime was produced by calcining marble waste obtained from the Al-Aoula Company Quarry (22º55ʹ11.70″ N, 58º86ʹ12.48″ E) in the Sur region, Oman, and used to prepare the lizardite–lime mixtures for carbonation experiments. For pH experiments, analytical-grade lime (extrapure) was procured from Daejung Company. Coastal seawater (pH = 8.01) used in all experiments was collected from Al Hail North Beach (23°38ʹ13.8ʺ N, 58°15ʹ11.1ʺ E) in Seeb, Muscat. The seawater was stored in a refrigerator wrapped in aluminum foil for five days before use, then filtered through Whatman filter paper (#541) and kept refrigerated until experimentation.

Material characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the F-1 sample indicates that lizardite is the dominant crystalline phase (Fig. 1), confirming the serpentinized nature of the material. The sample was further compared and validated against the XRD pattern of lizardite from Snarum, Norway, obtained from Nichika, Japan. Complementary scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) reveals a fibrous to platy texture characteristic of serpentine-group minerals, with spectra showing a magnesium-rich composition consistent with lizardite. The photomicrograph of the thin section reveals a rock fabric dominated by lizardite, with the presence of relic olivine grains preserved within the serpentinized matrix (Fig. 2).

a Outcrop of the F-1 sample from the Fanja area, Oman. b SEM micrographs of the F-1 sample. c EDS spectrum of the F-1 sample. d Microphotograph showing lizardite (LIZ) as the dominant mineral phase with minor remnants of its precursor olivine

Influence of particle size and surface area on material reactivity

It should be noted that BET surface area and detailed particle size distribution data were not determined in this study. While the lizardite material was mechanically ground and sieved to < 280 μm to ensure experimental consistency, variations in specific surface area may still influence mineral dissolution and carbonation kinetics, as surface area is a primary control on silicate reactivity (Pokrovsky and Schott 2004; Ali et al. 2025). Therefore, the results presented herein are interpreted in a relative framework across experimental conditions rather than as absolute rate-normalized values. This limitation is consistent with previous experimental and enhanced weathering studies, where grain size fractions are commonly used as a proxy for reactive surface area in the absence of BET measurements (Hangx and Spiers 2009; O’Connor et al. 2001).

Comparative evaluation of silicate materials for enhanced weathering and CO₂ mineralization

Previous experimental studies have demonstrated that olivine, basalt, and serpentinite all possess significant potential for enhanced weathering and mineral carbonation, although each material exhibits distinct reaction behaviors and practical limitations. Olivine generally shows the fastest dissolution kinetics and high theoretical CO₂ sequestration capacity due to its Mg-rich composition, particularly under elevated temperature, pressure, and acidic conditions (Hangx and Spiers 2009; O’Connor et al. 2001). However, its performance is often constrained by silica passivation layers, reduced dissolution rates under neutral to alkaline conditions, and the requirement for energy-intensive pretreatment or high-pressure systems. Basalt has also demonstrated promising carbon mineralization potential, including successful field-scale CO₂ sequestration with rapid formation of stable carbonate minerals (Matter et al. 2016; Snæbjörnsdóttir et al. 2020). Nevertheless, basalt systems commonly involve complex multi-mineral reaction pathways, secondary mineral formation, and dependence on injection-based infrastructure and water–rock interaction control. In comparison, lizardite-rich serpentinite represents an abundant ultramafic resource with demonstrated suitability for engineered enhanced weathering systems. Previous investigations have shown that serpentinite can achieve effective Mg extraction and carbonate formation under controlled conditions, particularly when combined with thermal or chemical activation (Park and Fan 2004; Pokrovsky and Schott 2004). Although serpentinite generally exhibits slower intrinsic dissolution kinetics relative to olivine, its wide availability, predictable dissolution behavior, and adaptability to engineered geochemical conditions make it a suitable candidate for enhanced weathering applications. These studies collectively support the rationale for focusing the present work on lizardite-rich serpentinite as a feasible and regionally accessible carbon sequestration material.

Methods

Carbonation experiments (open system)

Three experimental runs were conducted under ambient atmospheric pressure, with continuous stirring, using hyperalkaline, alkaline, and acidic seawater.

Hyperalkaline water (seawater + lime, EW1)

The initial pH of natural seawater was 8.01. To generate a hyperalkaline medium without the direct addition of synthetic bases, a natural feedstock approach was adopted. Preliminary pH optimization was carried out using a lizardite–water–lime system, employing commercially available lime. Subsequent experiments utilized a lizardite-to-lime mixture (Liz–Lime; 4:1 ratio).

A total of 10 g of the Liz–Lime mixture was added to 500 mL of seawater in a flask and maintained at 60 °C for 30 days under ambient atmospheric pressure, with continuous stirring at 250 rpm using a magnetic stirrer (Fig. 3). The flask was covered with perforated aluminum foil to minimize evaporative losses while permitting gas exchange with the atmosphere. The selected reaction temperature (60 °C) exceeds typical surface seawater conditions (< 30 °C in the Arabian Sea) and therefore represents an accelerated reaction regime rather than in situ ocean conditions.

a Optimization of pH in the lizardite–water–lime system using varying amounts of commercially sourced lime. b Relationship between pH and the amount of lime added to lizardite. Abbreviations: SW = seawater; COM = commercial

Throughout the experiment, approximately 700 mL of seawater was lost due to evaporation and was periodically replenished to maintain a constant reaction volume of 500 mL, thereby preserving a water-to-sample ratio of approximately 50. Upon completion, the suspension was cooled to room temperature, and the solid residue was filtered (Whatman No. 50), followed by drying at 105 °C in an oven. The dried material was then ground to a fine powder using an agate mortar and pestle for subsequent mineralogical and chemical characterization (Fig. 4).

Stages of the carbonation experiment: a lizardite–seawater–lime system maintained on a hot plate under continuous stirring, b dried experimental residue, and c finely pulverized powder prepared for subsequent characterization

Alkaline water (seawater, EW2)

A 10 g portion of the F-1 sample was combined with 500 mL of seawater in a flask and continuously stirred at 250 rpm on a hot plate maintained at 60 °C for a duration of 30 days. The flask was covered with perforated aluminum foil to allow gas exchange while minimizing evaporative water loss. Despite these precautions, approximately 700 mL of additional seawater was periodically added to maintain the solution volume at 500 mL throughout the experiment. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and filtered using Whatman No. 50 filter paper. The solid residue was subsequently oven-dried at 105 °C and ground to a fine powder using an agate mortar and pestle for further mineralogical and geochemical characterization.

Acidic water (seawater + hydrochloric acid, EW3)

Seawater was acidified to a pH of approximately 4.5 by the addition of 13 drops of concentrated HCl, following pH optimization as illustrated in Fig. 5. An additional 1000 mL of seawater was prepared and adjusted to the same pH (~ 4.5) to serve as a stock solution for compensating evaporative losses during the experiment conducted at 60 °C. Subsequently, 10 g of lizardite was introduced into a flask containing 500 mL of the acidified seawater. The mixture was maintained at 60 °C under continuous stirring at 250 rpm for 30 days to promote dissolution and reaction processes.

Variation in seawater pH following the addition of HCl

Carbonation experiment (closed system, EW4)

A closed system experiment was conducted to simulate enhanced weathering under high CO2 pressure (200 psi), static conditions, and its results were compared with those from an open system experiment (EW2) performed under continuous stirring at atmospheric pressure (CO₂ = 425 ppm ≈ 0.00625 psi) and seawater pH (8.01). A total of 8 g of sample F-1 was reacted with 400 mL of seawater in a 500-mL stainless steel autoclave reactor maintained under static conditions at 60 °C for 30 days. The reactor was sealed and pressurized with CO₂ gas up to 200 psi (Fig. 6a). According to Henry’s law (C = kH P), the equilibrium concentration of dissolved CO₂ (C) in the liquid phase is directly proportional to its partial pressure (P) in the gas phase, where kH is Henry’s constant, a temperature-dependent parameter (Carroll et al. 1991; Sander 2015).

a High-pressure CO₂ (200 psi) reactor setup for closed-system enhanced weathering experiments. b, c CO₂ pressure over 30 days, showing an initial sharp drop within the first hour, followed by an exponential increase and gradual decline

Initially, the CO₂ pressure exhibited a rapid decline from 200 psi to approximately 100 psi, suggesting a fast dissolution rate of CO₂ into the seawater (Fig. 6b). The decrease in headspace pressure reflects the progressive transfer of CO₂ from the gas phase to the aqueous phase. Subsequently, a temporary increase in pressure (from 100 to 112 psi over 4.5 h; Fig. 6c) was observed, likely due to the thermal expansion of the gas phase and partial degassing of previously dissolved CO₂ as the reactor temperature increased from ambient to 60 °C (Duan and Sun 2003).

Thereafter, the pressure gradually declined at a slower rate, reaching a quasi-steady state at approximately 92 psi after 30 days. This slow pressure decrease indicates that CO₂ dissolution approached equilibrium, as the concentration gradient between the gas and liquid phases diminished. The observed pressure–time curve leveling off near 92 psi is consistent with CO₂–aqueous phase equilibration behavior governed by Henry’s law, where gas-phase pressure stabilizes once the liquid phase becomes nearly saturated (Carroll et al. 1991; Sander 2015).

Characterization methods

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD)

XRD analyses were carried out at the Earth Sciences Research Centre, Sultan Qaboos University, using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 benchtop diffractometer. The instrument was equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source and a scintillation counter (SC) detector, operating at 40 kV and 15 mA. Prior to analysis, the samples were finely ground in an agate mortar and pestle to achieve uniform grain size and ensure homogeneity. The powdered samples were then mounted on clean glass holders and scanned over a selected 2θ range at a rate of 2.5° 2θ min⁻1. Diffraction data were processed using PDXL Basic software to determine key parameters, including peak position, intensity, integrated area, and full width at half maximum (FWHM). Mineral phase identification was performed by comparison with standard reference patterns from the PDF-4 Minerals (2025) database licensed by the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD®).

Scanning electron microscopy–energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDS)

The microstructural and compositional characteristics of the samples were examined at the Surface Laboratory, College of Science, Sultan Qaboos University, using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JEOL JSM-7800F, Japan) coupled with an Oxford Instruments EDS system (UK). Analyses were performed at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a working distance of 10 mm. To minimize surface charging and enhance image quality, nonconductive samples were sputter-coated with a thin platinum layer using an SPI-module coater (USA) prior to imaging. High-resolution micrographs and elemental maps were obtained to assess particle morphology, surface texture, and elemental distribution within the samples.

Loss on ignition (LOI)

LOI was determined to quantify volatile constituents released upon heating. Approximately 1 g of each dried sample was placed in a precleaned ceramic crucible and heated in a Nabertherm (Germany) furnace. The temperature was increased to 950 °C within 30 min, followed by a 2-h dwell time. After cooling to room temperature in a desiccator, the samples were weighed and reheated for an additional hour at the same temperature to ensure complete removal of volatiles. The LOI value was calculated based on the percentage of weight loss, which reflects the release of structural water, organic matter, carbonates, and other volatile components.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

TGA was conducted to characterize the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of the samples using an SDT Q600 V20.9 Build 20 instrument at Sultan Qaboos University. Approximately 6–10 mg of finely powdered material was placed in alumina crucibles and heated from 25 to 950 °C at a constant rate of 25 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 100 mL/min. Continuous recording of the mass change as a function of temperature allowed for the identification of dehydration, dehydroxylation, and decarbonation processes, corresponding to distinct thermal events within the sample.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The functional groups and molecular structures of secondary minerals formed during the enhanced weathering experiments were characterized using FTIR. Residual solid products recovered after the experiments were analyzed using a Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer installed at the Department of Chemistry, College of Science, Sultan Qaboos University, Oman. Infrared spectra were collected over the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm⁻1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻1. Each spectrum was acquired by averaging 32 scans to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. The obtained spectra were subsequently used to identify characteristic vibrational modes associated with carbonate, silicate, hydroxyl, and other mineral phases formed during weathering and carbonation reactions.

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES)

The concentrations of major dissolved elements in the experimental filtrates were determined using a PerkinElmer Optima 3000 ICP-OES housed at the Chemical and Biological Services Center (CBSC), Sultan Qaboos University, Oman. Prior to analysis, filtrate samples were passed through 0.45-μm membrane filters to remove suspended particulates and subsequently acidified with ultrapure nitric acid (HNO₃) to preserve dissolved constituents. Samples were analyzed together with calibration standards prepared from certified multielement stock solutions covering the expected concentration ranges of the analytes.

The ICP-OES instrument was calibrated using external standards, and analytical quality control was maintained through the analysis of procedural blanks, duplicate samples, and calibration verification standards. During analysis, samples were nebulized into an argon plasma, where constituent elements were atomized and excited at temperatures exceeding 6000 K. The emitted element-specific optical radiation was measured at characteristic wavelengths, and elemental concentrations were quantified through comparison with calibration curves generated from the certified standards. Major and trace elements, including Mg, Ca, Na, K, Si, Sr, S, and B, were determined and reported in mg L⁻1.

Results and discussion

XRD profiles

Carbonation in hyperalkaline saline medium (open system)

The untreated lizardite–lime mixture exhibits strong reflections corresponding to lizardite and lime, with minor peaks of portlandite (Fig. 7). In contrast, the residues from the carbonation experiment show weakened lizardite reflections and the emergence of magnesite, indicating progressive alteration of lizardite under hyperalkaline seawater conditions. The absence of lime peaks further indicates that the lime component has undergone extensive carbonation, resulting in the formation of aragonite. Overall, the XRD patterns reveal partial decomposition of lizardite and the development of secondary carbonate minerals through a coupled hydration–carbonation process, consistent with earlier studies on serpentine alteration and CO₂ mineralization (Kelemen and Matter 2008; Hövelmann et al. 2012; Lacinska et al. 2017). Several sharp peaks corresponding to NaCl are also observed in the EW1-R pattern, indicating the presence of residual halite derived from the seawater used in the experiments. These peaks do not represent any geochemical transformation within the silicate–carbonate system but rather reflect the saline nature of the experimental medium.

XRD patterns of the untreated mixture of lizardite and lime (4:1) and the carbonation reaction residue (EW1-R) after 30 days of exposure to seawater and air at 60 °C under continuous stirring (250 rpm)

Carbonation in alkaline saline medium (open system vs. closed system)

The XRD pattern of the carbonation residue illustrates the mineralogical changes that occurred before and after the enhanced weathering process (Fig. 8). Initially, sample F-1 exhibits prominent lizardite peaks; however, these main reflections notably decrease in intensity after reaction, indicating partial dissolution of lizardite during its interaction with seawater. This behavior is consistent with incongruent dissolution, wherein Mg2⁺ and Si(OH)₄ are released into the solution (Daval et al. 2013). New diffraction peaks corresponding to magnesite appear, indicating the reprecipitation of Mg2⁺ from solution in the presence of CO₃2⁻ derived from seawater or bicarbonate ions. Additionally, Ca2⁺ present in seawater reacts with bicarbonate to form Ca-bearing carbonates, primarily aragonite. Shelkovite, a hydrated magnesium carbonate, was identified, indicating that CO₂ mineralization occurred during the reaction. Similar hydrated magnesium carbonates—including nesquehonite, dypingite, lansfordite, hydromagnesite, and barringtonite—have been reported in previous studies (Rausis et al. 2020; Osselin et al. 2022; Pokharel et al. 2024), supporting the observation of CO₂ mineralization in both systems. Notably, stronger carbonate signals were detected in the closed system, likely due to the retention of CO₂ under pressure. These findings confirm mineralogical transformations consistent with carbon capture processes. Additionally, new peaks corresponding to halite appeared, presumably resulting from evaporation and salt precipitation.

XRD patterns of the initial lizardite mixture (F-1) and the corresponding carbonation residues formed under open-system (EW2-R) and closed-system (EW4-R, 200 psi CO₂) conditions

Carbonation in acidic saline medium (open system)

The XRD patterns reveal distinct mineralogical transformations resulting from the reaction of lizardite with seawater and air at 60 °C over a 30-day period (Fig. 9). The untreated sample displays sharp and well-defined reflections characteristic of lizardite, with prominent peaks at 2θ ≈ 12.1° and 24.5°, consistent with the trioctahedral serpentine structure. The intensity and sharpness of these peaks indicate high crystallinity and limited preexisting alteration.

XRD patterns of untreated lizardite (F-1) and the solid residue (EW3-R) obtained after reaction with seawater and air at 60 °C for 30 days under acidic conditions

After 30 days of seawater exposure, the diffraction pattern of the reacted residue exhibits a pronounced attenuation of the diagnostic lizardite reflections, implying partial dissolution of the serpentine layers under the experimental conditions. Concurrently, new reflections corresponding to magnesite emerge, suggesting that Mg2⁺ released from lizardite breakdown reprecipitated as carbonate. Additional peaks near 2θ ≈ 30°, 42°, and 62° are attributed to aragonite, indicating that Ca2⁺ derived from seawater reacted with dissolved inorganic carbon to form secondary carbonates.

These mineralogical changes collectively demonstrate that lizardite underwent incongruent dissolution, releasing Mg2⁺ and Si(OH)₄ species that subsequently reprecipitated as magnesite and amorphous Mg–Si hydrates, while Ca-bearing carbonates formed through interaction with seawater bicarbonate (Daval et al. 2013). The observed peak broadening and low-intensity diffuse features further suggest the formation of nanocrystalline or amorphous alteration products, consistent with low-temperature aqueous alteration processes (Rausis et al. 2020).

Such transformations underscore the dual pathways of mineral carbonation and hydroxide formation, both fundamental to enhanced weathering and carbon dioxide removal mechanisms in marine environments (Geerts et al. 2025). The appearance of magnesite phases indicates that lizardite–seawater interactions at 60 °C can effectively facilitate CO₂ sequestration through the generation of stable secondary carbonates under ambient pressure, mildly acidic conditions.

SEM images

The SEM observations reveal pronounced textural and mineralogical transformations of fibrous lizardite (Fig. 10a) upon interaction with seawater under systematically varied pH and CO₂ conditions, reflecting progressive dissolution–precipitation processes in the experimental residues. These transformations illustrate how fluid composition, system openness, and CO₂ availability collectively control mineral stability and secondary phase formation during lizardite weathering.

SEM images illustrating the morphological evolution of lizardite and experimental residues after reaction with seawater under varying pH and CO₂ pressure conditions. a Untreated lizardite (F-1) showing fibrous–platy textures typical of serpentine. b EW1-R (hyperalkaline, open system) displays densely packed aggregates of secondary carbonates dominated by aragonite (arag) and subordinate magnesite (mag). c EW2-R (alkaline, open system) shows scattered precipitates of aragonite and magnesite on partially altered lizardite surfaces. d EW3-R (acidic, open system) exhibits etched lizardite fibers with fine-grained aragonite, minor magnesite, and traces of calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂]. e EW4-R (alkaline, closed system, 200 psi CO₂) presents compact carbonate coatings, primarily aragonite, with subordinate magnesite crystals

Under hyperalkaline conditions, extensive alteration of the lizardite matrix results in dense aggregates of newly formed secondary carbonates, dominated by aragonite and subordinate magnesite (Fig. 10b). This mineral assemblage suggests enhanced Mg2⁺ mobilization from lizardite dissolution and localized carbonate supersaturation facilitated by high pH and Ca2⁺ enrichment (Giampouras 2020 ). The pH elevation was achieved through lime addition, which not only promotes carbonate precipitation but also serves as a sustainable alternative to synthetic chemical reagents by utilizing locally available industrial by-products such as marble waste (Renforth et al. 2011; Power et al. 2013a). Such lime-assisted alkalinity enhancement accelerates lizardite dissolution and carbonate nucleation, processes well-documented in natural and engineered alkaline environments (Pokrovsky & Schott 2004; Oelkers et al. 2008).

In the alkaline open system, both aragonite and magnesite occur as scattered overgrowths on partially altered lizardite surfaces (Fig. 10c). The coexistence of these phases likely reflects microscale variations in CO₂ availability and local pH buffering capacity. Similar heterogeneous textures have been reported in seawater–serpentine experiments and natural serpentinization systems, where CO₂ diffusion kinetics and ion activities govern carbonate mineral selection and morphology (Oelkers et al. 2008).

The acidic system exhibits distinct dissolution features with minimal evidence of secondary mineral precipitation, indicating intensified proton-promoted breakdown of the lizardite lattice (Fig. 10d). Similar behavior has been reported for serpentine exposed to high acid concentrations, where a rapid surface exchange of magnesium with H⁺ ions is followed by a slower release of magnesium from the crystal interior over time (Bales and Morgan 1985; McCutcheon et al. 2015; Li et al. 2023). Nevertheless, the minor presence of Ca(OH)₂ and fine-grained carbonates suggests transient alkalinity at microsites (Fig. 9), likely generated by localized neutralization reactions as divalent cations are liberated (Moras et al. 2022).

In contrast, the closed system under elevated CO₂ pressure (200 psi) yields compact aragonite-rich coatings enveloping the reacted surfaces (Fig. 10e). The formation of these dense, carbonate-rich textures signifies enhanced carbonate ion activity and near-complete consumption of reactive Mg2⁺ and Ca2⁺ under CO₂-saturated conditions. Such textural evidence supports the efficacy of high-CO₂, low-fluid-mobility systems in promoting mineral trapping of CO₂, similar to the carbonation processes observed in the CarbFix and analogous mineral sequestration experiments (Harrison et al. 2013; Gislason and Oelkers 2014; Matter et al. 2016; Ali et al. 2025).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that lizardite–seawater interactions under alkaline and CO₂-enriched conditions foster the formation of secondary carbonate minerals through a coupled dissolution–precipitation mechanism. The findings highlight the potential of lizardite-based enhanced weathering as a viable pathway for permanent CO₂ sequestration in marine or coastal environments, particularly in EW1 sample when lizardite is mixed with industrial alkaline residues. This approach integrates carbon capture with waste valorization, aligning with emerging strategies for scalable, nature-based carbon removal technologies (Power et al. 2013a; Renforth and Henderson 2017).

Physicochemical parameters

LOI and salinity

The combined LOI and salinity data provide insights into mineralogical transformations and fluid–rock interactions occurring during CO₂–lizardite–seawater reactions. LOI represents the proportion of volatile constituents, mainly structural water, CO₂ incorporated in carbonates, and hydrated phases, within the solid residues (Fig. 11a). The initial feedstock (Liz-Lime; sample EW1) shows a moderate LOI of 10.7 wt%, which increases markedly to 30 wt% in the reacted product (EW1-R) under hyperalakline conditions (pH = 12), indicating substantial formation of volatile-bearing secondary minerals such as hydrated magnesium carbonates and hydroxides during the early stages of CO₂–lizardite interaction. This enrichment reflects both CO₂ mineralization and water incorporation into newly formed secondary phases. In comparison, samples EW2 (open system) and EW4 (closed system), derived from feedstock F-1 with an initial LOI of 13.4 wt%, exhibit lower LOI values (16.9 wt% and 15.4 wt%, respectively), implying more rapid carbonation in Ca-rich hyperalkaline systems relative to the slower weathering of lizardite under alkaline conditions (pH = 8.01), consistent with previous studies on CO₂–serpentine reactions (Rausis et al. 2020; Osselin et al. 2022; Pokharel et al. 2024). Under acidic conditions (EW3; pH = 4.5), the slightly elevated LOI (19.6 wt%) suggests enhanced lizardite dissolution facilitated by the greater availability of H⁺ ions.

a LOI values of feedstock and carbonation experiment residues obtained at different reactive conditions. b Salinity variations in corresponding filtrates compared with the seawater

The initial seawater (SW) exhibited a salinity of 39.4 g/kg (Fig. 11b), consistent with typical marine conditions (Millero et al. 2008). Following the CO₂–lizardite–seawater reactions, the filtrates showed variable salinity depending on the system configuration and reaction environment. In the open-system experiments, EW1-F displayed a notable increase in salinity to 43 g/kg, which can be attributed to both evaporative concentration (total seawater volume reduction from 1200 to 500 mL over 30 days) and enhanced dissolution of lizardite under hyperalkaline conditions. This dissolution releases soluble ionic species such as Mg2⁺, Ca2⁺, and CO₃2⁻ into the aqueous phase, promoting early-stage mineral breakdown and CO₂ uptake (Pokharel et al. 2024; Harrison et al. 2013).

In contrast, filtrates from EW2-F (38 g/kg; alkaline conditions) and EW3-F (39.5 g/kg; acidic conditions) showed either slightly lower or nearly unchanged salinity relative to the initial seawater, reflecting differences in dissolution–precipitation dynamics. The marginal decrease in EW2-F suggests limited ion accumulation due to the precipitation of carbonate and hydroxide phases, whereas the relatively stable salinity in EW3-F implies that dissolution and ion release under acidic conditions were remained balanced.

In the closed system experiment (EW4-F), which utilized only 400 mL of seawater, salinity decreased slightly compared with the initial value. This reduction likely indicates consumption of dissolved ions during carbonate precipitation under high pressure (Kaszuba et al. 2013) and re-equilibration between solid and aqueous phases within the confined reaction environment.

pH and HCO3−–CO32− species

The pH values show notable variations before and after the enhanced weathering experiments, indicating dynamic shifts in the acid–base equilibrium of the CO₂–lizardite–seawater system (Fig. 12a). Before the reaction, the initial solutions were predominantly alkaline to hyperalkaline (pH 8.01–12), except for sample EW3. During the reaction, the dissolution of Mg-silicates resulting from lizardite breakdown, coupled with the partial hydrolysis of Mg2⁺ and SiO₄4⁻ species, leads to the release of hydroxide ions (Harrison et al. 2021; Pokharel et al. 2024). Following the reaction, the pH values declined markedly in EW1-F (8.39) and moderately in EW2-F (7.21) and EW4-F (7.13), whereas EW3-F exhibited an increase from an initial pH of 4.5 to 6.74. The pronounced initial decrease in pH across the filtrates can be attributed to CO₂ dissolution and subsequent acidification via the formation of carbonic acid and its dissociation into H⁺, HCO₃⁻, and CO₃2⁻. This low-pH stage indicates a transient dominance of dissolved CO₂ and a reduced buffering capacity within the system. The subsequent pH increase, particularly observed in EW3-F, is likely associated with carbonate mineral precipitation and the re-establishment of equilibrium within the bicarbonate–carbonate buffering system (Huijgen and Comans 2005; Osselin et al. 2022).

a Variation in pH of seawater filtrates before and after the enhanced weathering experiments compared to the initial seawater. b Concentrations of dissolved inorganic carbon species (HCO₃⁻ and CO₃2⁻) in seawater filtrates before and after the enhanced weathering experiments

Carbonate ions become predominant over bicarbonate ions once the solution pH exceeds the second dissociation constant (pKa2) of carbonic acid. This relationship, expressed as [CO₃2⁻]/[HCO₃⁻] = 10(pH – pKₐ₂), indicates that carbonate concentration increases exponentially with rising pH. At 25 °C, pKa2 is approximately 10.3, so carbonate species dominate above pH ~ 10.3 (Stumm and Morgan 1996). For instance, at pH 10.5, carbonate concentration is about 1.6 times higher than bicarbonate, increasing to five times at pH 11 and nearly fifty times at pH 12. However, pKₐ₂ decreases slightly with increasing temperature, pressure, and salinity, shifting carbonate dominance to around pH 10.0–10.2 in seawater (Millero 2007; Zeebe and Wolf-Gladrow 2001). In the case of EW1-F, carbonate concentrations are approximately 12 times higher than bicarbonate, slightly below the theoretical ratio expected at pH 12. This discrepancy may be linked to calcium carbonate precipitation due to lime addition, as also suggested by the elevated LOI values of the residue (Fig. 11a). Conversely, bicarbonate concentrations are significantly higher in EW2-F, EW3-F, and EW4-F, following the order EW3-F < EW2-F < EW4-F. A comparison between open- and closed-system conditions under alkaline environments (EW2-F and EW4-F) indicates that the closed system maintained substantially higher CO₂ pressure (200 to 92 psi), resulting in filtrates enriched in bicarbonate species (~ 35 times).

Thermogravimetric (TGA) profiles

The TGA profiles (Fig. 13) provide insight into the phase evolution and extent of carbonation across samples subjected to different reaction conditions. The analyzed materials include: (i) F-1-UT, representing untreated lizardite sand; (ii) Liz–Lime, a mixture of lizardite and marble waste (4:1) designed to elevate seawater pH to ~ 12; and (iii) reaction residues (EW1-R to EW4-R) obtained under varying pH regimes, system configurations (open vs. closed), and CO₂ availability at 60 °C. The baseline samples (F-1-UT) exhibit characteristic mass loss features associated with lizardite dehydroxylation and indigenous carbonate phases, consistent with established thermal behavior of serpentine minerals and carbonates, while the Liz–Lime mixture additionally shows signatures attributable to portlandite and pre-existing carbonate phases. Quantitatively, the initial CO₂ contents are low (1.4 and 2.0 g/100 g, respectively; Table 1), confirming minimal carbonation prior to reaction.

Following reaction, all residues show enhanced mass loss in the carbonate decomposition temperature window, indicating secondary carbonate formation. The extent of CO₂ sequestration derived from TGA increases in the order: EW4-R (2.5 g/100 g) < EW1-R (4.2 g/100 g) ≈ EW2-R (4.3 g/100 g) < EW3-R (6.0 g/100 g). This trend highlights a strong dependence of carbonation efficiency on reaction conditions. The highest CO₂ uptake in EW3-R reflects the role of acidic conditions in promoting proton-enhanced dissolution of lizardite, thereby increasing Mg2⁺ release and facilitating carbonate formation, in agreement with well-established dissolution kinetics of silicate minerals (White and Brantley 2003; Oelkers et al. 2008). In contrast, hyperalkaline (EW1-R) and mildly alkaline (EW2-R) systems exhibit comparable carbonation extents, suggesting that although high pH can suppress silicate dissolution rates, it promotes carbonate precipitation once supersaturation is achieved, consistent with carbonate geochemistry and precipitation kinetics (Morse et al. 2007; Power et al. 2013b).

A notable observation is that carbonation in the open, mildly alkaline system (EW2-R) exceeds that in the closed system under elevated CO₂ pressure (EW4-R) by approximately 40%. Although seemingly counterintuitive, this behavior can be attributed to differences in mass transfer and reaction kinetics. In the open system, continuous gas exchange, coupled with enhanced mixing and potential evaporation-induced concentration effects, promotes more rapid attainment of carbonate supersaturation and accelerates nucleation and growth processes, as widely observed in open-system weathering and carbonation environments (Schuiling and Krijgsman 2006; Hartmann et al. 2013). Conversely, in the closed system, despite high CO₂ partial pressure (200 psi), limited mass transfer and slower reaction kinetics may delay the buildup of supersaturation, thereby restricting net carbonate precipitation within the experimental timeframe, consistent with transport-limited carbonation behavior reported in mineral carbonation studies (Oelkers et al. 2008; Power et al. 2013b).

Overall, these results demonstrate that carbonation efficiency is governed not solely by CO₂ availability, but by the interplay between pH-controlled dissolution kinetics, transport processes, and saturation state evolution. The TGA-derived CO₂ values further indicate that experimental conditions mimicking dynamic, open system environments may be more effective for promoting carbonate formation than static, high-pressure systems, at least over the timescales investigated, in line with current understanding of enhanced weathering processes (Hartmann et al. 2013; Power et al. 2013b).

FTIR evidence of carbonation and lizardite alteration during enhanced weathering

The FTIR spectra of EW2-R, EW3-R, and EW4-R indicate limited and poorly developed carbonate formation, consistent with incipient CO₂ mineralization rather than well-crystallized carbonate phases (Fig. 14). A weak and broadened feature in the ~ 870–900 cm⁻1 region, attributed to the ν₂ out-of-plane bending mode of CO₃2⁻, is the most persistent carbonate-related signal; however, its overlap with Si–O vibrations in the ~ 900–1100 cm⁻1 range reflects a strong contribution from residual serpentine minerals (e.g., lizardite), complicating definitive carbonate assignment. The characteristic ν₃ asymmetric carbonate stretching band (~ 1400–1500 cm⁻1) is absent or highly subdued in all samples, while a dominant absorption near ~ 1630 cm⁻1 is consistently observed. This band is more plausibly linked to H–O–H bending of molecular water and hydroxyl-rich phases than to crystalline carbonate, indicating that any carbonate species are likely present in hydrated, disordered, or surface-associated forms rather than as well-ordered minerals. In this context, the spectral behavior is broadly consistent with the possible transient formation of a shelkovite-like Mg-carbonate hydroxide phase, which is known to exhibit strong water-related absorptions and attenuated, broadened carbonate bands due to its highly hydrated and structurally disordered nature.

FTIR spectra of lizardite-rich enhanced weathering samples showing carbonate-related vibrational regions (ν₂, ν₃, and ν₄) and overlapping silicate (Si–O) features around 900–1100 cm−1. The inset illustrates the principal vibrational modes of the carbonate ion (CO₃2⁻)

Additional support for low carbonate crystallinity comes from the weak ν₄ bending mode (~ 710–730 cm⁻1), which further suggests minimal development of solid carbonate phases. In ultramafic systems, such spectral suppression is commonly associated with early-stage carbonation, where carbonate formation is hindered by silicate dominance, adsorption effects, and overlap with Si–O vibrations (Russell 1974; Nakamoto 2009; Madejová, 2003). Contributions from adsorbed bicarbonate, surface-complexed carbonate species, and interlayer water may also contribute to band broadening and spectral ambiguity in carbonate-associated regions.

The persistence of the ~ 3680 cm⁻1 band confirms the presence of structurally bound Mg–OH groups, indicating incomplete transformation of lizardite. In parallel, broad O–H stretching between ~ 3400–3600 cm⁻1 and the strong ~ 1630–1650 cm⁻1 band reflect significant structural hydration, consistent with hydrous Mg-carbonate or carbonate–silicate transitional phases. Such features resemble those observed in poorly crystalline hydrated Mg-carbonates, including shelkovite-like phases observed in XRD patterns of EW2-R and EW4-R (Fig. 8), where hydrogen bonding and structural disorder shift and broaden diagnostic carbonate vibrations (Russell 1974; Nakamoto 2009; Madejová, 2003).

Overall, the spectral characteristics suggest that carbonation remains limited to early-stage, low-crystallinity Ca–Mg carbonate or surface-associated species, with strong overprinting by silicate matrix signals. This indicates kinetically constrained carbonation of lizardite under the experimental conditions, with no clear evidence for substantial formation of well-ordered carbonate minerals.

Filtrate chemistry, secondary mineral formation, and trace element partitioning

The filtrate geochemistry (Fig. 15) reveals strong controls of pH, atmospheric exchange, and CO₂ pressure on lizardite dissolution and secondary mineral formation during enhanced weathering. The initial pH values show a progressive increase from the acidic system (EW3) to the alkaline systems (EW2 and EW4), with the hyperalkaline treatment (EW1) exhibiting the highest starting pH due to the addition of lime (CaO). At the end of the experiments, all systems showed lower pH values relative to their starting conditions, indicating ongoing mineral buffering reactions, CO₂ uptake, and water–rock interaction except acidic system. The decline in pH was particularly evident in the hyperalkaline treatment, likely reflecting carbonation reactions that consumed hydroxyl ions during formation of bicarbonate and carbonate species (Matter and Kelemen 2009; Power et al. 2013b).

Initial and final pH values, net dissolved Mg, Ca, and Si concentrations, dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) in experimental filtrates, and corrected loss on ignition (LOI) of solid residues from lizardite-enhanced weathering experiments conducted under open (O) and closed (C) system conditions. Seawater (SW) is included as a reference composition. Net dissolved Mg, Ca, and Si concentrations were calculated by subtracting the corresponding seawater concentrations from the measured filtrate values, while corrected LOI values were determined by subtracting the indigenous LOI of the starting lizardite material from the measured LOI of the experimental residues

The acidic open system exhibited the highest dissolved Mg concentrations, indicating extensive proton-promoted dissolution of lizardite and associated Mg–silicate phases (Oelkers et al. 2008). Elevated Ca concentrations in the acidic filtrates similarly reflect dissolution of Ca-bearing phases or desorption of surface-bound Ca under low-pH conditions. Despite enhanced dissolution, the acidic residue showed comparatively low corrected LOI values, suggesting limited retention of secondary carbonate phases within the solid products (Fig. 15). In contrast, the hyperalkaline system displayed the highest corrected LOI but lower dissolved Mg and Ca concentrations in the filtrates, consistent with precipitation of carbonate- and hydroxide-bearing secondary phases. The elevated LOI is partly attributable to hydration of added lime to form portlandite [Ca(OH)₂] and subsequent carbonation reactions that promoted sequestration of dissolved Mg and Ca into carbonate minerals such as calcite and hydrated Mg-carbonates (Taylor 1997; Hewlett and Liska 2019). The alkaline open system exhibited intermediate behavior, reflecting a balance between silicate dissolution and secondary mineral precipitation.

Comparison of the open alkaline system (EW2) and the closed, CO₂-pressurized alkaline system (EW4) reveals distinct weathering and carbonation pathways. EW2 is characterized by higher dissolved Mg and Ca concentrations, greater corrected LOI, and higher calculated CO₂ uptake per 100 g of sample, whereas EW4 exhibits substantially higher DIC and dissolved Si concentrations. The elevated Mg and Ca concentrations in EW2 indicate more extensive release of divalent cations during lizardite dissolution and/or less efficient sequestration into secondary carbonate phases. The higher LOI value of EW2 further suggests the formation and retention of hydrated secondary minerals, particularly brucite [Mg(OH)₂], which commonly forms under alkaline conditions and contributes to mass loss during heating through dehydroxylation (Power et al. 2013b; Daval et al. 2011). In contrast, the elevated DIC in EW4 reflects enhanced dissolution of pressurized CO₂ and increased bicarbonate and carbonate ion concentrations in solution, resulting in greater carbonate saturation and promoting precipitation of Mg- and Ca-bearing carbonate phases (Stumm and Morgan 1996; Kelemen and Matter 2008). This process likely accounts for the lower dissolved Mg and Ca concentrations observed in EW4. The higher dissolved Si concentration in EW4 suggests that silicate dissolution continued under elevated CO₂ conditions, while Mg and Ca were preferentially removed from solution through carbonation reactions. The comparatively lower LOI and calculated CO₂ uptake of EW4 indicate that a significant fraction of the carbon remained stored as dissolved inorganic carbon rather than being fully incorporated into solid carbonate products at the time of filtration. Collectively, these results indicate that EW2 favored the accumulation of hydrated and carbonated solid phases, whereas EW4 promoted a CO₂-rich aqueous environment characterized by enhanced DIC accumulation, continued silicate dissolution, and more efficient partitioning of Mg and Ca into secondary carbonate minerals. Similar behavior has been reported in experimental and natural mineral carbonation systems, where elevated CO₂ fugacity increases DIC concentrations and carbonate saturation while modifying dissolution–precipitation kinetics in Mg-rich silicates (O’Connor et al. 2001; Kelemen and Matter 2008; Power et al. 2013b).

The normalized concentrations of B, Sr, Na, S, and K reveal distinct patterns of elemental mobility during lizardite-enhanced weathering relative to the seawater baseline. Following correction for seawater replenishment using a factor of 2.4 (1200/500), the filtrate compositions were normalized and directly compared with seawater values (Fig. 16). Sodium concentrations exhibited only minor enrichment relative to seawater and generally remained near baseline values, reflecting their conservative nature and minimal involvement in weathering-induced precipitation reactions. Similarly, sulfur and potassium exhibit only minor deviations from seawater concentrations, suggesting limited participation in weathering and carbonation reactions, although the slight sulfur enrichment observed in the acidic treatment (EW3-F) may reflect enhanced dissolution and mobilization under low-pH conditions. In contrast, boron is enriched in all experimental systems, with the greatest enrichment occurring in the hyperalkaline treatment (EW1-F). This enrichment is consistent with the high solubility and stability of borate species under alkaline conditions, which promotes boron mobility in solution (Spivack and Edmond, 1987). Although the open-system experiments were conducted at 60 °C under ambient conditions, where evaporation may have contributed to solute concentration, the near-conservative behavior of Na, K, and S after normalization indicates that evaporation alone cannot account for the pronounced boron enrichment. Instead, the elevated B concentrations likely reflect enhanced geochemical mobilization under high-pH conditions rather than simple evaporative concentration. Strontium exhibits the opposite behavior, showing depletion relative to seawater in all treatments except EW3-F, with the strongest depletion occurring in the CO₂-pressurized closed system (EW4-F). This trend is consistent with preferential removal of Sr from solution through incorporation into secondary carbonate minerals, where Sr commonly substitutes for Ca within carbonate crystal lattices (Morse and Bender 1990). Collectively, these elemental distributions identify boron as the most mobile and consistently enriched trace element, whereas strontium is systematically depleted through carbonate precipitation. The observed patterns demonstrate that pH and CO₂ availability exert primary controls on trace-element partitioning during lizardite weathering and carbon mineralization, while evaporation at elevated temperature likely played a secondary role in modifying solute concentrations in the open-system experiments. Similar relationships among alkalinity, trace-element mobility, carbonate formation, and evaporative concentration have been documented in both natural and engineered mineral carbonation systems (Power et al. 2013b; Renforth 2019; Beerling et al. 2020).

Conclusions

This study investigates CO₂ mineralization during serpentine–seawater interaction under controlled marine conditions, building on well-established evidence that carbonate phases such as aragonite and magnesite can form during alteration of ultramafic minerals, particularly under elevated CO₂ pressures. Rather than reiterating these known processes, the present work provides new insights through the integration of (i) lizardite-rich material derived from the Oman Ophiolite, (ii) the use of natural coastal seawater, (iii) alkalinity enhancement via lime produced from locally sourced marble waste, and (iv) systematic pH manipulation to simulate realistic marine environments.

The results demonstrate that carbonate precipitation is enhanced under acidic and ambient conditions, accompanied by an increase in pH; however, the pH values remain below neutral levels. In contrast, alkaline systems exposed to both ambient CO₂ conditions (open systems) and elevated CO₂ conditions (closed systems) exhibit a concurrent decrease in pH, indicating a potential risk of acidification. The incorporation of lime derived from marble waste effectively moderates the pH decline, despite the overall decreasing trend, thereby improving both the efficiency and environmental sustainability of the mineralization process. These findings underscore the importance of integrating CO₂ sequestration approaches with alkalinity management strategies to maintain the chemical stability of marine systems.

A significant outcome of this study is the identification of shelkovite as a secondary reaction product, a phase that has rarely been reported in comparable experimental systems. Its occurrence warrants further mechanistic and thermodynamic investigation to better constrain the reaction pathways and environmental conditions governing its formation.

Overall, this work advances the field of ocean-based enhanced weathering by demonstrating how region-specific materials, realistic seawater chemistry, and controlled pH conditions can be combined to optimize carbon sequestration while minimizing unintended environmental impacts. These findings contribute to the development of more practical, site-relevant, and sustainable approaches for marine carbon dioxide removal.

Recommendations

Although the present study demonstrates the enhanced weathering behavior and carbon sequestration potential of lizardite-rich serpentinite under controlled laboratory conditions, direct extrapolation to field-scale applications should be undertaken with caution. Natural systems, particularly marine and coastal environments, involve complex hydrodynamic and biogeochemical processes that may significantly influence mineral dissolution rates and carbonation efficiency. Factors such as dilution by ocean currents, variable temperature and pH conditions, biofilm and microbial development on mineral surfaces, sediment transport, and heterogeneous water–rock interactions may alter reaction kinetics relative to laboratory observations. In addition, long-term monitoring and verification of carbon sequestration efficiency in field applications remain challenging due to spatial and temporal variability in geochemical conditions. Therefore, while laboratory experiments provide important mechanistic insights into lizardite weathering behavior, future pilot-scale and field-based investigations are required to evaluate the scalability, environmental impacts, and long-term performance of serpentinite-enhanced weathering systems.
