Chlorella pyrenoidosa improves meat quality and flavor precursors of fattening lambs by regulating jejunal microbiota and muscle metabolism via the gut–muscle axis
Authors: Jiantao Zhao, Shu Li, Jinglei Wang, Weibing Zeng, Yayin Qi, Xiaolin Xu, Jianhua Fan, Cheng Chen, Wenju Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study elucidates the mechanism by which Chlorella pyrenoidosa enhances meat quality in finishing lambs via modulation of the jejunal microbiota and muscle metabolism. In a 60-day trial, 32 lambs were fed diets supplemented with 0%, 1%, 3%, or 5% C. pyrenoidosa. C. pyrenoidosa supplementation significantly improved meat color, elevated amino acid (AA) and polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) levels, and reduced saturated fatty acid content in the Longissimus dorsi muscle. 16S rRNA gene sequencing identified an enrichment of specific jejunal microbes, including Bifidobacterium and Mogibacterium. Integrated microbiota-metabolome analysis indicated that C. pyrenoidosa modulated the D-amino acid metabolism pathway. Transcriptome analysis revealed the upregulation of key genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation (carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1B, CPT1B) and amino acid synthesis (glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 1, GOT1). In conclusion, C. pyrenoidosa enhances lamb meat quality by remodeling the jejunal microbiota and concurrently promoting both fatty acid β-oxidation and AA synthesis in muscle.

Introduction

Global meat consumption is projected to rise with growing populations and demand (Zhang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, rising living standards have amplified consumer demand for high-quality lamb, with increasing emphasis on superior flavor profiles and health benefits (Fernandez-Lopez, 2023; Rodrigues et al., 2023). Although intensive farming systems have significantly enhanced the production efficiency of fattening sheep, they have also introduced issues such as aberrant intramuscular fat deposition, lighter meat color, reduced tenderness, and imbalances in flavor compounds (Watkins et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2022). Consequently, these problems severely restrict the economic benefits of lamb (Echegaray et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). Therefore, exploring functional feed additives to improve meat quality has become a research focus in the field of animal nutrition.

The composition of amino acids (AA) and fatty acids in muscle directly determines the taste, flavor, and nutritional value of lamb (Gkarane et al., 2019). In recent years, feed additives such as plant extracts and probiotics have been widely used to regulate the composition of AA and fatty acids in muscle, thereby improving meat quality (Dou et al., 2023a). However, active plant ingredients are often degraded by rumen microorganisms, which reduces their bioavailability (Lambo et al., 2024). Furthermore, rumen biohydrogenation limits the deposition of unsaturated fatty acids in muscle (Wood et al., 2008). While the rumen plays a critical upstream role in lipid metabolism, the small intestine (particularly the jejunum) serves as the primary site for the absorption of AA, small peptides, and fatty acids escaping rumen biohydrogenation. Moreover, the jejunum is a key interface for host-microbe interactions that directly influence post-absorptive metabolism (Chen et al., 2025; Yersin & Vonaesch, 2024). Most existing studies focus on single-tissue analyses and lack an integrative exploration of the associations between gut microbiota and muscle metabolism. The emerging concept of the ‘gut-muscle axis’, which describes the crosstalk between gut microbiota and host muscle metabolism, offers a novel perspective for nutritional research. While this concept has been explored in human health (Liu et al., 2021), the potential associations between gut microbiota and muscle metabolism, and their relevance to meat quality in ruminants, remain largely unexplored.

Chlorella pyrenoidosa, a unicellular microalga, is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), high-quality protein (containing over 50%), a balanced AA profile, and antioxidant pigments such as astaxanthin and β-carotene (Ji et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2026). C. pyrenoidosa exhibits considerable potential for improving the meat quality of ruminants. Previous studies have confirmed that Schizochytrium sp. can regulate the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes (e.g., FADS2, ELOVL2, SCD, CPT1α) in the muscle of Hu sheep, thereby promoting the deposition of n-3 PUFA (Fan et al., 2019). Additionally, some studies have found that algae can inhibit rumen biohydrogenation, and that alterations in the rumen fatty acid profile are associated with changes in the bacterial community structure (Boeckaert et al., 2008). However, whether C. pyrenoidosa can modulate the jejunal microbiota and subsequently influence muscle metabolism through the gut-muscle axis remains an open question. Therefore, it is feasible to intervene in the muscle quality of ruminants by supplementing with C. pyrenoidosa, an approach that requires further research. However, it remains unclear whether C. pyrenoidosa improves meat quality by simultaneously modulating the gut microbiota and host muscle metabolism, and how these two systems might be interconnected.

Thus, this study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the effects of dietary C. pyrenoidosa supplementation on the meat quality, jejunal microbiota, and muscle metabolism of fattening sheep. To achieve this, we employed an integrated multi-omics approach, combining 16S rRNA gene sequencing of the jejunal microbiota with metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses of the Longissimus dorsi muscle. We hypothesized that dietary supplementation with C. pyrenoidosa would reshape the jejunal microbiota, increase the intramuscular content of AA and polyunsaturated fatty acids, and consequently improve the quality and flavor profile of lamb.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and feeding

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the College of Animal Science and Technology, Shihezi University (Approval No.: A2025–910) and were performed in strict accordance with its guidelines. All experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with the European Union's welfare guidelines (Directive 2010/63/EU). The experiment was conducted at a commercial farm (Xincheng Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd., Aksu, China). A total of 32 healthy male Dubo × Hu crossbred lambs (age: 90 ± 3 days; initial body weight: 26.98 ± 1.76 kg) were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments (n = 8 per group) in a completely randomized design. The treatment groups received the same basal TMR supplemented with 0% (W0), 1% (W1), 3% (W3), and 5% (W5) C. pyrenoidosa powder. The C. pyrenoidosa strain used in this study was originally isolated from the Xinjiang desert region. Following isolation and purification, the algal biomass was cultivated in a closed photobioreactor system, harvested, and subsequently spray-dried to produce the powder. The trial consisted of a 10-day adaptation period followed by a 60-day formal feeding period. The daily feeding procedure involved first offering 20% of the basal diet, followed by the daily portion of C. pyrenoidosa pellets evenly dispersed into the trough. Once approximately 5% of the feed remained, the remaining 80% of the basal diet was provided. Animals had ad libitum access to feed and water. The basal diet was a TMR pellet formulated for fattening lambs (Product No. 416, Aksu Taikun Feed Co., Ltd., Aksu, China). The composition and nutrient levels of the basal diet are presented in Table S1.

At the end of the experiment, following a 12-h fast and 2-h water deprivation, six animals per group were randomly selected for slaughter. The animals were humanely slaughtered on-site at the experimental farm (Xincheng Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd., Aksu, China) by trained farm staff in accordance with standard commercial practices, which included captive bolt stunning followed by exsanguination. Within 30 min of slaughter, Longissimus dorsi muscle samples were collected from the region between the 11th and 13th thoracic ribs. Subsamples for RNA-seq and metabolomic analyses were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C, while samples designated for meat quality assessment were stored at 4 °C.

Meat quality measurements

Meat quality assessments commenced at 45 min after slaughter. The pH value of the Longissimus dorsi muscle was recorded with a portable pH meter (Testo 205, Shanghai, China). Color coordinates (L, a, b*) were evaluated at the identical site using a colorimeter (NR110, 3NH, Shenzhen, China). The concentration of free AA was quantified in compliance with the Chinese National Standard GB 5009.124–2016 (“Determination of Amino Acids in Foods”) using an L-8900 AA analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). For the analysis of long-chain fatty acid profiles, a protocol from Wang et al. (2023). Briefly, 0.75 g of freeze-dried muscle was portioned into a 50-mL centrifuge tube. The sample was treated with 8 mL of 0.5 mol/L sodium methoxide solution (Yuanye Bio-Technology, Shanghai, China), vortexed for 5 min, and then held at 50 °C for 15 min. Once cooled, 8 mL of a 2% sulfuric acid-methanol solution (Yuanye Bio-Technology, Shanghai, China) was introduced, followed by another 5-min vortex and a 1-h incubation at 50 °C. After the sample returned to ambient temperature, 4 mL of n-hexane (HPLC grade, Yuanye Bio-Technology, Shanghai, China) was added. The mixture was manually inverted for mixing, after which 4 mL of distilled water was introduced and the tube was vortexed for 5 min. Centrifugation at 3500×g for 5 min allowed for the collection of the upper n-hexane phase. This organic layer was passed through a new tube containing a mixture of 0.25 g activated carbon and 0.25 g sodium sulfate anhydrous (Aladdin, Shanghai, China). The tube was agitated until the solution cleared (approximately 20 s) and then stored overnight at 4 °C. After this storage, a second centrifugation at 3500×g for 5 min was performed. The resulting supernatant was moved to a 2-mL tube and centrifuged at 13,000×g for 10 min. The final clarified supernatant was then carefully transferred to a vial for subsequent gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis on an Agilent 8890-7000E system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Jejunal microbiota

Genomic DNA from jejunal content microbiota was isolated with a commercial kit (E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit, Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). The concentration and purity of the DNA were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). We then amplified the bacterial 16S rRNA gene's hypervariable V3-V4 region employing primers 338F (5‘-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5‘-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) (synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) with TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase and related buffers/dNTPs (Beijing TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., China) on a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) or ABI GeneAmp® 9700 system (Applied Biosystems, USA). The resulting PCR products were subjected to high-throughput sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 system (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), which was conducted by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. Downstream bioinformatic processing, which included estimating α-diversity, assigning taxonomic categories, and analyzing differential abundance, was executed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com).

Metabolomic analysis

Fully ground muscle tissue (100 mg) was homogenized with an extraction solution (acetonitrile:methanol = 1:1, v/v; acetonitrile and methanol, LC-MS grade, Fisher Chemical, USA) containing the internal standard L-2-chlorophenylalanine (0.02 mg/mL, CNW Technologies, Germany). After vortexing and low-temperature ultrasonication (5 °C, 40 kHz; SBL-10DT, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), the samples were incubated at −20 °C and then centrifuged to collect the supernatant (Centrifuge 5430R, Eppendorf, Germany). The supernatant was concentrated under a stream of nitrogen gas (JXDC-20 nitrogen blowdown instrument, Shanghai Jingxin Industrial Development Co., Ltd., China) and reconstituted for LC-MS analysis.

The analytical platform for metabolite separation comprised a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) interfaced with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), which features a hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap design. An HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was used to achieve chromatographic resolution. The composition of the two mobile phases was as follows: phase A contained a 95:5 (v/v) mixture of water and acetonitrile (Fisher Chemical, USA), acidified with 0.1% formic acid (Fisher Chemical, USA), while phase B was a blend of acetonitrile, isopropanol (Fisher Chemical, USA), and water in a 47.5:47.5:5 (v/v/v) ratio, also modified with 0.1% formic acid. During the separation process, a constant flow rate of 0.40 mL/min was sustained, and the column oven was set to 40 °C. Data acquisition by the mass spectrometer covered a range from m/z 70 to 1050, with both positive and negative ionization modes being utilized.

Initial processing of the raw data was conducted with Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, USA) to perform peak detection, alignment across samples, and data normalization. Subsequently, multivariate statistical techniques were applied, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). The selection of differentially abundant metabolites was based on an integrated approach that merged variable importance in projection (VIP) scores, derived from OPLS-DA models built in SIMCA-P+ (v16.0; Umetrics, Sweden), with results from univariate analysis (two-tailed Student's t-test, applying thresholds of VIP > 1 and P < 0.05). Putative identification of these metabolites was accomplished by searching against the KEGG database and the HMDB database. Furthermore, Fisher's exact test was employed to evaluate the enrichment of relevant metabolic pathways. The above data processing and analysis were performed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com).

Transcriptomic analysis

RNA was extracted from muscular tissue using the MJZol total RNA extraction kit (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., China). For tissue homogenization, a TL-48R grinding instrument (Shanghai Wanbai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was used. The extracted RNA's concentration and purity were quantified on a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The quality and integrity were further assessed with a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to determine the RNA Integrity Number (RIN). Subsequently, cDNA libraries were constructed with the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). These libraries were then subjected to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus system (Illumina Inc., USA) using the NovaSeq Reagent Kit (NovaSeq X Plus), yielding 150-base pair paired-end reads. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation was performed on selected key genes—solute carrier family 27 member 1 (SLC27A1), carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1B (CPT1B), glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 1 (GOT1), and hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase trifunctional multienzyme complex subunit alpha (HADHA)—to confirm the accuracy and reliability of the transcriptomic data. The primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Meat quality indices, including AA and fatty acid profiles, were collated and subjected to a one-way ANOVA in SPSS (v26.0). Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Significant differences between treatment groups (P < 0.05) were identified using Duncan's multiple range test, while Tukey's HSD test was applied for comparisons of jejunal microbiota α-diversity (Chao and Shannon index).

Transcriptomic analysis involved aligning raw sequencing reads to the Ovis aries genome (ARS-UI_Ramb_v2.0) using HISAT2. Gene expression was quantified as TPM with RSEM. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were filtered using thresholds of |log₂(fold change)| > 1 and an FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method). Functional enrichment analysis for GO terms and KEGG pathways was then performed on the DEGs using the clusterProfiler R package, with an FDR < 0.05 considered significant.

Integrative analysis of the jejunal microbiota and muscle metabolome was conducted on the MetOrigin 2.0 platform (http://metorigin.met-bioinformatics.cn/). The workflow encompassed: 1) origin analysis predicting the microbial, host, or dietary source of differential metabolites; 2) functional annotation of metabolite-related pathways; and 3) construction of microbe-metabolite co-metabolism networks to visualize metabolic flux and functional associations (Yu et al., 2024).

Results

Effect of C. pyrenoidosa on meat quality of the Longissimus dorsi muscle in fattening sheep

Dietary supplementation with C. pyrenoidosa significantly improved the meat color and AA composition of the Longissimus dorsi muscle in fattening sheep (Table 1). The L, a, and b* values increased with the dose of C. pyrenoidosa, peaking in the W5 group, where they were significantly higher than in the W0 group (P < 0.01). In contrast, pH value was not significantly affected (P > 0.05). The contents of umami- and sweet-related AA—including glutamic acid (Glu), aspartic acid (Asp), alanine (Ala), and glycine (Gly)—were highest in the W5 group, showing increases of 212.87%, 130.92%, 362.75%, and 334.62%, respectively, over the W0 group (P < 0.01). The contents of essential AA—namely, leucine (Leu), lysine (Lys), valine (Val), isoleucine (Ile), and threonine (Thr)—were significantly higher in the W5 group than in the W0 group (P < 0.01). The total amino acid (TAA), essential amino acid (EAA), and flavor amino acid (FAA) contents increased with the dose of C. pyrenoidosa, all peaking in the W5 group, where they were significantly Effect of C. pyrenoidosa on fatty acid composition and lipid-metabolizing enzyme activities

Dietary supplementation with C. pyrenoidosa significantly altered the fatty acid composition and the activities of key lipid-metabolizing enzymes in the Longissimus dorsi muscle of fattening sheep (Table 2; Fig. S1). The contents of saturated fatty acids (SFA)—namely, C16:0, C17:0, C18:0, and C22:0—exhibited a quadratic response to the increasing C. pyrenoidosa dose, with the lowest levels observed in the W1 group, where they were significantly lower than in the W0 group (P < 0.05). The contents of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) —trans-C18:1, cis-C18:1, and total MUFA—were lowest in the W1 group. The contents of C18:3 n-3, C20:3 n-3, C20:5 n-3, C18:2 n-6, and C18:3 n-6 were significantly higher in the W5 group than in the W0 group (P < 0.05). The contents of n-3 PUFA, n-6 PUFA, the n-6/n-3 ratio, and total PUFA were highest in the W5 group, with significant increases of 11.89%, 37.81%, 22.51%, and 34.82%, respectively, compared with the W0 Changes in jejunal microbiota and their associations with muscle AA and fatty acid composition

Jejunal microbial community

Analysis of the jejunal microbial community via 16S rRNA gene sequencing demonstrated that dietary supplementation with C. pyrenoidosa significantly altered its overall structure (PCoA; R = 0.184, P = 0.006; Fig. 1A). At the phylum level, the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota (51.71%–61.90%) decreased with increasing supplementation levels, whereas that of Firmicutes (37.41%–46.65%) increased (Fig. 1B). At the genus level, the jejunal microbiota was predominantly composed of Olsenella, Aeriscardovia, Acetitomaculum, Pseudoscardovia, Coprococcus, and the Ruminococcus_gauvreauii_group (Fig. 1C). The non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test identified significant differences (P < 0.05) in the relative abundance of several genera among the groups, including Bifidobacterium, the Family_XIII_AD3011_group, Mogibacterium, Candidatus Saccharimonas, Lactobacillus, norank_f_Oscillospiraceae, the Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, and Succinivibrionaceae_UCG-001 (Fig. 1D). Functional correlations between key microorganisms and muscle nutritional traits

Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant associations (P < 0.05) between specific jejunal bacterial taxa and the AA and fatty acid profiles in the Longissimus dorsi muscle. For AA, the Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group, Howardella, and norank_o_Coriobacteriales were positively correlated with most flavor-enhancing AA (Glu, Asp, Ala, and Gly) and essential AA (Leu, Lys, Val, and Ile). Notably, Howardella was also negatively correlated with Met and Tyr (Fig. 1G), a finding that aligns with the observed reduction in these AA in the W5 group (see Table 1). For fatty acids, norank_o_Coriobacteriales was positively correlated with C18:0, total PUFA, n-6 PUFA, and the n-6/n-3 ratio. In contrast, the Eubacterium_nodatum_group was negatively correlated with SFAs (C16:0, C18:0) and n-3 PUFA (C18:3 n-3 and C20:3 n-3) (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, Bifidobacterium and the Family_XIII_AD3011_group showed significant positive correlations with the PUFA/SFA ratio, consistent with the elevated PUFA levels measured in the treatment groups.

Integrated metabolome–microbiome analysis revealing the gut–muscle axis mechanism

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the gut–muscle axis regulation of muscle metabolism by C. pyrenoidosa, we integrated untargeted metabolomic and 16S rRNA gene sequencing data using the MetOrigin2.0 platform.

Metabolite origin analysis classified the metabolites into host-associated (n = 62), microbiota-associated (n = 105), and food source-associated (n = 139) categories. Venn enrichment analysis revealed that 62 metabolites were enriched in bacteria-host cometabolites, while 43 metabolites were categorized as bacterial metabolites (Fig. 3A, B). Pathway analysis revealed that 6, 8, and 35 relevant metabolic pathways were associated with the host, bacterial, and co-metabolism databases, respectively (Fig. 3C). Notably, multiple AA metabolic pathways were enriched in both the bacterial and co-metabolism categories. These included “D-amino acid metabolism”, “Arginine and proline metabolism”, “Phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis”, “Lysine biosynthesis”, “Lysine degradation”, and “Cysteine and methionine metabolism”. Among these, the first three pathways exhibited the highest enrichment significance (log0.05(p) > 1; Fig. 3D). Sankey network analysis illustrated that C. pyrenoidosa significantly influenced two reactions (R01583 and R02825) within bacterial metabolic pathways, thereby modulating “D-amino acid metabolism” in the Longissimus dorsi muscle (Fig. 3E, F). For reaction R01583, C. pyrenoidosa supplementation likely promoted pyroglutamic acid metabolism by enriching Howardella and Lachnospiraceae_UCG-002, potentially activating D-glutamate cyclase [EC 4.2.1.48]. In reaction R02825, Mogibacterium was associated with the upregulation of D-proline reductase, 2,4-diaminopentanoate dehydrogenase, and 2,4-diaminopentanoate dehydrogenase (NAD+), thereby facilitating the conversion of 5-aminopentanoic acid. Within the co-metabolic pathways, C. pyrenoidosa significantly modulated four key reactions (R00260, R00451, R00460, and R00579; Figs. 3G-J). In reaction R00260, C. pyrenoidosa, by modulating 32 bacterial genera, was associated with the activation of glutamate racemase, thereby enhancing L-glutamic acid metabolism. In reactions R00451, R00460, and R00579, the activation of diaminopimelate decarboxylase, aspartate kinase, and amino-acid racemase promoted l-lysine and l-glutamine metabolism.

The overall network results support a model in which C. pyrenoidosa regulates both bacterial and co-metabolic pathways. Specifically, within bacterial pathways, C. pyrenoidosa markedly increased the relative abundance of Dialister, Solobacterium, Corynebacterium, Streptococcus, Bifidobacterium, Actinomyces, and Mogibacterium, while decreasing Megasphaera. These shifts promoted the metabolism of L-homoserine, pyroglutamic acid, and 5-aminopentanoic acid, thereby influencing “D-amino acid metabolism”, “Lysine degradation”, and “Cysteine and methionine metabolism” (Fig. 3K). Within co-metabolic pathways, C. pyrenoidosa significantly increased the abundance of Bifidobacterium, Pyramidobacter, Mogibacterium, Solobacterium, Dialister, Streptococcus, Candidatus Saccharimonas, Actinomyces, and Corynebacterium while reducing Megasphaera. This concerted microbial remodeling collectively enhanced the metabolism of 5-methylthioadenosine, L-methionine, l-glutamine, L-glutamic acid, aminoadipic acid, and l-lysine, which in turn influenced “D-amino acid metabolism”, “Lysine degradation” and “Cysteine and methionine metabolism” (Fig. 3K).

Differential expression analysis of genes related to fatty acid breakdown and AA synthesis metabolic pathways

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the observed metabolic phenotypes—specifically, decreased MUFA and increased AA contents—we investigated the expression of genes related to fatty acid activation and AA synthesis. The expression of key genes involved in fatty acid activation—namely, acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 1 (ACSL1) and SLC27A1—was significantly upregulated in the W3 and W5 groups compared with the W0 group. Furthermore, the expression of CPT1B, which encodes a key rate-limiting enzyme for mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, and its transporter, solute carrier family 25 member 20 (SLC25A20), was also upregulated. Consistent with this enhanced fatty acid activation and transport, the expression of genes encoding core enzymes of the mitochondrial β-oxidation spiral—including acyl-CoA dehydrogenase long chain (ACADL), enoyl-CoA hydratase short chain 1 (ECHS1), and hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADH)—was also upregulated (Fig. 5A). The upregulation of these genes indicates enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation. Notably, acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA produced during β-oxidation enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, thereby providing energy and carbon skeletons for AA synthesis. Furthermore, compared with the W0 group, the expression of genes involved in AA synthesis was upregulated in the W3/W5 groups: specifically, aldehyde dehydrogenase 18 family member A1 (ALDH18A1), pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase 2 (PYCR2), and glutamate-ammonia ligase (GLUL) for Glu, Pro, and Arg; COT1, COT2, cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS), and 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-homocysteine methyltransferase (MTR) for Asp, Thr, Met, and Lys; and ALS2 C-terminal like (ALS2CL), alsin Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor ALS2 (ALS2), and methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase subunit 2 (MCCC2) for Val, Ile, and Leu. The upregulation of these genes is consistent with enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation and AA synthesis, suggesting a potential metabolic shift where β-oxidation may support the biosynthetic process. Discussion

Through integrated multi-omics analysis, this study systematically demonstrates that C. pyrenoidosa supplementation improves lamb meat quality by modulating the jejunal microbial community structure, optimizing the composition of muscle AA and fatty acids, and regulating the expression of genes governing fatty acid β-oxidation and AA synthesis. The supplementation doses (0%, 1%, 3%, and 5%) were selected based on previous studies showing beneficial effects of microalgae at 1% inclusion in ruminant diets (Kholif et al., 2020), combined with the need for a gradient design to establish a dose-response relationship and identify the optimal level, while considering the practical safety threshold of 5% for feed additives in animal production. Our results confirmed a clear dose-dependent enhancement, with 5% supplementation showing the most pronounced improvements, consistent with other microalgae studies (Carvalho et al., 2018). These findings collectively establish a theoretical foundation for applying microalgae, particularly C. pyrenoidosa, as functional feed additives to enhance meat quality in ruminant production (Buono et al., 2014).

Meat quality is largely determined by tenderness and flavor, attributes governed by the muscle's composition of AAs and fatty acids (Liang et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024). The AAs profile directly influences flavor through the generation of flavor compounds and the contribution of specific tastes; elevated AAs levels are known to enhance meat taste and color (Lu et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2020). Our results demonstrate that C. pyrenoidosa significantly increased meat color parameters (L, a, b*) and elevated the contents of key FAA and EAA. Concurrently, the fatty acids profile impacts both flavor and health value and is amenable to dietary modification in ruminants (Luciano et al., 2013; Smith et al., 2011). We found that C. pyrenoidosa effectively modified the muscle fatty acids profile by increasing the levels of n-3 and n-6 PUFA while reducing saturated fatty acids, a finding consistent with reports for other microalgae like Aurantiochytrium limacinum in beef (Carvalho et al., 2018). Notably, the elevated serum levels of GLU, TP, and GLB indicate that C. pyrenoidosa promoted nutrient absorption and overall metabolic status in fattening sheep.

Notably, serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was significantly elevated in the W5 group. While AST elevation can indicate hepatic stress, it is not a liver-specific enzyme and is also abundant in muscle, heart, and kidney tissues. Importantly, studies have shown that AST activity in lambs is closely associated with muscle development and physical activity rather than solely reflecting liver function (de Souza et al., 2020). Furthermore, dietary interventions that enhance protein deposition in lambs have been shown to concurrently increase serum AST and total protein levels, suggesting that elevated AST may reflect enhanced AA metabolism and protein turnover (Dou et al., 2023b). This finding aligns with our transcriptomic data showing upregulated GOT expression in muscle. The concurrent increases in serum TP and GLB further support enhanced protein metabolism and immune function. Thus, the increased serum AST may reflect systemic enhancement of AA metabolism and mitochondrial activity rather than hepatocellular injury (Fink et al., 2025). All animals remained clinically healthy throughout the trial with no signs of morbidity, suggesting the 5% supplementation level was well-tolerated. Nevertheless, this observation warrants further investigation in future long-term studies.

16S rRNA sequencing revealed that C. pyrenoidosa induced a structural shift in the jejunal microbial community, marked by an increased relative abundance of Firmicutes and a decreased abundance of Actinobacteriota. Such a community shift is often linked to an enhanced capacity for dietary carbohydrate degradation (Mora-Flores et al., 2023; Turnbaugh et al., 2006). Specific genera, including Bifidobacterium and Mogibacterium, were significantly enriched in the jejunum. Correlation analysis further identified positive associations between these bacteria and muscle AAs and fatty acids (Han et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Xiong et al., 2025). Furthermore, C. pyrenoidosa reduced the relative abundance of genera such as Megasphaera in the jejunum (Elmhadi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). Untargeted metabolomics of muscle tissue revealed that C. pyrenoidosa elevated 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA) and reduced methionine levels, suggesting promoted methionine metabolism (Bauchart-Thevret et al., 2009; Mavrakis et al., 2016; North et al., 2016). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the identified differential metabolites were primarily enriched in fatty acid biosynthesis, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and D-amino acid metabolism, aligning with previous reports (Lamminen et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2026).

To delineate host-microbiota metabolic interactions, we utilized MetOrigin2.0, a comprehensive web server designed to help users discriminate microbial metabolites and explore related bacteria contributing to specific metabolic processes (Yu et al., 2024). Using the MetOrigin2.0 platform, we performed an integrated analysis of jejunal microbiota and muscle metabolites which revealed that Howardella was positively correlated with pyroglutamic acid yet negatively correlated with methionine and tyrosine in muscle, suggesting a potential link to enhanced muscle antioxidant capacity, a function that has been associated with pyroglutamic acid metabolism (Liu & Kim, 2023). Furthermore, Mogibacterium was positively correlated with 5-aminopentanoic acid, suggesting its involvement in regulating AA synthesis in muscle, which aligns with the improved AA profile observed in the Longissimus dorsi. Pathway analysis showed that D-amino acid metabolism, lysine degradation, and cysteine and methionine metabolism pathways were enriched in both microbiota and co-metabolism networks. These results collectively support the notion that C. pyrenoidosa improves muscle AA composition via modulation of the gut microbiota (Nicholson et al., 2012).

To elucidate the intramuscular metabolic mechanism linking the reduction in saturated fatty acids to the increase in AAs, we performed transcriptome analysis of muscle tissue. The results showed that C. pyrenoidosa upregulated the expression of ACSL1, SLC27A1, and CPT1B, indicating enhanced fatty acid activation and mitochondrial transport (Li et al., 2023). Subsequently, the upregulated expression of ACADL, ECHS1, HADH, and ACAA2 suggested that C. pyrenoidosa promotes the mitochondrial β-oxidation spiral. The resulting acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, and ATP could thereby provide energy and carbon substrates for the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and biosynthetic pathways. Furthermore, we observed enhanced expression of genes driving key AA biosynthetic pathways: GOT, CBS, and MTR, facilitating the conversion of oxaloacetate to Asp, Thr, Met, and Lys; ALDH18A1, PYCR2, and GLUL, facilitating the conversion of α-ketoglutarate to Glu, Pro, and Arg; ALS2CL, ALS2, and MCCC, facilitating the conversion of pyruvate to Val, Ile, and Leu. Collectively, these findings support a model wherein the coordinated upregulation of genes for fatty acid β-oxidation and AA synthesis enables the former to provide energy and carbon skeletons for the latter. Although multi-omics evidence strongly supports this hypothesis, the direct causal relationship from fatty acid catabolism to AA synthetic metabolism remains speculative. Future studies will employ metabolic flux techniques (e.g., stable isotope tracing), which are crucial for quantitatively tracking the flux of carbon atoms from fatty acids into AA pools and explicitly verifying this hypothesis. (Park et al., 2025).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that C. pyrenoidosa improves lamb meat quality by altering the jejunal microbiota, increasing the content of FAA and EAA in muscle, and modifying the muscle fatty acid profile via a reduction in saturated fatty acids and an increase in polyunsaturated fatty acids. Integrated analysis of the jejunal microbiota and muscle metabolome revealed enhanced AA metabolism pathways, while transcriptome data suggested that C. pyrenoidosa promotes AA synthesis, potentially supported by enhanced fatty acid catabolism in muscle. This study not only enhances our understanding of gut-muscle axis interactions but also provides a theoretical foundation for the application of microalgae as functional feed additives.
