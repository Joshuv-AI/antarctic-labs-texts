Gene-in-gene coding generates dual-isoform Fha condensates to control type VI secretion system assembly
Authors: Tong-Tong Pei, Qiao-Yu Chen, Xing-Yu Wang, Amy Ma, Jia-Xin Liang, Zi-Yan Ye, Yu-Zhao Liu, Jing-Tong Su, Xiaoye Liang, Ying An, Jun Zhu, Tao Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While eukaryotes employ alternative splicing to diversify protein functions, analogous strategies in bacteria remain underexplored. Here we identify a conserved intragenic coding mechanism in Vibrio cholerae that generates two isoforms of the essential scaffold Fha and show that these isoforms cooperate through liquid–liquid phase separation to promote the assembly of the type VI secretion system (T6SS). The full-length isoform, FhaL, seeds assembly by engaging the membrane complex, whereas an internally translated isoform, FhaS, enhances secretion efficiency by strengthening specific interactions with baseplate components. This isoform partitioning is ecologically critical; a mutant producing only FhaL is impaired in bacterial competition, susceptible to eukaryotic predation, and defective in host colonization. Both isoforms form condensates, and a single residue change within a C-terminal helix abolishes condensate formation and significantly reduces T6SS activities. The internal translation and condensate-forming residues are strictly conserved across >10,000 V. cholerae isolates and active in diverse Vibrio species. These findings define a translational–biophysical mechanism that tunes a widespread contractile protein nanomachine for ecological success.

Introduction

One of biology’s most striking themes for expanding functional complexity is to extract functions from existing genetic material rather than simply increasing genome size. In eukaryotes, alternative splicing generates protein isoforms from single genes, while liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) enables proteins and RNAs to self-organise into dynamic condensates that spatially and temporally coordinate key cellular processes1–3. In bacteria, internal translation and overlapping coding sequences can likewise generate multiple protein products from single loci, but their molecular mechanisms are poorly defined and seldom explored in the context of biophysical processes like LLPS4–7. Whether bacteria have evolved strategies that simultaneously exploit compact coding architectures and condensate-based regulation remains unknown.

As the causative agent of cholera, Vibrio cholerae has fueled seven pandemics and continues to threaten global health, while thriving in aquatic natural reservoirs8–10. Its success depends on a suite of virulence and adaptation mechanisms, among which the type VI secretion system (T6SS) is central11,12. This contractile nanomachine assembles into a cell-spanning and membrane-anchored spear-like structure and injects toxic effectors into competitors and host cells, providing both ecological advantage and pathogenic potential13,14. Despite its importance, the molecular events that initiate and regulate T6SS assembly remain poorly understood15,16. In Acidovorax citrulli, the scaffold protein Fha and its cognate kinase PpkA undergo LLPS and coordinate the initiation of an “inside-out” mode of T6SS assembly17,18. However, a direct causal link between LLPS and assembly has yet to be established. Moreover, V. cholerae lacks a homologous kinase, raising the question of how Fha functions in systems that lack upstream regulatory enzymes and whether condensates play a mechanistic role.

However, dissecting the causal role of LLPS in bacterial systems remains challenging. Even point mutations that disrupt condensates inevitably alter the native protein sequence and may introduce secondary effects on folding, stability, or partner recognition. Recent advances in de novo protein design offer a powerful approach to overcome these limitations. AI-driven platforms such as RFdiffusion and ProteinMPNN can generate protein binders that engage specific structural motifs for modulating LLPS behaviours19–22. These tools provide an orthogonal way to perturb bacterial condensates and their associated assembly of complex protein machineries.

Here we show that V. cholerae employs in-frame tandem translation within fha to produce two isoforms, a full-length FhaL and a shorter FhaS. These isoforms partition function, with FhaL required for initiation of T6SS assembly and FhaS enhancing efficiency, microbial competition, and host colonisation. Both isoforms undergo LLPS, and a single amino acid substitution (L341A) within a C-terminal helix abolishes condensate formation and sharply reduces T6SS activity. We designed de novo protein binders using RFdiffusion–ProteinMPNN to selectively target this helix, which effectively disrupted condensates and impaired T6SS assembly. Comparative analyses of more than 10,000 V. cholerae fha sequences reveal strict conservation of the internal start site and its upstream ribosomal binding sequence, indicating strong selective pressure for this strategy across V. cholerae. Similar tandem translation was validated not only in a panel of V. cholerae strains but also across other Vibrio species. By integrating gene-in-gene translation with phase separation, V. cholerae has evolved a unique means to control T6SS activities, a mechanism not observed in prior regulatory modules but key for promoting survival, spread, and virulence of V. cholerae. These findings reveal a translational–biophysical mechanism for controlling a conserved bacterial contractile machine and suggest a broader evolutionary principle that parallels with eukaryotic systems.

Results

Intragenic coding of fha produces two isoforms, FhaL and FhaS

Fha is essential for T6SS activity in V. cholerae, but its mechanism remains poorly understood23. During purification of C-terminally His-tagged Fha expressed in E. coli, we consistently observed two distinct products: a full-length protein and a shorter variant (Fig. 1a). N-terminal Edman sequencing of the smaller product identified methionine 262 as the initiation site, indicating internal translation from an in-frame start codon. To confirm this, we generated an fhaM262A mutant, which produced only the full-length isoform (Fig. 1a). In parallel, N-terminal FLAG and C-terminal His tagging showed that FLAG immunoblotting detected only the full-length product, whereas His immunoblotting detected both; both tags detected only the full-length protein in the M262A mutant (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Mutation of the predicted ribosome-binding site upstream of M262 (fhaE258A) likewise abolished the shorter isoform, further supporting its translational origin (Supplementary Fig. 1b). We next validated isoform expression in V. cholerae T6SS-model strain V52. Plasmid-borne 3V5-tagged fha expressed in a Δfha mutant of strain V52 produced both isoforms, whereas the M262A and E258A variants produced only FhaL (Fig. 1c). To test expression under native conditions, we constructed fha_sfGFP fusions at the chromosomal locus. Chromosomal point mutants confirmed isoform identity: the fhaL allele (M262A) expressed only the full-length GFP-tagged isoform, whereas the fhaS allele (M1A) expressed only the shorter isoform (Fig. 1d).

Finally, we asked whether isoform production occurs in other V. cholerae strains. Using polyclonal antisera against Fha, we detected both long and short isoforms in the seventh-pandemic El Tor O1 strain C6706 expressing a plasmid-borne T6SS-activating regulator QstR24. As expected, M262A and M1A variants in this background expressed only the full-length or the shorter isoform, respectively (Fig. 1e).

Together, these results demonstrate that V. cholerae fha generates two isoforms by intragenic translation, revealing an in-frame gene-in-gene coding strategy in V. cholerae.

Long and short isoforms partition initiation and enhancement of T6SS assembly

To define the role of Fha and its isoforms in T6SS biogenesis, we visualised chromosomally tagged Fha_sfGFP together with mCherry2-labelled sheath protein TssB. Fluorescence microscopy revealed discrete cytoplasmic foci that consistently formed at the initiating site of sheath polymerization (Fig. 2a). Fha foci formation was unaltered in ΔtssM, ΔtssA, and ΔtssE mutants defective in T6SS assembly (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Additionally, assemblies of the membrane complex protein TssL_sfGFP and the baseplate protein sfGFP_TssG were undetectable in the absence of Fha (Fig. 2b, c). To dissect isoform contributions, we constructed chromosomal mutants expressing only FhaL, FhaS, or FhaN (the N-terminus, residues 1–261), each fused to sfGFP in the background of TssB_mCherry2. FhaL and FhaS each formed fluorescent foci, whereas FhaN was diffuse (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 1d). Quantification showed that the FhaL-only strain produced initiation foci that colocalized with sheaths but at substantially reduced numbers relative to wild type, whereas the FhaS-only strain failed to initiate sheath assembly (Fig. 2d, e). Notably, the FhaN-only strain produced a slightly reduced number of sheath assemblies to the FhaL-only strain (Fig. 2d, e).

We next examined functional effects. The fhaS mutant (lacking FhaL) exhibited a complete loss of Hcp secretion and killing against E. coli, while the fhaL and fhaN mutants retained significantly reduced but detectable activity (Fig. 2f, g, Supplementary Fig. 1e). To provide further support, we performed complementation analysis. Wild-type Fha restored Hcp secretion and antibacterial activity in the Δfha mutant, FhaL provided partial rescue, and FhaN yielded low yet detectable recovery (Fig. 2h, i, Supplementary Fig. 1f). In contrast, FhaS alone failed to restore T6SS activity (Fig. 2h, i, Supplementary Fig. 1f).

To test whether increased levels of FhaL could compensate for reduced T6SS activity in the absence of FhaS, we expressed plasmid-borne FhaL in the chromosomal fhaL background to generate an additive FhaL condition. Immunoblotting with an anti-Fha antibody showed that plasmid-borne FhaL was present at levels comparable to chromosomally expressed FhaL, resulting in an approximate twofold increase in total FhaL abundance relative to the empty vector control (Supplementary Fig. 1g). Notably, overexpression of FhaL did not enhance Hcp secretion, whereas expression of wild-type Fha significantly increased secretion (Supplementary Fig. 1g). These results indicate that FhaL alone is insufficient to drive efficient T6SS activity.

Together, these data demonstrate that FhaL (containing the N-terminal domain) is sufficient to support a basal level of assembly while FhaS enhances secretion and antibacterial activity. The presence of both isoforms is necessary for a fully competent T6SS.

The C-terminal domain drives phase separation and isoform cooperation

The punctate localization of Fha suggested a condensate-based assembly. Using a lacO-repeat array25, we estimated that individual Fha foci contain between 80–2500 Fha molecules in V. cholerae cells, consistent with biomolecular clustering (Supplementary Fig. 1h, i). Purified FhaL and FhaS each formed oil-like droplets in vitro in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas FhaN did not (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 2a). Pull-down and bacterial two-hybrid assays showed direct interactions between FhaL and FhaS but not between FhaN and either isoform (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 2b). In vivo, plasmid-borne expression of wild-type Fha enhanced Hcp secretion in an fhaN mutant background, whereas expression of FhaS alone did not (Supplementary Fig. 2c). L341 is a key residue required for Fha condensate formation

To mechanistically determine the role of phase separation in T6SS control, we searched for LLPS-defective point mutations within the C-terminal region. Structural modeling revealed a leucine-rich helix spanning residues from M320 to E350 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Individual leucine to alanine substitutions identified L341A and L342A as strong LLPS-disrupting mutations, as plasmid-expressed FhaL341A_sfGFP or FhaL342A_sfGFP displayed diffuse cytoplasmic distribution in the V52 Δfha strain (Supplementary Fig. 3b). We further introduced the chromosomal fhaL341A mutation into the fha_sfGFP tssB_mCherry2 background, which did not affect the expression of FhaL and FhaS (Supplementary Fig. 3c). This mutation abolished Fha foci, reduced the number of TssL and TssG foci, and markedly impaired sheath assembly (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 3d, e).

Functionally, the fhaL341A mutant exhibited severe defects in Hcp secretion and inter-bacterial competition, both of which were fully rescued by plasmid-borne wild-type Fha (Fig. 3d, e; Supplementary Fig. 3f). Moreover, inducible expression of FhaL341A_sfGFP in the V52 fha_sfGFP strain caused pre-existing Fha_sfGFP foci to gradually dissolve, whereas expression of sfGFP or wild-type Fha_sfGFP had no such effect (Supplementary Fig. 3g), indicating that L341A acts as a dominant negative disruptor of condensate integrity.

To further assess the functional contribution of LLPS to individual Fha isoforms, we designed two complementary experimental conditions by examining FhaL and its LLPS-defective variant, as well as FhaS and its LLPS-defective form in the presence of FhaL, given that FhaS alone is insufficient for T6SS activity. Inducible expression of wild-type FhaL partially restored Hcp secretion in the Δfha mutant, whereas the LLPS-defective mutant FhaL L341A showed reduced activity despite comparable protein levels, indicating that LLPS contributes to FhaL-mediated T6SS function (Supplementary Fig. 3h).

We next tested FhaS in the fhaL background. Expression of wild-type FhaS, as well as wild-type fha (co-expressing both isoforms), restored Hcp secretion to similar levels, whereas the LLPS-defective mutant FhaS L341A failed to enhance secretion (Supplementary Fig. 3i).

Together, these results indicate L341 as a critical residue for Fha condensate formation and demonstrate that LLPS contributes to the functional activity of both Fha isoforms in promoting T6SS assembly.

Design of Fha-leucine-rich-helix-targeting binders using fold-conditioned RFdiffusion

Although the L341A substitution effectively disrupted Fha condensate formation, this mutation alters the primary sequence of Fha and could introduce unintended structural or regulatory effects beyond perturbation of the helix interface. To independently test whether the leucine-rich helix is intrinsically required for condensation, we developed an orthogonal strategy to selectively target this helix without modifying the helix sequence, by using a fold-conditioned RFdiffusion-ProteinMPNN work-flow to generate de novo protein binders (Fig. 3f)20. Guided by our mutational analysis identifying L341, L342 and L334 as critical for condensate formation, these three hydrophobic residues were designated as hotspot positions to promote the formation of a shape-complementary binding interface. Our analysis generated 12,000 candidate binder sequences (Supplementary data 1), which were subsequently evaluated using AlphaFold2 initial-guess models and ranked based on interface confidence metrics, including high structural confidence and low predicted alignment error at the interface. Ten designs that consistently met these criteria were selected for experimental characterization in V. cholerae V52 (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary data 2).

Designed binders impair the formation of Fha condensates and T6SS assembly in vivo

Each binder was expressed from an arabinose-inducible plasmid in V52 fha_sfGFP tssB_mCherry2 strains, allowing simultaneous imaging of Fha condensates and sheath assembly. All ten binders reduced the number of Fha foci to varying degrees, and the magnitude of condensate disruption correlated with a proportional reduction in sheath assembly frequency (Fig. 3g, h, Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). Among the designs, Binder7 exhibited the strongest effect, resulting in predominantly diffuse Fha signals and a significantly reduced number of sheath structures, indicating impaired T6SS activities (Fig. 3g, h, Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). This is further confirmed by Hcp secretion that was reduced by Binder7 expression (Fig. 3i). Notably, expression of Binder7 in the fhaL341A mutant, which is already defective in condensate formation, did not further decrease Hcp secretion (Fig. 3i). Consistent with these observations, Binder7, but not Binder4 or Binder5, impaired Fha foci formation in E. coli, resulting in diffuse cytoplasmic localization of Fha_sfGFP (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Western blotting analysis confirmed that Fha_sfGFP protein levels were comparable across strains expressing different Binders (Supplementary Fig. 5e), indicating that the observed differences in localization reflect condensate disruption rather than altered protein abundance. These data demonstrate that targeted perturbation of the helix interface is sufficient to disrupt Fha condensates and impair T6SS assembly.

Isoforms exhibit distinct recruitment for membrane and baseplate components

The observation that chromosomally tagged Fha localised to the base of extending sheaths suggests its association with the T6SS apparatus. Mass spectrometry of Strep-tagged Fha complexes identified multiple interacting components, including the inner membrane proteins TssL and TssM, baseplate proteins TssA, TssF, and TssK, the secreted effectors VasX and TseL, and the ATPase ClpV (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Domain mapping revealed that both FhaL and FhaN interacted robustly with TssL, whereas FhaS showed a weaker but still detectable interaction (Fig. 3j). All three variants interacted with TssE but FhaN showed much weaker interaction, suggesting that the C-terminal region of Fha is important for TssE binding (Fig. 3j). Bacterial two-hybrid assays confirmed that FhaL and FhaN interact with the cytoplasmic domain of TssL, whereas FhaS did not (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This difference may reflect that the two-hybrid assay used only the cytoplasmic domain of TssL. Additionally, pull-down assays showed that FhaL341A interacts with TssL and TssE at levels comparable to wild-type Fha (Supplementary Fig. 6c), indicating that the L341A substitution does not disrupt these key T6SS protein–protein interactions even though it abolishes phase separation.

To test the functional significance of these differences, we reconstituted droplet recruitment in vitro. GFP-tagged TssLcyto (the cytoplasmic domain of TssL) partitioned efficiently into FhaL droplets but less into FhaS droplets (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). Control MBP_sfGFP was excluded from FhaL droplets but weakly enriched in FhaS droplets, indicating distinct selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e).

Together, these results demonstrate that the C-terminal domain of Fha mediates phase separation, that this property is crucial for T6SS function, and that isoforms partition their interactions with T6SS components. FhaL primarily engages membrane partners such as TssL, likely through the N-terminal domain, whereas both FhaL and FhaS interact with the baseplate proteins such as TssE, providing a molecular basis for their functional cooperation.

Dual-isoform cooperation enhances survival and colonisation in competitive environments

We next evaluated the ecological significance of isoform cooperation. In inter-bacterial competition, the fhaL mutant (expressing only FhaL) displayed significantly reduced survival against environmental V. cholerae, V. parahaemolyticus, and Aeromonas dhakensis (Fig. 4a–c, Supplementary Fig. 7a–c), reflecting compromised T6SS activity and increased susceptibility to competition. In interactions with eukaryotic predators, the fhaL mutant was more susceptible to amoeba (Fig. 4d). In a murine colonisation model, it exhibited markedly reduced intestinal loads compared with wild type (Fig. 4e), consistent with previous reports that T6SS activity is important for intestinal colonisation of V. cholerae by antagonizing commensal microbiota26. Gene-in-gene coding is evolutionarily conserved across Vibrio species

Given the broad ecological benefits of isoform cooperation, we next examined whether this system is preserved by evolutionary selection. Comparative analysis of more than 10,000 V. cholerae fha sequences using per-site Shannon entropy revealed absolute conservation of the internal initiation codon (M262) and its ribosome-binding site and the key residue for LLPS (L341), in contrast to normal levels of variation observed across the rest of the fha gene (Fig. 5a). The adjacent residues L342 and L334, which also contribute to condensate formation, are fully conserved at the amino acid level. Isoform production was experimentally confirmed in a panel of clinical and environmental V. cholerae isolates (Fig. 5b). Extending this analysis across the genus, heterologously expressed fha homologs from V. fluvialis, V. vulnificus, and V. anguillarum each produced full-length and internally initiated isoforms, which were eliminated by mutation of the internal start codon (Fig. 5c). Additionally, to validate the functions of these isoforms, we constructed a chromosomal fha_sfGFP fusion in V. anguillarum strain MHK3 and confirmed both isoforms by Western blotting analysis (Fig. 5d). Both the long and short isoforms of Fha are required for full T6SS activity, as chromosomal mutations M1A and M263A, which produce only the short or long isoform respectively, significantly reduced T6SS activities in bacterial competition and Hcp secretion assays (Fig. 5d, e, Supplementary Fig. 7d). Discussion

Bacteria deploy sophisticated protein secretion machines whose functional complexity rivals that of many eukaryotic systems. The T6SS exemplifies this sophistication because it builds a cell-spanning contractile apparatus that can physically penetrate Gram-negative, Gram-positive, and even fungal cell envelopes27–31. Despite the importance of such machines, most known regulatory paradigms emphasise transcriptional control, while much less is understood about how bacteria fine-tune assembly and activity once the components are already present in the cytosol. This gap is particularly critical for studying bacterial pathogens, which must rapidly activate secretion systems to survive highly fluctuating environmental and host-associated pressures. Here, we reveal a translational–biophysical regulatory mechanism that coordinates T6SS assembly. We show that V. cholerae uses intragenic coding to generate two isoforms of the scaffold Fha and that the two isoforms cooperate to regulate the T6SS activity (Fig. 6). The full-length isoform FhaL nucleates assembly by engaging membrane-associated components, whereas the shorter isoform FhaS enhances secretion efficiency through interaction with baseplate proteins and formation of condensates. This division of labor provides a mechanistic explanation for how a single scaffold coordinates the spatial and temporal dynamics of T6SS assembly.Fig. 6Isoform cooperation of Fha through phase separation promotes V. cholerae fitness and pandemic success.A The fha gene produces two isoforms through intragenic translation: the full-length FhaL and the shorter FhaS. Our data establish a direct mechanistic link between phase separation and T6SS efficiency. Both FhaL and FhaS form liquid-like condensates, and a single leucine-to-alanine substitution (L341A) within a conserved C-terminal α-helix disrupts condensate formation and diminishes T6SS activities. The dominant-negative activity of FhaL341A further supports condensate properties. Consistently, AI-designed binders targeting this helix also disrupted Fha condensates and impaired T6SS assembly in vivo. While secondary effects on Fha interactions cannot be ruled out, these results provide two orthogonal means to strongly support condensate formation as an organising principle for T6SS assembly and illustrate the utility of computational binder design as a complementary tool for modulating T6SS or other condensate-mediated protein machine assemblies.

How has V. cholerae evolved into one of the world’s most successful human pathogens? By integrating isoform diversity with condensate-mediated assembly, V. cholerae expands its regulatory toolkit beyond canonical transcriptional and post-translational control to promote the efficiency of a complex secretion system that is crucial for survival and virulence. The strict conservation of the internal initiation codon and upstream ribosome-binding site across V. cholerae genomes, coupled with the detection of dual isoforms in diverse Vibrio species, suggests that dual-isoform cooperation is not a recent innovation but rather an evolutionarily maintained solution for tuning the T6SS assembly.

Despite the mechanistic advances presented here, several limitations remain. First, while Fha condensates are clearly crucial for T6SS activity, the biophysical determinants of condensate stability and dynamics are not fully resolved. We do not yet understand whether material properties (such as viscosity, turnover rates, or maturation state) change during the T6SS assembly cycle or in response to physiological cues, particularly under nutrient limitation or other stresses. Second, because Fha interacts with but substantially outnumbers inner-membrane T6SS components32, it is plausible that cytosolic Fha may function as a molecular glue to capture the relatively less-mobile membrane proteins and low-abundant cytosolic components, facilitating their incorporation into an initiation site. Within the condensate, initial weak multivalent interactions would transition to stronger and canonical protein-protein interactions among structural proteins. However, the precise temporal sequence and spatial coordination of these recruitment events remain incompletely defined. Third, to what extent condensate formation influences effector loading and competition is also unclear33–36. Future structural, single-molecule, and high-resolution imaging approaches will be required to define how condensates orchestrate the dynamic T6SS process. Finally, although Fha is conserved in many T6SSs and known to be crucial to T6SS functions, not all T6SSs encode an Fha homolog17,37–40; whether alternative LLPS regulators exist in these systems, or whether they rely on other mechanisms (such as higher protein expression and spatially restricted localization at the pole) need to be determined.

Together, these findings from V. cholerae highlight that bacteria can expand regulatory capacity under genomic constraints by integrating gene-in-gene coding architecture coupled with biophysical control, representing an under-appreciated principle of bacterial evolution. This also represents a bacterial parallel to isoform diversification and biomolecular clustering observed in eukaryotic systems, revealing a generalizable strategy for organising complex molecular machines in compact genomes.

Methods

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Pennsylvania. All procedures complied with institutional guidelines for animal care and use.

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

V. cholerae, A. dhakensis, and E. coli strains were cultured in LB medium (1% [w/v] tryptone, 0.5% [w/v] yeast extract, and 0.5% [w/v] NaCl), V. parahaemolyticus strains were cultured in LB3 medium (1% [w/v] tryptone, 0.5% [w/v] yeast extract, and 3% [w/v] NaCl), while V. anguillarum MHK3 strains were cultured in TSB medium (1.7% [w/v] tryptone, 0.3% [w/v] soy peptone, 0.25% [w/v] glucose, 1.5% [w/v] NaCl, and 0.25% [w/v] K2HPO4) unless otherwise stated. Antibiotics were supplemented at the following concentrations: streptomycin (100 µg/ml), kanamycin (50 µg/ml), gentamicin (20 µg/ml), and carbenicillin (50 µg/ml). Plasmids were constructed using Gibson assembly and validated by Sanger sequencing. Precise gene deletion mutants were generated via homologous recombination using the pDS132 suicide vector. A schematic overview of the T6SS architecture, including the components that were genetically tagged or deleted in this study, is provided in Supplementary Fig. 8. A comprehensive list of plasmids and strains used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The primers used in this study are provided in Supplementary Data 3. All primers used in this study were synthesized by Tsingke Biotech (China).

Bacterial competition assay

Killer strains were cultured in liquid medium to the exponential phase, while prey strains were collected from overnight LB-agar plates. For V. cholerae interspecies competition, V. cholerae V52 was mixed with E. coli MG1655 at a 10:1 ratio (LB-agar, 3 h, 37 °C), with A. dhakensis SSU at 1:10 (LB-agar, 3 h, 37 °C), and with V. parahaemolyticus RIMD2210633 at 1:10 (LB3-agar, 14 h, 37 °C). For V. cholerae intraspecies competition, V. cholerae V52 was mixed with CH-GX at a 1:10 ratio (LB-agar, 3 h, 37 °C). For V. anguillarum interspecies competition, V. anguillarum MHK3 was mixed with E. coli MG1655 at a 10:1 ratio (TSB-agar, 3 h, 28 °C). Following incubation, survival was quantified by tenfold serial dilution on selective media. Error bars represent the mean ± standard deviation from at least three biological replicates.

Fluorescence microscopy of T6SS assembly

Strains were grown in LB medium to an OD600 of ~1. Cultures were centrifuged at 4500 × g for 2 min, re-suspended in LB medium to an OD600 of ~10, and spotted onto 1% agarose–0.5× PBS pads for imaging. Fluorescence microscopy was performed using a Nikon Ti-E A1R HD25 + SIM S microscope, equipped with the Perfect Focus System, a CFI SR HP Apochromat TIRF 100×C objective lens (NA 1.49), and 488/561 nm excitation lasers. For Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Fig. 5, an Olympus IX85 Inverted microscope was used, featuring the 100× oil objective lens (NA 1.5). GFP and mCherry filter sets were used for fluorescence excitation. All imaging experiments were performed at least twice, with representative results shown.

Protein purification

Strep-tagII-tagged or His-tagged proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) using pET vectors. Cultures were grown in LB medium to an OD600 of ~0.5 at 37 °C, followed by induction with 1 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4500 × g for 15 min. For the purification of Strep-tagII-tagged proteins, the pellets were re-suspended in PBS buffer (10 mM Na2HPO4, 140 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, pH 7.4) and lysed by sonication. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 30 min, and the resulting supernatants were incubated with Streptactin beads (Smart-lifesciences). Bound proteins were eluted with PBS containing 2.5 mM biotin. For the purification of His-tagged proteins, pellets were re-suspended in Tris buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) and subjected to sonication. Lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 30 min, and the supernatants were incubated with Ni-NTA beads (Smart-lifesciences). Proteins were subsequently eluted with Tris buffer containing varying concentrations of imidazole.

In vitro liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) assay

Purified proteins were dialyzed against LLPS buffer (10 mM Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, 200 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) and concentrated using centrifugal filter units equipped with Ultracel-10K membranes to a final concentration of approximately 80 µM. The concentrated proteins were subjected to twofold serial dilution in LLPS buffer and subsequently mixed 1:1 with 20% dextran-70 prepared in the same buffer, unless otherwise specified. The mixtures were immediately loaded onto glass slides for imaging. Imaging was performed using a ZEISS LSM 980 with Airyscan 2 microscope fitted with a 63×/1.40 oil objective and a 488 nm laser. All imaging experiments were repeated at least twice, and representative images are shown.

Western blotting analysis

Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE (Yeasen Biotechnology) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk in TBST (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% [v/v] Tween-20, pH 7.6) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies in 1% (w/v) non-fat milk in TBST. Signal detection was carried out using Clarity ECL reagent (Bio-Rad). Monoclonal antibodies against FLAG (#SLAB0102, 1:10,000 dilution) and 6×His (#SLAB2803, 1:10,000 dilution) were obtained from Smart-lifesciences, V5 (#AE017, 1:10,000 dilution) was ordered from ABclonal, and RpoB (#663905, 1:20,000 dilution) was purchased from BioLegend. The polyclonal anti-Hcp and anti-Fha antibodies were custom-generated by Shanghai Youlong Biotech and used at 1:10,000 dilution. HRP-linked secondary antibodies were acquired from ZSGB-Bio (#ZB-2305 [mouse] and #ZB-2301 [rabbit], 1:20,000 dilution).

Protein pull-down assay

Epitope-tagged constructs were generated by cloning genes of interest into pET, pBAD, and pBBR1MCS2 vectors as specified. For protein expression, E. coli BL21(DE3) carrying pET plasmids was grown in LB medium to OD600 ~ 0.6 at 37 °C and induced with 1 mM IPTG overnight at 20 °C. E. coli T-Fast carrying pBAD plasmids was grown under similar conditions and induced with 0.1% arabinose for 3 h at 30 °C. For pBBR1MCS2, E. coli T-Fast cultures were grown in LB for ~6 h at 37 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, re-suspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, 1× protease inhibitor [Thermo Scientific], pH 8.0), and subjected to sonication on ice for lysis. After centrifugation, supernatants were incubated with Ni-NTA beads (Smart-lifesciences) for 1 h at 4 °C with rotation. Beads were washed four times with wash buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and eluted with elution buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 400 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Input and eluate samples were analyzed by Western blotting. For pull-down experiments in V. cholerae V52, Strep-tagII-tagged Fha and sfGFP were enriched from cell lysates using Streptactin beads (Smart-lifesciences). Bound proteins were analyzed via LC-MS/MS at Jingjie PTM BioLab.

Protein secretion assay

For V. cholerae V52 strains, cultures were grown in LB with appropriate antibiotics at 30 °C to OD600 = 1.0 and collected by centrifugation at 2500 × g for 8 min. Pellets were re-suspended in fresh LB and incubated at 30 °C for 2 h with or without 0.01% (w/v) L-arabinose. For experiments shown in Fig. 3i, to induce binder expression in V52, 0.1% (w/v) L-arabinose was included both during culture growth and the subsequent static induction period. Cultures were centrifuged twice at 10,000 × g for 2 min to separate pellets and supernatants. Both fractions were mixed with SDS-loading buffer and defined as “Cell” and “Sec” samples, respectively. For V. anguillarum MHK3 strains, cultures were grown in TSB with appropriate antibiotics at 28 °C to OD600 = 1.0 and collected by centrifugation at 2500 × g for 8 min. Pellets were re-suspended in fresh TSB and incubated at 28 °C for 2 h. Cells were pelleted at 10,000 × g for 2 min and re-suspended in SDS-loading buffer as “Cell” samples. Supernatants were clarified again, precipitated with 20% (v/v) TCA at −20 °C for 20 min, centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, washed twice with acetone, air-dried, and re-suspended in SDS-loading buffer. All samples were boiled for 10 min before SDS-PAGE analysis.

Fluorescence quantification

Fluorescence measurements were carried out following a previously published protocol25. The lacO array was engineered to contain repeated operator sequences separated by 18-nucleotide randomized spacers. A mutant variant of LacI (LacImut), which lacks the C-terminal tetramerization domain but preserves DNA-binding affinity comparable to wild-type LacI, was fused to sfGFP and used to calibrate the sfGFP molecule number required to generate detectable foci of defined intensity in cells. LacImut_sfGFP was expressed from the pBAD24 plasmid and induced with 0.002% L-arabinose in V52 strains harbouring different numbers of integrated lacO repeats. Fluorescence signals from LacI and Fha foci were extracted using Fiji (ImageJ, version 1.54p), and all quantifications were performed on raw, unprocessed images. The intensity of each Fha or LacI focus was calculated after subtracting the background fluorescence measured from the surrounding cytoplasm. Note that the standard curve was established over 6–26 molecules. Consequently, estimates for larger foci may be less accurate.

Bacterial two-hybrid assay

Proteins of interest were fused to the T18 and T25 fragments of Bordetella adenylate cyclase41. Plasmids encoding the fusion proteins were co-transformed into the reporter strain BTH101. Three independent colonies per transformation were grown in 500 μl LB at 37 °C for 5 h. Three microliters of each culture were then spotted onto LB plates containing ampicillin, kanamycin, IPTG (0.04 mM), and X-Gal (80 µg/ml), followed by incubation for 6 h at 30 °C and an additional 10 h at room temperature. Experiments were performed in triplicate, and a representative result is shown.

Amoeba plaque assay

V. cholerae V52 cultures were grown overnight at 37 °C, harvested by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 1 min), and re-suspended in 1 ml PBS. A 100-µl aliquot of bacterial suspension was spread onto SM/5 plates and air-dried at room temperature. D. discoideum cells were collected from SM/5 plates containing WM6026, pelleted by centrifugation (500 × g, 4 min), washed five times with PBS, and re-suspended to ~1.5 × 107 cells/ml. Serial fivefold dilutions of amoebae were spotted onto SM/5 plates pre-seeded with bacteria. Plates were incubated at 22 °C for 3–7 days, after which plaque formation was assessed. The minimal number of D. discoideum cells capable of forming plaques on the bacterial lawn was recorded.

Infant mouse infections

Infant mouse infections were performed following a previously published protocol42. Postnatal day 5 (P5) CD1 (ICR) mice (Mus musculus domesticus outbred background) were weaned for 2–3 h prior to oral inoculation with V. cholerae V52 (5 × 109 CFU). Mice were maintained under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at 23 °C with 50% humidity prior to infection, and subsequently incubated at 30 °C for 16–18 h post-infection. Sex of the animals was not determined due to their age. CD1 mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Pennsylvania (approval number: 805287).

Binder protein sequence design

De novo binders were designed using a fold-conditioned RFdiffusion–ProteinMPNN workflow. The AlphaFold3-predicted structure of the Fha leucine-rich helix was used as the conditioning input to guide scaffold generation. RFdiffusion was used to generate 4000 initial scaffolds with geometries compatible with helix engagement. L334, L341, and L342 were specified as hotspot positions during the diffusion process to promote the formation of a shape-complementary binding interface. Sequence optimization of the resulting scaffolds was performed using ProteinMPNN with amino-acid–biased sampling, yielding a total of 12,000 candidate sequences (Supplementary Data 1). Structural evaluation of the designs was carried out using AlphaFold2 initial-guess models. Designs were filtered based on multiple confidence metrics, including high per-residue structural confidence (pLDDT >0.9), high interface predicted TM-score (i_pTM >0.85), and low interface predicted alignment error (i_PAE < 5 Å). Ten designs that consistently met these criteria were selected for experimental characterization (Supplementary Data 2).

Bioinformatic analysis

All gene sequences of V. cholerae V52 were retrieved from the draft genome assembly (GenBank NC_002505.1 and NC_002506.1), managed and analyzed by Benchling. The ribosome binding site (RBS) within the fha gene was identified by manual inspection of the nucleotide sequence, based on canonical bacterial RBS motifs such as AGGA, AGG, and GGA. For comparative analysis of V. cholerae fha sequences using per-site Shannon entropy in Fig. 5a, V. cholerae genomes (n = 10,117) were downloaded from NCBI and annotated with Prokka (version 1.14.6) to obtain protein (.faa) and CDS (.ffn) files. Full-length Fha proteins were retrieved by BLASTx (coverage >99%) against the.faa files (10,026 sequences) and aligned with MAFFT (version 7.525). CDS sequences were then threaded onto the protein alignment to maintain codon-level correspondence (Supplementary data 4).
