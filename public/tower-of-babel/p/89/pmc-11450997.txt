3D-printed orthoses and prostheses for people with physical disability in rehabilitation centers: a scoping review
Authors: Juarez de Souza Pereira, Amanda Sarkis Moor Santos Xavier, Ricardo da Silva Monteiro, Vanessa Vianna Cruz, Manoel Francisco de Souza Pereira, Adriana Dutra Tholl, Jonas Felisbino, Wiliam César Alves Machado
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimsThe World Health Organization points out that, by 2030, two billion people will need at least one assistive product. 3D printing can be used to meet the demands when dispensing these products.PurposeThis review aims to map the use of 3D printing in the manufacture of orthoses and prostheses for people with physical disability at rehabilitation centers.MethodsPublications that deal with the use of 3D printing for the manufacture of orthoses and prostheses were used, preferably studies from 2012 to 2022.ResultsThe majority of studies, 56.25%, were quantitative and 46.25% were evaluative research. None of the studies were characterized as developed at rehabilitation centers. 75% of them had the participation of people with physical disability. The use of 3D printing was, for the most part, for the development of assistive technologies for the upper limbs at 56.25%, while 31.25% were for the lower limbs.ConclusionThe assistive products developed were orthoses and prostheses for the wrist, hands, fingers, upper limbs, writing devices, sockets, knees, and feet. Although there were positive results in their performance, some limitations related to strength, stiffness, and resistance were observed.

Introduction

Orthotics and prosthetics in people with disability (PwD) in Brazil are based on the assumption that most of the coverage to provide orthoses and prostheses is carried out by public healthcare policies established by the Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde – SUS). The orthopedic workshops linked to the Specialized Rehabilitation Centers (Centros Especializados de Reabilitação – CER) are the healthcare establishments responsible for the production of orthoses and prostheses [1, 2].

The role of orthoses and prostheses as technical-aid resources that greatly contribute to the adaptation of persons with physical disability is well known, particularly when they are dispensed to users in a timely manner and enable their use in early intervention. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines assistive technologies (AT) as technologies and products that facilitate or promote the functionality of PwD so as to enable their autonomous performance towards inclusive participation at equal conditions in society [3].

It is noteworthy that, while orthoses are characterized by their goal in facilitating the execution of daily activities [4], prostheses are characterized by their capacity of partially or completely replacing the segment of upper or lower limbs [5].

The WHO estimates that, in 2030, two billion people will require at least one assistive product. Currently, over one billion people worldwide require ATs and only one out of ten has access to such products. That growing demand is closely related to population aging [6].

Expanding and expediting the supply of ATs to PwD, besides fulfilling the healthcare guidelines that aim at integrality of care, fills a gap at services and CERs, taking into account that, in the state of Rio de Janeiro, many healthcare regions lack orthopedic workshops linked to those healthcare requirements.

It is important to note that, in Brazil, rehabilitation services are organized based on CERs for care towards PwD, which is responsible for promoting access, dispensation, adaptation, and maintenance of orthoses and prostheses for that population [1]. Furthermore, access to orthoses and prostheses reduces the waiting time of users and consolidates their social and economic role, taking into account that failure in accessing such services tends to make those persons more dependent, isolated, and socially vulnerable [7].

Likewise, expanding access to quality and affordable devices such as orthoses and prostheses through universal health coverage systems such as SUS to PwD meets the commitment of countries that signed the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities of the United Nations, such as Brazil [8].

The use of materials over the years has greatly advanced in order to achieve appropriate shapes and textures for the users of those resources, from the wood employed in the manufacture of orthoses and prostheses to aluminum, myoelectric systems, silicone, and carbon fiber, among others [9]. More recently, the advent of three-dimensional printing has enabled orthoses and prostheses to be produced using additive manufacturing.

Rapid prototyping (3D printing) can be used to meet the demands of AT dispensing. This type of technology allows complex objects to be more affordable to produce [10], resulting in significant cost reduction and quicker dispensation to users. In addition, it also allows adaptations to be refined at the individual level so as to eliminate the use of some materials that may cause discomfort or intercurrences [11].

With that in mind, given the little availability of papers on the subject in the databases of the field, this scoping review aims to map how 3D printing has been employed in the manufacture of orthoses and prostheses for PwD at rehabilitation centers.

It is important no point out that, in Brazil, the National Health Survey (Pesquisa Nacional de Saúde – PNS-2019) carried out by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística – IBGE) on PwD revealed that about 3.8% of persons 2 years old or more had compromised lower limbs and 2.7%, compromised upper limbs. Regarding orthoses and prostheses, the survey showed that 54.0% of those who reported using prostheses obtained them through SUS, at 45.7% orthoses, 28.1% prostheses for upper limbs, and 23.2% orthoses for upper limbs [12].

Although many regions in Brazil feature CERs, many lack traditional orthopedic workshops, which compromises access to orthoses and prostheses. Such demand could be partially met by 3D printing.

Method

This is a scoping review carried out based on its previously created protocol, which can be accessed at the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform under registration https://osf.io/9ge6j/ and according to the Digital Object Identifier (DOI) https://osf.io/9ge6j/. The checklist of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was used to report the results of the review [13].

Through the acronym PCC, where P = population, C = concept, and C = context, namely, person with physical disability, 3D printing for the manufacture of orthoses and prostheses, and rehabilitation centers, respectively, the following research question was devised: How has 3D printing been employed for the manufacture of orthoses and prostheses for people with physical disability at rehabilitation centers? The search strategy aimed to find published or non-published primary studies and opinion, quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method papers. An initial search limited to Portal BVS (Biblioteca Virtual em Saúde – Virtual Health Library) was performed to identify papers on the subject. The words in titles and abstracts of relevant papers and the index terms employed to describe the papers were used to develop a complete search strategy. The search strategy, including the keywords and index terms identified, was adapted to each information source included and the search was executed in February 2023. Studies published in Portuguese, English, and Spanish were included. The studies selected were published between 2012 and December 2022, the period comprising the publication of the ministerial ordinance and the implementation of the Network of Care for People with Disability (Rede de Cuidados a Pessoa com Deficiência – RCPD). The databases searched included Portal BVS, PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science. The sources of non-published studies and grey literature searched included the Brazilian Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations (Biblioteca Digital Brasileira de Teses e Dissertações – BDTD). The descriptors employed were the ones available in the Descriptors of Health Sciences (Descritores em Ciências da Saúde – DeCS) and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH): “Disabled Persons,” “Three-Dimensional Printing,” “Orthotic Devices,” “Artificial Limbs,” and “Rehabilitation Centers.” The descriptors defined were combined with Boolean operators (AND/OR).

Studies with goals in the use of orthoses and prostheses other than serving persons with physical disability to replace limbs or aid in a joint or body segment, such as odontology purposes, surgical simulations, or review studies were excluded. Socioeconomic, cultural, geographic, racial, and gender aspects were not taken into consideration. Most studies selected for full-text reading came from Embase and data extraction used a form. The studies recovered were exported to the reference organizer Zotero®, which contributed to the management of references and their bibliographic data. After duplicate studies were excluded, those that failed to meet the inclusion criteria were manually removed.

The papers selected underwent reading of the title and abstract, selection of papers from the references, and full-text reading. During study selection, two reviewers read the titles and abstracts guided by the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After that, they verified the eligibility of the studies for full-text reading based on the contribution of a third reviewer to elect the final number of studies. Disagreements were settled by a fourth reviewer. Finally, all studies included were read in full by the reviewers for data extraction.

Data extraction was based on a form so as to guide the extraction of information considered relevant regarding bibliography, paper characteristics, and other information that might contribute to mapping how 3D printing has been employed to manufacture orthoses and prostheses for people with physical disability at rehabilitation centers. Disagreements were settled via consensus by the principal reviewers of the study.

After extraction, the data were analyzed and interpreted based on the design oriented by the goal of the study so as to answer the guiding question of the present review. The data presented were organized based on flow diagrams and tables listing the main findings.

The present scoping review dealt with freely accessible data on online databases and, thus, did not require approval of the Committee of Ethics and Research. The authors involved declare no conflict of interest.

Results

The search on the databases recovered 104 studies, of which 6 were excluded as duplicates and 98 were selected for reading of titles and abstracts. That step excluded 70 studies whose titles and abstracts were not considered eligible. Twenty-eight studies were selected for full-text reading, which excluded 12 more that were not available in full, review studies, studies focusing on surgeries, and studies unrelated to orthoses and prostheses for PwD. In the end, 16 studies met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1).

The studies included were published between 2010 and 2021 and comprised 13 papers (81.25%), two dissertations (12.5%), and one thesis (6.25%). As detailed in Table 3, the works included were carried out in nine countries: Germany [14], Brazil [10, 15, 16], China [17], United States [18–22], France [23], Italy [24], United Kingdom [25, 26], Taiwan [27], and Turkey [28].

The country with the most studies was the United States with five, followed by Brazil with three, the United Kingdom with two, and the other countries with only one each. It is noteworthy that the studies included from Brazil were the dissertations and the thesis. Both the journals and the institutes involved are mostly related to the area of technology, biomechanics, and engineering in contrast with studies proposed by the healthcare area.

The column “Participants” in Table 3 reports the participation of PwD characterized by amputation of the forearm in one (6.25%) study, one (6.25%) study on transradial amputees, one (6.25%) with absence of a transradial limb, two (12.5%) studies on transtibial amputation, one (6.25%) transfemoral amputation, one (6.25%) foot amputation, and one (6.25%) on hand and fingers amputation. Another two (12.5%) studies had the participation of PwD due to cerebral palsy, being one (6.25%) hemiplegic and one (6.25%) tetraplegic. One (6.25%) study had occupational therapists as participants, while two (12.5%) had no people as participants and one (6.25%) had a non-disabled user.

Table 3 also shows a broad variety of the application of 3D printing in the studies according to the inclusion criterion. Most (nine, 56.25%) studies dealt with upper limbs, while five (31.25%) dealt with lower limbs and one (6.25%) was related to various splints and orthoses. Another study (6.25%) pointed out in its approach that most papers analyzed were also related to the development of prostheses for upper limbs.

Of the studies that used 3D printing for upper limbs, two (12.5%) were related to prosthetic hands, one (6.25%) was on wrist and hand, two (12.5%) were on upper limbs, one (6.25%) dealt with a socket, one (6.25%) was on writs, hands and fingers, one (6.25%) was on a writing device, and one (6.25%) was related to the arm. For lower limbs, two (12.5%) studies employed 3D printing for foot prostheses, one (6.25%) for knee, and two (12.5%) for sockets. In one study (6.25%), it could not be identified whether the splints and orthoses developed were for upper or lower limbs, while one study (6.25%) had no development of orthoses or prostheses.

It was found that five studies (31.25%) did not report shortcomings of the orthoses and prostheses, as seen in Table 3. The remaining studies pointed out as shortcomings: one (6.25%), long-term fatigue not assessed; one (6.25%), low force and prehension; one (6.25%), unfinished tasks; one (6.25%), low resistance and heavier material; one (6.25%), change in force was not significant; one (6.25%), unsynchronized finger knuckles; one (6.25%), poorly tensioned rubber bands; one (6.25%), rigidity compromises performance; one (6.25%), thick alveolus wall; and one (6.25%), untested efficacy.

Discussion

The expected growing demand for ATs worldwide requires governments to implement policies aiming at meeting such needs. Incorporating technologies in the manufacture of 3D-printed orthoses and prostheses at the CERs may help expand the provision of such products to PwD. Knowing how that technology has been employed contributes to disseminating knowledge and fosters new studies on the matter.

The present study recovered papers from Europe, Asia, Middle East, and North and South America. In the Americas, Brazil was the second country in number of studies, only behind the USA. The Brazilian studies were recovered from the BDTD, which is part of the information systems of theses and dissertations that exist at education and research institutions in the country. Most studies came from the fields of engineering, biomechanics, and technology in contrast with those proposed by healthcare fields. A similar result was observed in Brazil in the work by Borges, who made an overview of the production of knowledge on 3D printing for the production of orthoses, prostheses, and special materials and their potential to be used within the RCPD. The main areas identified in the studies analyzed by Borges had engineering as protagonist, followed by design, computer science, and robotics [10].

Several physical disabilities were observed, from amputations of upper limbs [20, 21, 24] to absence of an upper limb [26] and amputations of the lower limbs [14, 18, 25, 27], in addition to cerebral palsy [15, 16], hemiplegia [23], and tetraplegia [28]. In this review, 56.25% of the studies focused on the manufacture of orthoses and prostheses with the use of 3D printing for upper limbs. A similar result was found in a study that identified the most often discussed subject was the development of prostheses for upper limbs [10].

It was observed that, in most studies, the use of 3D printing was successful and produced positive results, particularly regarding manufacturing time. The evaluation by Oslen et al. reported that the time between scanning and the raw digitalization of sockets for upper limbs was approximately 30 min, with an average production time among the participants of approximately 15 h [26]. In the study by Poier, which produced orthoses for wrist, hand, and fingers for children with spastic cerebral palsy, the mean time for 3D printing was 10 h [16]. For Thomann et al., who developed 13 prototypes, the highlight was the rapid time of manufacture of a dynamic upper-limb orthosis, however, the authors pointed out the 13 cycles required to propose a usable product, suggesting that a new version of a prototype could be made in one day [23].

The case study by Yoshikawa et al. reported a shortcoming in the manufacture of a 3D-printed prosthetic hand with realistic appearance when carrying out tasks that could not be finished due to the weight of the objects, which made them slip from the prosthetic hand [24]. Thomann et al. reported a shortcoming of the dynamic upper-limb orthosis because the rubber bands attached to part of the hand are not sufficiently tensioned, which compromises the thumb fully grasping the object [23]. Prehension force was questioned during the flexing and extension of the fingers and it was reported that the knuckles of that segment moved out of sync [17, 21], in addition to the low durability of the externally powered mechanical components controlled by the shoulder [20]. Hitzeroth et al. observed in a foot prosthesis that, compared with conventional prostheses, rigidity may compromise performance [14]. Ramakrishnan et al., in turn, when discussing a 3D-printed knee, concluded that, to achieve the same resistance, larger, bulkier projects must be made, which makes them heavier than conventional prostheses [25]. In order to improve rigidity, Hitzeroth et al. recommend adjusting the rigidity of the 3D-printed material according to participant weight [14].

Regarding the main recommendations, corroborated by most studies, Degerli et al. warn that more studies must be carried out on the efficacy, dimension, weight, safety, durability, ease of use, and comfort of the device with long-term follow-up [28]. To Tzeng et al., more amputees should be invited to take part in the validation of the CAD/3DP (computer-aided design and 3-dimensional printing) method [27]. Oslem et al. recommended the clinical and research communities collaborate to create digital tools that are useful for patients and physicians [26]. Other studies underscore the applicability of 3D printing in the development process of ATs for patients with neurological impairment. The devices manufactured using such technology have optimized the function of patients, standing out for the ability to be customized in addition to their low cost [29, 30].

Conclusion

3D printing facilitates the production of orthoses and prostheses through additive manufacturing, enabling the creation of intricate objects at a reduced cost. This technology allows for personalized and refined adaptations at the individual level, eliminating the use of materials that may cause discomfort. Research has demonstrated the development of various 3D-printed orthoses and prostheses for individuals with physical disabilities, including devices for wrists, hands, fingers, upper limbs, writing aids, sockets, knees, and feet.

However, challenges such as material-related issues with force, rigidity, and resistance persist. The scoping review underscores the necessity for further research on the efficacy, dimensions, weight, safety, durability, ease of use, and comfort of 3D-printed orthoses and prostheses, with long-term follow-up to evaluate their effectiveness.

Overall, the review indicates that, while 3D printing technology shows promise for manufacturing orthoses and prostheses for individuals with physical disabilities, more comprehensive studies and advancements are required to overcome existing challenges and enhance the functionality of these assistive products.
