Barriers to long-term follow-up among patients in cardiovascular-metabolic clinics: a qualitative study based on the COM-B model
Authors: Dongxiao Xu, Wentao Ma, Congying Liu, Huixian Chen, Xiaotian Zhang, Sumei Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic diseases are major chronic conditions requiring long-term follow-up to optimize management and prevent complications, yet discontinuation of follow-up is common and may compromise patient outcomes. Evidence on the specific barriers to sustained follow-up in the Chinese healthcare context remains limited. This study aimed to explore these barriers using the COM-B (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behavior) model, to provide insights for improving follow-up strategies and chronic disease management.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted using semi-structured interviews. Sixteen patients who had discontinued follow-up at a cardiovascular-metabolic clinic in Beijing completed in-depth interviews. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using content-structuring qualitative content analysis.ResultsAnalysis identified three COM-B domains comprising 10 barrier-related and 6 facilitator-related subthemes. Capability barriers included limited disease knowledge and age-related constraints. Opportunity barriers centred on logistical burdens and limited family support, while facilitators involved easier appointment access and caregiver assistance. Reflective motivational barriers included distrust and cognitive bias between traditional Chinese medicine and Western medicine and a low perceived benefit of follow-up, while automatic motivational barriers involved weak habit formation for routine clinic attendance.ConclusionPatients’ loss to follow-up reflected the combined effects of limited disease understanding, weak self-management ability, logistical and access barriers, insufficient family support, and belief-driven motivational challenges. Improving follow-up adherence requires coordinated, patient-centred strategies that enhance understanding, ease practical barriers, and strengthen motivation through supportive and trust-based care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14417-1.Keywords: Ccardiovascular-metabolic diseases, Collaborative outpatient clinic, Loss to follow-up, Qualitative study, COM-B model, Behaviour change framework

Background

With the development of China’s social economy and the increasing prevalence of unhealthy lifestyles [1], cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs), including coronary heart disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and stroke [2], have become the leading causes of death and disease burden among Chinese residents [3].

Currently, the management of CMDs is no longer limited to symptomatic treatment of single diseases but instead emphasizes multiple comorbidities as the starting point, highlighting lifestyle interventions alongside multidimensional therapeutic strategies such as blood pressure, lipid, and glucose control, as well as weight management. Against this backdrop, interdisciplinary models of comprehensive disease management have gradually been applied and promoted in clinical practice. In 2020, a tertiary general hospital in Beijing established the nation’s first well-structured cardiometabolic outpatient clinic, adopting a “one physician–one nurse” collaborative model, with a multidisciplinary team jointly participating in patient management [4].

This clinic developed a nurse-led continuity-of-care system, in which specialized nurses conducted health risk screening using standardized assessment tools, implemented individualized interventions, provided psychological support, conducted dynamic follow-up, and applied intelligent re-examination management, with the aim of improving service accessibility and the effectiveness of health management [5]. Although the follow-up process in the cardiometabolic clinic has been structurally optimized, the patient loss-to-follow-up rate remains as high as 12%, reflecting the shortcomings of the existing follow-up model in maintaining patient adherence.

At present, research on follow-up in patients with CMDs in China mainly focuses on quantitative analyses of adherence status and influencing factors [6], while few studies have incorporated behavioral theories to qualitatively explore the barriers and underlying mechanisms that patients face during long-term follow-up [7]. The “Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behavior” (COM-B) model posits that behavior change can occur only when individuals possess adequate capability, opportunity, and motivation simultaneously. The COM-B model provides a systematic framework to analyze the key mechanisms of behavior initiation and maintenance. Its concise structure and logical clarity have been widely validated in studies on patient self-management and chronic disease adherence, making it well-suited for explaining the multiple barriers to loss-to-follow-up behaviors [8].

Given that loss-to-follow-up behaviors are strongly influenced by subjective experiences and contextual factors that cannot be fully explained by quantitative research, qualitative research offers an opportunity to gain in-depth insights into patients’ lived experiences and behavioral logic. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the barriers to long-term follow-up among patients attending a cardiometabolic clinic using qualitative research methods guided by the COM-B model, with the goal of providing theoretical support for optimizing medical service processes and improving continuity of nursing care.

Methods

Participants

A purposive sampling strategy was adopted. Patients who had attended the cardiometabolic clinic with physician–nurse collaboration in a tertiary general hospital in Beijing between January 2023 and June 2024 but discontinued follow-up for various reasons were recruited.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with a confirmed diagnosis of cardiometabolic disease requiring long-term follow-up; (2) patients who had previously participated in follow-up at the cardiometabolic clinic of Peking University Third Hospital; (3) patients who had either failed to attend follow-up appointments on schedule or refused follow-up; and (4) patients who were willing and able to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients unable to articulate their subjective experiences; (2) patients with speech, hearing, or psychiatric disorders; and (3) patients unable to complete the entire interview process.

During the study period, approximately 1,000 patients attended the cardiometabolic clinic, of whom 120 (12%) were classified as lost-to-follow-up and constituted the accessible pool for sampling.

A research nurse (XDX) screened medical records to identify potential participants who met the inclusion criteria and contacted eligible individuals by telephone to introduce the study and assess willingness to participate. Interviews were then scheduled with those who agreed to participate.

In line with qualitative research methodology, no a priori sample size was specified. Instead, participant recruitment was guided by the principle of thematic saturation. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently, with saturation assessed on an ongoing basis. Saturation was reached after the 15th interview, when no new codes or conceptual insights emerged. One additional interview was conducted to confirm saturation, resulting in a final sample of 16 participants.

This study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (M2024929), and all participants provided informed consent.

Development of the interview guide

A qualitative descriptive design was adopted [9]. Based on a review of the literature and discussions within the research team, a preliminary semi-structured interview guide was developed. The interview questions were designed to correspond to the three dimensions of the COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation). In this study, the COM-B model was applied as a theoretical framework to guide data collection and analysis for identifying determinants of follow-up behavior, rather than for designing or evaluating behavior change interventions. The draft guide was reviewed by three behavioral science experts to reduce subjectivity in classification. The semi-structured interviews used in this study were developed specifically for this research based on the study objectives and relevant theoretical framework. The interview guide was not previously published or used in any other studies. Pilot interviews were conducted with three patients who had discontinued follow-up at the cardiometabolic clinic, and further refinements were made to the guide after expert consultation. These three pilot interviews were used solely to refine the wording, sequencing, and probing depth of the interview questions and were not included in the final analytic sample. Based on the pilots, we simplified technical terms, adjusted the order of questions to improve conversational flow, and added prompts to elicit more detailed descriptions of reasons for discontinuing follow-up.

Data collection and quality control

Data were collected through one-to-one semi-structured telephone interviews. All interviews were conducted solely by Researcher 1 (XDX), a trained research nurse with prior experience in qualitative interviewing. She had completed systematic training in qualitative research and communication skills and had a clinical background in cardiometabolic disease management, enabling her to build rapport with participants. The interviews were conducted from a private, sound-insulated consultation room within the hospital research center to ensure a quiet environment and protect confidentiality. During the interviews, she also took brief field notes on notable pauses or emotional expressions to support subsequent interpretation; however, these notes were used only as contextual supplements to the verbatim transcripts and were not coded as a separate data source.

Before the formal interviews, participants were fully informed of the study’s purpose, content, confidentiality measures, and recording procedures, and verbal informed consent was obtained. Because all interviews were conducted by telephone, the ethics committee approved the use of audio-recorded verbal informed consent instead of written consent. Interviewers respected and encouraged participants to express their authentic views, avoided suggestive or leading questions, listened attentively, and used probing and confirmation techniques when necessary to ensure accuracy.

Each interview lasted 20–30 min, was audio-recorded in full, and notes were taken regarding participants’ tone and emotional changes. Data collection continued until no new themes emerged, at which point saturation was considered achieved.

Data analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 h of the interview, which ensured a high level of accuracy and contextual recall. Audio files and transcripts were anonymised, encrypted, and accessible only to members of the research team; original audio files were permanently deleted after transcription to protect participant privacy.

The data were analysed according to Kuckartz and Rädiker using a content-structuring qualitative content analysis with a deductive–inductive approach [10]. In this process, the data were structured according to predefined theoretical dimensions and subsequently analysed within this structured framework.

The COM-B model provided the initial deductive category system, comprising the three main dimensions of Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation. These dimensions served as the overarching analytical structure. First, transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the content. Second, an initial coding framework was developed based on the COM-B dimensions. Third, transcripts were coded line by line using MAXQDA 24, assigning meaning units to the relevant theoretical categories.

To ensure rigor and consistency, the research team held regular meetings to discuss coding discrepancies, adopting a consensus approach [11]. Disagreements were resolved through iterative comparison of original transcripts with the theoretical framework until consensus was reached [12].

Transcripts were prepared verbatim by XDX. To ensure accuracy, MWT cross-checked 20% of transcripts. Data analysis was conducted collaboratively by XDX, CHX, and LCY.

Themes

Analysis identified three main themes and nine subthemes based on the COM-B framework. Illustrative quotes are provided to highlight participants’ perspectives.

Theme 1: Capability-related barriers

Patients reported limited understanding of disease progression, inconsistent self-management skills, and difficulty interpreting medical advice as key barriers. At the same time, several participants described facilitators, including clear explanations from familiar clinicians, simplified educational materials, and opportunities to ask questions during earlier visits. These capability-enhancing experiences helped some patients maintain follow-up despite structural challenges.

Limited disease knowledge and self-management skills

Many participants lacked a comprehensive understanding of cardiometabolic diseases and the importance of continuous follow-up. Some perceived their condition as “not serious” once symptoms improved, leading to discontinued visits.

“After taking medicine for a while, I felt much better. I thought it was unnecessary to keep going back to the clinic.” (P06)

Physical limitations and comorbidities

Some participants reported that physical discomfort, mobility issues, or multiple chronic conditions limited their ability to attend follow-up visits.

“I have knee problems, and going to the hospital is very inconvenient. Sometimes I just can’t manage it.” (P11)

Theme 2: Opportunity-related barriers

Opportunity-related barriers included transportation difficulties, complex clinic procedures, and insufficient family or workplace support. However, facilitators emerged in cases where family members reminded patients of appointments, assisted with transportation, or helped coordinate schedules. Participants also perceived the clinic’s traffic-guidance volunteers and simplified triage procedures as enabling smoother access, reducing the perceived burden of follow-up.

Logistical and financial constraints

Distance to the hospital, long waiting times, and high costs were common barriers that discouraged follow-up.

“It takes half a day just to get to the hospital and back. With work and family, it’s really hard to spare the time.” (P03)

Lack of family or social support

A number of participants emphasized that insufficient support from family members or caregivers reduced their motivation to continue follow-up.

“If my family can’t go with me, I often give up. Going alone feels too troublesome.” (P08)

Limited accessibility of follow-up services

Participants noted that limited availability of appointment slots or lack of community-based alternatives made it difficult to maintain regular follow-up.

“Sometimes I want to book an appointment, but there are no available slots. Then I just let it go.” (P14)

Competing priorities in daily life

Work, caregiving responsibilities, and other life obligations often took precedence over regular clinic visits.

“I’m too busy with work and family. Health is important, but I just can’t fit it in.” (P02)

Theme 3: Motivation-related barriers

Motivational barriers were shaped by treatment beliefs, low perceived benefit of follow-up, and doubts regarding the necessity of long-term monitoring. In contrast, motivation-enhancing facilitators included trusting relationships with clinicians, positive previous treatment outcomes, and encouragement from family members. These helped sustain patients’ intention to return for follow-up even when capability and opportunity constraints were present.

Low perceived benefit of follow-up

Several participants believed that follow-up visits provided little additional value, especially when they had already established a medication routine.

“Every time I go, the doctor just tells me to keep taking the medicine. I feel like it doesn’t make much difference.” (P05)

Inconsistent provider continuity and lack of trust in doctors

Some participants reported lack of trust in doctors, especially when different physicians provided inconsistent treatment or frequently changed during follow-up.

“The doctors kept changing, and I felt the new ones were not responsible. They just prescribed the same drugs as before, without really following my case.” (P02)

Cognitive bias toward TCM vs. Western medicine

Influenced by traditional Chinese cultural beliefs, some patients exhibited cognitive bias between traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and Western medicine. They perceived TCM as addressing the “root cause” of disease, whereas Western medicine was viewed as providing only symptomatic relief. Concerns about the long-term side effects of Western medications led certain patients to discontinue prescribed drugs once their condition stabilized, refuse further follow-up, and instead seek treatment through TCM.

“Traditional Chinese medicine addresses the root cause, while Western medicine only treats the symptoms—for example, if the foot hurts, it treats only the foot; if the hand hurts, it treats only the hand. That’s how I see it.”(P02)

“She may have stopped all the Western medications… now she is taking only Chinese patent medicines.”(P06)

Psychological burden and stigma

Some participants described feelings of anxiety, fear, or stigma related to repeated hospital visits, which hindered their willingness to continue follow-up.

“Going back again and again makes me feel like I’m really sick. It affects my mood.” (P12)

Summary of findings

Overall, patients’ discontinuation of follow-up emerged from interrelated barriers across all COM-B domains. Capability limitations centred on weak disease understanding and self-management skills. Opportunity constraints involved geographical distance, logistical challenges, and restricted social support. Motivational barriers were shaped by treatment beliefs, perceived low benefit, and weak habit formation. These domains interacted in reinforcing ways—for example, logistical obstacles amplified doubts about the value of follow-up, while limited knowledge undermined confidence in sustaining follow-up routines.

Discussion

This study applied content-structuring qualitative content analysis with a deductive–inductive approach, using the COM-B model to systematically classify and interpret barriers to long-term follow-up. The model was not used to design or evaluate behavior change interventions, but to support a structured understanding of why follow-up was difficult to sustain. Three interrelated dimensions capability, opportunity, and motivation were found to jointly shape discontinuation behaviors [13, 14]. Our findings not only corroborate existing evidence on adherence challenges in chronic disease management but also extend the literature by contextualizing these barriers within the Chinese healthcare system and the evolving model of multidisciplinary, nurse–physician collaborative clinics [15, 16].

Belief-driven motivational barriers were central to patients’ decisions not to return for follow-up. In our study, a salient reflective motivational barrier was patients’ preference for TCM coupled with skepticism toward long-term Western pharmacotherapy. Many participants described TCM as addressing the “root cause” of illness and expressed concerns that chronic use of Western drugs—particularly lipid-lowering therapy—would cause harm, which led some to discontinue prescribed medication once symptoms stabilized and withdraw from structured follow-up.Similar beliefs have been widely documented among Chinese populations, where TCM is embedded in everyday explanatory models of illness and prevention and is frequently preferred for chronic disease care [17, 18]. These cultural beliefs are associated with lower necessity beliefs about Western medicines and higher odds of non-adherence. However, this study highlights the unique interplay between reflective motivational factors, including cultural preferences (e.g., TCM use) and perceived adequacy of self-management, which are particularly salient in Chinese patients [19–21]. Opportunity-related barriers such as geographical distance, complex clinic procedures, and insufficient family or social support are consistent with findings from global studies [22–26], but our results underscore the critical role of family support in enabling older patients to attend follow-up, an issue less frequently emphasized in Western contexts [27–29]. Motivation-related barriers align with evidence that both reflective and automatic motivation are necessary to sustain adherence [30, 31], yet this study illustrates how the absence of habit formation may undermine even patients with high initial trust in the healthcare system [32–40].

The results suggest that improving follow-up adherence requires integrated strategies targeting all three COM-B dimensions. Capability-targeted interventions should focus on enhancing patients’ disease knowledge, self-management skills, and understanding of follow-up procedures. Reflective motivation-targeted interventions should address beliefs about TCM and Western medicine, align patient expectations with biomedical care, and build trust in healthcare providers. Opportunity-enhancing interventions may include decentralizing stable cases to community clinics, streamlining appointment and referral systems, and expanding telehealth services to overcome geographical and temporal barriers [41]. Automatic motivation-targeted interventions could include personalized reminders, peer-support programs, and behavioral reinforcement techniques to cultivate habitual follow-up behaviors. These recommendations align with current policy priorities in China’s hierarchical healthcare system and may inform the design of scalable chronic disease management models [39, 42].

Although the COM-B model is a behavior change model, the aim of this study was to classify and understand the barriers and facilitators of follow-up behavior rather than to design or evaluate behavior change interventions. Therefore, the focus remained on elucidating mechanisms rather than intervention development, which is an important next step informed by these findings.

Implications for practice

Findings from this study suggest several integrated strategies to enhance long-term follow-up. First, capability-oriented interventions should provide simple, visual and tailored education delivered by stable clinical teams, enhancing patients’ comprehension and confidence in self-management. Second, opportunity-focused strategies may include streamlining appointment procedures, strengthening family involvement, and introducing support roles such as nurse coordinators to reduce logistical burdens. Third, motivation-enhancing approaches such as culturally sensitive communication, reinforcement of treatment benefits, and trust-building continuity with familiar clinicians—can address belief-based concerns and promote routine follow-up behaviour. Combining these strategies aligns with patient-identified facilitators and reflects the synergistic nature of the COM-B system.

Strengths and contributions

A key strength of this study is the application of the COM-B model as an analytic framework, which allowed for a comprehensive and theory-driven understanding of follow-up barriers. By incorporating patient narratives, the study provides nuanced insights that complement existing quantitative findings and highlight culturally embedded factors, thereby advancing the evidence base for behavior-informed intervention design in cardiometabolic care.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be noted. First, the study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital, and the sample predominantly comprised older, retired patients, limiting generalizability to younger or more socioeconomically diverse groups. Second, the retrospective nature of interviews may have introduced recall bias or self-rationalization in participants’ responses. Third, although thematic saturation was achieved, the use of telephone interviews and a relatively focused interview duration (20–30 min) may have constrained the emergence of more nuanced or divergent narratives. These methodological choices were pragmatic given participants’ mobility constraints, but may have reduced contextual depth and limited the ability to probe complex emotional or cultural influences. In addition, the study did not employ methodological or theoretical triangulation, which may limit the robustness of interpretation. Although analytic triangulation involving three researchers was performed, the reliance on a single interviewer may have introduced subtle bias. Finally, the small, clinically homogeneous sample and the focused interview duration may have constrained analytical depth. Finally, although the COM-B framework provided valuable structure, triangulation with quantitative data could further strengthen explanatory power. Future studies should adopt multicenter designs with larger and more heterogeneous samples, and integrate mixed-methods approaches to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study shows that loss to follow-up among patients with cardiometabolic diseases arises from the combined effects of limited illness understanding, practical and organizational constraints, and belief-driven motivational challenges. These mechanisms interact in reinforcing ways, with capability gaps amplifying logistical burdens and shaping patients’ perceived value of continued care. Family support, trust in clinicians, and positive prior experiences emerged as important facilitators that can buffer these barriers. Enhancing long-term follow-up therefore requires integrated, patient-centred approaches that strengthen disease understanding and self-management confidence, reduce procedural and access-related burdens, and reinforce motivation through culturally attuned, trust-building communication and continuity of care.
