Molecular-level understanding of the aging bone and regeneration mechanisms using computational methods
Authors: Filip Stojceski, Harry Zaverdas, Andrea Danani, Alessia Mengoni, Mario Ledda, Giuseppe Falvo D’Urso Labate, Athanasios Kalogeras, Konstantinos Theofilatos, Seferina Mavroudi, Gianvito Grasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone degeneration diseases, such as osteoporosis, are skeletal disorders characterized by diminished bone mass and increased susceptibility to fractures and represent a growing global health challenge, particularly in aging populations. The development of effective therapeutic strategies necessitates a deep understanding of the complex biological processes underlying bone remodeling, regeneration, and homeostasis. To address these challenges, computational approaches have played a crucial role in advancing our understanding of bone biology and improving therapeutic strategies. This review explores these contributions across three main areas: (1) elucidating the structural organization and interactions within the bone matrix, particularly between collagen and hydroxyapatite; (2) investigating the regulatory roles of non-collagenous proteins, such as bone morphogenetic proteins, osteocalcin, osteopontin, and fibronectin, in bone mineralization; and (3) facilitating drug discovery and development for bone regeneration by targeting key pathways and molecules, including sclerostin, RANKL, and estrogen receptors. Molecular dynamics and docking have helped identify and optimize natural and synthetic therapeutic agents for these critical pathways. Additionally, we apply bioinformatics tools to analyze bone regeneration and degeneration pathways, emphasizing the need for more accurate computational techniques to reconstruct their interactome. As these techniques continue to evolve, integrating advancements in machine learning, molecular dynamics, and multi-scale modeling, their potential to bridge the gap between experimental research and clinical application is becoming increasingly apparent. A multidisciplinary approach that combines computational predictions with experimental validation and clinical data is poised to drive the development of personalized and effective osteoporosis therapies, ultimately reducing the global burden of this debilitating disease. Introduction

Bone is a dynamic and complex tissue that serves multiple essential functions, including mechanical support, protection of vital organs, harboring of bone marrow, and storage of calcium and phosphate [1]. Structurally, bone comprises a composite matrix primarily of hydroxyapatite (HAP) crystals and organic components like collagen and non-collagenous proteins. The intricate interplay between these components contributes to the mechanical strength, flexibility, and resilience of bone tissue. In this connection, bone homeostasis is regulated through the balanced interplay of osteoclasts and osteoblasts [2]. Osteoclasts initiate the process by resorbing the bone matrix, while osteoblasts are specialized in filling the resorption area with new bone tissue [3]. Maintaining a functional balance between these cells is crucial for bone health. Disruption of this balance can result in dominant osteoclast activity, triggering bone metabolic diseases like osteoporosis.

Bone degenerative (BD) diseases, such as osteoporosis (OP), are progressive skeletal disorders characterized by reduced bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, leading to increased fracture risk [4]. Affecting millions worldwide, OP imposes a significant healthcare burden, particularly among aging populations [5]. The disease arises from an imbalance in bone remodeling, where bone resorption outpaces bone formation. Current therapeutic strategies aim to either reduce bone resorption or stimulate bone formation, and understanding the molecular basis of these processes is critical for advancing treatment options [6]. A wide array of treatments has been developed for osteoporosis, including bisphosphonates, calcitonin, and selective estrogen receptor modulators [6, 7]. Novel approaches targeting proteins like sclerostin, which inhibit bone formation pathways, have also gained traction [8]. While current therapies have improved patient outcomes, it is arguable that their efficacy is limited by a lack of mechanistic understanding at the molecular level. In this context, computational methods offer a powerful platform to bridge this knowledge gap, providing molecular-level insights into bone matrix components and their interactions. This framework is further enhanced by integrating bioinformatics and machine learning, which can identify diagnostic biomarkers and predict osteoporosis risk in patients [9, 10].

This review synthesizes computational research on bone health, unifying three key aspects: (1) the molecular interactions within the bone matrix, particularly between HAP, collagen, and non-collagenous proteins; (2) the role of these proteins in bone remodeling and mineralization; and (3) the computational approaches applied to drug discovery and the treatment of BD diseases such as OP. By integrating these perspectives, we aim to highlight the synergies between fundamental bone biology and computational advancements in drug development. In detail, the review is divided into two parts: the first focuses on computational studies of bone-related proteins and their interactions with HAP, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying bone structure. The second part focuses on computational studies of proteins targeted or involved in BD and OP therapy, including calcitonin, estrogen receptor-targeting compounds, receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand, and sclerostin inhibitors.

By integrating structural biology, computational chemistry, bioinformatics, and therapeutic perspectives, this review offers a comprehensive account of the intersection between computational methods and OP treatment, highlighting emerging opportunities for innovation in this field. The interplay between these computational approaches not only enhances our understanding of bone biology but also accelerates the design of effective, targeted therapies for BD diseases. Through this exploration, the review underscores the significance of computational approaches in not only deciphering molecular interactions but also guiding the development of effective and targeted therapies for BD while providing a framework for future interdisciplinary research in this domain.

Computational methods considered in this review

Computational methods encompass a broad spectrum of theoretical and numerical approaches aimed at describing, predicting, and rationalizing biological phenomena at different spatial and temporal scales. In this review, we focus specifically on atomistic and coarse-grained simulation approaches that have been applied to investigate the structural, dynamical, and mechanistic aspects of bone-related systems. The core computational framework analyzed in this review is molecular dynamics (MD) simulation.

Beyond classical equilibrium MD, this review also covers molecular docking and a range of enhanced sampling techniques that have been employed to overcome the intrinsic time-scale limitations of classical simulations. These approaches mainly include (I) steered molecular dynamics, which applies external forces to probe mechanical properties, binding-unbinding events, and deformation mechanisms of bone matrix components; (II) umbrella sampling, which enables the calculation of free energy profiles along predefined reaction coordinates; and (III) metadynamics, which accelerates rare events and reconstructs free energy landscapes associated with adsorption processes, conformational transitions, and molecular recognition processes. Together, these techniques allow for a quantitative description of the energetics and kinetics underlying protein-mineral and protein-protein interactions, bone mineralization, and drug-target binding.

The scope of this review is intentionally focused on computational studies that are directly connected to bone homeostasis, regeneration, degeneration, and OP. Accordingly, we selected works that address key molecular components of the bone matrix, growth factors involved in osteogenesis, and therapeutic targets relevant to OP treatment. Bone matrix key components: computational methods

The bone matrix, a complex and dynamic composite material, is essential for providing mechanical strength and structural integrity to bone tissue. Its primary components, including collagen, HAP, and non-collagenous proteins, such as bone growth factors, fibronectin, osteocalcin, and osteopontin, work synergistically to maintain bone’s unique mechanical properties. Computational methods, particularly MD simulations and advanced force fields, have become indispensable in studying these interactions at both atomistic and multiscale levels, providing insights into bone matrix organization and behavior. CHARMM-GUI Nanomaterial Modeler offers a versatile framework for constructing inorganic, organic, and biological nanomaterial surfaces and uses the INTERFACE forcefield [11] to further expand the capabilities for simulating protein-HAP interactions [12]. Furthermore, the forcefield for HAP was further enhanced by Lin and Heinz enabling detailed simulations of the conformational and mechanical aspects of bone matrix proteins, such as collagen, in their interactions with HAP [13]. Understanding the interactions and roles of these bone matrix components, including collagen, non-collagenous proteins, and HAP, is crucial for advancing bone biology and developing treatments for bone-related diseases.

MD simulations have proven to be a valuable tool in this endeavor, allowing researchers to explore the atomic-level interactions that govern the behavior of bone-related proteins and compounds. MD simulations offer insights into the structure, function, and interaction of biomolecules, revealing how changes at the molecular scale can lead to macroscopic effects such as bone weakening or enhanced regeneration. Collagen proteins in the extracellular matrices of bones

Collagens represent the most abundant structural family of proteins in bone and play a critical role in providing strength and resilience to the extracellular matrix. Among them, Collagen Type I is the most prevalent in bone tissue, accounting for approximately 95% of the total collagen content [14, 15]. Other collagen types, including III and V, are also present in bone at low levels and appear to regulate the diameter of type I collagen fibrils [14, 15]. In bone, the interaction between HAP and collagens plays a pivotal role in determining its mechanical and structural properties. Molecular simulations and experimental studies have shown that the presence of HAP influences the mechanical response of collagen, providing bone with its characteristic resilience. Early computational investigations elucidated the load-deformation behavior of collagen, revealing how HAP modulates tropocollagen-water interactions and overall mechanical response [16, 17]. Subsequent studies further delineated key influencing factors such as strain rate, pulling direction, mineral surface characteristics, collagen length, and fibril geometry on deformation mechanisms and energy dissipation [18–20]. A subsequent combined experimental and computational work revealed that the mechanical properties of bone are not solely dependent on these three components (HAP, collagen, and water) but also on their specific arrangement at molecular level [21].

Furthermore, Almora-Barrios and De Leeuw achieved a key breakthrough in understanding the HAP nucleation and cluster formation of calcium phosphate at a collagen molecule in a water environment [22]. These findings suggest that the molecular mechanism of HAP nucleation at collagen templates is a complex process, and the HAP-collagen interface plays a crucial role in early bone mineralization stages. A later study highlighted that hydroxy ions are electrostatically attracted to calcium ions, with distances between them closely resembling those in HAP [23]. This interaction is thought to play a crucial role in the nucleation process of HAP. Shortly thereafter, Xu et al. provided direct computational evidence of intrafibrillar Ca-P nucleation within the collagen matrix, highlighting the essential role of charged amino acid side chains in the nucleation process [24]. In this context, Xue et al. also confirmed the formation of Ca − P clusters on the collagen surface, particularly around charged residues, with a notable occurrence when the Glu − Arg pair is present in the same region [25]. Moreover, they also highlighted that the HAP nucleation process on collagen fibrils and HAP cluster size is strongly influenced by the interplay of PO4−3, HPO4−2, H2PO4−1, HCO3−, and CO3−2 ions [26, 27].

Building on these insights into nucleation and cluster formation, subsequent studies have delved into how mineralized collagen structures leverage these molecular interactions to achieve enhanced mechanical properties under varying mineral densities and environmental conditions. Qin et al. emphasized the effect of geometry, various crystal surfaces, and hydration on the mechanical properties of collagen-HAP interfaces from an atomistic perspective [28]. They found that the presence of HAP significantly increases both the tensile modulus and strength compared to a tropocollagen molecule alone [28]. Nair et al. explored how mineralized collagen protein matrix modulates its mechanical characteristics under tensile loading at various mineral densities [29, 30]. The results of these studies indicate that as mineral density increases, the tensile modulus of the network rises steadily, surpassing that of pure collagen fibrils [29, 30]. Furthermore, studies of mineralized collagen fibrils by Milazzo et al. provide further detail on how bone handles transient loads, with findings pointing to a molecular basis for viscoelasticity within the fibrils, which helps buffer impacts and mechanical stresses [31, 32].

Recent MD studies have added more insight, with Alcântara et al. exploring how intrafibrillar and extrafibrillar HAP further strengthens collagen fibrils by reinforcing the mineral-collagen network, contributing to the development of nanoscale bone models that mirror these complex interactions [33, 34]. Tavakol and Vaughan also examined how different mineralization patterns affect bone biomechanics, concluding that the spatial distribution of HAP is as critical as its presence for overall structural integrity [35, 36]. In detail, their findings showed that the mineral phase restricts collagen sliding during deformation, enhancing tensile strength and distinguishing the roles of mineralized protofibrils in load-bearing from non-mineralized protofibrils in crack deflection within mineralized collagen fibrils [35, 36]. Lastly, Huang et al. and Liu et al. have studied the biomineralization process, where collagen not only provides a scaffold for HA deposition but also regulates mineral growth, offering a controlled mineralization environment that enhances bone’s mechanical behavior at the molecular level [37, 38]. These collective insights underscore the sophisticated interplay between HAP and collagen in bone, where structural organization at both intra- and extrafibrillar levels defines its mechanical strength and resilience. Non-collagenous proteins in the extracellular matrices of bones

Growth factors for bone tissue regeneration

In the field of bone tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) is the most extensively studied and clinically approved growth factor [39, 40]. This is due to its strong ability to promote stem cell differentiation into new bone tissue, significantly lowering medical costs in clinical practice [41]. These factors have paved the way for MD studies focused on the stability and conformational dynamics of BMP-2. Early advancements in the field were achieved by investigating the BMP-2 monomer and homodimer in both vacuum and water environments [42, 43]. Knowing the factors that influence BMP-2 structure becomes essential for designing bone scaffolds with osteoinductive properties. In this connection, understanding the interactions of BMP-2 and inorganic surfaces at atomistic level is of great significance to the rational design of novel products in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering [44]. Early MD studies focused on characterizing how BMP-2 interacts with HAP and other materials of medical relevance [45–49]. More recently, Gu et al. have significantly advanced this understanding, providing detailed mechanistic insights into interfacial dynamics between BMP-2 and HAP [50].

Building on these findings, Huang et al. extensively investigated the adsorption and desorption dynamics of BMP-2 on various nano-textured HAP surfaces at the atomic level [51], demonstrating that the design of the HAP surface can modulate BMP-2’s adsorption behavior and, thus, its bioactivity [52]. In a similar way, they developed a matrix of magnesium-substituted [53], strontium-substituted [54], and zinc-substituted [55] HAP to provide a theoretical guideline for a high-efficiency and high-bioactivity immobilization of BMP-2 upon engineered HAP surfaces for bone regeneration. While most MD studies have concentrated on HAP due to its pivotal role in bone composition, several other studies have shifted their focus to different biomaterials. For instance, Marquetti et al. investigated the adsorption of a BMP-2 molecule to a graphite [56], silicon dioxide [57], silicon nitride [58], and gold [58] surfaces to provide new insights into the nanoscale interaction of BMP-2 with these substates for medically relevant materials and applications in orthopedic implants and regenerative medicine. A further promising biomedical material is titanium dioxide (TiO2) due to its capability to be modified with various functional groups. Recent studies have explored the interaction between bone BMP-2 and hydroxyl- or phosphite-grafted TiO2 surfaces [59, 60]. These studies demonstrate that the function of a protein can be controlled by deliberately modifying the material surface, offering insights into the design of new materials through function-oriented surface adjustments [60]. Despite BMP-2 is a cost-effective and efficient growth factor in the field of bone tissue engineering, it is also highly susceptible to premature degradation and requires high doses to achieve significant clinical outcomes, which can lead to serious side effects [41]. In this connection, a recent work discovered a unique strategy to stabilize and sequester recombinant BMP-2 by enhancing its molecular interactions with hyaluronic acid [61]. Thus, while BMP-2 offers substantial benefits in bone tissue engineering, further advancements in material design and protein stabilization are necessary to fully realize its potential in clinical settings.

Building on the MD insights gained from BMP-2, it is also important to explore the properties and potential applications of BMP-7, another key growth factor in bone regenerative medicine. The initial pioneering MD simulation on the adsorption mechanism of BMP-7 to HAP surfaces in aqueous environment highlighted the importance of HAP’s surface ion composition in modulating protein interactions [62]. Another study explored the effects of atomic-level nano-structured HAP surfaces on BMP-7 adsorption. The findings indicated that the nano-structured morphology of HAP significantly affects the interaction strength between BMP-7 and HAP substrates [63]. These results enhance our understanding of BMP-7 adsorption behaviors on nano-structured HAP surfaces and provide theoretical guidance for designing novel bioceramic materials. Indeed, a recent study provided new insights at an atomistic level into how the Ca-P surfaces are affecting the BMP-7 biological properties [64].

Osteocalcin

Transitioning from the collagen-HAP interactions that form the core mechanical backbone of bone, another vital structural protein, osteocalcin, also plays a key role in the extracellular matrix (ECM) of bones. Osteocalcin, a small protein secreted by osteoblasts, is known for its high affinity to HAP and is involved in the mineralization process through its interactions with calcium and phosphate ions on the HAP surface.

Zhao et al. used free energy calculations to investigate osteocalcin’s effect on calcium phosphate ion complex formation, providing insights into its potential to facilitate crystal growth and influence bone strength [65]. Their findings underscore osteocalcin’s role in promoting mineral complex growth, which is crucial for bone formation and mineral density regulation [65]. Alamdari and Pfaendtner further explored this role by examining the adsorption of the α−1 domain of osteocalcin on HAP and titania surfaces, focusing specifically on the impact of glutamate carboxylation, a post-translational modification important for calcium binding [66]. Their study revealed that increased charge density due to glutamate carboxylation enhances osteocalcin’s affinity for both HAP and titania, which may facilitate osteocalcin-mediated bone mineralization [66].

The structural role of osteocalcin in bone biomechanics has also been examined in the context of systemic conditions. Tavakol and Vaughan’s study on bone biomechanics found that osteocalcin contributes significantly to bone’s structural integrity, but its function is impaired in type-2 diabetes, which can alter the mineral-matrix interaction and reduce bone quality [67]. This study showed that in type-2 diabetes, glycation of osteocalcin may occur through covalent crosslinking between arginine and N-terminal regions [67]. This process disrupts α-helices, resulting in reduced protein affinity for the HAP surface. Expanding on the mechanistic understanding of osteocalcin, recent work by Tavakol et al. investigated whether osteocalcin serves as a promoter or inhibitor of HAP growth [68]. They found that osteocalcin’s influence on HAP crystallization is complex and depends on local conditions, suggesting that osteocalcin can act as both a promoter and inhibitor of mineralization depending on the specific molecular environment [68].

These studies collectively highlight osteocalcin’s multifaceted role in bone biology, from facilitating ion complex growth and promoting mineralization to maintaining structural resilience and adapting to metabolic conditions. Fibronectin

Fibronectin is another critical non-collagenous protein in bone’s ECM, playing a pivotal role in regulating bone mineralization and biomechanics. Its presence and structure in the ECM support critical biological functions, including the signaling and mechanical integration of cells with their surrounding matrix, which is essential for tissue development and repair. Fibronectin plays a crucial role in bone tissue through its interactions with HAP, promoting cellular adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, which are essential for bone formation.

The adsorption behavior of fibronectin on HAP surfaces has been extensively investigated, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions. For instance, Shen et al. used molecular simulations to explore protein adsorption on HAP surfaces, offering a foundational understanding of how fibronectin binds to mineral structures, which affects cellular signaling and adhesion at the bone matrix level [69]. They found that the charged –COO− and –NH3+ groups are the strongest HAP surface interactors, followed by charged guanidino, neutral amino, and hydroxyl groups [69]. Further exploration by Liao et al. employed computational studies to examine fibronectin binding specifically to HAP surfaces, revealing that adsorption varies with HAP’s crystallographic orientation, suggesting that certain surface properties can enhance protein binding affinity and configuration [70].

Environmental factors affecting fibronectin-HAP interactions have also been examined in recent studies. Basu et al. explored the impact of electric fields on fibronectin adsorption on a HAP surface, finding that electric polarization enhances fibronectin binding to HAP, potentially influencing its orientation and stability on the HAP surface [71]. In a later study, Basu et al. investigated HAP surfaces modified with strontium ions, observing that strontium substitution significantly alters fibronectin’s binding dynamics and affinity for HAP, a finding with implications for developing bioactive bone biomaterials [72]. Within this context, the strontium-doped HAP surfaces were found to better support fibronectin adhesion compared to pure HAP. Together, these studies highlight the complexity of fibronectin-HAP interactions and underscore the importance of surface properties, environmental factors, and ionic modifications in controlling fibronectin behavior on bone surfaces. Osteopontin

Osteopontin, also known as bone sialoprotein I, is a prominent non-collagenous protein in bone’s ECM, contributing to mineralization, mechanical resilience, and cellular interactions essential for bone health. Osteopontin’s phosphorylated structure facilitates its role in binding to HAP and mediating cellular adhesion, a property that supports bone’s dynamic remodeling process. The protein is particularly notable for its influence on mineral surface interactions, especially through calcium and phosphate binding, which play a vital role in bone growth and repair.

The molecular mechanisms of osteopontin in mineralization have been examined extensively. Grohe et al. identified that osteopontin’s phosphor-peptides control calcium oxalate crystal growth by selectively adsorbing on specific crystal faces, indicating a similar face-specific mechanism in HAP nucleation and growth within bones [73]. Building on this, Yang et al. used MD simulations to show that bone sialoprotein peptides promote HAP nucleation, with peptide conformation affecting the efficiency of mineral attachment and growth [74, 75]. The results suggest that the nucleating motif of bone sialoprotein is more likely to facilitate the nucleation of an amorphous calcium phosphate cluster, regardless of its conformation [74, 75].

In exploring the osteopontin-HAP interface’s mechanical properties, several studies have shown that osteopontin contributes significantly to bone’s mechanical behavior by enhancing energy dissipation and resisting deformation under stress. Lai et al. carried out MD simulations to study impact of osteopontin on the mechanical behavior of HAP interfaces, revealing that osteopontin promotes stability and strength at nano-scale constraints, a characteristic critical for supporting bone under high loads [76, 77]. The formation of new bonds between HAP and osteopontin under loading is thought to be a key mechanism contributing to the high fracture resistance seen in bone and other biological materials [76]. Further investigations into osteopontin’s role at the protein-HAP interface showed that osteopontin improves interfacial mechanical resilience in response to tensile and compressive stresses, underscoring its importance in maintaining bone integrity under biomechanical strain [78, 79]. The results suggest that osteopontin residues can bind HAP surfaces, but the surface nanostructure significantly influences interfacial interactions and mechanical response [78]. The HAP layers with a higher number of defects increase the surface roughness but decrease both pulling force and energy dissipation [78].

More recent work by Tavakol and Vaughan highlights osteopontin’s unique role in dissipating energy at HAP interfaces, an essential feature for reducing microdamage and preventing crack propagation in bone structures [80]. This study also demonstrated that individual osteopontin proteins can dissipate substantial energy at HAP interfaces, without the formation of the protein network [80]. These works underscore multifaceted function of osteopontin in bone, from facilitating mineral growth and improving mechanical resilience to enhancing energy dissipation, which collectively contribute to the durability and adaptability of bone under various physiological conditions. Bone degeneration and osteoporosis treatments: computational methods

OP is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and structural deterioration, leading to increased bone fragility and fracture risk [4]. Therapeutic approaches to OP primarily aim to prevent bone loss, promote bone formation, or a combination of both by targeting various molecular pathways implicated in bone metabolism. Advances in computational methods, particularly MD simulations and other in silico approaches, have provided deeper insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying OP treatments, allowing researchers to analyze the structural and dynamic interactions of drugs with key biological targets at an atomic level [81].

This section explores computational studies focused on pivotal drug targets in OP treatment, including the well-established bisphosphonates that inhibit bone resorption by binding to HAP, drugs targeting key proteins for the osteoclast differentiation and function, estrogen receptor modulators that address post-menopausal bone loss, as well as emerging targets such as sclerostin, and others. Bisphosphonates

Bisphosphonates are a class of drugs widely used to treat OP due to their high affinity for HAP, the primary mineral component of bone [87]. Their mechanism of action involves binding to HAP surfaces, thereby inhibiting bone resorption by osteoclasts [88]. Computational studies, particularly MD simulations, have provided detailed insights into how bisphosphonates interact with HAP at a molecular level, offering valuable information on binding affinities, orientation, and stability of bisphosphonate molecules on mineral surfaces.

Chen et al. utilized molecular modeling to investigate the interaction of seven bisphosphonates with the HAP (100) face, a primary crystalline surface in bone minerals. Their findings illustrated distinct binding preferences among bisphosphonates, driven by differences in molecular structure and functional groups, which influence drug efficacy in preventing bone resorption [89]. In a similar vein, Lin applied MD simulations to nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates, demonstrating that nitrogen atoms enhance binding affinity by facilitating stronger electrostatic interactions with the HAP surface [90]. These insights suggest that nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates may offer enhanced therapeutic potential due to their increased binding strength [90]. Further studies by Dong et al. explored the binding behavior of 1-hydroxyethane-1,1-diphosphonic acid (HEDP), a specific bisphosphonate, on nano-HAP surfaces. In the presence of HEDP, both HEDP and Cu2⁺ are adsorbed onto the HAP surface, significantly increasing the Cu2⁺ content, which adds potential value for metal ion regulation in bone remodeling contexts [91].

Calcitonin

Calcitonin, a peptide hormone involved in calcium homeostasis, has been a long-standing therapeutic agent for OP. It reduces bone resorption by directly inhibiting osteoclast activity. Despite its efficacy, challenges such as rapid degradation, aggregation, and short half-life limit its clinical utility. Recent computational and experimental studies have provided molecular insights into overcoming these limitations and enhancing calcitonin’s therapeutic potential.

Cao et al. investigated the development of supramolecular nanoparticles composed of calcitonin and dipeptides for long-term controlled release. Their findings demonstrated that these nanoparticles could significantly enhance the stability and sustained release of calcitonin, providing prolonged therapeutic effects [92]. Similarly, Liu et al. designed a hexapeptide-conjugated calcitonin system for targeted OP therapy. This approach enabled targeted delivery to bone tissues while reducing systemic side effects, highlighting the potential of molecular conjugation strategies for improving calcitonin efficacy [93].

Paul and Paul used MD simulations to explore the self-assembly mechanisms of human calcitonin, offering critical insights into its aggregation behavior. Their work revealed specific molecular interactions and conformational changes that drive aggregation through specific mutations, which could inform the design of more stable calcitonin formulations [94]. Complementing these efforts, Lee focused on designing high-affinity calcitonin analog fragments targeting calcitonin family receptors, uncovering novel therapeutic strategies for next-generation peptide agonists that enhance receptor-binding efficiency [95].

Recent studies have also delved into the effects of amino acid substitutions on calcitonin’s structure and aggregation dynamics. Huang et al. used MD simulations to study the impact of F16L and F19L substitutions on human calcitonin, finding that these modifications enhanced conformational stability and reduced aggregation propensity [96]. Therefore, these mutations emerge as an important target for future amyloid-resistant human calcitonin analogue design [96]. Similarly, Yan et al. investigated Y12L and N17H substitutions, demonstrating their influence on calcitonin’s oligomerization behavior and potential implications for drug design [97]. These mutations stabilize the α-helices formation in specific regions that disrupt the aromatic interactions responsible for the calcitonin’s aggregation [97]. These substitution strategies offer promising avenues for developing next-generation calcitonin therapies with improved stability and reduced side effects.

Sclerostin

Sclerostin, a glycoprotein primarily expressed by osteocytes, is a potent inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is crucial for bone formation. By antagonizing this pathway, sclerostin plays a pivotal role in regulating bone homeostasis, making it a prime therapeutic target for OP treatment. Computational studies have significantly contributed to identifying and optimizing sclerostin inhibitors, leveraging both structure-based approaches and virtual screening.

Yooin et al. conducted an in-depth analysis of 29 plant-derived compounds as potential sclerostin inhibitors. Their work revealed that baicalin interacts with residues outside the canonical Pro-Asn-Ala-Ile-Gly motif in the Loop2 region of sclerostin, providing novel insights into alternative binding sites for therapeutic intervention [98]. This study emphasized the potential of baicalin as promising inhibitor of sclerostin for innovative anti-sclerostin therapies [98]. Recently, Muniyasamy and Manjubala identified flavonoids from Oroxylum indicum as potential sclerostin inhibitors using a combined in silico approach [99]. Interestingly, among the identified candidates, baicalein was highlighted for its high binding affinity to sclerostin, further underscoring its therapeutic potential [99]. In subsequent study, they elucidated the anti-sclerostin mechanism of baicalein, demonstrating its interaction with the LRP6-sclerostin complex in the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the pathway’s osteogenic potential [100]. Adhish and Manjubala further contributed to the field by examining the potential modulatory effects of taurine on sclerostin using an in silico approach [101]. Their findings suggested that taurine may inhibit sclerostin, offering a novel angle for therapeutic development. This study also discovered seven taurine analogues with enhanced pharmacological activity compared to the parent compound through screening techniques [101]. Additionally, the same authors explored the impact of single-point mutations within the GKWWRPS and PNAIG motifs of Loop2 in sclerostin [102]. Their findings suggest that modifications within the GKWWRPS motif could destabilize the PNAIG motif, thereby directly influencing the interactions between sclerostin and LRP6 proteins, opening new avenues for anti-sclerostin therapeutic strategies.

Repurposing FDA-approved drugs for sclerostin inhibition has also garnered interest. Yadalam et al. identified Amphotericin B among several candidates, which target sclerostin and modulate the Wnt pathway to promote alveolar bone formation [103]. Their computational workflow demonstrated the feasibility of repositioning existing drugs, accelerating the path to clinical applications in bone-related diseases [103]. Further advancements were made by Yu et al. who focused on the Loop3 region of sclerostin to identify small-molecule inhibitors. Using structure-based virtual screening, they discovered promising candidates with potential anti-osteoporotic effects. A novel natural product (THFA) was identified as potent sclerostin Loop3-selective inhibitor [104]. Subsequent modification allowed the discovery of an effective and Loop3-selective small molecular inhibitor (AACA), with improved binding affinity compared to the hit THFA [104]. These findings highlight the importance of targeting specific structural motifs within sclerostin for designing effective inhibitors.

Compounds targeting receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)

Receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) is a critical regulator of bone metabolism, primarily driving osteoclast differentiation and activation [105]. By binding to its receptor RANK on osteoclast precursors, RANKL promotes bone resorption, making it a key target in therapies aimed at mitigating osteoclast-mediated bone loss [106]. Computational approaches have provided valuable insights in understanding the molecular mechanisms of RANKL inhibition.

Melagraki et al. identified plant-derived natural products as potential inhibitors of both tumor necrosis factor and RANKL using in silico techniques [107]. Their work highlights the high therapeutic potential of one compound in inhibiting RANKL-dependent osteoclast differentiation [107]. Moreover, Wang et al. provided structural insights into the interaction of zoledronic acid with RANKL through computational simulations, revealing its mechanism in inhibiting osteoclast activity [108]. Their findings revealed a direct interaction between zoledronic acid and RANKL, suggesting that the pharmacological effects of zoledronic acid may be closely associated with RANKL [108]. Zinnia and Islam investigated the role of fenugreek steroidal saponins in inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption by targeting CSF-1R [109]. Their study highlighted how this interaction reduces the RANKL/osteoprotegerin (OPG) ratio, a key factor in osteoclastogenesis, thereby demonstrating the potential of fenugreek saponins as a therapeutic agent for bone health maintenance and OP management [109]. In this context, Munasinghe et al. conducted an extensive atomistic MD study to elucidate the binding interactions between human RANKL and its decoy receptor OPG [110]. By varying the number of OPG molecules bound to the RANKL trimer, they observed that the binding affinity decreases with an increasing number of OPG molecules. This finding suggests a concentration-dependent regulation of osteoclastogenesis, providing deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms governing bone remodeling and potential therapeutic interventions targeting the RANKL/OPG/RANK pathway [110].

Furthermore, Rinotas et al. discovered small-molecule inhibitors of RANKL with a superior therapeutic index using structure-based drug design [111]. Their study emphasized the development of compounds that effectively inhibit RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis in a dose-dependent manner while minimizing off-target effects [111]. Similarly, Jiang et al. identified novel RANKL inhibitors through in silico analysis, presenting potential candidates with high specificity for RANKL and favorable pharmacokinetics and toxicity [112]. Huang et al. further advanced the field by pinpointing a unique binding site on soluble RANKL that could be targeted to inhibit RANK-RANKL interactions [113]. Through MD simulations, they identified a binding site that enables a small molecule to selectively disrupt the interaction between soluble RANKL and RANK, without affecting the membrane-bound RANKL-RANK interaction [113].

Compounds targeting estrogen receptors

Estrogen receptors (ERs) play a central role in bone metabolism, influencing osteoblast and osteoclast activity [114]. The decline in estrogen levels, particularly in postmenopausal women, is a significant contributor to OP. Consequently, compounds targeting ERs have become a focal point in OP treatment research. Recent advancements in computational methods, including molecular docking and MD simulations, have provided valuable insights into the interaction mechanisms of ER-targeting compounds.

Docking studies have been instrumental in identifying potential ER-targeting compounds from various natural sources. Cai et al. combined experimental and molecular docking analyses to assess the estrogen-like activity of compounds in Fructus Psoraleae [115]. Their work revealed strong binding affinities of Fructus Psoraleae phytochemicals, such as isoflavone, to ERs, supporting their anti-OP potential [115]. Similarly, Zhao et al. employed network pharmacology and molecular docking to elucidate the mechanism of Polygonati Rhizoma in treating osteoporosis, identifying key bioactive compounds with high ER binding potential [116]. Chen et al. focused on three di/tripeptides derived from yak bone collagen, identifying bioactive peptides capable of stimulating osteoblast proliferation through ER pathways via in silico screening and docking approaches [117].

Further advancements have been made by integrating docking studies with MD simulations, which provide dynamic insights into ligand-receptor interactions. Li et al. used both techniques to study the therapeutic potential of Erzhiwan, a traditional Chinese medicine, in treating OP. Their findings highlighted the stability and activity of key bioactive compounds in binding to ERs, offering mechanistic clarity [118]. Li et al. also explored the role of Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium against diabetic osteoporosis. By combining network pharmacology, docking, and MD, they identified active compounds with robust and stable interactions with ERs, further validating their therapeutic efficacy [119]. Lastly, Hu et al. investigated the anti-osteoporotic effects of hesperidin, demonstrating its ability to regulate estrogen signaling pathways. Their study integrated experimental and computational approaches to uncover the molecular basis of hesperidin’s activity, underscoring the significance of ER-targeted pathways in OP therapy [120].

Compounds targeting other proteins

In addition to the ER, numerous other proteins serve as critical targets for OP treatment. Recent computational studies have explored compounds that interact with several targets, revealing novel therapeutic opportunities. In this connection, the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) plays a vital role in bone remodeling by regulating calcium homeostasis. Qi et al. identified a novel osteogenic peptide derived from bovine bone collagen hydrolysate through targeted screening [121]. This peptide was shown to enhance osteogenesis through a potential allosteric activation of CaSR [121]. MD simulations and stability analyses provided insights into the peptide’s binding mechanism and structural stability, supporting its potential as a therapeutic agent in osteoporosis.

Qiu et al. discovered a new class of cathepsin K inhibitors from Rhizoma Drynariae [122]. Cathepsin K (CTSK), a protease involved in collagen degradation during bone resorption, is another prominent target for OP treatment [123]. Their study combined molecular docking, MD, and in vitro assays to identify compounds with high binding affinity for CTSK, making them promising candidates for mitigating bone resorption in osteoporosis [122]. Their findings clearly showed that Kushennol F and Sophoraflavone G significantly suppressed osteoclastogenesis and effectively inhibited CTSK-mediated bone resorption [122]. Paramasivam and Perumal further expanded on this target by investigating compounds from Oldenlandia umbellata L., which not only inhibited CTSK but also targeted glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), a regulator of osteoblast differentiation and apoptosis [124]. Their in silico study highlighted the dual-targeting potential of deacetylasperuloside to CTSK and GSK3β, providing a comprehensive strategy to address both bone formation and resorption [124].

Recently, Men et al. identified a potential anti-osteoporotic peptide from Moringa oleifera leaf proteins, using network pharmacology, molecular docking, and MD simulations [125]. This peptide was shown to interact with SRC as potential primary target, thereby reducing osteoclast-mediated bone degradation [125, 126]. Subsequent cellular assays confirmed their potential to enhance bone formation while suppressing resorption [125]. Furthermore, Liu et al. used bioinformatics and MD simulations to analyze the effects of compounds from Jianshen Decoction on MYC protein [127]. Their study elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which quercetin and stigmasterol modulate MYC activity, contributing to improved bone repair and reduced risk of osteoporotic fractures [127]. In addition, Peng et al. discovered novel neo-clerodane derivatives from Ajuga decumbens that inhibit OP by targeting peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ) [128]. These compounds were designed and evaluated using a combination of molecular docking, MD, and biological assays, showcasing their efficacy in promoting bone health. This study marks the first reported instance of natural products exhibiting anti-osteoporotic effects via PPARγ [128].

Lastly, Feng et al. investigated the effects of compounds from Zhibai Dihuang Pill, a traditional Chinese medicine, on AKT1 using network pharmacology and MD simulations [129]. Their study unveiled the molecular mechanisms by which diosgenin inhibits AKT1 phosphorylation, highlighting its potential to enhance osteoblast activity and promote bone health [129]. Similarly, Yu et al. explored the therapeutic potential of berberine for OP, identifying several target proteins including, AKT1, MAPK1, and PTGS2 among other proteins [130]. Their bioinformatics and MD simulation study demonstrated berberine’s ability to modulate these pathways, contributing to improved bone health [130]. Additionally, Wang et al. analyzed compounds from Achyranthis bidentata Radix and Morinda officinalisRadix, identifying also in this case multiple target proteins including the previously cited one [131]. Using network pharmacology and MD simulations, they revealed the molecular mechanisms that may play a therapeutic role by modulating immune-inflammatory pathways, promoting cellular biological processes, and enhancing osteoblast differentiation [131].

Bioinformatics analysis of bone degeneration-related proteins and their interaction network

To map the interactome of BD-related proteins, we built a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network that focuses on OP-related targets, with the methodology detailed as follows. For the construction of the protein interaction network of OPs, the first step involved the collection of all the proteins that are targets of the various OP treatments. The literature mining resulted in 44 proteins, which are presented in Table S5 of the SI, along with their corresponding UniProt IDs [132]. A set of potential PPI combinations was created by combining these IDs with all 20,421 reviewed human UniProt Entries (as of December 2024). From the set potential interactions, the positive interactions were identified based on STRING DB v12.0 [133]. STRING DB is a widely used and comprehensive database for predicting PPIs using experimental data sources, computational predictions, and text mining. Filtering of the STRING DB was performed beforehand by retaining only human PPIs where both interacting proteins are identified by their UniProt ID. Based on these criteria, from the 883,048 potential OP combinations, 416 were identified as positive. An interaction network was created combining these 416 interactions with the 558 positive PPIs of the OP interactors with themselves (also identified based on STRING DB).

The Cytoscape [134] network of these 974 positive PPIs is presented in Fig. 4 and Fig. S1 of the SI. The interaction network was further clustered using the Markov Cluster Algorithm (MCL) [135] with granularity 2.5 and revealed 37 clusters with > 2 interactor nodes (appearing numbered, 1–37, Fig. S1 of the SI). Four of these clusters (Clusters 29, 33, 35, and 37) do not contain any OP-related proteins. The clusters containing the most interactions were Clusters 1 (with 23 nodes), 2 (with 16 nodes), and 3 (with 15 nodes). Despite their magnitude, Clusters 1 and 3 each contained only one OP-related protein: transcription factor Jun (JUN) in Cluster 1 and cAMP-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PRKACA) in Cluster 3, highlighting their central role in OP proteins interaction networks. Discussion

This review underscores the pivotal role of computational methods in advancing our understanding of bone biology and informing the development of effective bone regeneration treatments for BD diseases such as OP. By leveraging techniques such as MD simulations, docking studies, and PPI networks, researchers have gained invaluable insights into the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying bone homeostasis and the effects of various therapeutic interventions. These computational approaches have provided crucial insights into both the collagen-HAP interactions governing bone’s mechanical resilience and the molecular-scale mechanisms of mineral nucleation and growth within the collagen matrix [16–38]. Despite the transformative impact of computational studies on collagen-HAP mechanics, a critical gap persists: how aging alters these interactions at the molecular level. For example, age-related glycation of collagen fibrils, a process implicated in reduced bone toughness, is rarely modeled in MD studies. Recently, Tang et al. have studied through MD simulations the effect of hydroxylysine-O-glycosylation on the structure of type I collagen molecule [158]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has yet examined how collagen glycosylation influences its interaction with HAP. We stress for simulations that incorporate post-translational modifications (e.g., glycation) to unravel their impact on collagen-HAP interfaces, which could directly affect therapies targeting matrix quality in OP. Simultaneously, data-driven machine learning approaches, which have successfully decoded complex glycation patterns in other biological systems [159, 160], offer a complementary strategy to predict the systemic effects of glycosylation on bone matrix quality, thereby enriching the molecular insights from simulation studies.

The destructive impact of glycation extends to other critical bone-related proteins, including osteocalcin and osteopontin. While these proteins play a central role in bone homeostasis, emerging evidence suggests that post-translational modifications, such as glycation, may significantly influence their mechanical properties and biological functions. Tavakol and Vaughan, for the first time, showed that the glycation sequence could negatively affect the energy dissipation behavior of osteopontin, which could have consequences for bone toughness [161]. This finding aligns with prior observations on collagen glycation, further underscoring the need for molecular simulations that explicitly account for these modifications. Interestingly, osteopontin has also been associated with vascular calcification and early ossification mechanisms [162]. Beyond osteopontin, recent studies have also investigated how glycosylation affects osteocalcin, showing that this modification influences bone fragility [163]. In this connection, Tavakol and Vaughan investigated the impact of osteocalcin glycation in the context of type-2 diabetes and demonstrated that glycation disrupts the α-helical structure of the protein, leading to a significant impairment in energy dissipation [67]. From a different perspective, the same authors were the first to determine the structure of the osteocalcin-osteopontin complex and found that it can dissipate significant energy at mineral interfaces [164]. Since glycosylation is known to influence the ability of osteopontin and osteocalcin to interact with HAP, it may also affect their interaction with each other. Therefore, it would be valuable to investigate how glycosylation impacts their binding capacity and its effect on energy dissipation.

An additional class of proteins involved in bone homeostasis is the BMP family, which serves as key growth factors for bone regeneration. MD simulations have provided valuable insights into stability, conformational dynamics, and adsorption behaviors of BMP-2 and BMP-7 on various substrates [42–64]. Despite the extensive focus on BMP-2 and BMP-7 in computational studies, MD simulations remain underutilized in exploring synergistic growth factor combinations, such as BMP-2 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). For instance, simulating the spatially controlled release of these two growth factors can bridge the gap between molecular insights and clinical bone regeneration strategies [165, 166]. MD could be employed to investigate the interactions between BMP-2 and VEGF, providing valuable insights into their co-release dynamics from 3D-printed scaffolds for bone regeneration. Furthermore, BMPs can interact with heparan sulfate (HS), which has been shown to enhance BMP-2-mediated osteogenesis [167–169]. Simulating BMP-2 or BMP-7 in the presence of biomimetic composites composed of HAP and HS could offer deeper insights into their binding dynamics and osteogenic potential. Understanding the interplay between BMPs, HS, and HAP at the molecular level could further optimize bone regeneration strategies by elucidating how these factors synergistically enhance osteogenesis [170].

Computational methods have also significantly impacted on the discovery and development of OP treatments. For instance, bisphosphonates have been studied using MD simulations to investigate their binding mechanisms with HAP [89–91]. Current models offer a foundation for understanding bisphosphonate interactions with HAP but fail to capture the long-term effects on bone quality. A critical area for future research lies in elucidating the mechanisms behind atypical femoral fractures, a clinically observed consequence of prolonged bisphosphonate use that remains unexplored in MD studies [171, 172]. A promising avenue of research to address this challenge is investigating how HAP surfaces modified by bisphosphonates modulate the anchoring capability of collagen fibrils, and how these altered surfaces influence collagen-HAP (functionalized with bisphosphonates) interactions. Integrating such mechanistic insights from MD with machine learning models trained on clinical data, as explored in recent predictive frameworks, could enable the development of preventive tools to forecast the risk of atypical femoral fractures [173–175].

Calcitonin, while effective in inhibiting bone resorption, faces clinical limitations due to rapid degradation and aggregation [176]. Computational studies have addressed these challenges by exploring peptide modifications and amino acid substitutions to enhance calcitonin stability and reduce aggregation [93–97]. In this context, Huang et al. demonstrated that targeting the central region of calcitonin is key to inhibiting its amyloid aggregation propensity [177]. Recent experimental work has provided a novel strategic direction for this optimization. Specifically, phosphorylation within this region resulted in the complete inhibition of calcitonin fibrillation [178]. A further critical finding showed that nitration of TYR12 effectively inhibits the self-assembly of human calcitonin [179]. MD simulations are poised to bridge the gap between these experimental findings on post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation and nitration with the rational design of next-generation calcitonin-analogue therapeutics. This underscores the ability of computational methods to both enhance existing peptide therapeutics and open new avenues for the discovery of agents against previously unexplored targets. In the realm of sclerostin inhibition [98–104], in silico works have identified potential inhibitors, including natural compounds like baicalein [98–100] and repurposed drugs [103], providing novel therapeutic avenues. These compounds target both Loop2 and Loop3 of sclerostin as potential inhibitory strategies [98–104]. However, recent evidence suggests that targeting sclerostin’s Loop3 offers a more advantageous therapeutic profile by preserving its cardiovascular protective action while promoting bone formation [180]. Therefore, future computational studies should prioritize the design and optimization of inhibitors that selectively target sclerostin’s Loop3. Beyond small-molecule and peptide-based inhibition strategies, romosozumab represents a major clinically approved therapeutic outcome of sclerostin-related research [181]. While romosozumab was primarily developed through experimental and clinical studies, recent computational work has extended a bone cell population model of bone remodeling to include a detailed PK–PD description of the drug, accounting for both the anti-anabolic and RANKL-mediated catabolic roles of sclerostin and introducing a local fracture risk estimator to simulate and compare romosozumab-denosumab treatment regimens in postmenopausal osteoporosis [182]. In this broader therapeutic context, RANKL remains a critical target for therapies aimed at reducing bone resorption due to its central role in osteoclast differentiation and activation [105]. Recent advancements through in silico methods in OP treatment include the development of a RANKL-targeting vaccine on the epitope of denosumab, which has demonstrated potential in preventing bone-resorptive diseases by inducing sustained antibody responses [183].

However, targeting ERs remains a cornerstone of OP therapy, particularly for postmenopausal women. Advances in computational techniques, such as docking and MD, have facilitated the identification of ER-binding compounds from natural sources and traditional medicines [115–120]. Beyond these well-established targets, researchers have identified promising opportunities in targeting other proteins involved in bone metabolism. These proteins include key players in bone metabolism, such as CTSK, GSK3β, AKT1, and PPAR-γ [121–131]. In particular, PPAR-γ has been identified as a crucial protein involved in the osteogenic differentiation of human mesenchymal stromal cells [184]. These studies have highlighted the potential of natural compounds and repurposed drugs in treating OP, offering promising avenues for drug discovery and development. While natural compounds and repurposed drugs offer valuable avenues for drug discovery, advancements in nanoplatforms for gene therapy represent another promising frontier in the development of targeted OP therapies. Indeed, the latest advancements in nanoplatforms for siRNA and miRNA delivery have demonstrated significant potential in treating OP by enabling precise modulation of gene expression involved in bone remodeling [185].

While computational and experimental studies have advanced our understanding of bone degeneration and regeneration, critical gaps persist due to the limitations of existing PPI identification methods. Traditional approaches, such as yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screens, tandem affinity purification (TAP), and mass spectrometry (MS) often suffer from false positives, false negatives, bias toward well-characterized proteins, and static interaction snapshots that overlook dynamic bone remodeling processes [186, 187]. This study presents a comprehensive interactome mapping of computationally studied OP-related proteins, leveraging PPI networks to unravel key molecular clusters and pathways. For instance, targeting “bottleneck” proteins like β-catenin or TRAF6 (Fig. S1 of the SI), which integrate multiple bone remodeling pathways in osteoclast, may yield dual-action therapies that restore homeostasis in OP [188, 189]. Moreover, the discovery of JUN as central hubs in Clusters 1 (Fig. 4) highlights its regulatory dominance. JUN’s interaction with FOS family members to form the AP-1 transcription complex aligns with its established role in osteoclastogenesis and bone remodeling, where RANKL and IFN signaling is controlled by FOS/AP-1 [190, 191]. In addition, the FOS/NFATc1 pathway plays a crucial role in regulating bone resorption and represents a promising therapeutic target for controlling excessive osteoclast differentiation in bone disorders [190, 192]. Despite being a valuable starting point, expanding and refining this interactome data is crucial for strengthening network analysis and gaining deeper insights into bone disorders.

As shown in the present work, existing BD markers are not tissue-specific, making it difficult to design targeted drugs and therapeutic methods. A further analysis on the BD markers and transcription factors is shown in SI – Section S2. The identification of new molecules and regulatory elements in BD diseases, along with a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms, can be obtained by high-throughput data at the single-cell level. Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) have revolutionized our understanding of cellular heterogeneity and communication in bone homeostasis [193, 194]. Studies using these methods have successfully mapped cell–cell communication pathways and co-localization patterns, particularly between osteoblastic lineage cells and other cell types [194, 195]. When combined with MD analysis, this approach can facilitate the testing of existing compounds targeting key proteins and support the rational design of novel agents aimed at modulating these interactions. This integrative methodology will not only enhance our mechanistic understanding of bone regeneration but will also accelerate the identification of innovative therapeutic strategies to address BD disorders.

Conclusions

In conclusion, computational methods have emerged as indispensable tools in advancing our understanding of bone biology and developing effective treatments for BD diseases, such as OP. The convergence of MD, molecular docking, bioinformatics, and network analysis with molecular biology and therapeutic research has paved the way toward a more comprehensive understanding of bone health and the development of effective strategies for preventing and treating bone-related diseases. These approaches not only enable the study of molecular interactions and structural properties but also allow for the identification of novel therapeutic targets and the optimization of drug candidates with higher specificity and efficacy.

By providing deep insights into molecular mechanisms, predicting drug interactions, molecular targets, and accelerating drug discovery, computational methods offer significant promise for improving the lives of individuals affected by this debilitating disease. However, several challenges remain, including better understanding on glycation effect on bone-related proteins, better integration of multi-scale modeling approaches, expanding and refining bone interactome, and improved validation of in silico predictions through experimental and clinical studies. The complexity of bone remodeling and drug interactions requires continuous refinement of computational techniques to bridge the gap between theoretical models and real-world applications. Continued advancements in computational techniques, coupled with a multidisciplinary approach that integrates experimental and clinical data, will be crucial for translating these findings into impactful clinical therapies.
