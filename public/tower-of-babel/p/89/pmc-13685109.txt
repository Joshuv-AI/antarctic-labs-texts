Misophonia in adolescents: clinical correlates and associations with autism-related behavioral characteristics within a clinical context
Authors: Rahime Duygu Temeltürk, Yağmur Özgür Karabiyikioğlu, Kübra Dilara Aynigül, Ezgi Kurtuldu Çeti̇ner, Esra Yürümez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMisophonia is characterized by intense emotional and behavioral reactions to specific sounds and has been associated with psychiatric symptoms and sensory sensitivity. This study investigated the prevalence of misophonia in a clinical adolescent outpatient sample and examined its associations with psychiatric symptoms and autism-related behavioral characteristics.MethodsThe study included 200 adolescents aged 12–18 years who were referred to a child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic. Psychiatric diagnoses were assessed using the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children—Present and Lifetime Version for DSM-5. Misophonia severity was evaluated with the Amsterdam Misophonia Scale–Revised, while emotional-behavioral problems and autism-related behavioral characteristics were assessed using the Youth Self-Report/11–18. Group comparisons were performed using the chi-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, and the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman’s correlation analyses and hierarchical logistic regression analyses were conducted.ResultsAdolescents with misophonia had higher rates of major depressive disorder. They also had significantly higher internalizing and externalizing symptoms and elevated autism-related behavioral characteristics than those without misophonia (all p < 0.001; Z = -5.62, -4.59, and − 5.70, respectively).Hierarchical logistic regression analysis revealed that higher internalizing problem scores independently predicted the presence of misophonia (Nagelkerke R² = 0.226, p < 0.001; OR = 1.07).ConclusionsMisophonia in adolescents appears to be closely associated with broader internalizing psychopathology and emotional-behavioral difficulties within psychiatric outpatient settings. Elevated autism-related behavioral characteristics in adolescents with misophonia support further research into the relationship between misophonia and neurodevelopmental characteristics. Such research may contribute to improving clinical assessment and understanding of behavioral patterns associated with misophonia.However, study limitations and inconsistencies in the literature highlight the need for further research in this area.Keywords: Misophonia, Adolescent, Autism-related behavioral characteristics, Psychiatric symptom, Clinical population

Introduction

Misophonia is conceptualized as a complex neurophysiological and behavioral syndrome, characterized by heightened autonomic arousal and unpleasant emotional responses to specific auditory stimuli [1, 2]. In misophonia, triggering stimuli are most commonly repetitive, socially mediated sounds generated by others, including eating sounds (i.e., chewing, smacking), nasal sounds (i.e., sniffling, breathing), and repeating clicking sounds (i.e., pen clicking, and finger tapping) [3–5]. Emotional responses of individuals with misophonia are predominantly characterized by experiences of anger, distress, anxiety, disgust, hatred, and a perceived loss of control [6–8] Progressively, these emotional and behavioral reactions may foster interpersonal difficulties and social withdrawal, which, in turn, can lead to functional impairments within family, academic, and social contexts [8–10].

Notwithstanding the substantial increase in research on misophonia in recent years, it remains a relatively novel and only recently recognized condition [3]. While some scholars have suggested that misophonia warrants classification as a psychiatric disorder [6], it has not yet been formally included within diagnostic classification systems [11, 12]. According to the specific diagnostic criteria proposed as the AMC (Amsterdam Medical Center) [6] and later expanded as the Amsterdam UMC 2020 criteria [9], exposure or anticipation of certain human-generated sounds (e.g., eating, breathing) triggers involuntary negative reactions, typically beginning with discomfort or disgust and often leading to anger, sometimes causing a sense of loss of control; the individual recognizes these reactions as excessive, tends to avoid triggers, and experiences significant distress or functional impairment in daily life [9].

Reported rates of misophonia in previous studies vary widely, ranging from 12% to 40% [5, 13, 14]. This considerable variability may reflect the differences in sample characteristics (e.g., community vs. clinical populations), age groups (adolescents vs. adults), and the instruments used to measure misophonia symptoms.

Although misophonia is recognized as a distinct clinical condition with specific underlying neurophysiological mechanisms, it exhibits a high degree of comorbidity with certain psychiatric symptoms [6, 7, 15, 16]. Accumulating evidence further suggests that conditions such as major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), anxiety disorders, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) occur at higher rates among individuals affected by misophonia [2, 8, 17].

Altered sensitivity to sensory stimuli—a core feature of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [11] raises the question of whether misophonia may represent a related manifestation. It has been reported that misophonia symptoms were more severe in individuals with autism than in those without [18]. Concurrently, studies have reported a positive association between misophonia severity and autism-related characteristics, suggesting potential overlap between the two conditions [9, 14, 19, 20]. Conversely, some findings indicate a negative correlation between autistic trait levels and misophonia symptoms [21], and Jager et al. [9] found no clear evidence of a distinct association, highlighting the need for further investigation into the relationship between misophonia and autistic characteristics. These heterogeneous findings may be attributable to substantial variability in the assessment of autism-related characteristics across studies. Previous research has used diverse methodologies, including self-report measures, parent-report questionnaires, clinical diagnoses, and behavioral screening profiles, all of which differ considerably in their conceptualization and measurement of autistic features. In addition, autism assessment tools and diagnostic approaches have evolved substantially in recent years, potentially limiting direct comparability between earlier and more recent studies.

Given these inconsistencies and methodological differences in the literature, this study aimed to further investigate the relationship between misophonia and autism-related behavioral characteristics in a clinical adolescent population. Specifically, the study examined the frequency of misophonia and, among adolescents with misophonia, the co-occurrence of psychiatric disorders, broader problem behaviors, and autism-related behavioral characteristics, relative to a control group without misophonia. The first objective was to determine the rate of misophonia within a clinical outpatient adolescent sample and to characterize the associated sensory triggers and emotional responses. The second objective was to identify psychiatric disorders that commonly co-occur with misophonia. The third objective was to examine internalizing and externalizing problems and autism-related behavioral characteristics potentially associated with misophonia. Finally, the study sought to identify potential predictors of misophonia within the clinical adolescent outpatient sample.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

The study included 200 adolescents (77 boys and 123 girls) aged 12–18 years who attended a child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic between April 2024 and April 2025. Inclusion criteria for the study were: age between 12 and 18 years; cognitive functioning within the clinically normal range and/or an IQ score above 80. Participants with a previously established diagnosis of ASD, psychotic disorder, or neurological disease were excluded from the study. Psychiatric diagnoses, including ASD, were evaluated through clinical psychiatric assessments, structured interviews, and review of available medical records. However, no autism-specific standardized screening instrument was used. Exclusion criteria also included the presence of comorbid medical conditions (e.g., otolaryngological disorders), uncorrected hearing or visual impairments, and lack of willingness to participate in the study.

After obtaining written informed consent from the parents or legal guardians of all participants, and written assent from all participating adolescents, the sociodemographic data form (SDF), developed by the authors, was administered to them during structured psychiatric interviews.

Psychiatric assessments were conducted by experienced child and adolescent psychiatrists certified to administer the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children—Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL-DSM-5). The presence and severity of misophonia were assessed using the Amsterdam Misophonia Scale—Revised (AMISOS-R). Participants completed the Youth Self-Report/11–18(YSR/11–18) to evaluate psychiatric problems and autism-related behavioral characteristics. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Ankara University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No. İ02-182-24; March 21, 2024) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Instruments

The SDF was administered to parents to collect sociodemographic information, including age, medical history, and family background. Socioeconomic status (SES) was determined with the Hollingshead-Redlich Scale (HRS) and categorized as low (≤ 22), medium (23–44), or high (≥ 45) [22].

The K-SADS-PL-DSM-5, a semi-structured interview, is widely used for the assessment of psychiatric symptoms and the diagnosis of psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents aged 6–17 years, in accordance with DSM-5 criteria [23]. Ünal et al. demonstrated that the instrument possesses satisfactory validity and reliability for use in Turkish populations, a test–retest reliability of 0.63–0.82 and an internal consistency coefficient of 0.67-1 [24].

The YSR/11–18 offers a standardized evaluation of problem behaviors in adolescents aged 11–18 years, based on self-reported data [25]. It incorporates a competency subscale assessing the adolescent’s activity level, social functioning, and academic performance. The instrument comprises 112 items measuring problem behaviors, each rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0–1–2). Problem-behavior items are categorized into the following syndrome subscales: anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behaviors, and aggressive behaviors. These subscales constitute the broadband scales for internalizing problems, externalizing problems, and total problems. Psychometric evaluation of the Turkish version was conducted by Erol and colleagues [26], reporting a test–retest reliability of 0.82 and an internal consistency coefficient of 0.89 for the total problem scores. In our study, autism-related behavioral characteristics were calculated using the sum of the YSR “Withdrawn/Depressed,” “Thought Problems,” and “Social Problems” T-scores, adapting an approach previously used in neurodevelopmental research based on the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) [27]. Although similar composite profiles have been investigated using the CBCL, to our knowledge, no comparable validation study currently exists for a YSR-derived composite. Furthermore, this composite was not intended to measure autistic traits or to serve as a proxy for an ASD diagnosis. Rather, it was used as an exploratory index of autism-related behavioral characteristics and should therefore not be interpreted as a diagnostic or validated measure of autism or autistic traits.

The AMISOS-R is a self-administered instrument developed to evaluate the presence and intensity of symptoms triggered by specific auditory stimuli [6]. The auditory stimuli encompassed a range of sounds, including eating sounds (chewing, smacking, slurping, and swallowing), nasal sounds (sniffling, breathing in, breathing out), throat sounds (harrumphing and coughing), a specific phonetic sound (‘k’ sound), repetitive clicking sounds (nails on a blackboard, pen clicking), crinkling sounds (paper or plastic), and ambient noises (clock ticking noise or similar devices). Following participants’ reports of the specific sounds to which they are sensitive and of the emotional responses elicited by these sounds, a 10-item rating scale is administered. Each item is scored on a 0–4 point scale, yielding a maximum possible total score of 40. Based on the total score, misophonia severity is categorized as follows: normal/subclinical (0–10 points), mild (11–20 points), moderate (21–30 points), and severe (31–40 points). The adaptation and validation study conducted in a Turkish adolescent population indicates that the AMISOS-R demonstrates robust psychometric properties, exhibiting high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) and strong test–retest reliability (r = 0.89) [27]. For the purposes of group classification, participants were categorized according to their AMISOS-R total scores. Individuals with normal/subclinical scores were classified as Misophonia (−), whereas those with mild, moderate, or severe scores were classified as Misophonia (+).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 30.0, with a significance level set at p = 0.05. Descriptive data were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. Since the assumption of normality was not met for continuous variables, comparisons between the two groups (Misophonia + and Misophonia−) were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlations among psychiatric symptoms, autism-related behavioral characteristics, and misophonia severity scores were assessed using Spearman’s correlation analysis.

To investigate predictors of the presence of misophonia, a binary logistic regression model was applied. A hierarchical logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine predictors of misophonia.

Sociodemographic variables were entered in Block 1, psychiatric diagnostic variables in Block 2, and dimensional psychopathology variables in Block 3. Sex was coded as a binary variable (male = 0, female = 1). Predictor selection for the hierarchical logistic regression model was based on theoretical relevance, prior literature, and statistically significant between-group differences identified in univariate analyses.

Only variables meeting these predefined criteria were included in the model. Variables were entered using the enter method, and no elimination procedure was applied. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable tolerance values (0.517–0.870) and variance inflation factor (VIF) values (1.149–1.934), indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants

Among the total sample of 200 participants, 123 (61.5%) were female and 77 (38.5%) were male, with a mean age of 14.56 ± 1.57 years. Analysis of clinical characteristics indicated that 192 participants (96%) had been diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder. Among the 200 participants, 95 (47.5%) had at least two psychiatric disorders, and 25 (12.5%) had three or more. The frequencies of psychiatric disorders were as follows: ADHD in 108 participants (54%), MDD in 76 participants (38%), anxiety disorders in 64 participants (32%), specific learning disorder (SLD) in 25 participants (12.5%), OCD in 12 participants (6%), conduct disorder in 8 participants (4%), communication disorders (including language, speech, and social communication disorders) in 6 participants (3%), and eating disorders in 5 participants (2.5%).

According to the AMISOS-R assessment, misophonia was identified in 94 participants (47%), of whom 60 were classified as having mild misophonia and 34 as having moderate-to-severe misophonia. The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants were detailed in Table 1.

Examination of the sensitive sound types assessed using the AMISOS-R indicated that, across the whole sample, eating sounds (56.0%) and repeating clicking sounds (26.0%) were the most frequently reported triggers. In the Misophonia (+) group, eating sounds (73.4%) were the most common triggers, followed by ambient noises (53.2%) and repeating clicking sounds (50.0%) (Fig. 1).

Sensitive sound types in the whole sample and the Misophonia (+) group

In both the whole sample and the misophonia (+) group, emotional responses to sensitive sounds were most frequently characterized by anger, followed by irritation, and least frequently by disgust. In the Misophonia (+) group, anger (78.7%) was the most frequent emotional response, followed by irritation (64.9%) and disgust (45.7%) (Fig. 2).

Emotional responses to trigger sounds in the whole sample and the Misophonia (+) group

When the total sample was divided into two groups—Misophonia (+) (n = 94) and Misophonia (–) (n = 106)—a significantly higher proportion of females was observed in the Misophonia (+) group (72.3%) than in the Misophonia (–) group (p = 0.004, χ² = 8.80, chi-squared test). Additionally, the rate of familial medical illness was significantly higher in the Misophonia (+) group (23.4%) than in the Misophonia (–) group (10.4%) (p = 0.013, χ² = 6.14, chi-squared test). However, the two groups did not differ significantly with regard to familial psychopathology, socioeconomic status, or family structure (all p > 0.05, chi-squared test).

Psychiatric disorders, behavioral problems and autism-related behavioral characteristics of the participants

When comparing the presence of psychiatric disorders between Misophonia (+) and Misophonia (-) groups using the K-SADS-PL-DSM-5 interview, a significant difference in the prevalence of MDD was observed. Rates of MDD were significantly higher in the Misophonia (+) group than in the Misophonia (-) group. Although rates of specific learning disorder (SLD), obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and eating disorders were higher in the Misophonia (+) group than in the Misophonia (-) group, these differences did not reach statistical significance. In terms of MDD diagnosis, the rate was significantly higher in the Misophonia (+) group (47.9%) than in the Misophonia (-) group (29.2%); χ²(1) = 7.34, p = 0.007, with a small-to-moderate effect size (φ = 0.19) (see Table 2).

Prediction of misophonia among the participants

A hierarchical binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine predictors of the presence of misophonia. In Block 1, sex significantly predicted misophonia status, χ² (1) = 8.92, p = 0.003, explaining 5.8% of the variance (Nagelkerke R² = 0.058). Female sex was associated with a lower likelihood of misophonia (OR = 0.41, 95% CI [0.23, 0.75]) (Table 4).

In Block 2, the MDD diagnosis was added to the model, which significantly improved model fit (Δχ² (1) = 3.94, p = 0.047). The overall model remained significant (χ² (2) = 12.86, p = 0.002), explaining 8.3% of the variance (Nagelkerke R² = 0.083).

In Block 3, YSR-derived dimensional psychopathology variables (internalizing and externalizing problems) were entered. This block significantly improved the model (Δχ² (2) = 24.26, p < 0.001), and the final model was significant (χ² (4) = 37.12, p < 0.001), explaining 22.6% of the variance in the presence of misophonia (Nagelkerke R² = 0.226).

In the final model, higher internalizing problem scores independently predicted the presence of misophonia (OR = 1.07, 95% CI [1.03, 1.11], p < 0.001), whereas sex, MDD diagnosis, and externalizing problems were not significant independent predictors. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated a good model fit: χ² (8) = 6.94, p = 0.543.

Discussion

This study examined a clinical sample of adolescents with histories of psychiatric disorders to investigate the frequency of misophonia, the types of auditory stimuli that elicited heightened sensitivity, and the associated emotional responses. We further compared adolescents with and without misophonia on the presence of co-occurring psychiatric disorders, internalizing and externalizing behavioral problems, and levels of autistic-like behavioral symptoms. Finally, we explored sociodemographic and individual predictors of misophonia. The discussion below considers our findings within this framework.

Understanding the frequency of misophonia is critical for elucidating its clinical significance, yet research to date has largely focused on adult and general population samples [14, 28]. Previous studies have documented considerable variability in reported rates of misophonia, with estimates ranging from approximately 3% to 43% [1, 29]. In our study, 47% of adolescents were identified with misophonia, aligning with two recent cross-sectional studies among university students reporting a prevalence of 42–43% [14, 29]. Concurrently, another research conducted among adults at a psychiatric outpatient clinic found that misophonia was present in approximately 40% of participants [13]. In this regard, our results align with contemporary clinical research and indicate that misophonia symptoms may be relatively common within psychiatric outpatient populations.

According to our results, eating-related sounds were the most frequently reported triggers in the Misophonia (+) group, consistent with previous findings [7, 9, 30]. Ambient sounds were subsequently identified as the second most frequent triggers. This finding also aligns with previous research, which similarly identified heightened sensitivity to ambient noises [14]. As noted in that study, cultural factors may partially account for ambient noise sensitivity being the second most common trigger observed in our sample. Indeed, the Misophonia (+) group exhibited significantly greater sensitivity across all sound categories—including nasal, throat, ambient, repetitive, and crinkling sounds—not only to eating-related stimuli. These findings are in concordance with a study that examined three categories of sounds—human-produced oral/nasal, human-produced non-oral/nasal, and non-human/nature sounds—in relation to misophonia, demonstrating significant associations across all categories [31]. Consequently, conceptualizing misophonia solely as a reaction to oral/nasal sounds is insufficient, as the condition evidently extends beyond these specific stimuli. Future research may benefit from tailoring this comprehensive sound database to individuals with diverse misophonic triggers, provided that stimuli from all three source categories are systematically incorporated, as recommended by Schröder et al. (2019).

According to the findings of our study three primary emotional responses to trigger sounds were identified among individuals in the Misophonia (+) group: anger (most frequently reported), irritation, and disgust. Although these results are broadly consistent with previous research, earlier studies have reported irritation and anger at nearly equivalent rates [9]. As expected, the Misophonia (+) group exhibited markedly higher rates of emotional responses compared to the overall sample.

Based on our findings, female adolescents exhibited a greater likelihood of misophonia than their male counterparts, which may be interpreted as evidence of higher rates of misophonia among females.

Although some studies have found no sex differences in rates of misophonia [6, 13], the majority of research indicates that it is more frequently observed in females [7, 28]. These inconsistencies, along with the potentially higher rates observed among females, may be attributed to variations in the ethnic composition of target populations, differing rates of comorbid disorders, sources of participant referral, lack of consensus on diagnostic criteria, absence of comprehensive clinical assessment, and the possibility that males may differ substantially in the expression of distress.

In our clinical sample, participants with misophonia exhibited significantly higher levels of MDD, whereas the rates of OCD and eating disorders did not reach statistical significance. Concurrently, internalizing problems emerged as a statistically significant predictor of misophonia, with moderate overall explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.23). Hence, misophonia may be conceptualized as primarily linked to internalizing disorders and their associated symptomatology. Although ADHD was the most prevalent psychiatric diagnosis in our clinical sample, its frequency did not differ significantly between adolescents with and without misophonia. This finding differs from several previous studies reporting an association between ADHD and misophonia [15, 17, 21]. One possible explanation is that our sample consisted exclusively of adolescents referred to a child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic, where ADHD and other psychiatric disorders were highly prevalent across participants, potentially reducing the discriminative value of ADHD for identifying misophonia [13, 15]. Future studies, including community-based samples and more diverse clinical populations, are needed to further clarify the relationship between ADHD and misophonia.

In the present study, a significant association was observed between MDD and misophonia, consistent with findings reported in previous research [5, 7, 13, 29, 32]. This relationship may be interpreted through multiple, potentially complementary mechanisms. One possibility is that heightened sensitivity to auditory stimuli may be associated with increased depressive symptoms, with specific trigger sounds directly eliciting internal distress. Alternatively, the presence of depressive symptomatology may increase susceptibility to certain sensory inputs, such as specific sounds. For instance, the elevated irritability often observed in MDD may be related to lower tolerance toward aversive sensory stimuli that would otherwise be considered mild or non-aversive [5]. In addition to MDD, obsessive-compulsive symptoms have also been reported to exhibit robust correlations with misophonia [5, 7, 29]. These associations suggest that misophonia may share certain phenomenological features with OCD and MDD, including obsessional thinking, irritability, and avoidance behaviors [6, 7].

However, such commonalities do not imply that these symptoms necessarily arise from a single underlying disorder. Indeed, the comparable strength of correlations across multiple constructs indicates that misophonia is related to a range of psychiatric conditions and cannot currently be attributed to a specific diagnosis [5]. Accordingly, further research and clinical investigations are needed to inform its accurate diagnostic classification.

Kluckow et al. reported a case series in which all three individuals exhibited eating-related auditory triggers that provoked pronounced aversive reactions, accompanied by coping strategies such as eating avoidance or maintaining a full mouth [33]. The authors proposed that misophonia may be associated with the clinical manifestations of eating disorders. Nevertheless, definitive evidence regarding elevated rates of misophonia among individuals with eating disorders remains limited. The co-occurrence of misophonia may instead contribute to the onset, maintenance, and overall clinical complexity of eating-related symptoms, potentially affecting clinical management. Although eating disorder diagnoses did not differ significantly between groups in the present study, the rate of eating disorders was numerically higher among adolescents with misophonia than among those without, consistent with this interpretation.

Notably, although nearly all participants in the sample presented with psychiatric disorders, psychiatric disorders were more prevalent among participants with misophonia. Similarly, a previous study reported that participants in the Misophonia (+) group exhibited higher levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms [13]. Another study revealed that adolescents with misophonia exhibited significantly elevated levels of MDD-related symptoms, generalized and specific forms of anxiety, and OCD symptoms, reflecting heightened internalizing psychopathology [20]. The heightened severity of psychiatric symptoms observed in the Misophonia (+) group may suggest that vulnerability to misophonia is exacerbated with increasing chronicity and severity of psychiatric disorders [13]. Alternatively, our findings align with existing literature, indicating that misophonia is closely associated with psychiatric symptoms and may warrant consideration as a psychiatric disorder [6, 7, 17]. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, the results should be interpreted with caution, as this design limits the ability to draw causal inferences.

Our current results indicate that autism-related behavioral characteristics derived from YSR profiles were markedly elevated in participants with misophonia, consistent with previous findings [14, 19]. This finding may reflect shared sensory hypersensitivities and cognitive patterns associated with autism-related characteristics, which may be associated with increased sensitivity to misophonic responses [20, 34, 35]. Specifically, individuals with higher autistic-like features may exhibit cognitive and sensory characteristics such as heightened attention to detail and increased auditory sensitivity, which may be associated with heightened emotional and behavioral reactions in misophonia [36]. Research indicates that sensory hypersensitivity is common to both conditions [19, 36]. However, it is unclear whether this reflects a distinct sensory phenotype, as similar symptoms can occur across disorders.

Nevertheless, these results support the notion that misophonia may share sensory-processing characteristics observed in some neurodevelopmental conditions [36, 37]. Moreover, understanding the association between autism-related behavioral characteristics and misophonia may help to inform more individualized clinical assessments and interventions and to emphasize the importance of considering sensory reactivity and difficulties with emotional regulation in affected adolescents. However, previous studies evaluating CBCL-derived autism-related profiles have reported only modest discriminative accuracy for ASD, particularly in clinically referred samples with substantial emotional and behavioral difficulties [38]. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as indicating an exploratory association between misophonia and autism-related behavioral characteristics; further studies using autism-specific assessment instruments are needed to clarify this relationship.

Participants with a formal ASD diagnosis were excluded to reduce diagnostic heterogeneity and examine misophonia-related symptoms within a broader adolescent psychiatric sample. However, this approach may have limited representation of individuals with elevated autism-related characteristics and reduced the generalizability of the findings to clinically diagnosed ASD populations. Additionally, previous research has shown that individuals with misophonia often display heightened attention to detail, cognitive inflexibility, and an enhanced ability to mentally reproduce sounds [19, 36, 39]—traits also associated with autistic features. Notably, autism-related features in the present study were not evaluated using gold-standard, autism-specific instruments such as the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) or the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ), nor did the assessment include subdomains such as attention to detail, social interaction, or communication. Consequently, interpretations regarding the specific features driving misophonia remain tentative.

Finally, regression analyses demonstrated that higher internalizing problem scores independently predicted the presence of misophonia. This finding suggests that broader emotional difficulties may play an important role in the clinical manifestation of misophonia in adolescents. Internalizing symptoms such as anxiety, depressed mood, emotional withdrawal, and heightened sensitivity to distress may reduce tolerance for repetitive or aversive auditory stimuli and intensify emotional reactions to triggering sounds.

Previous studies have similarly reported strong associations between misophonia and internalizing psychopathology, particularly depressive, anxiety, emotional dysregulation, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms [5, 7, 13, 20, 30]. Notably, although MDD diagnosis was associated with misophonia at the univariate level in our study, it did not remain an independent predictor after broader internalizing psychopathology was included in the regression model. This finding may indicate that dimensional emotional difficulties, rather than categorical psychiatric diagnoses alone, are more closely related to the clinical expression of misophonia. Overall, these results underscore the importance of comprehensive assessment of internalizing symptoms and emotional regulation difficulties in adolescents presenting with misophonia complaints.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the first to investigate misophonia in a clinical adolescent population, moving beyond the predominance of research conducted in adults and community samples. By systematically examining the frequency of misophonia, psychiatric comorbidities, and associations with autism-like characteristics within a well-characterized outpatient cohort, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature. Importantly, the study provides novel insights into the relationship between autism-related features and misophonia, offering a fresh perspective on the interplay between sensory sensitivity, neurodevelopmental features, and psychiatric symptomatology in adolescence. The main strength of this study lies in its rigorous clinical methodology, including structured diagnostic interviews administered by experienced psychiatrists and validated self-report measures with strong psychometric properties. The relatively large sample size enhances the statistical power and reliability of the findings, while the detailed assessment of both psychiatric comorbidities and autistic-like characteristics enables a nuanced exploration of clinical correlates. Together, these methodological features ensure robust data quality and increase the relevance of the findings for both research and clinical practice.

Despite its contributions, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample primarily consisted of adolescents recruited from a single-center child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader community or multi-center clinical populations. Future research should aim to examine misophonia symptoms in broader, community-based populations. Another important limitation is that autism-related behavioral characteristics were estimated using a YSR-derived composite rather than a dedicated autism assessment instrument. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of clinically validated autistic traits or ASD symptomatology. Although similar autism-related profiles have previously been investigated using the parent-report CBCL, to our knowledge, no comparable validation study exists for a YSR-derived composite of autism-related behavioral characteristics. In addition, previous studies evaluating CBCL-derived autism-related profiles have reported only modest discriminative accuracy for ASD, particularly in clinically referred populations with substantial emotional and behavioral difficulties.

Consequently, the present composite should be considered exploratory, and the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution until further psychometric validation is available. In addition, the YSR-derived autism-related behavioral characteristics composite includes the Withdrawn/Depressed subscale, which also contributes to the YSR Internalizing broadband scale. Consequently, partial construct overlap exists between these variables, which limits how far associations between autism-related behavioral characteristics and internalizing problems can be interpreted as reflecting entirely independent psychopathological dimensions. Future studies should employ standardized autism-specific instruments to clarify the relationship between misophonia and autism-related characteristics.

Beyond these measurement-related limitations, ASD exclusion was based on clinical evaluation and medical records rather than on standardized autism-specific screening instruments. Therefore, undiagnosed ASD characteristics cannot be fully ruled out in the sample. This issue may be particularly relevant for female adolescents, as autism may be underrecognized in this group due to social camouflaging and atypical symptom presentation. Furthermore, medication use and current psychiatric treatment status were not systematically controlled for in the analyses, which may have influenced emotional symptoms and behavioral responses associated with misophonia. Future studies should consider adjusting for psychotropic medication use and ongoing treatment variables. Misophonia symptoms were also assessed exclusively via self-report measures; subsequent studies would benefit from incorporating multi-informant assessments and clinician-administered interviews to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Finally, all data were collected at a single time point, precluding examination of temporal dynamics; future research should employ longitudinal designs to investigate potential changes in misophonia symptoms.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that misophonia symptoms are relatively common within psychiatric outpatient settings, underscoring the need for heightened clinical awareness and consideration of this condition in routine practice. In other words, it is suggested that screening individuals with misophonia for psychiatric disorders, assessing psychiatric patients for misophonia symptoms, and cross-referencing the results may provide valuable insights and contribute to improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Furthermore, higher levels of internalizing symptoms independently predicted misophonia, underscoring the potential role of broader emotional difficulties in its clinical manifestation. In conclusion, autism-related behavioral characteristics were significantly associated with misophonia, highlighting the importance of assessing these features to better understand the cognitive and sensory processes potentially related to the condition. Moreover, clinical approaches considering emotional regulation difficulties, internalizing psychopathology, sensory reactivity, and autism-related behavioral characteristics may contribute to more comprehensive assessment and management strategies for adolescents with misophonia.
