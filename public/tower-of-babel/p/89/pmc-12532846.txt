Distinct inflammatory profiles across migraine states: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Emily Stuchfield-Denby, Bruno Pereira, Damien Bouvier, Radhouane Dallel, Xavier Moisset
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough several meta-analyses have emphasised the role of inflammation in migraine, none has accounted for the diversity of its clinical manifestations. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to provide a comprehensive and reliable assessment of cytokine levels in migraine.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase and Cochrane up to July 4–5, 2024. Original studies providing data on cytokine levels in migraine were included. Non-inclusion criteria covered studies on hemiplegic migraine, controls with tension-type headache, and unclear cytokine identification. The meta-analysis did not cover studies involving less than 10 participants per group or lacking accessible numeral data. Summary data were extracted from each study and pooled using a random-effects model. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated to compare cytokine levels across all relevant biological samples and comparison groups.FindingsFifty-seven studies were included in the narrative review and/or meta-analysis, covering 2850 migraine patients and 2814 controls. In episodic migraine patients versus controls, blood levels of interleukin-6 (SMD 0·36 [95% CI, 0.07; 0·64], P = 0·013, I²=74·0%), transforming growth factor β (SMD 0·68 [95% CI, 0·44; 0·93], P < 0·001, I²=0·0%) and C-X-C motif ligand 8 (narrative review) were elevated. Levels of interleukin-6 (narrative review; SMD 1·40 [95% CI, 0·95; 1·86], P < 0.001, I²=0·0%) and adiponectin (SMD 0·59 [95% CI, 0·19; 0·98], P = 0·003, I²=36·0%) were elevated in chronic versus episodic migraine. Levels of interleukin-1β (SMD 0·53 [95% CI, 0·15; 0·91], P = 0·006, I²=0·0%), interleukin-10 (narrative review) and salivary levels of interleukin-6 (narrative review) were elevated in ictal versus interictal migraine. No differences were found in peripheral blood cytokine levels between migraine with aura and migraine without aura.ConclusionsThis study highlights the association of distinct cytokine profiles with migraine disease (interleukin-6, transforming growth factor-β, C-X-C motif ligand 8), migraine attacks (interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, interleukin-10) and migraine chronification (interleukin-6, adiponectin). Differences in inflammatory profiles across migraine clinical states have significant implications for understanding disease mechanisms, progression, and potential therapeutic interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02136-7.Keywords: Migraine, Inflammation, Cytokines, Chemokines

Background

Migraine is a frequent disorder that affects 14–15% of the population worldwide [1]. While most individuals experience episodic migraine, 2·5% of these migraine sufferers transition to chronic migraine each year [2], characterized by 15 or more headache days per month for more than 3 months, with features of migraine headache on at least 8 days/month (Headache Classification Committee of the International Headache Society, IHS) [3]. Chronic migraine accounts for 5·4 to 9·5% of migraine patients and approximately 1·2% of the general population [4]. Despite significant advances in understanding migraine pathophysiology, the mechanisms involved in migraine triggering and chronification remain unclear. Studies have shown an increased prevalence of migraine in patients with chronic inflammatory diseases [5, 6], and an elevated risk of chronic migraine in certain individuals [7], suggesting potential immune dysregulations in migraine. Consistent with clinical findings, animal studies further support the involvement of inflammatory cytokines in the pathophysiology of migraine. These cytokines, whether released during systemic inflammatory responses or induced by migraine-specific triggers such as nitroglycerin administration or cortical spreading depression, contribute to migraine by activating and sensitizing the trigeminovascular system [8].

In recent years, many human studies have explored the role of cytokines in migraine, revealing altered levels of cytokines in migraine patients compared to controls [9], as well as in chronic migraine [10], migraine with aura [9], and during migraine attacks [11], suggesting that inflammatory processes may be involved in migraine pathophysiology. More recently, systematic reviews and meta-analyses [12–17] have also examined cytokine levels in migraine and controls, and ictal versus interictal migraine. However, other important comparisons such as chronic versus episodic migraine, and migraine with versus without aura have never been considered. In addition, these systematic reviews and meta-analyses frequently combined chronic and episodic migraine populations, age groups and sample types (e.g., serum, plasma, cerebrospinal fluid [CSF], urine), with no systematic analysis of all cytokines, introducing potential bias.

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of rigorously selected studies with appropriate methodology, we aim to provide a comprehensive and reliable assessment of cytokine levels in migraine, across all relevant biological samples and comparison groups.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement [18] and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024531632).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across three databases: PubMed (on 4th July 2024), Embase and Cochrane (on 5th July 2024). For PubMed and Embase, two tailored search algorithms were applied – one designed to retrieve all original studies excluding reviews and meta-analyses, and another specifically aimed at identifying reviews and meta-analyses (Appendix 1). To ensure completeness, the reference lists of all included studies, as well as selected literature reviews and meta-analyses, were manually screened to identify any additional relevant publications.

Study selection

We included all original studies comparing cytokine levels in Human biological samples from subjects with migraine, with no restriction on publication date. Studies were included in our narrative review if they reported cytokine levels as categorical variables – classified as increased, unchanged or decreased. Studies providing numeral data on cytokine levels, as percentage of total cells or as absolute counts, were included in our meta-analysis. Studies were not included if they were book chapters, conference papers, letters, case reports, ongoing clinical trials, reviews or meta-analyses. After assessment, studies were not included if: they were written in neither French nor English, there were less than 10 patients in a study group, migraine patients had hemiplegic migraine, non-migraine controls had tension-type headache, the type of cytokine studied was unclear, or if cytokine values were not given in numbers, as data were not recalculated from graphs. This study selection process was discussed beforehand between E.S.D. (clinician), X.M. (clinician), R.D. (clinician) and B.P. (biostatistician). Systematic reviewing was conducted by E.S.D.; any doubts regarding eligibility or content were discussed with X.M. and B.P. Methods for cytokine measurements were discussed with D.B. (biochemistry biologist).

Data collection and outcomes

Data were extracted to a standardised worksheet. The following items were extracted from each study, where available: study details, participant characteristics, characteristics of migraine, sample type and characteristics, cytokine levels, method used for cytokine measurement, and data relating to Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) items. If missing data prevented the inclusion of a study, we attempted to collect them by contacting the authors. The primary outcome for this review was the levels of cytokines, reported and treated as categorical (qualitative assessment for the narrative review) and/or continuous variables (quantitative assessment). The secondary outcome was the identification of cytokines involved in migraine disorder, a migraine attack, and migraine chronification, according to cytokine levels reported and treated as categorical (qualitative assessment) and/or continuous variables (quantitative assessment).

Risk-of-bias assessment

Studies meeting the inclusion criteria were graded for methodological quality and assessed for internal validity using the SIGN methodology checklists for controlled trials (n°2) [19], case-control (n°4) [20] and cohort studies (n°3) [21].

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses took account of between- and within-study variability. Random-effects models were used to assess standardized mean differences (SMD) and their 95% confidence intervals on cytokines, using DerSimonian and Laird approach. Heterogeneity in the study results was assessed by forest plots and I². Study bias was assessed by funnel plots, confidence intervals, and with the Egger regression test as a formal statistical test. Sensitivity analyses were conducted according to these results. Statistical analyses used Stata software (version 15, StataCorp, College Station, TX, US). Two-sided type I error was fixed at 5%. The widths of confidence intervals were not adjusted for multiplicity and may not be used in place of hypothesis testing. Results were interpreted according to the rules of thumbs with caution due to number of studies and/or number of patients for some studies (extensive methods in Appendix 2). Figures were created using Stata software (version 15, StataCorp, College Station, TX, US) and R (version 4.5.0, R Core Team (2024). _R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing_. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. https://www.R-project.org/).

Analyses

To avoid age bias in cytokine levels [77], our analyses were exclusively performed with studies involving adults and adolescents above 16 years. To avoid confusion regarding differential cytokine levels in central and peripheral blood [78, 79], and possible differences in initiating mechanisms in spontaneous and induced migraine [80], our analyses were based entirely on blood of peripheral origin when blood samples where involved, and considered spontaneous migraine when ictal measurements were addressed. Out of 57 selected studies, 53 were included in our narrative review, among which 39 were also included in our meta-analysis (Table 1).

Migraine patients with versus without aura

Overall, the narrative review found no difference in peripheral blood cytokine levels between migraine patients with versus without aura (eFigure 1). Consistently, the meta-analysis showed no difference in IL-6 and TGF-β peripheral blood levels in these groups (standard mean difference [SMD] 0·05 [95% CI, −0·26; 0·36], p = 0·762, I²=0·30% and SMD 0·07 [95%, CI, −0·30; 0·44], p = 0·720, I²=0·00%, respectively) (eFigure 2).

Interictal episodic migraine patients versus controls

The narrative review demonstrated that TGF-β (two studies) and CXCL8 (three studies) blood levels were consistently increased in interictal episodic migraine patients versus controls (eFigure 3). The meta-analysis revealed that interictal episodic migraine patients had significantly increased peripheral blood levels of IL-6 (SMD 0·36 [95% CI, 0·07; 0·64], P = 0·013, I²=74·0%, nine articles) and TGF-β (SMD 0·68 [95% CI, 0·44; 0·93], P < 0.001, I²=0·0%, two articles) compared to controls (eFigure 4–8).

Chronic versus episodic migraine

The narrative review demonstrated consistently higher blood levels of IL-6 in chronic migraine compared to episodic migraine (three studies) (eFigure 9). The meta-analysis further confirmed elevated IL-6 levels (SMD 1·40 [95% CI, 0·95; 1·86], P < 0.001, I²=0·0%, two articles) and revealed that ADP levels were higher (SMD 0·59 [95% CI, 0·19; 0·98], P = 0·003, I²=36·0%, three articles) in chronic migraine versus episodic migraine (eFigure 10–11).

Ictal versus interictal migraine

The narrative review showed that IL-10 blood levels were consistently increased during versus outside a migraine attack (two studies) (eFigure 12). Similarly, salivary IL-6 levels were consistently increased during a migraine attack (two articles) (eFigure 13). As for the meta-analysis, it showed elevated IL-1β blood levels in ictal versus interictal migraine (SMD 0·53 [95% CI, 0·15; 0·91], P = 0·006, I²=0·00%, two articles) (Fig. 2, eFigs. 14, 15 and 16). Discussion

This study identified distinct cytokine profiles among migraine patients. Cytokine types and levels varied between migraine patients and healthy controls, as well as across migraine subtypes (episodic versus chronic) and phases (ictal versus interictal), but not based on the presence or absence of aura. In interictal episodic migraine patients compared to controls, peripheral blood levels of IL-6, TGF-β, and CXCL8 were elevated. IL-6 and ADP levels were higher in chronic versus episodic migraine. During migraine attacks, peripheral blood levels of IL-1β and IL-10 increased, along with salivary IL-6.

Comparing to the existing literature

Our results on IL-6 were consistent with previous meta-analyses [12, 13, 17], showing elevated levels in migraine patients compared to controls as well as in ictal versus interictal migraine. IL-10 and ADP levels were unchanged in migraine patients versus controls, which was also in line with the literature [12, 17]. While increased TGF-β levels had already been evidenced in recent narrative reviews comparing migraine patients to controls [12, 15, 16], they had not previously been analysed in a meta-analysis. Our meta-analysis confirmed increased TGF-β levels specifically in interictal episodic migraine patients versus controls. In addition, we identified a consistent increase in CXCL8 levels in migraine patients, as also reported by Biscetti et al.; [16] however, available data were insufficient to perform a meta-analysis.

We showed unchanged levels of IL-1β in migraine patients and controls, but increased levels of this cytokine in ictal versus interictal migraine, findings consistent with recent meta-analyses [12, 13]. These results, however, contrasted with those of Geng et al., [17] reporting elevated IL-1β levels in migraine patients versus controls. This discrepancy may be due to the limited number of studies included in their analysis (two studies), compared to the larger dataset used in other reviews, including ours (four to five studies).

TNF-α levels were unchanged in ictal versus interictal migraine, as described in a previous meta-analysis [13]. While previous reviews reported elevated levels of TNF-α in migraine patients versus controls [12, 13, 17], our analysis found no such difference. These discrepancies may be linked to methodological differences, particularly our stricter inclusion criteria. We selected studies including participants with episodic migraine in the interictal period, minimizing bias related to migraine chronification and the inflammatory processes specific to migraine attacks. In contrast, other reviews included studies with mixed populations of episodic and chronic migraine, as well as ictal and interictal periods [12, 13]. In addition, recent meta-analyses combined pediatric and adult data, and mixed central and peripheral blood samples. One study presenting cytokine data solely through graphs was excluded from our analysis to avoid approximating values in the absence of numeral data. Finally, we included studies not considered in previous reviews, the reason for their exclusion from other reviews was not specified.

It can be challenging to compare the results of our meta-analysis with those of previous ones, as differences in findings may arise from variations in search strategies or the inclusion of more recent studies.

Shedding light on the implications of cytokines in migraine pathophysiology

In this study, we identified distinct cytokine profiles associated with different migraine states (migraine disease, migraine attacks, and migraine chronification). No difference was found between migraine with aura and migraine without aura groups, suggesting a pathophysiological continuum between both presentations, also supported by the high prevalence of migraine without aura in patients who experience migraine with aura [81].

IL-6 and TGF-β were associated with migraine disease. Both cytokines are key regulators of T cell differentiation, promoting the development of pro-inflammatory T helper (Th) 17 cells over regulatory T (Treg) cells [82]. Increased IL-6 and TGF-β levels in migraine suggest a Th17/Treg imbalance and an underlying pro-inflammatory tendency, as observed in autoimmune diseases [83]. CXCL8 directs neutrophil recruitment to inflammatory sites [84] and takes part in the pathophysiology of various autoimmune disorders, such as multiple sclerosis and rheumatoid arthritis [85]. Elevated CXCL8 levels in interictal episodic migraineurs further support the existence of an underlying pro-inflammatory state in migraine.

In contrast to other migraine states, this study evidenced that pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 were increased in ictal migraine. Calcitonin gene related peptide (CGRP), which is secreted during a migraine attack, plays a central role in neuroinflammatory processes. IL-1β can induce CGRP release from trigeminal ganglion neurons [86], while CGRP itself can promote sterile “neurogenic inflammation” in migraine by stimulating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [87]. Animal studies have shown that CGRP triggers cytokine release from T cells [88, 89], and it has also been shown to induce IL-1β release from satellite glial cells when injected into the trigeminal ganglion in rats [90]. IL-10’s main role is to suppress excessive inflammatory responses; [91] its increase during a migraine attack may be seen as an attempt to counterbalance IL-1β production and restore homeostasis. Salivary IL-6 levels were also increased in ictal migraine while no elevation was found in blood. Studies have shown better cytokine detection rates in the saliva compared to blood in health and disease, suggesting that saliva may be a more sensitive and less invasive method for cytokine detection and evaluation of systemic inflammation [92, 93].

This study was the first to examine cytokine levels in both chronic and episodic migraine patients, and in other biofluids than blood. We found that IL-6 and ADP were associated with chronic migraine. Among risk factors for migraine chronification, chronic stress and sleep deprivation are associated with increased blood levels of IL-6 [94–96], which, in the context of immune imbalance, may promote central sensitization and increase susceptibility to recurrent migraine attacks, ultimately establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and neural hypersensitivity. The ictal/interictal state of chronic migraine patients at sampling was unspecified in two publications; [10, 74] however, even when drawing blood interictally, the high frequency of attacks in chronic migraine implies that the sampling may occur in close temporal proximity to a migraine attack. Beyond its implication in glucose and lipid metabolism, ADP displays pro- and anti-inflammatory properties, and participates in immune modulation [97]. However, whether increased levels of ADP reflect a primary pro-inflammatory response or regulatory mechanisms remains unclear.

Cytokines having altered blood levels in migraine patients highlight complex interactions between inflammation and the involvement of vascular, smooth muscle, and immune cells in migraine pathophysiology (eTable 4). Elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines may contribute to vascular dysfunction by promoting endothelial activation and increasing smooth muscle reactivity, which could play a role in migraine-associated vascular changes. These mediators also influence immune cell recruitment and activation, perpetuating a cycle of neuroinflammation [98]. This interplay underscores a multifaceted network that may drive both the onset and chronification of migraine symptoms.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this systematic review and meta-analysis lies in its comprehensive assessment of cytokine levels, acknowledging the intricate interplay between inflammation and immunity in migraine pathophysiology. Importantly, all relevant biological fluids were considered and analyzed separately, recognizing that quantification methods and results may vary depending on the fluid examined. To ensure clarity and reliability, studies presenting ambiguous data, such as outcomes shown only in graphical form without numerical values, were excluded.

This review also has several limitations. First, our search method did not include grey literature, articles written in other than English or French could not be assessed, and studies with unclear or incomplete data were not considered, which may have introduced selection bias. Moreover, pre-analytical settings were rarely reported, despite their potential impact on biomarker quantification. Factors such as the time interval between sample collection and freezing, as well as storage temperatures, can affect measurement accuracy [99, 100]. Importantly, for female participants, the menstrual cycle phase during sampling was often unspecified, even though hormonal fluctuations may affect cytokine levels [101]. Finally, as in most meta-analyses, data were pooled from studies employing diverse analytical methods, with ELISA being the most frequently used, despite its lower sensitivity compared to more advanced technologies [102].

Conclusions

The present study indicates that several cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TGF-β, CXCL8 and ADP) may contribute in the pathophysiology of migraine. Distinct cytokine profiles were observed between migraine patients and healthy controls, as well as across migraine subtypes, but not based on aura status. This suggests distinct cytokine-driven mechanisms that are independent of aura. However, further studies are needed to account for confounding factors such as sex, hormonal fluctuations, obesity, and coexisting inflammatory or immune-related conditions. Advancing our understanding of these immunological mechanisms could lead to new treatments, particularly for chronic migraine.
