Wheat's war against stripe rust: Integrating host immunity, genomics and breeding for durable resistance
Authors: Farkhandah Jan, Mukesh Rathore, Dinesh Kumar Saini, Ayushi Singh, Naeela Qureshi, Nikita Aggarwal, Mahpara Bashir, Mehnaz Shakeel, Vikas Gupta, Sundeep Kumar, Rajeev Kumar Varshney, Reyazul Rouf Mir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWheat (Triticum aestivum L.), a foundation of global food security, faces persistent threats from stripe rust caused by Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst). The pathogen thrives in cool and humid environments and regularly causes epidemics that lead to severe yield losses. This review synthesizes current knowledge on stripe rust resistance, covering pathogen biology, host immunity, and recent advances in genetics and genomics, with emphasis on the rapid emergence of new virulent Pst pathotypes that frequently overcome deployed resistance genes. We outline the complexity and evolutionary agility of the Pst genome, which drive recurrent resistance breakdowns. On the host side, we summarize multilayered immune signaling, including pattern recognition receptors, major resistance genes and quantitative trait loci, and downstream defense cascades. Multiomics studies highlight biphasic defense responses and regulatory networks that distinguish resistant and susceptible genotypes. Breeding strategies ranging from classical selection to quantitative trait loci mapping, genome‐wide association studies, marker‐assisted selection, genomic selection, and genome editing are evaluated for their contributions to durable resistance. We also highlight emerging roles of transcription factors, epigenetic regulation, and gene regulatory networks. Advances in genome sequencing, pan genomics, and allele mining are accelerating the discovery of novel resistance sources and expanding understanding of wheat diversity. Finally, we discuss how climate change and ongoing pathogen evolution require integrated disease management and continuous innovation in resistance breeding. Overall, this review connects fundamental biology with applied breeding to support durable stripe rust resistance and sustainable wheat production worldwide.

INTRODUCTION

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most important staple crops worldwide, playing a critical role in global food security and economic stability (L. Huang et al., 2025). Wheat is the most widely cultivated food crop and serves as a key source of plant‐based protein and carbohydrates for billions globally (Shewry & Hey, 2015). In 2023, global wheat production approached 800 million tonnes, underscoring its central role in meeting the food demands of major economies such as China, India, and Russia. Wheat also dominates international agricultural trade, surpassing most other staple crops (Yamini et al., 2025). Its economic significance is further reflected in its support for livelihoods, rural economies, and food supply chains across diverse regions (Ivanov et al., 2022).

Despite its global importance, wheat remains vulnerable to several biotic stresses, notably stripe rust caused by Puccinia striiformis West. f. sp. tritici Eriks. (Pst), which remains particularly destructive (F. Jan et al., 2025). Stripe rust can cause yield losses exceeding 70% during severe epidemics and significantly diminish grain quality by infecting both leaves and spikes of susceptible wheat varieties (X. Chen, 2020). The disease is especially damaging in temperate and subtropical wheat‐growing regions, including North America, South Asia, and Europe. Favorable environmental conditions and the emergence of new virulent pathotypes often accelerate the onset and severity of outbreaks (Priya et al., 2024). The resulting reductions in wheat productivity and grain quality not only jeopardize local and global food security but also impose substantial economic burdens on farmers and the broader agricultural sector (Bouvet, Holdgate, et al., 2022). Over the past few decades, significant efforts have been made to understand host–pathogen interactions and to breed for resistance against stripe rust (Wellings, 2011).

While classical approaches have identified and deployed numerous Yr (yellow rust) resistance genes (Sánchez‐Martín & Keller, 2019), their effectiveness is often short‐lived due to the rapid breakdown of race‐specific resistance driven by the high evolutionary potential of Pst. Adult plant resistance (APR) genes and quantitative resistance loci offer more durable alternatives, but their partial and complex inheritance has often hindered their widespread use in breeding programs (St. Clair, 2010; Hoyos‐Villegas et al., 2022). To overcome these limitations, breeding strategies have evolved from traditional methods such as selection, hybridization, and backcrossing to integrated approaches that harness modern genomics (Akhtar et al., 2023). High‐throughput genotyping, genome‐wide association studies (GWASs), and quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping have accelerated the discovery of novel resistance alleles. Marker‐assisted selection (MAS) has further enabled the precise and efficient deployment of resistance genes/QTLs. Today, multi‐omics platforms, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, are uncovering complex defense mechanisms and regulatory networks (Satrio et al., 2024). Additionally, emerging tools such as pan‐genomics, haplotype‐based breeding, genome editing, and epigenetic regulation are reshaping resistance breeding strategies (Bisht et al., 2023). Integrating these advanced tools with conventional breeding is now seen as essential for achieving durable resistance and sustainable wheat production in the face of evolving disease pressures (Bhavani et al., 2021).

Although several recent reviews have addressed stripe rust resistance in wheat, most have focused on specific aspects rather than offering a comprehensive perspective. Therefore, this review aims to present an integrated overview that unifies pathogen biology, host immune signaling, genomic innovations, and practical breeding strategies, including gene editing and haplotype‐based selection, within the broader context of global epidemiological trends and climate change (Figure 1).

STATUS OF STRIPE RUST SPREAD AND IMPACT

Stripe rust is responsible for significant yield losses worldwide, with annual economic damage from cereal rusts estimated at up to $5 billion (X. Chen, 2020). The disease now affects wheat in over 60 countries and is especially problematic in major producing regions such as China, Pakistan, North America, and parts of Europe (L. Huang et al., 2025). The pathogen thrives in temperate climates with cool, moist conditions, allowing it to persist and spread rapidly across seasons (F. Jan et al., 2025).

Regional hotspots and recent outbreaks

India: Stripe rust has affected wheat in India since the 19th century, with repeated epidemics recorded in the northwestern plains and Himalayan foothills. Major outbreaks in 2008 and 2010–2011 caused severe losses in Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, and Jammu & Kashmir, with damages exceeding ₹2300 million in some seasons (Ahamad, 2021; Leharwan, 2023).

China: The northwest (Qinghai, Gansu, and Ningxia) and southwest (Yunnan, Guizhou, and Sichuan) regions of China serve as primary reservoirs for the stripe rust pathogen (G. Zhan et al., 2022). From these regions, the inoculum spreads eastward to major wheat‐producing areas, including the Huang‐Huai‐Hai Plain, often triggering frequent and severe epidemics (L. Huang et al., 2025). On average, annual yield losses in China can reach around 1 million metric ton. Notably, the 2021 outbreak affected approximately 4.5 million ha and caused substantial yield reductions (J. Zhang et al., 2025).

Pakistan: The Himalayan region, particularly northern Pakistan, is widely recognized as a major center of genetic and virulence diversity and a probable site of origin for Pst (S. Ali et al., 2014). Virulence has even been detected against resistance genes such as Yr5 and Yr24, which are rarely or never deployed in local or global wheat production, underscoring the region's role as a reservoir of globally novel virulence factors. Stripe rust remains a recurring threat in Pakistan, especially in the Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa provinces (M. Ali, 2022), where favorable climatic conditions and extensive wheat cultivation contribute to frequent disease outbreaks (A. Abbas et al., 2023). The 2005 epidemic alone caused an estimated loss of approximately $100 million (Bux et al., 2012).

Central and West Asia: In Central and West Asia, the 2010 epidemic led to yield reductions of 20%–70%, with Turkey alone incurring $10 million in losses (Kavak, 2009).

North America: North America has also experienced recurrent outbreaks, particularly in the US Pacific Northwest, where stripe rust can overwinter and serve as a primary inoculum source (De Wolf et al., 2023; Sharma‐Poudyal et al., 2014). In Mexico, yield losses of up to 60% have been documented (Huerta‐Espino et al., 2020). In Canada, the most severe epidemics occurred in 2010 and 2011, with losses ranging from 2% to 35%, depending on the resistance levels of the wheat varieties grown (Brar et al., 2019). Recent reports indicate expanding outbreaks in states such as Nebraska (X. M. Chen et al., 2010; Sharma‐Poudyal et al., 2014), highlighting the persistent risk to wheat productivity.

Africa and Europe: In Africa and Europe, the emergence of the Warrior strain has driven recent epidemics in the United Kingdom, Ethiopia, and Turkey, with regional yield losses ranging from 10% to 50% (Belay, 2023; Khanfri et al., 2018). The 2010 epidemic was particularly devastating in Syria and Turkey, where up to half of the wheat harvest was lost (Solh et al., 2012), while Ethiopia, Morocco, and Uzbekistan reported losses between 35% and 45% (Belay, 2023). Turkey has experienced several epidemics in recent decades, with crop losses of 10%–30% and national grain losses estimated at 1–2 million tons (Belay, 2023; Solh et al., 2012; Wellings, 2011). In Ethiopia, the 2010 outbreak affected more than 600,000 acres and resulted in approximately $3.2 million in fungicide expenditures (Wendale et al., 2016). In Kenya, the recent detection of the PstS16 race in 2023 has raised serious concerns, as this race has overcome many previously effective resistance genes. PstS16 was first identified in Pakistan and Afghanistan in 2018 and later reported in several European countries, including Ethiopia, Denmark, Sweden, and the United Kingdom in 2020, signaling its potential to compromise wheat cultivars that were once considered resistant (M. Hovmøller et al., 2021). A significant recent development in Europe is the widespread breakdown of the Yr15 resistance gene in the United Kingdom (Davis et al., 2025). Yr15 has long served as a cornerstone of stripe rust defense and has been predominant in many cultivated wheat varieties (Hansen, 2025). With nearly half of the United Kingdom's wheat acreage planted to Yr15‐containing cultivars, this breakdown is particularly concerning and underscores the risks associated with heavy reliance on single major resistance genes.

STRIPE RUST PATHOGEN: BIOLOGY, VIRULENCE EVOLUTION, AND POPULATION DYNAMICS

Biology of Puccinia striiformis West. f. sp. tritici Eriks

The stripe rust pathogen, Pst, is an obligate biotrophic basidiomycete fungus that exclusively infects wheat and related grasses (X. Chen, 2020). Its strict dependence on living host tissue makes in vitro study of Pst particularly challenging. The disease cycle primarily involves the production of urediniospores, which serve as the main agents of dispersal and infection (Huerta‐Espino et al., 2020). Wind‐dispersed urediniospores enable long‐distance spread, allowing the pathogen to traverse continents and infect distant wheat‐growing regions (Kayim et al., 2022). Stripe rust infection is favored by cool temperatures (10–18°C) and prolonged leaf wetness (at least 6 h of dew), conditions often found in spring and early summer in key wheat regions (F. Jan et al., 2025). Under such favorable conditions, the pathogen can complete its life cycle in as little as 10–14 days. In regions with mild winters, Pst can overwinter in living wheat tissues, surviving temperatures as low as −5°C, and serve as a source of primary inoculum for subsequent epidemics (Ma et al., 2015). Although Pst has a sexual stage involving alternate hosts such as Berberis or Mahonia species, this stage is thought to play a minor role in most wheat‐producing regions, where asexual reproduction predominates, although its epidemological importance may vary regionally (Bouvet, Holdgate, et al., 2022).

Pst and other rust fungi possess unusually large and complex genomes compared to other plant pathogens. These genomes are characterized by high levels of heterozygosity and abundant repetitive sequences (Guan et al., 2025). Genome sizes among rust fungi range from 70 to 893 Mb, with Pst genome assemblies varying from approximately 53 to 156 Mb depending on the isolate and sequencing platform (W. Zheng et al., 2013). This large genome size is primarily attributed to the extensive accumulation of transposable elements (TEs), especially long terminal repeat retrotransposons, which can constitute between 17.8% and 85% of the genome (Guan et al., 2025). Comparative genomics has shown that Pst genomes may contain over 30% repetitive content. Moreover, gene family expansions are especially prominent in virulence‐related categories such as effectors, transporters, and transcription factors (TFs) (Yadav et al., 2022). Recent advances in high‐throughput sequencing technologies, such as PacBio, Illumina, and Hi‐C platforms, have enabled the construction of nearly gap‐free, phased genome assemblies. These genomic insights have uncovered extensive structural variation and gene expansions, offering a molecular basis for Pst’s adaptability and frequent resistance breakdowns in wheat (Guan et al., 2025). The inherent genomic plasticity of Pst, characterized by extensive structural variation and virulence gene expansions, facilitates the rapid emergence of novel pathotypes capable of overcoming deployed resistance genes across diverse wheat‐growing environments.

Pathotypes and virulence evolution

Pathotype evolution in Pst is primarily shaped by natural selection imposed by resistant wheat cultivars, creating a co‐evolutionary arms race between host and pathogen (Burdon et al., 2014). Rapid evolution is facilitated by TE‐mediated genome changes and the positive selection of virulence‐associated genes, including carbohydrate‐active enzymes, proteases, and secreted effector proteins (Krattinger & Keller, 2016). This rapid diversification of effector genes enables the pathogen to bypass host resistance, often aligning with gene‐for‐gene interaction model that allows it to overcome newly deployed resistance genes in wheat (Valent, 2025). Genomic studies have shown that many expanded gene families in Pst encode putative effectors and uncharacterized proteins that likely contribute to host adaptation and the emergence of new pathotypes (Cantu et al., 2013).

The emergence of such pathotypes has led to the breakdown of previously effective resistance genes in various regions worldwide (F. Jan et al., 2025). For instance, in India, virulent pathotypes like Pst46S119, Pst78S84, and Pst110S119 have overcome widely deployed resistance genes such as Yr9 and Yr27 (Gangwar et al., 2019). In China, dominant races like CYR34 and Suwon11‐1 have triggered regional epidemics by defeating Yr26/Yr24‐mediated resistance (L. Chen et al., 2023). Similarly, in the United States, the emergence of novel molecular groups such as the MG3 sub‐lineages since 2010 has posed additional challenges for stripe rust management (T. Liu et al., 2021). Recently in June 2025, the United Kingdom witnessed the rapid emergence and spread of Pst strains capable of overcoming the previously robust Yr15 resistance gene (Hansen, 2025). Evidence of reduced effectiveness of Yr15 has been reported, with increased disease severity observed on some Yr15‐carrying cultivars across parts of the national wheat acreage. Continuous surveillance of evolving pathotypes is critical, as even minor genetic variations can render major resistance genes ineffective. The emergence of such pathotypes across regions underscores the genetic flexibility of Pst populations. Understanding how these populations evolve and migrate is critical for resistance management.

Population dynamics and evolution

Pst populations exhibit high genetic diversity and rapid pathotype turnover. These dynamics are driven by genome structural variation, mutation, and a mix of clonal and, in some regions, sexual reproduction (X. Bai et al., 2021). Genetic differentiation is observed among regional Pst populations, reflecting local adaptation and restricted gene flow (C. Wang et al., 2020). For instance, in the United States, western Pst populations exhibit greater genetic diversity and are more divergent from eastern populations than from those within their own region. In India, the Kashmir lineage of Pst is genetically distinct from pathotypes prevalent in Punjab and Himachal Pradesh (Ramesh et al., 2025).

Temporal shifts in pathogen populations are influenced by factors such as gene flow, selection pressure imposed by host resistance, and environmental conditions (C. Wang et al., 2020). In the United States, isolates collected between 2010 and 2017 represented 1454 multilocus genotypes, with 86%–95% of them being novel each year. Dominant clonal lineages, such as MG3, have replaced historical populations within a decade, demonstrating the pathogen's remarkable capacity for rapid adaptation (T. Liu et al., 2021). Gene flow between regions can introduce new virulence traits, while the deployment of resistance genes in wheat cultivars accelerates the evolution of virulent pathotypes (Krattinger & Keller, 2016). Though clonal reproduction predominates, regional migration facilitates the spread of virulence, emphasizing the need for coordinated global surveillance and management strategies to ensure durable resistance in wheat (Burdon et al., 2014). Together, the pathogen's complex genome, high evolutionary capacity, and population‐level diversity underscore the need for robust surveillance and durable resistance strategies in wheat breeding.

HOST–PATHOGEN INTERACTIONS IN STRIPE RUST

The interaction between Pst and its wheat host begins when wind‐dispersed urediniospores land on the surface of wheat leaves (Hau & de Vallavieille‐Pope, 2006) (Figure 2). Under favorable conditions, such as cool temperatures (10–18°C) and prolonged leaf wetness (6–8 h of dew or rain), the spores germinate and produce germ tubes that grow along the leaf surface in search of stomatal openings (Levetin, 2016). Once a stoma is located, the germ tube forms an appressorium over the pore and penetrates into the substomatal cavity. There, it develops a substomatal vesicle and extends intercellular hyphae between plant cells (Hardham, 2007). Specialized feeding structures, known as haustoria, arise from these hyphae, penetrating host cell walls to extract nutrients and support fungal development. As the pathogen colonizes the host, it secretes a diverse set of effector proteins into the apoplast and cytoplasm to suppress plant immune responses, manipulate host metabolism, and avoid detection (P. N. Dodds et al., 2024). Some effectors, such as PSTG_11208 and PstCEP1, specifically target host proteins to disrupt defense signaling or prevent the activation of resistance mechanisms (Ngou et al., 2022).

Despite these strategies, the plant mounts defenses by recognizing pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), triggering PAMP‐triggered immunity (PTI) (Couto & Zipfel, 2016). When the plant harbors resistance (R) genes that detect specific effectors, it triggers a stronger response called effector‐triggered immunity (ETI). ETI involves nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeat (NLR) oligomerization into immune complexes (often called “resistosomes”) that can function as ion channels or promote ion flux (especially Ca2+ influx) across membranes, leading to rapid changes in cytosolic ion concentrations (Locci & Parker, 2024). This is followed by activation of signal transduction cascades, including mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) phosphorylation events, post‐translational modifications of other proteins, and production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Downstream, there is transcriptional reprogramming of defense‐related genes and phytohormone pathways, deposition of callose at infection sites, and in many cases a localized programmed cell death called the hypersensitive response (HR) (Remick et al., 2023). Key resistance genes, such as TaPAL, regulate the synthesis of lignin and phenolic compounds through the phenylpropanoid pathway, strengthening cell walls to restrict pathogen movement (Cui et al., 2015). Nonetheless, Pst can evolve new effectors to evade detection, enabling it to form yellow‐orange pustules (uredinia) along leaf veins. These pustules release fresh spores, continuing the disease cycle (Ray, 2024). Resistant cultivars confine infection with little damage. In contrast, susceptible varieties suffer unchecked disease progression, leading to reduced photosynthesis, early senescence, and yield losses (Cui et al., 2015).

Pathogen invasion, perception, and signal transduction in plants

Pathogen invasion and plant immune perception

Plants rely on a sophisticated innate immune system that enables them to detect and respond to pathogen invasion, helping prevent disease and reduce yield losses (Cook et al., 2015). This system consists of two major layers of defense: extracellular perception through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and intracellular detection mediated by NLR proteins. Together, these components form the basis of the “zig‐zag” model of plant immunity (Cesari, 2018). Upon pathogen invasion, plant PRRs on the cell surface detect conserved molecules known as PAMPs or microbe‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) such as fungal chitin or bacterial flagellin (P. N. Dodds et al., 2024). For example, the FLS2 receptor in Arabidopsis detects the flg22 peptide from bacterial flagellin, initiating a cascade known as PTI (Robatzek et al., 2006). Activation of PRRs leads to rapid ion fluxes, the production of ROS, and the initiation of MAPK signaling cascades. These signals activate defense‐related genes, including WRKY transcription factors, and strengthen cell walls via callose deposition and lignin formation (Chetan et al., 2024).

However, pathogens like Pst can suppress PTI by delivering effector proteins into the apoplast or host cytoplasm. In response, plants have developed a second layer of defense: intracellular perception mediated by NLR proteins, which recognize pathogen effectors either directly or indirectly (Chetan et al., 2024). In the direct recognition model, NLRs bind to specific effectors, whereas in the indirect model, the effectors modify host target proteins, referred to as guardees or decoys, which are then recognized by NLRs. This recognition triggers ETI, a more robust and specific immune response that often includes localized programmed cell death (known as the HR) to limit pathogen spread (Poretti et al., 2021).

Signal transduction in plants

Pathogen recognition initiates a complex and tightly regulated signaling process known as signal transduction (Figure 3). Calcium ions act as secondary messengers, initiating downstream signaling pathways that activate protein kinases such as calcium‐dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) and MAPKs. These kinases, in turn, regulate the expression of defense‐related TFs (Mir et al., 2023). Key TF families, including WRKY, MYB (myeloblastosis transcription factor family), and NAC (NAM, ATAF1/2, and CUC2), orchestrate the expression of genes encoding pathogenesis‐related (PR) proteins, phytoalexins, phenolic compounds, and enzymes involved in cell wall reinforcement. Together, these transcriptional responses contribute to inhibiting pathogen growth and spread (Bergey et al., 2014).

Salicylic acid (SA) plays a central role in amplifying defense responses and establishing systemic acquired resistance (SAR), priming the plant for faster, broad‐spectrum responses to future infections (D. Tang et al., 2017). Other phytohormones, including jasmonic acid (JA), ethylene, and abscisic acid (ABA), also contribute to the modulation of defense responses. One hallmark of early defense is the oxidative burst, which is the rapid accumulation of ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) at infection sites. ROS and RNS reinforce cell walls and may initiate the HR, a localized form of cell death that restricts pathogen spread (Poretti et al., 2021). Transcriptomic approaches such as RNA sequencing and microarray analysis have revealed that wheat responds to rust infection in two distinct phases, namely, an early defense phase and a later defense phase (Chetan et al., 2024). These studies highlight changes in genes involved in pathogen detection (e.g., PRRs), signal transduction (e.g., MAPKs and CDPKs), and defense output (e.g., PR proteins, oxidative enzymes, and cell wall‐modifying enzymes) (R. Liu et al., 2022). Comparative transcriptomic analyses of resistant and susceptible wheat genotypes show that resistant genotypes rapidly upregulate defense genes and strengthen physical and biochemical barriers immediately after pathogen recognition, whereas susceptible genotypes exhibit delayed or weakened responses, allowing rapid pathogen colonization (H. Zhang et al., 2014). These findings align with broader advances in plant immunity, showing that extracellular and intracellular immune pathways are interconnected and converge on shared downstream signaling components. This integration allows plants to dynamically tailor their defense responses to specific threats, thereby enhancing their resilience against evolving pathogens (Chetan et al., 2024). Characterizing immune receptors and their associated signaling networks has also opened new opportunities for breeding and engineering wheat varieties with improved resistance to rust (Han et al., 2025). Pathogen detection and downstream signal transduction form the backbone of the plant immune system (Y. Deng et al., 2020). Together, these molecular defenses limit pathogen spread and prime the plant for future attacks, supporting more sustainable crop production under ongoing biotic stress.

Wheat‐specific immune mechanisms against stripe rust

Wheat's anti‐stripe rust immunity displays several mechanistic hallmarks that distinguish it from many other plant‐pathogen systems. First, the hexaploid architecture of bread wheat (AABBDD) and resulting homoeolog redundancy both buffer and diversify immune function: multiple homoeologues can provide functional redundancy for major resistance loci while also allowing neo‐functionalization that yields unique resistance mechanisms identified in wheat germplasm. This genomic complexity also explains why wild relatives and landraces remain such rich reservoirs of novel resistance alleles and haplotypes for breeding (Ramírez‐González et al., 2018).

Second, wheat deploys distinct molecular strategies that couple defense to core physiology. A notable example is the WKS1/Yr36 pathway, in which a kinase‐START protein phosphorylates the PSII extrinsic protein PsbO to suppress photosynthesis and thereby limit pathogen success. This represents a mechanistic trade‐off between defense and energy metabolism that is particularly well documented for stripe rust in wheat (Y. Yan et al., 2023). Similarly, classic APR loci such as Yr18 encode pleiotropic transporters and resistance mechanisms that provide slow‐rusting, broad‐spectrum protection rather than classical hypersensitive cell death, distinguishing wheat APR from many single‐gene ETI systems (Rinaldo et al., 2017).

Third, the obligate‐biotrophic, haustorium‐based infection biology of Pst places haustorium‐delivered effectors and intimate host‐pathogen interfaces at the center of the interaction. Haustoria enable Pst to deliver large repertoires of effectors and small RNA species into host cells, promoting sophisticated cross‐kingdom regulation: Pst produces microRNA‐like RNAs (milRNAs) that suppress host defense transcripts, while the host adjusts small RNA populations to regulate defense genes, creating a dynamic two‐way small‐RNA dialogue with strong functional consequences for susceptibility and resistance. These haustorium‐centric and sRNA‐mediated mechanisms are especially pronounced in the wheat–Pst pathosystem (Mueth & Hulbert, 2022; Q. Xu et al., 2019). Finally, recent studies show direct effector‐mediated remodeling of host chromatin and histone marks to up‐regulate susceptibility factors, revealing an epigenetic layer of manipulation by Pst that provides novel mechanistic targets for breeding (W. Duan et al., 2024).

Taken together, polyploidy‐driven gene redundancy, physiology‐coupled APR mechanisms (e.g., WKS1/Yr36 and Yr18), haustorium‐centric effector/sRNA exchanges, and pathogen‐directed chromatin remodeling define a distinctive mechanistic landscape for wheat resistance to stripe rust and justify targeted strategies such as fine‐mapping of genomic hotspots, epigenetic profiling, and effector‐guided breeding to achieve durable resistance.

GENETICS AND MECHANISMS OF STRIPE RUST RESISTANCE

Genetic and molecular mechanisms

Genetic resistance remains the cornerstone for managing stripe rust in wheat, providing a sustainable, environment friendly, and cost‐effective solution. Rust resistance is broadly classified into two types: all‐stage resistance (ASR) and APR, which differ significantly in their genetic architecture, mode of action, and durability. ASR, also referred to as seedling resistance, is typically race‐specific and expressed throughout the entire plant's life. It is controlled by single, major‐effect genes and provides strong protection against specific Pst races (Norman et al., 2023; Rehman et al., 2024). However, its durability is limited due to the pathogen's rapid evolution (Y. Wang, Liang, et al., 2021). In contrast, APR is quantitative in nature, activates during later developmental stages, and provides partial but broad‐spectrum resistance governed by multiple minor‐effect genes (A. Wang et al., 2025; Waqar et al., 2018). APR is considered more durable because it applies less selection pressure on the pathogen population (Basnet et al., 2022). High‐temperature adult‐plant (HTAP) resistance is a well‐known form of APR that performs more effectively under elevated temperatures and has been successfully utilized in regions like the northwestern United States (X. M. Chen, 2005). To date, over 700 QTLs related to stripe rust resistance have been reported (www.wheatqtldb.net; K. Singh et al., 2022), and 86 Yr genes have been formally designated (L. Long et al., 2024), though only 12 have been cloned (Table 1). ASR is mediated through gene‐for‐gene interactions. Most Yr genes encode intracellular immune receptors with nucleotide‐binding site and leucine‐rich repeat domains, enabling them to recognize specific pathogen effectors and trigger robust immune responses. ASR genes such as Yr5, Yr10(YrNAM), Yr15, Yr17, Yr26, and Yr36 have been cloned, with Yr5 and Yr15 showing broad‐spectrum efficacy due to recognition of conserved effector molecules (Dibley et al., 2024; Marchal et al., 2018; C. Wang et al., 2020). However, virulence to Yr15 has been reported in some Pst populations, including those in the United Kingdom, indicating the need for strategic gene deployment and pyramiding to sustain resistance (Hansen, 2025). The Yr36 gene, cloned via map‐based cloning, encodes WKS1, a kinase‐START protein that promotes H2O2 accumulation and cell death by phosphorylating tAPX, PsbO, and ZEP1 (Chang et al., 2023; S. Wang et al., 2019). However, ASR alone may not ensure long‐term protection, so pyramiding of multiple Yr genes (e.g., Yr5 + Yr15 or Yr26 + Yr48) has been employed to enhance durability, supported by mMAS and genomic selection (GS) approaches (Mu et al., 2020).

The availability of diagnostic molecular markers from locus‐specific tags such as Sequence‐Tagged Sites/Expressed Sequence Tags (STS/EST) markers and Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs) used for foreground selection, to high‐throughput single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) assays (notably kompetitive allele specific PCR [KASP]) for rapid large‐scale genotyping and background recovery has greatly accelerated and de‐risked the introgression of disease resistance loci into elite cultivars by enabling precise foreground selection and faster background genome recovery (Chhetri et al., 2023; Thulasinathan et al., 2023). APR being polygenic, includes genes like Yr18, Yr29, Yr30, and Yr46, many of which encode ABC transporters, kinases, or transcription regulators that enhance basal immunity. Yr18, Yr29, and Yr46, for example, offer pleiotropic resistance not only to stripe rust but also to leaf rust, stem rust, and powdery mildew (Draz et al., 2024; Krattinger et al., 2009; R. P. Singh et al., 2013). Yr30 is another APR gene associated with the stem rust resistance gene Sr2 complex, also conferring partial resistance to powdery mildew (Pm48) (Qureshi et al., 2025). Recent omics studies have revealed that resistant wheat genotypes activate pathways related to MAPK signaling, redox balance, cell wall reinforcement, and SAR. TFs such as WRKY, MYB, and NAC are pivotal in regulating these defense pathways, with WRKY TFs particularly responsive to SA and JA signaling (P. Zhang et al., 2021). GWAS and QTL mapping, aided by SNP arrays and whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), have uncovered numerous loci responsible for quantitative resistance across global wheat germplasm (Shewabez et al., 2025). The integration of ASR and APR through strategic gene stacking and pyramiding is now critical for breeding wheat varieties with durable, broad‐spectrum, and climate‐resilient resistance. This integrative approach safeguards yield, reduces fungicide dependence, and supports sustainable wheat production globally (S. Abbas et al., 2024).

Biochemical and physiological mechanisms

Wheat plants respond to Pst through a coordinated network of biochemical and physiological mechanisms that restrict pathogen colonization and minimize damage. These defenses are initiated by early pathogen recognition through receptor‐mediated signaling, which activates a cascade of responses including oxidative signaling, structural reinforcement, metabolic reprogramming, and sustained defense activation. Together, these processes enhance the plant resistance, limiting pathogen development and disease progression (Nabi et al., 2024).

A central component of early defense is the oxidative burst, marked by rapid accumulation of ROS like H2O2, superoxide (O2 −), and hydroxyl radicals at infection sites. These ROS act as both antimicrobial agents and signaling messengers, triggering downstream defense pathways (S. Ali et al., 2024). This oxidative burst is tightly linked to MAPK cascades and calcium‐dependent signaling, which reprogram transcription of defense‐related genes. These pathways often trigger the HR, causing local cell death to block pathogen spread (Nazarov et al., 2024). To mitigate ROS‐induced cytotoxicity, wheat plants maintain redox balance through an antioxidant defense system comprising enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and peroxidase (POD), along with non‐enzymatic antioxidants like ascorbate and glutathione. Resistant cultivars typically exhibit higher antioxidant activity, allowing more efficient regulation of ROS signaling and cellular protection (S. Jan et al., 2023; Sahu et al., 2022).

Simultaneously, transcriptional activation of PR proteins, including chitinase, β‐1,3‐glucanase, phenol oxidase, and phenylalanine ammonia‐lyase, contributes to early pathogen containment by degrading fungal structures and limiting proliferation (J. Zhang et al., 2025). These responses are complemented by structural defenses such as papillae formation and callose deposition, which create physical barriers to fungal entry (R. P. Singh et al., 2025). The phenylpropanoid pathway is also activated, producing lignin, flavonoids, and other antimicrobial secondary metabolites to reinforce cell walls (R. Liu, Lv, et al., 2023). Transcriptomic studies show significant upregulation of genes related to phenylalanine metabolism and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis within 24 h of infection in resistant cultivars (S. Jan et al., 2023). Resistant plants also reprogram their metabolism, adjusting photosynthesis and carbon allocation during defense. Such shifts prioritize defense over growth by enhancing immune responses and reducing nutrient availability for the pathogen.

In summary, stripe rust resistance in wheat involves integrated defense strategies, involving oxidative signaling, redox regulation, PR protein induction, structural reinforcement, metabolic reprogramming, and developmentally coordinated responses. A recent study by F. Jan et al. (2025) showed that defense responses vary across wheat ploidy levels. Compared to tetraploid (Triticum durum) and hexaploid (T. aestivum) genotypes, diploid wild wheat (Aegilops tauschii) exhibited stronger biochemical defenses and greater resistance. While tetraploid wheat showed moderate antioxidant activity, the hexaploid genotypes accumulated higher ROS levels, displayed lower antioxidant capacity, and had reduced photosynthetic pigments. These findings highlight the value of wild relatives in breeding programs for discovering novel resistance genes.

Transcription Factors (TFs) and regulatory networks

TFs are central regulators of plant immune responses, orchestrating the activation or suppression of numerous defense‐related genes. In wheat–Pst interactions, key TF families such as WRKY, AP2/ERF, BES/BZR, MYB, and bZIP play pivotal roles in modulating resistance or susceptibility. These TFs function within intricate signaling networks, often in coordination with coactivators and repressors, to ensure precise and timely activation of defense pathways. Among these TFs, WRKY TFs are particularly well characterized for their rapid and dynamic response to pathogen attack. They can act as either activators or repressors depending on the specific gene targets and context. For instance, TaWRKY19 functions as a negative regulator of resistance (N. Wang, Fan, et al., 2022). It is upregulated during infection by virulent Pst strains and binds to W‐box elements in the promoter of TaNOX10, a gene encoding nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase involved in ROS production. By repressing TaNOX10, TaWRKY19 attenuates the oxidative burst, thereby compromising early immune responses. Overexpression of TaWRKY19 increases susceptibility, whereas clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR‐associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9)‐mediated knockout of its homeologs enhances resistance, validating its role as a repressor (N. Wang, Fan, et al., 2022).

In contrast, WRKYs such as TaWRKY70 and TaWRKY45 are upregulated during avirulent Pst interactions and are believed to activate PR genes, contributing to immunity (J. Wang, Tao, et al., 2017). The functional importance of WRKYs is further underscored by the discovery of YrU1, an NLR immune receptor that carries an integrated WRKY domain. This unusual domain architecture likely serves as a decoy to intercept pathogen effectors targeting host WRKYs (C. Wang et al., 2020), highlighting the evolutionary pressure on WRKY TFs as key regulatory hubs in plant defense. Other TF families also contribute significantly to stripe rust resistance. The AP2/ERF family, for instance, includes TaAP2‐10, which is specifically induced during incompatible interactions with avirulent Pst strains. Silencing TaAP2‐10 via virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) increases lesion size and reduces ROS accumulation, whereas intact TaAP2‐10 expression is associated with a stronger oxidative burst and pathogen restriction (Z. Hu et al., 2023). Similarly, TaAP2‐15 functions with overexpression increasing resistance and silencing increasing susceptibility (Hawku et al., 2021). These AP2/ERF TFs are likely involved in ethylene‐ and ABA‐mediated signaling, highlighting their integrative role in both biotic and abiotic stress responses.

Brassinosteroid (BR) signaling, although traditionally associated with plant growth, also contributes to immune regulation via the BES/BZR TF family. TaBZR2, a BR‐responsive TF, has been identified as a positive regulator of stripe rust resistance (X. Bai et al., 2021). Induced by Pst infection and PAMP stimuli, it activates the chitinase gene TaCht20.2, which degrades fungal cell walls. The overexpression of TaBZR2 increases chitinase activity and confers broad‐spectrum resistance, whereas RNAi silencing reduces immunity (X. Bai et al., 2021). These findings highlight how TFs linked to growth can be repurposed to increase defense. These TFs function within broader regulatory circuits involving coactivators and upstream signaling cascades. A prominent coactivator is NPR1 (non‐expressor of PR1 genes), which plays a central role in SA‐mediated signaling. In wheat, NPR1 homologs are targeted by Pst effectors, such as Puccinia NPR1‐interactor (PNPi) protein, which binds NPR1 and suppresses PR gene induction, weakening SA‐mediated defenses (R. Liu, Lv, et al., 2023; X. Wang et al., 2016).

Another key cofactor is SGT1, which supports the stability and nuclear accumulation of resistance proteins. A MAPK signaling module involving TaMKK2 and TaMAPK6 activates SGT1 via phosphorylation, enhancing resistance (Shu et al., 2025). However, the Pst effector HASP215 interferes with this cascade by blocking TaMAPK6 activation, thereby impairing SGT1 function and dampening host immunity (Shu et al., 2025). Additional regulatory components, such as wheat homologues of EIN3 (TaEIL1) and receptor‐like kinase‐associated proteins such as TaBIR1, further contribute to fine‐tuning immune signaling. TaEIL1 appears to function as a negative regulator (X. Duan et al., 2013), likely attenuating ethylene‐mediated defenses, whereas TaBIR1 supports receptor‐mediated signaling and promotes resistance (Sun et al., 2023).

Together, these examples emphasize the multilayered complexity of transcriptional regulation, where TFs, coactivators, kinases, and other signaling intermediates act in a coordinated network to translate pathogen recognition into defense gene activation. Advances in gene editing, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and protein–protein interaction assays are rapidly expanding our understanding of these networks. Dissecting how key regulators such as TaWRKY19, TaAP2‐10, TaBZR2, NPR1, and MAPKs interact within broader immune pathways offers powerful strategies for engineering durable and broad‐spectrum resistance in wheat.

Epigenetic regulation

Epigenetic regulation, heritable changes in gene expression that do not alter the underlying DNA sequence, plays a crucial role in modulating plant immunity. Key mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and small RNAs, all of which influence the activation, silencing, or priming of defense‐related genes. These regulatory layers are emerging as essential components of the wheat–stripe rust interaction, with mounting evidence that they are also targets of pathogen effectors. DNA methylation in the cytosine followed by guanine (CG [5′‐C‐G‐3′]), cytosine followed by any nucleotide except guanine, then guanine (CHG [5′‐C‐H‐G‐3′]), and cytosine followed by any two nucleotides except guanine (CHH [5′‐C‐H‐H‐3′]) contexts is a key regulator of gene silencing. Although direct evidence for wheat–Pst interactions is limited, studies on wheat‐leaf rust have shown that CHH demethylation at promoters and transposons is correlated with defense gene activation (K. Singh et al., 2022). These findings suggest that demethylases may derepress immune responses, whereas hypermethylation could silence resistance genes. Interestingly, the developmental regulation of Lr48 by stage‐specific methylation hints at potential parallels with HTAP resistance to stripe rust (K. Singh et al., 2022). In the transposon‐rich wheat genome, RNA‐directed DNA methylation (RdDM) and the epigenetic regulation of nearby elements may also influence defense loci. Therefore, understanding the methylation status of resistance genes and surrounding TEs may help reveal critical epigenetic switches involved in immunity.

Histone modifications represent another key layer of gene regulation. Acetylation (e.g., H3K9ac) typically promotes transcriptional activation, while methylation marks (e.g., H3K27me3) are associated with gene repression. Under stripe rust attack, chromatin near defense gene promoters is believed to transition toward a more open and transcriptionally active configuration. A notable study by W. Duan et al. (2024) provided direct evidence of pathogen‐mediated chromatin remodeling where it was demonstrated that the Pst effector PstGTA1 enters the wheat nucleus and enhances the expression of the susceptibility gene TaSIG by promoting H3K4 acetylation at its promoter. This provides the first direct evidence that a fungal effector manipulates host chromatin to facilitate disease.

Although detailed histone modification profiles in Pst‐infected wheat are lacking, studies in other pathosystems have shown that defense genes typically acquire active marks during infection. Chromatin‐modifying enzymes, such as histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs), are known to modulate defense gene expression. HATs generally act as coactivators of defense, whereas HDACs may suppress immune gene expression (X. Zhang et al., 2024). In some cereals, HDAC inhibitors enhance disease resistance, likely by maintaining defense gene loci in an open chromatin state. Moreover, the structural similarity between PstGTA1 and SNF2 chromatin remodelers further underscores the importance of chromatin accessibility in regulating plant immunity (W. Duan et al., 2024).

Small RNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) also participate in the wheat response to stripe rust. Differential expression of miRNAs between resistant and susceptible genotypes suggests that post‐transcriptional regulation plays a key role in modulating immunity (Spychała et al., 2023). Some miRNAs suppress negative regulators of defense, whereas Pst itself produces milRNAs, such as Pst‐milR1, which can silence host genes to enhance infection (B. Wang, Sun, et al., 2017). Additionally, siRNAs may guide RdDM to modulate gene expression epigenetically. The cross‐talk between small RNAs and epigenetic modifiers is increasingly evident, where small RNAs can regulate the expression of histone‐modifying enzymes, while chromatin state in turn influences small RNA biogenesis, adding further complexity to the regulatory landscape during host–pathogen interactions.

One particularly promising area of epigenetics is epigenetic memory and immune priming. Upon initial pathogen exposure, wheat may enter a primed state in which defense genes respond more robustly upon subsequent attacks. This primed state is likely maintained by stable chromatin marks, such as H3K4me3 and H3K9ac, at defense gene promoters (Espinas et al., 2016). Although this phenomenon has not yet been fully elucidated in wheat–Pst interactions, the concept is supported by SAR and evidence of transgenerational inheritance of epigenetic marks (Shilpa et al., 2022). This opens exciting possibilities for breeding cultivars that are preconditioned for rapid immune activation.

From a practical standpoint, identifying stable epigenetic markers, such as hypomethylated promoters of NLR or PR genes, could complement DNA‐based selection. Moreover, breeding for cultivars with durable epigenetic priming may lead to enhanced resilience without the need for genetic modification. Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and small RNA activity, play crucial roles in modulating wheat defense against stripe rust. Pathogens have evolved effectors that exploit these regulatory systems, further highlighting their importance. Harnessing epigenetic regulation and memory could offer innovative avenues for breeding long‐term, broad‐spectrum resistance in wheat.

BREEDING FOR STRIPE RUST RESISTANCE

Conventional approaches

Conventional breeding remains a foundational strategy for combating Pst, a major threat to global wheat production. Key methodologies include selection‐based strategies (pure line and mass selection), various forms of hybridization (including wide and backcrossing), and population‐based approaches such as multiline and composite breeding (Jamil et al., 2020). Selection‐based breeding typically begins with identifying parental lines carrying known resistance traits, often sourced from diverse gene pools. Gene banks provide access to three key gene pools: cultivated wheat (primary), closely related species (secondary), and wild relatives (tertiary) (R. Kumar et al., 2024). Wild relatives in the tertiary pool are especially valuable for introducing durable resistance alleles absent in modern cultivars. Hybridization, which began in the late 19th century, remains a central strategy in conventional breeding for combining favorable traits from genetically diverse parents, particularly resistance and yield attributes (Afzal et al., 2022). To stabilize resistance traits after hybridization, breeders use methods such as pedigree selection, bulk population, single‐seed descent, and ear‐to‐row selection. These techniques facilitate progressive fixation of resistance alleles across generations.

Gene pyramiding, though often commonly associated with molecular breeding, can also be effectively achieved through conventional means by combining multiple resistance genes via repeated cycles of crossing and selection (Beukert et al., 2020). For example, Ae. tauschii, the donor of wheat's D genome, has played a critical role in conventional resistance breeding by contributing stripe rust resistance genes (Y. Chen et al., 2019). For instance, genes like Yr28 and Yr36 originally derived from wild species, have proven effective in providing durable resistance, while Yr15 though historically highly effective, has recently been overcome by virulent Pst races in certain regions, limiting its broad applicability (Hansen, 2025; Mondal et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2024). Effective deployment of wild‐derived genes depends on understanding genome architecture and facilitating gene transfer via homoeologous recombination. Backcrossing is commonly used to transfer major resistance genes into elite susceptible lines through repeated crosses and selection (D. M. Kumar et al., 2023). While effective, this method is most suitable for traits controlled by single major‐effect genes.

While conventional breeding has laid the foundation for many successful cultivars, it is not without challenges. Most notably, it is time‐intensive, often requiring 10–12 years to develop and release a new resistant variety. Moreover, phenotypic screening is often confounded by environmental variability, limiting reliable selection under inconsistent disease pressure (Bhardwaj et al., 2019). The rapid emergence of new Pst races further challenges the pace of resistance breeding through conventional means. Despite these challenges, numerous successful cultivars demonstrate the continued relevance of conventional strategies. Kharchia 65, a salt‐tolerant Indian wheat variety, exemplifies successful backcross breeding by integrating rust resistance from donor lines (Sharma et al., 2024). Wide hybridization techniques led to the development of synthetic hexaploid wheats such as Shumai 1701 and N2496, which integrated novel Yr genes like Yr28 and Yr36 from wild relatives (Ae. tauschii), significantly enhancing rust resistance (S. Hou et al., 2023). Moreover, multiline and composite crossing approaches have yielded cultivars like Toropi, which incorporates complementary APR genes and has exhibited stable performance across diverse environments (Kolmer et al., 2018). Together, these cases highlight the enduring value of conventional breeding in delivering rust‐resistant cultivars with improved resilience and productivity.

Marker‐assisted breeding

MAS and marker‐assisted backcrossing (MABC) have transformed stripe rust resistance breeding by enabling the precise and efficient introgression of resistance genes. Unlike traditional phenotypic selection, which is labor‐intensive, time‐consuming, and highly influenced by environmental variability and pathogen evolution (Shahin et al., 2024), MAS utilizes DNA markers tightly linked to resistance loci (Yr genes or QTL) to accurately identify and select resistant genotypes. This molecular approach is particularly effective in pyramiding multiple resistance genes, combining race‐specific ASR genes such as Yr5, Yr10, Yr15, Yr51, and Yr55 with durable, non‐race‐specific APR genes like Yr18, Yr29, Yr30, Yr36, and Yr46 (Klymiuk et al., 2018; Kokhmetova et al., 2021; Krattinger et al., 2009).

Pyramiding of genes is a promising strategy to increase the durability of resistance, and the combination of genes such as Yr18, Yr28, and Yr36 has proven to be an efficient approach for enhancing resistance to stripe rust (F. Wang et al., 2023). The development and validation of functional markers for key genes like Yr5 and Yr15 (L. Chen et al., 2023; Salarpour et al., 2025; G. S. Zhang et al., 2020) have accelerated MABC and gene stacking efforts. High‐throughput genotyping platforms, such as diversity arrays technology sequencing (DArTSeq) and tunable genotyping‐by‐sequencing (tGBS), now enable efficient tracking of these genes across large breeding populations (Szoke‐Pazsi et al., 2024). The advances in genomics‐assisted breeding have greatly expanded the ability to identify, map, and deploy resistance genes at scale. Furthermore, the availability of the wheat reference genome sequence has significantly enhanced gene discovery and functional annotation, accelerating genomic‐assisted breeding strategies. The development of wheat pan‐genomes from 10 diverse wheat varieties, including high‐quality genome assemblies (Walkowiak et al., 2020), represents a major advance. These resources provide a comprehensive view of genomic diversity and greatly facilitate the identification of presence‐absence variations and structural variants (SVs) associated with important agronomic traits.

The use of Chinese Spring ph1b‐induced lines has facilitated homoeologous recombination between wheat and its wild relatives, enabling the transfer of novel resistance genes from alien species (Rahmatov, 2016; Yazdani et al., 2025). The ph1b mutation suppresses the Ph1 locus, which normally prevents pairing between homoeologous chromosomes, thus promoting introgression from genera such as Aegilops and Thinopyrum. These advances have led to the release of high‐yielding, stripe rust‐resistant cultivars through MABC in regions such as China and South Asia, including Zhongmai 578 and the Indian variety HD3226 (Y. Li et al., 2025). Integrating MAS with GS and speed breeding has further accelerated genetic gains. Juliana et al. (2020) reported that combining MAS and GS improved selection accuracy for stripe rust resistance by over 20% compared to conventional methods. However, the success of MAS and MABC depends on the ongoing discovery of novel resistance genes from landraces and wild relatives, development of co‐dominant and breeder‐friendly markers, and monitoring of virulence shifts in Pst. Thus, marker‐assisted breeding remains central to wheat improvement, offering a durable strategy for managing stripe rust amid evolving climates and pathogen threats.

Sources of novel resistance to stripe rust

The ongoing emergence of new stripe rust races poses a significant threat to global wheat production and has led to the breakdown of many major resistance genes in adapted wheat germplasm (Hubbard et al., 2015; Losert et al., 2017). Domestication and modern breeding have significantly narrowed the genetic base of cultivated wheat, reducing its adaptability and phenotypic diversity. Genetic resources stored in global gene banks serve as crucial reservoirs of diversity for combating both biotic and abiotic stresses (N. K. Rao, 2004). Among these, wheat landraces and wild relatives harbor rich sources of novel alleles for stripe rust resistance (Juliana et al., 2020; Manickavelu et al., 2016; Pasam et al., 2017; Sthapit et al., 2014; Sthapit Kandel et al., 2017).

Introgression of novel alleles from wild relatives into modern cultivars is essential to overcoming the genetic bottleneck imposed by domestication. However, introgression breeding is often limited by low genomic resolution and poor understanding of the effects of introgressed segments. Nonetheless, wild relative introgression breeding remains vital for accessing genetic variation from secondary and tertiary gene pools (Hao et al., 2020; Valkoun, 2001). One of the most historically important wheat collections is held at the N.I. Vavilov Institute of Plant Genetic Resources in Russia, which holds approximately 38,430 wheat accessions, comprising 76% bread wheat, 16% durum wheat, and 7.9% wild and primitive wheat from diverse geographic and biological backgrounds (Mitrofanova, 2012). It includes local cultivars, breeding lines, artificial allopolyploids, and mutants, many of which have been widely used for addressing biotic stresses (Mitrofanova, 2012; Riaz et al., 2017, 2018; Sadovaya et al., 2015). In a study by Jambuthenne et al. (2022), Vavilov wheat accessions exhibited a wide range of responses to stripe rust at both seedling and adult stages across environments, with 65% demonstrating strong resistance, most of which were landraces. Another major germplasm source is the Watkins Collection of hexaploid wheat landraces, which has emerged as a particularly valuable asset for characterizing resistance to stripe rust. Acquired in the 1930s from local markets in 32 countries across Asia, Europe, and Africa, this collection represents a snapshot of global wheat diversity prior to modern breeding interventions (Winfield et al., 2018). Compared to modern European bread wheat cultivars, the Watkins landraces show significantly greater phenotypic and genetic diversity for a range of agronomically important traits, including disease resistance (Wingen et al., 2014). Importantly, several studies have utilized this collection to successfully identify novel loci associated with stripe rust resistance (U. K. Bansal et al., 2013, 2011; Randhawa et al., 2015; Toor et al., 2013), further establishing its significance as a genetic reservoir for future breeding efforts aimed at improving durable resistance.

Several studies confirm landraces as rich reservoirs of resistance loci (Pasam et al., 2017; Talas et al., 2011). For instance, the Chinese landrace Baidatou has exhibited durable resistance to multiple Pst races for decades in Longnan, Gansu province (Q. Li et al., 2018). Y. Wang, Liang et al. (2021) evaluated 143 Chinese landrace accessions and identified 17 with stable, high‐level field resistance and four with seedling resistance to CYR32 and CYR34. Similarly, Turkish landraces represent valuable reservoirs of untapped genetic diversity. A comprehensive 5‐year survey collected nearly 2000 landrace populations from 55 Turkish provinces, revealing novel genomic regions valuable for breeding (Sehgal et al., 2016). Wild relatives have also been exploited in durum wheat (Triticum turgidum subsp. durum) resistance breeding. El Haddad et al. (2021) assessed the usefulness of deploying crop wild relatives (CWRs) in durum wheat breeding by evaluating a panel of 60 accessions comprising elite cultivars, advanced breeding lines, and CWR‐derived lines for resistance to rust diseases, including stripe rust. The findings highlighted the role of CWR‐derived lines in enhancing genetic diversity and improving resistance to foliar diseases. Ipek et al. (2023) screened 54 Turkish durum wheat varieties and 11 T. turgidum var. dicoccoides accessions for Yr5, Yr10, and Yr15. None carried Yr5, while 12 and 17 varieties carried Yr10 and Yr15, respectively; seven carried both. Among these, 28 varieties showed resistance to natural Pst infections, whereas all wild emmer accessions were highly susceptible.

The multiple derivative lines platform has been instrumental in harnessing the genetic diversity of wild emmer wheat (WEW). Balla et al. (2022) identified several novel traits and marker‐trait associations (MTAs) for Pst resistance within this population. Notably, WEW accession PI 487260 exhibited broad‐spectrum resistance to multiple Pst isolates (Klymiuk et al., 2022). Several Yr genes have been identified from wild relatives, including Yr70 (Aegilops umbellulata), Yr37 (Aegilops kotschyi), Yr38 (Aegilops sharonensis), Yr40 (Aegilops geniculata), Yrtri (Aegilops triuncialis), and Yr28 and YrAS2388 (Ae. tauschii) (Arora et al., 2021; M. Bansal et al., 2020, 2017; L. Huang et al., 2011; S. Kaur et al., 2018; Kuraparthy et al., 2007; M. Liu et al., 2013; Marais et al., 2010, 2005). At Punjab Agricultural University, the Aegilops speltoides accession PAU 3603 displayed resistance to prevalent Pst pathotypes in Punjab (B. Kaur et al., 2024). Aegilops peregrina (UUSS genome), a wild tetraploid species, also harbors valuable Pst resistance alleles (Narang et al., 2019). Aegilops longissima, native to the Levant and closely related to the D‐genome of bread wheat, exhibited resistance to Pst, Puccinia triticina (Pt) and Puccinia graminis f. sp. tritici (Pgt) races (Page et al., 2023). D. Wu et al. (2024) developed a wheat–T. elongatum translocation line (T1BS·1EL), which conferred strong resistance at both seedling and adult stages. This was confirmed using genomic in situ hybridization, FISH, oligo‐painting, and the 55K SNP array analysis.

A diverse panel of 766 synthetic hexaploid wheat lines, derived from T. turgidum × Ae. tauschii crosses, was evaluated over 3 years at rust‐inoculated field sites in Australia, Ethiopia, India, Kenya, Nepal, and Pakistan. From this set, 94 lines exhibiting resistance were selected (Sandhu et al., 2024). Additionally, a nested association mapping (NAM) population was developed by crossing the Japanese variety Norin 61 with 24 diverse modern and traditional varieties from Nepal, Pakistan, China, and Japan, providing a high‐resolution platform for dissecting rust resistance across South and East Asia (Nie, 2025; Nomura et al., 2025; Shimizu et al., 2021; Tsujimoto, 2021; Walkowiak et al., 2020). A recent study using this Asian NAM population by Jung et al. (2025) identified both known resistance genes (Yr46 and Yr18) and two potentially novel QTLs for Yr resistance on chromosomes 3D and 5B. Notably, the resistant allele on 3D was unique to a traditional Nepalese variety, while the 5B QTL was present across several families, highlighting the potential of Asian germplasm and the effectiveness of pyramiding multiple resistance loci for durable resistance in high‐pressure environments.

In parallel, doubled haploid (DH) spring wheat populations were evaluated in Germany and Egypt to identify resistance alleles against the aggressive Warrior race PstS10 (Draz et al., 2021). Ji et al. (2024) developed a novel wheat–rye derivative line, LCR4, through hybridization between the triticale cultivar Currency and the bread wheat cultivar Jimai 22. LCR4 displayed robust resistance at both seedling and adult stage against race V26 and a mixture of Pst races. Collectively, these efforts highlight the vital role of landraces, wild relatives, synthetic lines, and diverse mapping populations in mining novel alleles for durable and broad‐spectrum Pst resistance in wheat.

Despite these advances, the full potential of gene bank resources remains underutilized. There is a pressing need for systematic characterization, multi‐environment (ME) phenotyping, and high‐resolution genotyping to fully exploit this genetic diversity. Integrating these genetic resources with modern tools such as GS, GWAS, haplotype‐based breeding, and gene editing will be key to sustaining wheat productivity under future challenges. The rich genetic diversity found in landraces, wild relatives, and synthetic wheat lines offers a wealth of untapped resistance alleles against stripe rust. However, unlocking their full potential requires precise genetic dissection and integration into breeding pipelines. Traditional phenotyping alone is insufficient to resolve the complex inheritance patterns of partial and multi‐gene resistance. In this context, genomic tools such as QTL mapping and GWAS have become indispensable. These approaches enable the identification of major and minor resistance loci, accelerate marker development, and support the deployment of broad‐spectrum and durable resistance in modern wheat cultivars.

GENOMICS‐ENABLED DISCOVERY AND DEPLOYMENT OF STRIPE RUST RESISTANCE

Genetic mapping strategies for stripe rust resistance

QTL mapping

Genetic resistance continues to be the most sustainable and eco‐friendly approach for managing stripe rust, forming the foundation for modern breeding efforts. QTL mapping has been instrumental in dissecting complex resistance traits and enables precise MAS for durable resistance (Khan et al., 2022; Krishnappa et al., 2021). The advent of next‐generation sequencing coupled with the availability of the wheat reference genome (International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium [IWGSC], 2018) and reduced genotyping costs, has substantially improved the resolution and accuracy of QTL detection and candidate gene identification. Initiatives like the “10+Genome” project (Walkowiak et al., 2020), along with the sequencing of key cultivars such as Fielder (Sato et al., 2021), Renan (Aury et al., 2022), Kenong 9204 (Shi et al., 2022), Chuanmai 104 (Z. Liu, Yang, et al., 2024), and Zhou8425B (Li et al., 2025), have enriched genomic resources and accelerated the identification of resistance genes. Although numerous QTLs for Pst resistance have been reported across diverse genetic backgrounds, their breeding utility depends on consistent expression across environments and availability of linked molecular markers (Krishnappa et al., 2023). To better utilize these discoveries, QTLs are commonly categorized based on stability, developmental stage, and environmental responsiveness. Several studies have identified QTLs consistently effective across different geographic locations and years. For instance, QYr.spa‐3A.2, derived from Triticum carthlicum ‘Blackbird’, was effective in both Canada and Mexico, indicating its broad adaptability. In contrast, QYr.spa‐2B, QYr.spa‐3A.1, and QYr.spa‐5B displayed region‐specific effectiveness, likely due to genotype‐by‐environment (G × E) interactions or local Pst pathotypes (Bokore et al., 2021). Similarly, QYr.GX‐2AS and QYr.GX‐7DS, derived from the Chinese landrace Gaoxianguangtoumai (GX), were stably expressed across three seasons and explained 15.5%–27.0% and 11.5%–13.5% of phenotypic variation, respectively (Y. Wang, Yu, et al., 2021). These QTLs were also supported by validated KASP markers, making them robust candidates for breeding. In a KU3067 × Apav backcross population, two QTLs, QYr.cim‐1BS and QYr.cim‐2AL, were consistently detected across multiple environments in Mexico, explaining 12.9%–20.5% and 5.4%–12.5%, respectively (P. Zhang et al., 2022). Similarly, QYr.tamu‐2B, reported by Mustahsan et al. (2023), was highly consistent and showed epistatic interaction with QYr.tamu‐2A1, contributing to both infection type and severity reduction. These loci offer strong candidates for broad‐spectrum resistance.

Several studies have identified QTLs that are typically race‐specific and expressed from the seedling stage through maturity. A notable example is the gene YrKU, mapped to chromosome 7BL from the KU3067 × Apav cross (P. Zhang et al., 2022). APR QTLs are often partial in effect but provide more durable resistance. Several such loci have been reported. For example, the Chinese landrace GX carries two stable APR QTLs, QYr.GX‐2AS and QYr.GX‐7DS, which together explain up to 27% of the phenotypic variance (Y. Wang, Yu, et al., 2021). Three linked KASP markers were also validated across 109 Chinese cultivars. Additionally, nine stable APR QTLs in diverse genetic backgrounds were reported by Kokhmetova et al. (2023). The KASP marker KASP_5910, developed for one of these loci, was validated across cultivars from several Chinese provinces and showed the highest frequency in Hubei Province (X. Zhou, Zhong, et al., 2021). HTAP QTLs are particularly important under global warming scenarios. In an AvS × WS recombinant inbred line population, Upadhaya et al. (2025) reported four HTAP QTLs, including QYrWS.wgp‐1BL, 2AL, 3AS, and 3BL, which were stable across seven environments in Washington and explained up to 27.8% of the phenotypic variance. Each of these QTLs was associated with two validated KASP markers.

Additional large‐scale QTLs with strong validation potential have also been reported. Subramanian et al. (2016) mapped major QTLs on chromosomes 3BS and 4BL that explained 28% and 25% of the phenotypic variation, respectively. A. Wang et al. (2025) identified seven QTLs on chromosome arms 1BL, 2BS, 2BL, 5BL, and 7BL across trials conducted from 2017 to 2022 in Yangling and Guiyang using the GBW16K SNP array. QTLs with high phenotypic variance, broad‐spectrum activity, and validated markers are particularly valuable for breeding. To better understand the distribution of major resistance loci, we conducted a comprehensive literature survey of QTL mapping studies related to stripe rust resistance in wheat. A total of 93 studies were identified that reported QTLs associated with resistance at both the seedling and adult plant stages. From these, we selected major and stable QTLs based on the criterion of a phenotypic variance explained (PVE) greater than 20%, as QTLs with higher PVE values are more likely to have consistent effects across environments. This refinement resulted in 31 studies that reported high‐impact, major, or stable QTLs.

A summary of the reported QTLs summarized in Table 2 reveals several recurrent genomic hotspots for stripe rust resistance distributed across the wheat genome. The highest density of QTLs was detected on chromosomes 1B, 2A, 2B, 3B, 5B, 6B, 7B, and 7D (Cheng et al., 2022; Farzand et al., 2021; Gebrewahid, Zhang, et al., 2020; Gebrewahid, Zhou, et al., 2020; L. Hou et al., 2015; Klarquist et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2018; L. Liu et al., 2020; Pang et al., 2022; Pawar et al., 2016; Prins et al., 2024; Subramanian et al., 2016; J. Tang et al., 2024; Tehseen et al., 2022; Upadhaya et al., 2025; Vazquez et al., 2015; Y. Wang et al., 2022; Y. Wang et al., 2022; X. Wang et al., 2025; L. Yang et al., 2017; M. Yang et al., 2019; P. Zhang et al., 2022; J. Zhou et al., 2021), indicating that these regions serve as major repositories of resistance loci repeatedly identified across diverse genetic backgrounds and mapping populations. Among them, chromosome 1B harbors multiple QTLs (QYr.haas1B‐1, QYr.haas1B‐2, QYr.hebau‐1BL, QYr.sdau‐1B, QYr.cim‐1BS, QYr.ufs‐1B.1, etc.), co‐localizing with known genes Yr24, Yr26, Yr27, and Yr29 (Gebrewahid, Zhang, et al., 2020; Pang et al., 2022; Prins et al., 2024; L. Yang et al., 2017; P. Zhang et al., 2022). Similarly, chromosome 2B includes several stable loci (QYrns.orz2BL, QYr.ufs‐2B.1, QYrak.nwafu‐2BL, etc.) overlapping with Yr5, Yr17, and Yr27 (Prins et al., 2024; Vazquez et al., 2015; X. Wang et al., 2025). Other strong resistance clusters were observed on 3B, 5B, 7B, and 7D, which correspond to genomic intervals carrying cloned or well‐characterized genes such as Yr15, Yr18/Lr34/Pm38/Sr57, Yr36, and Yr46 (Gebrewahid, Zhang, et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2018; L. Liu et al., 2020; Pang et al., 2022; Pawar et al., 2016; Upadhaya et al., 2025; X. Wang et al., 2025). The recurrence of QTLs across these specific chromosomal segments highlights the polygenic yet convergent nature of stripe rust resistance, suggesting that these regions are central to durable resistance mechanisms and represent prime targets for fine mapping, haplotype dissection, and gene pyramiding in breeding programs.

Despite significant progress, challenges remain in cross‐background validation and integration into breeding pipelines. Future efforts should focus on the integration of QTL mapping with genomic selection, pan‐genomics, and gene‐editing tools like CRISPR/Cas to accelerate the deployment of durable resistance. Functional validation and fine mapping of key QTLs, especially those showing epistasis or broad‐spectrum resistance, will enhance their utility in precision breeding.

Genome‐wide association studies

While QTL mapping provides targeted insights, GWASs offer broader, high‐resolution approaches by leveraging natural diversity. GWAS powered by high‐density SNP arrays and diverse genetic panels, complements biparental QTL mapping by enabling the high‐resolution dissection of Pst resistance. GWAS leverages natural variation and historical recombination to provide higher mapping resolution and uncover a broader range of alleles than conventional QTL mapping (Tomar, Singh et al., 2021). However, GWAS can be confounded by population structure and may yield false positives or detect loci with minor effects, necessitating rigorous statistical control and validation.

A wide array of GWAS has successfully identified Pst resistance loci in diverse genetic resources, including synthetic hexaploid wheat (Bhatta et al., 2019; Zegeye et al., 2014), global landraces (Jordan et al., 2015; Pasam et al., 2017), Ethiopian durum wheat (W. Liu, Maccaferri, Bulli, et al., 2017), advanced lines from exotic crosses (Ledesma‐Ramírez et al., 2019), Chinese landraces (L. Long et al., 2019), emmer wheat (W. Liu, Maccaferri, Chen, et al., 2017), elite spring and winter wheat from various regions including North America and International Center for Agricultural Research in the Dry Areas (ICARDA) (Godoy et al., 2018; Jighly et al., 2015), Indian gene bank wheat germplam (Jan et al. 2025) and International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT)‐derived germplasm (Juliana et al., 2017). This underscores GWAS's utility in mining both elite and exotic genetic diversity.

Several notable studies exemplify GWAS's utility. For example, Jia et al. (2020) identified 12 stable loci across 11 chromosomes for APR in Chinese cultivars using a 90K SNP array, with each locus explaining 3.6%–10.3% of phenotypic variation. Similarly, Habib et al. (2020) evaluated 465 genotypes against nine Pst races, identifying 20 SNPs on 12 chromosomes for seedling resistance and 30 SNPs on 18 chromosomes linked to APR. Y. Wang, Liang et al. (2021) detected 19 ASR loci and 32 APR loci in 143 Chinese landraces, five of which were potentially novel QTL. In Egypt, where resistance breakdown was observed in several cultivars, Abou‐Zeid and Mourad (2021) screened 103 global spring wheat lines and identified five stable SNPs consistently associated with resistance over 2 years.

Franco et al. (2022) conducted a GWAS on 245 Argentinian bread wheat lines, detecting eight significant markers to local Pst races, with five liwunked to ASR and three to APR, explaining up to 32.6% of phenotypic variation. In another study, Atsbeha et al. (2023) reported 148 significant SNPs, clustering into 54 putative QTLs, many of which were stable across growth stages and potentially represent broad‐spectrum resistance loci. Yao et al. (2025) employed high‐resolution multiplex SNP sequencing in 465 genotypes and identified 143 MTAs. Among them, 28 QTLs (seven for ASR and 21 for HTAP) were validated across diverse races and environments. Additionally, 10 KASP markers were developed for eight HTAP QTLs, enhancing their utility in breeding. F. Jan et al. (2026) conducted a multi‐environment GWAS in a diverse panel of 329 wheat genotypes and identified 49 marker–trait associations (MTAs) for stripe rust resistance using a 35K SNP array. Among these, 12 stable MTAs for stripe rust resistance across chromosomes 2A, 3B, and 4B, including a putatively novel locus. A deep literature mining was conducted to retrieve GWAS focusing on stripe rust resistance in wheat at both the seedling and adult plant stages. A total of 157 GWAS publications were identified. To ensure the reliability of the associations, we selected only those stable MTAs that met the Bonferroni‐corrected significance threshold, thereby minimizing the likelihood of false positives. This stringent selection yielded 13 studies reporting high‐confidence, stable MTAs. The key MTAs identified from these studies are summarized in Table 3. The summary of MTAs presented in Table 3 demonstrates several consistent genomic regions associated with stripe rust resistance across diverse GWAS panels, highlighting them as key hotspots for both ASR and APR. Most significant associations were detected on chromosomes 1B, 2A, 2B, 3B, 5B, 6B, 7B, and 7D (Bagwell et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2016; Juliana et al., 2020; Khan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2020; Manickavelu et al., 2016; Marone et al., 2024; Miedaner et al., 2024; Page et al., 2023; Qiao et al., 2024; Tene et al., 2022; W. Wang et al., 2024; J. Wu et al., 2021), largely overlapping the chromosomal regions where QTLs have also been frequently reported. These findings highlight the presence of potential resistance hotspots that are repeatedly captured by both linkage mapping and association studies. Notably, several of these chromosomes coincide with those carrying known Yr genes, including Yr5, Yr7, Yr15, Yr18, and Yr36, reported in earlier literature (Bagwell et al., 2025; Juliana et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Marone et al., 2024; Tene et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2021). While the current tables do not provide explicit colocalization data, the convergence of multiple MTAs on these key genomic regions suggests that they harbor functionally relevant alleles contributing to durable resistance. Collectively, these findings highlight recurring genomic regions across multiple studies that may harbor key resistance loci or genes, enhancing the interpretive value of the tabulated data.

To fully exploit GWAS‐derived insights, future research should prioritize multi‐year, multi‐race validation and integrate functional genomics to convert loci into durable breeding gains. Ultimately, combining GWAS for broad discovery with biparental mapping for precise validation represents a robust strategy to advance stripe rust resistance breeding. The success of genetic mapping strategies for stripe rust resistance hinges on the availability of robust genomic resources. Thus, advances in wheat genomics have played a pivotal role in enhancing the resolution, efficiency, and applicability of resistance gene discovery.

Advances in wheat genomics

Genome sequencing and pangenomics

Advances in genome sequencing have significantly accelerated the discovery and use of disease resistance genes in wheat. A major milestone was the 2018 release of a near‐complete reference genome (cv. Chinese Spring) by the International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium, which enabled precise marker development and map‐based cloning of resistance genes (IWGSC, 2018). However, due to widespread structural variation and presence/absence variation (PAV), a single reference cannot capture the full genetic diversity across wheat germplasm. This limitation led to the development of pangenomic resources, such as the Wheat 10+ Genomes and Wheat Panache projects, which revealed that many genes, particularly resistance genes in dynamic clusters, are missing from some cultivars (Bayer et al., 2022; Walkowiak et al., 2020). For example, YrNAM (Ni et al., 2023), an ancient resistance gene with a unique NAM‐BED (no apical meristem domain fused to a BEAF and DREF DNA‐binding) domain architecture, was discovered in wild relatives like Ae. sharonensis but is absent in most modern wheat. Its discovery, enabled by the sequencing of trait‐associated mutations anchored near the Yr10 locus, illustrates how pangenomics can reveal critical genes overlooked in single‐reference approaches (Ni et al., 2023).

Pangenome resources have also improved trait mapping, both linkage mapping and GWAS, by providing comprehensive datasets of SNPs and SVs associated with resistance traits. Using high‐density SNP arrays and WGS, several studies have identified novel stripe rust resistance loci across the genome (K. Singh et al., 2022; J. Wu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025), offering new targets for allele mining. Although over 85 Yr genes are known, the full spectrum of natural resistance likely includes many more loci, highlighting the importance of continued genomic exploration. The sequencing of diverse cultivars has facilitated the assembly of complex loci, such as polygenic APR regions and introgressions from wild relatives. For instance, although Yr18/Lr34 was cloned in 2008 as an ABC transporter, reference genomes later revealed promoter and copy number variations (CNVs) distinguishing functional from nonfunctional alleles (T. Fang et al., 2020). Similarly, Yr15 (from wild emmer) and YrF (from Ae. tauschii) were cloned using pangenomic and synteny data (Y. Li et al., 2024). Today, pangenomic and graph‐based resources allow simultaneous interrogation of multiple wheat genomes for resistance gene presence, overcoming the limitations of single‐reference analyses and facilitating gene pyramiding (Bayer et al., 2022; Tiwari et al., 2024).

Traditionally, cloning resistance genes in wheat has been time‐consuming and challenging due to linkage drag, genome complexity, and repetitive DNA sequences. However, recent innovations have significantly accelerated this process. One of the most impactful breakthroughs has been the development of MutRenSeq and related methods, which combine ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis with targeted sequencing to identify mutations in candidate resistance genes. Due to its polyploidy nature, wheat can tolerate high mutation loads, enabling the recovery of loss‐of‐function mutants that directly reveal causal genes. This strategy led to the successful cloning of Yr5, Yr7, YrSP, and Sr45 in a landmark study (Steuernagel et al., 2017) and has since been further enhanced by speed breeding and exome capture. In a recent example, Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated a rapid workflow combining EMS mutagenesis, accelerated generation advancement, and sequencing to clone Sr6 in under 179 days, a notable feat in hexaploid wheat (Wang et al., 2025). Similar approaches have been applied to clone stripe rust genes. For instance, YrNAM was cloned via sequence trait‐associated mutation, a technique involving mutant sequencing and short‐read mapping to the Yr10 region (Ni et al., 2023). As of early 2024, only seven of the 86 catalogued Yr loci had been cloned, including Yr10, Yr5, Yr7, YrSP, Yr15, and Yr18, but this number is steadily increasing. Recent examples include YrU1, an NLR with an integrated WRKY domain cloned via long‐read sequencing, and YrAS2388R, derived from Ae. speltoides, identified through comparative genomics and transformation‐based validation (Lopez et al., 2023). These examples highlight the growing importance of long‐read assemblies, target enrichment sequencing, and pangenomic comparisons in resistance gene discovery. While fine mapping using recombinant populations or bulk segregant RNA sequencing (BSR‐Seq) remains valuable for narrowing down loci, once a region is reduced to ∼1 cM, annotated genome assemblies can help predict candidate genes, typically NLRs rich in NB‐ARC domains. For putative loci like Yr28 or YrC51, intervals can now be mined for NLR‐like genes and prioritized for validation.

Allele mining and functional validation

Allele mining complements gene discovery by exploring genetic variation within diverse germplasm collections to identify novel or functionally superior alleles. In the context of stripe rust, allele mining operates at two levels: (i) identifying new Yr genes and (ii) uncovering functional allelic variants of known resistance genes. Wild relatives and landraces (e.g., Triticum dicoccoides, Triticum speltoides, and Triticum monococcum) are key resources to identify resistance loci that may represent either new genes or allelic variants of existing loci. For instance, Yr15 from wild emmer was found to encode a novel kinase‐START protein (Klymiuk et al., 2018). At the second level, identifying beneficial alleles of known resistance genes is critical for achieving durable resistance. For instance, Yr18 exists in both functional and non‐functional allelic forms, and the former encodes an active ABC transporter, while the latter is rendered inactive due to premature stop codons (T. Fang et al., 2020). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‐based screening has facilitated the widespread deployment of resistant alleles, contributing to durable, slow‐rusting APR. Similar efforts have targeted Yr36 (WKS1) and recently cloned genes such as YrNAM, YrU1, and YrAS2388R. Following the cloning of YrU1, gene‐specific markers revealed its presence in Central Asian landraces, identifying them as valuable donors for breeding programs. Graph‐based pangenome resources, such as Wheat Panache, enhance allele mining by enabling PAV analysis, particularly useful for structurally diverse resistance (R) genes (Bayer et al., 2022). A major challenge in allele mining is the race‐specific nature of many Yr genes, making them susceptible to breakdown by evolving Pst races. To address this, breeders increasingly focus on pyramiding multiple resistance genes, particularly combining ASR and APR loci to enhance durability. Modern genomic tools support this approach by providing diagnostic markers for resistance pyramids (e.g., Yr18, Yr29, and Yr48) and facilitating MAS and GS based on GWAS‐identified QTL/MTAs. For instance, if a QTL on chromosome 5B confers HTAP resistance, SNP‐based assays can be used to screen and select individuals within breeding populations for that trait. This integration of molecular data with breeding pipelines has significantly accelerated the development and deployment of rust‐resistant cultivars.

Despite these advancements, significant challenges persist. Pst continues to evolve rapidly, with emerging virulent races threatening the durability of existing deployed resistance genes, thereby highlighting ongoing need for proactive allele mining. Furthermore, many resistance loci are polygenic or quantitative in nature, comprising small‐effect QTLs that are inherently challenging to clone and validate individually. Genomic prediction (GP) models offer a practical solution by capturing the cumulative effects of these minor QTL without the need for individual gene resolution. Maintaining a global perspective is also critical, as both rust pathogen populations and effective resistance genes exhibit regional variation. Pangenomic efforts involving diverse wheat germplasm from Asia, Africa, the Americas, and Europe have successfully identified region‐specific resistance sources. In summary, the convergence of genome sequencing, pangenomics, and allele mining has transformed stripe rust resistance research, enabling more targeted and efficient breeding strategies. As of 2025, numerous new Yr genes, including not only classical NLRs but also kinases and transporters, have been successfully cloned (Ni et al., 2023), and a wide array of QTL and MTAs have been identified through GWAS (J. Wu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). These discoveries are now being systematically incorporated into breeding pipelines through MAS, gene pyramiding, and GS strategies. Ongoing challenges, such as genetic complexity and evolving pathogen populations, are increasingly being addressed through advanced tools, including long‐read genome sequencing, CRISPR‐based gene validation and stacking, and graph‐based pangenomics. The continued integration of these genomic strategies holds great promise for enhancing the resilience of global wheat production systems and ensuring long‐term food security.

While genomic insights have transformed our understanding of resistance loci and their allelic diversity, the translation of these discoveries into breeding outcomes requires cutting‐edge tools and strategies. The following section explores how genome editing, GS, and haplotype‐based breeding are bridging this gap from gene discovery to varietal deployment.

Translational tools and breeding applications

Genome editing

Genome‐editing technologies have revolutionized plant biology and offer significant potential for accelerating genetic improvement. These tools facilitate precise DNA modifications by inducing site‐specific double‐strand breaks (DSBs) using programmable sequence‐specific nucleases (SSNs) (Saurabh, 2021). The resulting DSBs are repaired by endogenous cellular mechanisms, leading to insertions, deletions, or substitutions at the target locus. The three major classes of SSNs used in plant genome editing are zinc‐finger nucleases, transcription activator‐like effector nucleases, and CRISPR/Cas systems (Kim & Kim, 2014; X. Zhan et al., 2021). Among these, CRISPR‐Cas has become the preferred platform due to its simplicity, high editing efficiency, and cost‐effectiveness. Its RNA‐guided mechanism allows for precise sequence‐specific modifications, including gene knockout, single‐base substitutions, and gene or allele replacement in vivo (Alariqi et al., 2025). Editing the wheat genome editing presents unique challenges due to its large, highly repetitive allohexaploid genome, which complicates precise targeting across homoeologous genes (Petersen et al., 2006). However, recent advances such as improved guide RNA (gRNA) design, development of novel Cas orthologs, and use of engineered variants with expanded protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) recognition have increased both the targeting flexibility and editing precision (Char & Yang, 2020; Gürel et al., 2020; Z. Liu, Liu, et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2021; Walton et al., 2020; D. Zeng et al., 2019).

Efficient delivery of CRISPR/Cas components is critical for successful genome editing in wheat. Agrobacterium tumefaciens‐mediated transformation is the most commonly used method, involving the transfer of T‐DNA from the Ti plasmid into the wheat genome (Gordon & Christie, 2015). However, emerging alternatives such as nanoparticle‐mediated delivery systems are gaining attention for their ability to enhance nucleic acid uptake, protect cargo from degradation, and improve transformation efficiency (Chakraborty et al., 2023; Yau et al., 2024). Another promising approach is ribonucleoprotein (RNP) delivery, which involves the direct introduction of pre‐assembled Cas proteins and gRNAs into cells. This DNA‐free method minimizes off‐target effects and avoids transgene integration, making it particularly suitable for editing elite wheat cultivars (Poddar et al., 2023). Furthermore, the development of high‐fidelity Cas variants and advanced computational platforms for gRNA design has further reduced off‐target activity and improved editing accuracy (L. Zhang et al., 2023).

In host–pathogen interactions, susceptibility (S) genes encode host proteins that are manipulated by pathogen effectors to facilitate infection. These genes often act as negative regulators of defense or as direct targets of pathogen effectors. Recessive resistance can arise from loss‐of‐function mutations in dominant S genes, reducing host susceptibility, a phenomenon known as effector‐triggered susceptibility (Eckardt, 2002). Knocking out S genes via CRISPR/Cas9 has become a powerful strategy for conferring broad‐spectrum, durable disease resistance in crops, including wheat.

Several studies have validated this strategy in wheat (Y. Hu et al., 2025; Yin & Qiu, 2019; Zaidi et al., 2018; J. Zhang et al., 2025). For instance, one such study identified TaPsIPK1, a wheat receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinase gene, as an S gene that facilitates Pst infection. The Pst effector PsSpg1 binds to TaPsIPK1, enhances its kinase activity, and promotes its nuclear translocation, where it phosphorylates the TF TaCBF1d, altering downstream gene expression and promoting susceptibility. CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout of TaPsIPK1 disrupted this effector‐mediated manipulation and conferred broad‐spectrum resistance to epidemic Chinese Pst races under field conditions with no observed yield penalties. Other examples include TaGW2, whose knockout not only enhanced resistance to leaf rust but also improved grain yield traits (N. Wang, Tang, et al., 2022); and TaWRKY19, where gene knockout led to increased resistance to stripe rust (S. Liu, Zhang, et al., 2024). Similarly, TaMPK1 knockout lines showed improved resistance to both stripe rust and powdery mildew (S. Liu, Zhang, et al., 2024).

TaCIPK14 has also been identified as a key negative regulator of wheat defense against Pst. CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout of all three homoeologs of TaCIPK14 generated mutant lines with race‐nonspecific and broad‐spectrum resistance to stripe rust. These mutants exhibited increased ROS accumulation and elevated expression of defense‐related genes. In contrast, overexpression of TaCIPK14 suppressed immune responses and increased susceptibility. These findings underscore the potential of targeting S genes, such as TaCIPK14, to develop wheat cultivars with durable and environmentally resilient resistance (He et al., 2023).

Beyond conferring resistance, genome editing serves as a critical tool for validating candidate genes identified through GWAS and QTL mapping. CRISPR‐based functional validation accelerates the transition from candidate discovery to trait deployment. Moreover, DNA‐free genome editing via RNPs offers regulatory and commercial advantages, as such edits may fall outside genetically modified organism (GMO) regulations in certain countries, enhancing public acceptance and easing commercialization (Adegbaju et al., 2024; Ahmad et al., 2021). Looking forward, genome editing is poised for further evolution through advanced techniques such as multiplex editing, base editing, and prime editing. These innovations allow for simultaneous editing of multiple genes and precise nucleotide changes, supporting trait stacking and fine‐tuning of gene function (Brock et al., 2025). When combined with precise delivery systems and robust regulatory pathways, genome editing stands as a cornerstone of next‐generation wheat breeding strategies for combating stripe rust.

Genomic selection

GS or GP, is a modern breeding strategy that enhances selection accuracy, reduces phenotyping costs, and accelerates breeding cycles (Meuwissen et al., 2001). GS is particularly valuable for improving complex quantitative traits, as it can accelerate the rate of genetic gain. This approach involves developing prediction models using both phenotypic and genotypic data to estimate marker effects, which are then used to calculate genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) for target traits (Azizinia et al., 2020). GS assumes that markers distributed throughout the genome are in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with causal loci. This allows even markers without significant individual effects to contribute to capturing overall genetic variance (Crossa et al., 2017; Robertsen et al., 2019). Model training relies on genotype‐phenotype data from a reference population, and prediction accuracy is typically assessed via cross‐validation, using the correlation between predicted GEBVs and observed phenotypes. The predictive performance of GS models depends on several factors, including population size, marker density, statistical model type, trait heritability, relatedness between training and prediction sets, and the presence of G × E interactions (Bassi et al., 2016; Daetwyler et al., 2008).

Given the polygenic nature and strong G × E interactions of quantitative traits like Pst resistance, GS offers a promising alternative to traditional MAS. In particular, ME GP models can account for environmental variability and improve prediction accuracy (Fones et al., 2020). Both linear models, such as genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) and ridge regression BLUP (RR‐BLUP), and semi‐parametric or nonlinear models, such as reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) regression and BayesB, have demonstrated reliable predictive performance for stripe rust resistance. Semi‐parametric models are particularly advantageous in capturing epistatic interactions (Juliana et al., 2017; Shahinnia et al., 2022). Incorporating known QTL as fixed‐effect covariates has been shown to improve prediction accuracy for stripe rust and other polygenic traits.

Several wheat breeding programs have implemented GS for stripe rust resistance using diverse genetic sources, including Afghan landraces (Tehseen et al., 2021), tetraploid wheat (Azizinia et al., 2020), and global gene bank accessions (Crossa et al., 2017) (Table 4). For instance, Tomar et al. (2021) applied GS to a panel of 141 advanced wheat breeding lines evaluated for APR to stripe rust. ME adjusted rust scores were used to train the models. Prediction accuracies reached 0.59 for GBLUP and RR‐BLUP and up to 0.63 for enhanced GBLUP (EGBLUP) and random forest (RF) models using 14,563 SNPs. The RF and EGBLUP models outperformed traditional models by ∼3%, demonstrating their utility for predicting complex traits.

(2022) identified two predictive SNP loci on chromosomes 2BS (RAC875_c1226_652) and 6AL (Tdurum_contig29607_413), which were validated across three independent populations. Lines carrying resistant haplotypes at both loci showed near‐complete immunity to Pst. Incorporating these markers as fixed effects in GBLUP‐based models improved prediction accuracy by 0.13–0.46. This example highlights how integrating informative SNPs with GS can optimize genotyping resources, particularly for breeding programs with limited budgets where high‐density genotyping may be cost‐prohibitive. Low‐density, trait‐focused genotyping combined with targeted GS offers a scalable and cost‐effective solution. Mahmood et al. (2022) evaluated 11 GP models and reported prediction accuracies up to 0.85 for APR, although prediction accuracy for seedling resistance was lower. Additional studies have examined how parameters like population size, population structure and marker density affect prediction accuracy in the USDA wheat germplasm collection. Prediction accuracies ranged from 0.45 in small populations with low marker density to 0.65 in larger populations with denser marker coverage. Optimizing subpopulation structure for training further improved accuracy to 0.80, suggesting that GS can increase selection intensity and efficiency, thereby boosting genetic gain (Muleta et al., 2017).

In biparental populations, GS models trained on F6 lines and validated on F7 generations across four environments showed higher prediction accuracies for Bayesian models (r = 0.59) compared to RR‐BLUP (r = 0.49). However, predictions across environments yielded lower accuracies (r = 0.36), reinforcing the importance of environment‐specific training. Incorporating G × E terms in Bayesian models moderately improved prediction accuracy (r = 0.57), emphasizing the need for models that are robust across variable environmental conditions (Fatima, 2025).

Beyond elite germplasm, GS holds immense promise in pre‐breeding and germplasm evaluation. It enables breeders to prioritize gene bank accessions and exotic donors by predicting resistance potential early, thereby reducing resource expenditure on low‐performing lines (Khan et al., 2024). Looking forward, the future of GS lies in its integration with high‐throughput phenotyping, enviromics, and advanced computational tools. Deep learning multi‐trait models and environment‐aware prediction frameworks are being actively explored to improve prediction accuracy for complex traits. Haplotype‐based models, in particular, offer the potential to improve predictive power by capturing linkage blocks rather than individual markers. These innovations will increase GS utility across breeding stages, from early‐generation selection to final product development, enabling faster and more resilient genetic gain in wheat improvement programs.

Haplotype‐based breeding

Modern crop breeding aims to assemble favorable trait combinations to develop improved cultivars with improved yield, resilience, and disease resistance. Many traits, including stripe rust resistance, are governed by genomic regions harboring diverse variants such as SNPs, insertions/deletions, and CNVs. These variants often occur in clusters that are inherited together as haplotype blocks of linked alleles, which can serve as more effective units of selection than individual SNPs (Bailey‐Serres et al., 2019; Varshney et al., 2014). Haplotype‐based breeding leverages the non‐random association of these alleles within LD blocks, enabling more precise selection, reduced recombination, and minimized linkage drag compared to single‐marker approaches (Weber et al., 2023). One of the key advantages of haplotype mapping is its ability to dissect the complex genetics of populations that have undergone multiple cycles of selection (Vo Van‐Zivkovic et al., 2025). Unlike conventional GWAS, which focuses on individual SNP‐trait associations, haplotype‐based approaches capture the combined effects of multiple linked variants, providing greater power to dissect complex traits in regions of high LD or structural variation (Bhat et al., 2021). Haplotype markers have shown improved accuracy for trait prediction compared to individual SNPs and have been used to identify superior combinations for traits such as yield, grain quality, and stress tolerance, including rust resistance (Athiyannan, Long, et al., 2022; Athiyannan, Zhang, et al., 2022; Bouvet, Holdgate, et al., 2022). However, relatively few studies have applied haplotype‐based mapping to stripe rust resistance in wheat (Ledesma‐Ramírez et al., 2019; Mahmood et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2021).

For example, in a large‐scale study, Vo Van‐Zivkovic et al. (2025) used haplotype‐based mapping across 35,986 inbred wheat lines evaluated in 10 ME trials. A total of 32 haploblocks were identified, of which 23 conferred environment‐specific resistance and nine showed stability across diverse environments. This work demonstrated the utility of integrating haplotype‐based analysis with G × E modeling to identify robust and context‐specific loci in Australian wheat germplasm. Bouvet, Percival‐Alwyn et al. (2022) investigated resistance sources in multi‐founder wheat populations and identified two to four common haplotypes per locus at four significant multi‐parent advanced generation inter‐cross (MAGIC) QTLs. Low haplotype diversity at these loci was observed in northwestern European cultivars, consistent with previous reports of genetic bottlenecks in that region (Scott et al., 2021). Notably, resistance‐associated haplotypes were rare in these lines, suggesting opportunities for diversifying the resistance base through targeted introgression. Mahmood et al. (2022) conducted an LD‐based haplotype GWAS and identified 13 trait‐associated loci, nine of which were detected exclusively through haplotype‐based analysis, likely missed by SNP‐GWAS due to limitations in LD structure or statistical power. This highlights the increased sensitivity of haplotype approaches in resolving complex genetic architectures. C. Zhou et al. (2022) identified 13 and 11 SNPs significantly associated with two stripe rust QTLs: QYrSM155.1 (2BL) and QYrSM155.2 (7AS). Haplotype analysis of these regions in a global panel of 1101 wheat accessions revealed three major haplotypes for QYrSM155.1 (2B_h1–2B_h3) and four for QYrSM155.2 (7A_h1–7A_h4). Accessions carrying 2B_h1 and/or 7A_h1 exhibited strong resistance. Further screening of 1306 current Chinese cultivars showed that resistance haplotypes were present in only 1.45% and 14.16% of the lines, respectively, highlighting an untapped opportunity to enhance resistance via marker‐assisted introgression.

Despite its promise, haplotype‐based breeding faces technical challenges. Phasing haplotypes accurately in heterozygous or diverse populations remains difficult. LD decay across generations can erode MTAs, and high‐resolution haplotyping requires dense genotyping or long‐read sequencing data. Overcoming these limitations will require advances in haplotype‐resolved pangenomics, statistical phasing, and computational inference tools (Meena et al., 2025). In the future, combining haplotype‐based selection with GP, functional validation, and pangenome graphs will be key to accelerating the development of next‐generation wheat cultivars with durable and broad‐spectrum resistance to stripe rust. While genomic tools and breeding strategies have greatly accelerated the discovery and deployment of resistance genes, achieving durable immunity also requires a deep understanding of the underlying regulatory networks. Transcriptional regulation, epigenetic control, and systems‐level omics approaches are increasingly recognized as crucial components in orchestrating effective and sustained defense responses in wheat.

OMICS‐DRIVEN APPROACHES FOR DURABLE RESISTANCE

Individual omics platforms, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and others, have provided valuable insights into the molecular basis of wheat–Pst interactions, each revealing a distinct perspective on host defense, from gene regulation to protein function to biochemical outputs. When integrated, these datasets offer a systems‐level view of the complex immune response. Transcriptomic analyses (e.g., RNA‐Seq and microarrays) have revealed divergent gene expression profiles between resistant and susceptible cultivars, supporting a biphasic defense model. In resistant lines, defense‐related genes are rapidly upregulated, while in susceptible genotypes, genes associated with nutrient acquisition or suppression of immunity are often induced (Nazarov et al., 2024). These transcriptional responses align with the two‐tiered immune system involving PAMP‐triggered immunity (PTI) and ETI, both of which rely on tightly regulated transcriptional programs. Recent technological advancements now enable scientists to dissect plant–pathogen interactions at single‐cell resolution or within a spatial framework, thereby offering a more holistic view of cellular responses and variability across tissues. Among these, single‐cell RNA sequencing has emerged as a transformative tool that permits high‐resolution transcriptional profiling at both the cellular and nucleotide levels. By enhancing sensitivity and accuracy, this technology has revolutionized transcriptome studies, providing unprecedented insights into the dynamic molecular dialogue between wheat and rust pathogens and allowing researchers to capture transcriptional heterogeneity with remarkable precision (Chetan et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023).

To capture rapid post‐transcriptional events, proteomics approaches, particularly phosphoproteomics, have emerged as a powerful approach. For example, Gan et al. (2024) identified MAPK and phosphatidylinositol signaling pathways that are preferentially activated in resistant lines, suggesting their involvement in early immune signaling. Broader proteomic studies using iTRAQ (Isobaric Tags for Relative and Absolute Quantitation) have revealed thousands of protein‐level changes during infection, many of which colocalize with resistance QTL, notably on chromosome 1BS (H. Zhang et al., 2019). Defense‐associated proteins, including PRs and antioxidant enzymes and flavonoid biosynthesis enzymes, all of which contribute to limiting pathogen growth and spread. Metabolomics adds another layer by uncovering the downstream biochemical changes that result from gene and protein activity. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry‐based untargeted metabolomics studies have shown that resistant cultivars, such as Koonap, accumulate defense‐related secondary metabolites (e.g., rutin and luteolin derivatives) at higher levels upon infection, whereas susceptible cultivars such as Gariep exhibit reduced accumulation of these compounds (Mashabela et al., 2023). Importantly, these metabolite shifts follow complex, nonlinear patterns over time across genotypes, underscoring the dynamic nature of the host metabolic response. While each omics layer contributes unique insights, integrating multiple layers yields a more holistic understanding of resistance mechanisms. For instance, Jiang et al. (2025) combined transcriptomics and metabolomics to uncover the role of tryptophan‐derived metabolites and melatonin in resistance. They identified TaASMT3 as a key regulator, with its function validated through gene silencing. Similarly, J. Liu, Wu et al. (2023) demonstrated that Pst infection induces phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis, with TaPAL playing a key role in resistance, as confirmed by VIGS. Proteomic profiling of wheat–Pst interactions has identified numerous differentially accumulated proteins during infection, many associated with key defense responses such as oxidative stress regulation, signal transduction, and cell wall reinforcement (Y. Yang et al., 2016). For example, PR proteins, antioxidant enzymes (especially SOD and PODs), and proteins involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway are consistently enriched in resistant wheat cultivars during Pst challenge (Liu et al., 2020). Y. Li et al. (2024) recently reported that proteins involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, particularly phenylalanine ammonia lyase (TaPAL), and those associated with lignin synthesis are strongly upregulated in resistant wheat lines. This enhanced activity contributes to strengthened cell wall barriers that restrict pathogen invasion, and the relationship was experimentally validated and linked to improved resistance outcomes. Additional proteomic studies have also identified changes in post‐translational modifications, such as protein phosphorylation, which play critical roles in modulating defense signaling cascades and regulating the activity of resistance‐related proteins during early stages of infection (Gan et al., 2024).

These integrative approaches reveal how transcriptional regulation, protein function, and metabolic reprogramming collectively orchestrate effective immune responses. Despite their promise, multiomics studies face several challenges. Temporal and spatial mismatches between mRNA, protein, and metabolite levels complicate data integration. The polyploidy and large genome size of wheat further hinder the precise mapping of gene expression, especially for homeologs. Additionally, proteomic and metabolomic studies remain underrepresented compared with transcriptomics, leaving gaps in our understanding of post‐transcriptional and biochemical defenses. Functional validation of omics‐derived candidates through gene knockdown, gene overexpression, or biochemical assays also remains essential to confirm causal roles and ensure translational relevance. Despite these limitations, multi‐omics approaches are reshaping our understanding of the wheat immune response to stripe rust. By integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data, researchers can construct detailed models of resistance pathways. Continued investment in multi‐layer data integration, along with functional validation, will be critical for translating these insights into improved breeding strategies and durable resistance in the field. While molecular and regulatory strategies are key to enhancing resistance, the durability and effectiveness of such resistance are increasingly challenged by climate change. Understanding how shifting climatic patterns influence stripe rust epidemiology is critical for future‐proofing wheat production systems.

Spatial omics: A frontier for disease resistance research in wheat

Spatial omics technologies are increasingly recognized as transformative tools for resolving cellular processes with high spatial precision. By capturing spatial heterogeneity and dynamic molecular changes, they have greatly advanced the study of complex biological systems (A. Rao et al., 2021; H. Zeng et al., 2023). Among these platforms, spatial transcriptomics has become the most widely adopted, while spatial proteomics, metabolomics, and epigenomics still face technical hurdles that limit their broader application. In plant sciences, spatial transcriptomics is emerging as a powerful approach for examining tissue‐specific gene expression within its native spatial context (S. Fang et al., 2022). In wheat, however, applications of spatial transcriptomics remain scarce. Existing studies have primarily focused on developmental processes such as spike and grain formation (Li et al., 2025; Long et al., 2024), root architecture (Ke et al., 2025), and coleoptile–Fusarium interactions (Wei et al., 2024). No comprehensive spatial transcriptomic atlas currently exists for wheat, representing a major gap in understanding spatial gene regulation in this globally important crop. Developing such an atlas would greatly enhance the discovery of key genes, proteins, and metabolites underlying agronomic traits, including stress tolerance, yield, and grain quality.

Applying spatial omics to biotic stress responses, particularly stripe rust, holds significant promise. These technologies can precisely localize immune responses at different stages of infection, identify the specific cell types targeted by the pathogen, and provide high‐resolution maps of defense gene expression. This would enable (i) delineation of regulatory networks governing wheat–pathogen interactions and (ii) expansion of the resistance gene repertoire available for breeding programs. Despite this potential, no published single‐cell or spatial omics studies have yet examined stripe rust resistance in wheat. Considering the complexity of the wheat polyploid genome and the spatial dynamics of Pst invasion, integrating spatial transcriptomics and single‐cell omics into wheat pathology research could revolutionize our understanding of host–pathogen interactions. These approaches may uncover cell‐type‐specific immune responses, reveal spatial regulation of defense pathways, and ultimately support precision breeding strategies based on spatially resolved resistance loci (Barmukh et al., 2025). Addressing this current gap is an important frontier for advancing durable resistance against stripe rust and other major diseases in wheat.

IMPACT OF CLIMATE CHANGE ON STRIPE RUST EPIDEMIOLOGY

Climate change, driven by greenhouse gas emissions, fossil fuel use, and deforestation, has increased global temperatures by approximately 1.1°C above pre‐industrial levels (Hussain et al., 2025; WMO, 2022; 2023). This warming is already impacting global agricultural productivity (Ortiz‐Bobea et al., 2021), with one of the most striking effects being the shifting dynamics of plant diseases. Stripe rust, caused by Pst, exemplifies this trend. With its ability for long‐distance dispersal, rapid evolution of virulent races, and adaptation to diverse climates, Pst has emerged as a major threat to global wheat production (S. Ali & Hodson, 2017). The infection biology of Pst is highly influenced by meteorological conditions, especially temperature and moisture. As global temperatures rise and precipitation patterns shifts, Pst outbreaks are affecting the seasonality, geographic distribution, and severity (Hunjan & Lore, 2020; Nnadi & Carter, 2021). Climate modeling and forecasting tools are now increasingly used to simulate disease–weather interactions and identify emerging hotspots.

Over the past decade, significant shifts in Pst distribution have been observed across the Indian subcontinent, North America, Europe, Serbia, and South Africa. Historically, Pst was considered a pathogen favoring cool, temperate climates, with higher temperatures believed to restrict its spread (Dennis, 1987). However, recent evidence indicates that Pst populations have adapted to warmer environments, increasing their aggressiveness and fitness, independent of virulence gene interactions (R. P. Singh et al., 2025; Walter et al., 2016). Bebber et al. (2013) reported poleward migration of several fungal pathogens, including Pst, due to rising temperatures and changing precipitation. Projections based on the 2016 stripe rust epidemic and climate projections suggest a northward shift in Pst's overwintering range, increasing the risk of earlier and more severe outbreaks in North America (Lyon & Broders, 2017).

This trend is echoed globally. In France, warmer Mediterranean climates have favored southern‐adapted Pst races (de Vallavieille‐Pope et al., 2018). In Europe, aggressive lineages like PstS1, PstS2, and the Warrior race group have established themselves in both temperate and warmer regions, with outbreaks reported in Serbia (Jevtić et al., 2020, 2017). Between 2016 and 2023, Serbia experienced significant climatic fluctuations, with winter temperature variations of up to 10°C and March–April precipitation ranging from 1 to 65.5 mm and 11.1 to 74.5 mm, respectively, conditions that likely favored the emergence of aggressive Pst races. Similarly, in India, thermal adaptation is evident in the emergence of pathotype 78S84, which can infect at higher temperatures (∼18°C) relative to the older dominant 46S119 pathotype (∼15°C) (Bhardwaj et al., 2019). New virulent pathotypes like 238S119, 110S84, and 110S119 have also overcome resistance in cultivars carrying YrSU, Yr2, Yr3a, and Yr4a (Gangwar et al., 2019). The detection of Kranich‐type races on both triticale and wheat in Europe (M. S. Hovmøller et al., 2016) and the spread of PstS1 and PstS2 to regions like Western Australia and the southeastern United States, regions once considered too warm, further highlight the pathogen's expanding thermal tolerance (Milus et al., 2009).

Evidence for physiological adaptation is mounting. Comparative studies showed that post‐2000 Pst isolates germinate faster, are more virulent, and have shorter latent periods at 18°C than isolates from the 1970s, with smaller differences at 12°C (Prasad et al., 2020). Gardner et al. (2023) also demonstrated that modern Pst races can recover from heat stress more rapidly than previously thought, further underscoring their evolving resilience. In South Africa, shifting rainfall patterns have been linked to the emergence of Pst (Boshoff et al., 2002), while projections suggest that declining dew point temperatures could increase the frequency of high‐infection days (PstHI), further exacerbating disease risk (Esmail et al., 2023), reinforcing the need to incorporate resistance to such conditions into breeding programs. Collectively, these findings highlight the rapid evolutionary responses of Pst to climate change and the increasing threat it poses to global wheat production. Changes in temperature and precipitation are not only driving more frequent and severe epidemics but also undermining the durability of resistance genes and expanding the pathogen's reach into previously unsuitable areas (Eastburn et al., 2011). As a result, breeding efforts must now contend with both biotic and abiotic pressures in tandem.

To build climate‐resilient disease management strategies, the following priorities must be integrated into breeding pipelines: real‐time pathogen surveillance systems to track virulence shifts and new Pst races,international data‐sharing platforms for harmonizing rust diagnostics and virulence monitoring,integration of climate forecasting models into disease early‐warning systems, anddevelopment of climate‐smart cultivars combining durable rust resistance with abiotic stress tolerance.

As climate change continues to expand the geographic range and aggressiveness of Pst, it underscores the urgent need for integrated disease management (IDM) strategies that combine genetic and non‐genetic tools to future‐proof wheat production.

Integrated Disease Management (IDM) AND DURABLE RESISTANCE

Effective disease management seeks to disrupt the interactions among the host, pathogen, and environment, conceptualized as the classical disease triangle. Among available strategies, the development and deployment of wheat cultivars with durable and broad‐spectrum resistance is widely recognized as the most efficient, economical, and environmentally sustainable approach for controlling Pst epidemics (Cobo et al., 2019; J. Zhou, Singh, et al., 2021). Genetic resistance not only suppresses disease outbreaks but also limits geographic expansion and seasonal recurrence (Cao et al., 2012). As discussed earlier, recent advances in genomic technologies, such as GWAS and QTL mapping, have enabled the identification of hundreds of resistance loci across diverse wheat germplasm (Arora et al., 2019), forming the backbone of resistance breeding.

However, the continuous emergence of new, highly virulent Pst races and the lack of universal resistance genes pose major challenges to long‐term disease control (Ellis et al., 2014). Over‐dependence on mega‐varieties carrying race‐specific R genes has led to “boom‐and‐bust” cycles, where initial success is followed by sudden resistance breakdown. Therefore, achieving durable and sustainable Pst management requires an IDM framework, an approach that combines host resistance with complementary strategies such as cultural practices, fungicide application, biological control, and emerging technologies to minimize environmental and societal impacts (Zhao & Kang, 2023).

Cultural practices and fungicide application

Cultural practices serve as the first line of defense in IDM by enhancing crop resilience and reducing initial pathogen load. Recommended measures include avoiding excessive nitrogen fertilization, eliminating alternate hosts and volunteer wheat that serve as disease reservoirs, and ensuring proper irrigation to prevent prolonged leaf wetness (Farber, 2016). Crop rotation also disrupts the lifecycle of the pathogen and may slow down genetic diversification over time in Pst populations (X. Chen, 2017). Fungicides remain critical under high disease pressure or when resistance fails. Their effective use depends on disease scouting, appropriate application timing, varietal resistance level, disease severity, crop developmental stage, and prevailing weather conditions (Thind, 2021). Modern fungicides like strobilurins and triazoles offer targeted high‐efficacy control of fungal pathogens with minimal environmental footprint (Ul Haq & Ijaz, 2020). Improved formulations have enhanced persistence and reduced application frequency and mitigated the risk of resistance development. Field trials show their ability to suppress Pst, reduce sporulation, and enhance yield and 1000‐grain weight (Tak et al., 2021).

Biological control

Biological control offers an eco‐friendly alternative that leverages naturally occurring microbial antagonists to suppress Pst. Several bacterial species, including Serratia marcescens, Bacillus megaterium, Paenibacillus xylanexedens, Bacillus subtilis, and Staphylococcus agent, have demonstrated strong inhibitory effects on Pst spore germination (Belay, 2023). Bacillus subtilis, in particularly, is well‐studied, with both cell‐containing and cell‐free fermentation liquids reducing disease severity by up to 87.7% under greenhouse conditions (H. Li et al., 2013; Reiss & Jørgensen, 2017). Fungal hyperparasites such as Lecanicillium lecanii, Typhula idahoensis, Microdochium nivale (Littlefield, 1981), Cladosporium cladosporiodes (H. Zhang et al., 2022), and Alternaria alternata also hold promise and have shown the ability to parasitize and suppress Pst urediniospores (L. Zheng et al., 2017). These biological agents could be developed into biocontrol formulations, particularly for use in low‐input or organic farming systems.

Precision tools for enhancing wheat resistance

Remote sensing, integrated with machine learning and imaging platforms is transforming disease monitoring by enabling accurate, rapid, and non‐invasive assessment of disease severity and resistance under field conditions (J. Deng et al., 2024; S. Kumar et al., 2025; T. Xu et al., 2025). These tools enhance early detection and support precision management, thereby strengthening the implementation of IDM. Early warning systems like the Wheat Disease Early Warning and Advisory System provide location‐specific alerts and decision support to farmers and breeders, enabling timely interventions and supporting precision disease management (Patpour et al., 2024). Despite recent advances, stripe rust continues to threaten global wheat yields. The path forward lies in integrated solutions that bridge molecular innovations, digital tools, agronomic practices, and surveillance systems. For IDM to succeed at scale, sustained investments in real‐time pathogen tracking, climate‐resilient cultivar development, and international data‐sharing platforms will be essential (Gilligan, 2024). Only through cross‐disciplinary collaboration can we build resilient wheat systems capable of withstanding the twin pressures of evolving pathogens and climate change.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Stripe rust, caused by Pst, continues to pose a major threat to global wheat production due to its rapid evolution, long‐distance dispersal, and increasing adaptation to warmer climates. Its ability to overcome race‐specific resistance genes and expand into new geographies has undermined traditional breeding strategies and raised urgent calls for more durable, forward‐looking solutions. Over the past two decades, significant progress has been made in unraveling the biology, population dynamics, and infection strategies of Pst. Advances in field‐based epidemiology, race surveillance, and climate modeling have helped track the global spread of virulent races and understand how climatic shifts are altering the geography and timing of stripe rust epidemics. Simultaneously, the discovery and deployment of APR genes and QTL have laid the foundation for more stable and broad‐spectrum resistance, though their effectiveness is often partial and environmentally influenced.

The integration of advanced genomic tools, including high‐throughput sequencing, GWAS, and pangenomics, has transformed the resistance breeding landscape. These platforms have enabled the identification of novel alleles, elucidation of complex resistance mechanisms, and fine mapping of key loci. Transcriptomic and proteomic studies have illuminated dynamic and biphasic defense responses in resistant wheat genotypes, while regulatory components such as TFs (e.g., WRKY, AP2/ERF, BZR), signaling kinases, and coactivators (e.g., NPR1 and SGT1) have emerged as critical control points. Moreover, the role of epigenetic modifications and small RNAs in modulating immune responses offers promising new avenues for resistance manipulation.

Modern biotechnologies, such as CRISPR‐based genome editing, MAS, and GS, now allow for the precise pyramiding of resistance loci and the tailoring of elite cultivars with enhanced disease resilience. Multi‐omics integration and remote sensing tools are also accelerating the transition from discovery to deployment, enabling early detection, precision management, and rapid breeding decisions. Yet, the durability of resistance remains fragile in the face of evolving Pst populations and accelerating climate change. Future success will depend not only on genetic innovation but also on holistic IDM strategies that combine host resistance with agronomic, chemical, biological, and digital tools. The development of climate‐smart cultivars, those that combine resistance with tolerance to abiotic stresses will be essential for resilience in changing agroecological contexts.

Looking ahead, research priorities should include: discovery and functional validation of novel resistance genes, especially from wild relatives and landraces,expansion of haplotype‐based breeding and pangenomic resources to capture hidden diversity,elucidation of regulatory and epigenetic mechanisms that control durable resistance,continued refinement of predictive models for disease forecasting under future climate scenarios, andstrengthening real‐time global surveillance, data‐sharing platforms, and farmer‐centric decision support tools.
