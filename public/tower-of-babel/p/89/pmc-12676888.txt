Unlocking survival in traumatic cardiac arrest: global insights, innovations, and unmet needs
Authors: Shouyin Jiang, Jintao Wei, Taiwen Rao, Xiangkang Lv, Bojin Chen, Xiaogang Zhao, Shanxiang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA) remains one of the most formidable challenges in acute care, with global survival rates persistently below 7.5% despite significant advances in trauma systems and resuscitative strategies. Through a comprehensive scoping review of literature from 2016 to 2025, this study delineates the shifting epidemiology, modern management, and key determinants of outcomes in TCA. Our synthesis reveals that TCA predominantly affects young males and results primarily from blunt mechanisms, with severe hemorrhage and traumatic brain injury as leading critical lesions. While pioneering interventions such as resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta and emergency resuscitative thoracotomy have been widely adopted, robust evidence for their survival benefit remains limited and context dependent. Early identification and correction of reversible causes, targeted hemorrhage control, and timely application of advanced life support measures-especially prehospital thoracostomy and vascular access-significantly increase the likelihood of return of spontaneous circulation and favorable neurological outcomes. Conversely, routine use of epinephrine shows at best inconsistent benefits and may be detrimental in blunt trauma. Survival is further influenced by initial rhythm, presence of signs of life, rapid patient triage to trauma centers, and tailored protocols for pediatric and older populations. Notwithstanding notable progress, major knowledge gaps persist in defining optimal intervention timing, reporting standards, and long-term neurologic recovery. The findings underscore the critical need for multicenter, prospective research, harmonized trauma registries, and global consensus to inform future guidelines and optimize outcomes for TCA patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05700-4.

Introduction

A uniformly defined traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA) is characterized by the sudden cessation of cardiac output following severe mechanical trauma and is distinguished from medical cardiac arrest by distinct differences in etiology, pathophysiology, and management priorities. The management of reversible etiologies of TCA necessitates rapid identification and immediate surgical or mechanical intervention, as conventional pharmacologic resuscitation is insufficient without addressing these definitive causes [1, 2]. Despite incremental advancements and the widespread implementation of algorithm-based management strategies, global survival rates for TCA stubbornly remain very low and unchanged across most published cohorts [3–5]. Striking epidemiological heterogeneity-attributable to patient age, trauma mechanism, and regional or socioeconomic factors-continues to magnify disparities in outcomes, further widening the survival gap between high-resource and resource-limited settings [6].

Over the past decade, there has been a proliferation of advanced resuscitative techniques using in severe trauma, including endovascular interventions and mechanical circulatory support systems [7, 8]. Nonetheless, high-quality evidence delineating the optimal timing and indications for key interventions-such as epinephrine administration, resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA), and Emergency resuscitative thoracotomy (ERT) in TCA-remains limited. Special populations-including pediatric, geriatric, and maternal patients-further complicate the landscape, with prognostic determinants and best-practice protocols insufficiently defined across these diverse groups. The translation of technological innovation and basic research advances into routine clinical practice is also hindered by the limitations of current preclinical TCA models and a lack of integration tailored to specific patient populations and scenarios. In this review, we synthesize current evidence concerning the epidemiology, clinical management, and technological innovations in TCA. By critically evaluating ongoing controversies, underscoring the challenges unique to specific populations, and outlining future research directions, our goal is to bridge foundational knowledge gaps and facilitate the evolution of targeted, evidence-based care for TCA [9].

Review methodology

This scoping review followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines [10]. PubMed and Web of Science Core Collection were systematically searched for studies on TCA published from January 2016 to August 2025. Eligible publications included peer-reviewed clinical or preclinical studies, systematic reviews, or guidelines directly addressing TCA, with sample size >20 where applicable. Case reports, narrative reviews, non-English papers, preprints, and studies lacking clinical outcomes or focused on non-TCA topics were excluded. Two reviewers independently screened articles, resolving disagreements by consensus. Of 487 records identified, 76 original studies and 8 guideline or consensus papers met inclusion criteria for final synthesis (see Fig. 1 for selection flow).

Epidemiology and survival outcomes

TCA represents a rare but devastating entity within trauma care, accounting for approximately 2–10% of EMS-attended trauma cases, with estimated annual incidences ranging from 2.0 to 8.0 per 100,000 population worldwide [6, 11–13] (Fig. 2, detailed in eTable 1 in the Supplemental Materials). Multi-national and regional registers consistently report a male predominance (70–85%), and a median age typically in the third to fourth decade of life-though a notable minority occurs in children and adolescents, especially young boys [12, 14, 15]. Mechanistically, blunt trauma remains the leading cause (comprising 60–90% of cases in most civilian settings), while penetrating mechanisms, such as stab or gunshot wounds, predominate in certain urban or combat environments [16–18]. Road traffic accidents are a primary etiology globally, accounting for 42–76% of TCA cases in several series, whereas falls and assaults make up smaller but significant proportions [19, 20]. The overwhelming majority of TCAs are associated with multi-system injury, with severe hemorrhage and traumatic brain injury being the most prevalent critical lesions; up to 85–95% of patients exhibit at least one of these components [12, 18, 21]. The in-field and in-hospital outcomes for TCA remain poor, though large-scale studies demonstrate that survival is not negligible. TCA most commonly presents with non-shockable initial rhythms, asystole (60–80%) and pulseless electrical activity (PEA) (15–35%), with shockable rhythms (ventricular fibrillation/tachycardia) occurring in only 1–7% of cases [12, 14, 22]. Approximately 60–85% of TCA cases occur prior to hospital arrival, with the remainder experiencing cardiac arrest in the emergency department [11, 12, 21].

The resuscitation attempts in out-of-hospital TCA were less than 30% [23]. Globally, the prehospital return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) rate following TCA is generally low. Several large registry and cohort studies describe rates of ROSC ranging from about 14.6% to 30.6% depending on system factors, trauma mechanisms, and type of receiving hospital [12, 14, 16, 24, 25] (detailed in eTable 2 in the Supplemental Materials). Comparative studies have also shown differences in ROSC rates between subpopulations: for example, pediatric TCA patients have demonstrated higher ROSC rates than adults (25.5% vs. 20.6%, p = 0.02) [12]. Certain prehospital interventions, such as vascular access and advanced airway management, are frequently performed, but their association with ROSC rates is inconsistent across studies [14, 25]. Notably, trauma centers report higher rates of ROSC (approximately 30%) compared to non-trauma centers (12%) in some regional analyses [24]. Additionally, the initial presenting rhythm is a key epidemiologic variable; non-shockable rhythms such as asystole are overwhelmingly predominant, but PEA is associated with higher rates of ROSC compared with asystole (49% vs. 26%, p = 0.007) [26].

Most TCA patients die before paramedic or hospital arrival [5, 27]. A review of 51,722 prehospital TCA cases reported a mortality of 96.2% [28]. Thirty-day survival rates from national or multicenter registries remain low, typically 3.4–7.5% [21, 22, 29]. Survival to hospital discharge is higher for in-hospital than out-of-hospital TCA (OR 6.3, 95% CI 1.3–31.2) [11]. Compared to medical OHCA, TCA has significantly lower survival both at admission and 30 days (adjusted OR 0.416 and 0.168, respectively) [30]. French data showed ROSC, survival to admission, and 30-day survival were 20.9%, 15.4%, and 1.7% among cases under 65 [12]; US registry data showed a 6.3% prehospital TCA discharge rate [31]. In Japan, EMS-witnessed TCA had a higher 30-day survival (10.9%) versus bystander-witnessed (7.2%) and unwitnessed (5.6%) cases [32]. Pediatric outcomes may be better, with a French cohort reporting 30-day survival of 3.5% in children vs. 1.6% in adults [12], though a US database showed 1.3% overall pediatric survival [33]. In military settings, survival is extremely poor; none of 149 prehospital TCA cases in an Israeli military study survived to hospital admission [34].

Among those who do survive, over 40–79% achieve favorable neurological outcomes; this proportion is higher if prehospital deaths are excluded, and similar across ages [4, 12, 22, 28, 35].

Clinical research updates on management of TCA

In recent years, our understanding and management of TCA have advanced considerably, propelled by a burgeoning body of clinical research and evolving guidelines [36–40]. Over the past decade, studies have largely validated core international recommendations, underscoring the critical importance of early identification and correction of reversible causes, prompt hemorrhage control, and judicious application of ERT and blood products (Table 1, detailed in eTable 3 in the Supplemental Materials). Nevertheless, substantial practice variability persists, and key evidence gaps remain. This section synthesizes seminal findings from contemporary research on the clinical management of TCA, integrating rigorous statistical analyses and highlighting unresolved challenges that warrant further investigation.

Airway management

While rapid airway control is generally advocated, recent large cohort and observational studies have refined our understanding of the benefit-risk ratio between modalities. A nationwide Japanese retrospective cohort analyzing 1,524 TCA patients compared prehospital endotracheal intubation (ETI) with supraglottic airway or manual management [41]. Prehospital ETI was associated with a higher likelihood of favorable neurological outcome at discharge (GOS ≥ 4: 1.4% vs. 0.9%; marginal OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.12 to 3.53), and increased survival to discharge (6.8% vs. 5.5%; marginal OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.08–1.90) [41]. However, for patients without severe chest injury or with short transport times, benefit was not demonstrable, raising the issue of selective application. Another Japanese registry study among EMS-witnessed TCA patients confirmed that while prehospital advanced airway management (AAM) increased ROSC on admission (AAM 21.0% vs. non-AAM 11.4%; OR 2.05, 95% CI 1.51–2.78), survival to discharge was not significantly altered (AAM 6.5% vs. non-AAM 5.8%; OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.70–1.76, p = 0.64) [42]. The critical importance of provider training and procedural decisiveness is further evidenced by reports of prehospital scalpel cricothyroidotomy. In a 20-year London prehospital audit, scalpel cricothyroidotomy had a procedural success rate of 97%, yet 56.9% of cases were already in TCA at time of intervention, and overall survival to discharge was only 6.9% [43].

Thoracic decompression and thoracostomy

Chest decompression is pivotal for the management of tension pneumothorax (incidence of 9.7% in TCA) or hemothorax, yet ongoing debate persists on the optimal technique and timing [44]. Recent evidence strongly suggests that finger thoracostomy and surgical thoracotomy are often superior to needle thoracostomy, particularly in preventing decompression failure. For adults, a Canadian autopsy series found that needle thoracostomy failed to decompress a massive hemothorax in 42% of cases, despite widespread usage on scene [17]. In Lausanne, a prehospital system with bilateral thoracostomy guidelines reported an increase in annual thoracostomy rates from zero to 100% over 2012–2024, with 66% of indicated patients receiving the procedure. Among those who underwent thoracostomy, 6.1% survived to 48 h [45]. Prehospital thoracostomy by paramedics appears safe and potentially effective [46]. Australian data found finger thoracostomy performed bilaterally in 73.8% of cases, with very low complication rates; of note, all patients who underwent prior needle thoracocentesis ultimately required surgical thoracostomy [47]. Pediatric evidence from the same group confirmed the technical feasibility and safety of pediatric thoracostomy, suggesting it should be the first-line decompressive intervention [48].

ERT and REBOA

Advanced resuscitative procedures for TCA management, such as ERT and REBOA have yielded controversial results, despite their broad integration into clinical management algorithms [49–51]. A landmark London cohort (n = 601) of prehospital ERT cases reported 5% overall survival to hospital discharge, increasing to 21% with isolated cardiac tamponade but only 1.9% in exsanguination. No survivors were recorded if ERT was performed beyond critical time thresholds: >15 min for tamponade and >5 min after exsanguination [18]. However, a small-sample study showed that although prehospital ERT (performed by a helicopter emergency medical service) enabled ROSC in 25% of patients, no trauma patient survived to hospital discharge during the 6-year study period [52]. A systematic review of 3251 patients receiving emergency department ERT supported survival benefits in use of ERT in penetrating trauma (OR 2.10; p 0.0028) and presenting with signs of life (SOL) (OR 5.36; p < 0.0001) [53]. However, in the United States, the increasing utilization of ERT has not translated to improved mortality among adults with penetrating TCA [54]. The debate between ERT and REBOA remains nuanced. A systematic review and meta-analysis by EAST found no significant difference in overall mortality between REBOA and no REBOA in unstable trauma patients (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.37–2.04), but REBOA for TCA was associated with a lower pooled mortality than ERT (OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.15–0.69) [55]. Large propensity-matched analyses in Japan echoed this: among 1,483 t-OHCA patients, survival to discharge for REBOA was 3.5% versus 0.7% for ERT (OR 4.78, 95% CI 1.61–14.19) [56]. Yet, in a US defense trauma cohort (n = 72), both interventions yielded similar, grim mortality rates (REBOA 88% vs. RT 93%) [57]. Notably, resource constraints-exemplified during the COVID-19 pandemic-were associated with reduced blood product use but unchanged RT survival rates (9–11%) [58].

Fluid resuscitation and hemorrhage control

Early and adequate volume replacement, bleeding control, and prevention of exsanguination are central tenants in TCA management. French registry data demonstrate that the usage of intraosseous access (IO) increased linearly from 7% in 2013 to 33% in 2021 for all vascular access in OHCA and was higher in TCA (12% vs. 5%). Yet, survival with IO remained lower than with peripheral access (1% vs. 7%, p < 0.0001), possibly attributable to greater clinical severity [59]. On-scene (aOR: 2.14 [1.31, 3.49]) but not en route vascular access has been shown to be associated with increased odds of ROSC at ED arrival [60]. In a large-scale nationwide observational study, prehospital hydration has been demonstrated to be associated with favorable survival outcomes in patients with a non-shockable rhythm [61]. Meanwhile, massive transfusion and prompt external bleeding control are associated with increased ROSC and survival. In a French registry of 4,922 TCA patients, intravenous fluid (OR 3.19), red cell transfusion (OR 2.54), and external hemorrhage control (OR 1.74) were all independent predictors of sustained ROSC (21.1% achieved on scene), with 72.2% of survivors achieving good neurologic outcomes at 30 days [62]. Pelvic binders and tourniquet application, when indicated (limb hemorrhage), have also been positively associated with improved short-term survival [62].

Vasopressors and adrenaline use

The role of adrenaline in TCA remains highly controversial, with several robust studies yielding conflicting outcomes. An analysis of a publicly available dataset comprising 4,942 TCA patients demonstrated a 5% reduction in the odds of ROSC) at emergency department arrival for each additional minute elapsed prior to epinephrine administration [60]. A sub-analysis of the PARAMEDIC-2 trial, encompassing 123 TCA cases, found that adrenaline administration tripled the likelihood of survival to hospital admission (24.2% vs. 8.9%; odds ratio [OR] 3.3, 95% CI 1.1–9.6); however, only a single patient survived to 30 days with a favorable neurological outcome [25]. In contrast, a multicenter retrospective analysis from the United States and Canada (n = 1,631) identified a significant association between prehospital adrenaline use and decreased survival to hospital discharge (5% vs. 16%, p < 0.001; adjusted relative risk [RR] 0.33, 95% CI 0.23–0.46), with the effect being most pronounced in cases of blunt trauma (adjusted RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.11–0.37) [63]. A Japanese registry of 1,030 adult traumatic OHCA patients similarly observed that epinephrine administration was linked to a significantly lower 7-day survival rate (1.1% vs. 5.3%; OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.08–0.48) [64]. In Taiwan, an analysis of 5,204 TCA cases found that prehospital adrenaline was not significantly associated with improved 1-month survival (1.5% vs. 0.9%; OR 1.495, 95% CI 0.758–2.946), but was associated with an increased rate of prehospital ROSC (9.8% vs. 0.9%; OR 3.784, 95% CI 2.102–6.812) [65]. These disparate findings underscore the ongoing uncertainty regarding the efficacy of adrenaline in TCA and highlight the need for further high-quality trials to define its optimal role in this critical patient population.

Timing, systems, and bundled care

Systematic adherence to evidence-based resuscitation bundles and regionalization of trauma care have marked effects on survival. Recent work from Taiwan demonstrated that bypassing non-trauma centers and directly triaging patients to dedicated trauma centers increased ROSC rates (30.6% direct/cross-regional vs. 12.0% non-TC) and was associated with improved survival (adjusted OR for direct transport: 2.91, 95% CI 1.54–5.49) [24]. Transferring of TCA patients requiring CPR through helicopter ambulance was associated higher survival (absolute risk reduction 0.066, 95%CI 0.008–0.125 [66]. In high-volume trauma centers, the introduction of formal thoracotomy or TCA management programs was associated with increases in improved hemodynamic parameters and survival rates, without increased procedural complications [67]. Adherence to guideline-based care bundles may exert a positive impact on survival outcomes in patients with TCA. However, substantial variability exists in global TCA clinical practices. For instance, ERT appears to be underutilized or without unified indications [68, 69] and approximately one-quarter of surveyed clinicians report never employing advanced life support for TCA management [70]. Furthermore, an observed discrepancy between recommended and actual emergency medical services (EMS) approaches to TCA care across the United States underscores the critical need for a standardized prehospital management protocol for TCA [71].

Predictors for ROSC and survival

Recent evidence indicates that ROSC following TCA is influenced by multiple demographic, clinical, and procedural factors (Table 2, detailed in eTable 4 in the Supplemental Materials). Pediatric patients demonstrate a significantly higher rate of prehospital ROSC compared to adults (25.5% vs. 20.6%, p = 0.02) [12]. The presence of SOL at the scene-such as gasping and spontaneous activity-is associated with increased odds of achieving sustained ROSC (OR for gasping: 1.40, 95% CI 1.02–1.94; p < 0.001) [62]. Initial cardiac rhythm is a major determinant: presenting with PEA or a shockable rhythm confers significantly higher rates of ROSC compared to asystole (PEA OR: 1.81, 95% CI 1.40–2.35; shockable rhythm OR: 1.83, 95% CI 1.12–2.98 [26, 62]. Prehospital interventions independently associated with ROSC include intravenous fluid resuscitation (OR: 3.19, 95% CI 2.69–3.78), blood transfusion (OR: 2.54, 95% CI 1.84–3.51), external hemorrhage control (OR: 1.74, 95% CI 1.31–2.30), and thoracostomy [62]. Early on-scene advanced life support, especially timely vascular access, further increases the likelihood of ROSC [60], while transport to trauma centers, whether direct or cross-regional, is associated with higher ROSC rates (30.6% and 30.5%, respectively, vs. 12.0% for non-trauma centers) [24]. Importantly, in patients without prehospital ROSC, trauma center admission, length of ED resuscitation, and resuscitation length >20 min were associated with in-hospital ROSC [72]. It should be noted that spinal immobilization in blunt trauma correlates with a significantly lower ROSC rate (OR: 0.48, 95% CI 0.27–0.87) [73]. These findings underscore the critical impact of age, initial rhythm, SOL, targeted prehospital interventions, and transport strategies on the likelihood of ROSC in TCA.

A comprehensive analysis of current evidence reveals that the primary determinants of survival in TCA are injury mechanism, demographic factors, prehospital and in-hospital interventions, and system-level factors. Blunt trauma predominates (around 79% globally), yet survival is significantly higher in penetrating trauma, and especially in those with treatable causes such as cardiac tamponade-survival in this group reaches 21%, compared to only 1.9% for exsanguination [12, 16–18]. The presence of a shockable rhythm or pulseless electrical activity (vs. asystole) significantly increases the likelihood of survival (aORs 3.98–12.0.98.0) [29]. Younger age, female, lower ISS, and SOL at hospital arrival are independently associated with increased survival (SOL: 8.8% vs. 1.3%, OR 1.96; age < 40 and penetrating injury confer probabilities up to 83.3% with both present) [2, 74, 75]. Pediatric patients demonstrate slightly superior 30-day survival compared with adults (3.5% vs. 1.6%, p < 0.01), though favorable neurological outcome rates remain low and comparable across ages [12]. Importantly, in both children and adults, survivors to discharge often achieve favorable neurological status (up to 45.8% in systematic reviews), emphasizing that early, aggressive, etiology-driven care in a trauma-system context is not futile [26, 35, 63, 75].

Early, multi-faceted, and systemic interventions are crucial for TCA survival. Especially, bystander CPR and minimizing prehospital transport time contribute to improved survival to discharge [29, 76]. Patients with TCA in urban centers were more likely to survive than those in rural areas [77]. Prehospital thoracostomy (especially by finger technique) has become the preferred approach for chest decompression, outperforming needle thoracostomy, which fails in 42% of massive hemothorax cases [17, 45, 75]. Prehospital endotracheal intubation confers improved neurological outcome and survival in patients with severe chest injury or transport time >15 min (marginal OR for favorable neurological outcome 1.99; for survival 1.43, both p < 0.05) [41]. Early establishment of vascular access for fluid resuscitation (ideally with blood products) further increases odds of survival [27, 32]. The use of epinephrine remains controversial-while it increases the odds of ROSC, mounting evidence demonstrates decreased survival to discharge, especially in blunt trauma cohorts (aRR 0.20–0.33, p < 0.001) [25, 63]. Advanced hemorrhage control modalities, such as REBOA, are associated with improved outcomes compared to ERT for TCA due to subdiaphragmatic hemorrhage (OR 0.32, 95%CI 0.15–0.69) [55, 57]. Lastly, direct or cross-regional transfer to trauma centers substantially reduces mortality relative to non-trauma centers, with multivariable analyses confirming this benefit without negative impact from transport distance within 10 km [24].

Advances in preclinical and transitional research

Over the past decade, experimental and translational research has substantially advanced our understanding of TCA, particularly by elucidating its complex pathophysiology and facilitating the development of optimized animal models and novel resuscitation strategies. Swine models have become the cornerstone of TCA research due to their anatomical and physiological similarity to humans, offering reproducible and clinically relevant insights. Notably, Shen et al. established a swine model of TCA using controlled hemorrhage and ventricular fibrillation, demonstrating successful ROSC in most subjects, but also revealing the extent of post-resuscitation myocardial and neurological injury [78].

The efficacy of basic life support techniques in TCA continues to be evaluated. Watts et al. found that, in hemorrhage-induced TCA swine, closed chest compressions were associated with increased mortality compared to intravenous whole blood administration. Intriguingly, the combination of chest compressions and fluid resuscitation produced inferior hemodynamics and outcomes versus fluid replacement alone, underlining the importance of prioritizing hemorrhage control over compressions in this setting [79]. Similarly, Bradley et al. compared open chest cardiac massage (OCCM) and closed chest compressions (CCC) using end-tidal CO₂ as a surrogate for cardiac output, reporting no significant difference in ROSC or resuscitation quality between the two techniques, and further questioning the superiority of OCCM in traumatic arrest [80].

Adjunctive interventions, such as the abdominal aortic and junctional tourniquet (AAJT), have also been explored. Rall et al. demonstrated that AAJT combined with rapid transfusion increased survival in swine TCA models to 83% versus 17% without AAJT, accompanied by improvements in blood pressure, carotid flow, and end-tidal CO₂ [81]. Clinical experience with prehospital AAJT application, albeit limited, has shown physiological improvements in end-tidal CO₂, suggestive of enhanced pulmonary and coronary perfusion; nevertheless, no significant changes in survival have been reported, likely due to persistent underlying pathology [82].

Advanced resuscitation strategies have centered on selective aortic arch perfusion (SAAP) and REBOA. Barnard et al. demonstrated that SAAP using oxygenated whole blood resulted in 100% ROSC and 90% 60-minute survival, substantially outperforming REBOA (0% survival) [83]. Additional studies corroborate the superiority of oxygen-carrying perfusates in reversing ischemia-reperfusion injury, with Hoops et al. showing equally robust outcomes using whole blood or hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers [84]. Manning et al. highlighted the critical role of calcium supplementation during SAAP when using stored blood, as intra-aortic calcium was necessary to counteract citrate-induced hypocalcemia and restore cardiac function [85]. Madurska et al. further reported strong associations between shorter arrest duration, higher rates of ROSC, and superior early survival in exsanguination and SAAP models [86]. The benefit of REBOA as a less invasive alternative has been substantiated in animal models, showing improved survival over controls, independent of chest compression technique [87].

Despite these technological advances, post-resuscitation organ injury remains a significant barrier to favorable outcomes. Recent experimental therapies have targeted these pathophysiological sequelae. For instance, sodium octanoate, ulinastatin, and modified glucose-insulin-potassium therapy have shown efficacy in attenuating multi-organ damage by reducing apoptosis, systemic inflammatory response, and ferroptosis in porcine and rabbit TCA models [88–90]. Pharmacokinetic investigations reveal that intravenous and intraosseous epinephrine delivery achieve superior and more reliable systemic exposure compared to endotracheal routes, though the clinical benefit of epinephrine appears marginal and is largely attributable to concurrent volume resuscitation rather than direct pharmacodynamic effects [91].

Key knowledge gaps and future research directions

Despite marked advances in trauma resuscitation and the global adoption of standardized protocols, the field of TCA continues to face persistent knowledge gaps that hinder the optimization of clinical outcomes (Fig. 3). Foremost among these challenges is the lack of consensus regarding the definition and classification of TCA in the literature. There exists wide variation in inclusion criteria, mechanistic categorizations, and operational thresholds (eTable 5 in the Supplemental Materials). Studies differ in critical parameters-such as whether they include out-of-hospital versus in-hospital arrests, selection of trauma subtypes (blunt, penetrating, burn), and criteria for arrest definition, which may involve the Glasgow Coma Scale, Utstein template adherence, or clinical judgment. Furthermore, there is inconsistency regarding the inclusion of cases with hypoxic, medical, or non-traumatic contributors, as well as pediatric populations. This heterogeneity complicates meta-analyses and undermines the interpretability of pooled data, ultimately impeding the translation of trial findings into clinical practice and obscuring the true effect of many interventions.

Beyond these definitional challenges, the reasons underlying the persistently poor outcomes associated with TCA remain controversial and incompletely elucidated. Escalating the intensity of trauma interventions does not reliably result in improved survival or functional recovery. Contributing factors to this disconnect may include unmeasured variations in trauma severity, disparities in prehospital and in-hospital care, context-specific barriers to optimal management, and a lack of granular data linking injury patterns, treatment timelines, and long-term prognosis. Additionally, uncertainty persists regarding the optimal timing and sequencing of major interventions for different trauma subtypes, underscoring the need for robust clinical validation and cautious implementation of emerging therapies.

Preclinical and experimental models have contributed valuable mechanistic insights and supported translational research. However, these models also present significant limitations. For instance, porcine models of TCA typically involve standardized, singular injury mechanisms in young, healthy animals, yielding pathophysiological responses that are directly attributable and more controllable. Such models frequently demonstrate high rates of ROSC and favorable short-term neurological outcomes, with injury often limited to reversible phenomena such as cerebral edema. In contrast, small-animal models frequently yield findings lacking clear clinical relevance. Human TCA, by comparison, results from complex, multisystem injuries often exacerbated by comorbidities, chronic inflammation, and pre-existing organ dysfunction, which markedly increase susceptibility to complications such as disseminated intravascular coagulation and organ injury. Consequently, the efficacy of resuscitative interventions and neurological outcomes in humans are highly variable and are influenced by numerous clinical and injury-specific factors. These substantial physiological and pathological differences between animal models and human TCA patients underscore the limitations of directly translating preclinical findings to clinical settings, thereby restricting the generation of robust, practice-informing evidence.

Another critical gap lies in the identification of patient subgroups most likely to benefit from specific-and often resource-intensive-treatment strategies. Regional variations in emergency medical service protocols, the scarcity of age-specific and pediatric analyses, and uncertainty surrounding prognostic markers such as initial cardiac rhythm and signs of life all further complicate clinical decision-making. Addressing these gaps necessitates future research efforts that prioritize international consensus on definitions, standardized reporting frameworks such as trauma-adapted Utstein templates, and large, prospective, multicenter trials stratified by mechanism, age, and geographical context. Importantly, outcome measures should be broadened to include neurologic, functional, and patient-reported endpoints for a more comprehensive assessment of intervention efficacy.

Progress in the field will depend on a paradigm shift towards prospective, randomized controlled and cohort studies, as well as multidisciplinary collaboration to harmonize practice across diverse healthcare settings. Ultimately, the establishment of consistent, context-sensitive, evidence-based guidelines-grounded in rigorous clinical and neurological data-is essential for facilitating meaningful international comparisons and improving outcomes for TCA patients. Conclusion

TCA remains one of the most formidable challenges in acute care, characterized by persistently low survival and significant heterogeneity in epidemiology, management practices, and outcomes across global settings. Despite major strides in algorithm-based resuscitation, the deployment of advanced interventions, and strengthening of trauma system integration, substantial gaps persist in our understanding of optimal patient selection, timing, and combinations of lifesaving therapies. Recent clinical and translational advances have clarified the promising-but as yet incompletely defined-roles of interventions such as thoracostomy, REBOA, and advanced airway management, especially as emerging evidence questions the utility of historic pharmacological strategies. A recurrent theme in the evolving literature is the critical importance of moving beyond short-term survival as the benchmark for success. Future efforts must prioritize comprehensive, multicenter studies with rigorous long-term neurological and functional outcome assessment, as well as tailored approaches for vulnerable populations including children and older adults. Enhanced granularity in trauma registries, harmonized definitions, and the global adoption of consensus-based reporting standards are essential to enable meaningful comparison and to close knowledge gaps that hinder the translation of innovation into real-world impact. Ultimately, achieving significant improvement in outcomes will demand not only technical and procedural innovation but also broad system-level reform that promotes equitable access, multidisciplinary collaboration, and data-driven continuous learning. With global partnership and an unwavering commitment to scientific rigor and equity, the field is poised to develop and implement the next generation of precise, context-specific protocols. Such efforts promise not only to increase survival but to profoundly improve the quality of life for survivors of TCA worldwide.
