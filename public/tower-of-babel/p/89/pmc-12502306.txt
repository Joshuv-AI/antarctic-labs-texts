Synergistic influence of minimum tillage and integrated nutrient schedules on crop performance, nutrient uptake, profitability and soil microbial diversity in a rice‒lentil cropping system
Authors: Urjashi Bhattacharya, Manish Kumar Naskar, Venugopalan Visha Kumari, Smritikana Sarkar, Pintoo Bandyopadhyay, Sagar Maitra, Laila A Al-Shuraym, Lamya Ahmed Alkeridis, Ahmed Gaber, Akbar Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuous use of chemical fertilizers in conventional tillage systems has jeopardized soil fertility, affecting productivity of crops. Puddling is currently a serious constraint for farmers in rice based cropping systems because of water deficits and soil structure destruction. Finding an alternative cultivation practice is imperative to improve this scenario for sustainability and food security.MethodsThe current study was conducted in consecutive two years (from 2018–2020) to evaluate the effects of minimum tillage and integrated nutrient schedules on the nutrient uptake, profitability and microbial diversity of the rice‒lentil cropping system in the lower Gangetic plains of West Bengal. The study was carried-out in a split-plot design and repeated all treatments in three times. Treatments such as CT Direct seeded rice – lentil (CT-DSR), MT Direct seeded rice – lentil (MT-DSR), CT Transplanted rice – lentil (CT-TPR) and MT Transplanted rice – lentil (MT-TPR) were assigned in main plots and no application of nutrients (Control), 100% RDF, 75% Nitrogen + FYM (75% N + FYM), 75% Nitrogen + FYM + Azospirillum (75% N + FYM + Azo) and 75% Nitrogen + FYM + Azospirillum + Zinc Sulfate (75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn) were assigned in sub-plots.ResultsThe MT treatments significantly gave better growth, yield and nutrient uptake values in the second year with MT- DSRMT-DSR recording highest growth (96.38 cm), yield (5.17 t ha−1) and nutrient uptake of rice, despite the CT treatments performing better in first year. Minimum tillage treatments showed better growth, yield and nutrient uptake in lentil throughout the experiment with MT-DSRMT-DSR treatment having maximum growth (32.81 cm & 34.98 cm), yield (8.08 q ha−1 & 8.817 q ha−1) and nutrient uptake in both years respectively. Treatment of 100% RDF obtained highest growth (96.53 cm & 32.98 cm), uptake and yield (5.17 t ha−1 & 8.23 q ha−1) respectively for rice & lentil during the first year, but 75%N + FYM + Azo + Zn surpassed all the other treatments in terms of the aforementioned parameters, in the second year. The MT DSR + 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment in the pooled analysis over two years had the greatest economic benefit (85,845.27 INR/ha), B:C ratio (1.85) and a Shannon diversity index of 0.070.ConclusionThe results of the study revealed that, to obtain long term sustained productivity, better soil health and income security of the smallholder farmers of Eastern India, minimum tillage along with integrated nutrient schedules can be a viable alternative in the rice‒lentil cropping system.Keywords: Minimum tillage, Integrated nutrient schedules, Stable yield, Income security, Rice‒lentil cropping system

Background

Since the Green Revolution of the 1960s, high doses of fertilizers, pesticides, and insecticides and excessive use of farm machinery have been necessary to address insufficient food production [47]. But continuous practice of monocultures, intensive cropping cycles, injudicious use of herbicides, chemical fertilizers and excessive tillage, over time, have deteriorated soil physicochemical properties further exacerbating the biological processes in the soil [78]. In approximately 24% to 39% of major grain (rice, wheat and maize) cultivated areas worldwide, yields have stalled considerably [56]. In India, 47% of the cultivated area of rice has reached yield stagnation, which has been attributed to conventional agricultural [78] . Furthermore, unsustainable food production is a threat to the ever-growing population, which is expected to reach 9.7 billion in 2050 [79].

Severe oxidation of conserved soil organic matter and destruction of the soil structure due to repeated tillage operations have compounding effects on overall soil properties, which are crucial for sustained crop production [30]. An estimated loss of 16–77% of soil organic matter is observed under conventional tillage operations [35]. So, to maintain a positive food production trajectory under these circumstances, agricultural scientists must focus on sustainable resource-conserving farming techniques, one of which is conservation tillage combined with integrated nutrient management. This process helps reverse this trend and converts the soil into a better carbon sink.

Minimum tillage is a type of conservation tillage in which only primary tillage operations are carried out and secondary tillage operations are regulated to a minimum. The retention of crop residues under minimum tillage has a direct effect on soil organic carbon, which is the core of soil fertility [39, 41]. Better soil structure is attributed to greater pore networks, aggregate stability and infiltration rates [32]. Nitrogen loss through leaching and volatilization, is minimal since the soil surface is not exposed. Mineralization of nutrients, which otherwise is more common following conventional tillage systems, is considerably less in this case. Hence, the total soil nitrogen and exchangeable cations are improved by minimum tillage [5]. Less disturbance to the soil in conservation practices stimulates microbial biomass carbon, microbial respiration activity, and increases microbial metabolic rates, resulting in better nutrient transformation and improving the productivity of the soil [28]. Hence, minimum tillage is an important resource conservation technology which improves soil physicochemical and biological properties which otherwise is severely affected by conventional tillage.

Furthermore, instead of injudicious use of chemical fertilizers, replacing a portion of inorganic nutrients with organic sources generally alters soil nutrient reserves by correcting nutrient deficiencies. This improves the solubility and availability of nutrients via a concomitant increase in the organic carbon content and soil microbial biodiversity. Organic sources also have residual effects on successive crops since soil organic matter acts as a slow-release nutrient source [81]. The balanced nutrient approach accelerates the natural nutrient cycling of soil, converting it from a degenerative system to a regenerative system [2]. Furthermore, tillage generally contributes 20% of crop production cost [33]. Higher fertilizer and fuel prices increase the cost of production by 4–5 times and considerably reduce the profitability of production systems [24]. So minimum tillage combined with integrated nutrient management also serves as a viable alternative for lowering production cost, improving productivity and profitability of cropping systems.

In India, rice has the largest cultivated area of 45.07 M ha, with West Bengal having the highest production of 16.65 million tonnes [22]. In recent years, West Bengal has been exposed to intense heat stress, irregularities in monsoons, and water deficits due to prolonged summers. Here, significant portion of rice cultivation is rainfed.

Puddling, though traditionally essential for rice cultivation, poses challenges in areas facing water scarcity and can be effectively substituted with minimum tillage practices. Soil fertility is highly correlated with cropping systems and land use management patterns [54]. Several researchers have suggested that monocropping has a dwindling effect on soil nutrient status, affecting crop productivity [1], and the importance of pulse components in cereal-based systems is promising for food security and agro-ecosystem stability. The inclusion of pulses in cereal-based production systems escalates system returns [80]. Owing to their short duration and capacity to grow in residual soil moisture with low inputs, pulses are grown as key crops after rice fallows [80]. Lentil is the second most widely grown rabi pulse crop after chickpea in India, with a production of 1.45 million tonnes and a productivity of 1001 kg/ha [22]. It is the most common pulse crop grown in the Aman rice fallow (June-November) in the rabi season, with a productivity of 855 kg/ha [22]. A study on effects of minimum tillage and different nutrient schedules was initiated with the objectives to improve a) crop performance, b) nutrient uptake, c) profitability and d) soil microbial diversity of rice‒lentil system in the lower Gangetic plains of West Bengal. The hypothesis of the study was that minimum tillage along with integrated nutrient schedules will tackle soil degradation and stalled yield in rice based cropping systems in this region by improving soil health.

Materials and methods

Experimental site description

The experiment was conducted at the Instructional Farm of Bidhan Chandra Krishi Viswavidyalaya, Jaguli, Nadia, West Bengal, during the kharif-rabi seasons of 2018–2019 and 2019–2020. The farm is located at 22° 93’ N latitude, 88° 53’ E longitude and 9.75 m above mean sea level (MSL). The experimental site is a medium-sized area with good irrigation and drainage facilities. The soil is a typical new alluvial, entisol and sandy clay‒loam texture (5.19% coarse sand, 36.17% fine sand, 26.19% silt and 32.45% clay) with a moderate water holding capacity. The soil was slightly acidic (pH = 6.59) with a low organic carbon content (0.51%) (Walkley and Black methods), alkaline KMnO4-N (243 kg ha−1), highly available P2O5 (Olsen’s P) (28.5 kg ha−1) and K2O (NH4OAC-extractable) (253.63 kg ha−1) were used.

Climatic conditions

The experimental site was located in a subhumid and subtropical zone because of its proximity to the Tropic of Cancer. It was in the Indo-Gangetic alluvial agroecological zone. In this region, the average annual rainfall varies by approximately 1440 mm. The hottest month is May, and winter is mild and short-lived. Usually, monsoons commence from the second week of June and recede in the last of September or middle of October. During the later part of March to May, premonsoon rain is quite frequent because of Nor-Wester showers. The rainfall is unsteady and variable. During the entire study period, the mean monthly temperatures varied from 9.15 °C to 32.96 °C. Rainfall was satisfactory during the winter months because of ‘depression’ in the Bay of Bengal. The Maximum rainfall occurred during the monsoon season, and the Maximum humidity occurred during July 2018 and August 2019 (89.18% and 88.94%, respectively), whereas the minimum humidity occurred during the winter months of the experimental years. Experimental design and treatments

The experimental design used was a split-plot design with three replications. The total number of main plots were four, viz. Experimental details

Crop management

Different tillage systems were applied randomly throughout the entire experimental field. Two ploughings with the help of tractor-drawn disc harrows were performed prior to two harrowings by a rotovator and one plank for uniform levelling of land under CT, whereas one ploughing and one harrowing along with levelling were performed under MT. Both the CT and MT were practiced for direct-seeded rice and transplanted rice separately for each. No further tillage operations were performed before lentil cultivation. There were two irrigation channels in between the three replication blocks. And buffer channels were prepared according to necessities. Each individual plots of size 15 m2 were further levelled for sowing. P2O5 and K2O were applied in the form of S.S.P. and M.O.P. at 40 kg ha−1 in all subplots except the control. Nitrogen in the form of urea was applied at 0, 80, and 60 kg ha−1 as per treatment requirements. Half doses of urea and full doses of P2O5 and K2O were applied as basal. Whereas the other half of urea was applied in equal split doses as topdressing during the active tillering stage and panicle initiation stage of rice. FYM was applied at 10 t ha−1 during land preparation. Azospirillum was applied at 2 kg ha−1 as a basal dose according to the treatments. Zinc sulfate heptahydrate was added at 20 kg ha−1 four to five days after Azospirillum application to avoid any negative effect on its population., No fertilizers were applied for lentil cultivation but the seeds before sowing were inoculated with Rhizobium culture @600 gm ha−1. Line sowing was practised in transplanted rice whereas direct sowing was done with the help of drum seeder. For lentil with the help of tynes rows were Made throughout the plot. The spacing followed for rice was 20× 20 cm and for lentil row to row spacing was 30 cm (line sowing) and later by thinning 10 cm of plant-to-plant distance was maintained. The varieties used in the experiments for rice and lentil were Satabdi (IET-4786) and HULL-57, respectively. Irrigation was provided as and when necessary for both the crops. Pendimethalin @ 1.5 kg a.i. ha−1 was sprayed as pre-emergence application throughout the experimental field 0–7 days after sowing/transplanting of rice, and Allmix was sprayed at 15–20 days after sowing/transplanting @ 4 gm a.i. ha−1 for DSR plots and 8 gm a.i. ha−1 for TPR plots. For lentil Pendimethalin was sprayed @ 1 kg a.i. ha−1 at 0–3 days after sowing. Additional hand weeding was followed as and when required for lentil.

Agronomic characteristics

Ten random plants were selected from each treatment from one meter row length, leaving two rows from all sides of the border for measuring various growth parameters of rice and lentil at different phenological stages (vegetative, reproductive and mature). The growth parameters, such as the plant height, dry matter accumulation, crop growth rate, germination percentage, biomass, number of nodules per plant, and root volume, and different yield parameters, such as the number of panicles per m−2, panicle length, number of filled grains per panicle, number of pods per plant, number of seeds per pod, and yield at physiological Maturity, were studied for rice and lentil accordingly. Plant height was measured from the ground surface to the tip of the plant with the help of a meter scale. For dry Matter accumulation, the plant samples were first dried under the sun for one or two days and finally in an oven at a temperature of 65± 1 °C until the dry weight became constant. Then, it was measured and converted to g m−2. To count the number of nodules plant−1 and study the root volume, the plants were gently excavated from the soil with the help of a khurpi so that their roots were not damaged. The soil clods from the roots were delicately removed while the roots were washed. The number of nodules per plant and the root volume were determined on the same day that destructive samples were taken. The plants were tied with a thread from a horizontal stand, and the roots were dipped fully and carefully in a 10 ml measuring cylinder containing water so that the roots did not touch the sides of the cylinder. The levels of water before and after the roots were dipped were recorded to determine the exact root volume. The yield parameters were recorded and averaged for each treatment. When 70%−80% of panicles and pods (rice and lentil, respectively) matured, harvesting was performed with the help of a sickle. The plants were cut from the ground surface, and they were kept in bundles on the dry threshing floor. These plants were sundried for three to four days, and the plots were changed to obtain yields of grains and seeds as well as straws and stalks for rice and lentil, respectively. Finally, the yield was converted to a per hectare basis.

Nutrient uptake estimation

Plant samples were taken during harvest for estimation of nutrient uptake. The samples were dried in an oven at 65 ± 1 °C until a constant weight was obtained. The samples were subsequently ground into a homogenous powder in a grinding machine and further digested with H2SO4 to estimate the nitrogen and diacid contents (HNO3: HClO4) to estimate the phosphorus and potassium contents. The nitrogen content in the plant samples was calculated as a percentage according to the modified Kjeldahl method [96]. The phosphorus content of the plant samples was determined calorimetrically via the vanadomolybdophosphoric yellow color method [34], and the potassium content was determined via a flame photometer [7]. The uptake (kg ha−1) of Major nutrients, N, P and K, by each crop was then determined for two consecutive years, 2018–19 and 2019–20, by multiplying the dry matter content of the crop at harvest by its corresponding nutrient element content.

Economic analysis

This process was performed separately for both crops. To calculate the cost of cultivation, gross and net returns, the cost of inputs in the local market, the value of produce and the wage rates needed were provided by the Directorate of Farms, Bidhan Chandra Krishi Viswavidyalaya, Mohanpur, Nadia, and West Bengal at the time of the experiments. The total cost of cultivation was calculated considering the expenditure on land preparation, seed material, sowing, weeding, gap filling and cost of fertilizer, manures, harvesting, etc., and bacterial inoculation depending upon the particulars of the treatments of individual crops. The gross return was calculated by multiplying the economic yield with the market price of a particular crop. Net return was calculated by subtracting the total cost of cultivation from the total product value for each treatment plot. The benefit cost was calculated by dividing the total product value by the total cost of cultivation against each treatment.

Soil microbial diversity

The soil microbial diversity of the rice-lentil system was determined by the Shannon-Weiner diversity index (H), which actually calculates the diversity of the microbial population (bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes) of the soil. Statistical analysis

The data obtained were subjected to statistical analysis via the ANOVA method for split-plot design [27]. For comparisons of different treatment means, Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) (p ≤ 0.05) was performed to determine the data variations among various treatments for different parameters. Correlogram plots were prepared with the help of the “ggplot 2” and “GGally” packages in R. Principal component analysis was performed via R software 4.3.2, which is based on a covariance matrix, to determine the relationships among Shannon’s diversity index, the respective yields of the rice‒Lentil system and the B:C ratio to determine the best treatment combination from the entire study. Subsequent ranking was performed based on the Shannon diversity index results and principal component analysis (PCA) to identify the most suitable tillage and nutrient management practices.

Impact on nutrient uptake

The data in Tables 7 and 8 show that there were significant variations among the different tillage and nutrient treatments. Rice in the CT-TPR treatment (Table 7) presented the greatest nitrogen (115.2 kg ha−1), phosphorus (16.23 kg ha−1) and potassium (97.3 kg ha−1) uptake in its aerial biomass during the first year. Conversely, the MT-DSR treatment performed best among the other tillage treatments in the second year. Rice was found to take up 12.23% (121.3 kg ha−1), 8.99% (18.29 kg ha−1) and 6.70% (108.2) more nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, respectively, in MT-DSR than in CT-TPR. Rice grown in the 100% RDF plots (Table 7) presented the greatest N (132.2 kg ha−1), P (18.20 kg ha−1) and K (107.6 kg ha−1) uptake in the first year, which was very closely followed by the FYM-treated treatments. However, in the second year, 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn maintained its statistical superiority over the other treatments with respect to N (147.1 kg ha−1), P (21 kg ha−1) and K (123.2 kg ha−1) uptake.

Discussion

Growth characteristics

The comparative evaluation of direct-seeded rice (DSR) and transplanted rice (TPR) revealed significant temporal variability in performance. In the first year, DSR exhibited suboptimal growth, primarily due to inadequate rainfall (Fig. 1), which led to severe soil moisture stress and consequently poor seed germination. However, the results were quite different in the second year, because of the better climatic conditions and benefits of minimum tillage, which started unfolding gradually from the second year. Conventional tillage (CT) practices are known to degrade soil structure by disrupting aggregates, compacting subsoil layers, and creating plough pans, thereby impeding root growth and water infiltration [42]. In contrast, minimum tillage preserves surface residues, enhances soil organic matter content, microbial activity, and nutrient cycling, and improves soil porosity and permeability [48]. Higher microbial enzymatic activity in soil as well as adequate soil moisture content are major driving forces for mineralization and nutrient uptake by crops [55]. Greater nutrient uptake results in greater cell division and, ultimately, greater plant height [98]. Greater plant height improves light interception and a deeper root system, increasing dry matter accumulation and the crop growth rate in minimum tillage plots. This could be attributed to increased photosynthate assimilation, reduced evapotranspiration losses, and improved in situ water conservation. Consequently, DSR under MT consistently recorded superior values for key growth parameters. Wang et al. [74] reported that, with each passing year, higher organic Matter under conservation tillage increased aggregate dynamics and, ultimately, the infiltration rate of soil by 60.9%. Superior soil health and better soil structure and water retention capacity might have increased the growth attributes of rice under minimum tillage in the following year. Similar long-term benefits of conservation tillage have been reported by Olson and Ebelhar [50], who observed that no-tillage systems, despite initial lag in performance, eventually surpassed CT in maize–soybean systems due to improved soil physicochemical properties. Li et al. [39] reported a 43.2% increase in maize biomass after four years of conservation tillage, attributable to crop residue retention and reduced moisture loss. Consistent with these findings, Zulhaedar et al. [77] observed 3.43% and 4.10% increases in rice plant height under MT during vegetative and reproductive stages, respectively, linked to improved soil structural attributes.

Azospirillum has the capacity to produce phytohormones such as cytokinins, auxins, and gibberrelins, which directly influence plant root growth and improve water and nutrient uptake [25], whereas zinc is crucial for carbohydrate metabolism, chlorophyll and protein formation and various enzymes that regulate fundamental biochemical pathways in plants [59]. This might be the reason for the greater plant height, dry Matter accumulation and CGR values in the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment. Specifically, the FYM-amended treatments yielded better results in terms of growth in the second year. The possible reason might be that FYM decomposes and releases nutrients slowly since it has a relatively high C:N ratio [75]. Similar findings were reported by Senthilvalavan et al. [82], who reported that, compared with other inorganic treatments, the application of FYM and Azospirillum resulted in superior growth characteristics in rice. Since FYM is a source of organic insoluble nitrogen that is bound to soil, it needs to be broken down into smaller substrates by exoenzymes before being taken up by plants, which is a plausible reason for its relatively high turnover rate [44]. This actually favours greater microbial growth over time, improving the nutrient availability and growth attributes of crops [61]. The results of the present study complied with the findings of Senthamizhkumaran et al. [62], who reported that the combined application of FYM and Azospirillum increased the plant height by 13.82% (during the active tillering stage) and 10.66% (during the panicle initiation stage), whereas the dry Matter production of rice increased by 6.03% and 4.60% during the panicle initiation and flowering stages, respectively, compared with that in the 100% RDF treatment.

The presence of a congenial soil environment under the MT-DSR treatment was the reason for the greater germination of lentil in both years. Direct seeded rice establishments have better soil physical structures with a proper ratio of soil aggregates and pores, which is beneficial for any succeeding crop. This, along with the slow decomposition of organic residues and higher organic carbon content under the MT system, might explain why residual lentil resulted in greater plant height, biomass and crop growth rates in MT-DSR. Similar results in residual lentil were reported by Bandyopadhyay et al. [6] in minimum and zero tillage treatments, which they attributed to better soil hydrothermal characteristics leading to higher relative water content in cells and subsequently better growth. Higher soil microbial biomass facilitates nutrient transformation in soil, which aids in the superior growth attributes of crops [1]. The effects of minimum tillage (followed by the preceding rice crop) on increasing various growth attributes of residual lentil were quite evident in the current study. Reduced tillage practices, crop rotation and proper moisture availability have favourable influences on nodule formation in lentils [57]. This might explain the greater number of nodules in the MT-DSR treatment than in the other treatments. Even the results of the present study are consistent with those of Subrahmaniyan et al. [72], who reported a greater number of blackgram nodules (1.52% and 15.94%) at 30 and 45 DAS in unpuddled transplanted minimum tillage rice systems than in puddled transplanted rice systems. These results are in accordance with Torabian et al. [73], who concluded that conservation tillage practices improved the nodulation of legumes with respect to better soil moisture and temperature regimes. Minimum tillage coupled with direct seeded rice establishment systems created equilibrium in the soil macro- and micropores, which led to lateral and downwards root development in the residual lentil, resulting in the highest root volume values.

The combined application of FYM and biofertilizers had notable residual effects on lentil. Prior studies by Sohu et al. [68] and Balasubramanian et al. [4] demonstrated improved root and shoot attributes in chickpea and blackgram, respectively, following FYM application. FYM likely increased soil organic carbon, acting as both a nutrient reservoir and an energy source for soil microbes. This organic matter acts as a slow-release nutrient source and reduces nutrient loss through leaching or volatilization [81], resulting in enhanced physiological responses in plants. The superior performance of lentil in the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment in the present study aligns with similar observations reported by Singh et al. [64, 66].

Yield attributes and yield

Several studies have indicated that puddling enhances transplanted rice yields by reducing percolation losses, improving nutrient availability, and ensuring better seedling establishment—benefits particularly evident under dry climatic conditions [15].. This might be the reason for transplanted rice establishments to perform better in both minimum and conventional systems during the first year. However, congenial climatic conditions coupled with the benefits of minimum tillage resulted in increased yields in direct seeded rice establishments in the second year. This finding was supported by the findings of Panneerselvam et al. [51], who reported that good agronomic management practices together with a well-suited climate can improve direct seeded rice yields even more than transplanted rice yields. Iqbal et al. [29] reported a greater yield in direct seeded rice (4.85 t ha−1) than in transplanted rice owing to the greater number of panicles m−2. Zheng et al. [76] and Nandan et al. [46] reported that any type of conservation tillage in the longer run promotes crop yields. Restricted soil manipulation in the case of conservation tillage, such as minimum tillage, purposefully increases soil bacterial, fungal, and actinomycete populations [8] along with various enzymatic activities (such as urease, catalase, dehydrogenase, phosphatase, and invertase [58] have been observed in conservation tillage systems and are positively related to soil nutrient availability and crop productivity. Conservation tillage practices pertaining to higher C:N ratios of crop residues lead to the immobilization of nitrogen, further inhibiting nitrogen leaching, denitrification and volatilization losses, thereby increasing the nitrogen content in the soil [84]. On the other hand, the available P is greater in the presence of crop residues because of less P fixation. Piegholdt et al. [53] reported 15% greater P content in the upper 0–5 cm soil layer under zero tillage than under conventional tillage. Hence, higher organic matter in conservation systems increases phosphorus and potassium availability, as it reduces adsorption to mineral surfaces by forming stable organic–metal complexes [83]. The improved nutrient availability and soil physical properties might have resulted in better photosynthate accumulation in the sink, resulting in a greater number of panicles m−2, panicle length and, ultimately, yield in MT-DSR. Similar results were reported by Samal et al. [60], Karki et al. [31] and Nandan et al. [46], where conservation tillage increased rice yield by improving various yield attributes.

The Maximum yield and its determining parameters were observed in the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment, probably due to the presence of Azospirillum, which fixes nitrogen, solubilizes phosphorus, and releases phytohormones and enzymes, facilitating root growth as well as nutrient uptake [14]. Zinc, on the other hand, plays a key role in enzyme activation, membrane stability, nitrogen metabolism and energy transfer, which affect various physiological processes and ultimately yield attributes of crops [85]. The combined application of inorganic and organic sources of nutrients in rice resulted in the greatest number of panicles m−2, panicle length and yield due to improved soil microbial biomass and structural makeup [3]. Sravan et al. [86] reported similar results in terms of higher yield attributes and yields of rice due to the integrated application of chemical fertilizers, FYM and Azospirillum, owing to their better growth characteristics than those of other treatments. Researchers have concluded that higher yields under INM practices occur because of the instant supply of nutrients by chemical fertilizers and the prolonged supply of nutrients throughout the crop cycle by organic sources of nutrients [70], which might be the reason why the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment performed best.

Puddling has been shown to adversely affect key soil physical properties such as permeability, macroporosity, hydraulic conductivity, and the water stability of soil aggregates. These effects are primarily attributed to increased bulk density and penetration resistance, resulting from the formation of compacted plough pans in the subsurface layers [16]. Such structural degradation poses significant constraints on the root development and productivity of subsequent crops. Infiltration controls nutrient leaching, crop water productivity and runoff [87], which is quite impaired in transplanted rice systems. These factors might be responsible for the poor yield of lentil in the transplanted systems. Minimum tillage and direct seeded rice establishment systems provide favourable environments for root growth. Since root growth and nodule formation are directly linked with better nitrogen fixation and nutrient and water uptake, a positive influence on seed yield was observed under MT-DSR. Similar results have been reported by several researchers [88]. Das et al. [18] reported the maximum number of seeds (1.37) and pods plant−1 (23.1) in lentils under minimally tilled rice plots. Similarly, Islam et al. [89] reported the maximum number of pods per plant (26.40), number of seeds per pod (1.89) and seed yield (1.60 t ha−1) in lentil grown after minimal tilling of Aman rice.

The soil organic carbon content is a critical attribute for increasing agronomic yields [37, 38] Application of farmyard manure (FYM) contributes stoichiometrically balanced nutrients, including micronutrients and organic nitrogen, while also enhancing microbial biomass and enzymatic functions in the soil. In addition, it also provides soluble nanoparticulate organic nitrogen and carbon to the subsoil by leaching, further fostering nitrogen and carbon cycling mediated by microbial enzymes [40, 44]. Various meta-analyses worldwide have shown that the rate of decomposition in the FYM has slowed during the lignin-dominant stage [75] and that the mineralization of nutrients is directly related to the decomposition pattern of organic matter [69]. This slow turnover ensures a sustained release of nutrients over time, providing long-term benefits to crops grown in rotation.

The positive residual effects of FYM, particularly when integrated with bioinoculants like Azospirillum, have been well documented in legume crops. This could explain the significantly higher yield and yield-attributing traits in lentil under the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment compared to other treatments. These results were congruent with the findings of Singh et al. [67], who reported the highest pod yield because better growth attributes were enhanced by greater nutrient uptake under the integrated application of organic and inorganic sources of nutrients. Further supporting evidence comes from Bilkis et al. [9] and Patel et al. [52], who reported significant residual effects of organic–inorganic nutrient integration on pulse productivity following cereal-based systems.

Nutrient uptake

The greater yield of transplanted rice in the CT-TPR treatment than in the other treatments in the first year was due to the benefits of puddling and the poor performance of direct seeded rice because less rainfall was the reason for the greater uptake of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in the CT-TPR treatment. However, in the second year, minimum tillage improved the soil environment conditions for better uptake of nutrients, resulting in the highest uptake in the MT-DSR treatment. Nutrient uptake influenced by various environmental factors, including nutrient availability, optimal soil temperature, and moisture content [12]. Minimum tillage helps regulate the soil temperature and increases the number of storage pores and transmission pores, which increases the microporosity and water content in the soil [90]. This finding is supported by several researchers, such as Green and Lafond [97], who reported that the presence of crop residues under conservation tillage increased the temperature by 0.29 °C; Likewise, in summer, the temperature was 0.89 °C higher than that under non-tilled land. Various researchers have reported that N, P and K availability is greater in conservation tillage treatments because of low leaching rates and better nutrient cycling [11, 18]. After six years of rotation, the soil available N, P, and K contents were 10%, 16%, and 12%, respectively, greater in conservation tillage treatments than in conventional tillage treatments [91]. may have contributed to the highest uptake of N, P, and K in both rice and lentil crops under the MT-DSR treatment. These results are in accordance with the findings of Li et al. [40] and Singh et al. [65].

In the initial year, the uptake of N, P, and K in FYM-treated plots remained low, Likely due to the slow decomposition and nutrient release from FYM. However, in the subsequent year, treatments integrating balanced sources of organic and inorganic nutrients particularly in the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment might have led to a vigorous root system, increased biomass accumulation and nutrient uptake in the following year. Guo et al. [92] emphasized the positive interaction of zinc with nitrogen fertilizers in facilitating the uptake of nitrogen and other nutrients in zinc-deficient soils, improving crop productivity, which might be another reason why the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment resulted in the highest nutrient uptake. The application of FYM, along with other organic and inorganic sources of nutrients, resulted in the highest uptake of N, P, and K in rice [26]. The robust root architecture in lentils and the greater availability of N, P, and K in the 75% N + FYM + Azo + Zn treatment might have resulted in the efficient absorption of nutrients. A positive influence of FYM and bioinoculants given in preceding rice crops on the uptake of nutrients in succeeding wheat was observed by Shah and Kumar [63]. The findings of the present study complied with those of Singh et al. [67], who confirmed that the application of integrated nutrient schedules comprising bioinoculants and zinc can result in the highest uptake of N, P, and K in the aerial biomass of lentil.

Economics

The better soil quality and lower cost of production have increased yields and net returns in minimally tilled direct seeded treatments that combine 75% nitrogen, FYM and Azospirillum. Maximum profitability generally depends on less use of inputs per unit area as well as a reduced cost per unit production, which is quite common under conservation tillage practices in conjunction with integrated nutrient Management. A greater number of tillage operations along with more input necessities associated with conventional tillage and chemical fertilization increase the cost of cultivation and result in fewer economic benefits to the whole agroecosystem of the farmers. Compared with the MT-TPR treatment, which resulted in the highest cost of production, the MT-DSR treatment resulted in a 13.14% reduction in the cost of production over the pooled analysis of the two years. Similar instances of 16% and 19% reductions in the cost of production in the minimum and no tillage treatments, respectively, were highlighted by Calcante and Oberti [13] in rice-based systems. According to various comparative tillage cost analyses, approximately 23%−30% of production costs are generally lower under conservation tillage systems [36], [93].

Soil Microbial diversity

Conservation tillage practices, owing to the limited manipulation of soil and crop residue retention, favour microhabitats for microbes in soil [10]. It influences the microclimate of soil and the decomposition of organic residues, which have significant effects on microbial diversity [94] (). According to Li et al. [39], microbial populations and diversity thrive on available and total carbon present in the soil, which increases the Shannon diversity index values of soils subjected to reduced tillage (0.072) compared with those subjected to conventional tillage. According to various meta-analyses, including long-term conservation tillage experiments, the Shannon diversity index of soil was found to increase under conservation tillage [23]. FYM acts as a source of nutrients and carbon for microbial proliferation, whereas growth-promoting rhizobacteria such as Azospirillum create nutrient-rich environments in the rhizosphere by increasing plant metabolite secretion [95]. This might be the reason for the greater microbial diversity in the minimum tillage, FYM and Azospirillum treatments. Similar results were reported in a rice‒lentil‒jute system by Datta et al. [19], who reported that the Shannon diversity index increased by 12.6% in treatments with FYM and biofertilizer compared with the control. Thus, the combined beneficial effects of minimum tillage, FYM and Azospirillum in the direct-seeded rice‒lentil system might MT-DSR + 75% N + FYM + Azoexplain why MT-DSR + 75% N + FYM + Azo presented the highest Shannon index value throughout the experiment.

Relationships between yield and other parameters under different tillage systems

Two years of experimental results clearly revealed that minimum tillage tends to increase the yield of crops each year because its congenial soil environment conserves organic matter, increases the microbial population and eventually increases nutrient and moisture availability to crops. Soil microbes aid in nutrient mineralization in a form that can be easily taken up by crops [21]. A healthy soil microbiome improves the root structure and foraging area, leading to increased nutrient uptake by plants [49]. This might explain why the MT-DSR treatment resulted in higher correlation values between the microbial population and nutrient uptake by crops. A greater correlation between various yield-related characteristics, yield and soil microbial populations under minimum tillage systems is an evident justification of its beneficial effects on soil properties, which further improves nutrient uptake and assimilation in the sink. Minimum tillage indeed has tangible advantages in increasing crop performance overall, if not in immediate years, but in the longer run if practised properly [71]. Higher microbial enzymatic activity and slow decomposition of organic matter, which provides nutrients to crops, are likely reasons for better photosynthate accumulation and yield [20] in both rice and lentil. Hence, the introduction of pulses as well as the practice of minimum tillage are clearly necessary for long-term soil health and crop productivity.

Sustainable treatments based on soil microbial diversity, yield and profitability

According to the PCA results of all the treatments based on Shannon’s diversity index, higher yield and B:C ratio principal component scores were observed in the minimally tilled treatments with FYM and biofertilizers throughout the experiment. Higher organic matter content was detected in the treatments with minimum tillage along with FYM, and the biofertilizer acted as a nutrient source for the greater microbial population. Higher microbial diversity, as indicated by Shannon’s diversity index, is responsible for better agroecosystem functioning through proper organic matter decomposition, nutrient cycling and transformation [17, 43]. This improves nutrient availability to crops, which further results in higher yields of crops and greater economic benefits to farmers. This might explain why the MT-DSR + 75% N + FYM + Azo treatment presented the highest principal component score after the second year, as did the pooled data. This finding suggests that to maintain sustainability, which means stable yields and incomes for farmers by maintaining soil health in the longer run, farmers must be encouraged to practise minimum tillage in direct seeded rice‒lentil systems along with the application of FYM and biofertilizers.

Conclusion

The experimental results indicated that although minimum tillage did not have a significant effect on the growth, yield or nutrient uptake of rice during the first year, its effect was quite significant in the second year. All the FYM treatments were superior to the treatments devoid of FYM in the second year because of the slow release of nutrients. The residual impact of minimum tillage and integrated nutrient management on increasing crop performance and nutrient uptake was clearly evident in lentils. From the experiment it was clear that minimum tillage affects soil health which otherwise is degraded in conventional tillage. Soil health has intricated relationship with nutrient availability, which might be the reason for improved overall crop performance. Further minimum tillage supplemented with integrated nutrient management reduces the cost of cultivation by lowering land preparation costs, fuel charges, labour requirements, fertilizer and irrigation needs; thereby fetching higher net returns and B:C ratio On the basis of the findings of this study, smallholder farmers of West Bengal can be recommended to practice minimum tillage along with the application of FYM, Azospirillum and zinc sulfate in rice‒lentil cropping systems only with proper and timely weed control measures for getting stable yields and fetching good income By exploring and implementing various locally adjusted CA practices, we can render soil as a carbon sink. This type of technology, along with pulses as component crops of cropping systems, is essential for maintaining the sustainability and long-term stability of agroecosystems. Therefore, future scope lies in conducting and evaluating more such site-specific experiments based on conservation agriculture for long-term basis.
