Synthesis of SiO2‑Modified CaO from the Aluminum Leaching Residue from Coal Ash for Its High CO2 Absorption Capacity
Authors: Yangang Mei, Songping Gao, Xiaohong Li, Yanfeng Xue, Zhiqing Wang, Yitian Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a promising technology for mitigating CO2 emissions, and Ca-based adsorbents are regarded as potential candidates for CO2 capture. This study focuses on the synthesis of Ca-based adsorbents using an aluminum (Al) leaching residue from coal ash. Through hydrothermal treatment of the Al leaching residue, tobermorite with a high Ca ratio was synthesized. By employing CO2-pressurized leaching followed by calcination at 900 °C, the fibrous SiO2-modified CaO structure was synthesized. The synthesis of SiO2-modified CaO and its CO2 absorption property were investigated by TG (Thermogravimetry), SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy), ICP (Inductively Coupled Plasma), and TEM (Transmission Electron Microscopy). The conversion of SiO2-modified CaO was 93.8% during the first CO2 adsorption, and the CaO conversion rate can still be maintained at 70.3% after 10 cycles. In comparison, pure CaO exhibited only a 42.9% conversion rate after 10 cycles. Due to the inhibiting effect of SiO2 on sintering, the synthesized SiO2-modified CaO showed a higher CO2 absorption amount after 10 cycles. After 15 cycles, the absorption amounts of pure CaO and Ca-based adsorbents were 30.7 and 32.5 g of CO2/(100 g of absorbent), respectively. After cycling, SiO2-modified CaO still had a fibrous structure, and it had a smaller diameter and higher specific surface area than pure CaO. The special structure of tobermorite can inhibit the sintering of SiO2-modified CaO and improve its cyclic stability in the process of CO2 absorption at high temperature. The synthesis of SiO2-modified CaO by using the Al leaching residue of coal ash offers novel approaches for treating coal-based wastes containing significant amounts of Ca and Si.

Introduction

Coal, oil, and natural gas provide over 80% of the energy supply for humans, and over 37.4 billion tons of CO2 is produced with the utilization of fossil energy. − Mass CO2 emissions aggravate the greenhouse effect and seriously affect the environment and climate. − There are great efforts focusing on the capture, storage, and utilization of CO2. − China’s coal consumption is about 3.2 billion tons, with a large amount of coal being consumed for thermal power generation and over 6 billion tons of CO2 being released from coal-fired power plants annually. , The huge amount of CO2 emission poses potential hazard to the environment. − Existing CO2 capture technologies include monoethanol amine-based absorption, temperature and pressure swing adsorption, and membrane adsorption, which require precooling of the effluent gases to a low temperature of about 40 °C. They are not suitable for CO2 capture from flue gas at high temperatures over 300 °C. The oxides, such as MgO, CdO, and CaO, are usually used to absorb CO2 at high temperature in laboratory investigation.

Among the various sorbents used at high temperature, CaO emerges as a promising candidate for its higher uptake capacity (0.78 g of CO2/g of CaO), lower cost, higher reserves, and friendliness to the environment. However, sintering can easily occur after multiple cycles of carbonation and calcination at high temperatures, leading to a reduction in the specific surface area of the CaO absorbent and a decrease in CO2 absorption capacity. − Thus, maintaining the high reactivity of the absorbent for CO2 capture is very crucial. Inert oxides such as SiO2, Al2O3, MgO, and TiO2 are commonly used to inhibit the sintering of CaO at high temperatures and improve the stability and CO2 absorption capacity after multiple cycles. Chen et al. synthesized CaO/MgO and CaO/MnO2 by a sol–gel process, and they exhibited excellent CO2 capture capacity with a carbonation conversion of 0.79 and 0.76 after 50 cycles, respectively. With the sol–gel process, they can produce composites with two or more types of particles well-dispersed, which facilitates the enhancement of the CO2 absorption capacity. Obtained by flame spray pyrolysis (FSP), Zr-doped CaO with Zr/Ca = 3:10 maintained over 50% CaO conversion after 20 cycles, and the ZrO2 dopant can inhibit the sintering of CaO and improve its durability. The utilization of the biotemplate can synthesize a unique hollow porous structure with inert Ca12Al14O33, where the synthesized CaO/Ca12Al14O33 sorbent retains 0.55 g/g under the severe calcination conditions after 10 cycles, and its special structure can promote the CO2 diffusion during absorption. By adding inert materials into CaO to generate doped and supported sorbents, the inert material acts as the framework and effectively reduces sintering of CaO sorbents. So, CaO sorbents with inert materials addition can achieve high CO2 absorption. Mohammad Heidari et al. synthesized a (ZrO2 + Al2O3 + CeO2)-based stabilized CaO absorbent with a fluffier structure. They show the most promising performance with a molar ratio of 20/1 Ca/(Ze + Al + Ce). By designing Ca2SiO4-stabilized CaO nanoparticles on the basis of the Kirkendall effect, hollow and template-free Ca/Ca2SiO4 nanoparticles still maintain 0.573 g CO2/(g sorbent) after the 20th cycle. By methods of hydration and sol–gel, the SiO2-decorated calcium-based absorbent recovered from eggshells shows excellent CO2 capture capacity and fluidization performance. By adding inert materials and controlling the structure of CaO sorbents, sintering can be reduced, the CO2 diffusion resistance decreased, and the crystallite size and distribution maintained. Consequently, CaO sorbents exhibit good absorption performance over multiple cycles. −

SiO2 and Al2O3 are commonly used as inert materials to prevent CaO sintering at high temperatures. ,, At the same time, the residue of Al leaching from coal ash is composed of Al, Ca, and Si, making it a promising candidate for synthesis of a CaO-based absorbent. The synthesis of the CaO-based absorbent from Ca-rich solid wastes helps save natural limestone and brings environmental benefits by utilizing large amounts of coal ash. Figure shows the matter transformation behaviors during Al leaching from coal ash by the lime-soda sinter process. During the lime-soda sinter process, the water-undissolved Al in coal ash transforms into water-dissolved NaAlO2. Al leaching residue (ALR) is composed of SiO2 and CaO, and it is converted into tobermorite by a hydrothermal reaction. Tobermorite is a layered silicate material with infinite CaO2 layers, which can transform into CaCO3. Upon calcination, it converts into CaO. Based on the composition of the Al leaching residue and its transformation behaviors, it is possible to synthesize an excellent CaO absorbent. CaO modified with SiO2 may possess enhanced CO2 absorption properties as SiO2 can act as a framework and effectively reduce the sintering of CaO absorbents at high temperature.

Matter transformation behaviors during Al leaching from coal ash by the lime-soda sinter process.

In this study, a CaO absorbent was synthesized by adopting the waste of Al leaching residue from coal ash. The residue underwent hydrothermal treatment to synthesize tobermorite with a high Ca ratio. Subsequently, tobermorite was converted into SiO2-modified CaCO3 through CO2-pressurized leaching at high temperatures. It transforms into SiO2-modified CaO after calcination at 900 °C. The synthesis of SiO2-modified CaO and its CO2 absorption property were investigated by TG, SEM, ICP, and TEM.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Tobermorite

SiO2-modified CaO was prepared by using ALR as a Si and Ca source. The process of the synthesis of SiO2-modified CaO is shown in Figure . The composition of ALR is shown in Table , and SiO2 and CaO contents are over 70%. During the hydrothermal process at 240 °C, ALR transformed into tobermorite; then, tobermorite converted into SiO2-modified CaCO3 by CO2-pressurized hydrothermal treating; and the SiO2-modified CaO-based CO2 absorbent was obtained by calcination at 900 °C. The preparation of tobermorite was carried out as follows: 5 g of ALR and 50 mL of deionized water were added to the lining of a hydrothermal reactor. The mixture was then heated to 240 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min. After reacting at 240 °C for 6 h, the mixture was cooled down to room temperature. The mixture was filtered and washed with deionized water three times, and the filter cake was dried at 120 °C for 8 h to obtain tobermorite.

Conversion of Tobermorite to the CO2 Absorbent

When tobermorite was prepared, it was used to synthesize SiO2-modified CaO. The preparation procedure was as follows: 2 g of tobermorite and deionized water (50 g) were added to the lining of a hydrothermal reactor. Then, CO2 was charged into the hydrothermal reactor to 2 MPa, and it was degassed to ordinary pressure. After three cycles of charging and discharging, CO2 was charged into the hydrothermal reactor to 2 MPa, and then, it was heated to the setting temperature. After the CO2-pressured hydrothermal reaction, SiO2-modified CaCO3 was obtained after the mixture was filtered. And the filtrate was set to 100 mL flask volumetric, and Ca content were measured by ICP to calculate leaching ratio of Ca. The SiO2-modified CaO-based CO2 absorbent was obtained after calcination at 900 °C in a muffle furnace for 30 min.

CO2 Absorption

The CO2 absorption experiment of the sample was measured using a METTLER DSC 3+. The experimental procedure was as follows: 8 mg of the sample was weighed and placed in a crucible, which was then positioned in the furnace of the instrument. Then, the furnace was heated to 900 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under a N2 atmosphere (50 mL/min). After keeping at 900 °C for 10 min, the temperature was cooled down to 700 °C, and the atmosphere was switched to CO2 (50 mL/min) for a 30 min carbonation experiment. Then, it was heated to 900 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under a N2 atmosphere (50 mL/min) to conduct decarbonation. The cycle was repeated 20 times for the CO2 absorption and desorption to measure the CO2 absorption performance.

Characteristics

N2 physical adsorption and desorption analysis was carried out using a physical adsorption apparatus (Beijing Jingwei Gaobo, JW-BK 132F). The specific surface area was determined by BET multipoint specific surface area, and the P/P 0 was 0.05–0.3. The pore size distribution and average pore diameter were calculated by the BJH method. The surface morphology of the sample was observed by a thermal field emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-7001F) and a transmission electron microscope (FEI Talos F200X G2). The phase transformation was investigated by an X-ray diffraction analyzer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation (30 kV, 15 mA, λ = 1.54056 Å) with a scan speed of 4 deg/min between 2θ = 10° and 80°.

Results and Discussion

The Preparation of Tobermorite

Figure displays the XRD patterns of ALR and its corresponding hydrothermal product synthesized at 240 °C. ALR is primarily composed of Na2CaSiO4 and Ca2SiO4. After hydrothermal treatment at 240 °C, the diffraction peaks of Na2CaSiO4 and Ca2SiO4 vanish and the ALR undergoes a transformation into tobermorite, as indicated by the appearance of its characteristic diffraction peaks. At the same time, the Na in ALR can be leached out. The recovered Na can be recycled in the process of catalytic gasification and Al leaching from coal ash. Tobermorite is one of the calcium silicate hydrates, and it can be synthesized using SiO2 and lime by hydrothermal treatment at 180–200 °C. When coal ash is chosen as the Ca source and Si source, it usually needs higher temperature to complete the conversion of tobermorite. In our previous study, ALR can transform into tobermorite at temperatures over 220 °C. Tobermorite has higher reactivity, and it can ionize and dissociate the Ca2+ ion in the presence of water, which is conducive to the further transformation of CaCO3. ALR is composed of calcium silicate and sodium calcium silicate in the form of block particles (Figure a,b). Tobermorite forms in the hydrothermal process at 240 °C, and it is in the form of flakes or fibrous shapes with high aspect ratio. Under hydrothermal treatment for 2 h, tobermorite exhibits an incomplete development and displays an irregular, fibrous, and flocculent morphology (Figure c,d). As the hydrothermal reaction time is prolonged to 6 h, there is a progressive enhancement in the formation of tobermorite, resulting in the emergence of a needle-like structure (Figure e,f).

Synthesis of SiO2-Modified CaO

SiO2-modified CaCO3 can be synthesized through a CO2-pressured hydrothermal reaction. CO2, being an acidic gas, dissolves in water under a high partial pressure to form H2CO3. H2CO3 can react with tobermorite and facilitate Ca leaching. As shown in Figure , when the temperature of pressured CO2 leaching is high, only a small part of the Ca in tobermorite can be leached, and most of Ca converts into CaCO3. At a CO2 pressure of 2 MPa for 6 h at 100 °C, the calcium leaching rate reaches only 7.8%. Combined with the phase composition before and after leaching, tobermorite is converted into CaCO3 during CO2-pressurized leaching. As a result, SiO2-modified CaCO3 is successfully synthesized. When the hydrothermal temperature is 20 °C, H2CO3 dissociates to produce HCO3 –1 and H+, and the acidic conditions are conducive to the leaching of Ca. With the extension of the leaching time, Ca leaching ratio swiftly increases. At the same time, the residue after leaching transforms into amorphous SiO2, corresponding to a peak at 2θ between 20° and 30° (Figure ). As shown in Figure , with a leaching temperature of 20 °C with 2 MPa of CO2, the leaching rate of Ca continues to increase with the leaching time. When the leaching time is 8 h, the leaching ratio of Ca is 81%, and when the leaching time increases to 16 h, the leaching ratio of Ca reaches 94%. In order to convert most of Ca into CaCO3, a high temperature of CO2-pressured leaching is chosen.

CO2 Absorption Property

Pure CaO was used to compare the CO2 absorption performance of SiO2-modified CaO. Figure shows the CO2 absorption performance of pure CaO during 20 carbonation and decarbonation. When CaO absorbs CO2 for the first time after calcination, the conversion of CaO is as high as 91.2%. With the increase in the number of cycles, the conversion of CaO decreases sharply. The conversion of CaO swiftly decreases to 64.9% after three cycles of carbonation and decarbonation. With the number of cycles increasing to 10, the conversion of CaO is only 42.9%. This decline in CO2 absorption performance is attributed to the tendency of CaO absorbents to sinter during carbonation and decarbonation, leading to denser particles and a reduced specific surface area. As a result, CO2 diffusion is hindered, which diminishes the stability of the CO2 absorption over multiple cycles.

CO2 absorption performance of pure CaO during 20 times of carbonation and decarbonation.

SiO2 usually acts as an inert material to resist CaO sintering. When tobermorite transforms into SiO2-modified CaCO3, the dispersion of CaO is enhanced. This high dispersion of CaO on the SiO2 surface improves its CO2 absorption performance over multiple cycles. Figure shows the CO2 absorption performance of various CaO samples during 20 carbonation and decarbonation. After 10 cycles of CO2-pressurized hydrothermal leaching at 100 °C and 2 MPa of CO2, the CaO conversion reaches 70.3%. In contrast, after 2 h of the reaction, the CaO conversion is 63.1%, while pure CaO has a conversion rate of only 42.9% after 10 cycles. Due to the limitation that the CaO content in the aluminum extraction residue is only 41.6%, the highest CaO content in the prepared Ca-based absorbent is only 60.7%. Compared with pure CaO, the Ca-based absorbent shows a better absorption amount after 10 cycles. After 15 cycles, the absorption amounts of pure CaO and the Ca-based adsorbent (Ca-100-8 h) are 30.7 and 32.5 g CO2/(100 g absorbent), respectively. This indicates that SiO2 as a dispersant can improve the CO2 absorption performance to a great extent. When the temperature of CO2-pressured leaching is low, most of the Ca will be leached out in solution (Figure ). Even if the CaO conversion can be maintained at a high level, the overall amount of CO2 absorption will be low due to the excessive loss of Ca in the CO2-pressured leaching. Figure a,b shows the SEM images of tobermorite after the CO2-pressured leaching. During CO2-pressured leaching, acicular tobermorite with a high length-to-diameter ratio fractures but retains its fibrous structure. The fracture surface in the process of CO2-pressured leaching can be clearly seen in Figure c. During high-temperature carbonation and decarbonation, pure CaO undergoes sintering, whereas SiO2-modified CaO can resist sintering to some extent. Despite the addition of SiO2, sintering still occurs. After 10 cycles, SiO2-modified CaO maintains some length-to-diameter ratio but no longer exhibits a regular needle-like or fibrous structure (Figure e,f). Additionally, small particles of SiO2-modified CaO show signs of adhesion due to high-temperature sintering.

After CO2 absorption, the main phase composition absorber is CaCO3, and SiO2 mainly exists as amorphous SiO2. After calcination, the reaction converts into CaO (Figure ). The CO2 absorption processes of pure CaO and SiO2-modified CaO are quite different. Figure shows the CO2 absorption processes of pure CaO and SiO2-modified CaO under different cycles. The CO2 absorption process of pure CaO is faster and the absorption balance can be reached quickly, while SiO2-modified CaO can reach a higher CaO conversion with a longer absorption time. Compared with pure CaO, the CaO content in the absorbent is only 60.7%, and SiO2-modified CaO has a higher specific surface area (69.2 m2/g). The structure of the absorbent is layered, and there may exist strong internal diffusion resistance during the CO2 absorption process so that the absorption process is prolonged. SiO2-modified CaO has good dispersion, and the addition of SiO2 can effectively inhibit the sintering of CaO at a high temperature. After completing the cycles of carbonation and decarbonation for 5 and 10 times, the decrease of CO2 absorption is not obvious, indicating that SiO2-modified CaO can effectively inhibit the sintering of CaO at high temperature and improve the cycling stability.

The pore size distribution of the CO2 absorbers affects the CO2 absorption performance. ALR is mainly composed of calcium silicate and calcium sodium silicate, and its specific surface area is only 9.62 m2/g (Table ). After hydrothermal treatment at 240 °C for 6 h, fibrous tobermorite with a high length–diameter ratio is prepared, and its specific surface area sharply increases to 65.4 m2/g. However, the specific surface area of pure CaO is only 6.2 m2/g, and after 10 cycles, it decreases to 5.1 m2/g. This reduction is primarily due to sintering and agglomeration occurring during repeated carbonation and decarbonation processes. During the CO2-pressured leaching, part of Ca leaches to the solution, and micropores form during this process, accompanied by the increase of specific surface area to 69.2 m2/g. At the same time, tobermorite transforms into CaCO3. After 10 cycles of carbonation and decarbonation, SiO2-modified CaO undergoes sintering (Figure f), resulting in a decrease in the specific surface area to 60.4 m2/g. At the same time, the conversion of CaO decreases from 93.8% for the first time to 70.3% after 10 times. Tobermorite is a layered silicate material with infinite CaO2 layers and Si3(O,OH)9 chains (Figure ). CaO2 layers are partly dissolved in an acid aqueous solution to form Ca2+. When it reacts with a CO2-pressured liquid at low temperature, Ca dissolves into the liquid, and SiO2 with high specific surface area can be synthesized. With high temperature during CO2-pressured leaching, tobermorite transforms into SiO2-modified CaCO3. It converts into SiO2-modified CaO after calcination at 900 °C, which has a high CO2 absorption capacity and cyclic stability in the process of CO2 absorption.

Conclusion

In this paper, ALR is used as the raw material to prepare fibrous tobermorite through the process of the hydrothermal reaction at 240 °C for 6 h. By CO2-pressured leaching at 100 °C with 2 MPa of CO2, tobermorite converts into SiO2-modified CaCO3. After calcination at 900 °C, SiO2-modified CaO shows good CO2 absorption and cyclic stability during the process of CO2 absorption. The CaO conversion of CO2 is 93.8% during the first CO2 adsorption, and the CaO conversion rate can still remain at 70.3% after 10 cycles, while the CaO conversion rate of pure CaO after 10 cycles is only 42.9%. Due to the limitation of the CaO content in the aluminum extraction residue, the synthesized SiO2-modified CaO shows a better CO2 absorption amount after 10 cycles. After 15 cycles, the absorption amounts of pure CaO and the Ca-based adsorbent (Ca-100-8 h) are 30.7 and 32.5 g of CO2/(100 g of absorbent), respectively. SiO2-modified CaO with a smaller diameter and higher specific surface area still has a fibrous structure similar to tobermorite. The special structure of tobermorite can inhibit the sintering of SiO2-modified CaO and improve its cyclic stability in the process of CO2 absorption at high temperature.
