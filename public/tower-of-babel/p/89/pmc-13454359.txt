A fully-CMOS spiking LIF neuron implementation for optimized STDP learning on memristor
Authors: Mehrzad Karamimanesh, S. M. Rasoul Moosavi, Ebrahim Abiri, Mahyar Shahsavari, Kourosh Hassanli, Prashant Kumar, Sung-Mo Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing demand for fast and energy-efficient computing has motivated the development of neuromorphic hardware inspired by biological neural systems. Spiking neural networks (SNNs), as the third generation of neural networks, offer an event-driven and highly parallel computing paradigm that is well suited for such applications. A key challenge in hardware SNNs is the efficient implementation of synaptic learning mechanisms, particularly spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), with minimal circuit complexity and energy overhead. In this work, we propose a fully CMOS leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron designed to enable local, on-chip STDP-like learning when interfaced with analog memristive synapses. The proposed neuron generates a bipolar output spike composed of both positive and negative voltage pulses, allowing direct modulation of memristor conductance without the need for complex peripheral circuits or explicit timing storage elements. The neuron operates in two distinct modes of training mode, which produces bipolar spikes to support synaptic updates, and inference mode, which generates a single unipolar spike while deactivating non-essential circuitry to reduce power consumption. To validate the proposed design, a proof-of-concept 15×4 spiking neural network incorporating a winner-takes-all (WTA) mechanism is implemented in 65-nm CMOS technology and evaluated using circuit-level simulations. The results demonstrate correct local synaptic adaptation, stable neuron operation under process variations, and successful pattern association during training and inference. The network processes each training pattern within 0.6 ms and performs inference within 0.32 ms.

Introduction

With the increasing volume of data and the complexity of computations, designing hardware that provides high computing speed and optimal power efficiency has become essential. Traditional architectures, such as the von Neumann, suffer from a separation between the computation unit and memory, resulting in high power consumption and slower processing speed1,2. Scientific studies suggest that no existing processor can match the human brain’s capabilities3. This fact has motivated researchers to develop faster and more efficient hardware inspired by neural architecture4.

Inspired by the human brain, neuromorphic computing integrates memory and processing, enables parallel and event-driven computing, and models information transmission in the brain5. Studies of the human brain have led to the development of SNNs, the third generation neural network6. SNNs can transmit information using even a single spike, allowing faster and more efficient hardware processors7.

The key components of biological neural networks are synapses and neurons. The brain’s learning processes are facilitated by high tens of billions of interconnected neurons communicating through high tens of trillions of synapses3. Artificial neurons are generally categorized into biologically plausible and biologically inspired types8. While biologically inspired neurons incorporate fewer biological features, their simplicity makes them suitable for designing more efficient hardware. Recently, very-large-scale integration (VLSI) circuit designers have increasingly focused on developing various components of SNNs5,9, such as the LIF neuron10–14, and synapses15. In artificial neural networks, each synapse models the connection strength between two neurons using its weight. SNNs pay particular attention to the temporal difference in spike occurrences in pre-synaptic and post-synaptic neurons. STDP, a widely used unsupervised learning algorithm in SNNs, adjusts each synaptic weight based on the temporal difference between spikes from the two connected neurons16. Therefore, effectively implementing the STDP mechanism in silicon can significantly reduce circuit complexity, area overhead, and power consumption. This reduction in complexity paves the way for constructing larger-scale and deeper architectures. Consequently, in recent years, various STDP synapse circuit architectures have been proposed, among which fully CMOS-based synapses represent a prominent category.

The STDP synapse architecture presented in15 is a fully CMOS-based design. However, this synapse uses a complex structure with three capacitors, significantly increasing the dimensions of the network and the overall circuit. For instance, two 15×3 networks implemented for pattern recognition required 135 capacitors in each network. A fully-CMOS structure of synapses has been introduced in17, offering low power consumption and small dimensions. Yet, this architecture adds considerable complexity to the system due to the large number of components involved and the need for biasing circuits.

Another approach to synapse implementation involves the use of emerging semiconductor-based devices, such as memristors. The memristor, a two-terminal device, is widely used in electronic circuits, notably for emulating biological synapses18. Among the different types of memristors utilized as synapses, analog memristor is particularly well-suited for implementing the STDP learning algorithm due to its ability to hold multiple states8,19. Therefore, the memristor is a desirable option, as it can significantly reduce circuit complexity. An essential characteristic of memristor functionality is that inducing appropriate memductance changes critically depends on the magnitude and polarity of the applied stimulus, meaning the stimulus must exceed the device’s threshold and be applied with the correct polarity across its two terminals to effect weight modulation20. Hence, it is important that the output waveform of the neuron aligns with this process; otherwise, additional circuitry is required to store timing information and apply the appropriate changes to the memristor. However, most designed LIF neurons do not generate a waveform suitable for this mechanism.

In10, an integrate-and-fire (I&F) neuron is introduced to model the biological neuron behavior. However, the spike output has not been connected to any synapse or network for evaluation; only the output of a single neuron has been assessed. A neuron structure using a memristor emulator is presented in21, but it has large dimensions and its output waveform has not been applied to modify the memristor’s weight. Its validation has been conducted on a 12×1 network with a single input pattern, and no training mechanism has been proposed. In13, a neuron equipped with a soft-reset mechanism has been proposed to minimize information loss caused by capacitor overcharging or improper discharging. But for network evaluation, a high-level PyTorch simulation has been conducted by modeling the proposed neuron, without performing any circuit-level assessments. Vohra et al. in12,22 developed a neural associative memory (NAM) using the LIF neuron designed in11 and the fully CMOS synapse introduced by15. The employed LIF neuron produces narrow output pulses with sufficient drive strength to effectively influence synapses and generate a measurable current flow. But, due to its narrow-width positive spike, it can only be used in the inference mode and is not capable of adjusting the memductance; thus, training is performed in an offline manner.

The hybrid memristor-CMOS LIF neuron proposed in23 successfully enables local and online weight updates on memristor by generating spikes with positive and negative values, provided that pre-synaptic spike overlaps with post-synaptic one. The utilization of spikes with both positive and negative polarities enables the local and online implementation of the STDP algorithm on memristor-based synapses. Nonetheless, the asymmetry in the generated spike waveform in23 has led to irregular weight updates in the weights. Also, this design requires a high excitation current, a very large capacitor (100 nF), and large resistances (in kΩ range), making it unfeasible to construct large networks. On the other hand, implementing STDP in this design relies on peripheral circuits such as buffers and current mirrors, which increases circuit complexity and area footprint.

Based on prior analysis, designing an appropriate neuron can address many aspects of SNNs and may lead to either a simplification or increased complexity of the network and its circuits. Furthermore, the type of spike generated can enable important objectives such as local and online learning, especially in memristor-based networks. Designing a neuron circuit in alignment with the training algorithm and synapse type facilitates the development of an efficient SNN with reduced complexity and area footprint, while maintaining acceptable accuracy and speed.

In this work, a fully-CMOS neuron is proposed, and an analog memristor is selected as a synapse because of its inherent compatibility with the STDP algorithm. The proposed neuron generates a waveform composed of a bipolar pulse with both positive and negative values for each spike, making it well-suited for a simplified implementation of the STDP learning algorithm on analog memristors that exhibit a threshold-based switching behavior. SNN with such neurons and memristor-based synapses can be implemented without relying on a complex design and additional peripheral circuits, which is well-suited for constructing larger-scale networks. Furthermore, the designed neuron has the ability to operate in two distinct modes, training and inference, by adaptively generating waveforms tailored to the requirements of each mode. The inference waveform consists of a single positive pulse and deactivates a significant portion of the circuitry, thereby reducing power consumption. Moreover, the neuron’s output spike is compatible with both fully-CMOS and memristor-based synapses. An overview of memristors

The term memristor is derived from the words ’memory’ and ’resistor,’ referring to a device that exhibits the properties of a resistor with memory capability, allowing it to store information in the form of resistance. In 2008, following the release of the first physical memristor by HP24, memristors garnered significant attention as a emerging element in electronic circuits. Thanks to their unique characteristics, memristors are widely utilized in various fundamental circuits, including logic gates, oscillators, and converters25. In neural network circuits, memristors are used as synapses with stored weights26. Also, their switching capability is commonly utilized in constructing various types of neurons, including IF and LIF neurons27,28.Fig. 1(a) Overall scheme of HP memristor structure. (b) Symmetric and exponential characteristics of memristors that can be desirable for use in STDP.

The characteristic of memristors is broadly categorized into two main types: volatile and non-volatile. Volatile memristors lose the stored data when the power supply is disconnected, whereas non-volatile memristors retain their previous state even when the power is cut off. The general structure of the memristor comprises a semiconductor sandwiched between two conductors. The semiconductor is divided into doped and undoped (gap) parts, where the doped region exhibits higher conductivity compared to the undoped region. Figure 1(a) shows a simple picture of HP memristor structure. D is the width of the semiconductor, and w(t) denotes the width of the doped area. When the memristor is affected by a stimulus, a conductive layer of doped atoms (oxygen in the HP model) is formed inside the semiconductor, establishing a connection between the two conductors. In volatile memristors, the conductive layer vanishes when the stimulus is removed, returning the structure to its initial state. In contrast, non-volatile memristors retain the formed layer unchanged. The changes in the conductive layers or filaments in memristors are either a function of voltage (voltage-controlled) or a function of current (current-controlled).

In biological neural networks, data is transmitted between consecutive neurons through synapses. To implement electronic neural networks, synapses of various types have been used. Transistor-based synapses17, or emerging devices such as resistive random-access memory (RRAM), phase change memory (PCM), threshold switching memristor (TSM), etc. have been utilized as synapses in electronic circuits26. Memristors with analog characteristics are more suitable candidates for synapses, as they can function like biological synapses by presenting various memristance or memductance values. Memristors that exhibit analog behavior can show gradual changes in memristance or memductance under stimulus. Additionally, the values of these two parameters can exponentially increase in the magnitude of the stimulus19. Non-volatile memristors have a positive threshold excitation for inducing changes29 and can retain the weights in a network even after the power source is disconnected. Memristors with a threshold for changes, where these changes occur exponentially and symmetrically, are suitable candidates for implementing synapses with STDP learning capability30. Physical models are defined by the structure and characteristics of a fabricated memristor19. Proposed design

CMOS circuit can be configured to implement various neuron models, such as LIF or Izhikevich, with a good mimicking of neuronal behavior. This section first addresses the implementation of a fully-CMOS-based LIF neuron. Subsequently, the process of applying STDP to memristor synapses and the resulting memductance weight modifications are examined, followed by an evaluation of the overall functionality of the designed circuits.

LIF neuron

Neuron model is an inseparable part of the SNN structure. Although not perfectly matching a biological neuron, the LIF neuron can still be a preferable choice due to its simplicity. The main parts of a LIF neuron circuit comprise a capacitor for membrane potential, a leakage circuit for discharge, and a spike generator for output. In this paper, a neuron capable of generating both positive and negative voltages during spiking pulse generation is designed to induce changes in the memristance, similar to the STDP algorithm. This neuron can provide the required voltage difference, both positive and negative, to adjust the memristance when a memristor serves as a synapse between two consecutive neurons. Hence, the need for peripheral circuits to switch the applied voltage polarity is eliminated; this point will be explained further in the next section. In contrast to other neurons10,11 the proposed neuron generates spikes with a broader pulse width, allowing it to influence the memristor (most fabricated memristors require a certain time period for significant changes35).Fig. 2(a) Proposed LIF neuron circuit. STDP learning

Artificial neural networks require algorithms to enable them to learn tasks such as classification, pattern recognition, etc. These algorithms adjust the network parameters towards optimal values for learning about input by iterating over the input dataset. In general, the training methods can be divided into two categories: supervised and unsupervised. In this article, the STDP unsupervised algorithm is utilized, considered a better choice for the following reasons:Using memristors as synapses simplifies the STDP implementation since their conductivity, measured in memductance, can be changed by applying voltages. The proposed circuit design does not substantially increase the system’s hardware overhead.The principles of biological neural systems are more compatible with the unsupervised learning rules, and STDP is one of the closest proposed rules to the brain structure.STDP is easier to implement and more suitable for hardware than the traditional learning methods like the backpropagation.As discussed in Introduction, biological synapses are reflected in artificial synapses through weight expression. The STDP rule in SNNs states that the weight of a synapse changes based on the time difference between pre- and post-neuron spikes. Greater weight changes occur when the time difference between pre- and post-neuron spikes is smaller. Moreover, weight changes can be either positive or negative. When the pre-synaptic neuron spikes earlier than the post-synaptic neuron, the changes are positive, and the weight increases. Conversely, when the post-synaptic neuron spikes earlier than the pre-synaptic neuron, the weight decreases5. It is important to note that the time difference is not unlimited and it has a specific limit. If the time difference of spikes in two neurons exceeds this limit, there will be no changes in weight. In prior CMOS-based synapse designs, it is typical to employ a capacitor to execute STDP for time representation, and the employed capacitor also serves as the weight holder17. However, using a capacitor occupies a large footprint and due to its charge leakage, it cannot be precisely controlled. Therefore, it can lead to undesired alterations in the weight. Furthermore, capacitors require periodic recharging to maintain their weight values, which leads to significant power consumption.

Using memristors as synapses simplifies the STDP implementation since their conductivity, measured in memductance, can be changed by applying voltages. The proposed circuit design does not substantially increase the system’s hardware overhead.

On the other hand, in memristor-based synapses, the weights are modeled by the conductance of the memristor (memductance) or its resistance (memristance). Hence, to implement the STDP learning rule, the weight changes are modeled by variations in memductance or memristance. Applying the STDP rule in these synapses is based on voltage changes on both sides of the synapse.

There are two main approaches for training memristor-based weights. One method is ex-situ training, where the optimal weights are first computed in software and then transferred onto the memristor devices. The final circuit is used exclusively for inference11, where online training is not applicable. Moreover, this method may lead to reduced accuracy due to its inability to account for device-to-device variations36. Consequently, the weights computed in software may differ from those ultimately implemented in hardware. An alternative approach for updating memristor states is in-situ training, which is performed in an online manner. Threshold-switching memristors (TSMs) possess a specific voltage threshold below which no changes occur in their state37. When the amplitude of the voltage difference exceeds this threshold, weight updates are induced. Therefore, if the voltage difference across the memristor exceeds the threshold during the simultaneous occurrence of pre- and post-synaptic spikes, synaptic weight changes can be induced in the memristor.

The proposed neuron in this paper can generate spikes with both positive and negative voltages. Thus, if there are spikes in both the pre- and post-neurons and the time difference is short, the magnitude of the voltage difference across the memristor will be equal to about 2VDD, which exceeds the threshold and changes the conductance of the memristor. When the pre-synaptic neuron produces a spike before the post-synaptic neuron, this voltage difference will cause the memristor’s conductance to increase, resulting in a potentiation operation. Conversely, if the post-synaptic neuron generates a spike before the pre-synaptic neuron, the voltage difference will decrease the memristor’s conductance, describing a depression operation. In other words, by generating spikes with both positive and negative voltages, the neurons can independently perform the STDP on the memristors and update the weights accordingly. On the other hand, if only one neuron spikes, the applied voltage will be below the threshold, and will not affect the memristor resistance.Fig. 4An overview of the interaction between pre- and post-synaptic neurons on the memristor, along with the necessary generated and control signals, enabling the application of STDP learning on the memristor.

The proposed method for updating weights enables the system to implement STDP without requiring complex additional peripheral circuits.

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{active}$$\end{document} signal is equal to VDD during standby mode and changes to VSS when a spike is generated. Simultaneously, the parallel switch is activated, allowing the pre-neuron spike to completely pass through and be applied to the anode side of the memristor. The signal generated by the post-neuron is fed back to the cathode side of the memristor, as shown in Fig. 6(d). This setup enables weight adjustment.

Figure 6(e) illustrates the post-neuron circuit, which is designed similarly to the pre-neuron shown in Fig 2(a), except that an NMOS transistor is placed in parallel with the capacitor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{mem}$$\end{document}, which is controlled using the RS (Reset) signal. Furthermore, the neuron also utilizes another output signal called TRIG. If the post-neuron does not generate the spike, the TRIG signal remains at VDD. The RS signal of each neuron is generated by the NAND operation on the TRIG signals from the other three neurons. When the output neurons do not produce the spike, the RS signal is equal to zero. Consequently, the transistor parallel to the capacitor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{mem}$$\end{document} remains off, allowing the capacitor to charge.

However, when a spike occurs at the output of a post-neuron, its TRIG signal goes to 0, which causes the output of the three connected NAND gates to be set to VDD. As a result, the capacitors of the other three neurons are discharged and their voltages do not reach the threshold required to produce the spike. In other words, when one neuron produces a spike, the other three output neurons are suppressed, thus establishing the WTA mechanism.

As described above, an SNN comprising 15 pre-synaptic and 4 post-synaptic neurons has been implemented using a memristor crossbar. This network is trained with the STDP algorithm through the proposed neuron model, without need for peripheral circuits or signals. It is also capable of executing the WTA mechanism. After the training is completed, the circuit enters the inference phase, and the amplifier, diode, and switch circuits in the network are turned off. As a result, the neurons produce only the positive pulse. Additionally, the feedback from the post-neuron is also deactivated.

∙ Tonic Bursting: One notable pattern observed in biological neurons’ spiking behavior is tonic bursting. Tonic bursting refers to an electrical activity pattern in neurons in which they produce repeated clusters of spikes at a relatively consistent and stable frequency. To better understand the behavior of pre- and post-synaptic spikes and their impact on the memristor, Steps 1 to 8 are indicated in Fig. 10(a) and are briefly described in the following; ① Initially, due to the inactivity of the system, the amplifiers are turned off, and the outputs of the neurons remain floating; ② Due to the absence of the post-synaptic spike, the switch remains off, causing the negative edge of the pre-synaptic spike to be filtered out; ③ The positive edge passes through the diode and experiences a drop. Since the output of the post-neuron is floating, its voltage follows the pre-synaptic spike value; ④ When the post-neuron generates a spike, the switch turns on, allowing the full signal value of the pre-synaptic neuron to pass through; ⑤ At this moment, the overlap between the signals of the pre- and post-synaptic neurons occurs, leading to a change in memristance; ⑥ As the pre-synaptic spike ends, the neuron’s output becomes floating; therefore, due to the presence of the post-synaptic spike, its voltage follows that; ⑦ The positive edge of the post-synaptic spike persists, and the floating output of the pre-synaptic neuron continues to follow it; ⑧ As the post-synaptic spike ends, its output also becomes floating, causing both terminals of the memristor to return to their initial floating state. Network performance

To test the proposed network, four 5×3 images has been used. Each image is flattened into a 15×1 column format, feeding 15 pre-neurons (input). A zero column is placed between each pair of consecutive images to allow capacitor discharge and to improve the separation of input data. For example, the input for the first pre-neuron, excluding the zero column, would be “0100” representing the first pixel of each of entries the four images. With the zero entries added, the input becomes “00100000” and is applied to the first pre-neuron. Each bit has a duration of 2 μs. The described process is shown in Fig. 12(a). The images comprise white pixels, representing high-resistance states (HRS), and black pixels representing low-resistance states (LRS).Fig. 12(a) Arrangement and preparation of input data for the network’s input neurons, aimed at training and validating the proposed components. At the beginning, pre-neurons generate spikes, causing currents to flow through the memristors. These currents, determined by the memristances in the path, are produced at the output nodes of the memristor. These electrical currents are summed and then injected into post-neurons, causing their capacitors to charge. When the membrane capacitor reaches the threshold, each post-neuron generates a spike that is fed back to the corresponding column of memristors. At the same time, switches are activated, fully applying the output signal of the pre-neurons to the other side of the memristors. Consequently, each spike generated by the post-neurons sets the conditions for weight adjustments in the connected memristors.

Initially, the memristor weights are set randomly. As input data progresses, the weights start to change from their initial values. With each iteration, the memristor weights gradually converge to the corresponding image pixel values, and after a sufficient number of iterations, they become aligned. As shown in Fig. 12(b), the process begins with random initial weights. After 85 iterations, the observed weight has shifted from its initial random state, moving closer to the desired values. In the next stage, after 150 iterations, the weights have nearly matched the ideal values. Once the training is done, the circuit shifts into the inference phase. The Train signal is disabled, and the Inference signal is enabled. With the activation of the Inference signal, neurons produce only the positive pulse, amplifiers are deactivated in the circuit, and feedback in the post-neurons is disabled, significantly reducing power consumption as previously described.

Suppose image S is provided as input. It is expected that only the desired post-neuron will produce spikes. However, among the three other images, some pixels share similar colors with those in image S, which causes some amount of current to be transmitted to the other post-neurons as well. While this current is weaker than the corresponding column, other neurons may still produce occasional spikes within a specific range. At this stage, the WTA mechanism ensures that only one output wins per image while suppressing the others. Thus, each time an image is applied to the pre-neurons, a spike is produced at the output neuron associated with the trained column for that image, as shown in Fig. 13. In simpler terms, the neuron corresponding to the target class spikes more frequently due to its optimized weights, and the WTA mechanism prevents activity in other neurons. This mechanism is essential and unavoidable at the end of the network and in the output, as it not only prevents incorrect weight updates during training but also aids in identifying the correct class in the inference phase. It should be noted that the presented network is evaluated as a circuit-level associative learning task rather than a conventional classification benchmark. Learning performance is therefore assessed based on successful pattern association and stable WTA behavior after convergence. Similar small-scale pattern association tasks have been commonly adopted in prior circuit-level SNN and memristive learning studies to validate hardware learning functionality. For the demonstrated four-pattern task, all trained patterns are correctly recalled after training, as shown in Fig. 13, corresponding to 100% correct association.

Although the demonstrated network is limited to a 15×4 configuration, the proposed architectural simplifications are applied at the neuron and synapse levels and are therefore independent of network size. Power consumption in the presented design is dominated by spike-driven activity rather than static biasing, suggesting proportional scaling with the number of active neurons. Timing behavior is governed by local neuron dynamics and synaptic interactions, which remain unchanged as the network grows. While dense memristive crossbar arrays introduce challenges such as wire resistance and IR-drop, these effects are well studied and can be addressed using established techniques such as array partitioning and hierarchical architectures, which are complementary to the proposed neuron design.Fig. 13The network output in inference mode after the training process, where the corresponding neuron solely generates the spike with the help of the WTA mechanism upon receiving the target inputs and determining the class.

The network output in inference mode after the training process, where the corresponding neuron solely generates the spike with the help of the WTA mechanism upon receiving the target inputs and determining the class.

After the training phase, the learned patterns have been evaluated under controlled pixel-flip noise to assess robustness. First, a single-pixel perturbation has been applied to each 5×3 pattern. Since each pattern contains 15 pixels, flipping one pixel corresponds to a 6.66% corruption level, yielding 15 distinct noisy variants per pattern. Under this condition, the network correctly classified 100% of the corrupted inputs, indicating full tolerance to minor input perturbations. The experiment has then been extended to two-pixel corruption, corresponding to a 13.3% pixel error rate and 105 possible noisy configurations per pattern. Even under this higher distortion level, the network preserved 95.3% correct classification accuracy, demonstrating strong resilience to moderate structural perturbations. Finally, three-pixel corruption has been evaluated. This corresponds to a 20% pixel error rate and results in 455 possible corrupted variations per pattern. At this level of noise, the classification accuracy decreased to 76.3%. While performance degradation is expected with increasing corruption, the network still maintained meaningful class discrimination even under substantial input distortion.

A complementary experiment has also been conducted, in which corrupted samples were introduced during the training phase. Specifically, the network has been trained using 1-pixel (15 variants per pattern), 2-pixel (105 variants per pattern), and 3-pixel (455 variants per pattern) corrupted inputs. Interestingly, after exposure to these noisy training samples, the network consistently recognized the original clean patterns during inference. This observation suggests that moderate stochastic perturbations during training do not degrade convergence; instead, they can enhance robustness and improve generalization behavior. However, it should be noted that beyond a certain corruption threshold, the beneficial effect reverses. As noise increases further, convergence becomes unstable, and learning dynamics deteriorate, leading to degraded classification performance.

As discussed in previous sections, the proposed neuron was introduced with a novel approach, generating each spike with positive and negative pulses. Based on the literature reviewed in the Introduction, no prior work, except23, followed a similar pattern; however, in that work23, the neurons were implemented using memristors rather than fully-CMOS technology, and the generated spike can only be used for the training phase. Therefore, a precise comparison of all parameters between the proposed design and previous works may not be entirely fair; yet, it can provide a general estimation of their performances and offer a relative perspective on the objectives, strengths, and weaknesses of each design.

The proposed neuron circuit has an energy consumption of approximately 5.72 pJ per spike during the training phase. In21,23, and13, circuit designs with energy consumptions of 4 nJ, 196 pJ, and 77.24 pJ, respectively, have been proposed. These designs exhibit high energy consumption due to using components such as voltage dividers, comparators, and high-capacity capacitors or resistors. If we compare the energy consumption based on the scaling rules of integrated circuits, the scaled energy consumption of our proposed design is less by more than 50% compared to13, while providing sufficient power to drive the memristor and enabling STDP-based training. The main energy consumer in the proposed circuit is related to the activator and amplifier components. As a result, in inference mode, with these circuits deactivated, the neuron consumes only 1.69 pJ of energy per spike. In addition, when there is no spike at the output, the neuron remains in a floating state, which helps reduce energy consumption. Among previous works, only10 has been implemented in 65-nm technology, similar to this work, with an energy consumption of 3.6 pJ in the inference mode, which is more than twice that of the proposed design in the same mode. Also, the output waveforms of10,11,17,39 are not suitable for modulating the memristor and require additional circuitry to apply changes.

In the proposed neuron circuit, although the number of transistors has slightly increased (35 in training mode), they are configured with minimum dimensions and in optimal mode. Should memristors be selected as the synapse, without considering the required peripheral circuits, the neurons in10,11,13,17,21, with transistor counts ranging from 14 to 27, operate only in the inference mode. The proposed neuron circuit uses only 16 transistors in the inference mode, which is quite acceptable. The proposed neuron, which uses a membrane capacitor with a value of 50 fF, can achieve the same output frequency as similar neurons. It should be noted that the use of memristors requires stimulation over a longer time window compared to fully CMOS-based circuits in order to induce changes in their memductance. In the same vein, networks utilizing memristive synapses typically operate at lower frequencies. For instance, in11,23,40, the system frequencies were reported to be around 5 kHz, 50 kHz, and 80 Hz, respectively, values significantly lower compared to the 0.4 MHz operating frequency achieved by the proposed design. On the other hand, during inference mode, since weight updates are no longer required, the proposed neuron can operate at frequencies of approximately 5 MHz. The proposed neuron generates both bipolar pulses with positive and negative voltages, making it suitable for the modification of memristor synapses and eliminating the need for extra circuitry and control signals. Circuits in10,11,13,17,21,39 cannot generate any negative pulse, necessitating additional circuitry alongside the memristor for network training. In23, the neuron generates a suitable output waveform for applying STDP to the memristor, but this waveform is achieved by using three voltage sources with added complexity. Among all previous works,41 achieved the most ideal implementation of STDP on memristor by generating spikes composed of positive and negative triangular pulses. However, due to its highly analog nature, relying on fundamental components such as an operational amplifier, an inverting amplifier, six resistors, and a capacitor, along with the requirement for ± 15 V supply sources to generate spikes at only a few hertz, the efficient on-chip integration of this neuron becomes extremely challenging.

In the proposed neuron design, the refractory mechanism has been simplified to strike a balance between hardware complexity and functional behavior. After a spike occurs, the neuron is inhibited for a short, fixed refractory period. This prevention of immediate reactivation allows other neurons to increase their membrane potentials. In prior works, such as11,17, the refractory period has been adjusted by applying a distinct voltage to control the capacitor discharge rate. However, this approach requires either additional on-chip voltage generation circuits or external voltage sources, which increases both circuit complexity and power consumption and is therefore considered a drawback. This highlights the simplicity and power-efficient nature of the proposed approach. However, in17, the designed STDP circuit is large and complex, requiring 16 transistors per synapse and an additional 16 per neuron, which poses significant challenges for integration into large-scale networks. In this implementation, each neuron is assigned a large capacitor to store the weights, and a sizable resistor is needed. The training time for each pattern is approximately 0.6 ms, and for 4 input patterns, the total time for all inputs is 2.4 ms. This network can only recognize specific patterns for which the weights have been trained. To analyze new data, the software needs to recalculate the weights. In12,22, the weights are updated online. However, the initial weights need to be all equal, and each column is trained separately, thus the entire network cannot be trained simultaneously. Conclusion

This paper presented a CMOS-based spiking neural network architecture aimed at enabling local and on-chip learning using an STDP-inspired mechanism with reduced circuit complexity. The primary objective was to simplify the core building blocks of SNNs while maintaining functional learning capability and energy efficiency. To this end, a fully CMOS LIF neuron was designed to generate bipolar output spikes, allowing direct modulation of memristive synapses without the need for additional peripheral circuits or explicit timing storage elements. The proposed neuron supports both training and inference modes. During training, bipolar spikes facilitate local synaptic updates on memristors, while during inference, unnecessary circuit blocks are deactivated and only a unipolar spike is generated, leading to significantly reduced power consumption. All circuit components were designed and simulated in a unified Cadence environment using TSMC 65-nm technology, with the memristive synapse modeled in Verilog-A. To validate the feasibility of the proposed approach, a 15×4 spiking neural network incorporating a WTA mechanism was implemented and evaluated. Circuit-level simulations demonstrated stable neuron operation, correct local synaptic adaptation, and successful pattern association during both training and inference phases. The network achieved online learning with an average training time of approximately 0.6 ms per pattern and required about 0.32 ms for inference after training. Considering the use of memristive synapses and the requirement for wider spike waveforms to induce synaptic modifications, the proposed design exhibits competitive performance compared to prior works while significantly reducing architectural overhead. By integrating spike generation and synaptic modulation at the neuron level, this work highlights a hardware-feasible pathway toward simplified and energy-efficient neuromorphic systems capable of local learning.
