The ‘golden hour’: a geospatial analysis of travel time to public emergency departments in Kenya
Authors: Tushara Surapaneni, Nancy Gakii Kinyua, Benjamin W Wachira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn Kenya and many other low- and middle-income countries, prehospital care systems are underdeveloped or entirely non-existent, leaving emergency departments (EDs) as the primary point of care for medical emergencies. The aim of this cross-sectional observational study was to use a geographic information system (GIS) to comprehensively analyse access to public EDs in Kenya within 1-hour and 2-hour travel times.MethodsUsing open-source GIS software, population, land cover, elevation and road network data were analysed to create maps of 1-hour and 2-hour travel time catchment areas around public EDs in Kenya. Travel time analysis was calculated using AccessMod with a combined walking and motorised transport model.ResultsApproximately 93.7% of Kenya’s population has access to a public ED within 1 hour, and 98.2% within 2 hours. Of the 6.3% of the population lacking access to a public ED within 1 hour, many reside in rural areas with suboptimal road conditions. There was a significant difference in the proportions within 1-hour and 2-hour travel times across all counties (p<0.001). There was a weak association between the number of facilities in each county and the population proportion within 1 hour (ρ=0.237, p=0.109) and 2 hours (ρ=0.230, p=0.119).ConclusionsBy mapping population distribution in Kenya against the availability of public EDs, geospatial analysis provides crucial insights into emergency care access gaps, guiding policymakers in identifying areas that require infrastructure investments or prehospital service enhancements.Keywords: Africa South of the Sahara, Delivery of Health Care, Global Health, Health Services Accessibility, Kenya

Introduction

Timely access to quality emergency care has the potential to reduce mortality associated with non-communicable illnesses such as stroke and ischaemic heart disease, trauma, infectious illnesses such as sepsis and lower respiratory tract infections and pregnancy-related complications.1,3 Globally, a large proportion of mortality and disability-adjusted life years is attributed to emergency medical conditions, with a high burden occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).4 In Kenya, data from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease study estimated approximately 1 in 5 deaths in Kenya were related to emergent conditions, including ischaemic heart disease (3.1%), stroke (4.9%), lower respiratory infections (5%) and injuries (6.3%).5

Prehospital care systems in Kenya and many other LMICs are underdeveloped or entirely non-existent, positioning emergency departments (EDs) as the primary point of care for acute medical and surgical emergencies.6 As a result, access to care is heavily dependent on the distance and travel time required to reach an ED. The travel time to reach an ED affects patients’ decision to seek care7 and is often inversely associated with health outcomes.8 A previous study of emergency care access in sub-Saharan Africa estimated national accessibility in Kenya, assuming uniform service availability across all public hospitals.9 The accuracy of such accessibility models depends heavily on accurate facility classification. As emergency care capacity varies widely across Kenyan health facilities, there is a need for updated travel time estimates using a consistent and verified definition of public EDs.

Healthcare accessibility is multifaceted and encompasses issues related to availability (the types of services that exist), accessibility (physical barriers and travel impedance), accommodation (suitability to meet patients’ needs), affordability (cost) and acceptability (related to patients’ perceptions).10 Approaches to measure healthcare accessibility include provider-to-population ratios, Euclidean distance, cost-distance algorithms, gravity models, network analysis and self-reports of patients and healthcare workers.11 A geographic information system (GIS) is a software capable of capturing, storing, analysing and integrating different layers of data tied to a location.12 Advances in geospatial technologies and the availability of open-source GIS software tools have led to numerous studies about healthcare accessibility across different contexts.13,18

Given the heterogeneity of emergency care capacity across Kenya and limitations of prior regional models to account for this variation, there is a critical need for updated, high-resolution analyses of geographic access to verified public EDs. The study aimed to use a GIS, integrating population, road network, land cover and elevation data, and applying a cost-distance model to assess geographic accessibility and 1-hour and 2-hour travel times to public EDs in Kenya with verified available emergency care services.

Methods

Study site

Kenya is a lower-middle-income country in East Africa with a devolved system of governance established under the Constitution of Kenya (2010), which created 47 semiautonomous county governments responsible for key service delivery functions, including healthcare. A map of the 47 counties of Kenya is shown in figure 1. These counties are further subdivided into administrative units comprising subcounties and wards. Subcounty boundaries have been periodically restructured to reflect population changes and administrative needs over the years, but the wards, Kenya’s smallest administrative units, have remained stable since their initial delimitation.

Map of Kenya with county boundaries.

The 2023 Kenya Health Facility Census Report reported a total of 14 883 health facilities across the country, of which 47% are public, 46% private and 8% faith-based or non-governmental organisations (NGOs).19 Faith-based and NGO hospitals play a crucial role in rural communities by providing affordable and essential medical services.20

1-hour and 2-hour travel times

The ‘golden hour’ refers to the critical first hour after a traumatic injury, during which prompt medical intervention significantly improves the chances of survival and reduces morbidity.21 In their ‘Global Surgery 2030’ report, the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery emphasised the importance of bolstering access to life-saving surgical procedures in LMICs.22 One of their core indicators for access is that at least 80% of the population can reach a facility within 2 hours that can do caesarean delivery, laparotomy and treatment of open fracture (Bellwether procedures). The Commission described Bellwether procedures as a proxy metric for facilities capable of providing essential surgical services. Subsequently, multiple studies have examined country-level spatial access to hospitals performing essential surgeries.23,25 In this study, travel time parameters of 1 hour and 2 hours were chosen to align with the ‘golden hour’ for trauma care and the Lancet Commission’s target time to access a facility capable of Bellwether procedures.

Data

Facilities

In this study, a public ED was defined as any hospital, whether public, faith-based or operated by an NGO, that had an emergency care facility (such as an ED, casualty department, accident and emergency department or outpatient department), was open 24 hours a day and had a functioning operating theatre capable of performing Bellwether procedures (caesarean delivery, laparotomy, treatment of open fracture). The initial facility inventory was derived from the Ministry of Health’s Kenya National Health Facilities Census.19 The locations of public EDs were obtained from the Emergency Medicine Kenya Foundation, which collaborated with Google, the Ministry of Health and BP Systems Online to identify and validate these facilities under its nationwide initiative, Project 47.26 Geolocation verification was conducted using Google Earth. This facility list focuses on public-sector, faith-based and non-governmental facilities providing emergency care and does not encompass privately owned facilities that meet similar clinical standards.

Population

Population distribution was modelled using the WorldPop 2024 gridded population dataset, downloaded from Humanitarian Data Exchange.27 This raster dataset provides projected 2024 population counts at 100-metre resolution, generated using a random forest disaggregation approach. The population surface is shown in online supplemental figure 1. WorldPop combines national census data with a range of geospatial covariates, including land use, infrastructure and settlement data, to create its projections. As with all modelled population products, some uncertainty exists in sparsely settled or rapidly urbanising areas, and projections may not fully capture recent demographic shifts.28

Administrative boundaries

Geographic boundaries for Kenya’s counties, subcounties and wards were obtained from shapefiles hosted on the Humanitarian Data Exchange.29 This administrative hierarchy was used to calculate population coverage at multiple levels. Subcounty boundaries, however, can vary between government sources, as they are aligned with health management and service delivery units that evolve over time. Discrepancies in subcounty boundaries across government and health sector datasets can affect comparability and temporal consistency. Wards are officially recognised as the smallest administrative unit in Kenya.

Urban/rural areas

Counties are not classified as either urban or rural by the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics. To compare access in urban and rural areas, we used the 2020 observed Global Human Settlement Model (GHS-SMOD) raster dataset from the Copernicus Global Human Settlement Layer.30 This dataset integrates land cover detection of built areas, settlement imagery and population distribution to classify cells by urbanisation. Its relatively coarse resolution of 1 km may obscure settlement heterogeneity, leading to potential misclassification of settlement areas, particularly in peri-urban and peri-rural transition zones.

Roads

Roads were mapped using OpenStreetMap (OSM) road network data.31 This dataset contains road vectors classified by type (highways, primary/secondary roads, local roads, tracks, etc), contributed and updated by the OSM community. OSM datasets are crowdsourced and may have uneven completeness. Major highways and arterial roads are well-mapped in Kenya, but smaller rural roads or footpaths can be missing or misclassified. Additionally, road quality is variably documented, which could affect travel speed assumptions. Despite these limitations, OSM provides one of the most detailed and up-to-date open road maps available for Kenya.

Land cover

To account for off-road travel conditions, we incorporated the European Space Agency WorldCover 2020 land cover map at 10 m resolution.32 This globally consistent dataset was derived from Sentinel-1/2 satellite imagery and classifies land cover into 10 primary categories (eg, tree cover, grassland, cropland, built-up areas, water bodies, etc), with an overall thematic accuracy of about 75%. The land cover map, while high-resolution, carries classification errors typical of automated products—for instance, some mosaic landscapes or seasonal land cover may be mislabelled. The reported 75% accuracy reflects that certain land cover types (such as shrubland vs grassland) might be confused in some locations.33 In this study, land cover classes were used to modulate off-road travel speeds in areas where no roads exist. Online supplemental figure 2 illustrates land cover across Kenya.

Elevation

Elevation data were obtained from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) digital elevation model at 1-arcsecond (30 m) resolution.34 SRTM provides near-global coverage, approximately 80% of the Earth’s land surface, at high resolution.35 This elevation raster reflects slope and other topographic factors that can influence travel time. The 30 m digital elevation model captures broad topographic variations, but very small terrain features, such as local gullies or road embankments, may be below its resolution. In this study, it was assumed that these minor elevation errors have a negligible effect on travel-time results. Online supplemental figure 3 shows the SRTM digital elevation model for Kenya.

Travel speeds

Travel speeds were assigned to each land cover and road class based on published literature, with adjustments reflecting typical conditions in Kenya and sub-Saharan Africa. Table 1 summarises the baseline travel speed scenarios, with values assigned based on prior studies.36,38 These values were used to build a friction surface representing travel impedance across the landscape. For the sensitivity analysis, a ±20% variation was tested, consistent with approaches by Macharia et al39 and Ouma et al,9 to account for terrain uncertainty, seasonality and behaviour differences.

Analysis

The core travel time analysis was performed using AccessMod, an open-access tool designed by the WHO, which can be used to model healthcare accessibility. Travel time was estimated using a cost-distance (least-cost path) algorithm. The model propagates accumulated travel time across a raster-based friction surface, identifying the minimum-time path from each pixel to the nearest public ED. The friction surface incorporated spatial variation in travel speeds based on elevation, land cover and road network data, which were uploaded into AccessMod. Travel times were calculated using a combined walking and motorised transport model to reflect realistic travel behaviours in Kenya, an approach previously described by Ouma et al9 and Ray and Ebener.40 The travel time service areas were then overlaid on population density in Quantum Geographic Information System (QGIS) and zonal statistics were applied to calculate the proportion of residents within 1 and 2 hours of a public ED. In this study, zonal statistics were calculated for wards and then aggregated by county for reporting. To account for cross-county care-seeking and edge effects at administrative borders, separate zonal statistics were performed to include a 5 km buffer around each county boundary. Population coverage was then recalculated for each county to include public EDs and populations located within this buffer. This approach is consistent with boundary-buffer methods used in healthcare accessibility studies in Ghana and other LMIC settings.41,43

To compare access between urban and rural regions across Kenya, the Copernicus Global Human Settlement raster was reclassified by grouping urban centre, dense urban cluster, semidense urban and suburban/peri-urban pixels as ‘urban’, and rural, low-density rural and very low-density rural pixels as ‘rural’. The reclassified raster was subsequently converted into polygons to enable the calculation of population coverage within urban and rural areas.

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using R V.4.4.2.

Results

County-level analysis

Based on gridded population data from WorldPop, which projects Kenya’s total population at 55 966 780, there are approximately 4.5 public EDs per million inhabitants in Kenya. Figure 2 depicts the locations of all 250 public EDs and their surrounding 1-hour and 2-hour travel time catchment areas. The population proportions within 1-hour and 2-hour travel times are summarised in tables2 3. Overall, approximately 94% of the population were within 1 hour, and 98% were within 2 hours of a public ED. 31 out of 47 counties had >95% of their population within a 1-hour travel time, and 41 counties had >95% of their population within a 2-hour travel time to a public ED. Nine counties had <80% of their population residing within 1 hour: Narok (79.1%), Kitui (77.2%), Baringo (74.8%), Mandera (71.7%), West Pokot (70.2%), Garissa (67.2%), Turkana (59.3%), Wajir (53.3%) and Marsabit (52.3%). For access within 2 hours, population coverage still fell below the threshold of 80% in Turkana (75.8%) and Marsabit (72.9%).

A Wilcoxon signed-rank test, adjusted to account for potential ties, revealed a significant difference in the proportion of people within 1 and 2 hours of a public ED (p<0.001). Across all counties, expanding the travel-time threshold from 1 hour to 2 hours increased the median population coverage by 0.28% (IQR of 9.2 percentage points). A Spearman’s rank correlation did not show a significant association between the number of facilities in each county and the population proportion within 1 hour (ρ=0.237, p=0.109) and 2 hours (ρ=0.230, p=0.119). Areas designated as urban from the settlement data layer had population coverage of 97.8% within 1 hour and 99.5% within 2 hours of travel time to a public ED, compared with 81.6% and 94.4% in rural areas, respectively. Under the +20% travel speed sensitivity analysis, coverage for urban areas varied minimally (97.0%–98.2% in 1 hour and 98.9%–99.7% in 2 hours), whereas coverage in rural areas varied more widely (74.0%–86.6% in 1 hour and 91.5%–96.4% in 2 hours).

The complete geospatial visualisations and results of population coverage by ward, subcounty and county can be accessed on an interactive online dashboard (https://geoportal.emergencymedicinekenya.org/catalogue/#/dashboard/8), which will be periodically updated.

Discussion

Approximately 94% of the population in Kenya is within 1 hour, and 98% is within 2 hours of a public ED. However, county-level analysis reveals significant disparities, with accessibility as low as 52.3% within 1 hour and 72.9% within 2 hours in some regions. Marsabit and Turkana did not meet the target of at least 80% coverage within 2 hours, primarily due to sparse hospital distribution, poor road infrastructure and challenging terrain, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to enhance accessibility. The weak correlation between the number of facilities per county and the proportion of the population within 1 hour (ρ=0.237, p=0.109) and 2 hours (ρ=0.230, p=0.119) suggests that simply increasing the number of public EDs may not significantly improve access unless they are strategically placed in underserved areas.

This study presents the first-ever database of geographic accessibility to public EDs in Kenya, incorporating national and subnational analyses to highlight regional disparities and identify areas requiring targeted interventions. Existing literature applying GIS to measure healthcare access in Kenya includes travel time analysis to all public and private health facilities,37 inpatient neonatal care facilities,44 maternal care facilities45 and Nairobi’s emergency care facilities.46 A previous study of sub-Saharan Africa estimated that 92.9% of the Kenyan population in 2015 were within 120 min of travel time to a public hospital providing emergency care.39 However, a significant limitation of that study was the assumption that all public hospitals are equipped to provide emergency care. According to the 2023 Kenya National Health Facilities Census report, access to emergency services was described as ‘precarious’, noting only 5.8% of all facilities had an accident and emergency unit, and only 49% of facilities had access to an ambulance.19 By using a more precise definition of public EDs rather than assuming all public hospitals in Kenya offer emergency services, our study provides a more accurate assessment of geographical accessibility to emergency care in Kenya.

The value of geospatial analysis as a critical tool for assessing geographic accessibility to public EDs and optimising emergency care delivery is highlighted in this study, particularly in LMICs, where limited prehospital emergency medical services and reliance on inadequately equipped healthcare facilities exacerbate morbidity and mortality among patients. By mapping population distribution against the availability of public EDs, geospatial analysis provides crucial insights into service gaps, enabling policymakers to identify areas needing infrastructure investments or prehospital service enhancements. These insights emphasise the importance of strategically placing public EDs that are adequately resourced with essential equipment, trained personnel and standardised emergency care protocols to provide timely and effective emergency care. Prehospital service enhancements can include strengthening community-based first responder programmes, which have been shown to improve response times and patient outcomes in LMICs.47,49 This data-driven approach offers a cost-effective means of strengthening emergency healthcare systems in LMICs, ensuring efficient resource allocation to improve patient outcomes and reduce preventable mortality.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, the study had several limitations. First, it focused on public EDs and excluded private facilities. There were also slight temporal discrepancies in the sources of data of the list of public EDs (2023), population numbers (2024), road conditions (2023) and land cover data (2021). Elevation data were assumed to be relatively stable over time. Another limitation was incomplete road network data from OSM for rural areas, which affected the accuracy of travel time analysis in those regions.

Travel time to reach emergency care services is the second type of delay within the three-delay framework, originally described by Thaddeus and Maine: (1) delay to seek care, (2) delay to reach care and (3) delay to receive appropriate care.50 While it was originally proposed in the context of maternal mortality, the three-delay framework has since been applied across different emergency care settings.51,53 This study provides quantitative insights into the ‘second delay’ to emergency care in Kenya, but studies in other contexts have shown that patients may choose to bypass the nearest facility based on factors related to cost or perceived quality of care.54

Additionally, while our model provided a comprehensive accessibility assessment, it did not incorporate real-time factors such as traffic congestion, seasonal road conditions or ambulance availability. The assumption of uniform transport accessibility across regions also did not fully account for variations in vehicle ownership and the affordability of transport services.

To account for individuals who may choose to cross county borders to seek emergency care, we separately calculated population coverage for the aggregated populations and facilities within a 5 km radius of county boundaries. However, this approach may have led to population overcounting and may not accurately reflect the average distance people are willing to travel for emergency services. Future studies could incorporate qualitative methods to better capture realistic care-seeking behaviours.

Conclusions

In summary, while 94% of the population in Kenya is within 1 hour and 98% within 2 hours of a public ED, substantial geographic disparities remain due to uneven hospital distribution, poor road infrastructure and challenging terrain, highlighting critical gaps in emergency care accessibility. By mapping population distribution against the availability of public EDs in Kenya, geospatial analysis provides crucial insights into emergency care access gaps, guiding policymakers in identifying areas that require infrastructure investments or prehospital service enhancements. Ensuring that public EDs are well-equipped and adequately staffed is equally essential to translating geographic accessibility into meaningful health outcomes. Future studies should integrate dynamic modelling techniques and real-time travel factors to refine accessibility estimates, while on-the-ground validation of service availability and the inclusion of local transportation variables could further enhance the accuracy of emergency care accessibility assessments.
