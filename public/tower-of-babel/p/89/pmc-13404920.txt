Perception of having an aesthetic procedure on social media and psychological symptoms: serial mediation by social media addiction and social appearance anxiety
Authors: Cansu Ağralı, Ahmet Kara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to examine the serial multiple mediating effect of social media addiction and social appearance anxiety on the relationship between the perception of having an aesthetic procedure on social media and psychological symptoms.MethodsA total of 267 Turkish female participants in emerging adulthood, aged between 18 and 25 years [AgeMean = 20.41, AgeSd = 1.66], voluntarily took part in the research. The data were collected using the Social Media Addiction Scale, Scale of Aesthetic Procedure Perceptions on Social Media, Social Appearance Anxiety Scale, and Brief Symptom Inventory. The mediating effect was examined using structural equation modeling (SEM) with the bootstrap approach to test whether mediation was present.ResultsSEM findings revealed that social media addiction and social appearance anxiety significantly explained the relationship between the perception of having an aesthetic procedure on social media and psychological symptoms, and acted as mediators in this relationship.ConclusionIn light of these findings, it is recommended that interventions aimed at reducing psychological symptoms in young women include controlling social media usage, enhancing digital awareness, and implementing psychoeducation programmes that strengthen body image.Keywords: Aesthetic procedure perceptions on social media, Social media addiction, Social appearance anxiety, Psychological symptoms, Emerging adulthood

Introduction

Social media platforms today are among the important tools that have reshaped the ways in which individuals connect and communicate, influencing their social relationships and becoming an integral part of daily life [1]. Although the widespread use of social media has made daily life easier, it has also brought with it various disadvantages. Particularly on platforms where visual content is heavily shared and dominant, physical appearance, body image and aesthetic perception come to the fore; users are frequently exposed to idealised representations of bodies and faces [2]. This situation may play a decisive role in body dissatisfaction and appearance-focused anxieties by increasing individuals’ tendency to compare themselves with others [2, 3]. A study highlights that increased social media usage may heighten social comparison, which could be one of the key predictors explaining the negative impact on individual well-being and body satisfaction [3]. Beyond increasing body dissatisfaction, social media may also shape how individuals perceive aesthetic procedures by repeatedly presenting them as common, socially approved, desirable, and accessible practices [4]. As platforms such as Instagram and Snapchat continue to influence social norms and beauty standards, understanding their impact on body image dissatisfaction and the acceptance of cosmetic procedures is becoming increasingly important [5]. These appearance-focused dynamics can be understood through established frameworks, including Social Comparison Theory, Objectification Theory, Self-Discrepancy Theory, and sociocultural models of body image disturbance.

In the present study, the focal predictor is conceptualized as the perception of aesthetic procedures on social media, which refers to the extent to which individuals perceive aesthetic procedures portrayed on social media as common, socially approved, socially valued, and readily accessible practices. This construct does not refer merely to exposure to aesthetic content or to an individual’s intention to undergo an aesthetic procedure. Rather, it captures the perceived normalization and social legitimization of aesthetic procedures within social media environments. Recent studies indicate that social media platforms increase the visibility of cosmetic procedures and may shape individuals’ attitudes toward aesthetic interventions by presenting them as increasingly ordinary, socially acceptable, and attainable practices [5–7].

This construct is conceptually distinct from related body image variables. Body dissatisfaction refers to negative evaluations of one’s own body or appearance [8]. Appearance comparison refers to the tendency to evaluate one’s appearance in relation to others, particularly idealized or edited images encountered on social media [9]. Internalization of beauty ideals refers to adopting culturally promoted appearance standards as personal standards for self-evaluation [10]. Cosmetic surgery acceptance refers to favorable attitudes toward undergoing cosmetic procedures [11]. In contrast, the perception of aesthetic procedures on social media refers to individuals’ beliefs about the social meaning, acceptability, and legitimacy of aesthetic procedures as represented in online environments.

The theoretical rationale for this construct is grounded in several complementary theoretical frameworks. Social Comparison Theory argues that individuals evaluate themselves by comparing their attributes with those of others, especially when objective standards are unclear [12]. In social media contexts, upward appearance comparisons are especially relevant because users are frequently exposed to idealized, edited, and appearance-focused images [13]. Objectification Theory suggests that repeated sociocultural emphasis on physical appearance may lead individuals to view their bodies from an observer’s perspective, thereby increasing body surveillance, body shame, and appearance-related anxiety [14]. Recent evidence also supports the role of self-objectification in the relationship between social media use and body image disturbance [8]. Self-Discrepancy Theory proposes that psychological distress may emerge when individuals perceive a gap between their actual appearance and internalized ideal appearance standards [15]. This mechanism is particularly relevant in visually oriented social media environments, where idealized beauty standards may intensify perceived discrepancies between the actual and ideal self [10]. Sociocultural perspectives on body image suggest that media-based appearance ideals, peer influences, and cultural beauty norms contribute to body image concerns through internalization and appearance comparison processes [5, 9]. Recent studies similarly show that social media ideals, influencer content, and selfie-related activities may contribute to body dissatisfaction, social appearance anxiety, and cosmetic surgery consideration [5, 6, 9]. Taken together, these theoretical perspectives suggest that perceiving aesthetic procedures on social media as normalized, desirable, and socially endorsed may reinforce appearance-based self-evaluation, increase concerns about physical appearance, and heighten vulnerability to psychological symptoms. From a broader social-psychological perspective, mediated social information may shape affective responses and self-perceptions by influencing whether individuals evaluate themselves through more humanizing or appearance-based lenses. In appearance-focused social media environments, idealized aesthetic content may encourage individuals to define their self-worth primarily in terms of externally observable appearance attributes rather than more holistic and person-centered characteristics [16, 17].

Furthermore, when examining the psychological effects of social media use, it is observed that excessive use can develop into addiction-like behaviour patterns over time [18]. Social media addiction is characterized by excessive and compulsive patterns of social media use that are associated with impairments in daily functioning and lower levels of overall well-being. This condition encompasses more than just spending excessive time online and may include compulsively checking for updates, experiencing anxiety when offline, and experiencing negative effects on real-life relationships and responsibilities [19]. Consequently, social media addiction has been associated with lower psychological well-being and with elevated levels of anxiety, stress, and depressive symptoms [20].

However, the appearance-focused effects of social media use bring the concept of social appearance anxiety to the fore. Social appearance anxiety is a type of anxiety defined as the fear of being negatively evaluated or rejected by others because of one’s physical appearance [21]. The idealised body and face images presented on social media can increase individuals’ sensitivity about their appearance and reinforce social appearance anxiety [22]. Higher levels of social appearance anxiety have been associated with poorer psychological health and elevated symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress.

Based on these theoretical perspectives, the perception of aesthetic procedures on social media may be associated with psychological symptoms through two sequential mechanisms. First, perceiving aesthetic procedures as normalized, desirable, and socially approved may increase engagement with appearance-focused social media content and strengthen problematic or addiction-like patterns of social media use. Second, increased engagement with such content may intensify social appearance anxiety by heightening self-monitoring, upward social comparison, and concerns about negative evaluation. Elevated social appearance anxiety may, in turn, contribute to greater psychological symptoms, including anxiety, depression, and stress. Therefore, the present study examines social media addiction and social appearance anxiety as mediating mechanisms linking the perception of aesthetic procedures on social media to psychological symptoms.

Although the proposed serial mediation model is theoretically grounded, it should be interpreted as one plausible pattern of association rather than evidence of a definitive causal sequence, given the cross-sectional design. The study therefore contributes by examining perceptions of aesthetic procedures on social media as a conceptually distinct construct within this theoretically informed model.

Method

Participant and procedure

This research was conducted in accordance with the ethical approval obtained from the Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee of [Omitted] University. Following ethical approval, participants were recruited through announcements shared on social media platforms. The recruitment strategy was selected because the study focused on perceptions of aesthetic procedures within social media environments and therefore required participants who were active social media users. The announcement included brief information about the purpose of the study, eligibility criteria, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time. Individuals who agreed to participate accessed an online survey link created via Google Forms. Informed consent was obtained electronically before participants completed the questionnaires. Because participation was voluntary and recruitment was conducted through social media announcements, the sample is not intended to be representative of all Turkish women in emerging adulthood.

The study sample consisted of 267 Turkish female participants in emerging adulthood, aged between 18 and 25 years [AgeMean = 20.41, AgeSd = 1.66], The sample was restricted to women in emerging adulthood because this developmental period is characterized by heightened identity exploration, appearance-related concerns, and intensive social media engagement. In addition, women are frequently reported to be more vulnerable to appearance-related pressures and cosmetic procedure-related social media content. Participation was voluntary, and no identifying information was collected. Participants’ student status was not assessed.

Social Media Addiction Scale (SMAS)

SMAS was developed by Tutgun-Ünal and Deniz (2015), and its psychometric properties have been evaluated. SMAS consists of 41 items and four dimensions (occupation, mood modification, relapse, and conflict). In exploratory factor analysis, SMAS was found to have a total explained variance of 59% [23]. SMAS has been determined to have a test-retest reliability value of 0.84. The Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficient for SMAS was calculated as 0.967 [23].

Scale of Aesthetic Procedure Perceptions on Social Media (SAPPS)

SAPPS was developed by Türk and Bayrakcı and has undergone psychometric testing [24]. SAPPS consists of 16 items and a four-dimensional structure (needs, publicity, accessibility, visibility). The exploratory factor analysis conducted for SAPPS revealed that factor loadings ranged from 0.558 to 0.897 [24]. The Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficient for SAPPS was found to be 0.93 [25].

Social Appearance Anxiety Scale (SAAS)

SAAS was developed by Hart et al. [26]. It was adapted into Turkish, and its psychometric properties were examined by Doğan [27]. SAAS has 16 items and is a one-dimensional scale. In this study, confirmatory factor analysis was conducted for the SAAS, which revealed that all t-values were significant, standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.35 to 0.91, and the fit indices (χ²/df = 4.19, NFI = 0.90, IFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.91, and CFI = 0.92) were at an acceptable level. Given the unidimensional structure of the Social Appearance Anxiety Scale (SAAS) in both its original form and the confirmatory factor analysis conducted in this study, a parceling method based on item-total correlations was used, yielding two parcels [28]. In the exploratory factor analysis of SAAS, the total variance explained was calculated as 53.4%. In confirmatory factor analysis, SAAS’s goodness of fit indices (RMSEA = 0.051, NFI = 0.98, CFI = 0.99, IFI = 0.99, RFI = 0.98, GFI = 0.93) were found to be consistent with the data [27]. The Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient for SAAS was observed to be 0.93 [27].

Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)

The BSI was developed by Derogatis and adapted into Turkish, with psychometric analyses conducted by Şahin and Durak [28, 29]. The BSI consists of 53 items and five dimensions (anxiety, depression, negative self-concept, somatization, hostility). The total variance explained by the anxiety subscale was 30%, the depression subscale 3%, the negative self-concept subscale 2.8%, the somatization subscale 2.2%, and the hostility subscale 2% [29]. Furthermore, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients were determined as 0.87 for anxiety, 0.88 for depression, 0.87 for negative self-concept, 0.75 for somatization, and 0.76 for hostility [29].

Data analysis

The study first performed preliminary analyses covering normality, correlation, Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient, and multicollinearity assumptions [30–32]. The serial multiple mediation effect of social media addiction and social appearance anxiety on the relationship between the perception of having an aesthetic procedure on social media and psychological symptoms was examined within the framework of the two-stage structural equation modelling approach proposed by Anderson and Gerbing [33]. In this regard, the measurement model was tested in the first stage; after the validity of the measurement model was confirmed, the structural model involving serial multiple mediation was analyzed in the second stage. The validity of the models was assessed based on various fit indices (χ²/df, RMSEA, CFI, TLI, RFI, NFI, and IFI) [34, 35]. Furthermore, t-values, standardized path coefficients, and factor loadings were considered during the model evaluation process [31].

Within the framework of serial multiple mediation models, comparative fit indices were utilized to determine which of the partial and full mediation models demonstrated superior fit. In this context, the model with lower (AIC and ECVI) values was accepted as the most appropriate model [35, 36]. To determine the statistical significance of the serial multiple mediation effect of social media addiction and social appearance anxiety on the relationship between the perception of having an aesthetic procedure on social media and psychological symptoms, a bootstrap analysis involving 1,000 resamples was conducted, and lower and upper confidence intervals were calculated within the scope of this analysis. The fact that the obtained confidence intervals did not include the zero value indicates that the aforementioned serial multiple mediation effect is significant [37].

Two-stage structural equation modeling

The aim of this study is to examine the serial multiple mediating effect of social media addiction and social appearance anxiety on the relationship between the perception of having an aesthetic procedure on social media and psychological symptoms. Within this scope, the research data were assessed using a two-stage structural equation modelling method. In the first stage, the validity of the measurement model was tested. After it was determined that the model showed sufficient fit with the data, the structural model was analyzed in the second stage [33].

First stage: measurement model

In the first stage of the research, the measurement model was tested. The analysis results indicate that the model fit indices are at acceptable levels (χ²/df = 2.75, NFI = 0.93, RFI = 0.92, IFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.96, and RMSEA = 0.081) [34, 35]. Furthermore, it was determined that the standardized factor loadings of the measurement model ranged between 0.66 and 0.97, and that the t-values for all factor loadings were statistically significant. Based on these findings, it was concluded that the measurement model demonstrated adequate fit with the data and was valid.

Discussion

The study also examined the mediating roles of social media addiction and social appearance anxiety in the effect of perceptions regarding cosmetic procedures on psychological symptoms. The results revealed that social appearance anxiety and social media addiction act as mediating variables in the relationship between perceptions of cosmetic procedures and psychological symptoms.

According to research findings, social media addiction acts as a mediating variable in the relationship between the perception of undergoing aesthetic procedures and psychological symptoms. In a study conducted by Gürkan and Çakmak, it was determined that the amount of time spent on social media is a key predictor of perceptions of aesthetic interventions. The research reveals a significant relationship between social media addiction, the desire for social approval, aesthetic perception, and the desire to undergo these procedures. The findings suggest that greater digital exposure may be associated with increased body image concerns, which, in turn, may be related to lower psychological well-being and stronger aesthetic preferences [39]. Similarly, the study by Merino et al. suggests that, in the context of rapidly advancing digitalization, platforms such as Instagram and Facebook may be associated with body image concerns, dissatisfaction, and psychological distress through repeated exposure to idealized images and social comparison processes [40].

In a study examining social media use, attitudes toward cosmetic surgery, and psychosocial variables such as self-esteem, body image, depression, and quality of life among social media users and individuals who had undergone cosmetic procedures, higher levels of social media addiction were found to be associated with a greater desire for cosmetic surgery and higher levels of depression. The study also reported that individuals who had undergone cosmetic procedures showed lower levels of depression and higher levels of self-esteem and body satisfaction, along with more positive attitudes toward cosmetic surgery and a stronger association between body image and quality of life [41]. Therefore, the present findings are consistent with prior research indicating that problematic social media use is associated with psychological symptoms and appearance-related evaluations in the context of cosmetic procedures. Psychological symptoms were assessed using the Brief Symptom Inventory as a global indicator of general psychological distress. Accordingly, the present findings pertain to overall symptom severity rather than to specific symptom dimensions. The extent to which appearance-related concerns are differentially associated with particular symptom clusters, such as somatization, remains an important question for future research.

The study determined that social appearance anxiety is a significant mediating variable in the relationship between the perception of having undergone cosmetic procedures and psychological symptoms. In the study conducted by Özer and colleagues, positive and significant relationships were found between social appearance anxiety and social media addiction, social appearance anxiety and the perception of having undergone cosmetic procedures, and social media addiction and the perception of having undergone cosmetic procedures. This finding indicates that as social appearance anxiety and social media addiction increase in individuals, the perception of undergoing aesthetic procedures also increases [42]. Another study also found positive and significant correlations between social appearance anxiety and perceptions of cosmetic procedures on social media; it was also found that the frequency of image editing software use significantly predicted perceptions of popularity and cosmetic procedures [25]. The study conducted by Demir et al. found a strong positive correlation between women’s desire to be liked, social appearance anxiety, and acceptance of cosmetic surgery. The findings indicate that acceptance of cosmetic surgery is influenced by psychosocial factors such as the desire to be liked and social appearance anxiety [43]. The literature indicates that social appearance anxiety plays a significant role in explaining the relationship between the perception of having undergone aesthetic procedures and psychological symptoms. This suggests that the effect of individuals’ perception of having undergone cosmetic procedures on psychological symptoms is not direct, but rather emerges indirectly, largely through social appearance anxiety. Therefore, social appearance anxiety may represent a potentially relevant psychological process and may have tentative implications for intervention targets, although no intervention effects or clinical outcomes were directly examined in the present study.

The most noteworthy finding of the study is that social media addiction and social appearance anxiety were found to have a significant sequential multiple mediation effect. These findings can also be interpreted within the broader context of visually mediated self-perception. The literature on “Snapchat dysmorphia” and “Zoom dysmorphia” suggests that digital environments, including filters, edited images, selfies, and video-conferencing platforms, may alter how individuals perceive and evaluate their appearance. Rice et al. (2021) showed that increased video-conferencing during the COVID-19 pandemic was linked to greater appearance-related concerns and interest in cosmetic procedures [44]. Similarly, Fardouly and Vartanian (2016) emphasized that social media use is associated with body image concerns, partly through appearance comparison processes [45]. In this context, aesthetic procedure-related content on social media may be understood not only as a source of exposure, but also as part of a broader digital environment that shapes self-perception, appearance monitoring, and cosmetic procedure-related evaluations. This result indicates that the effect of the perception of having undergone aesthetic procedures on social media on psychological symptoms occurs through a sequential mechanism. Accordingly, perceiving aesthetic procedures on social media as normative, socially approved, and accessible may be associated with more intensive engagement with appearance-focused social media content and more problematic patterns of social media use.

Engagement with appearance-focused social media content may be accompanied by heightened appearance monitoring, greater concern about others’ evaluations, and higher levels of social appearance anxiety, which may in turn be associated with elevated psychological symptoms. This sequential mediation pattern suggests that problematic social media use and social appearance anxiety may function as interrelated psychological processes in the association between perceptions of aesthetic procedures on social media and psychological symptoms. However, due to the cross-sectional design, this sequence should not be interpreted as establishing temporal or causal ordering. Alternative explanations are also possible. For example, individuals with higher social appearance anxiety may engage more frequently with appearance-focused social media content, develop more favorable perceptions of aesthetic procedures, and report higher psychological symptoms. Similarly, pre-existing psychological distress may be associated with both problematic social media use and greater sensitivity to appearance-related content. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to test these alternative temporal models.

Although no studies have been found in the literature that address these parameters simultaneously, social media addiction has been found to play a mediating role in the relationship between the perception of undergoing cosmetic procedures and psychological symptoms [39–41]; Similarly, studies have shown that social appearance anxiety is a significant mediating variable in the relationship between the perception of having undergone cosmetic procedures and psychological symptoms [25, 42, 43]. Therefore, the findings suggest that perceptions of aesthetic procedures in social media environments may be associated with psychological symptoms through interrelated processes involving problematic social media use and social appearance anxiety. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as indicating that favorable perceptions of aesthetic procedures are inherently pathological. Rather, the results point to the relevance of social media-driven appearance pressures in understanding psychological vulnerability, while also recognizing that aesthetic procedure-related decisions may reflect autonomous, informed, and personally meaningful choices. Consistent with previous research, some individuals may also report improved body satisfaction, self-esteem, and psychological well-being following aesthetic procedures.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study examined a theoretically informed model in which perceptions of aesthetic procedures on social media, problematic social media use, social appearance anxiety, and psychological symptoms were statistically associated among Turkish women in emerging adulthood. The findings suggest that problematic social media use and social appearance anxiety may help explain the association between perceptions of aesthetic procedures on social media and psychological symptoms; however, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of a confirmed sequential psychological mechanism.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken to indicate that favorable perceptions of aesthetic procedures are inherently maladaptive or that decisions regarding aesthetic procedures are necessarily driven by social media-related pressures. Rather, the results highlight one possible pathway through which social media-based appearance norms may be associated with psychological vulnerability, while recognizing that aesthetic procedure-related decisions may also reflect autonomous, informed, and personally meaningful choices.

In light of these findings, the results may have tentative implications for preventive and psychoeducational efforts aimed at promoting critical media literacy, healthier patterns of social media use, and more adaptive body image processes among young women. Interventions targeting social appearance anxiety, including cognitive-behavioural approaches, as well as educational initiatives that encourage critical evaluation of aesthetic content on social media, may also be beneficial.

Limitations

One of the main limitations of this study is that certain potential control variables were not included in the analyses. Variables such as age, time spent on social media, platform type, prior cosmetic procedures, body mass index, socioeconomic status, relationship status, baseline mental health status, and baseline body dissatisfaction may influence the observed associations. In addition, the sample was restricted to Turkish women aged 18 to 25 years, which limits the generalizability of the findings to men, older adults, adolescents, and individuals from different cultural contexts. Since participants were recruited through social media platforms, the sample may also be subject to self-selection bias and may overrepresent frequent social media users or individuals who are already interested in appearance-related content and aesthetic procedures. In addition, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding temporal ordering, causal direction, and the psychological processes implied by the serial mediation model.

Another limitation is the reliance on self-report measures. Self-report data may be influenced by recall bias, social desirability, and common method variance, which may contribute to shared method effects and artificially strengthen the observed associations among the study variables. Additionally, the fact that social media “addiction” was assessed using a self-report scale may reflect the individual’s perceived compulsive use rather than a clinical disorder. This may have caused the relationships between variables to appear stronger than they actually are. The study also did not include behavioral social media data, such as objectively recorded time spent on social media, frequency of exposure to aesthetic content, or engagement with influencer and cosmetic procedure-related posts. In addition, the study did not include direct measures of participants’ exposure to aesthetic procedure-related content on social media.

Future studies should include these control variables in the model to conduct more comprehensive assessments. They should also use more diverse samples, clinical evaluations, longitudinal or experimental designs, and behavioral measures in addition to self-report measures.
