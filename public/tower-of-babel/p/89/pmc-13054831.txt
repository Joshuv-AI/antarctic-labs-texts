Generation of Nano‐Antibodies Targeting the SNMP1 Membrane Receptor of Spodoptera littoralis Using Extracellular Vesicles
Authors: Alexis Dogliani, Laura Fradale, Céline Valeille, Christian Cambillau, Emmanuelle Jacquin‐Joly, Alain Roussel, Josiane Kassis‐Sahyoun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHandling membrane proteins (MPs) in their native environment is crucial for ensuring proper folding and stability. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) offer a superior alternative to detergents and artificial lipid environments. Expressing recombinant MPs on the surface of EVs allows for their display in a native context, making them ideal for llama immunization to generate nano‐antibodies (Nabs) targeting MPs. In this study, we focused on the sensory neuron membrane protein 1 (SNMP1) from Spodoptera littoralis, a key player in transferring hydrophobic pheromones from pheromone‐binding proteins to olfactory receptors, thereby triggering behavioral responses in moths. Targeting this protein with Nabs could provide a potential biocontrol strategy by limiting moth reproduction. Llama immunization was carried out using EVs displaying SNMP1 on their surface. A multi‐layer approach was then employed, incorporating an anchor protein co‐displayed with the target protein to specifically expose the EVs of interest for biopanning through phage display and selection via ELISA. Nabs against SNMP1 were successfully isolated, and their interactions with the membrane protein were characterized using bio‐layer interferometry (BLI) with the same multi‐layer approach. Furthermore, epitope binning by BLI aligned with predictions made by AlphaFold3.Keywords: bio‐layer interferometry, biopanning, ELISA, extracellular vesicles, membrane proteins, nano‐antibodies, SNMP1

Introduction

Membrane proteins (MPs) are integral to numerous biological processes, including cell signaling, molecular transport, and immune recognition. Due to their functional importance and localization at the cell surface, they are key targets for therapeutic and diagnostic development in medicine. However, studying and manipulating MPs pose significant technical challenges. Their inherent hydrophobicity and dependence on the lipid environment to maintain their native conformation often complicate their isolation and characterization (Gulezian et al. 2021). To address these issues, researchers typically generate truncated constructs by removing hydrophobic domains, or they use detergents to extract proteins from membranes and stabilize them in aqueous buffers. Unfortunately, such methods can alter the conformation and function of MPs (Boulos et al. 2023).

Since their discovery, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have garnered significant attention from researchers. These small particles, including exosomes and ectosomes, are naturally produced by nearly all living cells (Yáñez‐Mó et al. 2015). EVs participate in a wide range of biological functions, including cell communication, immune recruitment, and the transport of nucleic acids and proteins (Raposo and Stahl 2023). EVs have emerged as promising tools in cell therapy, drug delivery, and disease diagnostics. In a previous study, we demonstrated that EVs could be used to display native recombinant membrane proteins on their surface, making them ideal candidates for antigen presentation and antibody generation (Delauzun et al. 2020).

Nano‐antibodies (Nabs), the variable domains of heavy‐chain antibodies found in camelids (Hamers‐Casterman et al. 1993), have proven to be powerful tools for detecting and interacting with biomolecular targets (Ingram et al. 2018). Their small size (∼15 kDa), remarkable stability, and ability to access epitopes often inaccessible to conventional antibodies make them especially suited for targeting challenging proteins, such as MPs (Rasmussen et al. 2011). Nabs generation involves multiple steps, including immunization, biopanning via phage display, and selection by ELISA (Desmyter et al. 2015; Pardon et al. 2014). However, generating Nabs against MPs remains challenging due to difficulties in obtaining these proteins in their native conformation.

In this study, we combined EV engineering and Nab generation to target the Sensory Neuron Membrane Protein 1 (SNMP1) of Spodoptera littoralis, a noctuid moth species within the Lepidoptera insect order. SNMP1 is an insect‐specific membrane receptor with two transmembrane α‐helices, and it belongs to a larger family of transmembrane receptors and transporters related to vertebrate protein CD36 involved in fatty acid transport (Rogers et al. 1997). Moth SNMP1 is located in the dendritic membranes of pheromone‐sensitive olfactory neurons housed in hair‐like structures called sensilla carried by the insect antennae. SNMP1 is believed to play a critical role in sex pheromone detection. In moths, sex pheromone is a key odorant signal for successful reproduction and species survival as it is emitted by one sex to attract the opposite sex at a distance for mating. Upon contact with the antennae, pheromone molecules enter the sensillum lymph via cuticular pores and are transported by pheromone‐binding proteins (PBPs) to reach the pheromone receptor complex located in the dendritic membrane of the sensory neurons (Leal 2013). SNMP1 is thought to facilitate the transfer of lipophilic pheromones from PBPs to the pheromone receptor complex (Benton et al. 2007; Gomez‐Diaz et al. 2016; Jin et al. 2008), thereby triggering pheromone‐induced behavior. Targeting SNMP1 with Nabs could confirm its role in pheromone detection and potentially inhibit its activity, offering a novel approach for controlling pest moth populations via pheromone communication disruption.

Here, we present a novel method using EVs to generate Nabs targeting MPs. We demonstrate that EVs presenting the SNMP1 protein in its native lipid environment were able to elicit a specific immune response in llamas. We then developed a multi‐layered approach using an anchor protein to immobilize EVs displaying SNMP1, which facilitated the biopanning and selection of Nabs. Finally, employing this approach, we successfully determined the kinetic parameters of the Nabs using bio‐layer interferometry (BLI). This led to the generation of highly specific Nabs binding to SNMP1 with nanomolar affinity. Our approach overcomes many limitations of traditional methods and opens new possibilities for developing Nabs against a wide array of MP targets.

Materials and Methods

Cloning

Plasmid

Following the method described by Delauzun et al. (2020), the pCAGGS‐mCherry vector (Addgene plasmid #41583) (Hitoshi et al. 1991) was used to express recombinant proteins on the surface of EVs. This vector, which carries ampicillin resistance, was digested with the restriction enzymes BglII (NEB #R0144S) and EcoRI (NEB #R0101S) for 1 h at 37°C. The linearized vector was then separated from the mCherry insert using 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis, and subsequently purified using the Nucleospin Gel and PCR Clean‐Up Kit (Macherey‐Nagel #740609.250) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol.

Genes

The genes encoding the proteins to be displayed on EVs were synthesized by Twist Bioscience. The gene encoding SNMP1 (SL3009302‐RA) was retrieved from the S. littoralis genome (accession number JALJOW000000000, annotation OGS3.0) (Meslin et al. 2022) and was designed with an N‐terminal Kozak sequence and a C‐terminal 6x His tag, while the ACE2 gene (Uniprot: A0A7I2V2E9) included an N‐terminal Kozak sequence and an N‐terminal ALFA tag (SRLEEELRRRLTE) (Götzke et al. 2019). Both genes were flanked by 5’ and 3’ hybridization sequences (5’‐CATTTTGGCAAAGAATTGCCACC‐3’ and 5’‐GATCTCGATCTTTTT‐3’, respectively) to facilitate in‐fusion cloning into the linearized pCAGGS vector.

Additionally, the genes encoding Nabs, each with a C‐terminal 6x His tag, were synthesized and cloned into the pET24+ vector between the BamHI and HindIII restriction sites by Twist Bioscience. The pET24+ vector carried kanamycin resistance.

Ligation Reaction

Gene fragments were inserted into the linearized pCAGGS vector using the In‐Fusion Snap Assembly Kit (Takara #638947). The ligation product was then transformed into Escherichia coli NEB5α (NEB #C2987) competent cells. Colonies selected on ampicillin plates were screened for the presence of the insert by PCR, using the primers pCAGGS‐Forward (5’‐GGTTCGGCTTCTGGCGTGTGACC‐3’) and pCAGGS‐Reverse (5’‐CCCATATGTCCTTCCGAGTG‐3’). Plasmids from positive clones were isolated using a miniprep procedure and sequenced by Eurofins Genomics, utilizing the same primers for confirmation.

Cell Culture and Transfection

HEK293‐EBNA cells (RRID: CVCL_6974) (Tom et al. 2008) were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco #31966‐021) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Dutscher #500105M1M) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Twenty‐four hours before transfection, the cells were rinsed once with DPBS (ThermoFisher #14040133) and then dislodged using a 0.05% trypsin‐EDTA solution (ThermoFisher #25300104) for 5 min at 37°C. After dislodging, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 500× g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of DMEM + FBS medium. The cell count was determined using an automated cell counter (Luna‐II automated cell counter, logos biosystems).

To optimize the expression and production of EVs, several improvements were made to the original protocol described by Delauzun et al. (2020). A total of 18 × 106 cells were seeded into T175 cm2 flasks (Dutscher #05540), resulting in 60%–70% confluence the following day, which is considered ideal for transfection. For the transfection of mammalian cells, PEI MAX (Polysciences #24765‐1) was used as a cost‐effective alternative to Lipofectamine 2000. This choice not only reduced costs but also enabled scaling up production by facilitating the transfection of larger culture volumes. Both DNA and PEI MAX were diluted separately in OptiMEM medium (ThermoFisher #51985034). Next, 1.5 µg/mL of DNA was mixed with 4.5 µg/mL of PEI MAX (a 3:1 weight ratio of PEI to DNA) and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Meanwhile, the DMEM + FBS medium was replaced with serum‐free Freestyle 293 medium (ThermoFisher #12338026). The DNA‐PEI MAX mixture was then gently added to the cells, making up 10% (v/v) of the total culture volume.

Collection of EVs

After 120 h of incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, the culture supernatant was collected, and EVs were harvested by differential centrifugation. The centrifugation was carried out in three stages at 4°C: first at 500 xg for 10 minutes to remove residual cells, followed by 3000 xg for 10 minutes to eliminate larger cellular debris, and finally at 10,000× g for 30 min to clear any remaining large contaminants. The supernatant was retained after each centrifugation step (Théry et al. 2006).

For the final step, the supernatant was ultracentrifuged (Beckman Optima XE‐90 Ultracentrifuge) at 110,000× g for 1 h and 30 min at 4°C to pellet the EVs. This ultracentrifugation process was also optimized from the protocol by Delauzun et al. (2020), reducing the run time from two 3‐h cycles to a single 1.5‐h cycle. This modification yielded comparable results in terms of both the quantity and quality of isolated EVs, significantly shortening the processing time while minimizing the risk of material loss. The EV pellet was then gently resuspended in 200 µL of PBS per T175 cm2 flask. The production yield was typically assessed by measuring absorbance at 280 nm (A280) using a Nanodrop (ThermoScientific).

Following each production cycle, EVs expressing the target protein were characterized using a variety of techniques to assess sample homogeneity in size. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and negative staining electron microscopy were used in combination, as they offer complementary insights. A western blot was also routinely performed to confirm the presence of the target protein on the EVs.

DLS

EV samples were characterized by DLS following ultracentrifugation. A 60 µL aliquot of the sample was used to assess the homogeneity and mean particle size of the EVs using a Zetasizer NanoZS (Malvern Instruments). The program collected three sets of data, each consisting of 15 individual measurements.

Negative Staining Electron Microscopy

To visualize EVs by electron microscopy, a carbon‐coated grid (Ted Pella Formvar/Carbon, 300 mesh, Cu #01813‐F) was glow discharged using a PELCO easiGlow Glow Discharge Cleaning System (Ted Pella Inc., USA) at 15 mA for 30 s. After glow discharge, 3.5 µL of the EV sample (A280 = 0.2) was applied to the grid and allowed to adsorb for 1 min. Excess liquid was carefully blotted off with Whatman filter paper. The grid was then quickly washed three times with distilled water, each time removing excess liquid with Whatman paper.

Next, the grid was negatively stained with 1% (v/v) uranyl acetate for 1 min, followed by gentle blotting to remove excess stain. The grids were then air‐dried for at least 20 min before imaging.

Electron microscopy was performed using an FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit microscope operating at 120 kV, equipped with a Veleta 2K × 2K CCD camera.

SDS‐PAGE and Western Blot

For Coomassie blue staining, samples were prepared for SDS‐PAGE in 1x NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (Life Technologies #NP0008) with 50 mM DTT. Electrophoretic separation was carried out using the mini‐PROTEAN electrophoresis system (Bio‐Rad) on an mPAGE 4%–20% Bis‐Tris gel (Millipore #MP42G15) at 180 V for 45 min in MES buffer. Following electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Ready Blue solution (Sigma‐Aldrich #RPS‐1L).

For western blot analysis, the samples were electrotransferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva #10600006) using the Mini Trans‐Blot Cell system (Bio‐Rad) at 200 mA for 1 h. The membrane was blocked with 3% (w/v) BSA in TBS + 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature.

The membrane was then probed with either a pentaHis‐HRP conjugated antibody (Qiagen #34460, RRID: AB_3699318) diluted 1:6000, or with llama Salsa serum as the primary antibody diluted 1:1000 or with Histidine‐tagged Nb‐ALFA at 10 µg/mL for 1 h at room temperature. In the case of serum, a secondary goat anti‐llama HRP‐conjugated antibody (Bethyl #A160‐100P, RRID: AB_67107) diluted 1:1000 was applied for an additional hour at room temperature. In the case of Nb‐ALFA, the pentaHis‐HRP was applied as the secondary antibody.

After antibody incubation, the membrane was washed twice with TBS + 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20 + 0.1% (v/v) Triton X‐100 for 10 min, followed by a final wash in TBS for 10 min. The blot was developed using chemiluminescence with the Immobilon Crescendo Western HRP substrate (Merck #WBLUR0100), and images were captured using the ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio‐Rad).

Llama Immunization

EVs displaying SNMP1 were produced as described above and purified via size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 16/600 column pre‐equilibrated with PBS buffer (Sigma‐Aldrich #P3813‐10PAK).

For long‐term storage, EVs expressing MPs were typically kept at 4°C with 0.01% sodium azide to prevent contamination. However, for llama immunization purposes, purified EVs were slowly frozen at −80°C using a CoolCell LX (#830159) without any sodium azide to ensure biosafety. Although freezing and thawing can affect EV integrity, this was not a critical concern for immunization, as the target protein reliably triggered an immune response.

Immunization of a Llama glama (Llama Salsa, from Chaleins, Pigase Society, France) was carried out through three injections at 21‐day intervals. Each injection consisted of 500 µL of EV‐SNMP1 at A280 = 0.5. Blood was collected 54 days after the start of immunization, 1 week after the final injection.

Blood Collection and Nab Phage Library Preparation

All steps were performed by the Nabgen Technology platform. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from 200 mL of whole blood using a Ficoll density gradient with Histopaque solution (Merck #10771). Total RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, #74104) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the purified RNA by reverse transcription using the SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, #18091050). The resulting cDNA served as a template for PCR amplification of the sequences encoding the variable domains of heavy‐chain antibodies (VHHs or Nabs).

Three sequential PCR reactions were performed using the Q5 High‐Fidelity DNA polymerase (NEB, #M0491S) and specific primers designed for each amplification step (Rajan et al. 2015):

‐CALL001 (5’‐GTC CTG GCT GCT CTT CTA CAA GG‐3’) and CALL002 (5’‐GGT ACG TGC TGT TGA ACT GTT CC‐3’) for the first PCR.

CALL001 (5’‐GTC CTG GCT GCT CTT CTA CAA GG‐3’) and CALL002 (5’‐GGT ACG TGC TGT TGA ACT GTT CC‐3’) for the first PCR.

‐SM017 (5’‐C CAG CCG GCC ATG GCT GAT GTG CAG CTG GTG GAG TCT GG‐3’), SM018 (5’‐C CAG CCG GCC ATG GCT CAG GTG CAG CTG GTG GAG TCT GG‐3’) and CALL002 for the second PCR.

SM017 (5’‐C CAG CCG GCC ATG GCT GAT GTG CAG CTG GTG GAG TCT GG‐3’), SM018 (5’‐C CAG CCG GCC ATG GCT CAG GTG CAG CTG GTG GAG TCT GG‐3’) and CALL002 for the second PCR.

‐A4short (5’‐C ATG CCA TGA CTC GCG GCC CAG CCG GCC ATG GC‐3’) and 38 (5’‐GGA CTA GTG CGG CCG CTG GAG ACG GTG ACC TGG‐3’) for the final PCR (Rajan et al. 2015).

A4short (5’‐C ATG CCA TGA CTC GCG GCC CAG CCG GCC ATG GC‐3’) and 38 (5’‐GGA CTA GTG CGG CCG CTG GAG ACG GTG ACC TGG‐3’) for the final PCR (Rajan et al. 2015).

After each PCR reaction, the corresponding DNA bands were purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR clean‐up kit (Macherey‐Nagel, #740609.50), following the manufacturer's protocol.

The amplified products were then cloned into the phagemid vector pHEN4 between the NcoI (NEB #R3193S) and NotI (NEB # R3189L) restriction sites. The ligation products were transformed into electro‐competent TG1 cells (Biosearch Tech #60502‐02) via electroporation, followed by culturing in the presence of the helper phage M13KO7 (NEB, #N0315S) to construct the Nab phage display library.

Phages displaying Nabs on their surface were precipitated from the culture supernatant by adding one‐fifth volume of 20% PEG8000 + 2.5 M NaCl solution. After incubation on ice for 1 h, the phages were pelleted by centrifugation at 3000× g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting phage pellet was resuspended with 20 mL sterile, ice‐cold PBS. The precipitation and resuspension steps were repeated to maximize recovery of the Nab phage library, which was subsequently used for biopanning via phage display.

Biopanning by Phage Display

A microplate (Thermo scientific #10547781) was initially coated overnight at 4°C with 50 µg/mL of streptavidin (Merck #SA101). The following day, after blocking for 1 h at 37°C with 2% milk, biotinylated Nb‐ALFA was added at 25 µg/mL and incubated for 30 min at 37°C. Subsequently, 100 µL of EVs co‐displaying the protein of interest along with ALFA‐ACE2, at an A280 of 0.1, were applied for 30 min at 37°C. Wells containing only PBS served as negative controls.

Between each step, wells were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20, followed by three additional washes with PBS to remove excess reagents. After washing, phages pre‐incubated in blocking buffer were added to the wells and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Unbound phages were washed away with seven washes of PBS containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20 and seven washes with PBS. Bound phages were eluted using 100 mM triethylamine solution (pH 10) and neutralized with 1 M Tris buffer (pH 7.4).

The eluted phages were immediately used to infect E. coli TG1 cells. Serial dilutions of the infected culture were spread on LB‐agar plates supplemented with ampicillin to allow for colony counting and determination of phage enrichment after each round of biopanning. The remainder of the culture was amplified overnight in 2YT medium supplemented with ampicillin and M13KO7 helper phages. The precipitated phages were used as input for the next round of biopanning.

After two additional rounds of biopanning, colonies from the second and the third rounds were selected for further analysis by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Nabs Screening by ELISA

Twelve colonies from round 2 and 22 colonies from round 3 were grown at 37°C in 2YT medium supplemented with ampicillin. Once the culture reached an OD600 of 0.6–0.8, Nab expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG, and the incubation temperature was lowered to 28°C for 16 h. Meanwhile, an ELISA microplate was coated overnight with 50 µg/mL of streptavidin at 4°C.

The next day, the microplate was prepared by layering streptavidin, biotinylated Nb‐ALFA, and the co‐expressed EVs, as aforementioned. Simultaneously, bacterial cultures were lysed through a freeze/thaw cycle, and the lysates were added to the wells of the prepared microplate. After washing to remove unbound components, a mouse anti‐HA antibody conjugated to HRP (Roche # 12013819001, RRID: AB_390917) was added at a 1:1000 dilution to recognize specifically the HA‐tag fused to Nabs.

The reaction was developed using BM Blue substrate (Merck #11484281001) and stopped with 1 M sulfuric acid. Absorbance at 450 nm (A450) was measured using a TECAN spectrophotometer (Spark #11484281001).

Expression and Purification of Nabs

Nab clones 13, 20, 22, and 24 were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) at 37°C in Turbo Broth medium (AthenaES #0104) supplemented with 0.4% (v/v) glycerol and antibiotics (kanamycin and chloramphenicol). When the bacterial culture reached an OD600 of 0.6–0.8, protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM IPTG, and the temperature was reduced to 28°C for 16 h.

Bacterial cells were then lysed by sonication, and the resulting lysate was purified using Ni‐NTA affinity chromatography (His trap FF crude IMAC column cytiva #17528601), pre‐equilibrated with 50 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl buffer, pH = 8 containing 5 mM imidazole. Elution of His tagged Nabs was done using 250 mM imidazole in the same buffer. This was followed by SEC on a Superdex 75 16/600 column pre‐equilibrated with PBS buffer (Figure S1). The purified Nabs were flash‐frozen and stored at −80°C for future applications (Jin et al. 2023; Kariuki and Magez 2021).

Biotinylation of Nabs

Nab biotinylation was performed using NHS‐PEG4‐Biotin (ThermoScientific #1863420) following the manufacturer's protocol. 2 mg/mL of Nabs were incubated with 1 mM NHS‐PEG4‐Biotin at a molar protein‐to‐biotin ratio of 1:3 for 30 min at room temperature. Following the reaction, excess biotin was removed using a Zeba spin desalting column (Fisher scientific #10341164).

BLI

Interaction studies were performed using BLI (Sultana and Lee 2015) on the Octet Red96 instrument (FortéBio). Streptavidin (SA) biosensors (Sartorius #18‐5019) were hydrated for 15 min in kinetic buffer (PBS + 0.1% (w/v) BSA + 0.02% (v/v) Tween 20).

For the binding assay, biotinylated Nabs were used as ligands and were loaded onto the SA biosensors at a concentration of 2 µg/mL. In parallel, 200 µL of EVs (A280 = 0.2) or 100 nM of SNMP1 in DDM, used as analytes, were placed into a black‐bottom 96‐well microplate (Greiner Bio‐One #655209). After Nab loading, a quenching step was performed with 10 µg/mL biocytin in kinetic buffer to block any unbound streptavidin sites. A baseline step was conducted to remove any unbound Nabs before the association step, during which the biosensors were immersed in wells containing the EVs or the soluble form of SNMP1. Finally, a dissociation step was performed by transferring the biosensors back into the kinetic buffer.

For the kinetic parameter assay, CoEVs were used as ligands by applying the multi‐layer approach on SA biosensors. Biotinylated Nb‐ALFA was first loaded onto SA biosensors at 5 µg/mL. After the quenching and baseline steps, CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2 were loaded at A280 = 0.2. Following another baseline step, the biosensors were immersed in wells containing varying concentrations of Nabs used as analytes, followed by a dissociation step in kinetic buffer.

For the epitope binning assay using the in‐tandem format, the experimental setup followed the same protocol as the kinetic parameter assay. CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2 served as ligands and were immobilized on SA biosensors using the previously described multi‐layer approach on biosensors. Pairwise analysis of the three Nabs was performed to assess competition profiles. As an example, CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2 were initially presented to Nab c24, used as an analyte. Following the association step, the CoEVs‐c24 complex was sequentially presented to the remaining Nabs, c20 and c22, to evaluate their competitive binding profiles. To ensure clarity in the competition results, analytes c20 and c22 were mixed with c24 to saturate the epitopes recognized by c24, allowing the subsequent association (or lack thereof) of the other two Nabs to be clearly observed.

All measurements were taken at 25°C with shaking at 1000 rpm. The data were analyzed using FortéBio Data Analysis Software, version 7.1.

Extraction and Purification of SNMP1 in Detergent

A total of 520 µL of EV‐SNMP1 with an absorbance at 280 nm (A280) of 1.62 were solubilized using 2% (v/v) DDM (n‐dodecyl‐β‐d‐maltopyranoside, NeoBiotech) at 4°C for 1 h. Following ultracentrifugation at 100,000× g for 1 h at 4°C, the supernatant containing the solubilized protein in detergent was carefully collected, while the pellet containing insoluble material was discarded. The supernatant was subsequently purified using Ni‐NTA affinity chromatography with magnetic agarose beads (Jena Bioscience # AC‐604‐5).

For purification, 40 µL of the bead suspension was pre‐equilibrated in PBS containing 5 mM imidazole and 0.02% (v/v) DDM. The beads were incubated with the supernatant for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with PBS containing 10 mM imidazole and 0.02% (v/v) DDM to remove unbound proteins. SNMP1 was then eluted with PBS containing 250 mM imidazole and 0.02% (v/v) DDM.

The eluted fractions were dialyzed overnight at 4°C against PBS using the pure A‐lyzer mini 6000 (Sigma‐Aldrich). The recovered SNMP1 in DDM was used at a concentration of 100 nM for interaction studies.

Structure Prediction of SNMP1 by AlphaFold3

Structures were predicted by AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al. 2024) on google servers at https://golgi.sandbox.google.com by introducing the sequence of full‐length SNMP1 and those of the Nabs. The figures were made with ChimeraX (Pettersen et al. 2021). Coordinates of predicted structures are accessible on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13767314). Interactions have been analyzed with PISA (Krissinel and Henrick 2007).

Results

Antigen Preparation for Llama Immunization

Production and Purification of EVs Displaying SNMP1

To immunize a llama, EVs displaying SNMP1 on their surface (EV‐SNMP1) were prepared as detailed in the Materials and Methods section.

Although the majority of the isolated sample consisted of EVs, minor contaminants could still be present (Welsh et al. 2024). To ensure purity for llama immunization, EVs were further purified using SEC on an S200 16/600 column pre‐equilibrated with PBS buffer. The EVs eluted at 44.88 mL, corresponding to high molecular weight particles (Figure 1A). The absorbance at 280 nm (A280) was measured to roughly estimate overall protein content. Fractions corresponding to the elution peak were pooled and concentrated to a final volume of 1.5 mL, with an A280 of 0.5.

Characterization of EVs

EVs were characterized before and after purification using several analytical techniques. DLS was employed to assess sample homogeneity and determine the size of the EVs. A comparison of the size distributions by volume, before (blue curve, Figure 1B) and after purification (orange curve, Figure 1B), revealed an improvement in EV homogeneity. The results indicated that the EVs ranged in size from 80 to 100 nm.

Negative staining electron microscopy further confirmed this size distribution. Additionally, it provided a clear assessment of sample purity, which was significantly improved after SEC purification (Figure 1C).

To confirm the presence of SNMP1 on the surface of the EVs, a western blot was performed using an anti‐His tag antibody. As shown in Figure 1D, a specific band corresponding to SNMP1 at ∼60 KDa was observed in both the EV sample isolated by ultracentrifugation and the purified EV sample.

In addition to detecting the protein of interest, EV‐SNMP1 samples were solubilized using the detergent DDM. The solubilized material was then separated by SDS‐PAGE, and the band corresponding to the estimated molecular weight of SNMP1 was excised and analyzed by LC‐MS/MS. Mass spectrometry confirmed that SNMP1 was the major protein in the analyzed band (data not shown).

To test freezing as a storage option, an aliquot was frozen, thawed, and analyzed. A slight increase in EVs size was observed by DLS after thawing (gray curve, Figure 1B) and the western blot confirmed that SNMP1 remains present on the EV surface (Figure 1D).

Following purification and characterization, the EV‐SNMP1 sample was divided into three aliquots of 500 µL each, with an A280 of 0.5. The aliquots were frozen slowly to preserve EV integrity until injection into the llama.

Generation of Nabs Targeting SNMP1

The generation of Nabs is a multi‐step process including llama immunization, Nab phage library preparation, biopanning by phage display, and Nabs selection by ELISA, as illustrated in Figure 2. Both biopanning and ELISA require the immobilization of the target antigen, which, in our case, involves immobilizing the EVs onto microplates.

The process of nano‐antibody generation. Schematic representation of the multi‐step process for the generation of nano‐antibodies. It begins by immunization of a llama with an antigen, 54 days later, the blood is collected and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) are isolated. mRNA is extracted from PBMCs and cDNA is synthesized by RT‐PCR. Nano‐antibodies are amplified by three PCR reactions and then cloned into phagemid vector PHEN4 to create a nano‐antibody phage library. Next, biopanning via phage display is performed where unbound phages are washed away and bound phages are eluted and amplified; they are used for the next round of biopanning. A total of 2–4 rounds can be realized in order to have enough enrichment. ELISA is then performed for the selection of positive clones. Finally, positive clones are sequenced and analyzed, then nano‐antibodies are expressed in bacterial cells and purified.

Design of an Anchoring Strategy for EVs

Lymphocytes from the llama blood collected 54 days after immunization were isolated using a Ficoll gradient. To assess the immune response to EV‐SNMP1, the remaining serum was used as the primary antibody in western blot analysis to evaluate the presence of anti‐SNMP1 antibodies. As shown in Figure 3A, the serum exhibited a specific response to EV‐SNMP1 compared to EVs displaying other proteins.

To address this non‐specificity, a strategy to specifically immobilize EVs displaying the protein of interest was designed. An anchor protein was co‐expressed on the surface of the EVs alongside the target protein. The anchor protein chosen was the human angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), modified with an ALFA‐tag at the N‐terminus. This took advantage of the high‐affinity interaction between the anti‐ALFA‐tag nano‐antibody (Nb‐ALFA) and the ALFA‐tag (Götzke et al. 2019). Co‐expression of the anchor protein ALFA‐ACE2 and the target protein SNMP1 was confirmed by western blot, using Nb‐ALFA as the primary antibody and penta‐His‐HRP as the secondary antibody. Figure 3B demonstrates the simultaneous detection of SNMP1 and ALFA‐ACE2 on the EV sample.

In a first attempt, Nb‐ALFA was immobilized at the bottom of the wells, and EVs co‐transfected with SNMP1 and ALFA‐ACE2 (CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2) were added. Unfortunately, this method did not elicit any detectable serum response, regardless of the type of EVs immobilized via Nb‐ALFA (Figure 4B). This lack of response was likely due to insufficient binding to the microplate surface, causing the EVs to be washed away during the washing steps.

To overcome this issue, a multi‐layered strategy was implemented to strengthen EVs binding to the microplate. First, the wells were coated with streptavidin, and Nb‐ALFA was labeled with NHS‐PEG4‐biotin, which specifically binds to streptavidin (Hiller et al. 1987). CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2 were then added, allowing the ALFA‐tag to interact with the Nb‐ALFA and immobilize the target EVs.

This multi‐layer strategy successfully produced a specific response to CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2 compared to non‐relevant EVs (Figure 4C). This method enabled the specific immobilization of the target EVs via the anchor protein, making it a promising approach for the following biopanning step.

Biopanning by Phage Display

Biopanning by phage display was performed on CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2, which were immobilized using the multi‐layer approach. After three rounds of biopanning, the eluted phages specific to the EVs were 10‐fold higher than those eluted from the buffer‐only negative control (Table S1).

Characterization of Nabs

Binding Assay by BLI

BLI experiments were performed to characterize interactions between the produced Nabs and SNMP1. This technique involves immobilizing a ligand on biosensors, which are presented to an analyte in a microplate. In this case, Nabs served as ligands while EVs were used as analytes.

The binding of Nabs c13 from Group 1 as well as c20, c22, and c24 from Group 2, was analyzed against CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2. For this assay, all Nabs were biotinylated and immobilized on streptavidin (SA) biosensors, which were then immersed in the CoEV solution (A280 = 0.2) to monitor the association phase then in the buffer to monitor the dissociation. As shown in Figure 7A, all Nabs demonstrated binding to CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2.

Following these results, the binding specificity of Nabs c13 from Group 1 and c20, c22, and c24 from Group 2, was further investigated using EVs displaying only SNMP1 (EV‐SNMP1), with Nb‐ALFA serving as a negative control. As demonstrated in Figure 7B, Group 2 Nabs showed specific binding to EV‐SNMP1, whereas Nab c13 from Group 1, similar to the negative control Nb‐ALFA, did not display any binding.

To validate these observations, the experiment was repeated with EVs displaying only ALFA‐ACE2 (EV‐ALFA‐ACE2). As depicted in Figure 7C, Group 2 Nabs did not bind to EV‐ALFA‐ACE2, while Nab c13 from Group 1, similar to Nb‐ALFA (now serving as a positive control), exhibited specific binding.

Further testing with the soluble domain of ACE2 confirmed that Nab c13 specifically targets ACE2 (Figure S2).

Kinetic Parameters

To determine kinetic parameters, precise knowledge of the analyte concentration is essential. However, this is challenging for EVs, as the exact concentration of the target protein on the EV surface cannot be accurately determined. As an initial step, SNMP1 extracted from EVs and purified using DDM was employed as the analyte, with Nab c22 chosen as the ligand. Biotinylated c22 was immobilized on SA biosensors, and presented to soluble SNMP1 at concentrations ranging from 32 to 0.5 nM, resulting in an affinity constant (K D​) of 1.66 nM (Figure 8A).

For kinetic characterization using EVs, the latter must be the ligand and the Nabs the analyte. For this reason, the multi‐layer approach was used on SA biosensors. First, Nb‐ALFA was immobilized on these biosensors, followed by the loading of CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2. CoEVs were then presented to Nab c22 at concentrations ranging from 32 to 0.5 nM. This yielded a K D​ of 0.617 nM (Figure 8B), which was comparable to the value obtained using soluble SNMP1.

Additionally, the kinetic parameters of Nabs c20 and c24 were measured using the same multi‐layer approach on SA biosensors. The obtained K D​ values were 8.6 nM for c20 (Figure 8C) and 1.7 nM for c24 (Figure 8D).

Epitope Binning Assay

To assess the diversity of the selected Nabs, an epitope binning assay was conducted using the in‐tandem format. CoEV‐SNMP1+ALFA‐ACE2 were employed as the ligand and immobilized on SA biosensors using the multi‐layer approach. The CoEVs were sequentially presented to two Nabs during each assay step. Pairwise analysis of the three Nabs (c20, c22, and c24) revealed a non‐competitive binding pattern, indicating that each Nab targets a distinct, non‐overlapping epitope on the SNMP1 ectodomain (Figure 9A–C). These interactions were further investigated with AlphaFold3 to provide insights into the structural details of their binding.

AlphaFold3 Predictions

The predicted structure models of the SNMP1 full‐length and Nabs were obtained using AlphaFold3. The complexes were predicted either with individual Nab or all together. For each complex AlphaFold3 performed five predictions and scored them according to the values of the pLDDT (Abramson et al. 2024). The quality of the complex prediction relies on the PAE score (Abramson et al. 2024), itself depending on converging positions of the components of the five predictions.

The prediction of the structure complex between SNMP1 and c24 exhibited optimal statistics as the pLDDT were excellent (above 80%) as well as the PAE, exemplified by the identical structures of the five complexes (Figure S3A). c24 bound to SNMP1 using its three CDRs and a lateral face of its core involving β‐strands 48–54 and 59–63 (Figure 10A,B,i1). Such a binding using a Nab core has been reported previously (Desmyter et al. 2017). c24 CDR3 protruded deeply in a SNMP1 crevice bordered mainly by residues from a big loop (residues 321–332) and an adjacent smaller one (residues 337–339) (Figure 10A,Bi1,). A total of 11% of c24 water accessible surface (WAS) was buried by SNMP1 (710 Å2), and conversely 794 Å2 of SNMP1 WAS were buried by c24.

Instead, although the prediction of the complex between SNMP1 and c20 exhibited optimal statistics as the pLDDT were excellent (above 80%), the PAE exhibited weaker features as the structures of the five predicted complexes did not converge on unique positions (Figure S3B). However, the two first best scored structures were identical, meaning that they are likely to be significant (Figure S3C). c20 bound to SNMP1 using mainly its long CDR3 and in a smaller extent its CDR1. In contrast with c24, its core was not involved in binding and c20 had a topology more perpendicular to SNMP1 (Figure 10C,D,i2). The medium part of its CDR3 formed half a turn with protruded only slightly in a SNMP1 cavity bordered mainly by residues from a β‐strand and a loop (residues 113–121), α‐helix 177–184 and loop 213–216 (Figure 10C,D,i2). A total of 12.5% of c20 WAS were buried by SNMP1 (785 Å2), and conversely 761 Å2 of SNMP1 WAS were buried by c20.

In contrast with previous results, the prediction of the structure of the complex between SNMP1 and c22 exhibited a poor PAE as the five predicted complexes did not converge on unique positions and the best scored solutions were also scattered (Figure S3D). Hence, this prediction has been discarded from our analysis.

When the SNMP1 structures were superimposed in both complexes, the positions of c24 and c20 were non‐overlapping on SNMP1, at opposite epitopes (Figure 10E).

Discussion

Expression and Production of EVs

The production of recombinant MPs presents significant challenges due to their hydrophobic transmembrane domains, which complicate handling and require the use of detergents for solubilization and purification (Carpenter et al. 2008; Padmanabha Das et al. 2020). Unfortunately, MPs solubilized in detergents are often unstable, with a high risk of aggregation during the production process (Ma and Xia 2008; Warne et al. 2008). EVs offer an alternative platform for expressing recombinant MPs on their surface without the need for detergents. Compared to traditional methods, which can take several months to produce a MP, EV‐based expression is faster, taking only about 1 week (Delauzun et al. 2020). Additionally, this approach provides a native‐like environment for MPs, allowing them to remain stable for extended periods (Delauzun et al. 2020). In this study, several steps from the original protocol by Delauzun et al. (2020) were optimized to enhance simplicity, reproducibility, and cost‐effectiveness.

While MPs need to be used fresh or stored at 4°C for short periods to preserve their functionality, EVs can be frozen at −80°C (Ahmadian et al. 2024). Although this method of storage may affect the particle size of EVs, potentially due to fusion phenomena during the freezing process (Gelibter et al. 2022), it remains a viable option for applications, such as immunization. Importantly, despite changes in particle size, western blot analysis confirms the presence of the target protein after thawing, ensuring that the immunogenicity of the EVs is not compromised.

Design of an Anchoring Protein

Western blot analyses confirmed successful immunization, as the serum specifically reacted with EVs displaying SNMP1 while showing no significant response to non‐relevant EVs. However, ELISA results were unexpectedly inconsistent, revealing a non‐specific serum response to EVs displaying various unrelated targets. This finding was surprising since the llama had not been immunized with these alternative EVs. The likely explanation for this non‐specific response was the presence of EV subpopulations in the injected sample that did not display the target protein. These subpopulations were not completely isolated during purification due to their similar size. Consequently, the llama's immune system developed a response against both the EVs displaying SNMP1 and the subpopulations lacking it, including native proteins on the EVs. Coating of these mixed populations resulted in non‐specificity in the ELISA responses, emphasizing the need for more specific presentation of the target protein. A purification tag could improve the binding specificity; however, we chose not to insert a tag on the extracellular domain of the target protein to avoid altering antibody recognition (Welsh et al. 2024). As an alternative, we decided to use an anchor protein carrying a universal tag, co‐expressed with the protein of interest on the surface of EVs. The ideal anchor protein needed to be easy to produce, of medium size to enhance EV binding to the microplate, without masking the protein of interest. ACE2 was chosen as the anchor protein, as it met all these required criteria and was the center of several projects in the lab during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Thébault et al. 2022). The high affinity between the ALFA‐tag and the Nb‐ALFA was exploited for specific purification (Götzke et al. 2019; Kilisch et al. 2021). To validate this strategy, ACE2 with an N‐terminal ALFA‐tag was co‐transfected with SNMP1, which carried a C‐terminal 6x His tag (located within the EVs to avoid altering the extracellular domain). CoEVs were then isolated, and western blotting confirmed the presence of both proteins.

Subsequently, an ELISA was performed by coating the Nb‐ALFA onto a 96‐well plate and adding CoEVs. Unfortunately, this approach failed to elicit any response from CoEVs to the serum regardless the type of EVs. This lack of response likely stemmed from insufficient binding between CoEVs and the immobilized Nb‐ALFA. It is possible that the small size of Nb‐ALFA (2–3 nm) and its rigid attachment to the flat plate surface hindered its ability to interact effectively with the much larger, spherical EVs.

To overcome this limitation, we developed a multi‐layer approach in order to improve flexibility and distance the Nb‐ALFA from the surface. In this approach, streptavidin was coated as the first layer, and Nb‐ALFA was biotinylated with NHS‐PEG4‐biotin, which introduced an additional 2.9 nm spacing between the streptavidin and Nb‐ALFA. This increased flexibility enhanced accessibility and enabled more effective immobilization of the CoEVs. This multi‐layer approach successfully enabled a specific serum response to CoEVs displaying SNMP1 and ALFA‐ACE2 compared to other targets. The addition of the anchor protein proved highly advantageous, serving as both a purification tag and an enabler of specific detection for various MP targets. This approach provides a versatile tool for future applications involving MPs.

Generation of Nabs Targeting SNMP1

The generation of Nabs is a well‐established process based on standard protocols (Jin et al. 2023; Pardon et al. 2014). To isolate Nabs against SNMP1, the multi‐layer approach was employed. After three rounds of biopanning, a 10‐fold enrichment of SNMP1‐specific phages was observed, which is notably lower than the typical 100‐fold enrichment seen for soluble proteins (Pardon et al. 2014). Subsequently, ELISA was performed to identify positive clones, revealing two distinct profiles. ELISA and BLI analyses showed that one group of clones was specific to SNMP1, while the other was specific to ACE2.

This result was unexpected, as the llama had only been immunized with EVs harvested from cells transfected with SNMP1. A possible explanation for this outcome lies in the heterogeneity of our EV samples. Since the separation techniques used—ultracentrifugation and size exclusion chromatography—are based solely on size, all EVs produced by the cells were collected. This included not only EVs carrying the protein of interest but also naturally occurring EVs produced by the cells. It has been shown that ACE2 is expressed at low levels in HEK293 cells (Sherman and Emmer 2021), which could result in residual ACE2 being present in the EV samples used for immunization. Additionally, as our approach employed ALFA‐ACE2 as an anchor protein, this may have contributed to the selection of Nabs specific to ACE2. However, although the multi‐layer approach used during biopanning allowed the selection of Nabs specific for the anchor protein, it did not prevent the selection of Nabs specific for the target of interest.

To improve future Nabs selection, several strategies could be considered. First, biopanning could be conducted in the presence of Nab c13 to block the ACE2 epitope. Alternatively, a depletion step using EVs displaying only ALFA‐ACE2 could be incorporated during biopanning (Panagides et al. 2022). This step would deplete phages recognizing the anchor protein, allowing the subsequent biopanning process to focus on the remaining phages specific to the target protein. Finally, EV samples used for immunization could be “cleaned” by selectively removing EVs naturally displaying ACE2 through purification using Nab c13.

Characterization of Nabs Targeting SNMP1

During BLI experiments, EVs were used either as analyte or ligand. When EVs were used as analytes, their highly multivalent nature explained the very slow dissociation of Nabs. Conversely, when EVs were employed as ligands, the multi‐layer approach proved effective for determining the kinetic parameters of their interaction with Nabs. The kinetic values obtained for c22 using EVs were fully comparable to those obtained with detergent‐solubilized protein, demonstrating that EVs can serve as reliable tools for future interaction studies. A remaining question is whether the conformation of the protein is similar in EVs and neuronal cells. Future studies in cells need to be conducted to shed light on this point.

To assess the diversity of epitopes recognized by the selected Nabs, a competitive binding assay (Bates et al. 2024; Thébault et al. 2022) was performed with Nabs c20, c22, and c24. The results indicated non‐competitive binding behavior among these Nabs, suggesting that they target distinct epitopes. In parallel, AlphaFold3 was used to predict the epitopes of the Nabs on SNMP1. While the predictions for c22 were of poor quality, those for c20 and c24 were more robust. These two Nabs were predicted to bind to distinct positions on the SNMP1 ectodomain, which aligns with the results obtained from the BLI experiments.

Such a non‐competitive binding behavior could be highly advantageous for in vivo studies, increasing the likelihood of effectively blocking SNMP1 function to better understand its function in moth pheromone sensing, and eventually to develop sustainable crop protection strategies against polyphagous pests, such as S. littoralis. In a previous study conducted on the insect model species Drosophila melanogaster, classical rabbit antibodies targeting the extracellular domain of SNMP1 from this species were passively infused in vivo through the recording electrodes in cis‐vaccenyl acetate (cVA) pheromone responsive sensilla during electrophysiological recordings. This study revealed that the response to cVA (a long carbon chain molecule) in these sensilla was disrupted, arguing for an effective role of SNMP1 in D. melanogaster pheromone sensing (Jin et al. 2008). In the moth Helicoverpa armigera, CRISPR‐Cas9‐induced mutation in SNMP1 leading to unfunctional protein has revealed impaired sensitivity of males to long‐chain sex pheromones as well (Liu et al. 2020), but not to shorter chain sex pheromones that are usually used by most moth species. Thus, the exact role of SNMP1 in moth sex pheromone detection is still awaited. The small nano‐antibodies we generated in this study are ideally suited to passively infuse efficiently in vivo through the recording electrodes during electrophysiological recording. Thus, they represent the perfect tool to further address this question. Disrupting moth sex pheromone communication via blocking SNMP1 function would represent an elegant way to reduce populations by preventing mating, as moths include major pests in agricultural production.

Conclusion

We provide in this study a new protocol for the generation of nano‐antibodies targeting membrane proteins via their expression at the surface of extracellular vesicles. EVs represent an ideal model for membrane protein expression, preserving their integrity and functionality. Our approach opens new avenues in the field of Nabs generation.

Targeting the moth SNMP1 protein, a membrane protein potentially critical for efficient reproduction in agricultural pests, opens up new ways for understanding its function and developing new environmentally friendly solutions for crop protection, based on pheromone detection disruption.
