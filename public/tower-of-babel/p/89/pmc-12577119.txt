Jumping hurdles: a grounded theory study of factors influencing school nurses’ role in pediatric obesity prevention in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdullah Alharbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric obesity is a major public health concern worldwide. In Saudi Arabia, school nurses can play a pivotal role in prevention, yet their practices are influenced by multiple contextual factors. This study explored the factors shaping school nurses’ attitudes and behaviors toward pediatric obesity prevention.MethodsA constructivist grounded theory approach was used. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 11 school nurses working in private primary schools in Saudi Arabia. Data were analyzed using Charmaz’s process, with theoretical sampling to refine emerging categories. A focus group with three experts was conducted to confirm and validate the generated theory.ResultsEleven participants contributed to the analysis. Three categories (Struggling, Avoiding, and Competing Priorities) and one core category (Lacking Support) emerged to collectively represent the social process, Jumping Hurdles: A Pathway to Professional Growth.ConclusionsThis substantive theoretical model advances understanding of the factors shaping school nurses’ roles in pediatric obesity prevention in Saudi Arabia. Findings highlight the need for actionable policies and school-level support to strengthen nurses’ contributions. For policymakers and school administrators, investing in structural support, targeted training, and resource allocation may enhance nurses’ capacity to engage effectively in obesity prevention and ultimately improve student health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04021-9.

Background

Pediatric obesity is a growing global public health concern, with its prevalence steadily rising across both developed and developing nations. Globally, overweight and obesity affect nearly one in five children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years, with more than 390 million in this age group classified as overweight or obese [1]. Children between 2 and 20 years of age with a body mass index (BMI) at or above the 95th percentile are considered obese [2]. Excess weight in childhood is associated with a range of serious physical and psychological health risks, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes, elevated cholesterol, coronary heart disease, asthma, mobility issues, and fatty liver disease [3, 4]. Additionally, obesity has been identified as a contributing factor in viral pandemics such as COVID-19 and H1N1 influenza, placing obese individuals at increased risk of severe complications and mortality [5, 6]. Many obese children remain obese into adulthood and face an elevated risk of early-onset non-communicable diseases such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes [7, 8]. Prolonged obesity in children can also lead to psychological consequences, including low self-esteem, body image dissatisfaction, bullying, anxiety, depression, and reduced quality of life [9, 10]. These risks underscore the urgency of global preventive strategies, particularly within schools, where children spend a significant portion of their time.

Multiple interacting factors contribute to pediatric obesity, ranging from poor dietary habits and physical inactivity to broader behavioral and environmental influences. Research shows that the consumption of energy-dense, low-nutrient foods combined with sedentary lifestyles is a key driver of excess weight among children [11, 12]. Unhealthy maternal feeding practices, often linked to binge eating, have also been shown to indirectly predict higher child BMI [13, 14]. Moreover, genetic predisposition and exposure to obesogenic environments further compound the risk of pediatric obesity [15, 16]. Family dynamics, including maternal depression, stress, and coping behaviors, can influence children’s eating habits and weight trajectories [17, 18]. Additionally, parental modeling and maternal education levels are associated with the development of children’s food preferences and body weight [19, 20].

In Saudi Arabia, the burden of obesity is substantial. Recent national data indicate that among adolescents, 23.1% are obese and 45.1% are overweight. Among children aged 2 to 14 years, 14.6% are obese, while 33.3% are classified as overweight [21]. This rapid increase has been attributed to lifestyle shifts, including greater consumption of calorie-dense foods, limited physical activity, and increasing screen time [22]. Additional studies reinforce these concerns, showing that Saudi society’s reliance on fast food and ready-made meals contributes to unhealthy eating patterns, while urban lifestyle factors including sedentary behavior and high-calorie diets have further accelerated obesity rates, particularly among youth [23, 24].

School nurses serve as essential leaders who connect health care and education, working to ensure the healthy development of children, families, and communities. A child’s overall health is closely tied to their ability to succeed in school, as healthy students tend to demonstrate better academic performance, improved behavior, and stronger cognitive skills [25]. By delivering specialized nursing services, school nurses play a pivotal role in supporting academic achievement through care that addresses students’ physical, mental, emotional, cognitive, and social well-being [26]. Globally, research shows that school nurses are frontline professionals in delivering school-based obesity prevention programs, implementing multicomponent interventions that include counseling, nutritional guidance, physical activity promotion, and parent engagement [27, 28]. Beyond routine health support, findings from a systematic review of intervention studies confirm that school nurses frequently serve as the leading or most utilized members of multidisciplinary teams, delivering diverse interventions such as motivational interviewing, guideline development, and workshops to promote lifestyle change in children and families [28].

In Saudi Arabia, research examining school nursing is generally scarce. A qualitative study conducted in the western region broadly explored the perspectives of school stakeholders and confirmed widespread recognition of the positive impact of school nurses on students, staff, and families, while also identifying structural barriers that limit the effective implementation of school nursing roles [29]. However, to date, no published studies have specifically addressed the role of school nurses in pediatric obesity prevention in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore the key factors shaping school nurses’ attitudes and behaviors toward pediatric obesity prevention within primary schools in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Design

Grounded theory is used to explain human behavior in its social context, making it a valuable framework for guiding nursing practice, which is inherently focused on human interaction [30]. This study adopted Charmaz’s constructivist grounded theory approach, which is flexible and emphasizes theory development from the researcher’s interpretations while examining the phenomenon in real life settings, social relationships, and networks [31]. The investigation consisted of two stages: Phase I involved conducting individual interviews, while Phase II consisted of a focus group discussion with experts in the research topic. Semi-structured interviews were chosen as the primary method of data collection because they provided both structure and flexibility, enabling the researcher to capture in-depth narratives and probe emerging concepts, which was especially suited to exploring participants’ attitudes and behaviors.

Ethics approval

Approval for this research was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Permission to contact and recruit participants was also obtained from the Saudi Ministry of Education.

Recruitment and sampling

Participants were recruited using purposive, and theoretical sampling methods, supplemented by a study announcement flyer. Phase I included licensed school nurses employed in private primary schools in Saudi Arabia who met the inclusion criteria of having at least one year of experience and being proficient in either Arabic or English. Nurses with less than one year of experience or inadequate language skills were excluded. Theoretical sampling was central to refining categories as they emerged during analysis; it allowed the researcher to pursue data that deepened incomplete categories and to explore new properties until no further variation was identified, ensuring the categories were conceptually robust [31]. In Phase II, a focus group of three experts was held via Zoom to confirm and refine the findings from Phase I. Eligible experts were required to have published at least one peer-reviewed paper related to the school nursing field.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were used to gather in-depth insights into school nurses’ perspectives and actions related to pediatric obesity prevention. They were chosen because this flexible format allowed participants to share experiences freely while enabling the researcher to probe emerging issues in depth. Open-ended questions and follow-up probes allowed for detailed exploration of participant views [31]. The interview questions were adapted from the author’s earlier dissertation work [32] and are provided in Appendix A. In Phase I, the primary question was, “Tell me about your experience in providing childhood obesity prevention in your school?” All interviews lasted approximately 60–90 min, were conducted face-to-face in the school nurse’s office or school clinic and continued until theoretical saturation was reached after nine interviews, with two additional interviews conducted to ensure no new information was obtained. Interviews were recorded using two digital audio devices. Eleven licensed school nurses participated in Phase I, four females and seven males, with diverse professional experience. Full demographic details are provided in Table 1. A demographic questionnaire was completed by each participant before their interview, and memo writing and field notes were maintained throughout the study to enhance the contextual understanding of the data. All interviews were conducted in Arabic, transcribed verbatim, and subsequently translated into English by the researcher. To ensure accuracy, a bilingual colleague independently reviewed the translations, and a back-translation procedure was used to confirm conceptual equivalence. Coding was performed on the English transcripts, while the original Arabic text was retained for reference.

Following completion of Phase I, Phase II involved a focus group of three experts conducted via Zoom to confirm and refine the findings. This step was included to enhance the credibility of the emerging theory, provide theoretically informed feedback, and validate the categories against the perspectives of professionals with scholarly expertise in the field. Experts were selected for their scholarly contributions to school nursing research, ensuring the feedback was grounded in professional expertise. Participants received a summary of the categories and the substantive theory by email to facilitate focused discussion. The session began with the question, “What are your thoughts regarding the categories and the substantive theory that emerged during the individual interviews?” and additional probes were used to further confirm the categories and refine the theoretical model.

Data analysis

In grounded theory, data analysis occurs concurrently with data collection [30]. A constant comparative method was applied, where interview data were systematically compared with other data, codes, and categories. Following Charmaz’s guidelines, the process began with initial coding, which involved closely examining segments of data and assigning descriptive labels, sometimes using participants’ own words [31]. This was followed by focused coding, in which related initial codes were grouped into broader categories. The final stage, theoretical coding, involved identifying relationships between categories to construct the substantive theory explaining the phenomenon. NVivo 12 software facilitated the organization and management of all coding activities.

Trustworthiness

Strategies to ensure rigor followed Lincoln and Guba’s criteria: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [33]. Credibility was supported through purposeful sampling, appropriate data collection procedures, member checking of transcripts, and ongoing memo writing and reflexivity. Dependability was achieved via member checks and systematic field note documentation to ensure process consistency. Confirmability was addressed through reflective analysis and journaling to minimize researcher bias, in line with Charmaz’s constructivist principles. Transferability was facilitated by providing detailed descriptions of the study context and participants, enabling readers to judge the relevance of the findings to other settings.

Results

This study developed a substantive theoretical model derived from individual semi structured interviews and a focus group discussion. The model illustrates the key factors shaping primary school nurses’ attitudes and behaviors in relation to their role in pediatric obesity prevention. Through the constant comparative analysis of participants’ narratives, initial codes were clustered into conceptually similar groups, which were then developed into three categories, Struggling, Avoiding, and Competing Priorities, and one core category, Lacking Support. For instance, the initial code “Struggling with Parents” was refined into the category Struggling, while the code “Avoiding Weight Stigma” informed the category Avoiding. The use of gerunds in category names reflects the ongoing actions described by participants. The use of gerunds to reflect actions and processes in grounded theory coding was emphasized, aligning with this study’s analytical approach [31]. These categories support the basic social process titled “Jumping Hurdles: A Pathway to Professional Growth” (Fig. 1). Struggling

Struggling was the first category identified from the data analysis. This category was shaped by multiple factors, including workload, dysfunctional relationships, and ineffective leadership practices. This category was constructed by grouping codes that reflected participants’ narratives of barriers within both the school setting and family environment. In this study, the struggling category reflects the difficulties school nurses encountered that limited their involvement in pediatric obesity prevention. These difficulties were associated with both the school environment and the students’ parents. Parents and the school environment play important roles in supporting school nurses’ efforts to apply obesity prevention strategies. School-related issues included heavy workload, the presence of unhealthy food in the canteen, and lack of cooperation from the school administration. Parent-related issues involved unhealthy practices such as sending unhealthy food from home, giving children extra money, and prioritizing food variety over nutritional value. Participants also described a general lack of cooperation from parents. Some of the participants’ quotes related to the struggling category are as follows:

Participant 1: I serve both primary and middle school students, so my day is always full. The workload makes it hard to focus on obesity prevention. I have diabetic students who need close monitoring, plus I handle emergencies like playground injuries and allergic reactions. Managing all of this while trying to supervise young children is challenging.

Avoiding

Avoiding was another category identified in the data analysis. This category emerged from codes describing how nurses chose silence or disengagement as a strategy when faced with resistance or potential conflict. It represents how participants faced challenges in implementing obesity prevention practices in schools, often choosing to refrain from doing so to prevent negative responses from students, parents, or the school administration. Participants explained that addressing obesity-related practices with parents could result in misunderstanding, weight stigmatization, or bullying. The following are selected participant quotes related to this category:

Participant 7: Obesity is a sensitive topic, and many parents avoid discussing it with their children. Some fear it could lead to bullying. They might say, ‘My child has already been bullied, and now you’re bringing it up again.’ So, I avoid the topic to prevent conflict or misunderstanding with parents.

Competing priorities

Competing Priorities was another category identified from the data analysis. This category was developed by clustering codes that highlighted nurses’ competing daily demands. It describes the multiple healthcare demands that school nurses manage in their daily routines, which limit their focus on obesity prevention. Participants reported that their work primarily revolves around conditions requiring immediate attention, such as diabetes, asthma, food allergies, and injuries. Within this context, obesity prevention was regarded as a lower priority due to time limitations and the urgency of other health concerns. The following are selected participant quotes related to this category:

There are always urgent things happening that need my immediate attention. Lacking support

Lacking Support is the core category identified from the data analysis. It refers to the absence of policies, funding, and resources necessary for implementing pediatric obesity prevention in schools. Support and funding from healthcare systems, public health, and health organizations, including the development and enforcement of policies, are vital to meeting children’s health needs in school settings [26]. This core category is central to all other categories including struggling, avoiding, and competing priorities, since the challenges described by participants were rooted in systemic gaps that left nurses without the necessary structures to fulfill their roles. Participants described a lack of organizational backing for obesity prevention, including insufficient funding, limited resources, and the absence of essential policies such as BMI screening, structured prevention programs, referral systems, and adequate school nurse staffing. The following are selected participant quotes related to the core category:

Participant 10: The school should supply essential tools for both the clinical and educational aspects of obesity prevention and management. This should include programs that engage students and, in particular, their families, along with lectures and lessons on the subject. I lack the resources necessary to implement these important measures. I believe policymakers should assist us by providing these tools and acting on my suggestions to enhance our role as school nurses in preventing obesity.”

This substantive theory is composed of one core category, Lacking Support, and three interconnected categories: Struggling, Avoiding, and Competing Priorities. Together, these categories capture the challenges that limited school nurses’ engagement in obesity prevention within Saudi primary schools. Lacking Support serves as the central category, shaping the other three categories. Struggling is closely tied to the core category, as participants described heavy workload, unhealthy school food environments, lack of administrative cooperation, and unhealthy parental practices as key barriers limiting their role in obesity prevention. Avoiding also connected to the core category, with avoidance behaviors emerging when nurses lacked formal guidance or organizational backing, leaving them hesitant to address obesity for fear of negative reactions from parents, students, or administrators. Finally, Competing Priorities reflected how insufficient staffing and organizational resources forced nurses to focus on urgent health demands, leaving obesity prevention as a lower priority. Lacking Support, as the core category, is central to all other categories Struggling, Avoiding, and Competing Priorities, since the challenges described by participants were rooted in systemic gaps that left nurses without the structures needed to fulfill their preventive role. Collectively, these categories form the social process described as “Jumping Hurdles: A Pathway to Professional Growth”, which illustrates how school nurses navigate obstacles while attempting to address pediatric obesity prevention.

Discussion

The substantive theory, Jumping Hurdles: A Pathway to Professional Growth, offers a conceptual explanation of how systemic, organizational, and interpersonal barriers shape school nurses’ attitudes and behaviors toward pediatric obesity prevention in Saudi primary schools. The model consists of one core category (Lacking Support) and three interrelated categories (Struggling, Avoiding, and Competing Priorities), which together capture the multifaceted challenges nurses encounter when attempting to promote healthy weight management among students. This theoretical framework contributes to understanding how limited institutional support and contextual factors collectively influence professional engagement in school-based obesity prevention.

Consistent with the Struggling category, Salemonsen et al. [34] found that parents of children with overweight or obesity often felt uncertain about their responsibility in managing their child’s weight and struggled to understand how family dynamics influenced eating behaviors. This finding supports the present study’s conclusion that school nurses viewed family environments and parental attitudes as critical yet challenging factors in implementing obesity prevention. Similarly, Burch and Stoeckel [35] reported that school nurses faced persistent difficulties when collaborating with families, particularly in communication, discussing healthy habits, and addressing parental reactions to health notifications. These findings parallel the current study’s results, where nurses experienced ongoing struggles to engage parents and families as active partners in obesity prevention, reinforcing that family engagement remains a critical yet underdeveloped component of school health practice.

Aligned with the Avoiding category, Gothilander and Johansson [36] found that school nurses in Northern Sweden frequently encountered stigma-related barriers when addressing childhood obesity. They reported feelings of frustration, powerlessness, and concern about providing unequal care due to obesity stigmatization, which often led them to avoid sensitive weight-related discussions. The present study similarly demonstrates how social stigma and cultural sensitivities contribute to avoidance behaviors among school nurses, who may choose silence to maintain harmony with parents and students. In line with this, Cho et al. [37] reported that Korean school nurses faced deep-rooted cultural stigma surrounding obesity and poverty, heightening their sensitivity and discouraging open discussions about weight with students and families. Such stigma-driven avoidance behaviors closely mirror those described by participants in this study, suggesting that cultural context strongly shapes nurses’ comfort level in addressing obesity-related issues.

Consistent with the Competing Priorities category, Tracey and Reid [38] emphasized that excessive workloads and competing clinical responsibilities often limit school nurses’ ability to focus on preventive initiatives such as obesity management. Their scoping review revealed that acute care demands, chronic condition management, and administrative duties frequently take precedence over long-term health promotion activities, positioning obesity prevention as a secondary or optional component of school nursing practice. This aligns with the current study’s findings, in which school nurses prioritized urgent health concerns, such as diabetes and asthma management, over preventive health education due to limited time and staffing.

The core category, Lacking Support, reflects the systemic and organizational barriers that constrain school nurses’ ability to implement obesity-prevention strategies effectively. Similar findings have been reported internationally. Saleh et al. [39] observed that in the United Kingdom, inadequate staffing and limited funding hindered school nurses’ capacity to deliver comprehensive preventive programs. Likewise, Bakarman et al. [40] found that U.S. counties with lower nurse-to-student ratios and reduced school health budgets had higher rates of childhood obesity. These results converge with the present study’s conclusion that insufficient institutional support, policy gaps, and shortages of both human and material resources represent major barriers to effective school-based obesity prevention efforts.

To overcome these hurdles, participants in this study emphasized the need for strong backing from healthcare decision-makers in Saudi Arabia. This support should encompass policies for BMI assessment, comprehensive obesity prevention initiatives, referral systems, and expanded staffing, as well as the establishment of a curriculum that integrates obesity control, nutrition, and physical activity while fostering cooperation between nurses and families. Participants also suggested that clear administrative endorsement and resource allocation could help institutionalize obesity prevention as a routine part of school health practice, rather than an optional initiative. Increasing staffing levels, improving healthcare regulations, and fostering cooperation with parents would enable nurses to provide more comprehensive care within schools and assume a broader scope of duties. For instance, additional personnel would free up time, allowing nurses to conduct screenings for long-term health conditions and deliver health-related education. Extending referral authority would further support obesity prevention and facilitate student access to appropriate healthcare providers. This aligns with the recommendations of the National Association of School Nurses, which emphasize that effective school-based obesity prevention requires adequate policies, sufficient resources, and institutional support [26].

Role theory explores how individuals fulfill role expectations within a particular social context [41]. It asserts that roles exist in all interactions or institutions and are associated with expected behaviors. The theory incorporates constructs such as role strain, role overload, role ambiguity, and role stress. Role strain refers to the personal difficulty in meeting assigned expectations and can arise from overload or uncertainty. Role overload occurs when responsibilities are challenging to meet due to limited time or insufficient resources. This framework provides a valuable lens for interpreting the findings of this study.

A clear connection can be drawn between role theory and the emerging substantive theory Jumping Hurdles: A Pathway to Professional Growth. Within the Struggling category, nurses encountered heavy workloads that hindered their ability to address obesity, reflecting elements of role overload. The Avoiding category highlighted partial withdrawal from professional responsibilities as a means of reducing stress and conflict, which corresponds with aspects of role theory. Ambiguity was also evident, as nurses described the absence of clear processes or protocols for obesity-related practices. The interaction between overload, ambiguity, and insufficient guidance produced cumulative role strain, restricting their ability to engage fully in obesity prevention. In the Competing Priorities category, nurses reported facing substantial demands that produced both overload and strain, thereby limiting their engagement in preventive work. The core category, Lacking Support, encompassed all categories and was linked to ambiguity, overload, strain, and stress. Missing referral guidelines and insufficient obesity-related standards illustrated ambiguity, while shortages in staffing reflected overload. These combined pressures culminated in stress, largely attributed to inadequate systemic backing from the healthcare infrastructure, particularly from policymakers responsible for school health in Saudi Arabia. Thus, the “Jumping Hurdles” model not only reflects the professional challenges of school nurses but also extends role theory by illustrating how institutional barriers and lack of support shape role performance within a specific cultural and educational context.

Implications

This research underscores the essential role of healthcare authorities in supporting initiatives to prevent pediatric obesity within school settings. The core category of the developed theoretical model revealed that insufficient backing from policymakers represents a major obstacle. Participants pointed to critical gaps such as limited referral mechanisms, the absence of structured obesity-prevention guidelines, shortages of school nurses, and inadequately resourced school clinics. Addressing these challenges requires the creation and enforcement of policies that incorporate BMI monitoring, well-defined referral pathways for students with obesity, and sustained funding to address workforce shortages by ensuring adequate placement of school nurses across primary schools in Saudi Arabia.

Additionally, the findings emphasize the need for policies that provide both human and material resources to support obesity prevention initiatives. In line with the leadership principle of the Framework for 21st Century School Nursing Practice, which calls for school nurses to advocate for policy changes that impact children’s health, nurses in this study stressed the necessity for policies that also incorporate educational strategies [26]. These include curriculum components aimed at increasing awareness among families, learners, and school personnel about healthy nutrition, physical activity, and weight stigma, while also emphasizing the joint involvement of parents and school nurses in obesity prevention. Ultimately, prioritizing these policy and educational actions is essential to empower school nurses and create supportive environments that advance pediatric obesity prevention efforts.

The findings of this study carry significant implications for school nurses and school health practice, particularly within primary schools. By drawing attention to key factors that shape school nurses’ involvement in tackling pediatric obesity, this study provides a foundation for developing effective strategies to strengthen their engagement in this area. Nurses in Saudi Arabia could utilize these insights to act as change agents, promoting policies and practices that safeguard children’s health and directly target obesity prevention. The substantive theoretical model generated from this study can help school nurses gain a clearer understanding of the challenges impacting their work, ultimately guiding initiatives to expand their contribution to pediatric obesity prevention. Moreover, these findings can help increase understanding among parents, students, educators, and health personnel regarding the vital role of school nurses in addressing obesity, while also shedding light on the barriers that may hinder their effectiveness.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The relatively small sample size may limit the transferability of the findings, and the focus on private primary schools may further restrict their transferability to other school contexts. Moreover, the cultural and geographic homogeneity of the sample may reduce the transferability of the findings to other regions or international contexts. Finally, as the study concentrated solely on primary school settings, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of school nurses who work with adolescents or older students.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the barriers that hinder school nurses’ involvement in pediatric obesity prevention, emphasizing the crucial role of policymakers in creating supportive school environments. By identifying systemic, organizational, and interpersonal challenges, the study highlights how insufficient institutional support, heavy workloads, and cultural sensitivities shape school nurses’ professional engagement in obesity prevention. The developed substantive theory, Jumping Hurdles: A Pathway to Professional Growth, offers a theoretical framework that can guide efforts to strengthen nurses’ roles in promoting child health within school settings. These findings hold broader implications for school nursing and public health, highlighting the need for policies that ensure adequate staffing, structured referral systems, and inclusion of obesity-prevention education in school curricula. Strengthening collaboration among policymakers, educators, parents, and healthcare professionals can enhance the effectiveness of school-based health promotion initiatives. Future research should build on this model by evaluating targeted interventions that empower school nurses, examining implementation strategies across public schools, and exploring how similar approaches may be adapted in other Middle Eastern and global contexts. Expanding this line of inquiry will help develop evidence-based policies that address pediatric obesity comprehensively while enhancing the capacity of school nurses as frontline public health practitioners.
