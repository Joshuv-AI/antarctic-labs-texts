Glucose-driven intra- and inter-islet beta cell synchronization in pancreatic tissue slices
Authors: Lidija Križančić Bombek, Nika Polšak, Jurij Dolenšek, Andraž Stožer, Marko Gosak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coordinated insulin secretion by the numerous islets dispersed throughout the pancreas generates the characteristic pulsatility of circulating insulin, which optimizes peripheral insulin action. While intra-islet communication is well established, the mechanisms supporting inter-islet coordination remain unclear. We investigated how different glucose stimulation patterns influence beta cell calcium dynamics across neighboring islets using multicellular calcium imaging in acute pancreatic tissue slices from NMRI mice, each containing two spatially separated islets. We compared islet responses under constant glucose stimulation and under periodic glucose pulses at either low amplitude or high amplitude. Fast, electrically driven oscillations showed no inter-islet alignment under any protocol but consistently exhibited strong coherence within each islet’s beta cell syncytium. The slow, metabolically driven component showed poor inter-islet coordination during constant glucose stimulation and under low-amplitude pulsations centered near 10 mM, but displayed partial alignment with low-amplitude pulses near the physiological range (8 mM). Robust inter-islet synchronization emerged only under high-amplitude glucose oscillations. These findings demonstrate that periodic glucose input can entrain slow β-cell rhythms across islets. However, because physiological glucose fluctuations are modest, such entrainment is unlikely to fully explain inter-islet coordination in vivo, suggesting that additional mechanisms support coherent, pulsatile insulin release at the whole-organ level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46512-y.

Introduction

The pancreatic islets of Langerhans are integral micro-organs responsible for nutrient homeostasis, primarily through the orchestrated secretion of insulin by beta cells1. Synchronized insulin release from many islets of Langerhans in the pancreas results in oscillatory insulin levels in the blood. Recent research has revealed that the regulation of insulin release is far more complex than previously understood and is highly dependent on intercellular communication both within and potentially between islets2–4.

The physiological importance of insulin oscillations lies in their effect on the sensitivity of peripheral tissues. Pulsatile insulin delivery enhances insulin action in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver compared with continuous exposure, thereby supporting glucose homeostasis and helping to prevent insulin resistance5. Loss of pulsatility disrupts this balance and contributes to pathological states such as type 2 diabetes6. Changes in the pattern of insulin oscillations, described as “secondary bursts” or “slower secondary oscillations,” have been observed under conditions of delayed feedback within the glucose–insulin regulatory loop. These changes appear as episodic clustering of insulin secretory bursts into slower oscillatory modes, representing an alternative or compensatory rhythm when normal islet synchronization is disrupted7,8. Such altered oscillations highlight the complex regulation of beta cell activity in diabetes and may contribute to the progressive decline in insulin efficacy due to reduced peripheral sensitivity9.

For effective oscillatory insulin secretion, the islets of the entire pancreas must operate in concert. While intra-islet communication is well established10–12, the mechanisms responsible for inter-islet communication remain far less clear. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the mechanisms underlying the synchronization of islets throughout the entire pancreas. Current evidence suggests that systemic mediators, including autonomic inputs, circulating hormones, cytokines, and metabolites, may be potential drivers of inter-islet synchrony in the absence of direct anatomical coupling13–16. A modeling study further suggested that cholinergic signaling via intrapancreatic ganglia could help align islet oscillations in vivo17. Additionally, circulating factors—including incretins, cytokines, and potentially extracellular vesicles—may facilitate a more global level of inter-islet communication, ensuring harmonized function throughout the pancreas14,18.

Another proposed mechanism for coordinating the activity of insulin-secreting beta cells across the pancreas relies on the feedback loop between pancreatic insulin secretion and hepatic glucose output2,3,19. This concept requires coordination at two organizational levels: synchronization of beta cells within individual islets (intra-islet), and among different islets distributed throughout the pancreas (inter-islet). Both are essential for producing coherent and detectable oscillations in plasma insulin, as observed in vivo. Furthermore, mathematical modelling studies first demonstrated that heterogeneous islets, each with slightly different natural oscillation periods, can become synchronized when exposed to a periodic glucose drive; in these models the liver–pancreas feedback loop was sufficient to align the phase and period of the whole population2,17,19. Experimental work in vivo and in isolated islets supported this concept by showing that oscillations in plasma glucose, often with periods of 5–15 min, appear in phase with insulin pulses in healthy humans and can influence islet activity20–22. More recently, microfluidic approaches have provided direct evidence: when single islets or groups of isolated islets were exposed to precisely controlled, periodic glucose waveforms, their intracellular Ca2⁺ oscillations—serving as a proxy for insulin secretion—locked to the external signal by adjusting both phase and period3,23–25. These studies established that islets can be forced into collective rhythmicity by an external oscillatory glucose cue despite displaying heterogeneity in natural frequencies. Notably, the capacity for such entrainment is recognized as a hallmark of healthy beta cell function, whereas loss of entrainability is regarded as an early sign of beta cell dysfunction in type 2 diabetes20,21,26.

Acute pancreatic tissue slices preserve the native cytoarchitecture and local microenvironment, providing a near-physiological setting in which beta cells retain their paracrine context and electrical coupling27,28. In contrast to isolated islets, in slices we routinely observe multiscale Ca2⁺ dynamics: a slow, metabolically driven component with periods of ~ 3–6 min29, a fast, electrically driven component with periods of seconds to tens of seconds27, and, recently, an ultrafast component with periods around ≈100 ms30. By preserving native architecture and revealing the full spectrum of Ca2⁺ oscillatory modes, the tissue-slice technique offers a unique opportunity to explore how these beta cell rhythms unfold into complex spatiotemporal patterns of multicellular activity within islets12,31. Remarkably, in individual tissue slices, it is sometimes possible to observe two spatially separated islets within a single optical field, allowing for the simultaneous, single-cell-resolved recording of their activity under identical stimulation conditions. We leveraged this configuration to probe inter-islet synchronization. Specifically, we compared constant glucose stimulation (8 mM and 10 mM) with pulsatile protocols delivered in 6-min cycles (3 min low/3 min high) at low amplitude (7.5–8.5 mM; 9.5–10.5 mM) and high amplitude (6–10 mM; 8–12 mM), centered around 8 mM and 10 mM. For each condition, we separated the fast and slow oscillatory Ca2⁺ components, quantified intra- and inter-islet coordination using coactivity metrics, and phase-locking between islet-averaged slow signals. This approach enabled us to test whether and to what extent oscillations align between neighboring islets and whether periodic glucose forcing can, in this near-native setting, entrain and synchronize their activity.

Material and methods

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in strict compliance with all national and European guidelines regarding laboratory animal care (Directive 63/2010/EU; PREPARE and ARRIVE guidelines), and every effort was made to enhance their welfare and minimize suffering32,33. The experimental protocol was approved by the Administration of the Republic of Slovenia for Food Safety, Veterinary Sector and Plant Protection (grant No.: U34401-12/2018/2 and U34401-3/2025/6).

Animals, tissue slice preparation, and dye loading

The experiments were performed using 12 adult NMRI mice (3 females, 9 males), aged 16–34 weeks. The animals were housed in groups of 2–4 in individually ventilated cages (Allentown LLC, USA; bedding: Mucedola Scobis Due, Italy) at a room temperature of 22–24 °C and a relative humidity of 45–65%, with a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice had ad libitum access to water and standard chow (Ssniff, Soest, Germany). Mice were euthanized by gradual-fill CO2 exposure, with 100% CO2 introduced into the chamber at a rate of approximately 20–30% of the chamber volume per minute until loss of consciousness, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, after which the abdominal cavity was accessed via laparotomy.

Fresh pancreatic tissue slices were prepared as described previously27,34,35. Briefly, after euthanasia, the pancreas was injected via the common bile duct with 1.9% low-melting-point agarose (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) prepared in an extracellular solution (ECS-6) containing (in mM) 125 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 6 glucose, 6 lactic acid, 3 myo-inositol, 2.5 KCl, 2 Na-pyruvate, 2 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 1 MgCl2, 0.5 ascorbic acid). The injected pancreas was immediately cooled with ice-cold ECS-6, constantly bubbled with a gas mixture consisting of 5% CO2 and 95% O2 to maintain proper pH, extracted from the abdominal cavity, dissected into 3–5 mm3 cubes and embedded into agarose blocks which were then cut into 140 µm thick slices using a vibratome (VT 1000 S, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). During slicing, tissue was continuously maintained in ice-cold ECS-6, minimizing potential accumulation of proteolytic enzymes released from damaged exocrine tissue, which can become problematic in static incubation protocols36.

Only slices with at least two closely positioned islets of Langerhans were selected for imaging. Because islets are rarely located within the same focal plane in a given tissue slice, they may occasionally appear to overlap in two-dimensional projections. To verify that the imaged islets were spatially distinct, the slice was examined under a stereomicroscope while being rotated along multiple axes. When required, confocal z-stack imaging was performed to confirm their three-dimensional separation.

For calcium dye loading, the slices were incubated in 3.33 ml of HEPES-buffered saline containing (in mM) 6 glucose, 150 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, and 1 MgCl2, pH adjusted to 7.4 with 1 M NaOH, and supplemented with 3.75 µl of dimethylsulfoxide, 1.25 µl of Pluronic F-127, and either 25 µg of Oregon Green 488 BAPTA-1 (OGB-1; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) or 25 µg of Calbryte 520 AM (AAT Bioquest, Pleasanton, CA, USA). The incubation was performed for 50 min on an orbital shaker at 50 rpm. After staining, slices were stored in HEPES-buffered saline at room temperature. Unless specified otherwise, all chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Calcium imaging

Imaging was conducted on an upright confocal microscope Leica TCS SP5 AOBS Tandem II, equipped with a 20 × HCX APO L water immersion objective (NA 1.0). The imaging depth was set at 2–3 cell layers below the tissue slice surface to avoid capturing superficial cells that may have been damaged during slicing. The optical imaging thickness was approximately 5 μm, with the laser power adjusted to achieve an optimal balance between photobleaching and signal-to-noise ratio. Time series data were collected at a frequency of 5 Hz, with a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. The calcium-sensitive dye was excited with a 488 nm argon laser. The emitted fluorescence was captured by Leica HyD hybrid detectors (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) within the range of 500–700 nm, as previously described by our group27,35,37.

Stimulation protocols

After staining, individual slices were transferred to the recording chamber under the microscope. The chamber was continuously perifused with carbogen-bubbled extracellular solution (ECS) at 37 °C via peristaltic pumps set to a constant flow rate of 17 µl/s.

The temporal profile of the glucose concentration changes in the recording chamber was calculated using a one-compartment mixing model (Supplemental Fig. 1). The chamber was treated as a well-mixed volume V 1ml, continuously perifused at a constant flow rate φ (17 µl/s). Both the V and φ were experimentally verified to reflect the recording setup. The inflow solution alternated between two concentrations, corresponding to stimulation and inter-stimulation periods. The timing of these periods was represented as a binary sequence defined by the duration of each stimulus, the interstimulus interval, and the total number of stimulation cycles. The concentration inside the chamber, C(t), and the inflowing concentration, Cin(t) were related through a mass balance:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}C(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\varphi}{V} \left( {C_{in}\left( t \right) - C\left( t \right)} \right)$$\end{document}

This differential equation was solved numerically using a fixed time step, starting from the baseline concentration present before stimulation. The resulting concentration profiles reflect the dynamic equilibration between the inflowing solution and the well-mixed chamber volume and were used to quantify the expected concentration experienced during each stimulation cycle.

For the constant-stimulation experiments, basal calcium activity was first recorded in ECS-6. The solution was then switched to a stimulatory ECS containing either 8 mM or 10 mM glucose (ECS-8 or ECS-10) for ~ 80 min, after which ECS-6 was reintroduced and imaging continued until beta cell activity ceased. We obtained three recordings for the constant-8 protocol (2 female and 1 male mice; 6 islets; 573 cells) and six recordings for the constant-10 protocol (1 female and 2 male mice; 12 islets; 1265 cells).

For oscillatory stimulation, we applied two small-step protocols (1 mM amplitude: 7.5 → 8.5 mM and 9.5 → 10.5 mM) and two large-step protocols (4 mM amplitude: 6 → 10 mM and 8 → 12 mM). In the 6 → 10 mM protocol, activity was recorded for ~ 10 min in 6 mM glucose, after which the ECS alternated between ECS-6 and ECS-10 every 3 min. A total of 15 cycles (3 min low glucose followed by 3 min high glucose) were delivered, yielding three recordings (3 male mice; 6 islets; 1,005 cells). For all remaining pulse protocols, basal activity was first recorded for ~ 10 min in 6 mM glucose before administering 15 cycles of 3-min low and 3-min high glucose according to the selected concentration pair. This produced five recordings for the 7.5 → 8.5 mM protocol (2 female and 3 male mice; 10 islets; 1,000 cells), four recordings for the 9.5 → 10.5 mM protocol (2 male mice; 8 islets; 911 cells), and five recordings for the 8 → 12 mM protocol (4 male mice; 10 islets; 1,837 cells).

Processing of recorded [Ca2+]i traces: extraction and binarization of the fast and slow oscillatory activity

Fluorescence signals from Calbryte 520 AM, representing time series for manually selected regions of interest (ROIs)—each corresponding to an individual beta cell—were exported with their spatial coordinates using custom software, ImageFiltering (copyright Denis Špelič). Time series containing substantial artifacts, low signal-to-noise ratios, non-responsive dynamics, or patterns inconsistent with beta cell activity were excluded after visual inspection. To extract the fast oscillatory component, the signals were processed with band-pass filtering via a zero-lag filter to isolate the fast oscillatory activity, typically with cut-off frequencies set to 0.05 and 2.0 Hz, and then binarized by setting values to 1 (active state) for periods of increased [Ca2+]i signals or 0 (inactivity) for periods of low-amplitude signals.

To extract the slow oscillatory component, we applied a sequential adjacent-averaging procedure. By using a relatively broad window size, Ts, and performing multiple rounds of sequential smoothing, we effectively isolated the envelope of the recorded [Ca2+]i signal, which represents the metabolically driven slow oscillatory component. Formally, this process is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s^{\left( k \right)} \left( t \right) = \frac{1}{N}\mathop \sum \limits_{{i = - T_{s} /2}}^{{T_{s} /2}} s^{{\left( {k - 1} \right)}} \left( {t + i} \right),$$\end{document}where N is the number of data points within the window Ts, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}^{\left(k\right)}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} represents the smoothed signal after the k-th iteration, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}^{\left(0\right)}\left(t\right)=f(t)$$\end{document} is the filtered input signal, and k = 1…m, where m is the total number of smoothing procedures. In our analysis, Ts was typically set between 90 and 180 s, and the procedure was iterated 3–6 times, depending on the temporal characteristics of the dataset.

The slow component of the [Ca2+]i signal was then binarized by defining the active phase as the period of calcium rise. Specifically, all local minima and maxima in time series were identified along with the midpoints between each pair of adjacent minima and maxima. The intervals between the midpoints and the corresponding maxima were set to 1, whilst the remaining portions of the signals were set to 0. Additionally, in detecting local minima and maxima, a prominence criterion was applied to exclude minor, insignificant calcium fluctuations. Evaluating synchronicity in [Ca2+]i activity

To assess intra- as well as inter-islet synchronicity of [Ca2+]i activity between individual cells, we employed coactivity analysis based on binarized traces of both the fast and the slow oscillatory components. The coactivity coefficients Cij were then determined for each cell pair:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{ij}^{{\left( {fast,slow} \right)}} = \frac{{\sum \left( {b_{i}^{{\left( {fast,slow} \right)}} b_{j}^{{\left( {fast,slow} \right)}} } \right)}}{{\sqrt {\sum b_{i}^{{\left( {fast,slow} \right)}} \sum b_{j}^{{\left( {fast,slow} \right)}} } }},$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{i}^{(fast,slow)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{j}^{(fast,slow)}$$\end{document} are the binarized fast and slow activity time series of cells i and j, respectively. This measure gives a value of 0 if there is no overlap in activity and a value of 1 if the cells are completely coactive. Based on the calculation of coactivity coefficients between all cell pairs, we constructed the coactivity matrix. To evaluate intra-islet vs. inter-islet synchronicity, we pooled all coactivity coefficients corresponding to cell pairs within the same islet (either islet 1 or islet 2) into one group. We grouped separately those coefficients that reflect coactivity between cells located in different islets. Furthermore, the coactivity matrices served as a basis for designing functional beta cell networks12,31,38, based on which we visualized how synchronous activity was distributed across the tissue. They were designed using the variable threshold (or fixed average node degree) approach, where the threshold was dynamically adjusted to ensure that the average number of connections per cell in each tissue slice equaled a predefined value (i.e., kavg = 6).

Phase synchronization of slow oscillatory components between distinct islets

To assess the degree of phase coordination in the slow component of calcium dynamics between two spatially distinct pancreatic islets located within the same tissue section, we computed the phase-locking value (PLV) between the respective average Ca2⁺ traces. The time series data were first normalized to the [0,1] interval, and peaks were identified using a prominence-based detection method. From the sequence of detected maxima, we constructed a continuous piecewise-linear phase function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi \left(t\right)$$\end{document} for each islet, in which the phase increased linearly from 0 to 2π between successive peaks. Specifically, for each interval [ti, ti+1] between consecutive maxima, the phase was defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi \left( t \right) = 2\pi \frac{{t - t_{i} }}{{t_{i + 1} - t_{i} }}, t \in \left[ {t_{i} ,t_{i + 1} } \right].$$\end{document}

We considered only the time points with both phase traces well-defined. The phase difference \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi \left(t\right)={\phi }_{1}\left(t\right)-{\phi }_{2}(t)$$\end{document} was then used to compute the PLV, defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PLV = \left| {\mathop \sum \limits_{t} e^{i\Delta \phi \left( t \right)} } \right|,$$\end{document}which quantifies the consistency of the phase relationship between the two signals over time, with values ranging from 0 (no phase locking) to 1 (perfect synchronization). This measure provides a robust estimate of event-based phase synchrony, capturing temporal alignment of slow oscillatory activity under different experimental protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to support the quantitative assessment of intra- and inter-islet coordination across stimulation protocols. Group-level comparisons were carried out using non-parametric tests, and effect sizes were quantified using Cliff’s delta as a measure of the magnitude of differences between conditions. Effect size was emphasized as a biologically meaningful assessment of coordination strength, complementing formal hypothesis testing. Full details of the statistical analyses are provided in Supplemental Text S1.

Results

To investigate how different glucose environments shape both intra- and inter-islet calcium dynamics, we performed multicellular calcium imaging in pancreatic tissue slices containing at least two spatially separated islets. This unique experimental design allowed us to monitor and analyze the simultaneous activity of hundreds of beta cells within each islet, while directly assessing potential coordination between neighboring islets exposed to the same extracellular milieu. We systematically applied a series of glucose stimulation protocols, all initiated from a sub-stimulatory baseline of 6 mM glucose, followed by either constant stimulation at 8 mM or 10 mM glucose, or by pulsatile stimulation with oscillatory glucose waveforms of 7.5–8.5 mM, 9.5–10.5 mM, 6–10 mM, and 8–12 mM, applied in 6-min periods consisting of 3 min at the lower and 3 min at the higher glucose level. Emphasis was placed on distinguishing between the electrically driven fast calcium oscillations and the slower, metabolically driven component, as these reflect complementary layers of beta cell coordination. In the following sections, we first visualize representative examples of two neighboring islets for selected stimulation protocols, while quantitative analyses are performed across the whole dataset comprising all recorded islets.

We first visualized and assessed the responses of two morphologically distinct islets located within the same pancreatic tissue slice (Fig. 2a) during stimulation with a constant 10 mM glucose concentration. Average calcium traces and raster plots revealed that the activity of the two islets within the same tissue slice was largely uncoordinated under constant glucose stimulation (Fig. 2b–d). The fast oscillatory component was entirely out of phase between islets, exhibiting substantial variability in both oscillation frequency and burst duration. These observations were corroborated by coactivity analysis. The coactivity matrix for the fast component (Fig. 2e) displayed two distinct blocks of high synchrony, corresponding to cell pairs within each islet. In contrast, inter-islet cell pairs showed markedly lower coactivity. A similar pattern was observed for the slow component (Fig. 2f), although the contrast between intra- and inter-islet coactivity was less pronounced, and overall synchrony was lower. Functional connectivity networks reflected the same separation: in the fast component, connections were confined almost exclusively within each islet, whereas in the slow component, only a minimal fraction of inter-islet links was detected—likely arising from incidental overlap of individual cell signals rather than genuine functional coupling. Similar results were obtained using a constant 8 mM glucose stimulation protocol (quantitative data shown later). Taken together, these results highlight that, under constant glucose stimulation, each islet appears to follow its own intrinsic rhythm rather than engaging in collective, tissue-wide coordination.

For the fast component, strong intra-islet synchrony and low inter-islet synchrony were consistently observed across all pulsatile protocols (Figs. 3e, 4e, and 5e). For the slow component, periodic stimulation enhanced inter-islet coactivity in a manner that depended on both the amplitude and the mean glucose level: low-amplitude pulsation around 10 mM failed to induce inter-islet coordination (Fig. 3f), whereas low-amplitude pulsation around ~ 8 mM resulted in partial coordination (Fig. 5f), and high-amplitude pulsation around 10 mM produced rather robust inter-islet synchonicity (Fig. 4f).

The corresponding functional connectivity networks reflect the same trend. For the fast component, connections were consistently confined to cells within each individual islet, with no inter-islet links detected under any protocol—consistent with the lack of phase alignment in the rapid, electrically driven oscillations. In contrast, the slow component exhibited protocol-dependent emergence of inter-islet links: under low-amplitude glucose pulsation centered around 10 mM (9.5–10.5 mM), functional connections remained restricted to intra-islet networks, whereas both the high-amplitude protocol (8–12 mM) and the low-amplitude protocol centered around a more physiological glucose level (7.5–8.5 mM) elicited the appearance of inter-islet links, indicating coordination between the slow calcium oscillations of neighboring islets. Together, these results indicate that periodic modulation of the glucose milieu can promote cross-islet alignment of metabolically driven dynamics, with both the amplitude and the absolute glucose level shaping the degree of inter-islet synchronization, even while the fast electrical oscillations remain locally confined.

A distinct pattern emerged for the slow component. Periodic glucose stimulation consistently enhanced intra-islet synchrony, regardless of the amplitude or baseline level of oscillations, indicating that rhythmic input promotes more coherent metabolically driven activity within each islet. The effect on inter-islet synchrony was strongly protocol-dependent. Low-amplitude stimulation centered around 10 mM glucose (9.5–10.5 mM protocol) failed to elevate inter-islet coactivity compared to constant stimulation. Interestingly, the low-amplitude protocol centered around a more physiological baseline of ~ 8 mM (7.5–8.5 mM protocol) significantly increased inter-islet coactivity. As expected, high-amplitude stimulation (8–12 mM and 6–10 mM protocols) induced the most pronounced rise in inter-islet synchrony, with the largest effect observed for the 6–10 mM protocol.

Phase synchronization analysis of the slow component corroborated these findings (Fig. 6c). Under constant stimulation (8 mM or 10 mM) and under the low-amplitude 9.5–10.5 mM protocol, phase locking between the two islets remained weak. Partial improvement was observed with the 7.5–8.5 mM protocol, whereas high-amplitude oscillations led to a substantial increase in phase synchronization, peaking in the 6–10 mM protocol, which produced robust inter-islet phase alignment. Collectively, these results demonstrate that periodic glucose modulations reliably enhance intra-islet synchrony, whereas inter-islet synchronization is more sensitive to stimulation amplitude and baseline glucose level. The requirement for relatively large oscillatory excursions to achieve strong inter-islet coordination suggests that additional regulatory mechanisms, potentially paracrine or neural signals, likely contribute to inter-islet communication beyond direct glucose entrainment.

To statistically substantiate the observed differences between stimulation protocols, we performed systematic comparisons of coactivity and phase synchronization measures across all conditions. Given the heterogeneous structure of the data, we deliberately emphasized effect size over formal significance testing in the interpretation of results. For coactivity measures, the very large number of cell pairs leads to extremely small p-values even for modest differences, whereas for phase synchronization the number of independent observations is necessarily limited (typically 3–6 recordings per condition), rendering hypothesis testing statistically marginal. Accordingly, effect size was used as the primary indicator of biologically meaningful differences, with full statistical results provided in the Supplemental text S1. Importantly, the statistical analysis consistently supported the qualitative patterns described above: fast oscillatory activity remained strongly synchronized within islets but showed negligible inter-islet coordination across all protocols, whereas slow oscillatory synchrony exhibited a clear and protocol-dependent enhancement, with the largest inter-islet effects observed under high-amplitude glucose oscillations and intermediate effects under low-amplitude stimulation centered around physiological glucose levels. Notably, coordination metrics did not differ systematically between recordings derived from the same mouse and those obtained from different animals, nor did we observe systematic differences between sexes in this dataset, indicating that the observed effects were not driven by animal-specific factors.

Discussion

Pulsatile insulin secretion is one of the most striking examples of how collective processes, operating at cellular and tissue scales, converge to orchestrate a vital systemic function. In this case, the coordinated dynamics of thousands of beta cell clusters distributed throughout the pancreas give rise to rhythmic hormone release that shapes the oscillatory profile of plasma insulin, a pattern crucial for maintaining peripheral insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis9,40–45. Yet, how the activity of the dispersed islet population becomes synchronized to produce coherent oscillations in circulating insulin remains incompletely understood. To address this question, we focused on the synchrony between neighboring islets located in the same pancreatic tissue slice. This experimental approach preserves the native cytoarchitecture and, at the same time, provides access to Ca2⁺ oscillations across multiple temporal scales—from slow, metabolically driven rhythms that correspond in period with insulin pulsatility, to fast electrically driven bursts that show glucose-dependence of their active time and determine the amplitude of insulin oscillations40. Together with the single-cell spatial resolution, these features make the tissue slice preparation a powerful platform for probing how local multicellular dynamics emerge within islets and how they may extend to inter-islet coordination.

Under constant stimulatory glucose, we observed that two islets within the same tissue slice operated independently. Fast, electrically driven calcium oscillations remained tightly synchronized within each islet but showed no alignment between islets. This finding is consistent with in vivo observations and the established view that fast beta cell oscillations are mediated by gap-junction-driven intercellular waves, which propagate efficiently within an islet but do not extend beyond its boundaries. Similarly, the slow, metabolically driven component also lacked inter-islet coordination under static glucose conditions, suggesting that both oscillatory modes are confined to local intra-islet regulation during constant glucose conditions. However, when exposed to periodic glucose oscillations, the slow calcium component became progressively aligned between islets. Importantly, we optimized our glucose stimulation protocol to better mimic physiological conditions. By selecting an optimal combination of the extracellular solution volume in the recording chamber and the perifusion rate, we managed to transform the conventional square pulse protocol into a roughly sinusoidal glucose profile (Supplemental Fig. 1). This allowed us to simulate near-physiological glucose oscillations, which are known to occur in vivo and are probably due to feedback between insulin secretion and hepatic glucose production41,46–49.

The degree of inter-islet synchronization depended on both the amplitude and the average value of glucose oscillations. Low-amplitude glucose pulsations centered around 10 mM glucose concentration were ineffective, while oscillations near physiological glucose levels (~ 8 mM) produced moderate inter-islet coordination. On the other hand, high-amplitude glucose oscillations (6–10 mM and 8–12 mM) resulted in robust phase locking between neighboring islets. These findings indicate that although neighboring islets operate as independent oscillators, their slow metabolic rhythms remain susceptible to external entrainment. Because the two islets were verified to be spatially distinct, the observed phase alignment is most consistent with shared external cues (e.g., oscillatory glucose or diffusible mediators) rather than direct physical contact between islets. Moreover, the strength of this entrainment increases with the relative amplitude of glucose oscillations, offering a compelling mechanism for inter-islet synchronization under physiologically relevant conditions. Although inter-islet synchronization emerged under selected pulsatile protocols, we did not observe a consistent progressive strengthening of coactivity over successive pulses, indicating that entrainment does not appear to accumulate monotonically over time but rather fluctuates in a non-stationary manner across preparations.

Sex-dependent differences in β-cell physiology have been increasingly recognized in recent years, including differences in electrical activity, metabolic regulation, and glucose responsiveness reported primarily in C57BL/6 J mice50,51. In particular, female β cells exhibit higher firing frequency and a more depolarized resting membrane potential at moderate glucose concentrations, although exocytosis and the overall glucose dependence of activity appear comparable between sexes, and many differences become prominent mainly with age or metabolic stress. In the present study, both male and female NMRI mice were included to enhance biological generalizability, and no systematic sex-dependent differences were observed in inter-islet connectivity metrics. Importantly, our stimulation paradigms probed clearly separated glucose regimes centered around ~ 8 mM and ~ 10 mM glucose, corresponding to moderate and elevated stimulatory ranges. Across these regimes, inter-islet coordination patterns were robust and reproducible despite inter-individual and sex-related variability, suggesting that the main conclusions are unlikely to be driven by strain- or sex-specific effects. Nevertheless, a fully balanced, strain- and sex-stratified investigation of inter-islet network dynamics would be highly informative and represents an important direction for future work. In addition to sex-related considerations, the choice of mouse strain also warrants discussion. Although quantitative differences in intracellular calcium signaling characteristics have been reported between NMRI and C57BL/6 substrains, such as differences in activation kinetics and in the relative contribution of oscillation frequency versus duration to glucose encoding52. However, these variations do not translate into fundamental differences in multicellular activity patterns or intra-islet functional network organization53. Given that inter-islet synchronization emerges from collective metabolic entrainment rather than from strain-specific molecular features, we do not expect qualitative differences in inter-islet coordination mechanisms across healthy mouse strains. Importantly, NMRI mice do not carry the NNT mutation present in C57BL/6 J mice, which can affect mitochondrial function and insulin secretion, thereby reducing potential strain-specific metabolic confounders54–57. Together, these considerations support the suitability of the NMRI model used in the present study for investigating fundamental principles of inter-islet synchronization.

The persistence of strong intra-islet synchronization across all protocols, coupled with the selective emergence of inter-islet synchrony only under periodic stimulation, underscores the hierarchical organization of beta cell dynamics: fast electrical activity ensures local coordination within islets. At the same time, slower metabolic oscillations provide the substrate for long-range synchronization across islets. The amplitude-dependent entrainment observed highlights the nonlinear nature of glucose sensing and beta cell metabolism. When the average glucose is high (e.g., 10 mM), the glycolytic and mitochondrial pathways may already be operating near saturation, reducing the system’s responsiveness to further small fluctuations. In contrast, near physiological glucose levels in mice (e.g., 8 mM)52, modest oscillations are sufficient to modulate key rate-limiting enzymes and induce coordinated rhythmic behavior. This interpretation aligns with prior modeling and microfluidic studies, which show that the window of entrainment depends critically on both the amplitude and frequency of glucose oscillations2,3,24,25.

Our data support the hypothesis that rhythmic glucose fluctuations, arising from feedback between pancreatic insulin secretion and hepatic glucose production, may serve as a global synchronizing cue for the entire islet population under basal or near-fasting conditions2,3. In addition to ultradian variations in plasma insulin, rapid oscillations in plasma glucose with periods of about 5–15 min have been observed in healthy humans, often in synchrony with insulin pulses2,3,21. Following a meal, slower ultradian oscillations (periods of 50–150 min) also emerge, and faster components were seen superimposed on these as glucose and insulin levels return to baseline58. Our results suggest that physiological glucose excursions, which typically have amplitudes below 1 mM, are likely insufficient on their own to establish strong inter-islet phase alignment, implying that additional regulatory mechanisms must contribute to whole-pancreas synchronization. Potential candidates include autonomic innervation via intrapancreatic ganglia13,14, paracrine signals such as insulin and ATP released from beta cells, or endocrine mediators including incretins and cytokines18,59,60. These multimodal inputs may act in concert with oscillatory glucose to reinforce and stabilize the temporal coordination of islet activity at the organ level. Importantly, oscillatory [Ca2⁺]ᵢ activity is a conserved feature across species. In human islets glucose also elicits rhythmic fast and slow Ca2⁺ oscillations that can be synchronized across subregions via propagating waves and produce structured functional networks, but human islet Ca2⁺ dynamics tend to be more variable and spatially clustered, likely reflecting species-specific cytoarchitecture and unavoidable variability in human donor preparations61–65. In vivo, insulin secretion has been shown to be entrainable by pulsatile glucose infusion22, but in vitro, the frequency of insulin secretory pulses could not be entrained to external glucose pulses66. However, the period of glucose oscillations was almost exactly two-fold the period of intrinsic insulin oscillatory pulses and it is possible that the glucose oscillations induced a 2:1 entrainment in this case and further studies on human islets are needed to further investigate their entrainability2.

Disruption of these oscillatory feedback mechanisms has significant implications for the development and progression of diabetes. Mechanistically, the loss of coordinated glucose–insulin rhythms can lead to desynchronization among pancreatic islets, thereby diminishing the efficiency of pulsatile insulin secretion, which is essential for optimal hepatic insulin sensitivity40,67. In type 2 diabetes, chronic hyperglycemia may impair inter-islet synchronization and flatten insulin oscillations9,68. Additionally, since intra-islet coordination appears to be crucial for the appropriate control of insulin release within each individual islet and for determining the amplitude of insulin pulses, disrupted intra-islet synchronicity may further contribute to disrupted pulsatility in type 2 diabetes40,49,65. Clinically, reduced insulin pulsatility correlates with impaired glucose tolerance and increased hepatic insulin resistance, suggesting that temporal organization of insulin release is a key determinant of metabolic control41,69. Restoring or mimicking physiological glucose–insulin oscillations may therefore represent a therapeutic strategy to re-establish synchrony and improve glycemic stability in diabetes.

In our study, we focused on pairs of islets within pancreatic tissue slices to enable calcium oscillation measurements at the single-cell resolution. This approach was necessary because the spatial resolution of confocal microscopy at lower magnifications is insufficient for resolving individual cellular events. While this technique allows for high-resolution insights into intra- and intercellular calcium dynamics, it inherently limits our ability to extrapolate findings to the global dynamics of insulin secretion across the entire pancreas. A notable limitation of the tissue slice method is the absence of in vivo physiological conditions, particularly the disruption of vascular and neural components during slice preparation. These components are essential for hormonal and neural feedback mechanisms that modulate beta cell activity and insulin secretion. The autonomic nervous system, in particular, plays a pivotal role in coordinating islet function, with direct neural connections between the gut and pancreas influencing glucose metabolism and insulin secretion. Moreover, sympathetic, parasympathetic, and sensory innervation contribute to both endocrine differentiation and dynamic regulation of beta cell function70,71. Complementary approaches that preserve the vascular and neural context required for whole-organ coordination include islet imaging through a window72 and pancreas exteriorization73,74. While extremely informative, these techniques lack single cell resolution and are limited to imaging individual islets at best. Transplantation of islets into the anterior chamber of the eye provides another approach to monitor invidual beta cells in near in vivo conditions75,76. The great advantage of longitudinal temporal imaging is limited by the ability to control stimulatory conditions delivered to beta cells, exclusion of modern high-efficiency ester-based dyes and motion artefacts of the eye, and no single-best method is currently available. Moreover, intravital imaging across large numbers of pancreatic islets during a systemic glucose challenge has revealed pronounced heterogeneity in individual islet secretion profiles in vivo, despite comparatively homogeneous responses of isolated islets studied in culture74. This heterogeneity is plausibly shaped by differences in local perfusion, innervation, and endocrine/paracrine microenvironments, in addition to intrinsic islet-to-islet variability. Our slice based approach in conjunction with the control over environmental conditions was ideal for testing entrainability of intrinsic oscillators, but it does not reproduce (and therefore cannot adjudicate) the extent of in vivo heterogeneity in glucose delivery, neural tone, and perfusion that likely modulates both response amplitude and phase relationships between islets. Recent work combining pancreas tissue slices with high-throughput calcium activity labeling further underscores the value of this preparation for linking functional responses to spatial and structural features across large numbers of islets, including small islets that are often underrepresented in isolation-based approaches77.

On rare occasions, our experimental setup allowed visualization of up to three structurally intact islets within the same optical field. This rare configuration encompassing three islets within the slice is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2. These islets exhibited communication patterns consistent with those seen in islet pairs, with no detectable synchronization under constant stimulation, thereby reinforcing our observations and suggesting that the principles of inter-islet coordination may extend beyond isolated pairs. Future studies combining tissue-slice imaging with in vivo approaches, such as functional imaging of intact pancreatic lobes or integrative organ-level modelling, will be essential to fully elucidate how local, multicellular dynamics are integrated into the systemic regulation of insulin secretion. Additionally, testing the synchronizability of islets from diabetic mice and islets from human donors shall enable us to further establish its importance under diabetic conditions and its translational relevance.
