From stigma to strength: voices of women in Malawi after obstetric fistula repair
Authors: Jodie Bigalky, April Mackey, Annie Namathanga, Buddhini Samarasekara Dissanayakalage, Chloe Meppem, Margaret Moyo, Ennet Chipungu, Pammla Petrucka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstetric fistula (OF) is a significant issue in sub-Saharan Africa, where healthcare barriers and stigma delay treatment. Women with OF often experience prolonged isolation and face challenges reintegrating into their communities following surgical repair. Exploring their experiences is essential for improving programming, education, research, and policy. Therefore, the purpose of this qualitative study was to explore the experiences of young women (ages 13–29) who underwent OF repair and participated in community reintegration programs at a Fistula Care Centre in Malawi.MethodsEighteen participants were recruited from one fistula care center and completed semi-structured interviews in English or Chichewa. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed thematically using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework. NVivo software supported data coding and organization, and analysis was conducted collaboratively through regular meetings with four coding team members to discuss interpretations, resolve discrepancies, and refine themes. The full team reviewed and confirmed the data analysis.ResultsParticipants ranged in age from 19 to 28 years, with a mean age of 22 years. Most (94%) resided in rural areas. Over half (61%) developed an OF after their first pregnancy, and 78% experienced a stillbirth. The average duration living with OF before repair was 7 months, and the average time since repair was 12 months. Five themes emerged from the data analysis: (1) awareness, accessibility, and barriers to care; (2) stigma, segregation, and self-isolation; (3) loss and restoration of independence; (4) family and community support; and (5) building skills for community reintegration. Overall, participants reported low awareness and access barriers, stigma and social isolation, and loss of independence, with post-repair experiences marked by restored autonomy, community reintegration, and development of skills to support social and economic participation.ConclusionsFindings highlight the need for targeted awareness campaigns, expanded community ambassador programs, improved provider education, and sustained reintegration support. Ongoing evaluation is essential to ensuring responsive, inclusive care for women with OF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-025-00880-9.

Background

Maternal health disparities and adverse pregnancy outcomes are alarmingly high in low- and middle-income countries, which contribute to almost 95% of maternal deaths worldwide [1]. Additionally, in these settings, morbidity related to childbirth remains high, driven by limited access to quality obstetric care and timely interventions [2]. Obstetric fistula (OF) is a severe, often overlooked childbirth morbidity affecting nearly half a million women [3], caused by prolonged labor that creates an abnormal opening between the birth canal and nearby organs, resulting in uncontrolled leakage of urine and/or feces.

Women affected by OF often come from socioeconomically disadvantaged communities where access to healthcare, skilled medical professionals, and essential resources, such as transportation, is limited [4]. Structural determinants, including shortages of trained personnel, insufficient emergency obstetric capacity, delays in accessing timely caesarean sections, and under-resourced health systems, contribute to prolonged labor and the subsequent development of OF [5]. These systemic challenges intersect with social inequities, including poverty, early marriage, adolescent pregnancy, low literacy levels, restrictive cultural norms, and limited awareness of and access to contraception, further increasing the risk of OF [4, 6].

Some regions of the world are disproportionately affected by OF due to specific sociodemographic and cultural factors. Africa, in particular, has a high prevalence, with an estimated 45 million women lacking access to adequate maternal healthcare and more than 30 million giving birth outside of healthcare facilities [7]. Annual estimates of new OF cases in sub-Saharan Africa range from 30,000 to 130,000, though the true number is likely much higher due to stigma and underreporting [8].

In Malawi, one of the poorest countries in the world [9], OF is a significant public health challenge. Although the prevalence is estimated at 0.6%, this figure, similar to estimates in other parts of Africa, is likely a significant underrepresentation [10]. Since 2011, more than 20,000 women have received OF treatment in Malawi, with 5,000 of these cases treated at the center where this research took place, largely through efforts supported by the United Nations Population Fund [UNFPA] [10]. The UNFPA [10] in Malawi has focused on both prevention and treatment by strengthening emergency obstetric services and supporting education and funding to expand access to surgical care. However, despite this ongoing work, there remains a continued need for coordinated and comprehensive efforts to effectively address OF [10].

Research conducted within Malawi highlights the scope and complexity of the country’s fistula burden. Qualitative studies provide insight into the lived experiences of affected women, documenting profound social and emotional consequences, including stigma, isolation, and the reliance on coping strategies to manage incontinence [11]. Following surgical repair, women report improvements in quality of life, yet continue to face ongoing physical, social, and relational challenges that are either directly or indirectly related to OF [12]. Provider-focused studies additionally identify structural barriers to care, including shortages of trained surgeons, under-resourced facilities, and limited referral pathways [5]. More recent research emphasizes that reintegration after repair remains complex, with persistent challenges such as unsuccessful surgeries, reluctance to pursue future pregnancy, and deeply rooted socio-cultural beliefs that may undermine long-term recovery [13].

OF is more than a physical injury; it is often a profound trauma that disrupts social connections, leaving an impact that surgical repair alone cannot heal. Existing literature highlights that women living with OF are frequently subjected to bullying, criticism, rejection, shame, and isolation, experiences often compounded by widespread misconceptions and harmful cultural beliefs [13, 14]. In many African contexts, including Malawi, women with OF are ostracized, excluded from social, religious, and economic activities, and, in some cases, labeled as “cursed” [13, 14]. Even within their own households, spousal rejection is common, resulting in disrupted marriages, often driven by a lack of awareness about the condition and its prognosis [15–18]. Hence, efforts aimed at increasing access to surgical repair, while essential, are insufficient on their own. Without addressing the experiences and psychosocial needs of women after surgery, surgical interventions alone fall short of supporting women’s full recovery and reintegration into their communities.

To complement surgical intervention, community reintegration programs have emerged as essential components to post-fistula care. These initiatives recognize that healing is a holistic process, requiring a multisectoral, gender-sensitive, human rights-based approach [19]. Such programs typically offer psychosocial support, vocational training, literacy education, and community sensitization efforts to help women navigate recovery and reintegration [20, 21]. They aim to create safe spaces where women can regain their physical, emotional, and spiritual well-being.

The need for reintegration support is particularly critical for adolescents and young adults. OF disproportionately affects younger, often primiparous women, largely because adolescent pregnancy and obstructed labor are closely linked to OF formation, making this demographic particularly vulnerable [22, 23]. Global and regional reports also emphasize that many women affected by OF are in their mid-teens to early twenties [23, 24]. Targeted reintegration support following surgical repair can increase the likelihood that younger women resume educational, employment, family, and community roles. Early support is essential, as recovery during adolescence or early adulthood can shape long-term social and economic stability.

Despite a global campaign by UNFPA [20] to end OF and the implementation of comprehensive programs that provide surgical intervention and post-operative community reintegration, such programs are neither universally accessible nor consistently evaluated through empirical research. Moreover, the experiences of women who undergo surgical repair and community reintegration remain poorly understood, particularly among adolescent and younger women [23]. In Malawi, there is a critical need to better understand how young women experience repair and reintegration. As this population continues to increase, it is essential to explore their unique perspectives to inform more responsive programming, education, research, and policy, ultimately contributing to improved and age-sensitive outcomes. Therefore, the purpose of this research was to explore the experiences of adolescent and young women (ages 13–29) in Malawi who have undergone surgical repair and participated in community reintegration initiatives following OF.

Methods

This study used a qualitative research design to answer the following research question: What are the experiences of young women (ages 13–29) in Malawi who have undergone surgical repair and participated in community reintegration initiatives following OF? The qualitative approach guided the use of semi-structured interviews and a thematic analysis to gain insight and generate rich, meaningful findings that can be used to inform future programs, education, research, and policy. To guide this exploration, this study was informed by the Social Ecological Model (SEM), which considers multiple levels of influence on health outcomes, including individual, interpersonal, community, and societal factors [25]. Applying SEM allows for a structured examination of how personal experiences, family and social networks, healthcare systems, and broader environments interact to shape women’s experiences with obstetric fistula and its associated care.

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The research team consisted of three Canadian researchers with experience in global health and women’s health research, as well as one Malawian researcher familiar with the local context and healthcare system. Two team members were staff at the Fistula Care Centre, providing in-depth knowledge of the services and community, and two research assistants from Canada supported data analysis and presentation. The team was mindful of potential cross-cultural dynamics and power imbalances that could influence data collection and interpretation. Reflexivity was maintained through regular discussions among team members to critically examine assumptions, interpretations, and potential biases. Collaborative data analysis, including input from the Malawian researcher and Fistula Care Centre staff, helped ensure that findings were contextually grounded and reflective of participants’ perspectives.

Sampling and recruitment

Participants eligible for inclusion in this study were young women between the ages of 13 and 29 who had undergone OF repair at one Fistula Care Centre in Malawi, within the previous 3 years. It should be noted that women from Mozambique often travel to Malawi to access OF care because services are provided at no cost in Malawi, whereas care in Mozambique requires payment. Since these women were receiving treatment and post-repair services at the Fistula Care Centre in Malawi, the research team determined that they could be included in the study. Participants were required to be able to provide informed consent in either English or Chichewa.

Recruitment was carried out through both in-person and phone-based outreach. At the Fistula Care Centre, staff provided a general overview of the study to all women who had undergone OF repair and were currently attending post-fistula care programming (29 women). A recruitment script was read to the group, after which women who expressed interest were invited to meet individually with a member of the research team to receive more detailed information and ask questions. For women interested in participating, eligibility screening was conducted at the beginning of each interview. Women who did not meet the criteria, such as those who had not yet undergone OF repair, were excluded. Of the nine women screened in-person, eight were eligible and participated.

For women who were no longer receiving care at the Fistula Care Centre, a staff member contacted them by phone to explain the study. Women were contacted if contact information was available and they lived within a three-hour travel radius, making transportation to the interview feasible. The same recruitment script used for in-person recruitment was read to ensure consistency in the information provided. Eligibility screening was conducted over the phone to confirm each woman met criteria prior to traveling. In total, 36 women were contacted by phone until 10 eligible and interested women were identified, all of whom traveled to the Fistula Care Centre, where they were rescreened to confirm eligibility, and completed an interview.

Overall, 18 women participated in the study: eight recruited in-person and ten recruited through phone-based outreach.

Data collection

A semi-structured interview guide (see supplementary file) was used in all 18 interviews. The guide was developed based on the findings from a scoping review that identified key components of OF repair programs, as well as through consensus among members of the research team. Interviews began with demographic information and progressed to open-ended questions exploring the participants’ experiences accessing care, receiving treatment, and reintegrating into their communities following OF repair. In addition to serving as a guiding framework, the interview guide was used alongside active listening and follow-up probing questions to ensure participants’ experiences were fully captured and understood.

All interviews took place in a private room at the Fistula Care Centre. This location was selected because it provided a safe, familiar, and comfortable environment for the women. Given Malawi’s classification as a least developed country with high rates of poverty, identifying a secure and accessible alternative location outside the center would have been challenging. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and lasted approximately 30–60 min. Translation from the local language, Chichewa, to English was required for all interviews except one. Each interview was conducted by an English-speaking researcher, with simultaneous translation provided by a second researcher fluent in Chichewa. Following the interviews, the research team transcribed the English portions of the recordings verbatim for analysis. Data saturation was considered to have been reached after 15 interviews when the research team noted that participants’ responses were becoming repetitive, with similar experiences and perspectives reported across interviews. This suggested that no substantially new insights were emerging. To confirm this, three additional interviews were conducted, and no new variations were observed, supporting that data saturation had been reached.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was used in this study, guided by Braun and Clarke’s [26] six-phase framework. This approach provided a theoretically flexible yet structured method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns in the qualitative data. These phases include familiarization with data, data coding, developing initial themes, reviewing themes, refining, defining, and naming themes, and producing the report [26]. The analysis was conducted by the first and second authors and two research assistants (the fourth and fifth authors) and was reviewed by the remaining members of the research team.

Phase one began with familiarization of the data. The first, second, and fourth authors engaged in an in-depth exploration by reading and re-reading the interview transcripts, taking notes to develop a comprehensive understanding of the data and participant experiences [26]. In phase two, initial codes were generated using NVivo software by the fourth author [26]. Once all the data were coded, phase three involved a broader level of analysis in which the first, second, fourth, and fifth authors organized and collated the codes into broader potential themes [26]. These authors met regularly throughout the coding and theme development process to discuss the relationship between the codes and ensure thoughtful development of the emerging themes. In phase four, the emerging themes were further reviewed and refined by the same four authors. This process was carried out at two levels. First, at the level of coded extracts to ensure internal consistency and coherence within each theme; and second, at the level of the entire data set to verify that the themes accurately represented the data as a whole. During this process, themes were split, merged, or redefined as necessary to ensure that final themes were well supported [26]. Phase five involved refining, clearly identifying, and defining each theme to capture the specific aspects of the data they represented and to ensure little overlap between themes [26]. This phase was completed by the first and fifth authors. Lastly, in phase six, the themes were synthesized into a final narrative by the same two authors to report the experiences of women in Malawi who have undergone OF repair. Data extracts have been used to illustrate the complexities of living with an OF, undergoing surgical repair, and reintegrating into the community.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Saskatchewan Research Ethics Board (Beh-5057), the Kamuzu University of Health Sciences College of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (P.10/24-1165), and the National Health Sciences Research Committee at the Lilongwe District Health Office (number not assigned). Results

Demographics

Participants ranged in age from 19 to 28 years, with a mean age of 22. All participants, except one, were from rural villages: 77.8% were from villages in Malawi, 16.6% from villages in Mozambique, and 5.6% from a city. The majority of participants (61.1%) developed an OF after their first pregnancy. At the time of the study, approximately 55% of participants had returned to their communities following OF repair, while the remainder were still receiving in-patient care. Eight of the participants were married; one participant reported her spouse had left while she had the OF but returned after the repair. Four participants experienced divorce because of the OF, with one remarrying after repair. Three experienced divorce unrelated to the OF, and another three were single. The average duration participants lived with an OF prior to repair was 7 months (range 1–36 months). At the time of the interviews, the mean duration since OF repair was 12 months. The majority of participants (77.8%) experienced a stillbirth associated with the pregnancy that led to the OF.

Awareness, accessibility, and barriers to care

Awareness and accessibility. Awareness and accessibility are interconnected in the context of OF and their treatment. An individual’s ability to access care and services is influenced not only by the availability of those services, but also by their awareness of the condition itself. Many participants were not aware of what an OF was, with one woman stating, “I thought maybe that's what happens when people give birth” (P1). Another participant described the broader lack of awareness within her community when she stated, “Because the problem in my area is not known. It was like, what is this now?” (P3). Participants also highlighted gaps in knowledge and understanding among healthcare providers. Several women recounted seeking care for physical complications caused by OF, only to be dismissed or discharged without treatment. One participant shared, “I delivered, and the problem was discovered, but nothing was done.” (P17). Another recalled, “I observed that whenever I lie down, I was just leaking. I told the nurse it was a problem. She said it may resolve. But of course, in the end, I was discharged.” (P16). Living in remote communities seemed to further exacerbate these challenges. As one woman explained, “The unfortunate thing is that many of us are in the remote, remote areas. I tried, at one time, to go to a hospital and try to talk women around. And on that single day, I was able to identify about six women who had some relations in their villages who were suffering from this problem” (P10). A lack of awareness about where to seek help was identified. As one participant shared, “Some people... they don’t know where they can go for help” (P1). Another stated, “I wish they could know that there's a center like this one [Fistula Care Centre]” (P11).

Increasing awareness. Participants were only able to access care after learning that services, such as the Fistula Care Centre, exist. Participants reported learning about the center through a variety of sources, both formal and informal. Several recalled being introduced to the center through patient ambassadors, stating “I heard it from a patient ambassador” (P1), and “An ambassador came to my village” (P3). Patient ambassadors were described as former OF repair patients and community health workers who are formally trained to raise awareness about OF and available treatment options across communities in Malawi. Ambassadors also help connect women affected by OF with the Fistula Care Centre. While ambassadors were recognized as a promising resource to increase awareness, many participants reported no contact with them. As one participant shared, “I never talked to ambassadors” (P4). Despite the lack of contact with ambassadors, many participants expressed a strong personal commitment to sharing their own experiences and raising awareness after their repair. One participant shared, “What is most important is to take the message to others who are also suffering as I was, because some people, they are out there, they don't know where they can go for help, but if you come here [to the Fistula Care Centre], you do get the necessary assistance” (P1).

Participants also became aware of the center through other means, such as radio broadcasts, hospital referrals, or word of mouth from previous patients. For example, one participant explained, “My father heard it on the radio, they were explaining about fistula repair” (P10). Another noted, “I was referred from one of the district hospitals” (P11). Others heard about the center directly from former patients in their communities: “Fortunately, there is somebody in my village who came here [the Fistula Care Centre] who has had a fistula repaired. So, we got in touch, and that person called here” (P16).

Barriers to care. Despite ongoing efforts to raise awareness, participants identified several barriers that impede access to OF care, including long wait times, living remotely, transportation challenges, and financial constraints. Long wait times were a common concern among participants and often resulted in significant delays in receiving care. Many women reported waiting months or even years after developing an OF before receiving treatment. One participant shared that she stayed home for “almost a year” (P6) before seeking treatment. Living in remote villages further compounded accessibility challenges. Participants described the difficulty of traveling long distances while leaking urine, arranging their own transportation, and covering related expenses. One participant recounted her journey: “I took many stops, yes, because I had to go to a larger health centre from the healthcare centre at my home. Then they referred me to the district hospital. From the district hospital, that's how I found here [the Fistula Care Centre]” (P11).

Travel to the Fistula Care Centre was often long and challenging. Participants relied on various forms of transportation including cars, ambulances, busses, and walking. For some, the journey lasted several hours; for others, it took up to 2 days and included overnight travel. As one participant explained, “normally we set out in the afternoon, around two, yes, then we arrive here [the Fistula Care Centre] the following day in the morning, around six” (P12).

Transportation arrangements varied significantly. Some participants arranged their own transportation, while others received assistance from hospitals or the Fistula Care Centre itself, often through patient ambassadors. Although the Fistula Care Centre provides services free of cost, many participants highlighted the financial burden of transportation as a barrier to timely care. One woman stated, “I used my own money to get the transport” (P13), while another added, “I came by bus, but I had to work somewhere first to get some money” (P8).

Stigma, segregation, and self-isolation

Stigma. Participants described experiencing stigma that led to social segregation and self-isolation. They frequently highlighted the stigma associated with living with an OF, with one participant stating, “It wasn’t easy, it was really tough” (P14), while another said, “People were laughing at me” (P5). One participant shared, “If I sit somewhere chatting with people, I would always wait for them to leave before I could stand and leave, because whenever I stand, people will see that I had soiled” (P16). Participants also described the stigma they felt within their communities. One woman explained, “I was stinky... people were just talking about it” (P8).

Segregation. Stigmatization led to social segregation. One participant described being segregated from her family, leaving her unsupported during this vulnerable time: “It wasn't easy, because people were segregating me because they said I was just stinky and so I didn’t have anyone that supported me” (P12). Many participants also discussed spousal segregation, with several reporting divorce. One participant stated, “When I developed this problem, the marriage ended” (P18). Beyond the family, participants shared experiences of broader segregation because of community-level stigma: “People were segregating me, so I was just homebound” (P9).

Self-isolation. Many participants described not socializing due to the physical symptoms and social stigma. This led to self-isolation. One participant shared, “It was a difficult sort of life, because I was always indoors” (P9), while others stated, “I was just isolated” (P4), and “I wouldn’t socialize with others” (P3). There were also many reports of how self-isolation kept participants from going out into the community: “I was not able to go to social functions. I would just stay home. I wouldn't go to church, I wouldn't go to the market. I couldn't go anywhere” (P17). If participants did choose to socialize, they often did so for short periods of time. As one participant noted, “I just made a decision to say, if I am to go somewhere, I will not stay long. I would just stay for a short period then leave” (P11).

Renewed belonging. A notable experience reported by most participants was the renewed sense of belonging and acceptance they felt when they returned to their communities after OF repair, because they were no longer stigmatized or segregated. All participants recounted being welcomed back into the community, including to church and social gatherings. One participant shared, “Everything went back to normal. I was able to go to church. I was able to engage in community activities” (P1), while others added, “I had a wonderful time here [at the Fistula Care Centre], and I was excited to go back home, because this time nobody was segregating me. I was living, living happily” (P3). One participant described remarrying after her OF repair, stating, “When I got the fistula, my husband left me, but after the repair, I got married again” (P6).

Loss and restoration of independence

Loss of independence. Living with an OF imposed physical and practical challenges that affected nearly every aspect of participants’ lives. The constant leaking of urine and/or feces compromised the ability to maintain independence, engage in work, or attend school. Participants described incontinence that had an impact on their daily functioning and compromised their ability to maintain independence. One participant shared, “I wasn’t able to do anything because of the fistula” (P12), and another noted, “Everything was done by my mother” (P9). The constant leaking made it difficult for many participants to engage in paid work, often forcing them to rely on others to take over their responsibilities. One participant described how her condition impacted her ability to work: “I had the one that involves the leaking of urine. It was a challenge for me to work, because with urine, it is always dribbling” (P2). Participants who were attending school prior to developing their OF were often forced to discontinue their studies. As one participant shared, “What I really wanted was to go back to school” (P10).

Restoration of independence. Following OF repair, participants who had previously described a loss of independence spoke of a renewed sense of autonomy and hope for the future. Their responses highlighted a shift in their ability to engage in meaningful activities. As one participant shared, “I am feeling very happy, very independent” (P10). Several women expressed excitement and gratitude at being able to return to school after their treatment, with some directly supported by the Fistula Care Centre. One participant noted, “The fistula centre paid for my education, and then I came back to work here [the Fistula Care Centre]” (P10). Another reflected, “I was very excited, but long at last, the problem was dealt with, yeah… and I also managed to go back to school” (P9).

Family and community support

Family support. Family support was the most frequently reported form of assistance, with mothers and spouses being the most commonly mentioned sources of support. Several participants emphasized the supportive role of their mothers: “My mother was very supportive” (P14), and “My mom took care of me” (P4). Discussions of spousal support were common, with participants describing both supportive and unsupportive experiences. Some women spoke positively about their husbands’ involvement, with one stating: “My husband was very supportive” (P15). However, others recounted a lack of spousal support, with their mothers often assuming the role of primary caregiver in the absence of a supportive partner: “My husband was not supportive at all, even when I was pregnant. So he wouldn't provide the soap for my washing, he didn't provide any money for me to buy whatever I will need. Even when I went to the hospital, my husband didn't provide any support. It was only me and my mother” (P18).

Community support. While mentioned less frequently, community support also played a role in some participants’ support experiences. One participant shared that her neighbors helped her with daily tasks such as shopping at the market: “I was assisted by my neighbors” (P13). Another participant highlighted the informational support she received from her community, which led to her accessing treatment: “Somebody who I go to church together with is the one who identified that I had that problem [the fistula], and they knew the ambassador, so she told me to meet the ambassador. Then that's how I found myself here [at the Fistula Care Centre]” (P1). Another participant spoke about the support she received from her community once she returned, after having her OF repaired: “Yes, actually, when I went back home, I was told that if I go back, I shouldn't do heavy weight for at least 6 months. So, people were supporting me because they didn't let me do certain things that maybe have impact on the repair that was done” (P6).

Types of support. When asked about the type of support received, most participants shared that the majority was related to daily chores. One participant noted, “My husband was very supportive. He would go and make sure we had soap to help me wash my clothes because of the leakage” (P14). The same participant also shared, “Much of the assistance [to do chores] was given by my sister-in-law” (P14). In some cases, support was provided indirectly. One participant described how her mother, although living far away, contributed by caring for the woman’s child so she could focus on her recovery: “I was basically taking care of myself, because where my mom is, it's a bit far. But the best that my mother did is that she took my child so I can take care of myself” (P17).

Building skills for community reintegration

Skills. Participants engaged in various learning opportunities at the Fistula Care Centre as part of their OF rehabilitation, aimed at better supporting their reintegration into their communities following repair. Some participants took cooking classes, with one sharing, “I was taught how to cook mandazi” (P10). They also learned how to make a stove to support their cooking upon returning home: “I also learned how to make a stove” (P2). Participants also developed other skills. One stated, “I am able to make some small dresses. I am able to make sanitary pads” (P9). Others added, "I am able to make dresses, trousers, and bags” (P1); “I learned how to do shoes” (P11); and “I trained how to do necklaces using beads” (P10). Agricultural training was also part of the rehabilitation process. One participant noted, "I have also learned how to plant seeds specifically for vegetables” (P17), and another said, “I have also learned how to do farming” (P16). Academic education was also offered. As one participant shared, “I learned also how to read and write” (P11). Upon returning to their communities, participants shared their knowledge and skills. One participant stated, “When I went home, I taught my husband how to sew” (P7). The same participant also extended this teaching to her community: “People are even much willing to learn from me... they are also learning about the skills that I acquired from here [the Fistula Care Centre]” (P7).

In addition to the skills gained at the Fistula Care Centre, participants also shared experiences of learning business management and budgeting to support them in starting and sustaining businesses upon returning to their communities. One participant shared, “I learned how to run a business and support myself” (P8). Several participants spoke about successfully applying these business skills. One said, “Out of the sewing, I managed to buy pigs, yeah, so I am doing pig farming. But also out of the sewing, I managed to do bricks. I have done 25,000 bricks. I am planning to build a house. And this year I planted maize, I planted tobacco, and I am expecting to get something out of that” (P7). Another participant shared how her sewing business is helping to support her education: “I am selling and out of whatever I sell, that's what I am able to pay for my school” (P9).

Support for skills. Many participants received supplies and financial assistance to support their businesses. One participant shared, “When I was going back [home], I was given a sewing machine” (P2). Another reported receiving a solar panel, which she now uses as a source of income: “I was given a solar panel with some charging areas” (P4). Others were supported with the supplies to start a tearoom business. As one participant described, “I was given all the materials, cups, flour, sugar, plates, and the money to buy firewood” (P3). Another added, “They gave me resources for the tearoom” (P5).

Challenges. Despite receiving education and supplies, some participants shared challenges they faced when trying to implement what they had learned when returning to their communities. One participant said, “I wasn’t given the wool to crochet” (P2), while another explained, “So it's when I went back home, I wasn't able to do any sewing because I didn't have the machine” (P5). Others highlighted additional challenges. One participant spoke about the lack of a space to run a business: "I feel it would be good if I were supported with a place to conduct my businesses” (P8). Another participant expressed a need for financial support, “I wish I was given money to start a small business” (P1).

Several participants reflected on the training provided at the Fistula Care Centre, expressing a desire for a broader range of practical and business skills better tailored to their individual needs and local economic realities. Additionally, because not all skills training was available consistently, some participants missed learning opportunities that could have further supported their rehabilitation and reintegration. For example, one participant stated, “I wish I was taught sewing” (P4). Others noted, “I wish I was told how to cook mandazi. I wasn't able to learn that” (P2), and “I hate that we are yet to be taught how to plant different seeds, and I am also yet to be told how to bake scones” (P11). One participant noted that the classes offered did not match her educational needs: “We were also doing classes, though, the classes didn't benefit me much, because they were just like classes that were offered to, maybe, to people who don't know how to read and write and I already had that education” (P10). Another participant emphasized the importance of offering a variety of skills, particularly business-related training, that could be adapted to the economic conditions: “Other business skills would have helped because at this time the sewing business has gone down. I could have been doing something else” (P6).

Gratitude and empowerment. Despite the gaps identified, participants overwhelmingly expressed gratitude and appreciation for the care and opportunities they received at the Fistula Care Centre. One participant shared, “I am very grateful for the support and help that I am getting from here” (P3), while another stated, “I am very appreciative of whatever happened to me. I didn't expect that I can be helped in such a way” (P4). The same participant specifically highlighted the sense of empowerment she gained: “The fact that I was repaired, and I was also empowered to do some businesses, that's helped me” (P4).

Discussion

Overall, the results highlight that women’s experiences following OF repair are shaped by both persistent barriers and meaningful supports. Key challenges included limited awareness of OF and treatment options, difficulties accessing care, stigma, and loss of independence. Positive experiences centered on family and community support, skills-building, and empowerment programs, which facilitated successful reintegration and restored autonomy. These results highlight the importance of raising awareness, accessible services, and targeted reintegration programs to optimize recovery and social participation following OF repair.

Awareness and sensitization programs

This study found an overwhelming lack of awareness related to OF and treatment options among participants, community members, and healthcare professionals. Other research has identified a similar lack of awareness and that low literacy and education levels, young age, null parity, and living remotely are all factors associated with lower levels of awareness [27, 28]. For example, a study conducted by Budu et al. [27] analyzed the magnitude of awareness among women of reproductive age in 14 countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Less than half of the participants reported having any awareness of OF (37.9%) with the prevalence of awareness ranging from 12.8 to 63.9% across the different countries. The population with the highest prevalence of awareness was among those aged 45–49 and multiparous women [27]. Not only were young (less than 19 years old), nulliparous women among the population with the lowest levels of OF awareness, but they were also among the most affected [27, 29]. Therefore, strategies to increase awareness of OF not only need to be strengthened, but they also need to specifically target younger women with lower levels of education and literacy, particularly those living in remote areas.

Limited knowledge and a lack of awareness related to OF contribute to misconceptions and perpetuate stigma and isolation experienced by affected women [30]. Sensitization programs aim to build awareness and understanding of a specific health condition by providing information on the condition itself, its cause, and treatment options [31]. Despite the numerous studies that address the lack of awareness surrounding OF, effective strategies to improve awareness are still needed. Sensitization programs may offer a promising strategy to address this gap. Although there is limited literature on sensitization programs specifically targeting OF, there is evidence supporting the effectiveness of sensitization programs in the context of other health conditions [31]. This suggests that similar approaches could be adapted and applied to the context of OF.

Sensitization programs aimed at increasing awareness could be effectively led by patient ambassadors [32, 33]. In a study conducted by Mgaya and Mwila [32] the majority (54%) of women learned about OF from patient ambassadors who either traveled to their communities or spoke on the radio during community broadcasts. To enhance awareness across rural and remote areas, patient ambassadors have proven effective in disseminating information and dispelling misconceptions through community outreach and via toll-free telephone lines across numerous sub-Saharan African countries [13, 32]. Despite these promising outcomes, the studies emphasize a need for an increase in the number of ambassadors, enhanced training, and broader geographical coverage [13, 32]. Findings from the current study echoed these concerns, with many women reporting they did not have access to a patient ambassador and were unaware of OF or the treatment options.

Posters, radio broadcasts, and media campaigns have also proven to be effective tools for disseminating information about OF and treatment options [18, 27]. These strategies can be incorporated into sensitization programs to increase awareness and understanding about OF, especially in remote areas. Radio broadcasts enhance knowledge and awareness, particularly for women living with OF who are socially isolated [18]. However, Sullivan et al. [17] found that the majority of information in broadcasts and on billboards related to OF is in English (not the local language), and thus women living with OF are often unable to understand the information [17]. Therefore, there is a need for linguistically understandable and culturally relevant dissemination to enhance understanding [17].

This study found that lack of awareness is further complicated by healthcare professionals’ poor knowledge of OF and available treatment options. This lack of awareness serves as a major barrier to identifying OF and making referrals, ultimately delaying timely access to care [18]. As a potential solution, the Fistula Treatment Network in Kenya and EngenderHealth in Nigeria and Uganda have focused on increasing awareness of OF among healthcare professionals. These programs introduced standardized screening and referral tools to help both healthcare professionals and community members more accurately identify women with potential OF [18, 33]. These initiatives could serve as effective models and be replicated in other regions of Africa to improve early identification, referral, and overall awareness of OF.

Accessibility of services

A combination of structural barriers, such as transportation challenges, financial constraints, and rural living, was found to create challenges for women in this study when accessing care. Similar challenges have been documented in other studies, with women reporting significant barriers in accessing timely and appropriate treatment [17, 34, 35]. To address these challenges, a study conducted in Uganda and Nigeria developed a comprehensive, women-centered intervention aimed at improving access to OF services [18]. Free self-referral fistula hotlines were implemented in both countries to improve access to care. Healthcare providers received education on OF and how to facilitate referrals to fistula care centers. Each woman with a suspected OF received a transportation voucher to a fistula care center, which also covered travel for one support person [18].

The limited availability of services for OF further exacerbates accessibility barriers, specifically for women in rural communities who remain underserved [36, 37]. Acknowledging this gap, the Panzi Hospital in the Democratic Republic of Congo created a mobile surgical outreach program aimed at delivering OF services to women in rural and remote areas [36]. Temporary clinics were established in local hospitals for approximately 3 months, based on an expressed community need or because of the identification of a high number of cases through health record surveys. During this time, up to 10 surgeries were performed per day over a period of 14–21 days, followed by 2–4 weeks of monitored recovery for each woman. Over the 6-year study period, care was provided to 1517 women across 41 clinics in 18 of 26 provinces [36]. Despite the overall effectiveness of the mobile outreach program, challenges related to resource availability and inconsistent funding persist, raising concerns about the long-term sustainability of the program [36]. The findings of this research and other studies highlight persistent barriers to accessibility, particularly for women in rural and remote areas [18, 36]. Innovative programs, such as mobile outreach initiatives, should be consistently implemented, evaluated, and adequately funded to improve access to OF care in rural and remote areas.

Reintegration and empowerment

All women in this study reported being accepted back into their communities and no longer experienced stigmatization or segregation following OF repair. These findings align with those reported by Drew et al. [12] who found that women were accepted and did not experience stigma or social exclusion within their communities following OF repair. It should be noted that both the current study and the Drew et al. [12] study were conducted in Malawi. Despite promising community acceptance following OF repair in these studies, these findings have not been consistent across the literature or in other sub-Saharan African countries [16, 38]. Debele et al. [38] and Jarvis et al. [16] found that even after successful OF repair, women continued to face social challenges associated with the stigma of having an OF. Some women reported that in order to gain acceptance into the community following OF repair, they needed to prove that they no longer had an OF or endured the symptoms associated with the condition. The consistent reports of successful reintegration in studies conducted in Malawi, compared to differing outcomes in other settings, suggest that regional or cultural factors unique to each country may play a role in influencing acceptance.

The length of time a woman lives with an OF may also impact their reintegration into the community. For example, a study conducted by Kaba et al. [39] in Guinea assessed how the lived experiences of women with OF influenced the success of community reintegration and acceptance following surgical repair. While these women physically recovered from OF, they continued to face stigmatization and social exclusion upon reintegration [39]. The women in this study lived with OF for an average of 7 years, with some living with the condition for up to 20 years prior to repair. As a result, these women likely endured prolonged stigma and social isolation, potentially contributing to the persistent stigmatization upon returning to their communities. Moreover, these women were only provided with surgical OF repair services, without any additional support prior to their reintegration [39]. These findings highlight the importance of timely access to OF treatment and reintegration support following repair to improve acceptance into the community.

This study found positive participant feedback regarding the skills they learned while at the Fistula Care Centre, noting their direct impact on the success of their reintegration. There is an abundance of literature that supports the need for women to be provided with a reintegration program after OF repair [13, 15, 39]. These programs offer psychological support, vocational training, skills development, literacy and numeracy classes, and economic activities [13, 39]. The skills, training, and economic opportunities from these programs build financial autonomy and independence, which helps women regain their place in society [39].

In addition to the reintegration programs, the EngenderHealth organization in Guinea implemented an innovative strategy to further support women after OF repair. Following hospital discharge, participants stayed with volunteer host families for 2–3 weeks as they gradually immersed into the community and participated in daily activities [15]. This program aimed to support women following OF repair in developing skills such as cooking, washing, and participating in social events while transitioning to independent living in the community with support from the host families. The program was found to enhance overall community reintegration outcomes as women expressed gaining self-confidence and being accepted by their communities [15]. However, Delamou et al. [15] found that successful community reintegration was also dependent on the outcome of the repair itself, as participants who remained incontinent following OF repair were unsatisfied with the reintegration program and continued to face stigma and isolation [15]. The participants in the current study were all continent when they returned to their communities, which may have contributed to their positive reintegration experiences and enhanced community acceptance. Therefore, high-quality care to improve incontinence, along with supportive reintegration programs, is crucial for improved community reintegration and acceptance outcomes.

A consistent theme in this study and across the literature is the importance of reintegration programs that foster women’s empowerment following OF repair [32, 39, 40]. This was emphasized by Debele et al. [40] who underscored the importance of educational and vocational support programs tailored to women’s individual needs. These supports were found to help women build independence and develop a sense of empowerment as they pursued further education or started their own businesses. The skills acquired through rehabilitation contributed to successful community reintegration [40].

Empowerment emerging from reintegration support can be viewed as both a psychosocial and economic outcome. Psychologically, programs help women regain confidence, agency, and a sense of belonging within their communities, reducing feelings of stigma and social isolation. Economically, the skills and vocational training provided enable women to generate income, gain financial independence, and sustain livelihoods, reinforcing their social standing and long-term autonomy. Empowering women through skill development is essential for supporting lasting reintegration, improving psychological well-being, promoting economic independence, and sustaining success within their communities.

Limitations

This study provides important insights into the experiences of women living with OF who received care at one Fistula Care Centre in Malawi. Although the findings offer valuable information, the authors acknowledge that they may not be generalizable to all women who undergo OF repair. This research specifically reflects the experiences of those who were able to access care and does not capture the voices of women who sought care at other facilities or who continue to face barriers in accessing treatment. Furthermore, because all participants received care from a single center, cultural and regional differences across Malawi or other countries may limit the broader applicability of these findings. Future research should aim to include larger, more diverse samples across Africa. Finally, as all but one interview required translation from Chichewa to English, some depth and meaning may have been lost in the translation process as certain culturally specific expressions or nuances do not have exact equivalents in English.

Conclusion

Persistent lack of awareness, stigma, and barriers to care continue to challenge women with OF in Malawi, particularly those in rural and remote areas. Sensitization programs, including community ambassadors and mass media, along with education for healthcare providers, are essential to increase awareness and improve timely access to care. Comprehensive reintegration programs that provide vocational training, skill development, and economic support help women regain independence and successfully reintegrate into their communities. Ongoing evaluation of these initiatives, combined with supportive policies and sustained funding, is critical to ensure effectiveness and long-term impact. These findings reinforce the importance of targeted, practical strategies to improve care and support for women with OF.
