Depression and the risk of myocardial infarction: evidence from a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2005–2020
Authors: Dan Zhu, Fei Ying, Liping Wei, Xiaolong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression is recognized as being linked to atherosclerosis and adverse cardiovascular outcomes; however, robust evidence from nationally representative cohorts regarding its association with myocardial infarction (MI) is still insufficient. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between depressive symptoms, measured by the PHQ-9, and the odds of having a history of MI using data from NHANES 2005–2020, while further exploring possible nonlinear patterns and potential modifiers of this association.MethodsWe included 37,139 adults aged 20 years or older (1,574 with MI) and performed weighted analyses accounting for the NHANES complex survey design. Exposure was defined as PHQ-9 scores, analyzed both continuously and by severity categories (none, mild, moderate, severe), with self-reported MI as the outcome. We fitted three progressively adjusted weighted logistic regression models and employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to evaluate nonlinearity. We further performed prespecified subgroup and interaction analyses, along with a series of sensitivity analyses.ResultsHigher depressive burden was significantly associated with higher odds of MI, with MI prevalence rising progressively across depression severity groups (2.89% vs. 4.39% vs. 5.80% vs. 7.08%, p < 0.001). In the unadjusted model, every 1-point increase in PHQ-9 score corresponded to a 6.2% increase in the odds of MI (OR = 1.062, 95% CI: 1.047–1.077; p < 0.001), while severe depression was linked to approximately a 2.56-fold higher odds compared with non-depressed participants. The association remained robust after sequential full adjustment for potential confounders. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed no evidence of nonlinearity (p > 0.05), supporting an approximately linear association. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent associations across most strata; however, significant interactions were detected for CHF, CHD, and higher V/MPA groups (P for interaction < 0.05), indicating attenuated associations in these groups.ConclusionIn a nationally representative sample of US adults, depressive burden was strongly associated with prevalent MI, with cardiovascular comorbidities and physical activity acting as potential modifiers of this association. These findings underscore the importance of integrating routine depression screening and holistic mind–body management into both general and high-risk populations, and highlight the necessity of prospective and interventional studies to clarify the temporal sequence and evaluate potential causality.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) ranks among the foremost global causes of mortality and disability. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, the incidence and mortality of MI remain unacceptably high, and its profound threat to public health is expected to persist over the next several decades [1, 2]. In recent years, psychosocial factors have emerged as critical determinants of cardiovascular health, among which depression has garnered growing concern. Beyond being a common psychiatric disorder, depression may act as a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease [3].

Epidemiological evidence consistently demonstrates a strong association between depression and cardiovascular disease. The prevalence of depression among individuals with coronary heart disease or heart failure has been reported to range from 20% to 40% [4]. A large-scale meta-analysis reported an overall prevalence of depression of about 20.8% among cardiovascular patients, with a significant association with all-cause mortality and a risk ratio greater than 2.0 [5]. Another systematic review reported that the prevalence of depression following MI ranged from 5% to 34%, and that depression was associated with substantially higher long-term risks of mortality and recurrence [6]. Recent evidence suggests that among patients undergoing interventional therapy for heart disease, depression is associated with a three- to five-fold higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events within one year [7]. Additional studies have demonstrated that patients with prior anxiety or depressive symptoms face a markedly elevated risk of recurrent cardiovascular events compared with asymptomatic individuals [8]. Furthermore, a multicenter cohort study in the United States found that approximately 15% of patients after MI or coronary artery bypass grafting experienced severe depression, which was independently linked to cardiovascular progression and mortality [9]. Recent trend analyses further indicate that depression-related cardiovascular mortality has been rising worldwide [10].

Mechanistic research has offered biological insights into how depression is connected with MI. Depression activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system, resulting in elevated blood pressure and heart rate that exacerbate cardiac strain [11]. In parallel, depression induces inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and platelet activation, thereby accelerating atherosclerotic progression [12]. Neurobiological evidence indicates that depression is linked to reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor levels and heightened immune activation, both of which may exacerbate myocardial injury and hinder repair [13]. Moreover, depression is frequently accompanied by adverse lifestyle behaviors—including smoking, physical inactivity, irregular diet, and sleep disturbances—that elevate cardiovascular risk through behavioral mechanisms [14]. Thus, depression may act as an independent risk factor for MI and simultaneously promote disease progression through diverse biological and behavioral mechanisms.

Interventions addressing depression have demonstrated beneficial effects in cardiovascular populations. Both physical exercise and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) improve depressive symptoms and may concomitantly lower cardiovascular event risk [15]. However, current evidence is largely derived from single-center or clinical samples with limited size and representativeness; moreover, heterogeneity in the definitions and measurement of depression, together with inadequate adjustment for confounders, constrains interpretation. Hence, large-scale studies based on nationally representative databases are essential to further elucidate the association between depression and MI risk.

Using data from NHANES 2005–2020, we assessed depression with the PHQ-9 and applied complex survey weighting, multivariable logistic regression, and restricted cubic spline models to comprehensively examine the association between depression and MI risk, complemented by subgroup and sensitivity analyses to ensure robustness. We hypothesized that increasing depression severity would be linearly associated with higher MI risk, and that prior cardiovascular disease and physical activity might serve as effect modifiers of this relationship. This study may provide novel epidemiological evidence to inform primary prevention of cardiovascular disease and the integration of mental health management.

Materials and methods

Data and sample sources

This study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). NHANES is a continuous, nationally representative survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. civilian population, including demographics, socioeconomic factors, dietary patterns, and multiple health indicators. To achieve representativeness, NHANES employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling design. The study protocol was approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and all participants gave written informed consent. Data from the survey are publicly accessible at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

For this analysis, we included participants aged 20 years or older from NHANES 2005–2020 cycles. The initial sample comprised 76,496 individuals. After excluding participants younger than 20 years and those missing PHQ-9 data or information on MI diagnosis, a final sample of 37,139 eligible participants was included, of whom 1,574 had a history of MI. Exposure factors

Depression was assessed at mobile examination centers (MECs) using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), which was administered in person by trained interviewers. Each PHQ-9 item was rated by symptom frequency on a scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), yielding a total score between 0 and 27. Based on prior studies, participants were categorized by PHQ-9 total scores into four groups: no depression (0–4), mild depression (5–9), moderate depression (10–14), and severe depression (15–27). In this study, depression was defined as a PHQ-9 total score of ≥ 10, encompassing both moderate and severe categories. Prior studies have demonstrated that this cutoff yields a sensitivity and specificity of 88% for depression diagnosis [16].

Outcome variables

The primary outcome in this study was MI. The definition of MI was derived from relevant questions in the Medical Conditions Questionnaire (MCQ). In detail, participants were classified as having MI [17, 18] if they responded “Yes” to the question: “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a heart attack (also called myocardial infarction)?” Based on this definition, individuals with MI were assigned a code of 1, whereas those without MI were assigned a code of 0.

Covariates

Guided by prior studies and clinical importance, we incorporated a range of potential confounding variables, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), uric acid (UA), smoking and alcohol use, vigorous or moderate physical activity (V/MPA), congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary heart disease (CHD), and hypertension. Demographic variables were derived from the NHANES demographics module, and information on CHF, CHD, and hypertension was obtained via standardized interviews administered by trained health professionals. All covariates were extracted from the NHANES database and subjected to necessary preprocessing and standardization prior to inclusion in the analytical models, in order to reduce potential confounding bias in the results.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were conducted with consideration of NHANES’s complex survey design, using weighted methods to guarantee the representativeness and robustness of the findings. Following the recommendations of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), appropriate sample weights (WTMECPRP), strata (SDMVSTRA), and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU) were applied to account for the complexity of the survey design. Participants were categorized into depression severity groups according to PHQ-9 scores. Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using weighted one-way ANOVA, while non-normally distributed variables were tested with weighted Kruskal–Wallis tests; categorical variables were compared using weighted chi-square tests. Continuous variables were reported as weighted means ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as unweighted frequencies and weighted proportions.

Subgroup analyses using multivariable logistic regression were performed to evaluate whether the association of PHQ-9 scores differed by key stratifying variables, including sex, race, and educational level. To further investigate the association between PHQ-9 scores and myocardial infarction (MI), three multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Multicollinearity of covariates was assessed prior to model fitting using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for sex, age, and race; Model 3 further adjusted for education, marital status, PIR, BMI, SBP, DBP, TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, UA, smoking status, alcohol use, V/MPA, CHF, CHD, and hypertension. PHQ-9 scores were analyzed as continuous measures and also categorized according to depression severity. Model results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied to examine potential nonlinear associations between PHQ-9 scores and MI risk. Finally, robustness of the findings was assessed through sensitivity analyses, including alternative covariate adjustments, exclusion of outliers, and stepwise regression. All analyses were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. All data analyses were carried out with DecisionLinnc version 1.0 [19], and results were validated repeatedly to ensure accuracy.

Results

Epidemiological survey results

We used data from the NHANES covering the period 2005–2020. As shown in Fig. 2, the annual prevalence of MI, depression, and their comorbidities increased steadily over time. Between the 2005–2006 and 2017–2020 cycles, the prevalence was almost twice as high. While descriptively informative, these parallel secular trends do not establish an association at the individual level, as they may reflect independent changes in disease detection, demographic shifts, survival rates, or reporting practices. This underscores the necessity of further individual-level multivariable research to explore their true co-occurrence.

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 37,139 participants were included in this study, comprising 27,922 without depressive symptoms, 5,915 with mild depression, 2,072 with moderate depression, and 1,230 with severe depression. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of participants stratified by depression severity. With increasing depression severity, significant differences were observed in several demographic, lifestyle, and clinical metabolic indicators.

In terms of metabolic and clinical features, greater depression severity was associated with higher BMI, DBP, and TG levels, but lower levels of HDL-C (all p < 0.05). For demographic and lifestyle characteristics, significant differences across depression strata (all p < 0.001) were observed in sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, education, PIR, smoking, drinking, and vigorous/moderate physical activity (V/MPA). With regard to comorbidities, higher depression severity was significantly associated with greater prevalence of hypertension, CHF, and CHD (p < 0.001). Importantly, the prevalence of MI increased progressively across depression severity groups (2.89% vs. 4.39% vs. 5.80% vs. 7.08%, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that increasing depression severity may be linked to a higher prevalence of MI.

Relationship between depression and the Odds of MI

We performed multivariable logistic regression analyses to further investigate the relationship between depression and MI. As shown in Fig. 3, in the unadjusted model (Model 1), PHQ-9 scores treated as a continuous variable were significantly associated with the odds of MI: each 1-point increment corresponded to a 6.2% increase in the odds of having an MI history (OR = 1.062, 95% CI: 1.047–1.077, p < 0.001). In stratified analysis, individuals with severe depression had a markedly elevated odds of MI compared with those without depression (OR = 2.557, 95% CI: 1.901–3.439, p < 0.001). These associations remained statistically significant after adjusting for sex, age, and race in Model 2, and for all potential covariates in Model 3, indicating that the link between depression and MI prevalence was highly robust.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted weighted multivariable logistic regression subgroup analyses to further assess the association of depression with the odds of MI across different population groups (Fig. 4). The results indicated that in subgroups defined by sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, PIR, BMI, smoking, drinking, and hypertension, the association between depression (PHQ-9 score or categories as exposure) and the odds of MI was directionally consistent, and no significant interactions were detected (P for interaction > 0.05).

In contrast, significant interactions (P for interaction < 0.05) were found in the subgroups of CHF, CHD, and V/MPA, implying that baseline cardiovascular conditions and physical activity may act as effect modifiers in the depression–MI relationship. In other words, the magnitude of the depression–MI association varied significantly depending on CHF/CHD status and physical activity levels. These findings imply that the relative impact of depression may be more apparent in CHD/CHF-negative individuals (lower baseline cardiovascular risk), whereas in CHD/CHF-positive individuals, higher baseline risk and concomitant treatment or behavioral adaptations may attenuate the magnitude of the association with depression. At the same time, regular physical activity may mitigate some of the adverse cardiovascular factors associated with depression, which could explain the lower effect estimates observed in V/MPA-positive participants.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were applied to examine potential nonlinear associations between PHQ-9 scores and the odds of MI (Fig. 5). Results indicated that across the unadjusted (Model 1), partially adjusted (Model 2), and fully adjusted (Model 3) models, no significant evidence of nonlinearity was detected (P for nonlinearity > 0.05). These findings support an approximately linear association between PHQ-9 scores and the odds of MI, indicating that modeling with a linear term is more appropriate. These results align with the monotonic trend shown in the stratified analyses, in which increasing depression severity corresponded to progressively higher odds of MI.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses for both the logistic regression and RCS results. First, in the logistic regression analyses, models were repeatedly fitted by stepwise exclusion of key covariates (such as BMI, TC, TG, CHF, CHD, hypertension, and smoking status) and by treating extreme values of continuous variables (excluding outliers and extreme observations). Results indicated that the positive association between PHQ-9 scores and the odds of MI consistently remained statistically significant (all p < 0.05), implying that the findings were robust to covariate selection and outlier treatment. Second, in the RCS analyses, models were re-fitted with alternative covariate sets and knot specifications were re-examined; across all scenarios, no significant evidence of nonlinearity was detected (P for nonlinearity > 0.05), in line with the primary analysis.

Discussion

This study, based on nationally representative data from NHANES 2005–2020, found a robust positive association between depression and self-reported history of MI. The associations remained significant whether PHQ-9 was modeled as a continuous score or categorized by severity, even after progressive multivariable adjustment, with multiple sensitivity analyses corroborating the robustness of the findings. No significant nonlinearity was identified in the restricted cubic spline analyses, indicating that under the current sample and knot specification, the increase in depression burden was more consistent with an approximately linear pattern in relation to the odds of MI. Subgroup analyses demonstrated broadly consistent directions; however, significant interactions were identified among participants with CHF, CHD, and higher levels of V/MPA. In these subgroups, the magnitude of the association between depression and MI appeared attenuated compared with participants without CHF/CHD or with lower physical activity levels. Overall, depression may constitute an independent and identifiable element associated with a history of MI, though the strength of this association is influenced by underlying cardiovascular conditions and behavioral factors.

The positive association between depression and MI observed in this study is consistent with the direction reported in several recent studies. On the one hand, both population-based and clinical evidence suggest that depression burden is linked to increased risk of incident or recurrent coronary events. For example, a prospective cohort in China demonstrated a positive association between depression and the risk of incident CVD/AMI, with risk increases more pronounced among women and individuals aged < 65 years [20], consistent with the subgroup differences observed in our study. On the other hand, systematic reviews and meta-analyses in populations with acute coronary syndrome or post-MI also suggest that the substantial burden of depression is associated with greater vulnerability to recurrent events [21, 22], consistent with the direction of our analyses in participants with prior MI. Moreover, symptomatic depression in patients with CHD has been associated with increased subsequent CVD risk, suggesting that even subthreshold depressive spectra carry prognostic significance [23]. Notably, evidence remains inconsistent regarding whether “treating depression” translates into hard endpoint benefits. A JACC review noted that although antidepressant treatment improves emotional symptoms, definitive evidence for consistently improving cardiovascular survival or event-free survival is still lacking [24]. In contrast, a recent study in JACC Advances emphasized that dynamic changes in depression (persistence/worsening) were more closely associated with MACE (including nonfatal MI), underscoring the need to evaluate depression trajectories and their temporal coupling with risk in a longitudinal framework [25]. Overall, the current body of evidence from epidemiological, clinical, and methodological domains corroborates our findings of an approximately linear, stratifiable association between depression burden and MI prevalence, while pointing to the need for prospective and interventional research to address the unresolved issues of “modifiability” and “temporal sequencing.”

The biological connection between depression and myocardial infarction (MI) is multifactorial, reflecting the overlapping effects of inflammatory, neuroendocrine, autonomic, platelet/coagulation, endothelial, and gut microbiota pathways. Recent integrative reviews propose that systemic low-grade inflammation (CRP/hs-CRP, IL-6), hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and autonomic nervous system (ANS) imbalance, platelet hyperreactivity, and endothelial dysfunction jointly mediate the pro-atherosclerotic and plaque-destabilizing effects of depression [26–28]. Further quantitative evidence reinforces the “inflammation–ANS dual pathway”: a 2024 meta-analysis showed that CHD patients with comorbid depression had higher CRP/hs-CRP and IL-6 levels and lower HRV, reflecting a phenotype of inflammation activation combined with vagal withdrawal, consistent with greater susceptibility to cardiovascular events [29]. At the intersection of metabolism and neurotransmission, the tryptophan–kynurenine (Kyn) pathway has been identified as a critical “metabolic bridge” connecting neuroinflammation in depression with cardiovascular injury, as its downstream metabolites affect coronary pathology and myocardial injury through NMDA receptor activation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [30, 31]. Parallel to this, platelet overactivation and increased adhesion molecules (such as P-selectin) both enhance thrombosis propensity and signal cross-talk imbalances in serotonin pathways. The latest systematic reviews and meta-analyses provide consistent evidence that soluble P-selectin and other circulating activation markers are elevated in CAD populations [32], supporting the biological plausibility of a depression-driven prothrombotic mechanism [33]. Furthermore, emerging research on the gut–heart axis indicates that the microbiome and its metabolites (e.g., TMAO, short-chain fatty acids) can modulate immune–neuroendocrine pathways, thereby affecting atherosclerosis and myocardial remodeling, while also correlating with depressive phenotypes, implying that dysbiosis may represent a common upstream driver of the depression–CVD link [34, 35]. In summary, current evidence supports a “multichannel” framework in which chronic inflammation and neuroendocrine imbalance form the core, amplified by platelet/coagulation activity, endothelial dysfunction, and gut–heart axis pathways. This framework not only accounts for the observed linear association but also provides a biologically plausible explanation for the buffering role of physical activity on inflammation and ANS, and for the attenuated relative effect of depression observed in populations with established CVD. Beyond biological mechanisms, it is crucial to emphasize the role of shared behavioral and socioeconomic vulnerabilities. As demonstrated in our baseline characteristics, increasing depression severity was clearly accompanied by worsening gradients in smoking, physical inactivity, obesity, and lower poverty income ratio (PIR). Rather than a direct causal pathway, these observable sociodemographic and behavioral factors may act as shared vulnerabilities or parallel pathways that simultaneously increase the likelihood of both depressive symptoms and cardiovascular events.

In addition, subgroup analyses revealed that the positive association between depression and MI was generally consistent across most population groups; however, attenuation of the association was observed in participants with CHF/CHD and higher V/MPA, which may be explained by dilution from elevated baseline risk and adherence to secondary prevention, survivor/selection bias, and the mitigating influence of physical activity on inflammation and autonomic dysregulation. No evidence of nonlinearity was detected in the RCS analysis, indicating compatibility of the current data with a linear association. It is important to note that the lack of detected nonlinearity does not establish linearity, as results remain constrained by sparse data at extreme values and knot placement. Using linear approximations for clinical risk stratification is feasible; however, prospective and interventional studies are needed to validate this approach and to identify potential thresholds. These differences underscore the modifying role of baseline cardiovascular conditions and behavioral factors on the “depression–MI” pathway, suggesting their integration into risk stratification and precision management.

Despite the strengths of a large, nationally representative sample and robust analytical approaches, several limitations remain. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Specifically, the temporal mismatch between the PHQ-9 (assessing symptoms over the prior two weeks) and MI (a lifetime self-reported diagnosis) prevents establishing a sequence of events. Thus, alternative explanations such as reverse causation—particularly the well-documented phenomenon of post-MI depression—cannot be ruled out. Second, relying on self-reported MI outcomes introduces potential recall, misclassification, and survival biases. Consequently, our findings must be strictly interpreted within the framework of MI prevalence rather than incidence or clinical risk prediction. Third, despite extensive multivariable adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., medication adherence, psychosocial stress, cardiac rehabilitation) is possible. Future prospective cohort studies should integrate biomarkers and advanced causal inference methods to evaluate mechanistic pathways. Fourth, our design could not capture dynamic changes in depression or treatment responsiveness. Longitudinal models and intervention trials are needed to evaluate cumulative exposure and whether depression management modifies cardiovascular outcomes. Finally, as this study utilized a U.S. population, multicenter and multiethnic external validation is necessary to confirm generalizability and refine stratification strategies across diverse healthcare systems.

Conclusions

Based on a nationally representative NHANES 2005–2020 sample, we found a robust positive association between depression burden (PHQ-9) and prior myocardial infarction. Overall, findings were consistent with an approximately linear association, with evidence of varying magnitudes of association in subgroups with CHF/CHD and higher levels of physical activity. These results highlight the need to incorporate routine depression screening and integrated mind–body care into comprehensive cardiovascular management for both the general population and high-risk groups. At the same time, caution is warranted due to limitations in causal inference and potential bias arising from the cross-sectional design and self-reported outcomes. Prospective cohort and interventional studies are urgently required to clarify the temporal sequence, evaluate potential causality, and explore longitudinal trajectories.
