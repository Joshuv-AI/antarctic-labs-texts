Soul is a master control gene governing the development of the Drosophila prothoracic gland
Authors: Wen Liu, Minyi Yan, Kirst King-Jones
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceSteroid hormones produced by endocrine glands regulate the transition from juvenile to adult stages in both mammals and holometabolous insects, known as puberty and metamorphosis, respectively. In Drosophila, the prothoracic gland (PG) is the principal organ that synthesizes ecdysone to drive metamorphosis, while the master control genes for PG development remain elusive. We demonstrate that Soul/Tap, a bHLH transcription factor heterodimer, is crucial for PG development and initiating metamorphosis. Furthermore, we found that a chitin-related gene and a POU transcription factor, both direct targets of Soul/Tap, are essential for PG morphology and function. Our findings reveal a transcriptional hierarchy that orchestrates the development of the principal insect endocrine gland, which is pivotal in managing the juvenile-to-adult transition.

Holometabolous insects represent the largest and most diverse taxon among all multicellular organisms and comprise species such as butterflies, bees, beetles, and flies. One of the most remarkable aspects of holometabolous insects is their ability to undergo complete metamorphosis, a transformation that radically alters their morphology, behaviors, and survival strategies. Holometabolous development is characterized by four distinct life stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult. The major developmental transitions such as the molts or pupation are driven by ecdysone, a steroid hormone that is synthesized by the Halloween enzymes in the prothoracic gland (PG). Metamorphosis is the developmental stage during which the larval body plan is remodeled into its adult form and represents a critical phase for insect adaptation and ecological survival (1). The precise onset of metamorphosis is regulated by intricate networks of hormonal signals and gene cascades, which coordinate the extensive tissue remodeling and organ development necessary for reaching sexual maturity (2, 3). Understanding the molecular mechanisms that control insect metamorphosis provides an excellent opportunity for studying the complex interplay of i) hormone action and their ability to modulate transcription factor networks, ii) how nutrient status impinges on developmental processes and developmental timing, iii) organogenesis and its regulation, and iv) how environmental conditions modulate these regulatory hierarchies.

A pivotal moment in the commitment to undergo metamorphosis is the attainment of critical weight (CW), a key developmental milestone first identified in the tobacco hornworm Manduca sexta and subsequently observed in other species such as Drosophila melanogaster (4, 5). Larvae must acquire sufficient nutrients to surpass the CW threshold, enabling them to initiate metamorphosis later, even under adverse conditions like food scarcity (6). Several factors influence the achievement of CW. The prothoracicotropic hormone (PTTH), a brain neuropeptide, plays a critical role by stimulating ecdysone production in the PG through the Torso-mediated MAPK pathway (7). PTTH secretion depends on nutritional status and is necessary for the normal attainment of CW (8). Under nutritional stress, autophagy is activated in the PG, which limits the entry of sterols that are needed as precursors for ecdysone production. This causes a developmental delay that larvae can exploit to search for a better-suited diet (9). Additionally, the insulin and TOR signaling pathways are significant regulators of CW attainment. Manipulating insulin signaling in a PG-specific manner alters PG size and disrupts the checkpoint for CW, as well as ecdysone biosynthesis, thereby affecting Drosophila metamorphosis (5, 10). This suggests a link between nutritional status and CW attainment. Overexpression of FoxO, a transcription factor negatively regulating the insulin and TOR pathways, delays CW attainment (11). Moreover, TOR signaling, a cellular nutrient sensing pathway, mediates CW exit via Snail (Sna), a transcription factor vital for the endoreplication of the PG genome (12, 13). However, these studies have primarily focused on the later stages of development, where PG development would normally be complete. In this study, we report that disrupting either Soul, a PG-specific bHLH transcription factor, or its partner (as we show here) Tap, acts earlier in PG development and thus acts as master control genes controlling the maturation and proper establishment of transcriptional hierarchies in this tissue.

The ecdysone signaling cascade, which drives developmental transitions such as molts and entrance into metamorphosis, is characterized by a hierarchy of transcription factors, including the Ecdysone Receptor (EcR) and its partner Ultraspiracle Protein (USP) (14), the ecdysone early response gene E74 (15), nuclear receptors DHR3, DHR4, FTZ-F1, and E75 (16), metamorphosis suppressor Chinmo (17), and the metamorphosis initiation factor Broad-Complex (Br-C) (18). As the principal tissue for ecdysone biosynthesis, the PG exhibits high and specific expression of ecdysone biosynthetic enzyme genes (aka Halloween genes), such as neverland (nvd), spookier (spok), shroud (sro), phantom (phm), shadow (sad), and disembodied (dib) (19). The expression of these enzyme genes is rigorously controlled at the transcriptional level (20). For instance, transcription factors Séan, Ouija Board (Ouib), and Molting Defective (Mld) cooperatively regulate the transcription of nvd and spok (21), while the POU domain protein Ventral veins lacking (Vvl) and the nuclear receptor Knirps (Kni) bind to the phm promoter to modulate gene expression (22). Vvl and Kni also play minor roles in mediating PG size, suggesting that PG growth is under transcriptional control; however, the transcription factor network driving PG growth and development remains largely uncharacterized.

Utilizing genome-wide microarray analysis, we previously identified 208 genes exhibiting PG-specific expression (23). In this earlier study, we identified CG33557 (hereafter named soul), which encodes a basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factor. Disrupting soul function in the PG caused developmental arrest, suggesting a role in the ecdysone production pathway (23). Given that bHLH proteins often act as heterodimers (24), we conducted a PG-specific RNAi screen targeting each of the 59 bHLH genes in D. melanogaster. This strategy yielded seven candidate lines with severe phenotypes, which included RNAi directed against soul, suggesting that these genes are critical for the regulation of metamorphosis. Expression profiling revealed that soul was the only bHLH gene with high and specific expression in the PG. We then demonstrated physical interaction between Soul and Tap since tap was one of the seven bHLH candidates. This interaction occurred in vivo in the PG, where the Soul/Tap heterodimer acts at the promoter of the cuticle protein gene Cuticular protein 49Ah (Cpr49Ah) and the POU domain transcription factor pou domain motif 3 (pdm3). Furthermore, Soul/Tap controls PG growth for CW establishment, ultimately regulating ecdysone production and Drosophila metamorphosis. Our study has uncovered a hitherto uncharacterized transcription factor hierarchy that controls PG development and CW establishment, offering insights into the molecular mechanisms governing the transition from a juvenile individual to a sexually reproductive adult.

Results

The Two bHLH Transcription Factors, Soul and Tap, Physically Interact and are Critical for Drosophila Development.

To investigate the roles of bHLH transcription factors in regulating ecdysone production and metamorphosis, we conducted a PG-specific RNAi screen (using “phm22-Gal4” as the driver, hereafter “PG>”) targeting all 59 bHLH genes individually. This screen identified seven candidates, including CG33557 (aka soul), with varying degrees of RNAi phenotypes (Fig. 1A). Two of the candidates were the circadian clock genes Clock (Clk) and cycle (cyc), which we demonstrated in an earlier study to act as key genes integrating ecdysone biosynthesis with circadian control in the PG (25). Taiman (Tai) is a well-characterized EcR coactivator (26). However, cousin of atonal (cato), target of Poxn (tap), and cropped (crp, aka AP-4) (27) have not been previously considered to act in the PG as regulators of metamorphosis. Subsequently, we analyzed the transcript abundance in the PG of these seven genes to assess whether they showed PG-specific expression. The PG is the largest gland of the dipteran ring gland, which harbors two additional endocrine tissues known as the corpora allata and the corpora cardiaca. Since it is not possible to separate the PG from the ring gland by dissection, we used the ring gland as a tissue source instead. Our qPCR results showed that soul is the only bHLH gene (out of seven) exhibiting transcript enrichment (10-fold) in the ring gland compared to the average expression in whole body samples (Fig. 1B). This indicates that Soul mainly functions in the PG and suggests a role in the transcriptional control of ecdysone biosynthesis, the CW checkpoint or PG development. The soul transcript enrichment in the PG aligns well with our previous results from ring gland-specific microarrays (23).

Given that Soul is a bHLH transcription factor, it is likely that it forms a heterodimer with another bHLH protein. Thus, we limited our analysis to the other six genes identified in our RNAi screen as candidate protein partners, based on the assumption that targeting a potential partner protein of Soul via RNAi should phenocopy soul-RNAi. To identify a partner protein for Soul, we coexpressed Soul with each of the six candidates in S2 cells and conducted protein coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays. This approach revealed that Tap was the only protein that coimmunoprecipitated with Soul (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To validate this result in PG cells, we expressed cDNAs encoding tagged versions of Soul and Tap, in the form of Soul-HA and GFP-Tap, followed by co-IP. This confirmed a robust interaction between Soul and GFP-Tap, whereas the GFP control was not detected (Fig. 1C).

Subsequently, we quantified survival rates of PG > soul-RNAi or PG > tap-RNAi animals. PG-specific tap-RNAi1 (VDRC, tapIR1) caused arrest at the second instar (L2) stage (Fig. 1A), whereas soul-RNAi (soulIR) larvae died mainly as third instar (L3) larvae. To match the lethal stages in subsequent experiments, we used a weaker tap-RNAi2 line (BDSC, tapIR2), allowing us to interrogate L3 stages of both RNAi lines. In both soul and tap RNAi cohorts, ~20% of larvae arrested development at the L2 stage, whereas ~60% died at the L3 stage (Fig. 1D). Only ~5% of the larvae progressed to pupal and adult stages. These arrested L3 larvae continued to grow until death, resulting in significantly increased body sizes (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). To validate the phenotypes of soul- and tap-RNAi, we generated Flippase Recombinase Targets (FRT)-flanked knock-ins (soulFRT and tapFRT) of the two genes, which allowed us to conditionally remove the transcription units of soul and tap via PG-specific expression of Flippase (FLP). PG-specific depletion of soul (PG > FLP; soulFRT) or tap (PG > FLP; tapFRT) arrested approximately 40% of larvae at the L2 stage and another 20 to 30% at the L3 stage, with no observable pupal or adult escapers (Fig. 1F). Similar to the RNAi-mediated knockdown of soul and tap, removing either gene caused larger body sizes in L3 larvae (Fig. 1G). Taken together, these findings indicate that Soul forms a heterodimer with Tap, and that both are required for initiating metamorphosis.

Loss of Soul- or Tap-Function in the PG Leads to Ecdysone Deficiency.

Given that the PG serves as the principal source of ecdysone to orchestrate larval development and metamorphosis, we hypothesized that the larval–pupal transition failed in PG > soulIR or PG > tapIR2 animals—at least in part—due to ecdysone deficiency. To test this, we supplemented fly media with precursors of ecdysone, including cholesterol (C) and 7-Dehydrocholesterol (7DC), as well as a biologically active form of ecdysone, 20-Hydroxyecdysone (20E). We then examined whether any of these compounds could rescue phenotypes associated with PG-specific depletion of tap or soul. This strategy did not result in significant improvements of adult survival rates (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D), however, supplementation with 7DC and 20E did reduce body size, with 20E restoring body size to levels comparable to the control group (Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 E and F). This partial rescue suggested that Soul/Tap impinge on ecdysone biosynthesis or its regulation. When we used 20E supplementation for the FLP/FRT-mediated depletion of soul and tap, we noticed increased survival of PG > FLP; soulFRT and PG > FLP; tapFRT animals from the L2 to the L3 stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 G and H), but, similar to the RNAi results, we saw no rescue to adulthood.

Since phm22-Gal4 (short: PG>) has some minor expression in the trachea, we repeated some of the RNAi experiments with spok-Gal4 to ensure that our findings were caused by PG-specific loss of function rather than an unintended knockdown in the trachea. spok-Gal4 is considered to be highly specific to the PG, albeit with weaker expression compared to phm22-Gal4 (28–30). Overall, gene knockdowns via spok-Gal4 were weaker but consistent with the phm22-Gal4-RNAi phenotypes. Specifically, we saw severe developmental delays and pupal lethality instead of L3 arrest for spok > soul-RNAi and spok > tap-RNAi (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 I and J). Similar to phm22-Gal4 driven RNAi, 20E feeding resulted in a partial rescue of both lines, where it improved pupal survival rates of spok > soulIR and spok > tapIR1 populations (SI Appendix, Fig. S2K). Finally, we used breathless-Gal4 (btl-Gal4) to trigger soul- or tap-RNAi in the trachea. This resulted, as expected, in no obvious phenotypes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 F and G). In conclusion, the developmental defects and molecular phenotypes we observed with phm22-Gal4-mediated RNAi were caused by PG-specific disruption of tap and soul.

To assess whether loss of soul or tap function affected ecdysone production, we analyzed the expression of several Halloween genes and 20E-responsive genes via qPCR in different developmental stages. In late L1 larvae, sad, nvd, and spok were not significantly different from controls, whereas phm was ~2.5-fold down-regulated. EcR and E74 expression was also unaffected in L1 samples of PG > soulIR or PG > tapIR2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). However, in late L2 larvae, all tested genes were consistently down-regulated, with spok, phm, sad, and E74 displaying ~5-fold downregulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D). Finally, in late L3 larvae, we observed the strongest effects on the ecdysone hierarchy (see below). Overall, RNAi efficiency for soul and tap showed a 50% and 60% reduction in the ring glands (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E), in line with typical RNAi efficiencies sufficient to result in phenotypes. The two bHLH RNAi lines showed no cross-reactivity, since soul-RNAi did not affect tap expression, and vice versa (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Importantly, we observed dramatic effects in the expression of five ecdysone biosynthetic enzyme genes in PG > soulIR or PG > tapIR2 ring glands, where the expression of nvd, spok, phm, dib, and sad was either abolished or severely reduced (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F). Furthermore, 20E-responsive genes, including EcR, E74, and Br-C, exhibited 2- to 10-fold lower expression in PG > soulIR or PG > tapIR2 fat bodies (SI Appendix, Fig. S3G). Therefore, the downregulation of ecdysone biosynthesis and signaling genes in second and third instar larvae is strongly correlated with the developmental arrest observed at these stages (Fig. 1 D and F). This is evidenced by the severe phenotypes observed in L2 and L3 larvae, which were not present in L1 larvae. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Soul and Tap are essential for the expression of five Halloween genes, indicating that ecdysone production is completely dependent on these two transcription factors.

Soul and Tap are Essential for PG Development and the Establishment of CW.

To investigate the mechanisms by which Soul and Tap regulate ecdysone production, we examined PG growth following PG-specific loss-of-function of soul and tap. GFP-labeled PG cells (via PG > GFP) revealed that both PG size (Fig. 2A) and PG nuclei count (Fig. 2B) were markedly reduced by PG-specific soul- or tap-RNAi compared to the control. Additionally, the PGs of PG > soulIR and PG > tapIR2 appeared fragmented, with unclear boundaries (Fig. 2A). Intriguingly, the PG remained small even though larvae continued growing (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). When we used the weaker driver (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), spok-Gal4 (instead of phm22-Gal4), in conjunction with soul- or tap-RNAi, the ring glands also displayed growth defects, but to a lesser degree (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Given the involvement of the PG in establishing the CW checkpoint, which in turn regulates ecdysone production and metamorphosis in Drosophila (5, 6), we hypothesized that Soul and Tap may play a role in CW attainment. Based on previous findings from our lab, CW is typically attained around L3-9–10 h (i.e., 9 to 10 h after the L2/L3 molt) at 25 °C (13). When we analyzed the expression patterns of soul and tap throughout larval stages, we found that soul and tap reached their expression peak at L3-4 h (4 h after the L2/L3 molt), approximately 5 to 6 h before CW attainment (Fig. 2 C and D). Interestingly, the expression peaks of phm and spok, two ecdysteroidogenic genes, at the early L3 stage, lagged behind the peaks of soul and tap (Fig. 2 E and F), consistent with the idea that Soul/Tap regulate the expression of ecdysteroidogenic genes. These findings suggest that Soul and Tap play a critical role in establishing the CW checkpoint for ecdysone production.

To independently test the roles of Soul and Tap in regulating CW attainment, we employed a temperature-sensitive approach, where we combined UAS-Gal80ts and UAS-RNAi transgenes with phm22-Gal4 to disrupt gene expression in a temporally controlled manner. We have previously established that larvae reared at 18 °C reach the CW checkpoint around 168 to 175 h after egg deposition (AED) (13). To ensure that expression of the RNAi transgenes could occur before and after CW attainment, we transferred PG > Gal80ts;UAS-RNAi flies to 29 °C at 144 h AED (mid L2) or 168 h AED (early L3), to activate RNAi before and after CW attainment, respectively (Fig. 2G). Triggering RNAi before and after CW attainment (a 24-h difference) are referred to here as “early” and “late” shift, respectively. In the control group, survival rates remained unchanged regardless of the timing of the temperature shift (Fig. 2H). However, RNAi against soul or tap at the “early” shift caused widespread lethality at the L3 stage (Fig. 2 I and J), but not at the “late” shift, indicating that Soul and Tap have critical functions during the CW checkpoint.

The Chitin-Based Cuticle Gene Cpr49Ah and the POU Domain Transcription Factor pdm3 are Key Downstream Targets of Soul and Tap During CW Attainment.

Next, we aimed to identify downstream genes of the Soul/Tap heterodimer via genome-wide expression profiling in response to PG-specific RNAi. After ensuring that both Soul and Tap were nuclear proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), we dissected ring glands at two time points from carefully staged third instar PG > soulIR and PG > tapIR2 larvae, L3-4 h (4 h after L2/L3 molt), and L3-44 h (44 h after L2/L3 molt) and analyzed samples via RNA-Seq (Fig. 3A). L3-4 h corresponds to the time point of the initial expression peaks of soul and tap, occurring during the precritical weight (pre-CW) phase before CW attainment (13). In contrast, L3-44 h aligns with the time point of the second expression peak of tap, which coincides with the large ecdysone peak that triggers metamorphosis at the end of larval development (31). Overall, the overlap between the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from soul- and tap-RNAi cohorts was high and exceedingly significant (Fig. 3B and Dataset S1). Gene Ontology analysis via DAVID showed that genes that are down-regulated at both the L3-4 h and L3-44 h time points were significantly enriched for genes acting in the ecdysone biosynthetic pathway and the ecdysone precursor cholesterol trafficking (SI Appendix, Table S1). At the 4-h time point, down-regulated DEGs were particularly consistent in the two RNAi lines, and the overlap (28 genes) was highly enriched for genes acting in the ecdysone/sterol pathway (spok, nvd, phm, sad, Niemann-Pick type C-1a (Npc1a), Start1, ouib, and Organic anion transporting polypeptide 74D (Oatp74D) (SI Appendix, Table S2 and Dataset S1). The 28-gene cohort also harbored pdm3, which encodes a POU-domain transcription factor with PG-specific expression (23), and Cpr49Ah, which produces a putative cuticle protein and ranked seventh in the gene list of DEGs (SI Appendix, Table S2), which we will discuss in the next section. Similar to the early time point, the 44-h overlap for down-regulated genes was enriched for ecdysone/sterol genes, including spok, Npc1a, Start1, phm, dib, sad, nvd, sro, sna, curled (cu), torso, and Glutathione S transferase E14 (GstE14, aka nobo) (32) (SI Appendix, Table S2 and Dataset S1), strongly suggesting that Soul and Tap act as a heterodimer in vivo. The finding that the Halloween genes and the related regulatory genes were dramatically affected at both time points is consistent with the notion that ecdysone production is abolished in soul- or tap-RNAi animals. We validated the RNA-seq results via qPCR, which provided highly consistent results between the two approaches (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B).

To investigate the potential mechanisms of Soul/Tap-regulated CW establishment, we focused on DEGs at the L3-4 h time point, which is just prior to CW attainment. As mentioned above, the 28 down-regulated DEGs at L3-4 h were enriched for genes in the ecdysone biosynthesis pathway (SI Appendix, Table S2 and Dataset S1). Apart from the Halloween genes that are needed to convert dietary sterols to ecdysteroids (spok, nvd, phm, and sad), we found Npc1a and Start1, which are involved in cholesterol uptake and transport (33); Ouib, which is a transcription factor that directly controls Halloween gene expression (21); and Oatp74D, which acts as an ecdysone importer (34). While these eight genes are well known for their roles in ecdysone biosynthesis, they have not been linked to CW establishment. Thus, we hypothesized that these ecdysone synthesis genes represent a secondary response to the disruption of Soul and Tap function. To identify potential direct targets of the Soul/Tap heterodimer, we screened 35 down-regulated genes at L3-4 h via PG-specific RNAi, after excluding genes for which no resources were available, the ecdysone-related genes mentioned above, and non-protein-coding genes. These 35 genes were derived from the 28 common down-regulated genes at L3-4 h, as well as an additional seven candidate genes obtained by eliminating the RPKM threshold during the DEGs filtering process (Fig. 3C and Dataset S3). In total, only two RNAi lines showed significant lethality, with most PG > Cpr49Ah-RNAi and PG > pdm3-RNAi larvae not developing past the second instar larval stage (Fig. 3C). This suggested that Cpr49Ah and pdm3 represent key downstream targets of Soul/Tap. Cpr49Ah encodes a predicted cuticle protein, whereas Pdm3 is the ortholog of human POU6F2, a POU-domain transcription factor.

To validate the RNAi phenotypes of Cpr49Ah and pdm3, we generated gRNA lines that would allow us to conditionally mutate these genes in PG cells via somatic CRISPR/Cas9. Using this strategy, we confirmed that Cpr49Ah or pdm3 gene disruption significantly reduced survival rates at the L3, pupal, and adult stages (Fig. 3 D and E). Although these developmental deficiencies were not as severe as those observed with PG-specific RNAi, over 60% of the population failed to reach the adult stage. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with 20E improved survival to adulthood by 10 to 15% (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), suggesting that Cpr49Ah and Pdm3 impinge on ecdysone production. We tested the specificity of the Cpr49Ah- and pdm3-RNAi phenotypes also by employing the spok-Gal4 driver. Knockdowns mediated by spok-Gal4 of Cpr49Ah or pdm3 were consistent with the results obtained with phm22-Gal4, as they resulted in delayed pupation and decreased survival rates in both pupal and adult stages (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C and D). Additionally, 20E supplementation significantly improved pupal survival rates (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). To ensure that none of the phenotypes were caused by tracheal expression of the phm22-Gal4 driver, we used the btl-Gal4 line to drive RNAi in the trachea. We did not observe any phenotypes when we induced Cpr49Ah- or pdm3-RNAi in the trachea (SI Appendix, Fig. S7F), even though the btl-Gal4 driver was strongly expressed in the trachea (SI Appendix, Fig. S7G). Taken together, these results indicate that PG-specific disruption of Cpr49Ah and pdm3 function caused strong phenotypic effects that could be partially rescued with 20E supplementation.

To determine whether Cpr49Ah and Pdm3 affected PG growth in a similar manner as Soul and Tap, we examined the morphology of PGs in PG > Cpr49Ah-RNAi and PG > pdm3 -RNAi larvae. However, activating RNAi via the strong phm22-Gal4 driver caused mainly L2 arrest (Fig. 3C), complicating the analysis of PG morphology. Therefore, we restricted Gal4 activity by crossing tubulin-Gal80ts into the phm22-Gal4 background, which allowed us to temporally control the induction of RNAi and bypass the L2 lethality. Following this approach, we observed lethality in third instar larvae. Remarkably, RNAi directed against Cpr49Ah or pdm3 affected PG growth, resulting in substantially smaller ring glands (Fig. 4A), and depletion of Cpr49Ah also led to abnormal PG morphology, which was replicated with the spok-Gal4 line driving RNAi (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). We should note here that we have – in the course of various projects – generated thousands of PG-specific RNAi lines, and that we very rarely observed small or broken ring glands as phenotypes (<10 lines). Furthermore, activating RNAi against Cpr49Ah or pdm3 via the “early” shift blocked the larval–pupal transition, whereas RNAi in the post-CW phase (=“late” shift) had no significant effect on survival (Fig. 4 B and C), comparable to the results we obtained when disrupting soul or tap. This observation coincided with the expression pattern of Cpr49Ah and pdm3, which showed that these two genes exhibited expression peaks at L3-4 h (Fig. 4 D and E), prior to CW attainment. In conclusion, Cpr49Ah and Pdm3 appear to be crucial actors during CW attainment, suggesting that the phenotypes we observed in soul and tap loss of function lines were mainly caused by their downstream targets Cpr49Ah and pdm3.

To test whether Cpr49Ah and pdm3 are critical downstream targets of the Soul/Tap complex, we examined to which extent transgenes expressing Cpr49Ah and pdm3 cDNAs could rescue the soul and tap loss-of-function lines. Expressing Cpr49Ah and pdm3 cDNAs individually in the PG failed to rescue the L3 arrest triggered by PG-specific soul- or tap-RNAi. However, coexpression of Cpr49Ah and pdm3 resulted in a significant rescue, with nearly 60% of larvae progressing to the pupal stage, and over 40% of animals forming adults (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Moreover, the size and morphology of the PG was almost completely restored to the control levels upon coexpression of Cpr49Ah and pdm3 cDNAs in PG > soul-RNAi or PG > tap-RNAi animals (Fig. 4F). Since individually expressed Cpr49Ah and pdm3 cDNAs were unable to counter the developmental deficiencies in soul- and tap-depleted animals, we conclude that both Cpr49Ah and pdm3 are primary responsive genes of Soul and Tap.

Given that pdm3 encodes a transcription factor crucial for Soul/Tap heterodimer-mediated metamorphosis, we further investigated whether pdm3 could regulate ecdysone biosynthesis via transcription factors known to act in ecdysone biosynthesis regulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Overall, PG-specific pdm3-RNAi caused significant downregulation of the Halloween genes (~2 to 5-fold) (Fig. 4 G and H). Consistent with this, key transcription factor genes governing ecdysone biosynthesis, including sna, ouib, séan, and vvl, were threefold and fourfold down-regulated, whereas mld and kni appeared unaffected (Fig. 4I). Similarly, we observed a noticeable reduction in the expression of sna, ouib, and vvl in the RNA-seq data of PG > soul- and tap-RNAi samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B), supporting the critical role of Pdm3 in mediating the transcriptional effects of the Soul/Tap heterodimer during ecdysone production.

Soul/Tap Complex Binds to the Promoter of Cpr49Ah and pdm3 for their Transcription.

To further investigate whether Cpr49Ah and pdm3 are direct transcriptional targets of Soul and Tap, we utilized a luciferase reporter assay to examine the promoter activities of Cpr49Ah and pdm3 in S2 cells in the presence or absence of Soul/Tap. We found that the ~2.1 kb upstream region of Cpr49Ah conferred promoter activity even in the absence of Soul and Tap, however, in the presence of Soul and Tap we saw a significant increase in the transcriptional activity of Cpr49Ah (Fig. 5A). The ~2.8 kb upstream region of pdm3 also harbored promoter activity, which was only evident when Soul/Tap were present. Importantly, individual expression of Soul or Tap was insufficient to up-regulate either target gene (Fig. 5B), suggesting that Soul and Tap function as a heterodimer when binding to the regulatory region of Cpr49Ah and pdm3. bHLH transcription factors typically bind to E-boxes present in promoters and enhancers. When we analyzed for the presence of E-boxes in the upstream regions of Cpr49Ah and pdm3, we identified canonical E-boxes, as well as two noncanonical E-boxes (Fig. 5C). Subsequent analyses using a series of promoter truncations showed that deletion of the noncanonical E-box-1 reduced the promoter activities of both genes by nearly 50% (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the canonical E-box and the noncanonical E-box-2 also contributed slightly to the promoter activities. We then used Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assays (EMSAs) to examine whether the noncanonical E-box-1 enabled binding to the Soul/Tap complex. Indeed, combining both Soul and Tap resulted in a retarded band, which was not observed with either protein alone (Fig. 5D). In addition, the retarded band disappeared when we used a 100-fold molar excess of the unlabeled probe (harboring the wild-type noncanonical E-box-1) as specific competitor, while the 100-fold concentration of the mutated probe (unlabeled probemut) was not able to compete, strongly suggesting that the Soul/Tap complex specifically binds to the noncanonical E-box-1 for the transcriptional regulation of Cpr49Ah and pdm3. Collectively, these data indicate that the Soul/Tap heterodimer directly binds to the upstream regions of Cpr49Ah and pdm3 to regulate their transcription.

Discussion

Soul and Tap are Master Regulators of PG Development and CW Establishment.

Overactivation of insulin signaling in the PG leads to enlarged PG, causing premature metamorphosis in Drosophila, ultimately resulting in smaller individuals (35). Conversely, specific inhibition of insulin signaling in the PG can lead to smaller PG, thereby delaying metamorphosis and resulting in larger insects, which can be rescued by adding 20E to the food (5, 10, 35). 20E can also suppress insect growth by antagonizing insulin signaling (10). Besides insulin signaling, other regulatory factors that govern PG activity and metamorphosis have been identified, such as the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) pathway. Silencing of key genes in the EGFR signaling in the PG significantly inhibits PG growth, causing the fruit fly to arrest at the L3 stage and fail to complete metamorphosis; adding 20E to the food can rescue the larval arrest (36). Both the insulin and EGFR pathways appear to preferentially act directly on the ecdysone biosynthetic process itself (2). This view is primarily based on the significant rescue effects mediated by 20E supplementation (10, 36). In this study, we showed that silencing soul or tap strongly inhibited PG growth and cause tissue fragmentation. Even when we utilized GFP to mark PG cells, it remained difficult to ascertain the tissue outline. To the best of our knowledge, such severe developmental defects have not been previously reported for the PG. When we supplemented media with 20E, we were able to reverse the excessive larval growth, consistent with the idea that tap- and soul-RNAi lead to a decrease in ecdysone synthesis. However, feeding 20E could not rescue the larval arrest caused by the loss of soul or tap function, suggesting that other crucial (unidentified) processes were disrupted in these animals. Furthermore, when we controlled Tap and Soul depletion in a spatial and temporal manner, we showed that this affected CW establishment. In summary, Soul and Tap play a crucial role in PG development, and interfering with their function has more dramatic effects compared to the disruption of known signaling pathways such as insulin and EGFR on PG development. However, whether Soul and Tap interact with insulin and EGFR signaling to regulate PG development remains to be established in future studies.

There are no prior reports for Drosophila Soul, except for its brief mentioning of RNAi phenotypes when we identified the gene (CG33557) in a previous study (23). The mammalian orthologs of Soul are Scleraxis (Scx) and transcription factor 15 (Tcf15), and both have critical roles in chondrogenesis, normal tendon differentiation and formation, somitogenesis, and paraxial mesoderm development (37, 38). Drosophila Tap and its mammalian ortholog Neurogenin are both required for neurogenesis (39, 40). Therefore, Soul, Tap, and its mammalian counterparts appear to regulate a range of developmental processes including organ development. The physical interaction between Soul and Tap we presented in this study has not yet been described for the mammalian orthologs. Given that this interaction seems to be spatially restricted, it seems plausible that Scx/Tcf15 and Neurogenin may interact only in specific tissues or at specific times during development. The PG is neuronally controlled and is thus equivalent to the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis in mammals (41). The HPG axis controls puberty, which is not unlike insect metamorphosis, which also ensures sexual maturation. Interestingly, the mammalian orthologs of Soul and Tap are mainly expressed in the brain, the pituitary and thyroid glands, and gonads (42), consistent with the idea that the Soul/Tap is an ancient regulator of endocrine gland development. According to FlyBase (43), Soul and Tap are found in various insect taxa, including Diptera, Coleoptera, Hemiptera, and Lepidoptera. Future studies in other insects will clarify whether the roles of Soul and Tap are conserved across insect groups.

Soul and Tap Activate Transcriptional Cascades to Regulate PG Development.

We conducted RNA-seq to identify the transcriptional consequences of soul- and tap-RNAi, which should be enriched for direct targets of this transcription factor heterodimer. When we analyzed this gene cohort via PG-specific RNAi, we demonstrated that loss of pdm3 and Cpr49Ah function phenocopied many aspects of soul and tap depletion, suggesting a role for these genes in regulating PG growth, CW establishment, and metamorphosis. Importantly, coexpressing pdm3 and Cpr49Ah in the PG rescued the developmental defects and metamorphic block caused by the loss of soul or tap function. Using a luciferase reporter system and EMSA, we demonstrated that Soul and Tap bind directly to the upstream regions of pdm3 and Cpr49Ah, followed by transcriptional upregulation. Taken together, we propose that Soul and Tap form a heterodimer that binds to the promoters of pdm3 and Cpr49Ah, thus controlling PG development in a transcription-dependent manner.

Cpr49Ah encodes a protein with homology to chitin-based larval cuticle proteins (44), suggesting it acts as a structural protein in the extracellular space. This is consistent with the idea that this protein is required for tissue integrity of the PG, explaining the severe morphological defects of the PG in PG > soul-RNAi and PG > tap-RNAi larvae. A recent study demonstrated that the chitin-binding protein Obstructor-A (Obst-A) is expressed in PG cells and acts as part of the extracellular matrix, crucial for maintaining the normal morphology and function of the ring gland, thus supporting ecdysone production (45). Interestingly, we also found that Obst-A expression was significantly reduced in late L3 larvae following the knockdown of soul (8.5-fold down, P < 0.01) and tap (12-fold down, P < 0.02) (Dataset S1). Based on these findings, we propose that Soul and Tap modulate PG morphology by influencing the properties of the extracellular matrix via the transcriptional regulation of chitin-related genes. pdm3 encodes a POU domain transcription factor that has important roles in regulating olfactory receptor gene expression and axon targeting in olfactory receptor neurons, as well as establishing neuronal diversity and synaptic connectivity (46, 47). Prior to this study, the only link between Pdm3 and ecdysone was our finding that the pdm3 transcript was highly enriched in the PG, but no studies have examined its roles in PG development and metamorphosis (20, 23). In this study, we found that pdm3 is a direct target of the Soul/Tap heterodimer, and is critical for the larval–pupal transition, likely due to its requirement for the proper expression of the Halloween genes (Fig. 4H) and other PG-specific transcription factors (Fig. 4I). Compared to the PG fragmentation phenotypes caused by the loss of soul, tap, or Cpr49Ah, RNAi directed against pdm3 had a relatively minor impact on PG morphology as it did not significantly affect the structural integrity of the PG. In summary, Cpr49Ah appears to establish or maintain the structural integrity of the PG tissue, whereas the role of Pdm3 appears to be more tightly linked to the regulatory hierarchy controlling ecdysone biosynthesis.

Based on the RNA-seq results in this study, Soul/Tap appears to also control the expression of ouib, a transcription factor that controls—in cooperation with Mld—the expression of spok, a Halloween gene. As such, it is possible that Soul and Tap regulate spok transcription indirectly via Ouib, since previous studies have found that Ouib specifically regulates spok expression (21). In addition, Soul and Tap also regulate the expression of multiple transcription factors to varying degrees, including sna, vvl, kni, mld, and séan (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Sna is a member of the C2H2 zinc finger superfamily of transcription factor. Sna affects PG size and CW exit by regulating DNA endoreplication within PG cells, thus impinging on ecdysone synthesis (13). POU domain protein Vvl and nuclear receptor protein Kni can directly bind to the promoters of ecdysteroidogenic genes such as phm to regulate transcription (22). Both Mld and Séan, also zinc finger proteins, cooperatively regulate nvd transcription (21). Silencing pdm3 resulted in widespread downregulation of Halloween gene transcription (Fig. 4H), and caused reduced expression of key transcription factors including sna, ouib, séan, and vvl around 1 to 2 h prior to CW attainment (L3-8 h, Fig. 4I), suggesting that Soul/Tap is a critical component of a transcription factor network controlling PG growth and metamorphosis.

Soul and Tap Regulate PG Development in a Spatiotemporally Specific Manner.

In Drosophila, a small 20E peak occurs prior to L3-8 h, which likely contributes to CW establishment in the pre-CW phase (31, 48, 49). In this study, we demonstrated that the expression of soul and tap peaks at L3-4 h, and knockdown of soul or Tap in the pre-CW phase rather than post-CW phase blocked PG growth and arrested larval development. Importantly, at L3-4 h, we found that the expression of the Halloween genes phm, sad, and spok was substantially down-regulated after soul or tap depletion, raising the possibility that Soul and Tap contribute to CW attainment via the production of the small 4-h ecdysone peak during the pre-CW phase. The underlying mechanism of the early L3 ecdysone peak remains unclear, but it is possible that it is tied to the expression or regulation of Cpr49Ah and pdm3.

Transcript levels of both soul and pdm3 are highly enriched (>10-fold) in the ring gland (4 h after the L2/L3 molt) compared to whole body samples, suggesting that the Soul/Tap-Pdm3 axis is part of a PG-specific transcription factor hierarchy driving PG development. Given the distinct PG phenotypes after Soul/Tap depletion, it seems plausible that this hierarchy likely operates independently of the insulin pathway, which results in PG overgrowth when impaired. Sna encodes another transcription factor with PG-specific expression, however, its function appears to be involved in CW exit rather than CW establishment (13). Sna has two expression peaks, one at the L2 stage, and the second peak occurring in L3 around 8 to 12 h after the molt, the latter coinciding with the exit of the CW checkpoint. These two sna expression peaks correlate with the occurrence of DNA endoreplication in PG cells, and knockdown of sna in the pre-CW phase blocked CW exit and consequently, prevented the larval-to-pupal transition (13). Expression peaks of soul, tap, and pdm3 occur at L3-4-h, which is prior to the expression peak of sna, suggesting Sna works downstream of Soul/Tap-Pdm3 signaling. This idea is supported by the observation that pdm3-RNAi caused a ~4-fold reduction of sna expression at L3-8-h (Fig. 4I). Taken together, we propose that Soul/Tap act upstream in a transcriptional cascade comprising Pdm3, Cpr49Ah, and Sna to regulate PG development.

PG Function Beyond Ecdysone Production.

If the PG’s sole role was producing ecdysteroids, one would expect that the majority of RNAi lines targeting gene function specifically in the PG should be rescuable by dietary supplementation with 20E. However, it is more often the exception than the rule that ecdysone feeding can rescue the lethality caused by PG-specific RNAi. Rescue attempts typically succeed when RNAi targets enzymes involved in ecdysone biosynthesis, yet they often fail when targeting other genes within the PG (7, 29). For instance, RNAi against EGFR leads to smaller PG and larval arrest. While feeding 20E in this case allows animals to form pupae, it does not suffice to reach adulthood (36). Similarly, in this study, we found that RNAi targeting soul and tap inhibits PG growth and the larval-to-pupal transition, resulting in larger larvae. Despite restoring larval size with 20E, pupation remains blocked. These observations suggest that the PG has other crucial functions besides producing ecdysone, suggesting the existence of other secreted hormones. The ring gland is enriched for at least nine transcripts that encode peptide hormones, including spatzle 5 and anachronism (23), suggesting that the PG is a complex hub of endocrine activity. Further investigation into these secretory proteins and their regulatory effects on metamorphosis is needed to fully understand the regulatory roles of the PG. Moreover, 7DC, produced in the PG as a precursor for ecdysone biosynthesis, may have functions beyond its role in ecdysone production. This hypothesis is supported by the observation that while 7DC can rescue loss of neverland function, 20E fails to do so (50). This is surprising since the ecdysone synthesis pathway is thought to begin with the production of 7DC from a suitable dietary sterol, and if this were a linear pathway, both 7DC and 20E should rescue. To elucidate the additional functions of the PG beyond ecdysone production, future research could examine the effects of PG-specific RNAi targeting secretory protein genes, followed by phenotypic, molecular, and metabolomic analysis. This integrated approach may shed light on the multifaceted roles of the PG in orchestrating metamorphosis.

Conclusion

In summary, our study reveals that the bHLH transcription factors Soul and Tap act in concert to regulate PG development and CW establishment, thereby orchestrating ecdysone production and metamorphosis in Drosophila (Fig. 5H). Through directly targeting key genes such as Cpr49Ah and pdm3, Soul and Tap control PG growth and CW attainment in a spatiotemporal-specific manner. This regulatory process involves the activation of transcriptional cascades, potentially implicating multiple transcription factors including Pdm3, Ouib, Sna, Séan, and Vvl. Importantly, our findings suggest that Soul/Tap-mediated PG growth may influence metamorphosis beyond ecdysone production. Overall, this study contributes to our understanding of CW establishment and insect metamorphosis at the molecular level, shedding light on the complex transcriptional regulatory network governing PG development and metamorphosis.
