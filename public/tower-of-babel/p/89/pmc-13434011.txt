The G protein-coupled estrogen receptor in the nucleus accumbens mediates the estrogen-induced increase in cataplexy-like behavior
Authors: Jun Kaminosono, Hikari Nagayama, Ikue Kusumoto-Yoshida, Hideki Kashiwadani, Tomoyuki Kuwaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstrogen functions not only as a female reproductive hormone but also as a psychological agent in the brains of both sexes. Here, we examined the potential effect of estrogen on cataplexy attacks, which are triggered by positive emotions in narcoleptic humans and animals. In orexin neuron-ablated mice, an animal model of narcolepsy, the number of cataplexy-like behaviors increased with estrogen administration and decreased with tamoxifen, an estrogen receptor (ER) modulator, in both sexes. The increase in cataplexy-like behaviors caused by estrogen in ovariectomized female mice indicated its action outside reproductive organs. In female mice, the estrous cycle influenced the frequency of cataplexy-like behaviors, with the highest count during estrus, when blood estrogen levels peak. In a pharmacological study with male mice, a G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER) antagonist reduced the frequency of cataplexy-like behaviors, while a GPER agonist increased it. Conversely, neither an ER alpha agonist nor an ER beta agonist showed any effect. The GPER agonist activated neurons in the nucleus accumbens (NAc). Finally, microinjection of a GPER agonist into the NAc increased the occurrence of cataplexy-like behaviors. Together, these results demonstrate that GPER in the NAc mediates the psychological effects of estrogen on cataplexy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54759-8.Keywords: Sex hormones, Psychological effect, Positive emotion, Estrous cycle, Narcolepsy type-1Subject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Estrogen functions not only as a female reproductive hormone but also as a psychological agent in the brains of both sexes. For example, postmenopausal depression, improved memory with estrogen supplementation1, social recognition and learning2, and emotional changes during the estrous cycle have been reported3. A deficiency in estrogen may lead to postmenopausal depression4. However, the exact mechanisms underlying estrogen’s psychological effects remain unknown.

We previously identified the rostral part of the nucleus accumbens (NAc) shell as a key region involved in triggering cataplexy5,6. It serves as a behavioral indicator of positive emotion in people with type 1 narcolepsy7,8 and in a mouse model of narcolepsy9,10. Using the Tet system and virus-mediated designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADD), chemogenetic activation of the NAc increased the number of cataplexy attacks in an animal model of narcolepsy6. Optogenetic activation of the NAc also increased the frequency of cataplexy attacks5. In later experiments aimed at better understanding the neural basis of positive emotion, we attempted to introduce the inducible Cre recombinase system into our setup. We eventually discovered that tamoxifen itself reduced the number of cataplexy attacks. Tamoxifen, used as a pharmacological switch to induce Cre-mediated recombination of the target gene by translocating the Cre recombinase-estrogen receptor complex into the nucleus11, acts as a selective estrogen receptor modulator and is used to prevent breast cancer in women and men12.

Our unexpected discovery of tamoxifen’s potential effect on cataplexy prompted us to explore the link between estrogen and positive emotion. In this study, we first confirmed that tamoxifen decreases cataplexy-like behaviors, whereas estrogen increases them, in both sexes of orexin neuron-ablated (ORX-AB) mice, an animal model of type-1 narcolepsy13. Next, we tested whether natural fluctuations in estrogen levels across the estrous cycle influence cataplexy-like behaviors in female mice, with or without milk chocolate, a positive emotion inducer. Although estrous cycle-related changes in cataplexy have been reported in female mice14, no mechanistic explanation has been provided. Third, we identified which of the three estrogen receptor subtypes are involved: the membrane-bound G protein-coupled receptor (GPER)15 and the intracellular alpha- and beta-receptors. Finally, we examined whether the GPER agonist activates the NAc and whether microinjecting the GPER agonist into the NAc affects cataplexy-like behaviors. In the third and fourth experiments, we used only male mice to avoid interference from the estrous cycle, enabling a more precise dissection of GPER-mediated mechanisms.

Results

Tamoxifen reduced, whereas estrogen increased, the number of cataplexy-like behaviors

We observed that tamoxifen administration significantly reduced the number of cataplexy-like behaviors in both male and female ORX-AB mice (Fig. 1a, b). In contrast, estrogen administration notably increased this behavior. Vehicle treatment produced a higher number of cataplexy-like behaviors in females (57.0 ± 10.1, n = 6) than in males (31.4 ± 3.7, n = 8), although the difference was not statistically significant (effect size [Cohen’s d] = 1.44, p = 0.052, Welch’s t test). Female variability was significantly greater than in males, with a higher coefficient of variation in females (0.433) than in males (0.332) (effect size [Cohen’s f] = 0.634, p = 0.043 by F test).

A repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant differences among drug treatments in male (F1.549, 10.85 = 45.96, p < 0.0001), naïve female (F1.127, 5.633 = 16.51, p = 0.0069), ovariectomized female (F1.027, 9.244 = 10.57, p = 0.0093), and sham-operated female (F1.143, 5.716 = 17.54, p = 0.0057). To explore whether high variability in females might be linked to fluctuating estrogen levels, ovariectomized females were used for cataplexy counting (Fig. 1c, d). As expected, ovariectomized females (16.2 ± 3.4, n = 10) showed fewer cataplexy-like behaviors than sham-operated controls (35.3 ± 7.3, n = 6) (effect size [Cohen’s d] = 1.40, p = 0.047, Welch’s t test). Tamoxifen did not further reduce cataplexy-like behaviors in ovariectomized females but did so in sham-operated females. Estrogen significantly increased cataplexy-like behaviors in both ovariectomized and sham-operated females. There was no difference in the duration of cataplexy-like episodes (see Supplemental Table 1 online).

4-Hydroxytamoxifen, the tamoxifen metabolite with higher affinity for estrogen receptors than tamoxifen16, also reduced the number of cataplexy-like behaviors in male mice (see Supplemental Fig. 1 online). Together, these findings support the idea that estrogen increases the occurrence of cataplexy-like behaviors in both male and female ORX-AB mice.

The estrous cycle affected the frequency of cataplexy-like behavior

To investigate whether the high variability in cataplexy frequency among females relates to fluctuating estrogen levels, we identified the estrous cycle in individual mice immediately after observing cataplexy-like behaviors. The cycle was determined by vaginal smear cytology (Fig. 2a). As expected, mice in estrus exhibited the highest number of cataplexy-like behaviors, whereas those in metestrus/diestrus showed the lowest (Fig. 2b).

Notably, this variation was more pronounced when chocolate, a known inducer of cataplexy17, was added to the regular diet. A potential interaction between estrogen and chocolate was also examined in male mice by combining estrogen administration with chocolate feeding (see Supplemental Fig. 2 online). Although both estrogen and chocolate increased cataplexy-like behavior, there was no interaction between the two. Therefore, we concluded that the effects are additive rather than synergistic.

Using a second group of animals, we measured plasma estrogen levels by ELISA (Fig. 3). Levels were significantly higher during estrus than during diestrus, and diestrus levels were not significantly different from those in males or ovariectomized females. These findings further confirmed that estrogen increases the number of cataplexy-like behaviors in mice.

A GPER agonist increased cataplexy-like behaviors, whereas neither an ER alpha nor an ER beta agonist did

Estrogen receptors are classified into three subtypes: membrane-bound G protein-coupled receptors and intracellular α- and β-receptors. To determine which receptor mediates the estrogen-induced increase in cataplexy-like behaviors, we administered GPER agonists and antagonists, along with ERα and ERβ agonists, and then observed behavior in male mice. We used only male mice from this point forward because the estrous cycle affects the number of cataplexy-like behaviors in females (Fig. 2), and estrogen also has an enhancing effect in male mice (Fig. 1). We used male mice to avoid endogenous fluctuations in estrogen in female mice and to perform a more precise dissection of estrogen receptor-mediated mechanisms.

As a result, a GPER agonist, G1, increased, and an antagonist, G15, decreased the number of cataplexy-like behaviors (Fig. 4). In contrast, both an ERα agonist, PPT, and an ERβ agonist, DPN, had no effect. These findings indicate a role for GPER in the estrogen-induced increase in cataplexy.

A GPER agonist activated neurons in the nucleus accumbens, whereas an antagonist inhibited those neurons

We previously showed that chocolate-induced cataplexy in ORX-AB mice results from activation of neurons in the NAc5,6. To determine whether the NAc is similarly involved in the estrogen-induced increase in cataplexy-like behaviors, we examined NAc activation using c-Fos immunohistochemistry in another cohort of mice. Male ORX-AB mice were given estrogen, tamoxifen, G1 (a GPER agonist), G15 (a GPER antagonist), or a vehicle control, and were sacrificed 2 hours later. The level of c-Fos expression in the NAc was higher in mice treated with estrogen and G1 than in controls (Fig. 5a and b), and lower in those treated with tamoxifen and G15. Most c-Fos-positive neurons also expressed GPER (see Supplementary Fig. 3 online), suggesting that GPER-expressing neurons in the NAc may contribute to estrogen-induced cataplexy. In addition, we examined the possible interaction between chocolate and estrogen on NAc activation (Fig. 5c and d). As expected, the chocolate plus vehicle-treated group showed greater c-Fos (55.6 ± 5.2/0.1 mm2, n = 6) than the vehicle-alone group (22.2 ± 1.8/0.1 mm2, n = 6). Adding estrogen to the chocolate treatment further increased c-Fos expression, whereas tamoxifen decreased it. These results indicated that the effects of chocolate and estrogen on NAc activation were additive, as was the case with cataplexy-like behaviors in female mice during the estrous cycle (Fig. 2).

Microinjection of a GPER agonist into the NAc increased the number of cataplexy-like episodes, whereas an antagonist decreased it

Finally, we examined the potential effects of estrogen receptor modulators injected into the NAc on cataplexy-like behavior, since systemic injection (Fig. 4) cannot specify the site of drug action. A pair of guide-cannula-injected male mice (Fig. 6a, b) were injected with estrogen, G1 (a GPER agonist), G15 (a GPER antagonist), or vehicle 30 min before behavioral testing. As a result, estrogen and G1 increased cataplexy-like behavior, whereas G15 decreased it (Fig. 6c). Adding chocolate to regular chow appeared to have an additive effect. As a control in another cohort of animals, drugs were injected into the dorsal striatum (see Supplemental Fig. 4 online). Neither G1 nor G15 affected cataplexy-like behavior, further supporting the involvement of the NAc.

Collectively, these results support the idea that GPER, at least in the NAc, mediates the estrogen-induced increase in cataplexy-like behavior.

Discussion

We showed that estrogen, at both pharmacological (Fig. 1) and physiological levels (Fig. 2), increases cataplexy-like behavior in male and female mice. Although the main source of estrogen in female mice appears to be the ovary (Fig. 3), estrogen’s effect on cataplexy seems to occur in the brain for the following reasons. First, estrogen-related drugs affected the frequency of cataplexy-like behavior in both sexes (Fig. 1). Second, estrogen increased cataplexy-like behavior even in ovariectomized females. Third, if plasma estrogen is the sole determinant of cataplexy, ovariectomized females (Fig. 1b) should show cataplexy similar to that in males (Fig. 1a), since they have similar plasma estrogen levels (Fig. 3). However, ovariectomized female mice had a lower frequency of cataplexy-like behavior than males under vehicle treatment, indicating that other factors beyond plasma estrogen are involved. We suppose that brain estrogen and/or brain GPER concentration may differ between males and ovariectomized females, although this hypothesis should be clarified in a future experiment. Fourth, cataplexy-like behavior is controlled by the brain, specifically the rostral part of the NAc5,6, and this area was activated by estrogen receptor agonists (Fig. 5). Finally, the enzyme aromatase, which produces estrogen, is present in the brains of both males and females18.

We found that estrogen’s effect on cataplexy depends on GPER activation rather than on intracellular receptors ERα or ERβ (Fig. 4). Estrogen’s antidepressant and anxiolytic effects are believed to be mediated by ERβ, whereas depressive and anxiogenic effects are associated with ERα19–21. However, reports on GPER are contradictory20,22,23. At least for cataplexy, a positive emotion-related behavior, the effect relies on GPER.

We confirmed GPER presence in the NAc (supplemental Fig. 3 online) and found that microinjecting GPER agonists into the NAc but not into the dorsal striatum increased cataplexy-like behaviors (Fig. 6 and supplemental Fig. 4). In contrast, a GPER antagonist decreased these behaviors, suggesting that the NAc is one of the active sites of estrogen in the brain that induces cataplexy-like behaviors. The presence of GPER in the NAc aligns with earlier reports18,24,25. Nevertheless, we do not exclude contributions from other areas, as GPER is also expressed in other brain regions, including the cortex, hypothalamus, and hippocampus24. We also examined the potential interaction between estrogen and chocolate, a known inducer of positive emotions (Figs. 2, 5 and 6, and supplemental Fig. 2). The results showed that the effects of estrogen-related drugs and chocolate on NAc activation and the frequency of cataplexy-like behavior were additive, indicating that these effects sum in the NAc and that greater NAc activation induces more cataplexy-like behaviors.

Methodological considerations and limitations of the study

Because tamoxifen and 4OH-tamoxifen reduce cataplexy-like behaviors (Fig. 1 and Supplemental Fig. 1 online), caution is warranted when using these drugs with the inducible Cre recombinase system, especially in psychological studies. Our previous data support the idea that cataplexy-inducing NAc neurons are GABAergic medium spiny neurons (MSNs)5,6, and MSNs are reported to express GPER25. However, we currently lack direct evidence that GPER-expressing MSNs trigger cataplexy. In other words, although we found GPER in NAc neurons (Supplemental Fig. 3 online), the specific features of these neurons, such as their neurotransmitters and connections, remain unknown. Additionally, we do not know the source of estrogen that binds GPER in the NAc. Since blood estrogen levels are positively related to the frequency of cataplexy-like behaviors (Figs. 2 and 3), they might be the source. However, the modulation of cataplexy may not scale linearly with receptor activation, since we did not perform a dose-response test in this study. Interestingly, in this context, the estrogen-synthesizing enzyme aromatase is expressed in the NAc of both sexes18, indicating another potential source of estrogen. This too remains an open question.

Clinical relevance

No clear sex difference in the incidence of human narcolepsy has been reported8,26,27. However, this and other reports14 clearly show that the estrous cycle is associated with fluctuations in the incidence of female cataplexy, at least in the mouse model of narcolepsy. A detailed examination of the incidence of female human cataplexy would promote a better understanding and more effective treatment of human cataplexy. The onset of human type-1 narcolepsy is reported to be 17 ± 9 years27. This period overlaps with sexual maturation, and thus estrogen and probably testosterone, a source of male estrogen, may be related to the etiology and development of the disease.

In sum, these findings demonstrate that GPER in the NAc mediates estrogen’s effects on cataplexy. GPER in the NAc may be a new target for the prevention and treatment of cataplexy, mood disorders, and depression.

Methods

Animals and ethics approval

For the animal model of narcolepsy, orexin neuron-ablated (ORX-AB) mice13 of both sexes were used. They were 8–12 weeks old at the start of the experiment and weighed 24–32 g. The original breeding pairs, a gift from Prof. Yamanaka at Nagoya University, were bred at Kagoshima University’s animal facility. A method for selective ablation of orexin neurons has been previously reported13. Briefly, orexin-tTA mice, which express tetracycline transactivator (tTA) exclusively in orexin neurons under the control of the human prepro-orexin promoter, were bred with tetO diphtheria toxin A fragment (DTA) mice (B6.Cg-Tg (tetO DTA) 1Gfi/J, The Jackson Laboratory) to produce orexin-tTA; tetO DTA mice. In these double transgenic mice (referred to as ORX-AB here), doxycycline was removed from their chow starting at birth, leading to ablation of almost all (> 97%) orexin neurons by 4 weeks of age13. The ablation of orexin neurons was confirmed in our previous studies5,6,10,28. All mice were housed in a room maintained at 22–24 °C, with lights on from 7:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m. Food and water were available ad libitum.

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guiding principles for the care and use of animals in physiological research published by the Physiological Society of Japan (2015) and approved by the Institutional Animal Use Committees at Kagoshima University (MD22024).

Behavioral observation of cataplexy

On the day of the experiment, ORX-AB mice in their home cages were individually placed into a soundproof recording chamber. Mouse behavior was continuously recorded with a video camera equipped with infrared lights around the lens (KL36IR, Ailipu Technology, Shenzhen, China) and monitored on a personal computer outside the soundproof chamber. Cataplexy was identified based on established criteria for mice29, which include several observable features. The first feature is an abrupt episode of nuchal atonia lasting at least 10 s. Atonia was considered to occur when mice were in a prone position, with their head and belly on the bedding and their limbs and tail typically stretched straight out from the trunk. This posture contrasts with a normal sleeping position, where mice are curled up, with limbs and tail folded underneath their trunk. Second, the mouse remains immobile aside from breathing movements during an episode. Finally, there must be at least 40 s of active wakefulness (movement) before the atonia episode. Additionally, care was taken to exclude behavioral arrests other than cataplexy-like behavior, such as sleep, freezing, and delta sleep attack (a peculiar sleep attack in the ORX-AB mice30, by observing collapsing body posture; the distance from nose to tail is greater than in standing or sleeping postures. The number of cataplexy-like behaviors was counted in a manner blinded to treatment.

Ovariectomy

Ovariectomy was performed according to the textbook31. Briefly, the mouse was anesthetized with isoflurane (2–3%) and positioned on its ventral side. A small midline dorsal skin incision was made, and subcutaneous connective tissue was gently dissected to expose the dorsolateral abdominal muscles. A peritoneal incision was made on one side of the abdominal muscles. The ovary was gently exteriorized through this muscle incision. The junction between the fallopian tube and the uterine horn was cut, and the horn was returned to the abdominal cavity. The same procedure was performed on the other side. The dorsal skin incision was closed with a staple. After surgery, the animal received antibiotics (penicillin G, 40,000 U/kg) and analgesics (buprenorphine, 0.05 mg/kg). Sham operation was performed in the same way, except that the fallopian tube was not cut. Behavioral measurement began after a recovery period of more than 2 weeks, when body weight had returned to preoperative levels.

Estrous cycle assessment

A vaginal smear was collected in the morning after the behavioral observation during the night (the mice’s active period). We assessed the estrous cycle stage using vaginal cytology32. Cytology is the primary method for determining the estrous cycle stage, based on the proportions of different cell types.

Determination of plasma estrogen concentration

Mice were deeply anesthetized with urethane (1.8 g/kg, i.p.), and blood was collected by transcardial puncture into a heparin-coated syringe. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1500 g, and the methanol-soluble fraction was extracted per the manufacturer’s instructions (Estradiol ELISA kit, 501890, Cayman Chemicals, RRID: AB_2832924). Samples were stored at −80 °C until assayed.

Estrogen receptor-related reagents

We used tamoxifen (Tm, Sigma T5684, 10 mg/ml), 4-hydroxy tamoxifen (4OHTm, a potent metabolite of Tm16, Sigma H6278, 10 mg/ml), 17beta-estradiol (E2, Nacalai 14541-61, 1 mg/ml), G1 (GPER agonist33, LNS8801, Cayman Chemical, 1 mg/ml), G15 (GPER antagonist33, GRB-G15, Selleck Chemicals, 1 mg/ml), propyl-pyrazole-triol (PPT, ER alpha agonist34, Cayman Chemicals, 1 mg/ml), and diarylpropionitrile (DPN, ER beta agonist34, Tokyo Kasei, 1 mg/ml). These compounds were dissolved in sesame oil (Nacalai 25620-65) and administered intraperitoneally (0.01 ml/g body weight) or microinjected into the NAc (see below) 30 min before behavioral recording began. When testing multiple drugs in one animal, at least 3 days were allowed between tests, and the order of administration was randomly assigned.

Microinjection into the NAc

Because GPER is widely distributed throughout the body35, the specific role of GPER in the NAc was examined by microinjection. To this end, a bilateral guide cannula (C235GS-5-2.0, Protech International Inc.) was implanted above the NAc (coordinates: anterior 1.20 mm, lateral 1.0 mm, and ventral 4.0 mm from bregma) under isoflurane (1.5–2.0%) anesthesia at least 7 days before the experiments. As a control in another cohort of the animals, drugs were also injected into the dorsal striatum (coordinates: anterior 1.20 mm, lateral 1.0 mm, and ventral 2.0 mm from bregma). Drugs were injected at 200 nl per side.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized with urethane (1.8 g/kg, i.p.) and euthanized by exsanguination. They were transcardially perfused with 25 mL of PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4), followed by 25 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brain was removed, post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight, and immersed in 30% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C for 2 days. A series of 40-µm sections was cut with a vibratome (SuperMicroSlicer Zero1; DOSAKA EM, Kyoto, Japan), and every fourth section was used for immunostaining. The brain sections were incubated in a blocking solution (1% normal horse serum and 0.3% Triton-X in 0.01 M PBS) for 1 h at room temperature (20–24 °C) and then immunostained. The primary antibodies included anti-c-Fos rabbit antibody (2250 S, Cell Signaling Technology, RRID: AB_2247211, 1:1000), anti-GPER goat antibody (LS-B5132, Life Span Bioscience, RRID: AB_10851647, 1:400), and anti-NeuN guinea pig antibody (266004, Synaptic Systems, RRID: AB_2619988, 1:400). The sections were washed with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies: anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with CF488 fluorescent dye (raised in donkey, Biotium 20015, RRID: AB_10559669, 1:500), anti-goat IgG conjugated with CF647 fluorescent dye (raised in donkey, Biotium 20048, RRID: AB_10853455, 1:500), or anti-guinea pig IgG conjugated with CF568 fluorescent dye (raised in donkey, Biotium 20377, RRID: AB_2934264, 1:500) for 90 min. The number of c-Fos-positive cells in the NAc was quantified in a manner blinded to treatment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism software v.10 (GraphPad, RRID: SCR_002798). We used a repeated-measures one-way ANOVA with the Geisser-Greenhouse correction to relax the sphericity assumption, or a one-way ANOVA to compare three or more groups. When ANOVA revealed a significant main effect, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Holm-Sidak’s multiple-comparison test. A paired or unpaired t-test (two-tailed) was selected based on the data structure. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess residual normality, and all data passed these tests (p = 0.05). No attempt was made to exclude potential outliers. No statistical methods were used to determine the sample size in advance. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Effect sizes were reported as eta squared for ANOVA, Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons, and Cohen’s f for the F test.
