Early-onset biliary tract cancers: clinical and molecular insights

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of early-onset biliary tract cancers (BTCs), including cholangiocarcinoma and gallbladder adenocarcinoma, is rising, but factors underlying this trend remain unclear.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective study of BTC patients across three Mayo Clinic sites (2008-2024). Patients were categorized as early-onset (aged <50 years) or older-onset (aged ≥50 years). Clinical characteristics, comorbidities, molecular alterations, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes were compared between groups.ResultsA total of 549 patients were included; 170 were enriched for early-onset cases, compared with a cohort of older-onset patients (n = 379) identified through the TEMPUS registry. Compared with older-onset patients [median age 68.0 years, interquartile range (IQR) 60.4, 73.0], early-onset patients (median age 40.5 years, IQR 34.5, 45.1) had significantly fewer metabolic comorbidities, higher frequency of FGFR2 alteration (18.8% versus 6.3%, P < 0.001) and lower frequency of KRAS alteration (15.3% versus 23.0%, P = 0.040). Tumor mutational burden was modestly lower in early-onset disease (3.0 versus 3.7 mutations/megabase, P = 0.003). Early-onset patients were more likely to enroll in clinical trials (25.9% versus 14.0%, P < 0.001) and received more lines of therapy (P = 0.005). Overall survival did not differ significantly between age groups. Among patients receiving first-line gemcitabine–cisplatin with immune checkpoint inhibition, progression-free survival was similar between early-onset and older-onset disease (7.6 versus 6.6 months; hazard ratio, 1.12; P = 0.59).ConclusionsEarly-onset BTC demonstrated distinct clinical and molecular profiles, but survival outcomes remained comparable to older-onset BTC. These findings highlight the need for further research into specific oncogenic drivers and tailored therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Incidence and mortality of biliary tract cancers (BTCs), including gallbladder adenocarcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma, are rising among younger individuals.1, 2, 3 The National Cancer Institute (NCI) defines early-onset cancers as those occurring in individuals aged 18-49 years. In the United States, analysis of the NCI Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database suggested annual percentage increases in early-onset intrahepatic bile duct and gallbladder cancers between 2010 and 2019 of 8.12% and 2.36%, respectively.4 Another recent report identified an additional 14 cancer types showing increasing incidence in younger groups in the United States,5 highlighting that this alarming trend is not unique to BTC.

A birth cohort effect is suggested by increasing BTC incidence over successively younger generations.1 The potential carcinogenic exposures in early life and young adulthood leading to this rise are not fully known. Obesity, diabetes mellitus, and metabolic syndromes are known risk factors for BTC,6, 7, 8 and there are increasing odds of developing these conditions in generations born in more recent decades.9,10 It is unclear if such comorbidities are seen more commonly with early-onset (age <50) than with older-onset (age ≥50) BTC.

Despite the increasing incidence of early-onset BTC, these cancers remain rare, and data are limited and often contradictory regarding disease biology and outcomes for these patients. For example, the Cholangiocarcinoma in the Young (CITY) study reported that patients with early-onset cholangiocarcinoma were more likely to have larger tumors and nodal involvement on pathological specimens after resection but similar recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared with older individuals.11 Another multi-institutional database study suggested early-onset intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma was associated with shorter RFS despite finding that younger patients had earlier T and N stage on resection specimens.12 In the advanced disease setting, a small study including 45 patients (aged <45 years) with early-onset cholangiocarcinoma suggested worse overall survival (OS) with systemic therapy for stage IV disease compared with older-onset patients.13 Conversely, the CITY study reported longer OS among younger patients presenting with unresectable or metastatic disease (17.7 months versus 13.5 months, P = 0.049).11 Another European study similarly suggested improved survival among early-onset patients with metastatic BTCs [22 months, 95% confidence interval (CI) 18-26] compared with average-onset (51-69 years old; 18 months, 95% CI 17-20, P = 0.01) and late-onset (≥70 years old) patients (15 months, 95% CI 13-17, P < 0.001).14

First-line systemic therapy for advanced BTC includes immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) with durvalumab15 or pembrolizumab16 on the backbone of chemotherapy with gemcitabine–cisplatin. There is also a growing list of molecularly targetable alterations in BTC.17, 18, 19, 20 It is possible that early-onset BTCs may exhibit unique molecular characteristics and pathogenic mutations. Higher rates of FGFR2 fusion and ATM and BRAF alterations were noted in a cohort of ∼76 individuals with cholangiocarcinoma, predominantly aged 40-50 years, as compared with older-onset patients.11 Another small cohort of 27 patients with early-onset intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma showed higher frequency of KMT2C and ASXL1 mutations compared with 167 patients with older-onset disease.13

The conflicting results described above were limited by small sample sizes and underrepresentation of gallbladder BTC. The purpose of the present study was to compare clinical and molecular characteristics and treatment patterns, including rates of clinical trial enrollment and outcomes, in patients with early-onset versus older-onset BTC.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

We performed a retrospective cohort study of patients with BTC (cholangiocarcinoma or gallbladder adenocarcinoma) treated at Mayo Clinic tertiary care centers (Florida, Minnesota, and Arizona) and the Mayo Clinic Health System. Key eligibility criteria included histological diagnosis of BTC with available clinical follow-up data and somatic genomic testing. Patients of all ages were identified via a registry of TEMPUS sequencing results ordered as part of routine clinical care. Using an electronic medical record search program (Advanced Text Explorer; Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN), the early-onset cohort was enriched with patients sent for alternative commercial sequencing assays (including FoundationOne, Caris, or other platforms). Patients included in this study were diagnosed with BTC between January 2008 and July 2024. This study was granted exemption by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB: 23-012565).

The primary independent variable was age at the time of diagnosis, which was categorized as early-onset (age <50) or older-onset (age ≥50). The sex assigned at birth was recorded. Demographic information, clinical information, treatment received, and outcome information were extracted by chart review. Four physicians (JN, PS, OM, and COD) compiled the records, and approximately 10% were re-reviewed to ensure consistency.

Outcomes

OS was defined as the time from diagnosis until death. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined from initiation of gemcitabine–cisplatin and ICI until first documentation of radiographic/clinical progression or death. For OS and PFS, patients who had not experienced an event at the time of the analysis were censored at last follow-up or last disease assessment, respectively. Clinician-assessed response outcomes included complete response, partial response, stable disease, and progressive disease. The use of the RECIST methodology was not required. Overall response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients with partial or complete response of any duration of all evaluable patients that received gemcitabine–cisplatin and ICI.

Molecular analysis

Alterations in the following genes were collected to determine their relevance as potential therapeutic targets: FGFR2 (fusion or mutation), IDH1/2, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRAF V600E, ATM, ERBB2/3, and KRAS. Information on mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR)/microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) and tumor mutational burden (TMB) was also collected.

Actionable genomic alterations were defined according to the ESMO Scale of Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) criteria21 as recommended by the ESMO guidelines,22 with FGFR2 fusion, IDH1 alteration, BRAF V600E mutation, and dMMR/MSI-H being considered ESCAT I and all other alterations considered ESCAT II-III.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians (interquartile ranges). Categorical variables are presented as counts and frequencies (%). Patient characteristics and gene alteration variables were compared using chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were two-sided for categorical variables as well as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. Since the eligibility criteria required genomic testing results, patients’ survival data may be left truncated. To account for the potential bias introduced by left truncation, patients entered the risk set at the time of tumor sample collection for genomic sequencing. Survival distribution was estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves, and the 95% CI for the median was estimated using the Brookmeyer–Crowley method. The hazard ratio (HR) for the time-to-event endpoint was estimated using a Cox proportional hazards model while adjusting for body mass index (BMI), tumor location (distal, perihilar, gallbladder, intrahepatic), stage (I, II, III, IV), type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, first-line systemic therapy, FGFR2 alteration, IDH1/2 alteration, and KRAS alteration. For categorical variables with more than two levels, the overall P value for the variable was calculated based on the chi-square test with appropriate degrees of freedom. Median follow-up time was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.

Given the recent Food and Drug Administration approvals of gemcitabine–cisplatin combined with durvalumab or pembrolizumab, we performed a subgroup analysis of patients treated with gemcitabine–cisplatin and ICI first-line therapy. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients with primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), which is more prevalent in younger patients and may harbor a unique genetic make-up, was also performed. We performed a second sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent TEMPUS genomic sequencing. All analyses were repeated in this subgroup using the same model specifications as in the primary analysis. A complete-case analysis was conducted in all analyses. There were no missing data in the variables used in the models; therefore, no patients were excluded in modeling. Analyses were carried out with SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R (version 4.4.1). Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered to be significant and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

BTC subtypes included 342 (62.3%) patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, 115 (20.9%) with perihilar cholangiocarcinoma, 51 (9.3%) with gallbladder adenocarcinoma, and 41 (7.5%) with distal cholangiocarcinoma. The anatomic distribution among patients with cholangiocarcinoma differed with age, with slightly higher rates of intrahepatic and perihilar cholangiocarcinoma seen in early-onset patients (P = 0.039).

No statistically significant differences were detected in sex or race between early-onset and older-onset patients. Etiology was not identified in 438 (79.7%) patients. Among patients with an identified etiology, young patients were more likely to have PSC [40 patients (23.5%) versus 15 patients (4.0%), P < 0.001].

BMI was lower in early-onset patients (25.7 kg/m2 versus 26.9 kg/m2, P = 0.013). Rates of type 2 diabetes [8 patients (4.7%) versus 78 patients (20.6%), P < 0.001], hypertension [24 patients (14.1%) versus 215 patients (56.7%), P < 0.001], cardiovascular disease [3 patients (1.8%) versus 80 patients (21.1%), P < 0.001], and hyperlipidemia [12 patients (7.1%) versus 180 patients (47.5%), P < 0.001] were also all lower in early-onset patients.

There were no statistically significant differences in stage at presentation or performance status at first systemic treatment between younger and older patients.

Genomic alterations

Genomic profiling comparisons between early-onset and older-onset patients are shown in Figure 2. Actionable molecular alterations were found in 58.2% of early-onset versus 53.5% of older-onset patients (P = 0.28). Tier I mutations were found in 35.9% of early-onset versus 22.2% in older-onset patients (P < 0.001). FGFR2 alterations (44 fusions, 12 mutations) were found in 18.8% of early-onset tumors versus 6.3% in older-onset tumors (P < 0.001). KRAS alterations were more common in older adults (23.0% versus 15.3%, P = 0.040), with 68.1% of KRAS alterations being G12X mutations. There were no statistically significant differences in the rates of IDH1/2, BRCA1/2, BRAF, ERBB2/3, or ATM alterations between the early-onset and older-onset cohorts. Approximately 2% of tumors in each group were MSI-H. However, TMB was slightly lower in early-onset patients (3.0 versus 3.7 mutations/megabase, P = 0.003; Figure 3). Oncologic outcomes

Including patients from all stages, median OS was not statistically different between early-onset patients (19.6 months, 95% CI 16.4-25.8) and older-onset patients (17.0 months, 95% CI 15.1-19.2) with HR 1.14 (95% CI 0.91-1.42, P = 0.26; Figure 4A). In multivariable analysis, earlier disease stages (overall P < 0.001), FGFR2 (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.44-0.89, P = 0.01), and IDH1/2 (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.44-0.89, P = 0.01) alterations were associated with better prognosis. There were no statistically significant differences in OS between younger and older patients with stage I-II or stage III disease (Figure 4B and C). Among stage IV patients, OS was longer for the early-onset patients (median 16.0 months, 95% CI 13.7-20.6) than for the older-onset patients (median 13.2 months, 95% CI 10.9-16.3) with an HR of 1.52 (95% CI 1.10-2.09, P = 0.01); however, the association attenuated after multivariable adjustment (P = 0.39; Figure 4D). Of note, 29 of 233 patients with stage IV disease included in the survival analysis received no systemic therapy and were managed with best supportive care. These findings were similar when the sensitivity analysis restricted to the TEMPUS-only cohort was performed (Supplementary Figure S3 and Tables S2 and S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100350).

There were 103 patients treated with gemcitabine–cisplatin and ICI (96 patients with durvalumab, 7 patients with pembrolizumab), with a median follow-up time of 31.4 months. The ORR was 32 of 93 (34.4%) among assessable patients who received gemcitabine–cisplatin and ICI, and the distribution of responses did not differ between early-onset and older-onset patients (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100350). Discussion

The current study represents one of the largest comprehensive analyses of clinical and molecular characteristics of early-onset BTC. The novel findings of this study show that compared with patients with older-onset BTC, patients with early-onset BTC have: (i) lower rates of clinical comorbidities; (ii) lower rates of KRAS alteration; (iii) received similar benefit from gemcitabine–cisplatin and ICI; and (iv) similar survival despite higher rates of later-line therapy and clinical trial enrollment.

It has been postulated that metabolic factors may be contributing to the rise in a number of early-onset cancers.23 Importantly, this study did not identify high rates of obesity, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, or diabetes among early-onset patients. This observation should not be interpreted as excluding a role for metabolic risk in early-onset BTC, as global population-level data has shown that the burden of early-onset BTC attributable to high BMI has increased across many regions.24 Early-life metabolic derangements cannot be excluded in our cohort, but the low comorbidity rates may suggest additional pathogenic drivers. A recent review of early-onset BTCs highlights the developmental origins of health and disease hypothesis, which suggests that adverse environmental exposures during critical developmental periods may permanently reprogram cellular functions, increasing susceptibility to BTC.8,25

In the present study, fewer than half of early-onset patients were referred for genetic counseling. Our practice has now generally shifted to offer counseling to all patients diagnosed aged 55 years or younger due to a prevalence of pathogenic germline variants of ∼12% in this group.26

We identified that KRAS alteration was a poor prognostic factor and that lower rates of KRAS alteration were found in early-onset patients (15% versus 23% in older-onset patients). MAPK pathway inhibitors, including MEK inhibitors (NCT0556440327) are in late stages of clinical development for BTCs harboring RAS alteration. Furthermore, new classes of pan-RAS and allele-specific RAS inhibitors are showing tremendous promise for other diseases such as pancreatic, colon, and lung cancers.28,29 Our findings suggest that a smaller but still significant proportion of early-onset patients may benefit from these new agents in the future. A sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent TEMPUS testing yielded numerically similar results but was not statistically significant; this is likely due to the smaller sample size in the TEMPUS cohort, resulting in reduced power to detect a difference. Relative enrichment of KRAS alterations in older-onset patients with cholangiocarcinoma has been reported in a Chinese cohort,30 while other studies such as those by Jayakrishnan et al.,31 Pappas et al.,11 and Pudlarz et al.14 have shown similar trends without reaching statistical significance. Early-onset colorectal cancer has similarly been associated with lower rates of MAPK alterations.32

We also confirmed that younger patients are more likely to harbor FGFR2 fusions.11,14,31,33 These alterations are generally associated with more favorable prognosis,34 particularly when patients receive matched targeted therapy.35,36 No statistically significant differences in ATM or BRAF alterations were seen between younger and older patients in the present study. In the CITY study, these mutations were more common in younger patients.11 Conversely, Pudlarz et al.14 reported higher rates of ATM alteration in older-onset patients. Differences in results reported between these studies and the present study may reflect differences in study population, sample size, and inclusion of patients with gallbladder adenocarcinoma.

We found that systemic therapy with gemcitabine–cisplatin and ICI has similar efficacy in early-onset and older-onset patients in terms of responses and PFS. Our finding of a statistically lower TMB among early-onset patients, therefore, may not be clinically meaningful in the setting of currently available forms of ICI. Notably, our ‘real-world’ cohort showed a PFS of ∼7 months and ORR of 34.4%, similar to the TOPAZ-1 trial15 in which PFS was 7.2 months and ORR was 26.7%.

Overall, outcomes for patients with early-onset BTC were similar to those seen for older-onset patients in our cohort, and the effect of age was not statistically significant on multivariable Cox analysis. Although there were higher rates of clinical trial enrollment and more intensive therapy for younger patients with more advanced disease, OS was not different between the groups. This may suggest minimal benefit from these additional measures or may conversely suggest that these additional measures were needed to achieve survival equivalency. Worse survival outcomes among early-onset patients have previously been reported by some groups,12,13 while others have suggested better survival for early-onset patients in the advanced disease setting.11,14 Taken as a whole, our work suggests that effects of younger age on survival are modest, if at all present, and do not outweigh other prognostic factors for these lethal diseases.

Our retrospective observational study has some limitations. It should be noted that our early-onset cohort was enriched to include patients with sequencing data from additional platforms to allow a larger sample size for comparison of molecular alterations, and thus the proportion of early-onset patients in the overall cohort is artificially high. When the analysis was restricted to a TEMPUS cohort without enrichment of early-onset cases, the survival patterns were similar. This multicenter cohort study involves three US tertiary care centers and may not fully represent all patients with early-onset BTC. We are a referral center particularly for patients with PSC and perihilar cholangiocarcinoma who are considered for liver transplantation, and selection bias may exist. Demographic factors may also introduce selection bias as our early-onset cohort of patients tended to have private insurance; therefore, results may not be generalizable to broader populations. Geographic heterogeneity in mutational patterns has been reported in early-onset colorectal cancer, and it is plausible that this may also be the case with early-onset BTC.37 This study only included patients with next-generation sequencing performed for clinical reasons. These tests are usually ordered for individuals with advanced disease. Thus, this may have led to worse survival being recorded for patients with early stages of disease than would be anticipated in a cohort that included patients without sequencing data. We also did not capture comprehensive pathological, local, or adjuvant therapy data for this retrospective cohort or rates of underlying cirrhosis.

Future directions should focus on the unique psychosocial challenges faced by patients with early-onset BTC. Younger adults are more likely to receive more aggressive treatments, but understanding how this impacts quality of life in the setting of similar survival to older-onset patients is unclear. Okano et al.38 recently reported that younger age was associated with higher time-toxicity (days spent in physical contact with health care facilities) for patients with advanced BTC receiving first-line treatment. For those with early-onset cancers that do achieve longer survival, economic consequences and income loss may persist.39

Conclusion

Early-onset BTCs are distinct from older-onset disease. Patients with early-onset disease tend to have lower rates of medical comorbidities, although they may present at similar stages of BTC. This suggests that factors other than metabolic profiles must be considered in the pathogenesis of these lethal diseases in a growing number of young people. Significant genetic differences were evident, with the younger group showing more frequent FGFR2 alterations while having lower rates of KRAS alteration and lower TMB compared with the older group. Despite higher rates of later-line therapy and clinical trial enrollment among younger patients, similar efficacy and survival outcomes were seen as with older adults. This finding should inform prognosis discussion with younger patients seen in clinic and reiterates the need for consideration of risks and benefits in all treatment settings.
