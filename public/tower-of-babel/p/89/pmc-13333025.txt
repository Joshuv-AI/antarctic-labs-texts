Vestibular Paroxysmia Presenting With Atypical Positional Nystagmus Resembling Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo
Authors: Shun Suzuki, Hideki Mori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 69‐year‐old man presented with recurrent vertigo triggered by positional changes, left aural fullness, and left hemifacial spasm. The supine roll test showed direction‐changing apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus lasting < 1 min. Brain MRI demonstrated neurovascular contact between the cranial nerve VII/VIII complex and the anterior inferior cerebellar artery. Carbamazepine 200 mg/day achieved complete symptom resolution within 2 weeks and remission at 6 months. This report describes vestibular paroxysmia presenting with nystagmus resembling that observed in benign paroxysmal positional vertigo and, to our knowledge, represents the first report of direction‐changing apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus in this disorder.

Background

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most common cause of peripheral vestibular syndromes and typically presents as brief attacks triggered by changes in head position [1]. Vestibular paroxysmia (VP) is also classified as a peripheral vestibular syndrome and can cause attacks lasting < 1 min; therefore, distinguishing VP from BPPV is clinically important.

VP is a rare cause of peripheral vertigo with established diagnostic criteria and effective pharmacotherapy, and it is thought to result from neurovascular compression of cranial nerve VIII by a blood vessel in the cerebellopontine angle [2]. VP typically manifests as recurrent, brief attacks of spinning or non‐spinning vertigo lasting from a few seconds to approximately 1 min. Attacks may occur spontaneously or be provoked by positional changes. In addition to vertigo, tinnitus, hearing loss, and unilateral hemifacial spasm may occur concomitantly [3].

Here, we report a case in which findings on the supine roll test suggested lateral semicircular canal BPPV; however, the clinical presentation and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) finding raised suspicion for VP, and pharmacotherapy successfully prevented recurrence of vertigo.

Case Presentation

A 69‐year‐old man with a history of chronic left‐sided sensorineural hearing loss presented with recurrent episodes of spinning vertigo accompanied by left aural fullness and left hemifacial spasm. Two years before presentation, he began experiencing attacks of spinning vertigo approximately once every few months. The attacks gradually increased in both frequency and severity, progressing to weekly episodes. Left‐sided hearing loss developed around the same time as the onset of vertigo and required the use of a hearing aid. During the several weeks before presentation, vertigo attacks occurred approximately once per week and required multiple hospitalizations. Before the current admission, evaluations by other physicians had not elicited the patient's complaints of left hemifacial spasm or left aural fullness, and a possible association with his known sensorineural hearing loss had not been considered. Because the supine roll test showed direction‐changing positional nystagmus that lasted < 1 min, he had been diagnosed with BPPV. The vertigo was spinning in nature and was provoked by positional changes. The patient's subjective report suggested attacks lasting several minutes; however, the exact duration based on history alone was unclear. At the initial evaluation, the examination was limited by nausea associated with acute vertigo. Left hemifacial spasm and left aural fullness were noted. Because the patient was highly distressed, positional testing could not be performed. Laboratory tests, including complete blood count, electrolytes, and inflammatory markers, were within normal limits. Given the severity of symptoms and the clinical course with recurrent hospitalizations, the patient was admitted for symptomatic treatment and further evaluation.

The day after admission, his symptoms improved, allowing a complete neurological examination. No spontaneous nystagmus was observed at rest. Bedside vestibular testing elicited direction‐changing apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus with minimal latency, lasting < 1 min on the supine roll test (Video S1). In contrast, the Dix–Hallpike maneuver was negative. Left hemifacial spasm was again observed during the examination. Apart from the known left sensorineural hearing loss, the neurological examination, including cranial nerve findings, motor and sensory function, and coordination, was unremarkable.

Brain MRI demonstrated neurovascular contact between the cranial nerve VII/VIII complex and the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) in the left cerebellopontine angle, with no evidence of a mass lesion (Figure 1). This imaging finding, together with the clinical constellation of paroxysmal vertigo, hearing loss, and hemifacial spasm, supported a diagnosis of VP.

Treatment with carbamazepine 200 mg/day was initiated. Within 2 weeks of treatment initiation, vertigo attacks resolved completely, and hemifacial spasm improved markedly. No apparent adverse events occurred. He remained asymptomatic at the 6‐month follow‐up, confirming the diagnosis of VP.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic difficulty posed by VP presenting with atypical nystagmus findings. A detailed history revealed that the patient had experienced left hemifacial spasm and left aural fullness before the current admission; however, he had not reported these symptoms to physicians because he did not consider them clinically relevant. These findings were diagnostically important and suggested a more complex pathological process involving the cerebellopontine angle.

According to the diagnostic criteria proposed by the Bárány Society [4], VP is diagnosed when all of the following are fulfilled: (A) at least 10 attacks of spontaneous spinning or non‐spinning vertigo; (B) duration < 1 min; (C) stereotyped phenomenology in a particular patient; (D) response to a treatment with carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine; and (E) not better accounted for by another diagnosis. Our patient fulfilled all diagnostic criteria for VP.

Although direction‐changing apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus with minimal latency is typical of cupulolithiasis in lateral semicircular canal BPPV, nystagmus in this condition usually lasts for more than 1 min (Table 1). Furthermore, the presence of hearing loss and unilateral hemifacial spasm, and most importantly, the complete response to carbamazepine, supported the diagnosis of VP rather than BPPV.

Direction‐changing apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus observed during positional testing in VP is an unusual finding that, to our knowledge, has not previously been described. Two mechanisms may explain this nystagmus. Head‐position changes may have modulated neurovascular compression of the cranial nerve VII/VIII complex by the AICA. Alternatively, unilateral vestibulocochlear nerve hyperexcitability in VP may have impaired cerebellar modulation of the vestibulo‐ocular reflex, producing nystagmus resembling that seen in cerebellar vermis hemorrhage [7] despite the absence of a structural cerebellar lesion.

This case highlights that direction‐changing apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus alone should not establish a diagnosis of BPPV; clinicians should actively seek additional neuro‐otologic signs, such as hemifacial spasm or hearing loss, and consider VP when such signs are present.

Conclusion

This report describes a case of VP presenting with direction‐changing apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus that is characteristic of lateral semicircular canal BPPV. Although nystagmus findings in VP do not usually resemble those observed in BPPV, accompanying symptoms, such as hemifacial spasm and hearing loss, may provide important diagnostic clues for VP. Even when nystagmus is provoked by positional changes, clinicians should carefully assess the history and other cranial nerve symptoms and include VP in the differential diagnosis.
