Differential impact of single interim spacer exchanges on survival outcomes in two-stage exchange arthroplasty for PJIs: a comparative analysis with minimum 5-year follow-up
Authors: Yu-Yi Huang, Kuan-Chen Huang, Yu-Chu Hsueh, Sheng-Hsun Lee, Shih-Hui Peng, Chih-Hsiang Chang, Yu-Chih Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManaging periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) in orthopedic surgery is particularly challenging, especially with two-stage exchange arthroplasty. This study examines the impact of different single spacer exchange strategies on reimplantation success, reinfection risk, and survival outcomes.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on 650 patients who underwent two-stage exchange arthroplasty for PJIs from 2000 to 2017. After exclusions, 94 patients with a single spacer exchange were identified. We used univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses and propensity score matching to evaluate reimplantation success, as defined by Delphi-based criteria.ResultsAmong the 94 patients, 77 underwent reimplantation surgery post-single spacer exchange. Of these, 29 had strategic spacer exchanges, and 48 had unplanned exchanges due to mechanical problems (22) or persistent infections (26). Unplanned exchanges due to persistent infections and a Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) over seven were significant predictors of poor reimplantation success and higher reinfection risk (p < 0.05). Patients transferred from other hospitals, those with the same pathogen causing persistent PJI as in the first stage, and those with the same pathogen identified in unexpected positive intraoperative cultures (UPICs) had increased reinfection risks (p < 0.05). Survival analysis indicated that unplanned exchanges due to persistent PJI significantly elevated failure rates (p < 0.05).ConclusionsWhile previous studies have suggested poor outcomes associated with spacer exchanges, our findings indicate that this is not universally the case. However, unplanned spacer exchanges, particularly those necessitated by persistent infections, result in significantly worse outcomes compared to other strategies. Therefore, unplanned exchanges require meticulous management to improve patient outcomes and reduce the risk of reinfection.Level of evidenceIII.Keywords: Two-stage exchange arthroplasty, Periprosthetic joint infection, Recurrence, Spacer exchange, Re-reinfection

Background

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a relatively rare yet severe complication in total joint arthroplasty (TJA), often necessitating revision surgery. Its occurrence ranges from 0.03% to 2.7% in primary knee and hip arthroplasty cases [1], and escalates following revision procedures, with reported rates of persistent or new infections between 7 and 25% [2] Patients with PJI face compromised joint function, significant pain, and an increased mortality risk, with a 10-year all-cause mortality rate of 45% in PJI patients compared to 29% in non-infected individuals [3]. Managing PJI requires an integrated approach, involving surgical and antimicrobial strategies tailored to the causative organism [4]. Two-staged exchange arthroplasty is a widely accepted and successful method for managing chronic PJI, with eradication rates ranging from 84.8% to 91.47% [5–7]. This approach is considered the gold standard for chronic PJI surgical treatment [2, 8, 9].

The interim stage of two-staged exchange arthroplasty involves implanting an antibiotic-impregnated spacer, which is vital for restoring soft tissue tension, reducing dead space, and providing localized antibiotic delivery. These benefits are crucial for both functional outcomes and infection eradication [4, 8–10]. However, the introduction of spacers has been linked to suboptimal outcomes in some cases. Approximately 16.9% of PJI patients undergoing surgical treatment require an interim spacer exchange due to various reasons, including persistent infection and mechanical complications like spacer dislocation and fracture [11–14].

Despite the intent of spacer exchange to facilitate repeat debridement and deliver an additional antibiotic load, the current literature suggests that such procedures are associated with unfavorable outcomes. These include high rates of reinfection, increased mortality, more salvage procedures, and often a failure to proceed to reimplantation in patients necessitating an interim spacer exchange [12]. Given these adverse outcomes, it is imperative to identify the risk factors contributing to the failure of spacer exchanges. This understanding is crucial in determining when such a procedure is warranted. Additionally, the decision to perform a spacer exchange must be carefully considered, as it not only delays reimplantation but also exposes the patient to the risks associated with additional surgery.

This study aims to (1) explore the clinical trajectory of periprosthetic joint infection after the performance of exchange arthroplasty and the subsequent placement of a spacer, and (2) compare the prognosis between patients requiring spacer exchange and those following the standard two-stage procedure without spacer exchange.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This comprehensive retrospective study was conducted from January 2000 to May 2017, focusing on patients diagnosed with PJI undergoing first-stage exchange arthroplasty of the knee with spacer implantation. The initial cohort comprised 650 patients. We excluded patients with unclear records or non-adherence to the protocol (16 patients); patients with previous revision surgery (71 patients); patients of native septic arthritis (12 patients); patients who underwent more than one spacer redo (12 patients). After applying exclusion criteria and ensuring adherence to the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) [15] and the International Consensus Meeting (ICM) [16] criteria for PJI diagnosis, the final study population included 539 joints (Fig. 1).

Patient groups

The study cohort was divided into the following groups by the reason of redo spacer, which could be further divided into: strategic spacer exchange (33 patients) and unplanned spacer exchange (61 patients). Furthermore, unplanned spacer exchange could also be classified into mechanical problems (22 patients) and persistent infection (26 patients); besides, the non-exchange Group (445 patients) was also analyzed.

Treatment protocols and spacer management

The two-stage exchange arthroplasty protocol commenced with the removal of all implants and infected tissues, followed by aggressive debridement. An initial antibiotic-impregnated polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) spacer was implanted using an empirical antibiotic regimen (e.g., vancomycin and ceftazidime) based on local epidemiology, which containing a specific antibiotic concentration based on the weight of the antibiotic powder relative to the cement powder (e.g., 4 g of vancomycin in 40 g of cement, resulting in a 10% concentration).

Intraoperative tissue samples were cultured for 7–14 days. The management of the spacer was strictly guided by these culture results and the subsequent clinical course, categorized into two types of exchanges:

Strategic Spacer Exchange: This was performed during the same hospitalization (shortly after the first stage) if the intraoperative culture results revealed pathogens that were resistant to the antibiotics impregnated in the initial empirical spacer. The goal was to replace the spacer with one containing pathogen-specific antibiotics to ensure effective local delivery. The decision for a strategic spacer exchange is primarily based on the culture results obtained from tissue samples during the first-stage surgery. Postoperatively, patients received four weeks of intravenous antibiotics followed by two weeks of oral antibiotics. Following the antibiotic course, detailed discussions were conducted with the patients and their families. A joint decision on the subsequent treatment approach—including potential reimplantation, a redo spacer exchange, or non-intervention for PJI—was made based on mutual consent.

Reimplantation was scheduled typically 12 weeks after the initial resection, contingent upon the absence of clinical infection and normalized serum inflammatory markers, in accordance with the criteria by Diaz-Ledezma et al. [17]. During the definitive reimplantation, implant selection was tailored to patient-specific factors such as age and bone condition. This phase replicated the initial stage of the culture analysis. UPIC—including microorganism identification, sample type, and antibiotic susceptibilities—were documented. Multiple UPICs were defined as positive growth identified in separate samples, while polymicrobial growth from a single sample was counted as a single UPIC event. Treatment for UPICs involved targeted antibiotics differing from standard postoperative prophylaxis and was often devised by infectious disease specialists without a uniform regimen.

Definitions of treatment failure and reinfection

For the outcome analysis, treatment failure was rigorously defined based on the Delphi-based ICM criteria. A case was categorized as a failure/reinfection if any of the following criteria were met after the definitive reimplantation:

Reinfection: Indicated by the presence of a sinus tract communicating with the joint, or the isolation of a pathogen from at least two separate tissue/fluid samples (or one sample with strong histological/purulence evidence).

Surgical Re-intervention: The necessity for any subsequent surgical procedure (e.g., irrigation and debridement, implant removal) due to PJI.

PJI-related Mortality: Death directly attributable to the joint infection or its treatment.

Patients requiring chronic antibiotic suppression therapy due to inoperable conditions were also classified as failures. Conversely, aseptic loosening or mechanical failure requiring revision without evidence of infection was censored from the reinfection analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. For multi-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, with Tukey’s HSD or Dunn’s test for post hoc analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors for reimplantation failure and reinfection.

The reasons for not undergoing reimplantation among 17 patients in the spacer exchange group were the following: medically unfit for reimplantation (n = 5), salvage procedures for persistent infection (2 fusions, 3 amputations), death during stages (n = 3), and decision to retain spacer either by the patient or the surgeon (n = 4).

Following reimplantation, the overall treatment success rate according to the Delphi-based definition was 92.6% (475/513) with a mean follow-up of 7.6 years (range 5.2–11.3). The reinfection rate was 15.6% (12/77) for patients with spacer exchange compared to 6.0% (26/436) for those without spacer exchange (p < 0.001).

Among the 48 patients who required an unplanned spacer exchange, the 22 cases due to mechanical problems occurred at a mean of 10.2 weeks (range 4–24) following the initial spacer implantation. In these cases, a spacer exchange was performed rather than direct reimplantation, despite no clear signs of infection, as a precautionary measure to perform a thorough debridement and washout to minimize any risk of a subclinical infection compromising the final prosthesis.

Further subgroup analysis with the reason for spacer exchange (Table 4), in patients with a spacer exchange for mechanical failure, the failure rate after reimplantation was 4.5% (1/22) compared to 34.6% (9/26) in patients who underwent an exchange for persistent infection and 6.9% (2/29) in those with strategic spacer exchange. After adjusting all confounders, the reinfection rate in patients with unplanned spacer exchange due to persistent PJI (HR 2.68, 95% CI 1.316–3.563, p < 0.001), Transferred from other hospital during interstage period (HR 2.187, 95% CI 1.378–2.651, p < 0.001), and two UPICs with same pathogen as first-stage (HR 2.886, 95% CI 1.132–3.647, p < 0.001; Table 5).

Discussion

This study investigates the outcomes of interim spacer exchange in two-stage exchange arthroplasty for chronic PJI, with a focus on the different strategies and their impact on reimplantation success and reinfection risk. Our main important findings include: (1) unplanned spacer exchanges due to persistent infection are associated with significantly higher risks of treatment failure and reinfection; (2) patients transferred from other hospitals and those with persistent infections caused by the same pathogen as the initial infection have increased reinfection risks; and (3) a CCI over seven correlates with poor outcomes and difficult to be reimplanted during staged resection arthroplasty.

It is important to distinguish a "strategic spacer exchange" due to antibiotic insensitivity from a state of "persistent infection." The former is a proactive, preventative measure taken when first-stage intraoperative cultures reveal the infecting organism is not susceptible to the antibiotics loaded in the cement spacer, even if the patient is clinically well. This is distinct from persistent infection, which is diagnosed based on a combination of clinical signs, elevated inflammatory markers, and positive cultures after the initial treatment stage.

Two-stage exchange arthroplasty remains the gold standard treatment for patients with chronic PJI. However, certain patients require interim spacer exchange before reimplantation, including unplanned spacer exchanges due to persistent infection or mechanical problems. Some patients need strategic spacer exchange when cultures reveal that the spacer is not sensitive to the infecting organism. In our study, 15% of patients required interim spacer exchange before reimplantation, which is consistent with the 10–17% reported by other authors [11, 12, 14, 17, 18].

Despite the frequency of this procedure, outcomes were worse in patients with interim spacer exchanges compared to those without spacer exchanges in two-stage exchange arthroplasty. George et al. [11] and Tan et al. [14] found high failure rates of reimplantation after spacer exchange; Klemt et al. [17] also showed that the reinfection risk was higher for patients with spacer exchange compared to those without. Additionally, passive range of motions (PROMs) were worse in patients with spacer exchanges. Our study also showed a high rate of reinfection after reimplantation and a shorter time to reinfection in patients with interim spacer exchanges. Clemente et al. [19] were the only ones to report relatively good outcomes for interim spacer exchange in two-stage exchange arthroplasty, showing an eradication rate of 78.8% and overall survivorship after reimplantation of 72.8%. These differences may be due to varying criteria for defining reimplantation success. In our study, failure of reimplantation was defined according to Delphi-based criteria.

Regarding the risk factors for treatment failure post-reimplantation after spacer exchange, George et al. [11] found it related to difficult-to-eradicate organisms (resistant or polymicrobial). Kozaily et al. [20] showed that host grade C patients were more likely to experience treatment failure post-reimplantation after spacer exchange. Our study found that treatment failure post-reimplantation was related to unplanned exchanges due to persistent PJI.

Both George et al. [11] and Klemt et al. [17] have shown that the CCI is an independent risk factor for requiring an interim spacer exchange in cases of chronic PJI. Kozaily et al. [20] found that a higher CCI was prevalent in patients who experienced reimplantation failure after an interim spacer exchange. They concluded that CCI should be considered when deciding whether to perform an interim spacer exchange. Therefore, in our study, a CCI over seven was correlated with difficulties in reimplantation during staged resection arthroplasty. However, it was not a significant risk factor for treatment failure post-reimplantation because patients with a CCI greater than seven were clinically considered unsuitable for reimplantation after spacer exchange.

A CCI over seven and unplanned spacer exchanges due to persistent infection were correlated with difficulties in reimplantation during staged resection arthroplasty. These findings align with previous studies. Fehring et al. [21] demonstrated a relatively lower rate of reimplantation in patients with host or extremity compromising factors. Additionally, Lin et al. [22] reported that only 45.2% received second-stage reimplantation. This indicates that the initial infection might not have been well-controlled or that unstable medical conditions, despite interim spacer exchange, made patients unsuitable for reimplantation.

We analyzed the 10-year survival of strategic spacer exchanges versus unplanned spacer exchanges and found that the failure rate was significantly higher for unplanned exchanges due to persistent PJI. In multivariate Cox regression analysis, only unplanned exchanges due to persistent PJI were significant risk factors for failure to undergo reimplantation and treatment failure post-reimplantation. Our results suggest that not all spacer exchanges result in poor outcomes, but unplanned exchanges due to persistent infection lead to worse outcomes.

Previous studies emphasized that difficult-to-eradicate organisms, including resistant or polymicrobial bacteria, likely contribute to persistent infection after placing an antibiotic spacer and necessitate interim spacer exchange [14, 17, 21, 23, 24]. Resistant microorganisms have been identified as factors contributing to the failure of two-stage exchange arthroplasty. However, our study did not show that antibiotic-resistant microorganisms were risk factors for failure to undergo reimplantation or for treatment failure post-reimplantation. This may be because, in cases of strategic exchange, we intervene early based on antibiotic resistance to replace the spacer.

Kim et al. [25] showed that culture-negative or culture-positive results did not significantly affect the outcome of the two-stage revision in patients with chronic PJI. Sabater-Martos et al. [26] conducted a systematic review that demonstrated patients with UPICs at the time of reimplantation, who had their antibiotic treatment withheld, experienced a lower failure rate. Our study showed that two UPICs with the same pathogen as the first stage were significant risk factors for treatment failure post-reimplantation. This finding suggests that persistent infection, rather than antibiotic resistance alone, may have greater clinical significance in predicting treatment failure post-reimplantation. Further studies are necessary to define the optimal antibiotic strategy in two-stage exchange arthroplasty.

Interestingly, our study showed that 14.3% of patients with spacer exchanges who underwent reimplantation had been transferred from other institutions. Of these, 73% (8 out of 11) were transferred due to treatment failure from persistent infection, requiring further management. Garceau et al. [27] demonstrated that patient transfer during the interstage of two-stage exchange arthroplasty for PJI leads to poorer outcomes, including higher rates of persisting or recurrent infection and soft-tissue complications, compared to continuous care. Florance et al. [28] also showed that PJIs transferred between stages demonstrated lower success rates and required more additional procedures. Our results also indicated that transferring care from other institutions significantly impacted treatment failure post-reimplantation. Surgeons should consider early transfer with the goal of continuous management by a single institution.

Limitations

First, the retrospective nature of this study is an inherent limitation. The indications for spacer exchange, particularly for "strategic" exchanges, were based on surgeon judgment and evolving protocols over the long study period, which may introduce selection bias. Second, we acknowledge that the "unplanned spacer exchange" group is heterogeneous, comprising failures due to both mechanical and infectious reasons. While we analyzed these subgroups separately, their initial inclusion under a single umbrella of "unplanned" reflects the complex, often multifactorial, challenges in clinical practice. A large-scale, prospective, randomized controlled trial would be necessary to eliminate these biases and provide definitive evidence on the optimal management strategy.

Conclusions

In summary, this study highlights that patients with unplanned spacer exchanges due to persistent infection have a significantly higher risk of failure to undergo reimplantation and treatment failure post-reimplantation at 10 years of follow-up. Patients with unplanned spacer exchanges due to persistent infections require precise management to improve outcomes and reduce reinfection risks.
