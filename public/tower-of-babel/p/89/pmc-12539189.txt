Risk-taking behaviors in relation to religiosity and familial support among university students: a latent class analysis
Authors: Mahsa Tebyanian, Shima Ataee, Abbas Abbasi-Ghahramanloo, Sima Afrashteh, Mohammad Sabaghan, Masoud Imani, Kazem Alizadeh-Barzian, Zahra Movahednia, Nima Daneshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-risk behaviors such as substance use, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and violence are common among university students and can lead to serious health issues. Research on high-risk behaviors among Iranian university students is limited, with many aspects still unexplored. This study aimed to identify patterns of these behaviors and examine how religiosity and familial support relate to risk-taking among university students in Khuzestan Province, Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from mid-2023 to mid-2024 involving 513 university students aged 18 to 29 years, selected through multistage random sampling. Data were collected via self-administered questionnaires assessing risk behaviors, religiosity, and familial support. Latent class analysis was used to identify behavior patterns, with model fit evaluated by likelihood-ratio chi-square, Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, and entropy. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.2.ResultsThe majority of students (78.1%) were classified as low-risk, while about 22% engaged in multiple high-risk behaviors. Higher levels of familial support and religiosity were associated with a lower likelihood of belonging to high-risk groups. Familial support significantly decreased the odds of being in the very high-risk class, whereas males were more likely to be in moderate or high-risk classes.ConclusionsMost students exhibited low-risk behaviors, but a notable minority engaged in multiple risks. Enhancing familial support and fostering religiosity may help reduce risk-taking behaviors. It is recommended that universities and health authorities develop culturally sensitive, family-focused programs to support student health and well-being.Keywords: Substance use, High-risk behaviors, Familial support, Latent class analysis, Religious

Background

High-risk behaviors are acts that increase the chances of developing health problems, experiencing injury or disability, and facing social consequences [1]. These behaviors—including cigarette smoking, hookah use, alcohol and illicit drug consumption, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, sexual risk behaviors, and physical fighting—are common among university students worldwide. These behaviors pose significant threats to students’ physical and mental health, academic performance, and social well-being [2, 3].

Recent studies show that high-risk behaviors are common among university students, with cigarette smoking affecting approximately 10–18%, hookah use 16–20%, alcohol consumption 8–54%, and risky sexual behaviors reported by about 50% of students worldwide. In Iran specifically, prevalence rates include 9.8–17.6% for cigarette smoking, 20% for hookah use, 8.3% for alcohol consumption, and 7–14.5% for risky sexual behaviors, highlighting a significant public health concern in this population [3–5]. These behaviors are recognized as serious public health issues in Iran, contributing substantially to preventable morbidity and mortality among young adults [6, 7].

The transition to university life often exposes students to new social environments and stressors, increasing their vulnerability to risk-taking behaviors [8, 9]. Recognizing these dangers is crucial for students to make informed choices and prioritize their health and well-being. By avoiding high-risk behaviors and promoting positive alternatives, students can maintain good health, achieve personal goals, and contribute to the overall well-being of their communities [10, 11].

Religiosity, which encompasses an individual’s beliefs, practices, and sense of community, is a significant protective factor against risk-taking behaviors. Numerous studies suggest that higher levels of religiosity are associated with lower instances of substance use and other high-risk behaviors, potentially due to the moral frameworks and social support systems provided by religious communities [12, 13]. Adults with religious beliefs who regularly participate in religious services tend to have lower rates of tobacco, alcohol, and other substance use, including cannabis, compared to individuals without any religious affiliation [14, 15]. Similarly, multiple studies among university students have demonstrated that religiosity influences the consumption of cigarettes and alcohol [16, 17]. Teaching about health is essential for maintaining physical and mental well-being, and incorporating ethical lessons with a religious viewpoint has been recommended as an effective method [18]. However, the impact of religiosity can vary depending on individual interpretation, cultural context, and personal experience [13].

Religiosity in Iran is deeply rooted in the country’s cultural and social fabric, with the vast majority of the population identifying as Muslim, primarily Shiite, and religion playing a central role in daily life, social norms, and national identity [19–21]. Regular religious practices such as daily prayer, attendance at mosques, and involvement in organized religious activities are highly prevalent among Iranians, with national surveys indicating that over 80% of Iranians engage in prayer as a fundamental aspect of their religiosity [21]. In health studies, Iranian researchers frequently utilize both locally developed and internationally adapted instruments to assess religiosity, reflecting the complex interplay between religion, spirituality, and well-being in the country’s context [22–24]. Thus, understanding religiosity in Iran requires not only attention to religious practices and beliefs but also to the broader socio-cultural and psychological domains shaped by the Islamic faith [25–27].

Similarly, familial support—including emotional backing, guidance, and open communication—serves as a protective buffer, helping students make informed choices and seek help during dilemmas [13, 28]. However, not all students lacking strong family support engage in risk behaviors, as individual resilience, peer relationships, and community factors also play important roles [29, 30]. Recognized as the fundamental social unit for raising and nurturing children, the family provides informal aid that addresses emotional, physical, practical, and informational needs [31]. This support is linked to enhanced well-being and improved physical and mental health outcomes, whereas family rejection increases the risk of adverse health consequences [32–34].

Despite the recognized importance of religiosity and familial support as protective factors against risk-taking behaviors, few studies have simultaneously examined their roles in the latent patterns of such behaviors among Iranian university students. This study seeks to address these gaps by posing research questions such as: (1) What distinct latent classes of risk-taking behaviors exist among university students in Khuzestan Province (2)? How do religiosity and familial support relate to membership in these latent classes? We hypothesize that higher levels of familial support and religiosity are each associated with decreased odds of engaging in high-risk behaviors and that their combined influence confers a stronger protective effect. These hypotheses build on prior findings that identify both factors as critical in promoting health and well-being within culturally specific contexts [7, 12, 35, 36].

This study addresses a gap in the literature by simultaneously examining the roles of religiosity and familial support as protective factors against risk-taking behaviors among Iranian university students. Specifically, it investigates (1) the distinct latent classes of risk-taking behaviors present in university students in Khuzestan Province, and (2) how religiosity and familial support relate to membership in these classes. Building on prior research identifying these factors as critical to health and well-being in culturally specific contexts, we hypothesize that higher levels of familial support and religiosity are each associated with lower odds of engaging in high-risk behaviors, with their combined influence providing an enhanced protective effect. Using Latent Class Analysis, this study uncovers subgroups of students based on multiple risk behaviors, aiming to inform culturally tailored interventions. The findings are intended to guide public health strategies and university policies to reduce risk behaviors and promote student well-being.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted among 513 university students in Khuzestan Province, southern Iran. The sample was selected through multistage random sampling to ensure representativeness of the target population. University students constitute a critical population for studying high-risk behaviors, as they are in a transitional life stage characterized by increased independence and heightened exposure to risk factors. Our focus on this demographic is consistent with numerous prior studies examining patterns of risk-taking behaviors in university settings both globally and regionally [7, 37]. The study was carried out over one year, from approximately mid-2023 to mid-2024, allowing sufficient time for comprehensive data collection and follow-up. An exceptionally high response rate of 85% was achieved, which significantly enhances the validity and generalizability of the findings, thereby strengthening the robustness of the conclusions.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated using the standard sample size formula for estimating a population proportion, where the expected prevalence of high-risk behaviors among university students was taken as 40% based on previous studies [2, 7], with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. This calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of approximately 369 participants. However, since latent class analysis (LCA) requires a larger sample size for stable and reliable class identification, and prior literature recommends a minimum of 500 participants for robust LCA models [38, 39], we increased the sample size accordingly and included 513 students. This approach balances traditional prevalence-based sample size estimation with methodological considerations specific to latent class modeling.

Ethical considerations and consent procedure

The questionnaires were administered to students in both electronic and paper formats for self-completion. Prior to distribution, verbal informed consent was obtained, with clear explanation of the study’s purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw without penalty. Only students who consented verbally received the questionnaire, and even then, they could decline to complete it at any time. Thus, consent was obtained in two stages: verbal consent followed by written implicit consent through questionnaire completion. This process ensured voluntary, informed participation while preserving anonymity and minimizing discomfort related to written consent in sensitive behavioral research.

Study tools

The questionnaire was developed by adapting the WHO Core Questionnaire and the Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) to reflect the context of substance use in Iran. Its content had been previously validated by a team of researchers in a separate study. Additionally, the questionnaire’s validity was assessed by MPH students specializing in public health [40]. we used this questionnaire to assess demographic information and information regarding high-risk behaviors including‏ cigarette smoking, hookah use, unhealthy diet, alcohol use, illicit drug use, Physical inactivity, Sexual risk behavior, and having physical fights.

Kendler’s general religiosity scale and Aneshensel and Sucoff’s 13-item questionnaire were used to measure the level of religion and family support of students, respectively. Kendler’s general religiosity scale evaluates the status of general religiosity of students through a 5-point scale with the following options: “completely agree, agree, neither agree nor disagree, disagree, and completely disagree.” The range of values for this questionnaire is between 28 and 140, where higher scores indicate stronger religious beliefs. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.97 [12, 41].

Aneshensel and Sucoff’s 13-item questionnaire assesses perceived familial support through 13 items rated on a five-point Likert scale from “completely disagree” to “completely agree.” Total scores range from 13 to 65, with higher scores indicating stronger perceived support. Sample items include statements about parental attention, understanding, and availability. Some examples of the scale items are as follows: “My parents pay a lot of attention to me”, “My parents know me well”,and “My parents are there wherever I need them”. The tool demonstrates good reliability and high internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of approximately 0.86 [42].

Statistical analysis

During the data analysis phase, we initially employed descriptive statistics to compare the mean and standard deviation scores of family support and religion across various variable levels. The differences were analyzed with a significance threshold set at 5%. This approach was designed to offer a thorough summary of the variables and their respective distributions. In the next step, Latent class analysis (LCA) was employed to consolidate various variables and uncover underlying latent constructs. This method posits that for a collection of categorical variables or symptoms, there exists a hidden structure comprising several mutually exclusive classes, where the observed characteristics in each class are independent of one another, a principle known as “local independence.” LCA transforms diverse categorical variables into uniform classes that are characterized by distinct item-response probabilities within each class. The analysis yields parameters such as overall class prevalence, which indicates the likelihood of an individual belonging to a specific class, and conditional probabilities, representing the likelihood of endorsing each item within those classes.

We took into account several factors, including the likelihood-ratio chi-square statistic (G2), Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and entropy, to evaluate the fit of the models. Additionally, we considered the interpretability of the results and the item-response probabilities in determining the most appropriate number of latent classes. Furthermore, factors including age, gender, familial support score, and religiosity score were included as covariates in the final model. The likelihood of their association with each latent class was determined using odds ratios (ORs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.2.

Regarding the relationship between family support scores and certain characteristics of students, the descriptive analysis showed that students exhibiting traits such as physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, physical conflict, cigarette smoking, hookah smoking, alcohol consumption, illegal drug use, and sexual risk behavior have lower family support scores compared to those who do not possess these characteristics. This difference in scores was statistically significant for all variables except for sexual risky behaviors and physical conflict.

Regarding the relationship between the score of religious beliefs and these characteristics, the analysis also showed that, in general, students who exhibited such behaviors had lower religious belief scores. However, this score difference was only statistically significant for physical inactivity, cigarette smoking, hookah smoking, and alcohol consumption. For characteristics such as unhealthy diet, physical conflict, illegal drug use, and sexual risk behavior, the relationship and score difference were not statistically significant (Table 1). To perform latent class analysis and identify the best model for the data, eight binary variables were used, and a range of 1 to 7 classes was employed to fit them. Based on the various measures of model assessment shown in Table 2, the 4-class model was selected as the best model to fit the data due to its interpretability and reasonableness of the results. Regarding the membership of individuals in the different classes of the 4-class model, the results in Table 3 showed that the low-risk class had the highest proportion of students (78.1%). Additionally, approximately 22% of the students in this study experienced concurrent engagement in risky behaviors. Item-response probabilities have indicated that the likelihood of having certain undesirable traits—such as lack of physical activity, physical conflict, unhealthy diet, alcohol consumption, and illegal drug use—is higher in the very high-risk class than in the other classes. On the other hand, the probability of cigarette smoking and hookah smoking in the moderate-risk and very high-risk classes is higher than that in the low-risk and high-risk classes. Regarding risky sexual behavior, item-response probabilities showed that the likelihood of engaging in such behavior is higher for individuals in the high-risk class than for those in other classes (Table 3). As shown in Table 4, the odds ratios for membership in different classes have been investigated for the desired covariates. A higher score in family support has reduced the odds of being in the Very High-Risk class (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97) compared to the Low-Risk class. On the other hand, being male has increased the odds of membership in the Moderate Risk (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.07–2.66) and High Risk (OR = 8.47, 95% CI: 4.37–16.40) classes compared to the Low-Risk class.

To provide an overview, the latent class analysis identified four distinct groups of risk-taking behaviors among students: the majority (78.1%) belonged to the low-risk class, while about 22% were distributed among moderate, high, and very high-risk classes. The very high-risk class demonstrated the highest probability of engaging in multiple risky behaviors, including physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, substance use, and physical conflict. Additionally, cigarette and hookah smoking were more prevalent in both the moderate and very high-risk classes compared to others, and risky sexual behavior was most likely in the high-risk class. Importantly, greater family support significantly reduced the odds of being in the very high-risk group (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97), while being male increased the likelihood of membership in the moderate (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.07–2.66) and high-risk (OR = 8.47, 95% CI: 4.37–16.40) classes.

Discussion

We investigated several important patterns of risk-taking behaviors among a sample of university students in the south of Iran. Four distinct classes were found: low risk (class 1), moderate risk (class 2), high risk (class 3), and very high risk (class 4). The range included students who were at risk for engaging in co-occurrences of high-risk behaviors, such as cigarette and hookah smoking, alcohol drinking, illicit drugs, sexual risk behaviors, Physical fighting, and Physical inactivity, to low-risk students who were generally not prone to engaging in these high-risk behaviors. Based on this analysis, being male significantly increases the odds of being a member of high-risk classes. In addition, our results highlight two attributes including religiosity and familial support that could decrease the odds of risk-taking behaviors. Of the four classes, the low-risk class had the largest number of participants, with 78.1%. This class was characterized by a highly unhealthy diet. It is worth mentioning that approximately 22% of students have experienced the co-occurrence of high-risk behaviors in this study. Specifically, our findings showed that 7% and 6.3% of university students are in high-risk and very high-risk classes, respectively.

LCA was used in some studies that are similar to the present study to evaluate this co-occurrence. In a similar survey of Iranian university students, Shekari et al., determined three latent classes. The classes were as follows: low risk, smoking, and high risk. According to this study, 1.5% and 18.0% of girls and boys were classified as high-risk, respectively [43]. Evans-Polce and his colleagues in a survey of American students using the LCA method believe that university students with high-risk behaviors are a heterogeneous population that requires targeted interventions. Five latent classes including Non/low users, Non-Hookah Tobacco Users, Extreme Heavy Episodic Drinking and Marijuana Users, Hookah and Marijuana Users, and Poly-Substance Users were identified in this study for the occurrence of risky behaviors [42]. In another similar study of Iranian university students, it was found that there were five latent classes for high behaviors: low risk, high risk, somewhat low risk, hookah user, and very high risk. High-risk and very-high-risk classes had a total of 7.7% and 2.5% of students [7]. Regina Alves also identified three latent classes with different probabilities of risk among Portuguese college students: low-risk behaviors (51.4%), moderate-risk behaviors (14.9%), and high-risk behaviors (33.7%) [44]. According to this evidence, there are various behavioral patterns of co-occurrence of high-risk behaviors among students using the LCA method.

A troubling finding in this study is that most students had a high probability of having an unhealthy diet. According to the evidence, the diet of university students is characterized by excessive consumption of fried foods, fast food, Junk food, snacks, sweet drinks, skipped breakfast, and the elimination of fruits and vegetables [44, 45]. Yun et al. showed that most students of the university despite good nutrition knowledge have unhealthy eating habits [46]. The results of another study showed that the presence of ready-made high-calorie meals, unhealthy snacks, stress, access to unhealthy food, time constraints, and the high price of healthy foods were the most important barriers to healthy eating among university students [47]. Also, based on the findings of previous studies, university students are at risk of unhealthy diets and, as a result, nutrient deficiencies and related health problems due to academic stress, time constraints, limited nutritional knowledge, cost, negative social norms, and peer influence [48]. Based on these findings, it seems that a healthy diet is not one of the priorities of university students, so it is necessary to create healthy nutrition interventions for this target group.

Moreover, our findings revealed that as the score of family support increases, the odds of membership in the very high-risk class decrease significantly. Previous studies have confirmed this finding in university students [49, 50]. According to various studies, family support is one of the most effective factors in preventing risky behaviors in individuals [7, 51]. Goodrum et al. showed that the existence of cohesion in the family and the strengthening of supportive relationships in families is a protective factor for improving social behaviors, reducing mental health problems, and reducing the occurrence of high-risk behaviors in people [37]. Ajisuksmo concluded in his study that parents play an important role in protecting their children from high-risk behaviors. Communication, support, and supervision from parents help children not feel alone when facing problems and challenges in their lives [52]. Our findings were corroborated by another study, which demonstrated that parental involvement in providing emotional support and monitoring children’s performance significantly reduces the occurrence of high-risk behaviors among them [53].

The present study offers nuanced insights into the role of religiosity in shaping risk-taking behaviors among Iranian university students, revealing partial protective effects consistent with prior research. Analysis of individual behaviors (Table 1) demonstrates that students engaging in substance-related risks—such as cigarette smoking, hookah use, and alcohol consumption—exhibit significantly lower religiosity scores compared to their abstinent peers. However, for other behaviors like physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, physical fighting, illicit drug use, and sexual risk behaviors, religiosity scores did not differ significantly, indicating that the influence of religiosity is not universal across all risk behaviors. This pattern aligns with findings from other studies, which suggest that religiosity’s impact on risk behaviors varies depending on the type of behavior and contextual factors [54, 55]. One possible explanation is that the low prevalence and potential underreporting of highly stigmatized behaviors—such as illicit drug use and sexual risk—may reduce statistical power to detect significant associations [55]. Additionally, these behaviors may be more strongly influenced by factors such as peer networks, mental health, or socioeconomic status, which could overshadow any protective effects of religiosity [54]. Furthermore, cultural norms may shape both the expression of religiosity and the likelihood of engaging in or reporting certain behaviors, leading to variation in observed associations across different risk domains. These considerations highlight the complexity of the relationship between religiosity and risk-taking and underscore the importance of addressing multiple influences when designing interventions for university students.

Conversely, when examining latent classes representing broader, multidimensional patterns of risk behavior (Table 4), religiosity does not emerge as a significant predictor of membership in moderate, high, or very high-risk groups. According to a survey in Iran, religious beliefs play a crucial role in supporting mental health, and the occurrence of high-risk behaviors is strongly influenced by individuals’ religious beliefs [35]. Some studies have revealed that individuals avoid risky behaviors due to fear of God committing sins, fear of parental disapproval, and fear of damaging their future, suggesting religiosity can affect perceptions of engaging in risk behaviors and the desire to do so [52]. However, the differential impact observed in our study underscores the complexity of religiosity as a multifaceted construct influenced by individual interpretation and socio-cultural context, particularly within Iran’s Muslim population, where religious adherence intertwines with daily social norms.

Finally, in terms of sex in the present study, males had higher odds of cigarette and hookah smoking, Physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, and sexual risk behavior compared to females. Numerous studies have confirmed that men are more likely to engage in high-risk behaviors than women [5, 56]. Vakili et al. showed a higher prevalence of high-risk health behaviors among boys than girls [57]. Hamzeh et al. believe that men are more likely to engage in risky behaviors due to various stressors caused by family, job, and social responsibilities [58]. In addition, these differences can be explained by the fact that there are physiological differences, favorable conditions for risky behaviors, more freedom, cultural acceptance, limited supervision, and wider social relationships among boys than girls [59–61]. Other studies have also confirmed the link between testosterone and risk-taking in men and aggressive behavior [62, 63].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretations of the relationships between religiosity, familial support, and risk-taking behaviors. Data collected via self-administered questionnaires may be influenced by reporting biases, such as social desirability and recall bias, especially for sensitive behaviors like substance use and sexual risk-taking. Additionally, cultural and religious norms may have led to underreporting of stigmatized behaviors, potentially resulting in underestimated prevalence and attenuated associations. Despite a high response rate of 85%, the voluntary nature of participation raises the possibility of selection bias if non-respondents differ systematically from respondents in relevant behaviors or beliefs. In addition, failing to account for possible confounding variables, such as psychiatric conditions, could have a negative impact on the validity of the findings. Future research employing longitudinal and mixed-method approaches across more diverse populations is recommended to understand these complex relationships better.

Conclusions

Using the LCA approach, this study identified four latent classes including low risk, moderate risk, high risk, and very high risk among Iranian students. High-risk and very high-risk classes had a prevalence of 7% and 6.3% respectively. Strengthening familial support may serve as an effective strategy to reduce risk-taking among university students. It is recommended that universities and public health authorities implement targeted, family-based, and culturally sensitive interventions to address these risks and improve student well-being. Beneficiaries of these findings include university administrators, health policymakers, and families seeking to support student health.
