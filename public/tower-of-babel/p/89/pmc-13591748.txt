Association of glycemic variability with 28-day mortality and adverse outcomes among non-diabetes patients after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass: a retrospective analysis on the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Shanshan Wang, Lina Lin, Shuang Jin, Lili Chen, Xiufang Huang, Xiuxia Lin, Na Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo analyze postoperative glycemic variability (GV) patterns in non-diabetic patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and determine the association between various GV metrics and adverse outcomes, including 28-day mortality.MethodsA retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, including 2,940 non-diabetic patients who underwent cardiac surgery with CPB. GV was quantified by mean blood glucose (MBG), largest amplitude of glycemic excursion (LAGE), standard deviation of blood glucose (SDBG), coefficient of variation (CV), and mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE). Cox proportional hazards models were used to determine the relationship between GV indices and clinical outcomes. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis examined non-linear associations, while receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess predictive accuracy for 28-day mortality.ResultsAbnormal MBG levels (hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia) and elevated GV metrics (LAGE, SDBG, CV, MAGE) were significantly linked to adverse clinical outcomes (P < 0.05). Results of Cox regression analysis demonstrated that LAGE and CV could strongly predict 28-day mortality compared to MBG, SDBG, and MAGE. RCS analysis revealed non-linear associations, with MBG exhibiting a U-shaped relationship. ROC analysis confirmed a superior predictive accuracy for LAGE (AUC = 0.845) and CV (AUC = 0.817) compared with other metrics (P < 0.05).ConclusionsPostoperative GV is an independent risk factor for 28-day mortality in non-diabetic CPB patients. LAGE and CV outperformed other GV indices in mortality prediction, highlighting the prognostic value of GV monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02373-0.

Introduction

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is an essential life-support technique in the realm of open-heart surgery, with over two million patients globally undergoing CPB-assisted cardiac procedures each year [1, 2]. As reported in the White Book of Chinese Cardiovascular Surgery and Extracorporeal Circulation, 263,292 cardiovascular surgeries were performed in 2022, of which 159,949 (60.7%) received CPB [3]. Inflammatory activation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation are key physiological stress responses triggered by this highly invasive procedure, all of which contribute to a higher incidence of postoperative complications. Studies have shown that post-CPB complications such as acute renal failure (ranging from 7.7% to 28.1%) [4], respiratory failure (11.8%) [5], neurological injury (6%) [5], delirium (ranging from 3% to 31%) [6], and perioperative mortality (ranging from 0.90% to 3.91%) [7]. Notably, the incidence of stress-induced hyperglycaemia has been on the rise, affecting about 60% to 80% of patients, a rate significantly higher relative to that recorded in non-CPB surgeries [8]. These complications prolong mechanical ventilation, increases the length of stay in intensive care units, longer hospitalizations, and elevated mortality rates [9, 10].

Glycemic variability (GV), which presents as fluctuations in blood glucose levels within or beyond the target range, can serve as an indicator of glucose homeostasis [11, 12]. GV, rather than persistent hyperglycaemia, has been shown to cause more extensive tissue damage, primarily through mechanisms involving endothelial injury and oxidative stress [13, 14]. Furthermore, GV can effectively predict adverse events compared to the mean glucose levels. In 2006, Egi et al. first illustrated that GV was a more reliable predictor of mortality than absolute glucose levels [14]. GV has been established as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality, as demonstrated by Kulkarni et al., whose analysis of 290,966 patients showed a 1.43-fold higher mortality risk in the highest GV quartile compared to the lowest [15].

Regarding postoperative complications, GV increases the risks of surgical site infections, delayed wound healing, enhances pain perception, and other complications [16, 17]. According to Bansal et al., GV is linked to extended ICU stays in patients undergoing cardiac surgery and an increased risk of AKI, as shown by higher creatinine levels [18]. A meta-analysis involving 16,411 patients further revealed that GV could independently elevate the risk of major adverse events (MAEs) [19]. Similar observations were obtained in cardiac surgeries involving CPB, especially in patients with preoperative hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels exceeding 6.5% [20]. A prospective study on CABG found no significant association between GV and MAEs, highlighting the inconsistency of evidence regarding GV in CPB-assisted cardiac surgery [21]. This inconsistency may arise from variations in GV measurement metrics (e.g., coefficient of variation [CV], mean amplitude of glycaemic excursions [MAGE], blood glucose risk index [BGRI]), differences in patient populations, or the duration of postoperative monitoring. Majority of studies on GV in cardiac surgery have focused on patients with preoperative hyperglycaemia (HbA1c > 6.5%). Although studies on non-cardiac surgery have established the GV cutoff values for risk stratification (e.g., BGRI ≥ 3.02) [22], analogous thresholds for CPB patients are not yet determined.

Against this context, we hypothesize that GV might independently predict 28-day mortality in non-diabetic patients following cardiac surgery with CPB, and that GV measurements could aid in risk stratification. Utilizing the MIMIC database cohort, this study aimed to: (1) explore the associations between GV and adverse outcomes; (2) quantify the association between GV and 28-day mortality; and (3) determine optimal GV cutoff points for clinical risk prediction. The results obtained from this research are expected to promote the development of evidence-based glycaemic management strategies aimed at reducing adverse outcomes following CPB.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using the MIMIC-IV version 3.0 database, a publicly available de-identified critical care database jointly developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, USA. It contains comprehensive clinical information for more than 94,458 ICU patients admitted between 2008 and 2022 [23]. One author completed the required CITI human subjects research training and was granted official access to the database through the PhysioNet platform (certification ID: 63770362). The original data collection was approved by the BIDMC Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent. Patients who underwent cardiac surgery with CPB were identified and enrolled based on ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients not admitted to any ICU, (2) patients with type I or type II diabetes, (3) ICU stays of less than 24 h, (4) patients with fewer than three blood glucose measurements, and (5) patients younger than 18 years. For patients with multiple admissions, only data from the first admission were collected.

Patient characteristics

The relevant medical information was extracted from the MIMIC-IV database using the Structured Query Language (SQL). The following data were obtained: (1) demographic information: age, gender, race, body mass index (BMI); (2) comorbidities were identified based on ICD-9 or ICD-10, including hypertension, heart failure, myocardial infarction, malignancy, chronic kidney disease (CKD), hyperlipidemia, stroke, pneumonia, and acute renal failure (ARF); (3) laboratory indicators included white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), platelet, hemoglobin, sodium, potassium, anion gap, potential of hydrogen (PH), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), oxygenation index, lactate, international normalized ratio (INR), urea nitrogen, creatinine; (4) vital signs included respiratory rate (RR), heart rate (HR), arterial blood pressure systolic (Abps), arterial blood pressure mean (Abpm), and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2); (5) severity of the disease included Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Score (SOFA), Glasgow coma scale (GCS) score and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), (6) medication therapy included glucocorticoid therapy and insulin therapy.

Exposure

GV was assessed using all real-time capillary blood glucose (CBG) measurements obtained during the entire postoperative ICU stay, which were collected as part of routine clinical care. Glucose data were included starting from the time of patient transfer to the ICU immediately after CPB surgery and ending at ICU discharge. Because monitoring frequency varied between patients, data collection intervals were driven by individual clinical needs rather than standardized protocols. To address the methodological challenges associated with variable measurement density and temporal distribution, a multimetric analytical approach was adopted in this study. The GV was quantified using the following methods: mean blood glucose (MBG), largest amplitude of glycemic excursion (LAGE), standard deviation of blood glucose (SDBG), coefficient of variation (CV), and mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE). The MBG reflected the patient’s overall blood glucose levels. The SDBG was used to measure the variability of glucose values relative to the mean level. The CV, a widely utilized metric for assessing variation, was determined as the ratio of SDBG to MBG. MAGE, a metric developed to assess significant glycemic fluctuations, was calculated as the mean of the absolute differences between successive glucose peaks and troughs that surpassed one standard deviation of the SDBG [22]. The LAGE revealed differences between the maximum and minimum glucose fluctuations (Supplemental Table 1). In the MIMIC database, blood glucose concentrations are presented in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL). To align with international standards, all blood glucose measurements were converted from mg/dL to millimoles per liter (mmol/L) based on the conversion factor: 1 mmol/L equals 18 mg/dL.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the 28-day mortality. The secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay (LOS), ICU LOS, the duration of mechanical ventilation (MV), the utilization rate of MV, the utilization rate of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and the incidence rates of hypoglycemia, sepsis, and AKI.

Statistical analysis

The baseline demographics and clinical features of the study population were analyzed using either the t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, which were expressed as the mean with standard deviations (SD) or median with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with proportions and analyzed with the Pearson χ² test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Prior to analysis, missing values were filled in using multiple chained equations since all variables had less than 20% missing data and were found to be missing completely at random (MCAR).

For initial analyses, all GV metrics were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4). To prevent disparities in the 28-day mortality rates observed within the ICU dataset, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was employed to obtain a balanced dataset, ensuring equitable representation of the survival and mortality cases. In contrast, conventional oversampling methods that merely replicate samples, the SMOTE algorithm selects a random instance from the nearest neighbors of each minority class instance and generates a new synthetic sample at a random point along the line connecting the two samples. Traditional oversampling methods often lead to excessive data expansion and greater model complexity, challenges that this methodology effectively circumvents [24].

Clinically relevant and prognosis-associated variables were included in the multivariate model as confounding variables. Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), with adjustments made for several confounding variables. Specifically, Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, race, and BMI; and Model 3 was further adjusted for age, gender, race, BMI, hypertension, heart failure, myocardial infarction, malignancy, chronic kidney disease (CKD), hyperlipidemia, stroke, pneumonia, ARF, MV, CRRT, glucocorticoid therapy, insulin therapy, and the number of glucose measurements. RCS models were fitted using GV as a continuous variable to assess nonlinear associations with 28-day mortality. Knot placement, ranging from 3 to 7, was optimized by minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

The effectiveness of the five GV metrics in risk discrimination was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each metric to determine its discriminatory power. Pairwise comparisons between the metrics were conducted using DeLong’s test to evaluate the statistical significance of the differences. All statistical analyses were performed in the R statistical package (R version 4.2.3), and 2-side P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

This study enrolled 2940 patients (Fig. 1), with major patient characteristics summarized in Table 1. The median age at surgery was 68 years, 69.76% were male, and 76.73% were White. The 28-day mortality rate was 1.60%. The median number of valid CBG measurements per patient during the entire postoperative ICU stay was 21.00 (IQR: 17.00–27.00), with a mean of 27.09 ± 25.02 measurements. Patients were divided into survival and non-survival groups based on 28-day survival outcomes. The non-survival group had a significantly higher BMI [30.05 (27.20, 32.65) vs. 28.34 (25.32, 31.73), P = 0.045] and higher prevalence of heart failure (P = 0.017), myocardial infarction (P < 0.001), CKD (P = 0.043), and ARF (P < 0.001) compared to the survival group. Significant between-group differences were also observed in WBC (P = 0.044), anion gap (P < 0.001), pH (P < 0.001), oxygenation index (P = 0.040), lactate levels (P = 0.010), INR (P < 0.001), BUN (P < 0.001), and creatinine levels (P < 0.001). The non-survival group had a higher respiratory rate (P = 0.002), but no significant differences in HR, SPO₂, Abps, or Abpm. Additionally, the non-survival group exhibited significantly higher scores in the SOFA (P < 0.001), APSIII (P < 0.001), GCS (P = 0.022), and CCI (P < 0.001) evaluations than the survival group.

Glycemic variability and outcomes

GV was calculated using five methods based on post-surgical glucose data from patients with at least three measurements, with outcome distributions provided in Supplemental Table 1. Patients were stratified into quartiles by MBG, LAGE, SDBG, CV and MAGE. Both low (Q1, < 6.65 mmol/L) and high (Q4, ≥ 7.34 mmol/L) MBG levels correlated significantly with adverse outcomes. Specifically, those in the hypoglycemia group showed a prolonged MV duration (9.00 h, IQR 4.83–19.45, P = 0.012), the highest rate of MV use (92.65%, P = 0.016), and the highest incidence of hypoglycaemia (25.31%, P < 0.001). Conversely, the hyperglycaemia group experienced the longest hospital stay (7.26 days, P < 0.001) and the highest incidence of AKI (76.33%, P = 0.045). However, the ICU LOS (P = 0.012), CRRT utilization (P = 0.649), or incidence of sepsis (P = 0.411) were not significantly different between the groups.

In relation to GV, elevated LAGE was associated with a significant rise in the MV duration, extending it from 6 h (IQR 4.00–10.75) in the first quartile (Q1) to 15.5 h (IQR 6.00–41.01) in the fourth quartile (Q4). Furthermore, the ICU LOS increased from 1.33 days (IQR 1.19–2.27) to 3.08 days (IQR 1.44–5.99), and the total hospital stay was increased from 5.83 days (IQR 4.57–7.91) to 9.08 days (IQR 6.10–14.79). Moreover, the application of CRRT increased from 0.13% to 6.56%, while the incidence of AKI increased from 67.16% to 80.19%. Notably, these changes were statistically significant (P < 0.001). Similar observations were identified for the other GV indices, including SDBG, CV, and MAGE, where higher variability was significantly correlated with poorer patient outcomes (Table 2).

Association between GV and 28-day mortality

Further analyses showed that the 28-day mortality and survival samples were matched using the SMOTE, with 2,893 cases for the non-survivor and survivor cohorts each. Multivariate Cox regression models were employed to examine the relationship of five GV indices with the 28-day mortality. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), the model adjusted for basic demographic characteristics (Model 2), and the model further adjusted for comorbidities, therapeutic interventions, and additional clinical confounders (Model 3), higher quartiles of all GV indices were strongly positively correlated with 28-day mortality, which revealed a clear trend of increasing mortality risk with ascending quartile levels (all P for trend < 0.001). Specifically, in the quartile analysis of MBG, the mortality risk was lower in the quartile analysis of MBG, the second (Q2) and third (Q3) quartiles compared to the first quartile (Q1) in Model 3 (HR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.68–0.85; HR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.78–0.97), whereas the fourth quartile (Q4) had a significantly increased risk (HR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.21–1.47). LAGE demonstrated the strongest predictive effect, with an adjusted HR of 22.85 (95% CI: 17.82–29.28) in the highest quartile (Q4). Similarly, the Q4 groups for SDBG, CV, and MAGE showed significantly elevated HRs of 8.15 (95% CI: 6.82–9.75), 17.60 (95% CI: 13.87–22.33), and 6.16 (95% CI: 5.22–7.27), respectively. Regardless of the covariates adjusted for, the HRs for LAGE and CV in the highest quartiles were significantly higher compared with those of other GV indices, suggesting that these are independent and robust predictors of 28-day mortality (Table 3).

Results of the RCS analyses further revealed nonlinear associations between various GV indices and 28-day mortality. Notably, the association of MBG with the 28-day mortality exhibited a “U-shaped” pattern, suggesting that both low and high MBG levels were associated with an elevated risk of mortality. In contrast, LAGE, SDBG, CV, and MAGE showed a monotonic, nonlinear relationships, with the mortality risk significantly increasing with the increase in these GV indices. This nonlinear trend was robust even after adjusting for fundamental demographic variables, including age, gender, race, and BMI, as well as significant clinical factors such as hypertension, heart failure, malignancy, CKD, glucocorticoid use, insulin therapy, CRRT, and MV. The results highlight that, beyond MBG, glycemic variability itself is a key prognostic factor. Specifically, elevated LAGE and CV values correspond to a markedly increased mortality risk (Supplemental Figs. 1–1 to 1–5).

Identification ability and cutoff points of GV

The predictive efficacy of five GV indices for the 28-day mortality was significantly investigated based on ROC curve analysis. The detailed results are shown in Table 4; Fig. 2, which revealed significant discrepancies among the GV indices. Notably, the LAGE index could effectively discriminate patients, with an AUC of 0.845 (95% CI: 0.808–0.888), and achieved the most balanced sensitivity (0.787) and specificity (0.776). Notably, its Youden index (0.563) was highest among the indices. Notably, the CV was ranked second, with an AUC of 0.817; achieving the highest sensitivity (0.830) among the five indices, with a relatively lower specificity (0.661). The analysis showed that SDBG and MAGE had moderate performance, with AUCs of 0.771 and 0.761, respectively; MAGE had a higher specificity (0.755) compared to SDBG (0.759). It was further observed that the MBG index had the lowest AUC of 0.517 (95% CI: 0.412–0.624), suggesting it had the poorest classification performance. Comparison of the of AUC values among the GV indices was performed using the DeLong test, which revealed significant differences between MBG and LAGE, SDBG, CV, and MAGE (all P < 0.001). No significant differences were found between LAGE and CV (P = 0.117), or between SDBG and MAGE (P = 0.484). In contrast, all other pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences. The optimal cutoff values were 7.50 for MBG, 6.67 for LAGE, 1.67 for SDBG, 0.22 for CV, and 2.98 for MAGE, as detailed in Supplemental Tables 2 and 3.

Discussion

Researchers analyzed the MIMIC-IV database to study the link between GV and 28-day mortality in non-diabetic patients after cardiac surgery with CPB. The findings showed that several GV indices could independently predict the 28-day mortality. Patients with higher GV experienced longer MV and ICU stays, increased needs for CRRT and MV, and higher rates of sepsis, AKI, and hypoglycemia. These results highlight the importance of managing GV in perioperative care, suggesting it may be more crucial than controlling average glucose levels.

Glycemic variability has been significantly associated with the 28-day mortality

A robust and significant association was found between the GV and 28-day mortality in non-diabetic patients. Even after extensive adjustments for multiple potential confounders (including demographic variables (such as age, gender, and BMI), underlying comorbidities (such as hypertension, heart failure, and hyperlipidemia), and treatment-related factors (such as MV, insulin therapy, and the number of glucose measurements), patients with higher GV were found to have a notably higher risk of mortality. GV emerges from these findings as an independent risk factor with notable prognostic value in assessing perioperative outcomes.

The MBG exhibited a significant U-shaped association with 28-day mortality, with the highest quartile (Q4, ≥ 7.34 mmol/L) with an unadjusted HR of 1.49 (95% CI: 1.36–1.64), which remained significant after adjusting for covariates. Notably, this HR was 15-fold lower compared that of the LAGE, demonstrating that MBG alone cannot account for postoperative mortality. The RCS analysis showed a U-shaped correlation between MBG levels and mortality, indicating an elevated risk at both hypoglycaemic and hyperglycaemic extremes. LAGE, as an indicator of extreme glycemic fluctuation, demonstrated an adjusted HR of 22.85 (95% CI: 17.82–29.28) for the highest quartile. The RCS analysis revealed a non-linear link between LAGE and 28-day mortality, indicating a threshold effect where high GV significantly raises the mortality risk. These results emphasize that extreme glycemic fluctuations are a crucial predictor of short-term mortality in non-diabetic patients following CPB in an ICU setting. The CV showed the second most significant association (HR = 17.60, 95% CI: 13.87–22.33) among GV metrics, with its standardized form enabling consistent use regardless of baseline glucose levels. This result aligns with the research conducted by Akirov et al. [25] and Singh et al. [26], who reported increased ICU mortality associated with higher quartiles of CV and SDBG. Although MAGE is influenced by the sampling frequency, a MAGE exceeding 65 mg/dL was linked to a 30-day mortality HR of 1.607 (P < 0.001) [27]. Compared to sole management MBG, reducing glycemic variability may contribute to a decrease in 28-day mortality [22]. Thus, managing glycemia should aim not only at optimizing absolute glucose levels but also at minimizing GV.

Glycemic variability is significantly associated with adverse outcomes

Among the explored GV indices, patients in the highest quartile of MBG (Q4, ≥ 7.34 mmol/L) had higher incidence of AKI and longer hospital stays (P < 0.05), likely due to hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and multi-organ damage [10, 28]. Indices reflecting GV magnitude (LAGE, MAGE) correlated positively with hospital LOS, MV duration, and incidence of sepsis and AKI. The SDBG and CV represent the degree of glycemic dispersion and relative variability, respectively. The highest quartile of CV (Q4, ≥ 23.3%) was associated with prolonged MV and increased need for CRRT (P < 0.05), with significant glycemic fluctuations potentially exacerbating ischemia-reperfusion injury and impairing neutrophil activity [29, 30]. A bimodal distribution of hypoglycemia risk was observed: the lowest MBG quartile had directly increased hypoglycemic events (25.31%), while the highest quartiles of LAGE (37.16%) and CV (36.60%) showed heightened hypoglycemia indirectly due to aggressive glucose-lowering, consistent with the NICE-SUGAR study indicating overly strict control raises hypoglycemia-related risks [31]. This highlights the need to balance efficacy and safety in perioperative glycemic control to avoid both hypoglycemia and significant GV. Even with adequate glucose control, GV remains associated with increased mortality and adverse outcomes, as different GV indices reflect distinct metabolic dysfunction features [32, 33]. Thus, singular glycemic control strategies are inadvisable, dynamic monitoring with multidimensional indices is needed for personalized management and improved prognosis.

An important unresolved question is whether GV causes adverse outcomes or merely reflects increased patient acuity. This retrospective study cannot establish definitive causality, and a bidirectional relationship likely exists: more severe illness and postoperative stress impair glucose homeostasis, while glycemic fluctuations themselves may exacerbate organ injury. Notably, the association between GV and 28-day mortality remained highly significant after adjustment for multiple markers of disease severity, indicating GV provides independent prognostic information. Regardless of causal direction, GV is a simple, real-time biomarker for identifying high-risk patients. Definitive proof that reducing GV improves outcomes requires prospective randomized controlled trials.

LAGE: The optimal metric for evaluating glycemic variability

Currently, there is no agreement on how GV relates to ICU mortality after CPB. Nonetheless, earlier research indicates that GV could independently predict negative outcomes after surgery, despite being based on various indices. For instance, Donati et al. [32] reported that the Glycaemic Lability Index (GLI) was the most correlated marker with hospital mortality (AUC = 0.61), slightly outperforming the SDBG and CV. A prospective cohort study of 1,963 adults showed GRI and CV had similar predictive value, with GRI modestly superior [34]. In the present study, LAGE showed superior efficacy (AUC = 0.845, 95% CI:0.808–0.888), outperforming CV (AUC = 0.817; 95% CI:0.763–0.867), MBG, SDBG, and MAGE (P < 0.05). It strongly predicts short-term mortality in non-diabetic patients post-CPB, intuitively capturing marked metabolic instability—consistent with Issarawattana et al. [35], who noted maximum glycemic difference (AUC = 0.69) robustly indicates GV in critically ill patients. Multivariate analyses confirmed LAGE’s strong association with 28-day mortality and adverse outcomes, supporting its role as a critical GV measure in this population. Despite extensive research on GV quantification, standardized clinical implementation methods remain inadequate. While indices like SDBG, CV, and MAGE are comprehensive, their bedside use is limited by complex calculations and resource-heavy data acquisition [36]. In contrast, LAGE is uniquely positioned as the most clinically replicable GV metric available to all frontline clinicians. It is calculated simply as the difference between the maximum and minimum glucose values over a defined period, requiring no specialized statistical software, complex algorithms, or continuous glucose monitoring devices. Any clinician can compute LAGE in seconds using only routine bedside capillary glucose measurements recorded in the electronic medical record, even with a basic spreadsheet or manual calculation. This unparalleled simplicity enables rapid, real-time assessment of glycemic instability at the bedside, facilitating timely interventions such as insulin titration or nutritional adjustments without disrupting clinical workflows.

Moreover, we identified novel cutoff values for each GV metric, which has not been previously reported in non-diabetic populations. These thresholds demonstrate the potential of GV metrics, particularly the LAGE, to improve clinical diverse populations. However, further multicentre prospective studies are necessary to validate these cutoffs and elucidate their utility in guiding perioperative glycemic management. In summary, LAGE demonstrates high sensitivity, operational simplicity, and strong prognostic value in non-diabetic patients after CPB, advocating for its broader clinical application to improve postoperative care and outcomes.

Additionally, separate analyses in diabetic patients are warranted to determine whether the prognostic significance of GV observed in this study extends to this important patient population.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study need to be acknowledged. First, the total number of glucose measurements per patient was lower than that recommended for long-term (1–2 weeks) outpatient GV assessment, which is an inherent limitation of studies focusing on the acute postoperative ICU period. Additionally, glucose data collection intervals were driven by individual clinical needs rather than a standardized research protocol, leading to variable measurement frequency across patients. We mitigated potential bias arising from both issues by excluding patients with fewer than 3 valid measurements and using multiple complementary GV indices. Second, although adjustments were made for various demographic variables and clinical characteristics, specific potential confounding factors, such as intraoperative management, dietary patterns, and nutritional intake, were not included due to data constraints. In future, researchers should aim to collect more comprehensive data regarding these potential influencing factors to promote the refinement of the adjustment model. Third, the statistical stability of the results may have been compromised by limited sample sizes in specific subgroups, which could result in the underestimation or overestimation of effect sizes. Even though SMOTE was used to balance the smaller groups in the dataset, some bias might still exist because ICU patients differ in how sick they are and the treatments they receive. Future work will focus on collecting more data and ensuring better balance between subgroups to make the results more reliable. Finally, we only evaluated five commonly used GV indices and did not assess other metrics such as M-value, J-index, or CONGA, which may provide additional insights into glycemic dynamics. Future studies using standardized continuous glucose monitoring protocols are needed to compare the prognostic performance of different GV indices in this patient population.

Conclusion

In this study, we explored the association between GV and adverse outcomes in non-diabetic patients after CPB, utilizing data from the MIMIC-IV database. A significant association was found between GV and 28-day mortality and adverse outcomes. These findings highlight the significance of including GV monitoring in ICU glucose management protocols to help identify high-risk patients and improve early interventions. GV indicators, such as the LAGE, provide sensitive detection of metabolic fluctuations and are practical for real-time bedside monitoring in the ICU. These results underscore the importance of GV as a prognostic indicator, acting as an independent risk factor in assessing patient outcomes during the perioperative period.
