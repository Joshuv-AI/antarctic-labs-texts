DNA Repair gene alterations and efficacy from gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel with/without durvalumab and tremelimumab in metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma

ABSTRACT
The CCTG PA.7 study was a randomized phase II trial comparing chemotherapy with and without dual immune checkpoint inhibition in patients with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (mPDAC). In follow-up to the published primary results of the trial, the analysis herein focused on long-term survival and exploratory analysis.Plasma sequencing analysis identified concurrent mutations in DNA damage repair genes BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA in 18/173 (10.40%) of patients, and presence of two or more mutations in these genes was associated with overall survival benefit for patients receiving immunotherapy (median overall survival 26.2 vs. 9.7 months; hazard ratio, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.16–0.68; p = 0.001; interaction p = 0.003). Partial response was observed in 7/11 (63.64%) patients with concurrent DNA damage repair gene mutations in the immunotherapy arm. As a prospective study in PDAC identifying a potential biomarker beyond mismatch repair deficiency for benefit from immunotherapy, these data highlight an actionable subgroup of mPDAC.

Introduction

Metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (mPDAC) remains a common and highly lethal disease, projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related death by 20301. Despite modest progress in chemotherapy options in the metastatic setting, new treatments for PDAC are needed2,3. To date, the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors has shown minimal benefit in mPDAC, outside of the mismatch repair deficiency (MMR) setting4–7. It remains to be determined whether the combination of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy with chemotherapy may induce greater immune sensitivity by interfering with the immunosuppressive effects of cancer-associated fibroblasts and the desmoplastic stroma characteristic of pancreatic adenocarcinoma8–11. The CCTG PA.7 trial (NCT02879318) was designed to investigate this hypothesis in a multi-center, randomized, phase II trial in which the efficacy of combination chemotherapy (gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel) plus dual immune checkpoint inhibition (durvalumab and tremelimumab) was compared versus chemotherapy alone12.

The primary analysis did not demonstrate a significant improvement in overall nor progression-free survival when immunotherapy was given in combination with chemotherapy. However, continued long-term patient follow-up has identified a subset of patients surviving longer than four years on study treatment. Here, we report the final survival analysis along with correlative analysis using circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) sequencing to assess biomarkers of long-term treatment response in this cohort.

Results

Chemotherapy combined with dual checkpoint inhibition does not impact survival in an unselected population of patients with mPDAC

The design for the CCTG PA.7 study was a randomized phase II trial comparing chemotherapy (gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel; G + N) with and without dual immune checkpoint inhibition (durvalumab and tremelimumab; D + T) as first-line therapy in patients with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (mPDAC). Long-term survival analysis was performed with a median follow-up time of 81.6 months. Across the trial, there was a total of 6/180 (3.33%) patients with overall survival (OS) greater than four years - five of which had OS greater than five years and four of which had OS greater than six years (Fig. 1A). Univariate cox proportional hazards analysis was performed in subgroups of patients (defined by performance status, age, sex, race or number of organ sites with metastases) to evaluate the association between treatment (G + N + D + T relative to G + N) and OS, and revealed no differences between treatment groups (Fig. 1C). At the time of data cutoff (September 21, 2024), 172 deaths were observed: 113/119 (94.96%) on G + N + D + T and 59/61 (96.72%) on G + N, with four patients known alive (three on G + N + D + T and one on G + N). A total of 4/119 (3.36%) patients on G + N + D + T were still alive five years after registration or randomization, which led to an estimate of five-year survival at 3.48% (95% CI, 1.33–9.12%), compared to 1/61 (1.64%) patients on G + N (five-year survival rate, 1.67%; 95% CI, 0.24–11.70%). In addition to the 180 randomized patients were 11 safety run-in patients treated with G + N + D + T. Two of the run-in group patients had OS times of 6.57 and 5.84 years, and run-in patients were included together with randomized patients in a supplementary landmark survival analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2).

DNA damage repair gene mutations detected in plasma are associated with immunotherapy response

Plasma-based baseline (pre-treatment) mutation data (somatic and germline-likely mutation calls; PredicineATLAS 600 gene panel) were available for 173 patients (115 G + N + D + T and 58 G + N; Supplementary Fig. 3). Only one of 173 tumors (0.58%) showed microsatellite instability (MSI) – this patient received G + N + D + T and had an OS of 3.16 years and was included in the analysis. Cox regression-based modeling with Lasso regularization is a useful tool for identifying and ranking features predictive of survival hazard, out of a larger set of features of interest13. To identify mutations uniquely associated with improved OS in the immunotherapy group, we applied this method to our dataset. Results of this analysis showed five genes that had somatic and germline-likely status associated with hazard exclusive to the G + N + D + T group, four of which were DNA damage repair (DDR) genes BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA (Fig. 2A). Given the relationship between DDR pathways and immunotherapy response in cancer, these four DDR genes were chosen for subsequent analysis14. Across all patients, nine (5.20%), 12 (6.93%), 16 (9.25%) and eight (4.62%) patients had germline-likely mutations in BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA, respectively. Somatic mutations were observed in four (2.31%), four (2.31%), 15 (8.67%) and four (2.31%) patients for BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA, respectively. To further assess DDR gene mutation across the cohort, we visualized somatic and germline-likely mutation status across the four predictive genes of interest, for patients ordered by increasing OS (Fig. 2B). Our analysis considered somatic and germline-likely mutation status of each gene separately, resulting in a total of eight mutation statuses to be individually counted for each patient (hereafter referred to as ‘hits’). In the G + N + D + T group, 28/115 (24.35%) patients had one hit, while 11/115 (9.57%) patients had two or more hits, the latter of which will hereafter be referred to as the ‘≥ 2 DDR gene mutation’ group. In patients treated with G + N, 6/58 (10.34%) and 7/58 (12.07%) patients had one or two or more hits, respectively. We compared objective response rates between treatment groups, for patients stratified by ≥ 2 DDR gene mutation (Fig. 2C). In the G + N + D + T group, 7/11 (63.64%) patients with ≥ 2 DDR gene mutations showed partial response compared to 28/104 (26.92%) in other patients (odds ratio, 4.67; 95% CI, 1.09-23.51; p = 0.033). With a 77% detectability rate for KRAS hotspot mutations in our study cohort, while remaining among the highest reported in PDAC to date, there remains room for improvement for ctDNA sensitivity in PDAC12,16. Meanwhile, tissue-based genome sequencing remains an established method for mutation detection. Archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue samples were available for whole-genome sequencing for a subset of patients that received plasma sequencing (n = 46) and offered an opportunity to validate the DDR gene mutation pattern observed in plasma. For patients with both datasets available, the timing between tissue and plasma sampling occurred at a median of 35.00 days (min = 6.00, max = 1152.00). Across all samples (n = 46), 12/15 (80.00%) germline-likely and 2/7 (28.57%) somatic mutations identified in plasma were also identified in matched tissue (Fig. 4). Discussion

The inability for an investigational treatment to confer significant clinical benefit in an unselected population of patients with PDAC remains a major challenge and includes the recent PA.7 trial of combination chemotherapy plus dual checkpoint inhibition. Despite the lack of benefit noted in an unselected patient population, a subset of patients experienced exceptional survival, reflected in the five-year survival of 3.48% in the experimental arm versus 1.67% in the chemotherapy arm. This highlights the importance of determining whether tumor biomarkers exist that could identify exceptional responders prior to treatment, enabling treatment selection that is tailored to an individual’s tumor. Here, we marshaled correlative ctDNA sequencing data to identify a concurrent pattern of DDR mutations across BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA that was strongly associated with long-term response to combination chemotherapy plus dual checkpoint inhibition in mPDAC.

The association between DDR pathway deficiency and immunotherapy response in PDAC is supported by a previous phase 1b/2 study, in which a six-month survival rate of 59.6% was observed for patients with platinum-sensitive advanced PDAC that were treated with niraparib plus ipilimumab17. However, in that trial, only patients without disease progression at 16 weeks on standard of care chemotherapy were enrolled, chemotherapy was discontinued at the time of enrollment, and no formal statistical contrast was made to contemporaneous control data. In addition, a small case series (n = 12 patients) showed efficacy of ipilimumab/nivolumab in patients with metastatic pancreatic or biliary cancers that had homologous recombination repair-deficient tumors18. While pancreatic tumors harboring DDR gene mutations have been shown to elicit better treatment response in general19,20, our observation is based on a formal comparison in a randomized control trial design and suggests an efficacy that is specific to patients treated to combination chemotherapy and immunotherapy, as compared to chemotherapy alone. MSI-high status has been associated with immunotherapy response in PDAC21. One patient with MSI-high status was identified in our study and was part of the 11 patients in the G + N + D + T group with ≥ 2 DDR gene mutations. The interaction, and potential confounding effect, between ≥ 2 DDR gene mutations and MSI-high status remains a limitation outside the scope of our study and will require better representation from MSI-high tumors to investigate.

The association between DNA damage repair aberrations (often manifesting in higher tumor mutational burden [TMB]) and immunotherapy response is well-documented for several cancer types. While rare in PDAC, mutations in POLE have been associated with response to immune checkpoint inhibition across a cohort of 16 different advanced tumor types22. Accumulation of such studies have resulted in the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of immune checkpoint inhibitor pembrolizumab in TMB-high solid tumors23. While a study of 21,932 patients with PDAC identified an association between TMB-high tumors and immunotherapy response, the TMB-high subgroup represented only approximately 1% of patients with PDAC, and the number of TMB-high PDAC tumors treated with immunotherapy remains small24,25. Here, we highlight a subgroup of mPDAC occurring at 10.40% frequency with tumors harboring mutations known to be associated with higher TMB (BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA), but these tumors did not show high TMB at baseline (first-line treatment initiation). It remains to be determined whether such tumors would accumulate mutations, and thus increase TMB, over the continued course of treatment, and this presents an important question for future research.

While identified in the plasma sequencing data, BRCA2 variants were not associated with OS in the G + N + D + T group, while BRCA1 variants were. While this result may be unintuitive given the relationship between BRCA2 variants and immunotherapy response in other advanced cancers, the relationship between BRCA2 mutation and immunotherapy response is rather limited in studies of PDAC. Importantly, studies investigating BRCA mutation status versus immunotherapy response in PDAC often combine immunotherapy with PARP inhibition, and treatments are given post-first-line treatment (NCT03851614, NCT03404960). Here, our study provides insight into the BRCA2 association (or lack thereof) with response to combination immunotherapy plus chemotherapy in the first-line (treatment naïve) setting, which has limited comparable studies (both in terms of disease type and treatment strategy) to form expectations with regard to BRCA1 versus BRCA2 association.

The aggressive nature of PDAC results in rapid clinical deterioration and pressure to promptly initiate first-line treatment upon diagnosis, leaving little time for tissue biopsy, sequencing and analysis that would be required for biomarker-guided treatment selection. Blood-based tumor profiling via ctDNA sequencing, therefore, represents an ideal strategy for biomarker identification in PDAC16. While there remains room for improvement regarding the sensitivity in detecting variants via ctDNA, our ability to validate the majority of DDR biomarker-positive samples between ctDNA and matched tissue whole-genome sequencing is evidence in support of these data being robust. Recent studies have converged on the rationale and need for biomarkers to aid in treatment selection for mPDAC, such as PASS-01 (molecular subtype) and POLO (BRCA1/2 mutation) trials26,27, and having biomarkers capable of detection via ctDNA will aid in the deployment of such strategies into the clinic.

We emphasize that the correlative analysis was exploratory and is limited by the lack of an independent study cohort in which the regression model could be validated. Variant detection via ctDNA sequencing is less sensitive when compared to tissue-based whole-genome sequencing, and we detected mutant KRAS at approximately 86% sensitivity, as the overall KRAS mutation frequency was 77% while expected to be closer to 90% for a cohort of mPDAC12. The frequency of the DDR gene mutation may therefore be underestimated in this dataset. Preservation of genomic material for sequencing by formalin fixation has negative effects on DNA integrity prior to sequencing28, and this represents a limitation for our variant detection in the archival tissue dataset. While efforts were taken to reduce confounding signal from CHIP mutations, CHIP identification remains an inherent limitation of plasma-based mutation detection. Finally, our study is limited by the assumption that each variant in BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA had an equally negative functional impact on protein activity. While established efforts were undertaken to restrict the analysis to likely loss-of-function variants, many variants were unable to be assessed for evidence-based functional impact. In fact, excluding variants based on functional impact was not feasible for this analysis, as few variants had this information available. As the pool of variants was limited by the inherently low mutation burden known to PDAC29, too few variants would remain for analysis if filtered by functional impact. Moreover, such impacts are predictions, and the true impact a given variant has in its specific cell type, microenvironment and genomic (other interacting mutations) context is largely undetermined. For these reasons, our analysis used a gene-based approach for germline-likely and somatic variant testing, with functional annotation performed to annotate variants when available. As the association between DDR gene mutation and immunotherapy response is reflected in a relatively small patient population, subsequent clinical trial investigation is warranted to validate these findings.

In conclusion, the presence of concurrent DNA damage repair gene mutations was strongly and uniquely associated with benefit to the combination of chemotherapy and dual checkpoint inhibitor therapy, and patients with this signature had a median overall survival of over 2 years. This represents an important prospective randomized clinical trial in PDAC identifying a potential biomarker beyond mismatch repair deficiency for benefit from immune checkpoint therapy. In the context of generally poor outcomes for mPDAC, in addition to the need for further confirmatory investigations, clinical testing for DNA damage repair gene mutations should be considered to facilitate treatment decision-making for patients with mPDAC.

Methods

Trial design and patient enrollment

PA.7 was a randomized phase II clinical trial conducted within 28 centers across Canada and received institutional review board approval, and was carried out per the Declaration of Helsinki and International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (NCT02879318). The study protocol was approved by the REB of participating centers, the Ontario Cancer Research Ethics Board and the UBC BC Cancer Research Ethics Board. Information on clinical trial registration and study protocol can be found at: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02879318. After a safety run-in of 11 patients diagnosed with mPDAC, an additional 180 patients were enrolled. Inclusion and exclusion criteria have been fully described previously12. Patients were randomized to receive gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2 D1, 8, 15), nab-paclitaxel (125 mg/m2 D1, 8, 15), durvalumab (1500 mg D1 q 28 days) and tremelimumab (75 mg D1 for first 4 cycles; G + N + D + T; n = 119) versus gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel alone (G + N; n = 61) in a 2:1 ratio (Supplementary Fig. 1). Randomization was dynamically balanced by ECOG (0 vs 1) and receipt of prior adjuvant therapy (yes/no) using the method of minimization and performed centrally by the Canadian Cancer Trials Group (CCTG). Patients were analyzed in the arm to which they were randomized, regardless of the treatment they received (intent-to-treat analysis). Overall survival was the primary endpoint. Secondary endpoints included progression-free survival, safety, overall response rate and quality of life. Patients provided written informed consent. The CCTG Data and Safety Monitoring Committee regularly evaluated the conduct and safety of the study.

cfDNA sequencing and variant calling

Blood samples were collected from patients at study baseline (after randomization but before the first dose of study treatment) and using two Streck tubes (10 mL) plus an additional EDTA tube (6 mL) with immediate spin-down. Circulating cell-free DNA was sequenced using the 600-gene PredicineATLASTM panel and processed using the Predicine DeepSea NGS analysis pipeline as described previously12. Briefly, circulating cfDNA was extracted using QIAamp circulating nucleic acid kits from plasma samples and five to 30 ng of extracted cfDNA were subjected for library construction, including end-repair dA-tailing and adapter ligation and sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 for NGS sequencing with paired-end 2 x 150bp kits. Candidate variants were called by comparing with the local variant background (defined based on plasma samples from healthy donors and historical data). Variants were further filtered by log-odds threshold30, base and mapping quality thresholds, repeat regions and other quality metrics. ctDNA-based mutations were lifted from hg19 to hg38 using liftover31. The individual gene affected by each variant (for instance, common clonal haematopoietic mutations of indeterminate potential (CHIP) genes DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1 and JAK2), together with the variant allele frequency, were used to label variants as potentially arising from CHIP and were excluded from analysis.

PredicineATLAS germline filtering included both population-based and VAF-based filtering. Variants were first screened against population and clinical databases (gnomAD, ClinVar, dbSNP, COSMIC). Variants with population allele frequency ≥ 1 × 10⁻³ were excluded from the somatic set (retained separately if clinically relevant). Benign/likely benign ClinVar entries were also excluded. The PredicineATLAS assay included a genome-wide germline SNP skeleton, based on which copy number segmentations and CNV-based tumor fractions were inferred. Copy number neutral variants with VAFs between 40–60% (heterozygous) or > 85% (homozygous) were labeled as germline-suspect. A wider VAF range was used for insertion/deletion (indel) variants (as higher indel VAF variations were likely due to probe capture efficiency variation). For variants with copy number changes, the VAF distribution of their nearby germline SNPs (within the same copy number segments) was also considered for germline filtering.

Variants in canonical CHIP genes (DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, JAK2 and PPM1D) were marked as likely-CHIP variants. Variants in other CHIP-related genes (such as TP53, CHEK2, ATM, SF3B1, SRSF2 and U2AF1) were considered as likely somatic unless their VAFs were significantly deviated from the CNV-based tumor fraction and VAF distributions of other likely somatic variants. To avoid over-filtering of variants, hotspot mutations (defined based on literature, public and in-house databases, excluding canonical CHIP genes) were exempt from VAF filtering. Variants showing short-fragment enrichment (90–150 bp vs 151–220 bp) were also retained as somatic mutations.

Machine learning-based Cox regression analysis

Cox regression models with Lasso regularization were used to rank gene-level mutations (somatic and germline-likely treated separately) by their association with hazard (overall survival) in the G + N + D + T and G + N groups separately using glmnet v4.132,33. As input for predictive modeling, all genes with somatic mutation in at least two patients who had overall survival greater than three years in the G + N + D + T group, or germline-likely mutation in at least two patients who had overall survival greater than three years in the G + N + D + T group, were selected for analysis (n = 19 genes). The status of somatic and germline-likely mutation was treated separately for each gene, for a total of 38 features. Regression models were constructed in G + N + D + T and G + N groups separately. Parameter family = cox was used along with alpha = 1. Default parameters resulted in a total of 58 and 60 values of the regularization parameter lambda, which generated the 58 and 60 different models for G + N + D + T and G + N treatment groups, respectively. Shrinkage parameter lambda controls the degree of inclusion for each predictive feature in regression, and multiple models are generated across increasingly stringent values of lambda. By visualizing the predictive utility of each mutation across models, the most predictive genes (or combinations thereof) were precipitated, which identified the four genes of interest (BRCA1, POLE, ATM and FANCA).

Whole-genome sequencing of archival tissue samples

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded archival tissue samples were sequenced as previously described for 46 patients that had available ctDNA data for comparison34. Briefly, tumor and matched normal reads were aligned to the reference genome build hg38 using minimap2 v.2.1535 and duplicate reads were marked using sambamba v.2.736. Variant calling was performed using Strelka2 v.2.9.1037 and Manta v.1.5.038, and Snpeff v.4.339. Variants were further annotated using vcf2maf v.1.6.22, VEP v.103.140 and ExAC (ExAC_nonTCGA.r0.3.1.sites.vep.vcf.gz). An ExAC total allele frequency threshold of 0.04% was used to exclude germline variants of high frequency in healthy populations, and only germline variants annotated as moderate or high impact by Strelka2 were included. For both somatic and germline mutations, only missense, nonsense, in-frame and frameshift variants were considered for analysis.

OncoKB variant annotation

Mutations (somatic and germline-likely) affecting genes of interest were subjected to evidence-based functional annotation using OncoKB v3.4.1 with genome build hg38 and default parameters15. Lolliplots were generated for each gene using trackViewer v1.22.1 with USCS exon coordinates (hg38), and coloring was based on the ‘MUTATION_EFFECT’ output from OncoKB.

TMB calculation

ctDNA-based tumor mutational burden score was defined based on the number of somatic coding single nucleotide variants, including both synonymous and nonsynonymous variants, within panel target regions to generate a tumor mutational burden score metric, as described previously12. Tissue-based tumor mutational burden was calculated according to the TMB harmonization project guidelines41.

Mutational signatures

Tri-nucleotide context for each single-nucleotide somatic variant were generated using BSgenome v1.54.0 and corrected for artifacts related to formalin fixation using FFPEsig42. Mutational signatures were calculated using mutSignatures v2.1.143 based on COSMIC v3.4 SBS signatures (hg38)44. Out of 86 possible signatures, 24 were chosen for final analysis based on having a median score greater than zero when all 96 possibilities were tested.

Statistical analysis

This study was designed to detect, with an 80% power and a two-sided 10% level, a hazard ratio of 0.65 between two treatment arms, which corresponded to an increase of the median overall survival from 8.5 months for the G + N arm to 13.1 months for the G + N + D + T arm and required observation of 150 deaths before the final analysis. Overall and progression-free survival were evaluated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using log-rank tests stratified by performance status (ECOG) and prior adjuvant therapy (yes/no). Stratified Cox models, stratified by ECOG and prior adjuvant therapy, were used to calculate hazard ratio and 95% confidence intervals. Cox proportional hazard subgroup analysis was performed using unstratified Cox models. Kaplan-Meier curves assessing overall and progression-free survival were generated using the GGally v2.0.0. Survival rates, median survival times and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated using survival v3.1. Comparisons at threshold overall survival values were compared using log-rank tests. Objective response rates between treatment groups were compared using Fisher’s exact tests (partial response versus rest). All statistical tests were two-sided. Multiple test correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg test correction where applicable. R v3.6.3 was used for data analysis.
