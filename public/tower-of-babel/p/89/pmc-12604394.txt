Task-sharing anaesthesia in conflict zones: a cross-sectional study of safety gaps and systemic failures in Wad Medani, Sudan
Authors: Alaa Mohamed, Sami Mahjoub Taha, Yassir Mohamed Hassan, Mohamed Bashir, Christella Alphonsus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Sudan, where physician anaesthesiologists are critically scarce (0.47/100,000 population), non-physician anaesthesia providers (NPAPs) deliver most perioperative care, a situation exacerbated by conflict-driven health system collapse. Despite global evidence supporting task sharing and task shifting, NPAP safety outcomes in fragile settings remain unquantified. This study aimed to quantify NPAP anaesthesia delivery, compare supervised and unsupervised safety outcomes, and identify systemic gaps in conflict-affected Sudan.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional clinical audit of 1,559 surgical cases across four referral hospitals in Wad Medani, Sudan (2022–2023). Data on provider roles, supervision, and adverse events were collected by direct observation and record review using tools adapted from WHO/ASA. Data were analyzed using Stata 17.0; risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Gezira.ResultsNPAPs independently managed 46% of cases (718/1,559) in a task-shifting manner, while performing 82% of intubations and 91% of spinal anaesthetics under task sharing. Adverse events were 2.8× more frequent under unsupervised NPAP care (34% vs. 12% supervised; RR 2.8, 95% CI 1.9–4.1), dominated by cardiovascular (58%) and airway crises (41%). Systemic failures included 94% underreporting of adverse events and 0% NPAP access to continuing education.ConclusionNPAPs sustain surgery in Sudan’s crisis but face lethal safety gaps. Urgent reforms require: (1) legislated scope-of-practice guidelines (e.g., Ethiopia’s model), (2) mobile supervision platforms (e.g., WhatsApp consults), and (3) NPAP integration into national surgical plans. These findings inform WHO strategies for conflict-affected states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13627-3.

Introduction

The provision of safe surgical and anaesthetic care is a cornerstone of any functional health system and is critical to achieving Universal Health Coverage [1]. However, profound inequities define global access, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) bearing over 90% of the surgical disease burden yet performing only a small fraction of procedures, leading to millions of preventable deaths annually [1, 2]. This crisis is driven by a fatal constellation of poverty, infrastructural deficits, and a critical shortage of skilled human resources [3], which is most acute in the specialty of anaesthesia.

The World Federation of Societies of Anaesthesiologists (WFSA) highlights devastating workforce disparities. While high-income countries may have over 20 physician anaesthesiologists per 100,000 people, many sub-Saharan African nations, including Sudan, have densities below 1 per 100,000 [4, 5]—far short of the Lancet Commission target of 20 specialist surgeons, obstetricians, and anaesthesiologists per 100,000 needed to meet basic surgical needs [1]. The consequences are cancelled surgeries, delayed obstetric care, and unacceptable anaesthesia-related morbidity and mortality [6].

In response, task sharing and task shifting have emerged as pragmatic strategies endorsed by the World Health Organization [7]. In anaesthesia, this has led to the critical role of non-physician anaesthesia providers (NPAPs)—including nurse anaesthetists and anaesthesia technologists—who form the backbone of perioperative care in many LMICs [8, 9]. Structured programs in countries like Malawi and Ethiopia demonstrate that with robust training, clear scopes of practice, and adequate supervision, NPAPs can safely manage most essential surgeries, significantly expanding access [10, 11]. However, this success depends on a functioning health system capable of providing regulation and support—elements systematically dismantled in fragile and conflict-affected states.

The Republic of Sudan presents a critical case study of these compounded failures. Even before the 2023 conflict, Sudan’s health sector was strained, with a physician anaesthesiologist density of approximately 0.47 per 100,000, forcing heavy reliance on NPAPs [5, 12]. The development of this workforce has been evolutionary. An initial model trained “anaesthesia assistants” through a two-year in-hospital program. However, a 1996 study in Khartoum linked the unsupervised practice of these assistants to high rates of complications and deaths, revealing the perils of task shifting without a robust regulatory framework [13]. In response, academic institutions established a more rigorous, four-year Bachelor of Science (BSc) degree program in Anaesthetic Sciences in 1998, aiming to create a cadre of “anaesthesia technologists” with enhanced clinical skills [14]. Despite these pathways, a critical failure has been the lack of national legislation to define a scope of practice, leading to professional dissatisfaction and attrition [14].

The current conflict has catastrophically shattered this already fragile system. The targeted destruction of major teaching hospitals in Khartoum has displaced an estimated 70–80% of the country’s physician anaesthesiologists [15]. Concurrently, the influx of war-wounded has massively increased surgical demand in receiving areas like Wad Medani, the capital of Gezira State. In this vacuum, NPAPs have become the de facto frontline providers of life-saving care, often operating in a profound policy and supervisory void. This scenario presents a critical research gap: while the necessity of NPAPs in conflict zones is clear, there is a stark absence of empirical data on the safety and outcomes of the care they deliver under such extreme duress.

Previous studies on anaesthesia in Sudan were limited to audits in the capital [13] and did not capture the realities of a distributed, crisis-affected system. Therefore, this study aimed to provide the first quantitative assessment of anaesthesia task sharing and shifting in conflict-affected Sudan by conducting a clinical audit of 1,559 surgical cases across four major referral hospitals in Wad Medani. The primary objective was to quantify NPAP-delivered anaesthesia, compare safety outcomes between supervised and unsupervised care, and identify critical systemic failures to inform urgent humanitarian interventions and workforce rebuilding strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional clinical audit of anaesthesia services across four tertiary referral hospitals in Wad Medani, Sudan, from November 2022 to February 2023. The facilities—Wad Medani Teaching Hospital, Wad Medani Maternity Hospital, Gezira Centre for Renal Diseases and Surgery, and the National Centre for Paediatric Surgery—were selected as the largest surgical centres in Gezira State, serving a mixed population of local residents, referred patients from conflict-free states, and displaced persons from conflict-affected regions.

Study population and sampling

The audit included a consecutive sample of all patients (n = 1,559) undergoing surgical procedures that required anaesthesia care at the four hospitals during the study period. We enrolled patients of all ages scheduled for both elective and emergency procedures. Eligible anaesthesia techniques included general anaesthesia, procedural sedation, and neuraxial anaesthesia. Spinal anaesthesia was the predominant and exclusive neuraxial technique utilized in these settings; other regional techniques were not part of routine practice and were therefore not captured. A fundamental inclusion criterion was the direct involvement of a qualified anaesthesia provider, either a physician or a non-physician. We excluded procedures conducted solely under local anaesthesia administered by the surgeon without a dedicated anaesthesia provider, as well as patients who were already intubated and ventilated prior to transfer from the intensive care unit.

Anaesthesia provider definitions

Anaesthesia providers were classified into three categories based on their educational and clinical backgrounds. Anaesthesiologists were defined as qualified medical doctors specialized in anaesthesia and intensive care. Anaesthesia Technologists were non-physician providers who had completed a four-year Bachelor of Science (BSc) degree in Anaesthetic Sciences. Anaesthesia Technicians were non-physician providers who had qualified through a two-year diploma-level training program. At the time of data collection, there were no non-specialist physicians or nurse anaesthetists in practice at the study sites.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured data collection form developed specifically for this study and adapted from internationally recognized instruments, including the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist [7], the ASA Standards for Basic Anesthetic Monitoring [16], the WFSA Anaesthesia Facility Assessment Tool (AFAT) [17], and the WHO International Classification for Patient Safety (ICPS). To ensure accuracy and mitigate the well-documented risk of under-reporting in this challenging setting, we implemented a triangulated method. Four trained research assistants, all BSc anaesthesia technologists, gathered data through a combination of direct observation of anaesthesia procedures, a thorough review of anaesthesia charts and operative logs, and structured interviews with providers conducted post-procedure to capture any missing information or events not formally documented.

The tool captured several key domains: provider characteristics, including qualification level and years of experience; task allocation data, documenting who performed the preoperative assessment, selected the anaesthesia technique, managed the airway, and conducted monitoring; outcome data focused on adverse events; and system factors, such as equipment availability and supervision mechanisms. Adverse events were defined per the WHO International Classification for Patient Safety as any unintended harm related to anaesthesia care requiring intervention. To ensure consistent identification, specific clinical criteria were applied. Cardiovascular instability was defined as systolic blood pressure falling below 90 mmHg or dropping more than 30% from baseline and requiring intervention with a fluid bolus or vasopressor, or sustained bradycardia below 50 beats per minute requiring atropine. Significant haemorrhage was classified as an estimated blood loss exceeding 500 mL in an adult, a proportional loss in a child, or any blood loss leading to hemodynamic compromise. Respiratory or airway events encompassed hypoxemia, indicated by an SpO₂ below 90% for more than 30 s, a difficult or failed intubation that required an alternative device or operator, unplanned postoperative reintubation, or a clinically significant episode of bronchospasm or laryngospasm. A drug or technique error was recorded for the administration of an incorrect drug or dose, or for a failed regional block that necessitated conversion to general anaesthesia.

This comprehensive approach ensured that for the primary study variables of provider role, supervision status, and adverse event occurrence—which were sourced from direct observation and provider confirmation—there were no missing data. However, data points reliant solely on routine charting were frequently incomplete, an issue we analyze separately. To ensure data quality, 10% of cases underwent duplicate, independent data collection, which demonstrated substantial inter-rater reliability (kappa coefficient, κ = 0.82).

The review of anaesthesia charts included an objective assessment of documentation completeness. A chart was classified as ‘complete’ only if the provider had filled in the core sections for patient demographics, anaesthesia history, surgical procedure, timings, vital signs, medications, and post-anaesthetic notes; charts with any of these sections blank were classified as incomplete. To quantify the gap between the occurrence and the documentation of adverse events, we first established the total number of events through our comprehensive data collection method and then determined the documentation rate by analyzing how many of these provider-confirmed events were explicitly recorded in the patient’s formal anaesthesia or surgical notes. The data collection form was designed with standardized dropdown lists to minimize errors, and data were entered directly into a password-protected electronic database using Google Forms, with access restricted to the research team.

To ensure clarity and consistency in data collection and analysis, key terms were explicitly defined at the outset of the study. The central distinction in our safety analysis was the presence or absence of direct supervision. “Supervised care” described cases where a non-physician anaesthesia provider (NPAP) was the primary clinician, but a physician anaesthesiologist was physically present within the operating theatre complex and available for immediate, direct intervention. In contrast, “Independent practice” (or “unsupervised care”) referred to cases where NPAPs delivered anaesthesia without a physician anaesthesiologist physically present in the theatre complex. It is critical to note that remote consultation, such as via telephone or messaging applications, was explicitly not classified as supervision for the purpose of this analysis.

These supervision models were further contextualized within the framework of task sharing and task shifting, in alignment with WHO principles. “Task sharing” was defined as a collaborative approach where NPAPs and physician anaesthesiologists worked together in the same physical space on a case. “Task shifting” was defined as the formal delegation of the responsibility for anaesthesia delivery from physician anaesthesiologists to NPAPs, who then managed the case independently, constituting the “unsupervised care” model described above.

The structured data collection tool, which incorporated these precise definitions to guide real-time observation and classification, is available as a supplementary file (Supplementary File 1).

Quality assurance

Rigorous quality assurance measures were implemented throughout the data collection process. To quantify inter-rater reliability, a random selection of 10% of cases underwent duplicate, independent data collection, which demonstrated substantial agreement with a kappa coefficient (κ) of 0.82. The research team also held weekly validation meetings with the lead investigators to review data entries, resolve discrepancies, and maintain consistency in interpretation. To objectively verify the reported system factors, on-site checks of anaesthesia equipment and medication availability were conducted and assessed against the WHO-WFSA International Standards for a Safe Practice of Anesthesia [18].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and proportions, were used to summarize case distribution, provider roles, and demographic data. The primary comparative analysis involved calculating the crude, unadjusted Risk Ratio (RR) for adverse events between unsupervised and supervised NPAP care. This was performed using a modified Poisson regression model with robust error variances to directly estimate the RR and avoid the overestimation associated with odds ratios from logistic regression. Crude RRs are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Gezira and the Gezira State Ministry of Health Ethical Committees. Patients and families were informed about the audit, and written consent was obtained where feasible. For emergency cases, a waiver of informed consent was granted by the Gezira State Ministry of Health Ethics Committee. All data were anonymized and stored on password-protected servers.

Sex/gender reporting (SAGER Compliance)

In line with SAGER guidelines, the gender distribution of the study population was recorded. However, the analysis of outcomes was not disaggregated by sex/gender.

Patient and public involvement

Non-physician anaesthesia providers (NPAPs), as integral frontline health workers, were involved in the adaptation of the data collection tool to ensure local relevance and provided input during the interpretation of findings. To disseminate the results to the community and patients, NPAPs are supporting the development of Arabic-language summaries to be shared through WhatsApp and health worker networks.

Anaesthesia workforce distribution

The anaesthesia workforce across the four hospitals comprised 99 providers, with non-physician anaesthesia providers (NPAPs) constituting the vast majority (93%). This included 78 BSc anaesthesia technologists (79%) and 15 diploma technicians (15%), supervised by only 7 physician anaesthesiologists (7%). Distribution was uneven, with the maternity hospital employing five of the seven specialists, while the paediatric surgery centre operated without any physician anaesthesiologists. Systemic failures in documentation and reporting

A profound systemic failure in clinical documentation was identified. Cross-referencing the 243 confirmed adverse events against the primary patient records revealed that only 17 events (6.9%) were formally documented in the anaesthesia charts or surgical notes. This 94% non-documentation rate indicates that the vast majority of complications remained invisible to the formal hospital system. This breakdown was further evidenced by a low rate of clinical escalation; consultation with a senior anaesthesiologist or technologist was sought in only 28% of adverse events.

Discussion

This study provides the first quantitative evidence of the critical, yet high-risk, role non-physician anaesthesia providers (NPAPs) play in sustaining surgical access during active conflict in Sudan. Our findings reveal a dual reality: NPAPs independently managed nearly half of all anaesthetics, demonstrating their indispensable role, yet this unsupervised care was associated with a 2.8-fold increase in adverse events. This elevated risk, coupled with a near-total systemic failure in documentation and reporting, paints a stark picture of patient safety in a fractured health system and underscores the urgent need for structured support frameworks tailored to conflict zones.

The distribution of anaesthesia providers and tasks across the four hospitals reveals an informal, ad-hoc system of risk-stratified task-shifting. The concentration of physician anaesthesiologists at the Maternity and Renal hospitals, which handled more complex and varied surgical cases, suggests that available supervision was strategically, if informally, reserved for patients with potentially higher acuity. In contrast, NPAPs at the Paediatric Centre and during emergency shifts across all sites operated with complete autonomy. This functional task-shifting, where NPAPs manage predictable, high-volume caseloads like spinal anaesthesia for caesarean sections to free physicians for complex cases, is a documented efficiency strategy in low-resource settings [19]. However, our data demonstrate that relying on this implicit, unregulated system is perilous. The significantly higher rate of adverse events in unsupervised practice underscores that the absence of formal protocols for determining which cases require supervision is a critical patient safety gap.

The profile of adverse events we documented offers crucial insights for targeted interventions. The predominance of cardiovascular instability (58% of events) diverges from the findings of the African Surgical Outcomes Study (ASOS), which identified respiratory complications as the most common cause of critical postoperative complications [20]. This discrepancy likely reflects the specific context of our study: a high-volume obstetric centre where spinal anaesthesia—a technique prone to hypotension—was the dominant technique, compounded by limited resuscitation capacity (e.g., scarce vasopressors). This finding challenges the routine delegation of spinal anaesthesia without robust support systems. While hemodynamic instability is a known pharmacological effect, its high incidence and poor management in our study cannot be divorced from the conflict context. This setting acts as a risk multiplier: the same physiological event becomes far more dangerous when it occurs in a vacuum of supervision (due to specialist displacement), without access to essential drugs (due to broken supply chains), and with limited capacity for resuscitation. Therefore, our data demonstrate that in fragile and conflict-affected settings, even procedures considered ‘basic’ require conflict-adapted support systems to be delivered safely.

Perhaps the most alarming finding is the near-complete breakdown of the clinical safety loop. The 94% non-documentation rate of adverse events and the low rate of consultation (28%) indicate that the vast majority of complications remain invisible to the formal health system, precluding any institutional learning or quality improvement. This failure is more severe than that reported in other post-conflict settings [21] and mirrors the “hidden mortality” often associated with fragile states. Our findings validate the critical need for simplified, conflict-resilient reporting mechanisms. The success of mobile-based data tools in Malawi [22] and SMS-based reporting in Haiti during infrastructure failures [23] provides a clear path forward. Implementing similar low-bandwidth, mobile-first systems in Sudan could make adverse event reporting feasible even amidst systemic collapse.

Our policy recommendations are therefore not merely aspirational but are grounded in evidence and precedent. First, the formalization of a scope of practice for NPAPs, modelled on the tiered licensing framework in Ethiopia [10], is non-negotiable. This would legally define the responsibilities of BSc-trained technologists and mandate physician consultation for specific high-risk procedures or emergencies, providing both clarity and legal protection. Second, supervision must be reimagined for the conflict context. With high mobile penetration in Sudan, leveraging platforms like WhatsApp for remote consultation is a practical and proven strategy; similar tele-mentoring approaches in other low-resource settings have improved safety outcomes [24]. This constitutes a form of remote supervision that could be immediately operationalized. Third, the chronic neglect of NPAPs must end. Investing in their continued professional development is essential. The successful integration of NPAP training into Rwanda’s Human Resources for Health program demonstrates how such investment builds resilient, sustainable workforce capacity [25]. For Sudan, this includes creating advanced degree pathways to foster leadership and specialization, provided these roles are formally recognized and funded.

A key consideration is whether our findings are specific to conflict or simply reflective of low-resource settings. While workforce shortages and poor documentation are ubiquitous challenges in LMICs [1, 3], the scale and simultaneity of the collapse in Sudan are unique to conflict. The bombing of tertiary hospitals, the displacement of the majority of specialists, and the destruction of medical records represent a catastrophic compression of system-level failures that intensifies the consequences of pre-existing gaps. Therefore, while the solutions we propose are relevant to many LMICs, their implementation in Sudan and similar settings is a matter of utmost urgency for crisis response and requires dedicated humanitarian funding.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Data were limited to Wad Medani’s urban centres; rural areas likely face even more severe shortages and worse outcomes. A key limitation in interpreting the risk ratio for adverse events is the potential for confounding by case mix. Our observational design means the assignment of cases to supervised or unsupervised care was not random. An informal, risk-stratified task-shifting model was likely in operation, where unsupervised NPAPs managed a higher proportion of predictable cases, while physicians were reserved for more complex procedures. This would, in theory, bias our results towards the null. The fact that we still found a strong, significant association suggests the effect of unsupervised practice is potent. Unfortunately, robust risk-adjustment using standardized acuity scores was not feasible due to fragmented pre-operative records and the absence of such scoring systems in this conflict-affected setting. Therefore, the reported risk ratio should be interpreted as a measure of the composite risk inherent in the current system of unsupervised practice, which includes both the intrinsic risk of the procedures and the potential risks associated with the absence of a specialist. We were unable to analyse mortality data due to record fragmentation, a common constraint in conflict research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that NPAPs are the bedrock of surgical care in conflict-ravaged Sudan, but they are operating in a system that sets them up to fail. The high rate of adverse events in unsupervised practice is a call to action, not a condemnation of NPAPs. The path forward requires immediate, evidence-informed measures: formalizing their role, enabling remote supervision through technology, and integrating them into the core of national surgical plans and humanitarian response strategies. As NPAPs become a permanent fixture in fragile health systems worldwide, their structured support, oversight, and professional development are not just ethical imperatives but practical necessities for achieving safe surgical care for all.
