Thiostrepton induces spindle abnormalities and enhances Taxol cytotoxicity in MDA-MB-231 cells
Authors: Hsiao-Hui Kuo, Jhong-Syuan Yao, Ling-Huei Yih
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThiostrepton (TST) is a known inhibitor of the transcription factor Forkhead box M1 (FoxM1) and inducer of heat shock response (HSR) and autophagy. TST thus may be one potential candidate of anticancer drugs for combination chemotherapy.Methods and ResultsImmunofluorescence staining of mitotic spindles and flow cytometry analysis revealed that TST induces mitotic spindle abnormalities, mitotic arrest, and apoptotic cell death in the MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cell line. Interestingly, overexpression or depletion of FoxM1 in MDA-MB-231 cells did not affect TST induction of spindle abnormalities; however, TST-induced spindle defects were enhanced by inhibition of HSP70 or autophagy. Moreover, TST exhibited low affinity for tubulin and only slightly inhibited in vitro tubulin polymerization, but it severely impeded tubulin polymerization and destabilized microtubules in arrested mitotic MDA-MB-231 cells. Additionally, TST significantly enhanced Taxol cytotoxicity. TST also caused cytotoxicity and spindle abnormalities in a Taxol-resistant cell line, MDA-MB-231-T4R.ConclusionsThese results suggest that, in addition to inhibiting FoxM1, TST may induce proteotoxicity and autophagy to disrupt cellular tubulin polymerization, and this mechanism might account for its antimitotic effects, enhancement of Taxol anticancer effects, and ability to overcome Taxol resistance in MDA-MB-231 cells. These data further imply that TST may be useful to improve the therapeutic efficacy of Taxol.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11033-024-09863-1.Keywords: Cell death, Mitotic spindle assembly, Taxol, Thiostrepton, Tubulin polymerization

Introduction

Thiostrepton (TST), a natural antibiotic produced by bacteria of Streptomyces genus, is a FoxM1 inhibitor [1, 2]. Expression of FoxM1 increases during S phase and peaks at G2-M transition, and the protein is phosphorylated during mitosis [3]. These processes contribute to tight spatiotemporal control of FoxM1, which transcriptionally activates a G2/M-specific gene network involving cyclin B1, CDC20, aurora kinase A (AURKA), polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1), multiple kinesins, and centrosome components [4]. FoxM1 is overexpressed in many cancers [5] and promotes several tumorigenic and malignant processes [6]. Since loss of FoxM1 has been shown to cause centrosome amplification and mitotic catastrophe [7], inhibition of its transcription factor function is one potential strategy to disrupt centrosomes and block the cell cycle at G2-M stages. In addition to inducing cancer cell death via downregulation of FoxM1 expression [8], TST is also a protein translation inhibitor [9] and causes cytotoxicity through induction of oxidative stress, heat shock response (HSR) [10], and autophagy [11]. Importantly, TST has been shown to reverse drug resistance in some cancer cell types [12, 13]. As such, TST may be a useful component for chemotherapy.

Breast cancer is one of the most common cancers in women worldwide. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for about 10–15% of all breast cancers and is associated with a poor prognosis, including high rates of recurrence and distant metastasis [14]. Taxol is one of the first-line anticancer drugs given to TNBC patients. However, metastatic disease frequently develops in patients with treatment failure [15]. Taxol targets tubulin to promote polymerization and stabilization of microtubules in proliferating cells, resulting in the disruption of mitotic spindle assembly, mitotic arrest, and cell death [16]. The ability of Taxol to induce catastrophic multipolar mitosis is fundamental to its therapeutic effect in cancer treatment [17]. However, tumor cells with supernumerary centrosomes can perform centrosome clustering to induce the assembly of pseudo-bipolar spindles and facilitate cell division and survival, and thus may be refractory to Taxol treatment [18]. In addition, Taxol resistance and toxic side effects often develop in patients receiving Taxol-based treatments, greatly diminishing the drug efficacy. Thus, drugs that can induce spindle abnormalities to prevent centrosome clustering, augment Taxol-induced antimitotic effects, or overcome Taxol resistance may be highly desirable as cancer treatments. TST may be one potential candidate of such anticancer drugs for combination chemotherapy.

MDA-MB-231 is a TNBC cell line in which half of the cell population carries extra centrosomes [19]. These extra centrosomes tend to cluster into pseudo-bipolar spindles during mitosis, which facilitates cell division and survival [20] and likely allows the cells to evade toxicity from anti-mitosis drugs [21]. Therefore, MDA-MB-231 was used in this study to explore whether TST can interfere with spindle assembly and the effects of TST on the antimitotic activities of Taxol. Our results show that TST disrupts microtubule polymerization in arrested mitotic MDA-MB-231 cells. TST also induces disarrangement of mitotic spindles and decreases the assembly of pseudo-bipolar spindles. Finally, our data demonstrate that TST can enhance the anti-mitotic effects of Taxol and overcome Taxol resistance.

Results

TST induces spindle abnormalities, mitotic arrest, and apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 cells

We first conducted the trypan blue exclusion assay to evaluate the effect of TST on MDA-MB-231 cell viability. Treatment of cells with TST for 72 h dose-dependently reduced viability (Fig. 1A). The IC50 of TST was 1.19 μM calculated using the GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2). The untransformed breast epithelial cell line MCF10A was used as a control. The results showed that TST at the dose range applied had little effects on MCF10A cells (Fig. 1A). To gain insights into how TST might induce death in MDA-MB-231 cells, TST was treated for 24, 52, or 72 h, and its effects on mitotic spindles, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis induction were examined. Of note, it was previously reported that about half of all MDA-MB-231 cells contain extra centrosomes [19]. Our data showed that in untreated MDA-MB-231 cells, about 60% of mitotic cells displayed normal bipolar spindles, 20% showed a bipolar-like structure but with mis-aligned or lagged chromosomes (Fig. 1B-ii arrow head and 1C), and only 20% had multipolar spindles (Fig. 1B-iii and C). The 20% of mitotic cells that displayed a bipolar-like structure but with mis-aligned or lagged chromosomes may reflect centrosome clustering in MDA-MB-231 cells. This result is consistent with the notion that MDA-MB-231 cells tend to cluster extra centrosomes into pseudo-bipolar spindles to facilitate cell division [20]. Treatment with TST for 24 h induced a significant increase in spindle disarrangement (Fig. 1B-iv and C), causing a reduction in the percentages of cells with bipolar and multipolar spindles and bipolar-like spindles with misaligned chromosomes. In the mitotic cell with disarranged spindle, the spindle poles came together in a very close distance but not in monopolar configuration with chromosomes scattered in the cytoplasm and the microtubules were disorganized with many nodes or clusters entangled but not in a radial array (Fig. 1B-iv). This result implies that TST may disturb the function of mitotic microtubules, disrupt spindle assembly, and prevent the formation of bipolar-like pseudo-bipolar spindles in MDA-MB-231 cells. In addition, cell cycle analysis revealed that 24-h TST treatment induced a dose-dependent decrease in G1 cells and accumulation of both G2 and mitotic cells (Fig. 1D), suggesting that TST blocks cell cycle progression at the G2 and mitotic stages. Flow cytometry analysis of cells treated with TST for 52 or 72 h revealed that the proportion of cleaved PARP-positive cells was also significantly increased (Fig. 1E), indicating that TST induces apoptotic cell death in addition to G2 and mitotic arrest. Together, the results suggest that TST may disrupt spindle assembly in MDA-MB-231 cells, preventing the assembly of pseudo-bipolar spindles, inducing mitotic arrest, and subsequently causing apoptotic cell death.

TST induces antimitotic effects independent of FoxM1

Since TST is known to be a FoxM1 inhibitor [2] and FoxM1 is a transcription factor that upregulates itself and G2/M regulators such as cyclin B1 and PLK1 [22], we next examined whether TST could alter the protein levels of FoxM1 and FoxM1-upregulated proteins to disrupt spindle assembly in MDA-MB-231 cells. Our results showed that the protein level of FoxM1 was not significantly changed after 24 h of TST treatment (Fig. 2A left panel and bFoxM1 in right plot), but the protein showed a super shift in molecular weight at concentrations higher than 1 µM (Fig. 2A, arrow head in left panel and sFoxM1 in right plot). Levels of cyclin B1 and PLK1 also were not significantly reduced at 24 h after treatment was initiated (Fig. 2A left panel and right plot). However, the level of phospho-histone H3, a mitosis marker, was significantly increased at 24 h of TST treatment (Fig. 2A). Thus, mitotic arrest appears to have been induced by TST at 24 h-treatment without significant downregulation of FoxM1, cyclin B1 and PLK1 protein levels.

To verify whether FoxM1 inhibition mediates the antimitotic effects of TST, MDA-MB-231 cells were either made to overexpress or depleted of FoxM1 before treatment with TST. Figure 2B shows the expression efficiency of two FoxM1constructs overexpressed in MDA-MB-231 cells. Despite the different expression levels for the ectopic constructs in the cells, TST induced similar extents of spindle defects (Fig. 2C) and mitotic arrest (Fig. 2D) at 24 h, apoptosis at 50 h (Fig. 2E), and cell death at 72 h (Fig. 2F) compared to control cells harboring empty vector.

MDA-MB-231 cells were also depleted of FoxM1 with shRNA-containing virion. Figure 3A shows the depletion efficiency of cellular FoxM1 with two different shRNAs and a combination of both. Depletion of FoxM1 decreased cell viability to 70% in TST-untreated MDA-MB-231 cells, but the cell viability was not further decreased in cells treated with 2 µM TST (Fig. 3B). This result suggests that FoxM1 is required for MDA-MB-231 cell viability and that 2 μM TST-induced cell death might not be or only partially mediated by FoxM1 inhibition. Depletion of FoxM1 had no effect on the induction of spindle defects in cells that were untreated or treated with 1 µM TST (Fig. 3C) compared to cells transduced with empty-vector-containing virions. Together, these results imply that FoxM1 might not be involved in TST induction of spindle defects in MDA-MB-231 cells.

Inhibition of HSR or autophagy enhances TST-induced spindle defects

TST is a reported inducer of both HSR [10] and autophagy [23]. To determine whether HSR or autophagy is involved in TST induction of antimitotic effects, the cell responses to TST treatment at various concentrations for 6, 24, and 52 h were analyzed in MDA-MB-231 cells by immunoblotting. Figure 4A shows that the basal FoxM1 protein level was considerably decreased at 52 h TST treatment (Fig. 4A, upper and lower right panel), indicating TST may inhibit FoxM1 only after at least 24 h treatment. But spindle defects and mitotic arrest were already induced by TST at 24 h (Fig. 1). Our results also showed that the inducible heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), a protein chaperone upregulated by proteotoxic stress, was considerably induced after 6 h TST treatment (Fig. 4A, upper and lower left panel), suggesting that TST causes proteotoxicity at time-points as early as 6 h. The autophagy marker SQSTM1 was decreased slightly at 6 h treatment and then increased at 24 and 52 h (Fig. 4A, upper and lower, middle and right panel). The level of LC3-II was not substantially changed at 6 h and 24 treatment but it was clearly elevated at 52 h (Fig. 4A, upper and lower right panel). Since the simultaneous accumulation of SQSTM1 and LC3-II indicates blockage of autophagy flux [24], the results thus imply that autophagy may be impaired by TST treatment in MDA-MB-231. The involvement of HSR and autophagy in TST-induced spindle defects were then examined by co-treating the cells with TST and chloroquine or bafilomycin A1 (CQ or Baf. A1, autophagy inhibitors), or PES (an HSP70 inhibitor). The results (Fig. 4B) showed that treatment with CQ or Baf. A1 alone did not affect the pattern of spindle abnormalities induced in MDA-MB-231 cells. PES significantly increased the proportion of cells with disarranged spindles. Co-treatment of each inhibitor with TST greatly enhanced the proportions of TST-induced disarranged spindles (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that TST may induce proteotoxic and/or autophagy-related mitotic damage, and therefore, inhibition of HSR or autophagy enhances the antimitotic effects of TST.

TST impairs tubulin polymerization and induces microtubule destabilization in arrested mitotic cells

Since we found that TST induces spindle abnormalities in MDA-MB-231 cells, we next examined whether TST exerts this effect by altering tubulin polymerization. We first tested whether TST might associate with tubulin using an assay to look for quenching of intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence of tubulin protein. The result showed that tubulin intrinsic fluorescence was only significantly reduced in the presence of 125 µM TST and it was not substantially changed by TST concentrations of 50 µM or less (Fig. 5A). We then examined whether TST could alter tubulin polymerization in vitro. Figure 5B shows that Taxol (a tubulin stabilizing drug) strongly enhanced and vinblastine (a tubulin depolymerizing drug) diminished both the rate and extent of tubulin polymerization. However, by comparison, TST showed very little effect on tubulin polymerization. From these in vitro studies, we therefore concluded that TST does not likely bind to tubulin directly, and it does not or only slightly affects in vitro tubulin polymerization.

A cellular tubulin polymerization assay [25] was also performed to elucidate the effects of TST on spindle assembly in MDA-MB-231 cells. Due to the intrinsic dynamic nature in early mitosis, the mitotic spindle tubulins are generally tyrosinated (non-modified) and acetylated. When spindle microtubules become gradually stabilized due to the establishment of chromosome attachments at the kinetochore at metaphase, they become increasingly detyrosinated and acetylated [26]. As shown in Fig. 5C, tubulin extracted from non-synchronized cycling cells almost entirely existed in soluble monomer form and were not acetylated but highly detyrosinated. While only 31% of tubulin from mitotic cells collected after Ro-3306 block and release was monomer; the remaining 69% was polymerized and were acetylated and detyrosinated, indicating that the cells may be in metaphase with microtubules stably attached to chromosomes. However, two-thirds of the tubulins in TST-arrested mitotic cells were in the soluble monomer form and were only slightly acetylated and not detyrosinated. The remaining one-thirds of tubulins in the polymerized form though were acetylated but not detyrosinated. These results indicated that the spindle microtubules in TST-arrested mitotic cells were less stable and might not establish a stabilized attachment to chromosomes. The tubulins extracted from vinblastine-arrested mitotic cells were all in soluble monomer form and not acetylated but were detyrosinated. Tubulin extracted from Taxol-arrested mitotic cells was primarily polymerized and highly acetylated and detyrosinated. Therefore, the assay faithfully reflected Taxol-mediated stabilization and vinblastine-mediated destabilization of tubulin polymerization, and the results suggested that TST may inhibit and destabilize cellular tubulin polymerization in mitotic MDA-MB-231 cells.

Stringent regulation of microtubule dynamics is critical for the accurate assembly of mitotic spindles [27]. Therefore, we assessed whether TST affects microtubule dynamics by applying cold treatment, which causes disassembly of the cold-sensitive spindle pole microtubule (pole-MT) but preserves the cold-resistant kinetochore-microtubule (k-MT) [28]. Untreated and TST-treated MDA-MB-231cells were subjected to cold treatment. In TST-untreated mitotic cells, 10-min cold treatment induced disassembly of the pole-MT, resulting in nearly 75% of the mitotic cells retaining the dense k-MT (Fig. 5D-ii). Exposures to 1, 2, and 5 µM TST for 24 h before cold treatment significantly decreased the portions of mitotic cells with dense k-MT to 69, 63, and 59%, respectively (Fig. 5E). TST treatment also resulted in cells containing a more loose and thin k-MT (Fig. 5D-iii), indicating that the k-MTs in TST-arrested mitotic cells were less stable and thus more sensitive to cold treatment.

TST enhances Taxol cytotoxicity and overcomes Taxol resistance in MDA-MB-231 cells

Toxic side effects and drug resistance are the two most important factors limiting the efficacy of Taxol. We therefore explored whether TST could benefit Taxol-based therapy by enhancing its toxicity. Figure 6A shows that Taxol treatment alone considerably reduced colony formation by the cells, and this reduction was further decreased by simultaneous treatment with TST. Treatment of MDA-MB-231 cells with 1 or 2 nM Taxol for 24 h induced a significant increase in the percentage of mitotic cells with multipolar spindles (Fig. 6B, dark grey bar) and a decrease in cells with normal bipolar spindles (Fig. 6B, white bar). On the other hand, TST treatment alone induced disarranged spindles in a large proportion of cells (Fig. 6B, slashed bar), in agreement with our earlier experiments (Fig. 1B-iv). Strikingly, co-treatment of cells with Taxol and 1 µM TST resulted in a significant decrease in the percentage of mitotic cells with normal bipolar spindles (Fig. 6B). In addition, the combined treatment caused an increase in the sum of percentages of mitotic cells with multipolar spindles and disarranged spindles. Both types of spindle defects might obstruct cell division and lead to cell death.

We also examined the effects of TST on a Taxol-resistant cell line, MDA-MB-231-T4R. The results showed that MDA-MB-231-T4R cells were more capable of forming colonies after 10 nM Taxol treatment than the parental controls (Fig. 6C). However, the parental and Taxol-resistant cell lines were equally sensitive to TST-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 6C). In addition, 10 nM Taxol treatment induced multipolar spindles in 80% of mitotic cells in parental MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 6D, left panel) but only 30% of mitotic cells in resistant T4R cells (Fig. 6D, right panel). Still, TST induced spindle defects, especially in the form of disarranged spindles, to similar extents in both parental and resistant cells (Fig. 6D, slashed bars). These results indicate that TST can overcome Taxol resistance.

Discussion

Induction of catastrophic mitosis and subsequent cell death is a major mechanism of action for Taxol in cancer treatment [29]. However, cancer cells that can assemble pseudo-bipolar spindles may be refractory to Taxol treatment [30]. In addition, treatment of patients with Taxol is associated with many toxic side effects as well as the development of drug resistance. Thus, drugs that can prevent the assembly of pseudo-bipolar spindles, augment Taxol-induced antimitotic effects, and overcome Taxol resistance may be highly advantageous for cancer treatment. In this study, we provide evidence that TST induces the formation of disarranged spindles and hence might prevent the assembly of bipolar-like pseudo-bipolar spindles in MDA-MB-231 cells, a breast cancer cell line with the ability to cluster extra centrosomes and form bipolar-like spindles during mitosis. Our data suggest that the antimitotic effect of TST may be related to its ability to inhibit tubulin polymerization and destabilize microtubules in arrested mitotic MDA-MB-231 cells. Most importantly, TST significantly enhanced the in vitro cell killing and antimitotic effect of Taxol in MDA-MB-231 cells and it effectively killed Taxol-resistant cells.

The presence of supernumerary centrosomes is a hallmark of human cancers [30]. Extra centrosomes in cells are tolerated and can promote tumorigenesis through the induction of chromosome mis-segregation, which is associated with chromosome instability, disruption of tissue architecture, and cell invasion [31]. In contrast, centrosome amplification is poorly tolerated in normal cells because of its catastrophic consequences on cell division; cells with extra centrosomes must overcome serious obstacles to efficiently proliferate [32]. To adapt and survive, cells may cluster extra centrosomes into two poles and assemble a bipolar-like spindle during mitosis, hence allowing for chromosome segregation to complete cell division with little or no chromosomes mis-segregation [33]. MDA-MB-231 is a breast cancer cell line with a large proportion of cells containing extra centrosomes (> 50%); these cells are able to efficiently cluster the extra centrosomes into two poles [20]. Preventing the assembly of bipolar-like spindles should selectively kill cancer cells, and multiple studies have highlighted the importance of microtubule dynamics for efficient centrosome clustering [18]. Previous studies revealed that cells exhibiting amplified centrosomes may be treated with griseofulvin [34] or bromonoscapine [35], drugs that gently disrupt microtubule dynamics and/or induce microtubule depolymerization, to induce declustering of supernumerary centrosomes. In the present study, we show that treatment of MDA-MB-231 cells with TST disrupts spindle structures and may prevent the assembly of pseudo-bipolar spindle and impede cell division. Our results showed that TST can inhibit tubulin polymerization and destabilize microtubules in mitotic-arrested MDA-MB-231 cells. Thus, TST may impede microtubule dynamics involved in centrosome clustering to induce the abnormal spindles and subsequent catastrophic mitosis and cell death.

FoxM1 is associated with cancer progression and mediates Taxol resistance in several cancer cell lines through different mechanisms [12, 36, 37]. Furthermore, TST has been shown to induce cancer cell death through inhibition of FoxM1 [8]. Our results showed that TST treatment significantly reduced FoxM1 protein level at 52 h, suggesting that TST might inhibit the transactivation activity of FoxM1 in MDA-MB-231 cells only after treatment for at least 24 h. However, spindle abnormalities and mitotic arrest were already induced by TST at 24 h treatment. Overexpression or depletion of FoxM1 did not affect TST antimitotic effects in MDA-MB-231 cells. It is most likely that inhibition of FoxM1 transactivation activity does not mediate TST-induced spindle defects. Additionally, inhibition of HSR or autophagy enhanced TST induction of disarranged abnormal spindles. TST has been demonstrated to induce proteotoxic stress, as evidenced by upregulation of several heat shock proteins and subsequent apoptotic cell death [10, 38]. TST has also been demonstrated to induce autophagy [11, 23]. Thus, in addition to inhibiting FoxM1, TST-induced damage that precedes induction of HSR and autophagy may trigger its antimitotic effects. Further studies will be required to fully elucidate the underlying mechanism.

Our cellular tubulin polymerization assay showed that tubulin polymerization was impaired in TST-arrested mitotic cells. However, the in vitro tubulin binding and polymerization assays revealed that TST probably does not directly bind to or interact with tubulin to inhibit polymerization. Our results suggested that in TST-treated MDA-MB-231 cells, HSR and autophagy may be induced prior to spindle defects and mitotic arrest. Importantly, inhibition of HSP70 or autophagy could significantly enhance TST induction of the abnormal spindles. These results suggest that proteotoxocity and autophagy are critical factors in TST induction of spindle defects. Autophagy suppresses tumor initiation but is also required to support uncontrolled cell growth and increased metabolic activities in established tumors [39]. Thus, there is an autophagy dependency for tumor maintenance. Our results therefore showed that inhibition of autophagy with small molecules could potentiate TST effects in MDA-MB-231 cancer cells. In addition, autophagy is an important pathway for maintenance of centrosome integrity, while autophagy deficiency results in dysregulation of centrosome composition and leads to mitotic centrosome fragmentation [40]. Depletion or inhibition of HSP70 impairs the function of mitotic centrosome and induces spindle defects [41, 42]. Hence, the concomitant induction of proteotoxicity and improper autophagy by TST may contribute to its antimitotic effects. It appears that the TST-induced spindle defects and its subsequent inhibition of FoxM1 transactivation function may act collectively on parental and Taxol-resistant MDA-MB-231-T4R cells to cause cell death. Thus, our data suggest that TST may be useful in combination with Taxol-based chemotherapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and chemicals

MDA-MB-231 and MCF10A cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and maintained as previously described [43]. A Taxol-resistant cell line, MDA-MB-231-T4R, was established as described [43]. MCF10A were culture in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and cholera toxin (100 ng/ml) at 37 °C with 10% CO2 and 90% air. Small molecules used in this study include bafilomycin A1 (Baf. A, Sigma, Saint Louis, MO, USA), chloroquine (CQ, Sigma), pifithrin-μ (PES, Tocris Bioscience, Bistro, UK), TST (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), Taxol (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), vinblastine (Sigma), and CDK1 inhibitor Ro-3306 (Cayman Chemical). To enrich for mitotic cells, the cell cycle was blocked in MDA-MB-231 cells by a 14-h treatment of 5 µM Ro-3306, followed by PBS wash and 30 min incubation in drug-free medium to release the block and allow mitotic entry [44]. The mitotic cells then were shaken off the culture plates and collected. Cell viability was analyzed by the trypan blue exclusion assay [43]. Cytotoxicity was analyzed by the colony formation assay [43] or detection of the apoptosis marker, cleaved-PARP (c-PARP)-positive cells [43], with a specific antibody (#9541, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) by flow cytometry.

Overexpression of FoxM1

Retroviral plasmids encoding wild-type FoxM1b (NM_202003.2) and FoxM1c (NM_021953.4) were prepared as described [43]. The corresponding cDNA sequences were verified and confirmed by DNA sequencing. MDA-MB-231 cells were transduced with virion-containing supernatant in growth medium supplemented with 2 μg/ml DEAE-dextran. At 24 h post-transduction, 1 mg/ml G418 was added to culture medium to select for stable clones. Alternatively, cells were subjected to analyses after transduction for at least two days.

Depletion of cellular FoxM1

Depletion of FoxM1 was achieved by transduction of MDA-MB-231 cells with VSV-G-pseudotyped lentivirus-based short hairpin RNA (shRNA), as previously described [43]. The shRNAs targeting FoxM1, shFoxM1-1 (TRCN-273982) and shFoxM1-2 (TRCN-273984), were purchased from the National RNAi Core Facility (Genomic Research Center, Academia Sinica). Cells were transduced with pLKO.1- or shRNA-containing supernatant in growth medium supplemented with 10 μg/ml polybrene. At 24 h post-transduction, 2 μg/ml puromycin was added to culture medium to select for stable clones. Alternatively, cells were subjected to analyses after transduction for at least two days.

Analysis of cell cycle progression and mitotic index

Cell cycle progression was monitored using DNA flow cytometry analysis of propidium iodide-staining cells and the percentage of cells in mitosis was measured by flow cytometry analysis of phospho-histone H3 (p-H3, the mitosis marker)-positive cells as previously described [43]. In brief, 3 × 105 cells were fixed with ice-cold 70% ethanol for 16 h, then permeabilized with 0.25% triton X-100 (15 min, 4℃) and immunostained with mouse antihuman phospho-histone H3 (1: 200 dilution, #9706, H3 phosphorylated on serine 10 (p-H3); Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA) for 3 h at room temperature in the dark, followed by incubation with allophycocyanin conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1: 3000 dilution, A-865, Invitrogen) for 0.5 h at room temperature in the dark. Cellular DNA was counterstained with 20 μg/ml propidium iodide. Levels of p-H3 and propidium iodide of individual cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Attune NxT, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the cell cycle distribution and the percentage of cells with p-H3 were determined using a computer program provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Immunofluorescence staining and analysis of mitotic spindles abnormalities

Cells seeded on glass coverslips were fixed, immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described [41]. Briefly, 2 × 105 cells seeded on coverslips were rinsed with PBS and then fixed and permeabilized in PTEMF buffer (20 mM PIPES pH6.8, 0.2% (w/v) triton X-100, 10 mM EDTA, 1 mM MgCl2, and 4% formaldehyde) for 15 min at room temperature. The cells were then stained with immunofluorescent antibodies. The primary antibodies used for staining included anti-α-tubulin (1:100 dilution, T5168, Sigma or GTX112141, GeneTex) and anti-γ-tubulin (1:100 dilution, T6557 or T3559, Sigma). Alexa-Fluor 488- or 633-conjugated goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG (1:200 dilution) were purchased from Invitrogen (A-11001, A-11008, A-21053, and A-21072, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All antibodies reactions were conducted in room temperature with full humidity in the dark for 1 h. Confocal images of the immunostained samples were obtained under a confocal microscope (Leica TCS-SP5, Mannheim, Germany). The numbers of cells with indicated spindle types were counted using a Zeiss Axioplan 2 Imaging MOT fluorescence microscope. Mitotic spindles at prophases, prometaphases, and metaphases were counted as normal spindles. Multipolar spindle, monopolar spindle, disarranged spindle, and bipolar spindle with misaligned chromosomes or with abnormal microtubule array were judged as mitotic spindle abnormalities. At least 300 mitotic cells were analyzed for mitotic spindles. The level of spindle abnormalities is expressed as the percentage of the mitotic cells containing abnormal spindle.

Immunoblotting

Cells lysing with SDS-PAGE sample buffer and immunoblotting were carried out as described [41]. Specific proteins were detected using antibodies against cyclin B1 (1:1000, sc-245, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Dallas, Texas), FoxM1 (1:1000, #5436, Cell Signaling Technology), HSP70 (1:1000, GTX104126, GeneTex), histone H3 phosphorylated at S10 (1:1000, #9701, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), LC3 (1:1000, #2775, Cell Signaling Technology), PLK1 (1:1000, A300-251A, Invitrogen), and SQSTM1 (1:1000, #8025, Cell Signaling Technology). GAPDH or PCNA were detected with anti-GAPDH (1:5000, GTX100118, Genetex) or anti-PCNA (1:2000, sc-56, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), respectively, as loading controls. Primary antibodies were reacted at 4℃ overnight. After thorough rinsing with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% tween), secondary antibodies were applied and reacted for 1 h at room temperature (1:2000). Secondary antibodies used were horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, 074–1516, SeraCare KPL, MA) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:2000, AP124P, Merck, Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Proteins bands were visualized by chemiluminescent detection (WBLUC0500, Merck, Millipore). Quantification of proteins bands were measured using GeneTools software (Syngene, Cambridge CB4 1TF, UK).

Analysis of TST association with tubulin and in vitro tubulin polymerization assay

The association of TST with tubulin was studied by monitoring the intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence of tubulin upon binding with the drug, as described previously [25]. When excited at 295 nm, tubulin displays a typical tryptophan emission spectrum with a maximum at 355 nm. Association of the drug to tubulin reduces the fluorescence emission.

A tubulin polymerization assay kit BK004P (Cytoskeleton Inc., Denver, CO, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions to examine the effects of mdivi-1 on in vitro tubulin polymerization as previously described [43].

Cellular microtubule stabilization assay

The effect of TST on cellular tubulin polymerization in MDA-MB-231 cells was assessed using the cellular microtubule stabilization assay [25]. After treatment, cells were harvested in lysis buffer (100 mm PIPES pH 6.9, 1 mm EGTA, 1 mm MgCl2, 30% glycerol, 0.5% DMSO, 1% NP-40, 1 mm GTP, and protease inhibitors), and the polymerized tubulin fraction (pellet) and the monomer tubulin fraction (supernatant) were separated by centrifugation at 180,000 × g at 37 °C for 1 h. The two fractions were adjusted to the same volume with Laemmli sample buffer. Then, the amounts of α-tubulin from equal aliquots of the polymerized tubulin fraction (P) and the monomer tubulin fraction (S) were determined by immunoblot analyses.

Microtubule cold treatment assay

The effect of TST on the stability of microtubules was examined with a cold treatment assay [45]. MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with TST as indicated; then, the cells were subjected to 10-min cold treatment, followed by immunostaining for mitotic spindles, as described previously [41]. Mitotic cells containing dense kinetochore microtubule fibers (k-MT) were counted. A decrease of k-MT-containing cell number indicates sensitization of the k-MT to cold treatment, owing to decreased stability of the k-MT.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times. The results are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The treated cell populations were compared with vehicle controls in each experiment. The differences between groups were analyzed by Student’s t-test or by one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) and Šídák’s multiple comparison test using GraphPad Prism 10.2. Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05.
