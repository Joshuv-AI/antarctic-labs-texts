Human-in-the-loop validation of a sequential multi-LLM medical education pipeline
Authors: Yoojin Nam, Taein An, Sung Il Hwang, Chawoong Jeon, Ji Won Jeong, Juhyun Jeong, Dong Yeong Kim, Pyeong Hwa Kim, So Yeon Kim, Sungkwan Kim, Yangwon Kim, Kwang Hwi Lee, Taehee Lee, Hee Sang Oh, Joon Hyung Park, Seunghyeon Shin, Yongsik Sim, Jeong Min Song, Seunghyun Song, Pa Hong, Namkug Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge language models (LLMs) can generate medical educational content at scale, but multi-dimensional human validation of multi-LLM pipelines has not been performed at scale. We evaluated a 7-stage sequential multi-LLM pipeline implemented exclusively with the Gemini family (Gemini 3 Flash and Pro for text; Gemini 3 Pro Image, the Nano Banana Pro preview, for images), producing 6000 flashcards and 833 infographics for radiology board preparation. Nine residents and eleven attending radiologists evaluated 1284 flashcards across 11 subspecialties in a two-phase design around fifth-stage (S5) feedback. Among 1100 evaluations of 980 text-level PASS cards, the evaluation-level false-negative rate (FNR) for blocking errors was 1.00% (95% CI 0.50–1.78%), exceeding the pre-specified 0.3% safety threshold; card-level (1.12%) and majority-vote (0.82%) analyses converged. S5 feedback was associated with increased blocking-error flags (39–54 evaluation-level observations, McNemar p = 0.003) and decreased technical accuracy and educational quality scores (both p < 0.001). In unadjusted analyses, attending radiologists identified more errors than residents (odds ratio 4.52, 95% CI 2.38–8.57), although workload-matched sensitivity analyses attenuated this gap. A preliminary image pilot of 19 items showed critical errors in both tables and infographics, with between-format comparisons underpowered and not significant after Holm correction; this image-related observation should be interpreted as exploratory. The conditional safety estimates, low-reliability reference standard, and rater- and workload-dependent patterns do not support fully automated deployment; automated feedback was associated with sharpened evaluator scrutiny, though the single-arm design cannot isolate useful cueing from anchoring or re-review effects.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are one component of a broader integration of artificial intelligence across the medical education continuum, ranging from clinical reasoning support to multi-domain assessment and content generation1. Within this landscape, LLMs are increasingly used to generate medical educational materials at scale. Applications include board-style question banks, clinical vignettes, flashcards, and summary tables2–5. A single LLM can produce thousands of items in hours, a volume that would take a faculty team months to author manually6,7. This capacity is particularly appealing in radiology, where board examination preparation demands coverage of over a dozen subspecialties, each with rapidly evolving diagnostic criteria8–10. Yet medical education is safety-critical. An error embedded in a flashcard (a misattributed imaging finding, an incorrect differential diagnosis) does not merely reduce learning efficiency. It can propagate through a trainee’s clinical reasoning and ultimately affect patient care11,12. The central tension, then, is straightforward: automated content generation offers scale far exceeding manual authoring, but without equally scalable quality assurance (QA), that scale becomes a liability.

Several approaches to automated QA have emerged. Rule-based filters catch formatting errors and known factual contradictions11. LLM self-evaluation, in which the same model scores its own output, provides a fast but often overconfident check13,14. More recently, systems employing multiple LLMs in sequence, where one model generates content and another reviews it, have gained traction15–17. These architectures are sometimes labeled “multi-agent systems,” but the term deserves scrutiny. True multi-agent systems involve bidirectional feedback loops and cooperative decision-making between autonomous agents18–20. Most reported LLM-based QA systems, including our own, are better described as sequential multi-LLM pipelines: each stage processes the output of the previous stage in a fixed, unidirectional flow without inter-stage negotiation or iterative refinement21. This distinction matters because it sets realistic expectations for what such pipelines can and cannot catch. A sequential pipeline may reduce certain error classes, but it cannot self-correct through dialog. The more pressing limitation, however, is empirical. Few studies have conducted large-scale human validation of LLM-generated educational content. Most published evaluations involve tens of items reviewed by a handful of experts, or they rely on automated metrics (BLEU scores, LLM-as-judge ratings) without human ground truth22–24.

Quality in AI-generated educational materials is not unidimensional. Text accuracy alone is insufficient: an anatomically labeled image may contain a misplaced arrow or an incorrect structure even when the accompanying text is flawless25–27. Indeed, MLLMs markedly underperform radiologists in image interpretation tasks and exhibit hallucinated findings even when reaching correct diagnoses28,29. The expertise level of evaluators also shapes the assessment. Residents and attending physicians may apply different standards, with attending physicians identifying subtle domain-specific errors that residents miss, while residents flag usability issues that specialists overlook30,31. To our knowledge, no study has combined text-level QA, image-level QA, and multi-level expert review for a single-LLM content generation pipeline at this scale.

We developed a 7-stage sequential multi-LLM pipeline that generated 6000 radiology board examination flashcards and 833 table infographics across 11 subspecialties. We aimed to evaluate the safety and quality of this pipeline’s output through multi-dimensional human review, comparing assessments between resident and attending physicians. We hypothesized that the pipeline’s built-in QA stages would reduce error rates to a pre-specified educational safety threshold but that human oversight would remain essential for detecting residual errors, particularly in image content and subspecialty-specific accuracy.

Results

Card characteristics and rater participation

We evaluated a total of 1284 flashcards generated by the sequential multi-LLM pipeline. Of these, the S5 automated QA stage classified 958 (74.6%) as PASS, 264 (20.6%) as card-regeneration (requiring text-content regeneration), and 62 (4.8%) as image-regeneration (requiring image-regeneration); the full subspecialty × classification breakdown is reported in Supplementary Table S6. Separately, 46 of the 6000 generated cards (0.8%) had no-S5 record because of processing errors and therefore did not enter the automated PASS/REGEN workflow; these were treated as an operational failure mode distinct from the conditional FNR analysis. The card pool comprised 754 BASIC-type and 530 multiple-choice question (MCQ)-type cards. Twenty physician raters participated in human evaluation: nine radiology residents and eleven attending radiologists. Text-content quality: false-negative rate

The primary operational safety endpoint was the evaluation-level false-negative rate (FNR), defined as the proportion of review opportunities in which the S5 automated system had classified a card as PASS, but the human evaluator identified a blocking error. Among 1100 pre-S5 review opportunities involving 980 text-level PASS cards that had received an S5 decision (comprising 958 pipeline-PASS cards and 22 image-regeneration cards whose text content was classified as acceptable), 11 evaluations identified blocking errors missed by the S5 system (e.g., a renal mass card with undetected vein thrombus; Fig. 1B), yielding an overall evaluation-level FNR of 1.00% (95% CI: 0.50–1.78%) (Table 1; denominators for all analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table S1). Residents contributed 999 evaluations and identified nine false negatives (FNR 0.90%, 95% CI: 0.41–1.70%), while attendings contributed 101 evaluations and identified two false negatives (FNR 1.98%, 95% CI: 0.24–6.97%). A single resident rater accounted for eight of the eleven false-negative flags (72.7%), indicating that blocking-error detection was highly rater-dependent.

The pre-specified conditional safety criterion required the entire 95% CI to fall below 0.3%. Because even the lower bound of the observed CI (0.50%) exceeded this threshold, the pipeline did not meet the criterion among cards with a valid S5 decision. As a complementary card-level analysis, 11 of 980 text-level PASS cards had at least one rater identify a blocking error (1.12%, 95% CI: 0.56–2.00%). When requiring a majority of raters to flag the error, the card-level FNR decreased to 0.82% (8/980, 95% CI: 0.35–1.60%), though this stricter definition applied only to cards evaluated by multiple raters. Three of the 11 flagged cards were evaluated by two raters, and in each case, only one rater flagged the error, a discordance consistent with the low inter-rater reliability observed throughout this study. As a pre-specified exploratory analysis to characterize reference-standard fragility, we performed a leave-one-rater-out analysis. Excluding the rater who contributed the most false-negative flags reduced the FNR to 0.304% (3/987 evaluations, 95% CI: 0.06–0.89%); excluding any other single rater changed the FNR by less than 0.02 percentage points (range: 0.91–1.12%). The safety criterion was not met under any leave-one-out scenario (all leave-one-out FNRs exceeded 0.300%). Taken together, these analyses suggest that the qualitative conclusion is directionally stable, but the exact point estimate remains sensitive to rater dependence and threshold variation (Fig. 3A).

To address the absence of formal adjudication on the 33 dual-rated calibration cards, a senior subspecialty-experienced radiologist who had not participated in the original evaluation independently re-reviewed all 33 items and combined their verdicts with the four prior post-S5 verdicts under both majority (≥3 of cast votes Yes) and any-rater (≥1 of 5 Yes) rules; complete details of the consensus procedure are provided in the “Reference standard and adjudication” section. The adjudicator returned 12 Yes/21 No/0 Uncertain verdicts across the 33 cards, with all 33 verdicts assigned a confidence of at least 3 (mean 4.5), so the three pre-specified sensitivity modes (primary, confidence ≥3, drop-Uncertain) yielded identical results. Under the majority rule, one DISCORDANT card (3 Yes/2 No after the adjudicator’s Yes was added) shifted into a consensus-Yes label, while the remaining DISCORDANT cards remained majority-No; the 33-card relabeling raised the headline majority-vote FNR from 0.816% (8/980, 95% CI: 0.41–1.60%) to 0.918% (9/980, 95% Wilson CI: 0.48–1.74%) (Supplementary Table S1C). Under the any-rater rule, six cards previously concordant-No among the four raters received an adjudicator Yes, raising the any-rater FNR from 1.020% (10/980, 95% Wilson CI: 0.56–1.87%) to 1.633% (16/980, 95% Wilson CI: 1.01–2.64%); per-card details for the seven discordant cards and the six previously concordant-No cards that were systematically missed by the four-rater pool are reported in Supplementary Table S1D, E. Both consensus-adjudicated estimates therefore remained directionally aligned with the four-rater post-S5 baselines and, like the unadjudicated estimates, did not meet the pre-specified 0.30% threshold under either decision rule.

Association of S5 feedback with evaluation shifts (exploratory)

As an exploratory within-rater analysis, we examined whether exposing evaluators to S5 feedback altered their subsequent quality assessments, using 1680 paired pre-to-post evaluations. Because the design was single-arm and sequential, the following estimates describe association rather than isolated causal effect. Contrary to the expectation that S5 feedback would reduce detected errors, the number of evaluation-level blocking-error flags increased from 39 (pre-S5) to 54 (post-S5) (McNemar test, p = 0.003; discordant ratio 19/4, OR = 4.75). Technical-accuracy scores decreased from a mean of 0.967 to 0.953 (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, r = 0.73, p < 0.001), and educational quality scores decreased from a mean of 4.718 to 4.672 (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, r = 0.63, p < 0.001); full test statistics are reported in Supplementary Table S12.

These shifts indicate that S5 feedback was associated with increased evaluator scrutiny. Structured change-reason logs recorded by evaluators provide descriptive context: of 260 documented rating changes, 98 (37.7%) cited S5 results as helpful for quality assessment, 50 (19.2%) cited S5 evidence specifically, and 34 (13.1%) reported discovering a blocking error through S5 feedback. Notably, 56 changes (21.5%) were attributed to the evaluator’s own re-review independent of S5; the remaining 22 changes (8.5%) cited other or unspecified reasons. These self-reported reasons are consistent with an attention-cueing mechanism, though the single-arm design cannot distinguish cueing from anchoring, expectancy, or re-exposure effects.

Inter-rater reliability

We assessed inter-rater reliability on a calibration subset of 33 items, each rated by 4 raters (3 residents and 1 subspecialty-matched attending). Pairwise mean correlation for technical accuracy (post-S5) was r = 0.50, while educational quality (post-S5) showed a near-zero pairwise mean correlation of r = −0.036 (Supplementary Table S8). For the binary blocking-error variable underlying the primary endpoint, Cohen’s κ on calibration items was −0.023 (percent agreement 95.5%), a paradox attributable to the low prevalence of blocking errors (2.3%) in the calibration subset, which deflates kappa toward zero regardless of agreement level. These low-reliability estimates indicate substantial subjectivity in how raters assessed content quality, particularly for educational value judgments. The weak agreement exposes a challenge inherent to evaluating AI-generated educational material: even among physicians trained with a common rubric, individual standards for what constitutes high educational quality diverged widely. The 5-rater consensus adjudication described above provides additional context for interpreting these reliability estimates. The 5-rater verdict distribution across the 33 dual-rated cards comprised 20 unanimous-No (0Y/5N), seven 1Y/4N, five 2Y/3N, and one 3Y/2N outcome; 8 of 33 cards (24%) therefore showed at least a 2-Yes minority position. Reading the distribution against the per-card content, the residual split votes localized to image-question pairing failure modes (Supplementary Table S1E) rather than to disagreement about whether a text-content statement was clinically correct, consistent with the interpretation that the rubric was applied to text content with internally stable judgments while the multimodal task of image-question pairing exceeded the practical sensitivity of the rubric and the four-rater pool.

Regenerated card outcomes

Cards flagged for regeneration by the S5 system underwent re-evaluation by human raters. We report regeneration outcomes at the evaluation level, consistent with the primary FNR analysis, because multi-rater calibration cards produced discordant accept/reject decisions for the same card, and aggregation to the card level would mask this inter-rater variability. For card-regeneration items, all 539 ratings received a response, of which 286 (53.1%) resulted in rejection and 253 (46.9%) in acceptance, with a mean quality score of 3.12 (SD 1.37) (Table 3). For image-regeneration items, all 68 ratings received a response; 28 (41.2%) were rejected and 40 (58.8%) were accepted, with a mean quality score of 3.47 (SD 1.31) (Fig. 4). The higher rejection rate for text-regenerated cards compared with image-regenerated cards suggests that text-content errors flagged by the S5 system were more resistant to correction through a single regeneration cycle.

Image quality

As a preliminary, exploratory analysis, nine residents evaluated 19 image items (nine tables, ten infographics; 171 evaluations); the limited sample precludes definitive comparisons but identifies patterns warranting larger-scale investigation (representative structured-content examples are shown in Fig. 2; three additional accepted regeneration pairs covering multiple sclerosis spinal cord, ulnar collateral ligament injury, and spine ossification subspecialties are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2 to illustrate the regeneration mechanism beyond a single subspecialty). Because the cohort comprised only 19 unique items, all image-related findings reported below are preliminary. The overall critical error rate was 6.4% (11/171; 95% CI: 3.3–11.2%), with descriptive rates of 1.2% for tables and 11.1% for infographics (Supplementary Table S13); no metric reached significance after Holm correction. Pairwise mean correlations on 12 calibration items rated by all nine raters were uniformly near zero (range −0.008 to 0.049; Supplementary Table S9), paralleling the low inter-rater reliability observed for text content.

For flashcard illustrations evaluated by all 20 raters, S5 feedback produced significant shifts across all four metrics (all Holm-corrected p ≤ 0.001; Supplementary Table S10), including increased blocking-error detection (OR 2.13, 95% CI: 1.37–3.45) and moderate effects on anatomical accuracy (r = 0.56), overall quality (r = 0.60), and text-image consistency (r = 0.47), mirroring the scrutiny-heightening pattern observed for text. Attending radiologists scored illustrations significantly more strictly than residents on every post-S5 metric (all Holm-corrected p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S11): they detected more blocking errors (OR 5.52, 95% CI: 4.27–7.14) and assigned lower anatomical accuracy (r = −0.36), overall quality (r = −0.44), and text-image consistency (r = −0.39) scores.

Evaluation time and scalability

Human evaluators spent a median of 33 s (IQR 19–66) on the pre-S5 phase and 12 s (IQR 9–25) on the post-S5 phase per card, with a total median of 53 s (IQR 31–98) per evaluation (n = 1655 paired evaluations with valid timestamps). The post-S5 phase was 64% shorter than the pre-S5 phase (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001, rank-biserial r = 0.66), consistent with focused review of S5-flagged issues rather than complete re-evaluation. Attending radiologists spent significantly more time per evaluation than residents (median 109 s [IQR 65–239] vs. 46 s [IQR 29–79]; Mann–Whitney U-test, p < 0.001, r = 0.52). The total observed human review effort was 58.6 h for the evaluated subset; extrapolating at the median per-card rate, reviewing all 6000 pipeline cards would require approximately 88 h (11 person-days) of physician time.

The consistently lower scores from attendings may reflect greater clinical experience and stricter quality thresholds, but assignment differences remain important. Because attending radiologists evaluated cards matched to their subspecialty while residents evaluated cards across all subspecialties, we performed a Mantel–Haenszel analysis stratifying by subspecialty to assess whether the expertise gap reflected domain-specific knowledge or general vigilance. The crude odds ratio for blocking-error detection (attending vs. resident) was 4.52 (95% CI: 2.38–8.57, p < 0.001). The Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio adjusting for subspecialty was 4.59, differing by only 1.5% from the crude estimate, suggesting that subspecialty matching alone did not explain the gap, although differences in workload and case mix remained. The attending-resident gap was not uniform across subspecialties: attendings detected substantially more errors in musculoskeletal radiology (20.0% vs. 2.0%), abdominal radiology (13.3% vs. 1.1%), and pediatric radiology (13.3% vs. 1.3%), while no attending-flagged errors occurred in interventional radiology, neuro/head-neck imaging, or genitourinary radiology despite resident-flagged errors in those fields.

Discussion

Among cards that successfully received an S5 output, the evaluation-level false-negative rate was 1.00% (95% CI: 0.50–1.78%), with a similar card-level estimate of 1.12% (95% CI: 0.56–2.00%). These conditional safety estimates exceeded the pre-specified 0.3% threshold, but they should be interpreted alongside two caveats: 46 of 6000 generated cards (0.8%) failed to receive any S5 record, and the human reference standard was low-reliability and only partially adjudicated — formal 5-rater consensus adjudication was performed on the 33-card dual-rated calibration subset, while the remaining 947 of 980 text-level PASS cards were evaluated by a single rater each and could not be retrofitted with multi-rater adjudication within the present design. An extended characterization of the 11 residual false-negative cards (Supplementary Table S4C) showed that S5 had assigned each of them the maximum technical-accuracy (1.0) and educational quality (5.0) score and had not flagged any image-related concern, indicating that the residual misclassifications are not borderline cases sitting near the composite 70/100 cut-off but cases in which the automated grader is confidently and systematically miscalibrated; approximately three quarters of the residual errors involved the image, consistent with multimodal accuracy gaps documented elsewhere32,33. In safety-critical medical education, where a single factual error can propagate to thousands of learners, even this qualified miss rate remains concerning. S5 feedback altered evaluator behavior: blocking-error detection increased from 39 to 54 (McNemar p = 0.003), while technical-accuracy and educational quality scores both decreased (all p < 0.001). These shifts admit several interpretations. Attention cueing — in which structured feedback directs reviewers to features they might otherwise overlook — is one plausible mechanism. Equally plausible, however, are anchoring bias (evaluators adjusting toward S5-generated scores), expectancy effects (evaluators who know the system flagged a concern becoming more critical), or simple re-exposure effects (a second look at the same card naturally revealing more issues). Without a control arm receiving no-S5 feedback, these mechanisms cannot be distinguished, and this ambiguity represents a fundamental limitation of the single-arm design. The time data offer partial discrimination: the post-S5 phase was 64% shorter than the pre-S5 phase (median 12 vs. 33 s, p < 0.001), a pattern more consistent with targeted spot-checking of flagged items than with full re-evaluation. A pure re-exposure effect would predict similar durations in both phases. What the data do support is a narrower claim: automated feedback was associated with increased evaluator scrutiny, but its causal mechanism remains undetermined.

Attending radiologists identified blocking errors at a substantially higher rate than residents (crude OR = 4.52, 95% CI: 2.38–8.57), and they scored image quality consistently lower across all four metrics (all p < 0.001, effect sizes r = −0.36 to −0.44). This expertise gap holds across multiple quality dimensions, but it should not be interpreted as a pure training-level effect. Residents, by contrast, tended to accept the Gemini-generated content with less critical scrutiny. Although our study was not designed to test automation bias experimentally, this pattern aligns with the automation bias literature, in which less experienced users are more likely to defer to automated outputs30. Because attending radiologists evaluated cards matched to their subspecialty, carried a much smaller workload, and reviewed a subsample of the resident pool, the observed gap could reflect a mixture of seniority, domain expertise, and assignment structure rather than general vigilance alone. A Mantel–Haenszel analysis adjusting for subspecialty yielded an OR of 4.59, virtually unchanged from the crude estimate, suggesting that subspecialty matching alone did not explain the attending-resident difference. The gap was not uniform, however: attendings flagged markedly more errors in musculoskeletal, abdominal, and pediatric radiology while detecting none in interventional radiology or neuro/head-neck imaging, indicating that even subspecialty-matched experts have blind spots. The practical implication is therefore narrower than a simple superiority claim: our data suggest that subspecialty-matched senior review may detect errors that a resident-only workflow misses, but this inference remains partially confounded by workload and case-mix differences. Institutions adopting Gemini family or other LLM-generated educational materials may nonetheless benefit from incorporating attending review into the workflow as a structural safeguard rather than an optional quality check; because our evidence is restricted to a single model family, this recommendation should be revisited as multi-vendor data accumulate. The time data reinforce the trade-off: attendings spent 2.4 times longer per card than residents (median 109 vs. 46 s, p < 0.001), and this additional time investment was associated with higher error detection. To examine whether the attending advantage reflected expertise alone or was confounded by per-card review effort, we conducted a pre-specified workload-matched sensitivity analysis restricting both groups to comparable per-evaluation durations (Supplementary Table S8B). Within tertile-matched and workload-equalized strata, the attending-vs-resident odds ratio attenuated to 1.49–3.02 from a crude PASS-only OR of 2.53, indicating that an appreciable share of the apparent expertise gap on PASS cards is attributable to the per-card review-time difference, while a residual attending advantage persists. All matched estimates were wide and crossed one, however, so the residual gap should be regarded as suggestive rather than confirmed; the framing is therefore best described as “expertise plus workload” rather than expertise alone, and any operational recommendation to layer attending review on top of automated quality assurance should be paired with a realistic per-item time budget and not be misread as a free benefit of seniority. At the observed median rate, reviewing the full 6000-card output would require approximately 88 physician-hours (11 person-days). As a rough comparator, manual MCQ authoring by faculty takes approximately 4 min per item34, which would translate to roughly 400 h for 6000 items. This comparison is approximate and should be interpreted only as an order-of-magnitude reference, not as a validated productivity metric — review of generated content and de novo authoring differ in cognitive demand and output — but it provides a rough sense of scale, suggesting that pipeline-assisted workflows shift the physician role from content creation to quality oversight, with a substantially lower time investment per item.

The evaluation of visual content, conducted as an exploratory pilot cohort of 19 unique items (171 evaluations) — substantially smaller than the 1284-card text QA dataset — suggested, but did not definitively establish, a discrepancy between content types. Infographics had a descriptive critical error rate of 11.1% (95% CI: 5.5–19.5%), compared with 1.2% for tables, but formal table-versus-infographic comparisons were not significant after Holm correction. Pairwise mean correlations for image quality metrics were near zero (range: −0.008 to 0.049), and evaluators disagreed almost completely on subjective image quality. These findings reflect a broader challenge: current MLLMs produce images with visually plausible appearance but unreliable anatomical detail25–27,35. Recent benchmarking studies reinforce this concern: MLLMs achieved similar diagnostic accuracy regardless of whether image inputs were provided, suggesting limited genuine visual processing32, and their vision capabilities remain heavily text-dependent and prone to hallucination33. Comparative studies of LLMs versus radiologists on visually driven tasks reach similar conclusions: although textual reasoning is often preserved, performance falls sharply on image-dependent tasks such as MRI artifact detection36, reinforcing the need for dedicated multimodal validation rather than text-only quality checks. For medical education, anatomical accuracy is non-negotiable. An image depicting incorrect anatomy does not merely fail to teach; it actively misteaches, consistent with evidence that misinformation persists in memory even after correction37. Until image generation models achieve verifiable anatomical fidelity, visual content from current multimodal pipelines such as the Gemini 3 Pro Image (Nano Banana Pro preview) system used here may warrant stringent review, and automated image quality assessment tools deserve dedicated development; because our image evaluation is confined to Gemini 3 Pro Image, generalization to other image generators is not warranted. We emphasize that this image-related observation is based on a small exploratory pilot of 19 unique items and should be interpreted as preliminary.

Regardless of the underlying mechanism, the observed association between automated feedback and heightened evaluator scrutiny has practical implications. Automated systems that flag content for human review face a lower accountability bar than those that attempt autonomous correction38,39. If future controlled studies confirm a genuine cueing effect, feedback-without-correction may represent the most realistic near-term function for sequential multi-LLM quality assurance in medical education, although the present evidence is restricted to a Gemini-only pipeline and replication across other model families is needed. Effective human-AI interaction requires users to understand, rather than blindly accept, AI-generated outputs40,41; feedback-without-correction may lower this bar by directing attention without demanding trust. Although our findings concern educational content rather than clinical decision-making, the same human-oversight principles increasingly apply to clinically actionable LLM applications — for example, recommending appropriate imaging modalities to reduce unnecessary contrast agent use and radiation exposure42 — broadening the relevance of validated quality assurance to the wider clinical workflow.

The methodological distinction between educational content validation and clinical decision-making also extends to the tolerance thresholds against which automated quality assurance should be calibrated. In educational content, the relevant harm is misinformation that propagates to learners and persists in memory after correction37; in clinical decision-making, the relevant harm is direct patient injury. Our 0.3% pre-specification was anchored by analogy to safety-critical healthcare benchmarks (pharmacy dispensing error rates43 and screening mammography false-negative rates44; “Methods” section and Supplementary Table S4B), but these analogies were chosen for their proximity to the educational context rather than as direct transpositions to clinical decision support, where tolerance is calibrated against population-level patient-outcome reporting rather than learner-facing factual accuracy. Thus, the 0.3% educational threshold should not be transferred to clinical decision support without separate validation tailored to that domain’s tolerance frame.

Several limitations constrain the generalizability and interpretation of these findings, and we organize them into four classes: model and setting, reference-standard fragility, design and temporal scope, and workload and adverse-event reporting.

First, with respect to model and setting, we evaluated a single pipeline implemented exclusively with the Gemini family (Gemini 3 Flash and Pro for text generation; Gemini 3 Pro Image, the Nano Banana Pro preview, for image generation) under a single-center Korean academic IRB. Although evaluators were recruited from six academic medical centers, the results may not transfer to other large language model families (e.g., GPT, Claude, Mistral, open-weight models), to other multimodal image generators, to other prompt strategies, or to specialties beyond radiology, and the evaluation was conducted in a single linguistic context (Korean radiology board curriculum). The study also lacked a head-to-head comparator: we did not benchmark this sequential multi-LLM pipeline against simpler workflows such as single-LLM generation without automated QA, rule-based filtering alone, or fully human-authored content2,3, so the incremental contribution of the multi-stage architecture relative to such baselines cannot be isolated from the present data and remains an inferential gap to be closed by direct comparative trials. The pipeline underwent iterative optimization — 27 versioned prompts, retrieval-augmented generation with curated evidence, and state-of-the-art multimodal LLMs — yet S5 sensitivity for blocking errors remained 71.8%, and the conditional FNR exceeded the pre-specified threshold, suggesting a structural ceiling of sequential architectures that process each stage’s output without bidirectional error correction rather than evidence of a deliberately weakened system.

Second, the reference standard against which the pipeline was judged was itself fragile, and we regard this as the most consequential limitation of the present study. The uniformly low pairwise mean correlations raise questions about whether any individual evaluator’s judgment can be treated as a reliable reference standard, and a reference-standard fragility analysis revealed that a single resident rater contributed 8 of 11 false-negative flags (72.7%). Excluding this rater reduced the FNR to 0.304% (3/987, 95% CI: 0.06–0.89%), while excluding any other rater changed the estimate by less than 0.02 percentage points; the safety criterion was not met under any leave-one-out scenario (all leave-one-out FNRs exceeded 0.300%). This concentration does not invalidate the qualitative conclusion, but it highlights a challenge intrinsic to low-prevalence error detection: when few errors exist, even modest rater-to-rater variation in detection threshold produces large swings in the point estimate. For the binary blocking-error variable specifically, Cohen’s κ on the calibration subset was −0.023 despite 95.5% raw agreement, a well-documented paradox attributable to the low prevalence of blocking errors (2.3%) in calibration items45; this prevalence-deflated kappa further illustrates the difficulty of establishing a reliable reference standard for rare events. Although 880 of 980 text-level PASS cards were evaluated by only one rater and we therefore could not perform formal consensus adjudication on the full sample, we obtained partial consensus on the dual-rated subset by adding a fifth, senior, subspecialty-experienced radiologist as an independent adjudicator on the 33 four-rater calibration cards (see “Reference standard and adjudication” section, and 5-rater consensus paragraph in the “Text-content quality: false-negative rate”; Cohen’s κ = −0.023 on the binary blocking-error variable across the calibration items). The 5-rater majority-vote FNR after this adjudication was 0.918% (9/980, 95% Wilson CI: 0.48–1.74%), directionally aligned with the four-rater majority-vote estimate of 0.82% but with a slightly wider upper bound; the 5-rater any-rater FNR was 1.633% (16/980, 95% Wilson CI: 1.01–2.64%), consistent with the four-rater any-rater estimate of 1.020%. Neither consensus-adjudicated estimate met the pre-specified 0.30% threshold; together, they bound the residual rater-dependence concern and removed the alternative explanation that the unmet threshold was an artifact of unadjudicated dual-rater discordance on the calibration subset, while the 947 cards outside the adjudicated 33-card subset remain unadjudicated and therefore continue to bound the overall reference-standard claim. Our quadruple reporting of evaluation-level (1.00%), card-level (1.12%), majority-vote (0.82%), and leave-one-out (0.30%) FNRs, supplemented by these consensus-adjudicated estimates, partially, but does not eliminate this circularity, and consequently, any single point estimate from the present design must be interpreted as bounded by these rater-dependent sensitivity scenarios.

A residual concern remains that the four-rater pool itself, even after S5 feedback, may be vulnerable to systematic blind spots, and the consensus adjudication offered direct evidence of this. Six of the 26 cards on which the four raters had unanimously agreed there was no blocking error (CONCORDANT_NO under the four-rater post-S5 verdict) were nonetheless flagged Yes by the senior adjudicator (Supplementary Table S1E; representative regenerated-image pairs for the affected subspecialties are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2). All six involved an image-text pairing failure rather than a text-content reasoning error and are categorized, following the structure of Supplementary Table S1E, as one cross-organ-system mismatch (persistent left superior vena cava), two cross-category mismatches within the same specialty (a same-territory hepatic vascular topic mismatch and a gamma-camera quality-control infographic outside the question’s nuclear-medicine target), one view-convention mixing case (mixed axial and coronal view conventions in a single labeled illustration), one left/right inversion under axial CT convention, and one anatomic-label swap for paired ligaments on a sagittal knee diagram. None of these errors were intrinsic to the text content, which was medically accurate in all six cases; they manifested instead at the boundary between the question prompt and the illustrative image. This pattern converges with the extended characterization of the original 11 false-negative cards (Supplementary Table S4C), which similarly indicated that approximately three quarters of the residual misses involved the image, and reinforces the interpretation that the failure mode of multi-LLM educational pipelines for radiology is not a generic deficit of the text-content evaluation framework but a localized and systematic inability of both the automated grader and four-rater human review to flag image-question pairing errors. Implementations should therefore not rely on improved text-content reliability alone; dedicated multimodal validation tools that interrogate the image and the question jointly remain a prerequisite for safe deployment.

Third, design and temporal scope impose further constraints. The cross-sectional design captured quality at one time point, and the two-phase evaluation was sequential rather than crossover, so recall of the pre-S5 assessment may have influenced post-S5 ratings and precludes causal attribution of post-S5 changes to the automated feedback itself. Our headline FNR is conditional on successful S5 execution; the 46 cards without an S5 record were excluded from the primary sampling frame as a separate deployment-relevant operational failure mode. Because sample size was constrained by evaluator capacity rather than a priori design, the primary inference relied on the precision of the 95% Clopper–Pearson interval around the observed FNR (0.50–1.78%) rather than on a hypothesis test; the lower bound remained above the 0.3% threshold, and the card-level (1.12%), majority-vote (0.82%), and leave-one-out (0.304%) sensitivity analyses all converged above threshold, supporting directional stability even under the most conservative rater-exclusion scenarios.

Fourth, with respect to workload and adverse-event reporting, the differential evaluation burden (150 cards per resident vs. 30 per attending) and the corresponding 2.4-fold per-card review-time gap may confound the expertise comparison; the workload-matched sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S8B) suggests that per-card review effort accounts for an appreciable share of the apparent gap. Future studies should use multi-rater designs with formal adjudication panels and balanced workloads. Beyond the rater-side workload, the S5 system itself imposes a substantial reviewer triage burden: with a positive predictive value of 4.8% and specificity of 66.4%, approximately 95% of cards classified as REGEN are false positives, translating into roughly 329 non-blocking flags per 1000 cards that nonetheless require physician adjudication. This skew toward over-flagging means that flagged items should not be treated as confirmed errors, and downstream interfaces should make the low PPV explicit to avoid alarm fatigue. No adverse educational events were reported during the study period; the 11 false-negative cards were distributed to learners before human review was completed but were corrected or replaced in the Anki deck within 48 h of discovery (categorization in Supplementary Table S4), and long-term effects on knowledge retention, along with educational effectiveness, were not assessed in this study. A companion educational-effectiveness pilot study by our group, evaluating learner outcomes from a portion of the same pipeline-generated content, has since been published46 the present manuscript focuses on safety and quality validation, while the downstream educational utility of the validated content is addressed in that separate investigation.

A sequential multi-LLM pipeline can generate radiology education content at scale, but our data do not support fully automated deployment in this specific learner-facing setting. The conditional FNR, low-reliability reference standard, rater- and workload-dependent evaluation patterns, and a preliminary signal of higher infographic error rates — based on only 19 unique image items and not significant after Holm correction — together support retaining human oversight; the image-related findings in particular should be regarded as exploratory and require dedicated, adequately powered confirmation. These findings are most directly relevant to clinician-facing educational content. Within that bounded scope, they highlight the absence of validated QA standards for learner-facing AI educational materials; our data do not address regulatory classification, which varies across jurisdictions and is evolving. In the absence of such standards, the minimal safeguard our data support is subspecialty-matched expert review layered on top of automated quality assurance, while recognizing that even matched experts exhibited subspecialty-specific blind spots and cannot serve as a complete reference standard; these conclusions generalize most confidently to multi-LLM pipelines producing clinician-facing radiology content in non-Western training contexts and should be re-validated before extension to other specialties, languages, or learner levels. Future work should employ controlled A/B designs (feedback vs. no-feedback arms) to isolate the causal effect of automated QA on human scrutiny, develop standardized evaluation rubrics to improve inter-rater reliability, conduct adequately powered educational-effectiveness trials with dedicated image quality cohorts, replicate the present design across multiple LLM vendors, image generators, languages, and institutions to establish external validity, benchmark sequential multi-LLM pipelines against simpler workflows (single-LLM generation, rule-based filtering, and human-only authoring) to quantify their marginal yield, characterize evaluator-level interaction personas that may modulate the effectiveness of automated feedback, and design bidirectional feedback architectures in which downstream evaluation results inform upstream generation47,48, moving these pipelines closer to true multi-agent systems15,49. Although MLLM diagnostic capabilities have improved rapidly over the past year50, the gap between visual plausibility and anatomical fidelity remains a major barrier to autonomous educational content deployment.

Methods

Study design and pipeline overview

We conducted a prospective, multi-dimensional validation study to assess the safety and quality of medical education content generated by a sequential multi-LLM pipeline. The pipeline comprised seven stages: Stage 1 (S1) generated structured medical knowledge tables from Korean radiology board examination curricula (accessed December 2025); Stage 2 (S2) produced question-and-answer flashcards; Stage 3 (S3) applied rule-based formatting; Stage 4 (S4) generated educational images via a multimodal large language model (MLLM); Stage 5 (S5) performed automated quality scoring with structured feedback; Stage 6 (S6) exported content to Anki flashcard format51,52; and a final distribution stage delivered the content to learners. The pipeline produced 6000 Anki flashcards and 833 table infographics spanning 11 radiology subspecialties (Fig. 5); the full subspecialty allocation is detailed in Supplementary Table S7. LLM-dependent stages used Google Gemini models (Gemini 3 Flash and Gemini 3 Pro for text generation via Chat API and Gemini 3 Pro Image (Nano Banana Pro preview, API model name models/nano-banana-pro-preview) for image generation via Gemini Image API; model cards available at https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/), while two stages (S3 policy resolution and S6 export) were deterministic compilers requiring no LLM. Exact model names, versions, temperature settings, and prompt configurations for each stage are reported in Supplementary Table S2, following the Minimum Reporting Items for Clear Evaluation of Accuracy Reports of Large Language Models in Healthcare (MI-CLEAR-LLM) guideline53 and the Reporting Checklist for Foundation and Large Language Models in Medical Research (REFINE)54. A meta-research study has documented widespread gaps in AI model transparency in radiology55, underscoring the importance of detailed reporting. All Google Gemini models used were proprietary; although model cards are publicly available, training data composition was not fully disclosed. No parametric adaptation (fine-tuning) was applied to any model; all were used with their pre-trained weights. Each card was processed exactly once through each pipeline stage; no repeated sampling or output selection was performed. The S5 module has not been submitted for regulatory clearance as a medical device and is not CE-marked or FDA-cleared; AI-generated educational content currently falls outside existing regulatory frameworks56. Non-parametric adaptation strategies included retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for S5 validation and extended thinking (chain-of-thought) for S1, S2, and S6 regeneration stages. Because the Korean radiology board examination curricula are publicly available, we cannot exclude the possibility that portions of this content were included in the models’ training data; however, the study evaluated end-to-end pipeline output quality rather than LLM accuracy on specific test items. Prompt development and evaluation were conducted on separate datasets: all prompts were iteratively refined on a fixed development set of 11 curriculum groups (December 29–31, 2025) using a structured three-round refinement cycle (S5R0 baseline, S5R1, S5R2), in which S5 validation outputs identified blocking-error patterns and guided prompt revisions under predefined stopping rules (blocking issue rate reduced to zero, marginal gain below threshold, or maximum of two iterations reached). Prompts were frozen before the FINAL_DISTRIBUTION run (January 5–7, 2026), and no further prompt modifications were made after evaluation commenced. Representative prompt texts for S1 and S5 are provided in Supplementary Table S3; the complete prompt set with version history is archived in the project repository.

Overview of the 7-stage sequential multi-LLM pipeline for medical education content generation.

The pipeline generated the FINAL_DISTRIBUTION content between January 5 and 7, 2026, and S5 automated classification was completed on January 7. Human evaluation commenced the following day and was completed by February 28, 2026, yielding an interval of 1–52 days between S5 classification and human assessment. Because the evaluated content was static, this interval did not affect the target condition.

The S5 module scored each card against predefined rubrics and assigned a binary classification. The composite quality metric was calculated as a weighted sum of technical accuracy (0–50 points) and educational quality (0–50 points), yielding a score on a 0–100 scale. Cards scoring at or above 70.0 were classified as PASS; those falling below this predefined threshold were flagged for regeneration (REGEN). The threshold was established a priori before S5 execution and was not modified during the study. Of the 6000 cards, S5 classified 5478 (91.3%) as PASS, 292 (4.9%) as card-regeneration, and 184 (3.1%) as image-regeneration; For card-regeneration, the pipeline regenerated the text content (question, answer, and explanation) while preserving the original image; for image-regeneration, only the educational illustration was regenerated with the text content unchanged. An additional 46 cards (0.8%) had no-S5 record due to processing errors. Because these 46 cards never received an automated PASS/REGEN output, we treated them as an operational execution failure mode rather than part of the primary classification-error analysis. Accordingly, the primary FNR should be interpreted as conditional on successful S5 execution rather than as an unconditional end-to-end pipeline failure rate. To bound the effect of this exclusion, we computed unconditional FNR sensitivity bounds incorporating the 46 no-S5 cards into the denominator as pipeline outputs that would have reached learners: under a best-case assumption that none of the 46 cards harbored a blocking error, the unconditional FNR was 0.96% (95% CI 0.48–1.71%); under a worst-case assumption that all 46 contained blocking errors, the bound was 4.97% (95% CI 3.79–6.40%). Both bounds remained above the pre-specified 0.3% threshold, indicating that the qualitative safety conclusion is robust to the treatment of no-S5 cards. We designed the validation study around two evaluation dimensions: text-content quality assessment by human expert review and image quality assessment through structured sub-studies.

Sample and evaluator allocation

We sampled 1284 unique cards from the 5954-card subset with a valid S5 decision for human evaluation. The sample included 326 of the 476 cards flagged for regeneration (264 of 292 card-regeneration + 62 of 184 image-regeneration), providing a high-coverage subsample of card-regeneration cards and a representative subsample of image-regenerated cards allocated through the specialist evaluation pool, plus 958 PASS cards selected through a deterministic cluster-aware algorithm (fixed seed 20260101) that stratified by the 11 subspecialties and two card types (BASIC and MCQ) to ensure proportional representation across the curriculum (Fig. 6). No cards with a valid S5 decision were excluded from the primary sampling frame; the 46 cards without an S5 record are reported separately as operational failures.

Validation study design.

Of 6000 generated cards, 5954 received a valid S5 decision, and 46 had no-S5 record due to a processing error. From the 5954 decision-bearing cards, 1284 were sampled for human evaluation (flashcards and structured content) (326 of 476 REGEN + 958 stratified PASS). The study comprised two dimensions: text-content QA (1680 evaluations by 20 raters) and image quality assessment (171 table/infographic evaluations). Key outcomes are summarized at the bottom.

Stratification was selected over simple random sampling for three pre-specified reasons. First, content-type heterogeneity across radiology subspecialties is well documented: blocking-error opportunities differ between modality-anchored domains (e.g., cardiovascular, neuroradiology) and text-anchored domains (e.g., physics/quality control, nuclear medicine), and proportional sampling within each subspecialty ensures that the FNR estimate is not dominated by any single domain. Second, within-subspecialty stratification by card type (BASIC versus MCQ) preserved the 50:50 generation ratio of the FINAL_DISTRIBUTION pool and prevented confounding between card-format effects and subspecialty effects. Third, near-complete coverage of card-regeneration items (264 of 292) was prioritized over a larger PASS sample because the card-regeneration class is the rarest decision category (4.9% of all S5 outputs) and is the most operationally consequential when miscategorized. Sample-size adequacy for the 1100 PASS-card rater-level evaluations was assessed by the Clopper–Pearson 95% CI half-width achievable for FNR estimation: at the observed event count (FN = 11 of 1100), the achievable CI is 0.50–1.78%, with a half-width of approximately 0.64 percentage points. This precision is sufficient to distinguish the observed FNR from the pre-specified 0.3% safety threshold (CIs do not overlap) and from a hypothetical 5% reference rate, providing decision-relevant evidence at the threshold of clinical interest. We did not perform a hypothesis-testing power calculation because the analysis is a precision estimation against a fixed safety bound rather than a comparison of two proportions.

Nine radiology residents (median postgraduate year [PGY] 4, range 2–5) from six Korean academic medical centers each evaluated 150 cards, yielding 1350 total evaluations. Eleven attending radiologists (median 6 years of post-fellowship experience, range 3–26), each representing a distinct subspecialty (neuroradiology/head-and-neck, breast, thoracic, interventional, musculoskeletal, genitourinary, cardiovascular, abdominal, physics/quality control/informatics, pediatric, and nuclear medicine), each evaluated 30 cards matched to their domain expertise, producing 330 evaluations. All evaluations were conducted independently via a structured web-based form (AppSheet, Google) without time constraints. Cards assigned to attending radiologists were subsampled from the resident evaluation pool to enable direct comparison between trainee and specialist assessments.

The asymmetric workload allocation (150 cards per resident vs. 30 cards per attending) was determined by three pre-specified considerations. First, attending radiologists in Korean academic centers carry clinical service obligations that materially constrain non-clinical research time, making a 150-card-per-attending design operationally infeasible without compromising response quality. Second, attending review was scoped to subspecialty-matched content, so the absolute card count required per attending was lower than for residents who reviewed across all 11 subspecialties. Third, the 30-card attending block was sized to provide 330 attending evaluations in total, with each subspecialty contributing 30 evaluations matched on subspecialty to the 150 evaluations contributed by the corresponding resident pool, supporting subspecialty-stratified attending-vs-resident comparisons. We acknowledge that this design produces a substantially larger per-rater workload for residents and a 2.4-fold longer median per-card review time for attendings, both of which can confound trainee-versus-specialist comparisons. To partially separate the expertise contribution from the workload contribution, we conducted a pre-specified workload-matched sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S8B) restricting both groups to comparable per-card durations.

Two-phase pre/post-evaluation design

Each evaluator completed a two-phase assessment for every assigned card. Before the main evaluation, all raters received a written rubric with definitions and examples for each metric (full rubric in Supplementary Table S5), followed by a 10-item calibration exercise with group feedback to align rating standards. In the pre-evaluation phase, evaluators rated cards fully blinded to both S5 classification status (PASS vs. REGEN) and S5 quality scores; conversely, S5 classification was performed automatically before human evaluation and was therefore inherently blinded to human judgments. Evaluators recorded three metrics: the presence of a blocking error (binary Yes/No, defined before evaluation commenced as a factual error that could distort clinical reasoning, such as an incorrect anatomical label, a misattributed imaging finding, or an erroneous differential diagnosis), technical accuracy (continuous, 0.0–1.0), and educational quality (Likert scale, 1–5). In the post-evaluation phase, evaluators reviewed the S5 automated feedback, which included quality scores, identified deficiencies, and suggested improvements, and could then revise their pre-evaluation ratings using the same three metrics. Evaluators retained full authority over their final assessments; the S5 feedback was designed to direct attention rather than prescribe corrections.

For cards classified as REGEN, evaluators performed an additional step. They judged whether to accept or reject the AI-proposed correction (binary Accept/Reject) and rated correction quality on a 1–5 Likert scale.

Calibration and inter-rater reliability

We designated 33 calibration items (three per subspecialty across 11 subspecialties). Each item was independently rated by three residents and one subspecialty-matched attending radiologist, producing 132 calibration evaluations (33 items × 4 raters). Because calibration items were drawn from the attending evaluation pool, each item received ratings from both training levels, enabling reliability estimation across the full rater panel. Each resident’s 11 calibration items were dispersed at randomized positions within their 150-card set to minimize order effects.

Primary safety endpoint

The primary endpoint was the false-negative rate (FNR), defined as the proportion of S5-classified PASS cards in which human reviewers identified a blocking error. We pre-specified a safety threshold of FNR less than 0.3%, meaning fewer than 3 per 1000 PASS cards could harbor undetected blocking errors to deem the automated classification acceptable for educational deployment. This investigator-determined threshold was selected by analogy with safety-critical healthcare domains. In pharmacy dispensing, a systematic review reported a pooled error rate of 1.6% across healthcare settings43. In cancer screening, the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium reported a mean false-negative rate of 0.8 per 1000 screening mammograms, with sensitivity benchmarks ranging from 77.3% to 94.1% depending on patient age44. Our threshold of 3 per 1000 cards was set substantially below these observed error rates, reflecting the potential for educational errors to propagate through clinical reasoning at scale and to be replayed across spaced-repetition exposures. To assess robustness to the threshold choice, we conducted a pre-specified sensitivity analysis under alternative thresholds (0.3%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0%); the qualitative safety conclusion was preserved for all thresholds at or below 1.0% under both point-estimate and CI-upper-bound decision rules (Supplementary Table S4B), indicating that the 0.3% specification lies within a stable range of safety-equivalent decisions rather than at a knife-edge boundary. Although South Korean guidelines address LLMs intended for use as medical devices57, AI-generated educational content falls outside existing regulatory frameworks56, and no established quality benchmark exists for this domain. S5 produced a continuous composite score that was deterministically classified as PASS (score at or above 70.0) or REGEN (below 70.0); no indeterminate category existed, and no human evaluator reported inability to classify a card. The sample of 1284 cards was determined by the available evaluation capacity of 20 raters rather than a formal a priori power calculation. Inference relied on the precision of the 95% Clopper–Pearson interval around the observed FNR rather than on a hypothesis-testing framework; post-hoc power computed on the observed effect size is not reported because such calculations are generally uninformative relative to the CI already presented. The width and position of the observed CI therefore constitute the primary evidence for the threshold conclusion.

Reference standard and adjudication

For FNR computation, we defined “text-level PASS cards” as the union of pipeline-PASS cards (n = 958) and image-regeneration cards whose text content was classified as acceptable by S5 (n = 22), yielding 980 unique cards. The pre-specified primary analysis used individual pre-S5 blocking-error ratings (i.e., the evaluator’s initial assessment before viewing S5 feedback) as the unit of observation because each review represented a distinct opportunity for an undetected blocking error to be surfaced during operational quality control. We do not interpret repeated ratings on the same card as statistically independent measurements of ground truth; accordingly, the evaluation-level FNR should be read as an operational screening estimate rather than an adjudicated error rate, and we report card-level, majority-vote, and leave-one-rater-out analyses alongside it. We used the pre-S5 assessment as the primary endpoint because it captures uncontaminated human judgment against which S5’s automated classification can be measured; a post-S5 FNR would conflate the pipeline’s intrinsic miss rate with the feedback’s influence on evaluator behavior. The reference standard for blocking errors was each evaluator’s independent clinical judgment applied to the evaluation rubric; no external adjudication panel or gold-standard answer key was used. We adopted a single-rater design for most cards because the majority of text-level PASS cards (880 of 980) were evaluated by a single rater, making multi-rater consensus infeasible. Ninety cards were evaluated by two raters and 10 calibration cards by four raters, yielding 1100 total rater-level evaluations of PASS cards (880 × 1 + 90 × 2 + 10 × 4). For cards evaluated by multiple raters, any single pre-S5 blocking-error flag was sufficient to classify that review opportunity as a false negative in the operational analysis. As sensitivity analyses, we computed a card-level FNR treating each unique PASS card as the unit of analysis, a majority-vote FNR requiring concordance among multiple raters, and a leave-one-rater-out FNR to assess single-evaluator influence; the corresponding point estimates and 95% CIs across all four units of analysis are reported in Supplementary Table S1B. We also report the post-S5 blocking error rates to enable comparison between pre- and post-feedback assessments. To partially address the absence of formal adjudication, we performed a 5-rater consensus adjudication on the subset of 33 dual-rated calibration cards (the cards evaluated by four raters; see the “Calibration and inter-rater reliability” section). A senior subspecialty-experienced radiologist who had not participated in the original evaluation reviewed each of the 33 cards and independently provided a blocking-error verdict (Yes/No/Uncertain), a severity score (1–5, blank if No), a confidence score (1–5), free-text reasoning when Yes, and optional comments. We then combined the four prior post-S5 verdicts with the adjudicator’s verdict to derive a 5-rater consensus under two pre-specified rules: a majority rule (consensus Yes if at least three of the cast Yes/No votes were Yes; Uncertain abstained) and an any-rater rule (consensus Yes if at least one of the five raters voted Yes), with three sensitivity modes (primary, confidence at least 3, and Uncertain dropped). The 33 cards reclassified under the consensus were then merged back into the 980-card text-level PASS denominator to yield consensus-recomputed FNR estimates. We acknowledge that this adjudication covers only the dual-rated subset (33 of 980 PASS cards); for the 947 cards outside this adjudicated subset, evaluator subjectivity remains a limitation only partially offset by the low base rate of blocking errors and the consistency of results across multiple sensitivity analyses.

Image quality assessment

Nine radiology residents each evaluated 19 image items (nine structured tables and 10 infographics) for a total of 171 evaluations. We assessed six metrics per item: presence of critical error (Yes/No), scope alignment failure (Yes/No), information accuracy (scored as 0.0, 0.5, or 1.0), visual clarity (Likert 1–5), educational value (Likert 1–5), and learning efficiency (Likert 1–5). Twelve items served as calibration items rated by all nine evaluators for inter-rater reliability estimation.

Within the card evaluation data, we captured image-specific metrics for each card: presence of blocking error, anatomical accuracy (continuous, 0.0–1.0), image quality (Likert 1–5), and text-image consistency (continuous, 0.0–1.0). We compared resident and attending radiologist ratings on image metrics from the post-evaluation phase.

Statistical analysis

We estimated the primary safety endpoint (FNR) with a Clopper–Pearson exact binomial 95% confidence interval (CI). Because some PASS cards received repeated ratings, the evaluation-level CI should be interpreted as a descriptive interval on review opportunities rather than as an adjudicated card-level ground-truth estimate. We pre-specified four sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the primary FNR estimate: (a) a card-level analysis treating each unique PASS card as the unit, (b) a majority-vote analysis requiring concordance among multiple raters, (c) a leave-one-rater-out analysis to quantify single-evaluator influence on the point estimate, and (d) a 5-rater consensus adjudication over the 33-card dual-rated subset, in which the four prior post-S5 verdicts were combined with the senior adjudicator’s verdict under both majority (≥3 of cast votes Yes) and any-rater (≥1 of 5 Yes) rules; consensus-recomputed FNR estimates were summarized with Wilson 95% CIs. These analyses were designed to characterize the fragility of the reference standard rather than to replace it with a fully adjudicated endpoint. For pre-post comparisons within the two-phase evaluation, we used McNemar’s test for the binary blocking-error outcome and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for ordinal and continuous outcomes (technical accuracy, educational quality). We quantified inter-rater reliability using pairwise Pearson correlations averaged across all rater pairs for continuous metrics derived from the calibration subsets. We chose this approach over traditional intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) because the partial-overlap design, in which different rater subsets evaluated different items, precluded standard ICC computation, which assumes a fully crossed or nested design58. For the binary blocking-error variable, we additionally computed Cohen’s kappa on the calibration subset, noting that very low event prevalence can deflate kappa independently of agreement level45.

For image quality comparisons, we applied the Mann–Whitney U-test for independent group comparisons between resident and attending evaluators, reporting rank-biserial correlation (r) as the effect size. For binary outcomes (blocking-error detection), we report odds ratios (OR) with the convention that OR > 1 indicates a higher detection rate in the comparison group (post vs. pre for temporal comparisons; attending vs. resident for expertise comparisons). Table-versus-infographic comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U-test with Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

We recorded timestamps at the start and completion of each evaluation phase (pre-S5 and post-S5) and computed per-card durations to characterize evaluation efficiency. Pre-computed duration columns in the data export were identified as unreliable; all durations were recalculated directly from timestamp differences. We compared pre-versus-post phase durations using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and resident-versus-attending durations using the Mann–Whitney U-test.

We performed all analyses in Python 3.14.3 using SciPy 1.17.1 and statsmodels 0.14.6, with the random seed set to 42 for reproducibility. We report exact p-values throughout, with two-sided testing at alpha = 0.05.

Reporting

We reported this study following the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guideline, adapted for AI content validation, with the S5 automated QA system treated as the index test and human expert evaluation as the reference standard. A completed STARD-adapted checklist (37 items) and a STARD-compliant participant flow appear in Supplementary Materials and Supplementary Fig. S1, respectively. We also completed the TRIPOD-LLM reporting checklist59, classifying the present study as research design = LLM evaluation (E) and primary LLM task = documentation generation (DG); the completed checklist, with item-by-item locations in the manuscript and justifications for the six main not-applicable items (6b, 10, 11, 14d, 15, 18) and one abstract not-applicable item (2 g), is provided as Supplementary File 4. The study was not pre-registered because it does not constitute a clinical trial; the protocol and pre-analysis plan are archived in the project repository.
