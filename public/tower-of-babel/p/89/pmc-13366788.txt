Socio-demographic and household determinants of stunting in children under five years: a pooled analysis of data from Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia
Authors: Lungisile Shange, Mluleki Tsawe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStunting, a form of chronic malnutrition, remains a major public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa. Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia are among the countries with the highest stunting rates globally. Few multi-country studies have examined household and socio-demographic determinants of stunting in sub-Saharan Africa, creating a gap in understanding cross-country patterns. This study aimed to investigate the socio-demographic and household determinants of stunting in children under the age of five across four countries in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsCross-sectional analysis was conducted in Stata 16 using pooled data from the Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia Demographic and Health Surveys (2015–2018), comprising 38,227 children under five. Stunting was assessed using height-for-age z-scores, with children below − 2 standard deviations classified as stunted. Normalised weights, applied via the svyset command, were used to account for complex survey design. Binary logistic regression models were used to assess associations between the variables. Covariates included age and sex of the child, mother’s education, orphanhood type, children under five in the household, co-residence typology, sex of household head, source of water for drinking, type of toilet facility, household wealth, place of residence, and country. Data were harmonised using DHS standard recode variables and pooled after applying normalised weights to ensure comparability across countries.ResultsThe overall prevalence of stunting was 36.7% (38.3% in Ethiopia, 37.1% in Malawi, 36.8% in Nigeria, and 34.6% in Zambia). Stunting was associated with child age (higher odds among those aged 18–23 months, AOR = 1.61, 95% CI = 1.44–1.79), maternal education (no education: AOR = 3.20, 95% CI: 2.60–3.94), poor household wealth status (AOR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.50–1.81). Having access to flush toilets (AOR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.69–0.93) was a protective factor in child stunting, whereas those who lived in households with pit latrine toilets had higher odds of stunting (AOR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.11–1.32). Unexpectedly, children not living with any parent had lower odds (AOR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.66–0.86) of stunting.ConclusionsChild stunting in Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia is shaped by maternal education, household wealth, living arrangements, and access to safe water and sanitation. By integrating co-residence typology and household composition, this study highlights the importance of household structure and underscores the need for targeted and context-specific interventions to reduce child stunting in sub-Saharan Africa.Keywords: Stunting, Malnutrition, Socio-demographic determinants, Children under five, Sub-Saharan Africa, Living arrangements

Introduction

Stunting is a crucial indicator of chronic malnutrition and reflects long-term socioeconomic disadvantage, affecting child health and development [1, 2]. Malnutrition is a significant global public health issue, especially among children under the age of five [3]. Chronic child malnutrition is one of the leading causes of stunting in children, which is often irreversible [4]. The World Health Organization [5] defines stunting as a condition in which children’s growth and development are hampered by poor nutrition, frequent infection, and a lack of psychosocial stimulation. Stunting increases the risk of abnormalities in growth and development, impaired development, and mortality [6]. It is also associated with long-term consequences such as developmental delays, cognitive impairment, and an increased risk of chronic diseases such diabetes and heart disease in adulthood [7]. Globally, stunting contributes to about one million child deaths per year [8]. Although global estimates show a decrease in child stunting among children under the age of five [9], progress remains uneven, with sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia continuing to have a higher burden [10–12].

In sub-Saharan Africa, malnutrition remains a significant issue and is not yet fully addressed [13]. The region is the only sub-region that has experienced a substantial absolute increase in child stunting in recent years [14], with an estimated increase of 3.1 million stunted children between 2012 and 2024 [12]. Persistent poverty, food insecurity, and limited access to health and education services continue to undermine progress [15, 16]. Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia were selected for this study due to their regional diversity, policy relevance, and high prevalence of child stunting. These countries are among those that exhibit a high burden of child stunting, with a prevalence of 38.4% in Ethiopia (36.8% in 2019), 37.1% in Malawi, 36.8% in Nigeria, and 34.6% in Zambia [17]. Poor health outcomes associated with stunting perpetuate intergenerational cycles of poverty and inequality, particularly in rural and underserved populations [18]. While several studies have examined stunting within individual countries [19–21], others have examined multiple countries [22, 23]. However, limited research has focused on these four countries or have examined household composition and living arrangements as key determinants of stunting.

Ethiopia (36.8%), Malawi (37.1%), Nigeria (36.8%), and Zambia (34.6%) are among the countries with the highest rates of stunting in Sub-Saharan Africa, ranking within the top 20 globally [17]. Approximately two out of every five Ethiopian children are stunted [24, 25], with severe food insecurity, low socioeconomic position, and limited maternal nutrition knowledge in rural areas contributing to this burden [26]. The Ethiopian government launched the Seqota Declaration in 2015, aiming to end child stunting by 2030, but recurrent droughts and weak multi-sectoral coordination have slowed progress [27]. Malawi has reduced stunting from 47% in 2010 to 37% in 2016 [28, 29], supported by the National Multisectoral Nutrition Policy and Strategic Plan (2025–2030). However, rural children remain disproportionately affected, mostly due to inadequate funding and fragmented program delivery, which limits effectiveness. Nigeria reports stunting prevalence of 37%, with rates peaking at 47% among children aged 24–35 months [30, 31]. Despite initiatives such as the Nutrition 774 program and UNICEF-supported interventions, weak health infrastructure, regional conflict, and low treatment coverage undermine impact [32]. Zambia has achieved reductions in stunting since the launch of its Scaling Up Nutrition (SUN) initiative, yet prevalence remains high at 40%, with male children more affected than females [33]. Rural-urban inequalities and uneven implementation across districts continue to challenge progress.

The inclusion of Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia was motivated by their regional diversity and policy relevance. Ethiopia represents East Africa, Malawi Southern Africa, Nigeria West Africa, and Zambia Central/Southern Africa, thereby capturing sub-regional variation in stunting determinants. These countries also rank among those with the highest stunting prevalence worldwide, making them critical cases for understanding household-level vulnerabilities. Furthermore, they differ in demographic profiles, health system capacity, and nutrition policy implementation, allowing for comparative insights that go beyond single-country analyses. Pooling data from these diverse contexts provides a broader understanding of how socio-demographic and household factors shape child stunting across sub-Saharan Africa. Household composition and living arrangements may influence child stunting through resource allocation and caregiving dynamics. The Resource Dilution Theory suggests that as the number of children in a household increases, each child may receive less attention, care, and nutritional support, which may increase the risk of stunting [34, 35]. In resource-limited settings, larger households and complex living arrangements may reduce the resources available per child, which may increase vulnerability to malnutrition. This theoretical framework is relevant in the sub-Saharan African context, where complex living arrangements and high fertility are common. This framework provides a lens for examining how household composition and living arrangements, together with other socio-demographic factors, determine child stunting.

This study seeks to inform targeted interventions and policies aimed at reducing stunting. The findings have the potential to guide policymakers in designing strategies that address household-level vulnerabilities, improve child health outcomes, and contribute to the achievement of global nutrition targets. Moreover, by examining living arrangements and co-residence typology, this study addresses an important gap in the literature and adds a familial and social dimension to existing literature on child malnutrition. The study aligns with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly Goal 2, which aims to end hunger, achieve food security, improve nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture [36]. It also supports the African Union’s Agenda 2063, which envisions a prosperous Africa based on inclusive growth and sustainable development [37]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the socio-demographic and household determinants of stunting in children under the age of five years across Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional secondary data analysis using nationally representative Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) data, conducted between 2015 and 2018, from four sub-Saharan countries. The selected countries were Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia.

Study population, sample, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The target population consisted of children under the age of five for whom information on stunting was collected. This comprised of de facto children under the age of five who were present in the household at the time of the survey and had valid height and age measurement. Children with missing or implausible anthropometric data were excluded. The sample size for the study was 38,277 children. Initially, the pooled data had 449,180 observations and 412,049 observations were excluded due to having issues with the height and age of the child. During data cleaning, a total of 55 observations were excluded due to having missing information on orphanhood status. Following these exclusions, the final analytical dataset contained no missing values across the variables included in the analysis. Therefore, no imputation or sensitivity analyses were conducted. Data, variables, and measurements

Data source and sampling procedures

The standard DHS data from four sub-Saharan African countries, conducted from 2015 to 2018, were pooled and included in this study. These DHS rounds were chosen because they provide comparable datasets collected around a similar time period and same DHS phase. Aligning the survey period and phase reduces temporal heterogeneity and improves comparability. In all four countries, the DHS used a two-stage stratified sampling design [29, 31, 38, 39]. Enumeration areas were selected in the first stage using probability proportional to size sampling, followed by a systematic selection of households in the second stage [29, 31, 38, 39].

Outcome variable

The outcome variable used in this study was child stunting. This variable is based on an anthropometric measure of children under the age of five. The outcome variable was derived based on height-for-age. Height-for-age z-scores were computed based on the World Health Organization’s (WHOs) 2006 Child Growth Standards. Children whose height-for-age z-score was below minus two standard deviations from the median were measured as stunted. The variable was dichotomised to reflect 1 as children who were stunted and 0 if not stunted.

Explanatory variables

The study included twelve explanatory variables. These included the age of the child (in months), sex of the child, mother’s education, orphanhood type, children under five in the household, co-residence typology, sex of household head, source of water for drinking, type of toilet facility, household wealth, place of residence, and country. Country was included as a fixed-effect covariate, in the regression model, to account for between-country differences. These variables were selected to reflect established pathways linking child stunting to caregiving resources, household socioeconomic position, and environmental exposures. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Stata version 16 [40]. Descriptive and bivariate analyses were used to measure the characteristics of the sample while inferential statistics, based on multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the independent associations. We fitted two multivariable models, (i) a living arrangements model focusing on orphanhood type, co-residence typology, and the number of under-five children in the household, and (ii) a fully adjusted model including all explanatory variables. Model diagnostics were performed to assess the adequacy of the fitted logistic regression model using a survey-adjusted Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (based on the user-written command svylogitgof) [41]. Country-specific sampling weights were normalised before pooling the data and were applied using the svyset command in Stata to account for clustering, stratification, and unequal probabilities of selection. This approach also adjusted for the complex survey design. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 and all associations were evaluated at p < 0.05. Furthermore, a multicollinearity test, using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), was conducted to test for collinearity between the explanatory factors. The analysis showed that there was no collinearity between the factors, with VIF values ranging from 1.00 to 1.74 (mean VIF = 1.22).

Prevalence of stunting among children under five

Table 2 presents the prevalence of child stunting, with a chi-square test, by background socio-demographic factors. The findings revealed that the age of child (in months), sex of child, mother’s education, number of children under five in the household, sex of household head, source of water for drinking, type of toilet facility, household wealth, and place of residence were statistically associated with child stunting in the selected countries (p < 0.05). Overall, approximately one-third (36.7%) of children were stunted. The findings revealed that child stunting was more prevalent among older children (i.e., those aged 18–59 months). Stunting was higher among children aged 24–35 months (45.4%) and was lowest among those aged 0–5 months (18.6%). Male children were more stunted (39.6%) than females (33.8%), while the prevalence decreased with maternal education, from 44.7% among children of mothers with no education vs. 14.4% among highly educated mothers). Stunting was higher among maternal orphans (43.9%) ()was 43.9%, while it was 36.6% among children who were not orphaned (both parents alive).

Moreover, the prevalence of stunting increased with the number of children under the age of five in the household. The prevalence of stunting among children who lived in households with three or more children under the age of five was 40.1%, while it was 35.1% among children who lived in households with one child under the age of five. Moreover, the prevalence of stunting among children who were not living with any parent was 39.3%, while it was 36.6% among children who were living with a parent. There was a slight difference between male-headed and female-headed households in the prevalence of child stunting. The prevalence of stunting among children from male-headed households was 36.9%, while it was 36.0% among children from female-headed households. Stunting prevalence was higher among children from households using unimproved water sources such as wells (41.6%) and among those without toilet facilities (39.8%), while lower among households using piped water (32.4%) and among those using flush toilets (20.9%). Stunting prevalence was higher among children from poor households (44.6%) and those from rural areas (40.0%), while it was lower among those from rich households (27.2%) and those from urban areas (28.2%). while it was 27.2% among children from rich households. Stunting prevalence varied modestly across countries, ranging from 34.6% in Zambia to 38.3% in Ethiopia.

Determinants of stunting among children under five

Table 3 presents the determinants of child stunting by living arrangements. This model adjusted for number of children under five in the household, co-residence typology, and sex of household head. The findings revealed that children who were paternal orphans had higher odds of stunting [AOR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.01–1.52] compared to those who were not orphans, although this association was statistically borderline. The odds of stunting increased with the number of children in the household. Children from households with three or more children under five years in the household had higher odds of stunting [AOR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.14–1.34] compared to those from households with one child under the age of five years in the household.

Concerning children’s living arrangements, the findings revealed that children from households with three or more children under the age of five had higher odds of stunting [AOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.07–1.27] compared to those from households with one child under the age of five in the household. Children who were not living with any parent had lower odds of stunting [AOR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.66–0.86] compared to those who were living with a parent. The results on source of water for drinking are unexpected; children from households that drank water from a borehole had lower odds of stunting [AOR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.79–0.98] compared to those from households that drank water from piped water. Moreover, children from households that drank water from the river had lower odds of stunting [AOR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.74–0.98] compared to those from households that drank water from piped water.

Concerning the type of toilet facility that the household has access to, children who lived in households with a flush toilet had lower odds of stunting [AOR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.69–0.93] than those who lived in households with no toilet facility. Children who lived in households with pit latrine toilets had higher odds of stunting [AOR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.11–1.32] than those who lived in households with no toilet facility. The findings further revealed that child stunting decreased with household wealth. Children from poor households had higher odds of stunting [AOR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.50–1.81] compared to children from rich households. Children from average-wealth households had higher odds of stunting [AOR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.11–1.34] compared to children from rich households. Concerning the country, children from Nigeria had higher odds of stunting [AOR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.13–1.35] compared to children from Zambia.

Discussions

This study provides multi-country evidence on the socio-demographic and household determinants of child stunting in four sub-Saharan countries and highlights how structural factors such as household composition, mother’s education, and socioeconomic conditions shape child nutrition. These factors interact within the resource-constrained households to reinforce inequalities in access to nutrition, care, and health-related resources. This study extends on prior research that has mostly focused on child, maternal, and socioeconomic characteristics by emphasising the caregiving context (living arrangements) and potential resource allocation within households. The findings also revealed that approximately one-third of children were stunted in the selected countries. Studies conducted in these nations found comparable results [33, 42]. At the multivariable level, we found that, consistent with previous research, the odds of stunting were relatively higher among older children and lower among younger children, which reflects cumulative nutritional deficits during early childhood rather than age-specific effects [43]. Lower stunting among younger children may reflect the protective effects of breastfeeding in infancy, whereas age-specific increase in exposure to inadequate complementary feeding and infections likely contribute to growth faltering in later childhood [44, 45].

We also found a relationship between a child’s sex and stunting, where male children had a higher probability of stunting compared to their female counterparts. This finding is in line with studies by Quamme and Iversen [14], Sambu [43], Thurstans and colleagues [45], and Nomura and colleagues [46], which also found that male children had a higher risk of stunting than females. This pattern reflects a combination of biological susceptibility and feeding practices. To explain why male children are more likely to be stunted than female children, evidence indicates that in many low-income countries, the duration of exclusive breastfeeding is significantly shorter for boys than for girls [47], thus making boys more susceptible to morbidity than girls. Moreover, cultural norms in some low-income settings may result in shorter durations of exclusive breastfeeding for boys than girls, potentially contributing to the observed sex disparities [48–50].

The findings further revealed that stunting decreased with the mother’s education. Children of mothers with primary and lower levels of education were more likely to be stunted compared to those whose mothers had higher levels of education. This finding is consistent with the findings from a study by Yani and colleagues [51], which showed an association between low levels of parental education, particularly maternal education, and child stunting. Educated mothers are more likely to prioritise their health, practice healthy feeding habits, and engage in beneficial activities during pregnancy and lactation [52]. Wand and colleagues [4] also noted that women with less than primary education were linked to hunger and food insecurity. Sarwar and colleagues [53] emphasised that a mother’s educational level significantly influences a child’s nutritional status. These findings emphasise the importance of investing in female education (SDG 4) and empowering women to improve child health outcomes, which have benefits for child nutrition and contribute to achieving zero hunger (SDG 2). Although orphanhood status was not a predictor of stunting when considering all the selected factors, when only considering the living arrangements, the findings showed that children who were paternal orphans were more likely to be stunted compared to those who were not orphans. This pattern points to economic and caregiving disruption following a father’s death, consistent with previous research which shows that orphaned children have higher rates of stunting and other forms of malnutrition [54–57]. In many settings, loss of the father reduces household income and care continuity, which can lead to food insecurity, lower diet quality, and reduced access to preventative health services [16, 58, 59].

The findings highlighted the importance of children’s living arrangements in determining child stunting. The number of children under five in the household and co-residence typology were key factors determining child stunting. Children from households with three or more children under the age of five were more likely to be stunted compared to those from households with one child under the age of five in the household. The probability of child stunting increased with the number of children under the age of five in the household. This finding aligns with findings from a previous study [60]. This is consistent with the Resource Dilution Theory, whereby as the number of young children in the household increases, the available resources tend to be thinly distributed among household members. In low- and middle-income countries, larger households often experience resource constraints, reduced caregiving quality, and increased exposure to infectious diseases, all of which may contribute to a higher prevalence of stunting [61]. In resource-constrained settings, these dynamics may also heighten competition for the limited nutritional and caregiving resources, thereby increasing children’s vulnerability to stunting.

Surprisingly, children not living with any parent were less likely to be stunted. This finding contrasts with evidence from Botswana, where children not living with any parent had higher odds of stunting compared to those living with both parents [62]. An explanation for this difference lies in the contextual differences in household vulnerability and caregiving arrangements. In many African contexts, child fostering within extended household settings is an established custom [62–64], where children may be placed in households with greater economic stability and caregiving capacity. In resource-constrained settings such as those examined in this study, children who are not living with parents may be placed within extended households that have better caregiving capacity and access to resources. These types of arrangements may protect children from economic shocks and caregiving disruptions after the death or migration of parent or primary caregiver. There is a need for longitudinal studies to establish the causal pathways linking orphanhood, household composition, and child nutritional outcomes.

The findings showed a relationship between the source of drinking water and child stunting. Children from households whose source of water for drinking was a borehole or a river were less likely to be stunted compared to those from households whose source of water for drinking was piped water. These associations should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect contextual or behavioural factors that are not captured in the DHS inherent differences in water quality. This result contrasts with studies that found higher stunting rates among children with unimproved water sources [65, 66]. Improved sanitation facilities, such as flush toilets, tend to be associated with reduced stunting [67, 68]. In low-resource settings, piped water systems may be prone to contamination or intermittent supply, leading to a higher demand for alternative water sources. In addition, households using boreholes and rivers may adopt coping practices such as boiling or filtering water before consumption. These findings underline the importance of addressing water quality and sanitation infrastructure as part of broader public health initiatives.

Regarding the type of toilet facility that the household has access to, children from households with a flush toilet were less likely to be stunted compared to those from households with no toilet facility. In contrast, children from households with a pit latrine toilet were more likely to be stunted compared to those from households with no toilet facility. These findings are consistent with other studies that found children living in households with unimproved toilet facilities had a higher probability of stunting than those with improved facilities [67, 68]. Similar patterns have been documented in South Asia, where flush toilets and piped sewerage were associated with lower stunting in children [69]. Improved sanitation is known to reduce children’s exposure to intestinal pathogens, which is a major driver of growth faltering in low-resource settings. While these biological pathways are well documented, our findings highlight their continued relevance in contexts where sanitation access remains closely related to household socioeconomic status.

Moreover, the study revealed that child stunting decreased with household wealth. Children from poor households were more likely to be stunted compared to children from rich households. Children from average-wealth households were more likely to be stunted compared to children from rich households. Evidence from developing countries consistently shows that household wealth is a strong predictor of child nutritional outcomes, mostly through its influence on food security, dietary diversity, and access to healthcare services [59, 70–74]. Economic disadvantage limits households’ ability to provide nutritionally adequate diets, timely healthcare, and safe living environments for children, which increases the risk of stunting and other forms of undernutrition.

The study further revealed significant country-level differences in the odds of stunting, with children from Nigeria being more likely to be stunted compared to those from Zambia. Previous studies conducted in developing countries have documented sub-national variations in child stunting, reflecting differences in national nutrition strategies, health system capacity, and socioeconomic [75, 76]. While Nigeria has implemented several nutrition-related policies, such as National Policy on Food and Nutrition and the recent Nutrition 774 initiative, persistent challenges exist; these include political instability, economic challenges, and inadequate healthcare infrastructure, which contribute to higher child stunting [77]. In contrast, Zambia’s lower stunting rates may result from more effective nutrition programs, child feeding practices, and better access to healthcare services, partly through the SUN initiative. Nonetheless, stunting prevalence in Zambia remains substantial especially in rural contexts, which indicates uneven progress within the country [39]. Further research is needed to explore the specific factors contributing to these country-level differences and to identify best practices that can be applied across different contexts. Understanding these contextual differences is important for tailoring interventions to country-specific needs.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings of this study have important implications for research and practice. Firstly, they highlight the need for integrated and multisectoral approaches that prioritise key determinants of child stunting, including maternal education, living arrangements, household wealth, and access to safe water and sanitation. Strengthening female education should be a priority, as educated mothers are better positioned to better access health information, use healthcare services, and adopt suitable child-feeding practices. Moreover, child nutrition and social protection policies should recognise the role played by extended households, especially in contexts with high orphanhood and complex household structures. Finally, the cross-country differences in stunting emphasise that uniform strategies may be ineffective across diverse contexts. Although this study offers a pooled regional perspective, policies aimed at addressing child stunting must be context-specific and grounded in country-level and sub-national household and caregiving realities. Policymakers and practitioners should therefore design interventions that are responsive to unique socio-economic, cultural, and environmental factors driving stunting.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the study include the use of large and nationally representative data from Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia, which enhances the generalisability of the findings. The inclusion of four countries across Eastern, Southern, and Western Africa supports regional diversity while but may limit broader generalisability to other regions. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified determinants and stunting. Moreover, the data relies on caregiver recall for household and child characteristics, which may introduce recall bias. Moreover, it is important to note that pooled analyses may mask substantial sub-national variation in stunting prevalence as well as policy implementation. Furthermore, the absence of longitudinal data prevents the assessment of growth trajectories and temporal pathways linking household conditions to child stunting.

Conclusion

This study provides multi-country evidence on the socio-demographic determinants of stunting in children under the age of five in Ethiopia, Malawi, Nigeria, and Zambia. The findings highlight the importance of addressing key factors such as maternal education, household wealth, living arrangements, and access to safe water and sanitation in shaping nutritional outcomes among children. By incorporating co-residence typology and household living arrangements, including household composition, this study extends existing literature and underscores the importance of household structure in child health. The findings underline the need for targeted and context-specific interventions that prioritise mothers education, poverty reduction, better living arrangements, and improved access to basic services, in shaping child nutritional outcomes. Future research should (i) explore the intersection of biological susceptibility and caregiving practices to provide targeted recommendations for reducing sex-based differences in stunting, (ii) investigate the specific mechanisms through which paternal orphanhood affects child stunting, (iii) examine the specific water-related factors contributing to stunting in these contexts, and (iv) employ longitudinal study designs to better capture the changes in household structure and caregiving over time in relation to child nutrition.
