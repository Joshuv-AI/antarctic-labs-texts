Agentic AI in oncology: autonomous workflows for clinical decision support and evidence synthesis
Authors: Saurav Kumar Mishra, Pragya Rai, John J. Georrge, Hamadou Mamoudou
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into oncology has recently undergone a significant conceptual shift, moving beyond static predictive modeling toward autonomous, goal-directed execution. While large foundational models have demonstrably advanced knowledge synthesis and structural biology, the emergence of Agentic AI–systems capable of reasoning, iterative planning, and independent tool use via large language models (LLMs)–represents a distinct functional paradigm. This transition from assistive algorithm to autonomous “scientist-in-the-loop” warrants rigorous examination in the context of precision oncology workflows[1,2].

The operational utility of Agentic frameworks is no longer theoretical but is evidenced by recent empirical validations in clinical decision support and evidence curation. In breast oncology, multi-agent architectures integrating retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) have demonstrated superior fidelity to established guidelines. In a study comparing Agentic-RAG against Graph-RAG and generic LLM implementations for National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline adherence, the Agentic workflow achieved 100% concordance with no hallucinated data or erroneous interpretation[3]. This precision stems from the division of cognitive labor among specialized agents tasked with retrieval, validation, and repair of unstructured clinical data. The exclusive use of synthetic patient charts allows rigorous benchmarking but fails to represent the heterogeneity, incompleteness, and sociodemographic confounders of real-world electronic health data[4]. Before making reliability claims, prospective validation on heterogeneous, multi-institutional clinical data is needed to avoid overfitting to synthetic distributions and algorithmic bias from LLM training corpora.

Beyond point-of-care decision support, the capacity of Agentic systems to compress the timeline of evidence synthesis is equally salient. The deployment of an LLM-driven agent for maintaining a living systematic review in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) illustrates a step-change in scalability. The system autonomously decomposed complex review tasks, annotated over 2,136 studies with >90% accuracy relative to human specialists, and synthesized a living decision support tool (DST) in less than 1% of the time required for conventional Cochrane-compliant methodology[5]. This approach circumvents the latency inherent in manual curation, enabling real-time integration of trial data with regulatory and guideline frameworks. Moreover, concerning the involvement of AI, the TITAN guideline is serving as a promising benchmark for the transparency standard[6]. Generalizability beyond the single-disease situation is unknown; however, a 10% annotation error rate propagated throughout a dynamic evidence base may bring clinically substantial mistakes in therapy recommendations. AI’s role in such jobs highlights the importance of the TITAN guideline for transparent reporting, which evaluates AI-driven evidence synthesis[6].

Integration issues hinder the translation of promising demonstrations into clinical practice. Autonomous agents need robust interoperability standards and real-time data pipelines to operate in older health information systems not designed for AI-native processes. Regulatory frameworks, such as the FDA’s Software as a Medical Device paradigm and emerging AI-specific recommendations, must define accountability when autonomous agents make treatment decisions, especially in multi-agent ensembles where suggestion provenance is unclear. Another barrier to clinician adoption is poor faith in “black-box” reasoning, and without future clinical utility trials proving safety, equity, and workflow compatibility, the adoption curve will remain shallow. While the TITAN guideline is important for reporting transparency, it cannot replace prospective, end-user-centered validation, the gold standard for clinical decision support adoption.

The broader trajectory suggests that Agentic AI is evolving into a collaborative ecosystem of computational specialists capable of tackling the multimodal, high-dimensional complexity of cancer biology, from hypothesis generation to biomarker identification and iterative drug design[7]. However, the validation of autonomous reasoning remains nontrivial. The path toward clinical translation is contingent not only on algorithmic robustness but also on the establishment of verifiable governance structures that address reproducibility, scalability, and the mitigation of silent failures in autonomous reasoning chains[8]. A critical discourse on the design characteristics and ethical guardrails governing these autonomous systems is both timely and necessary for the readership of the journal.
