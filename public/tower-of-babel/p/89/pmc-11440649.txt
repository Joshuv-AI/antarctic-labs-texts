Artificial Meshed Vessel-Induced Dimensional Breaking Growth of Human Brain Organoids and Multiregional Assembloids
Authors: Lei Xu, Haibo Ding, Shanshan Wu, Nankun Xiong, Yuan Hong, Wanying Zhu, Xingyi Chen, Xiao Han, Mengdan Tao, Yuanhao Wang, Da Wang, Min Xu, Da Huo, Zhongze Gu, Yan Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain organoids are widely used to model brain development and diseases. However, a major challenge in their application is the insufficient supply of oxygen and nutrients to the core region, restricting the size and maturation of the organoids. In order to vascularize brain organoids and enhance the nutritional supply to their core areas, two-photon polymerization (TPP) 3D printing is employed to fabricate high-resolution meshed vessels in this study. These vessels made of photoresist with densely distributed micropores with a diameter of 20 μm on the sidewall, are cocultured with brain organoids to facilitate the diffusion of culture medium into the organoids. The vascularized organoids exhibit dimensional breaking growth and enhanced proliferation, reduced hypoxia and apoptosis, suggesting that the 3D-printed meshed vessels partially mimic vascular function to promote the culture of organoids. Furthermore, cortical, striatal and medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) organoids are respectively differentiated to generate Cortico-Striatal-MGE assembloids by 3D-printed vessels. The enhanced migration, projection and excitatory signaling transduction are observed between different brain regional organoids in the assembloids. This study presents an approach using TPP 3D printing to construct vascularized brain organoids and assembloids for enhancing the development and assembly, offering a research model and platform for neurological diseases.Keywords: TPP 3D printing, vascularized organoids, artificial vessels, biomaterial engineering organoids, brain organoids

Introduction

Brain organoids derived from human stem cells recapitulate the structural and functional features of human brain, and brain region-specific organoids could be further fused to form assembloids for studying neuronal migration and neural circuit. Brain organoids and assembloids are extensively utilized in exploration of brain development, human evolution, neurological disorders and drug screening. Nevertheless, the absence of blood vessels hampers the provision of oxygen and nutrients to organoids and assembloids, leading to necrosis in the core region and restricting the growth and maturation of brain organoids and assembloids in long-term culture,1 which limits the application in the studies of neurological diseases and drug screening. Thus, vascularization of brain organoids and assembloids is essential.

Efforts have been made to improve the nutrient delivery in brain organoids. Recently, microfluidics have been utilized in organoids culture to control dynamic flow, but this approach was often limited by high-throughput production and lack of a real three-dimensional (3D) culture environment.2 Another approach to achieve vascularized brain organoids is to directly coculture vascular cells with brain organoids for modeling the interaction between neural cells and vascular cells.3−6 However, complete vascular lumen structures have not been observed in the vascularized brain organoids.7 In addition, vascular cells and brain organoids could not share the same culture medium throughout the full-process differentiation. Therefore, consistently generation of vascularized brain organoids with lumen structures is desired.

3D printing is an advanced technology that enables the fabrication of biological structures with intricate architecture and functionality, closely resembling native tissues.8−10 It is worth noting that 3D printing could achieve high-throughput production and generate desired lumen structures for utilization in any culture media in a 3D environment. Despite the significant advancements in 3D printing, the challenges of creating micropores of 100 μm or smaller in vascular structures persists.11 Two-photon polymerization (TPP) 3D printing technology, a light-assisted printing method based on laser direct writing (LDW),12 has emerged as a solution. The ability of TPP to achieve high resolution beyond diffraction limits enables it to print intricate microporous patterns in 3D tubular structures for modeling microscale vascular systems.12,13 However, TPP 3D printing has not yet been applied in brain organoids. Therefore, the strategy of TPP 3D printing might become a solution to achieve vascularized brain organoids and assembloids.

In our study, we demonstrate the construction of vessels using TPP 3D printing. Moreover, 3D-printed meshed vessels are biocompatible for long-term coculture with brain organoids. The vascularized organoids exhibit dimensional breaking growth, increased proliferation, alleviated hypoxia, and reduced apoptosis. Furthermore, we generate vascularized Cortico-Striatal-MGE three brain region assembloids with a predefined spatial arrangement. Our study introduces an advanced avenue for engineering complex brain organoids and assembloids, offering a platform for studying brain development, neurological diseases and drug screening.

Results and Discussion

Design and Fabrication of the 3D-Printed Vessels

One of the major bottlenecks of brain organoids is the limited internal nutrient supply that subsequently forms necrotic cores to constraint the scalability of organoids during a long-term culture (Figure 1 and Figure S1a-e).14 In order to enhance the nutrient supply in the core region of brain organoids, we fabricated a 3D-printed vessel from a photoresist IP-S by TPP 3D printing technology. Before applying 3D-printed vessels in the culture of organoids, we first simulated the effect of 3D-printed vessels on the diffusion of culture medium into organoids by multiphysics simulation software (Figure S1f, g). The simulation results showed that the presence of 3D-printed vessels enhanced the glucose distribution and availability within the organoid (Figure S1f, g). In addition, the simulation results suggested that the lactate concentration, which is metabolized from glucose, within the organoid reached its minimum when the micropore diameter of the 3D-printed vessel sidewall was set at 20 μm (5.6131 mol/m3 in I and 5.4745 mol/m3 in II), indicating the most favorable metabolite clearance effect (Figure S1g, h and Tables S1–2).

Conceptual drawing of the scheme for 3D printing vascularized brain organoids. Organoids grow larger during long-term culture, leading to the formation of necrotic cores in the central region of organoids due to insufficient nutrient supply. The process of forming necrotic cores in brain organoids was improved by 3D-printed meshed vessels.

Based on the simulation results, we optimized the design and specifications of the 3D-printed vessels to align with the meshed characteristics of the human brain microvasculature (Figure 2a).15,16 Considering the diameters of the central (260–280 μm) and peripheral (150–180 μm) cortical arteries,16 we set a vessel diameter of 200 μm and incorporated 20 μm micropores on the vessel sidewall to model the meshed capillary in the brain (Figure 2b). The length of each vessel segment was adjusted to 2–3 mm, corresponding to the size of the cultured brain organoids.17−19 3D-printed vessels without micropores on the sidewall were designed as a control to eliminate the possibility of the vessel material influencing brain organoid development.

Artificial control vessels and 3D-printed vessels were obtained using a photoresist IP-S after two-photon polymerization and photoresist development (Figure 2c). The morphology of the 3D-printed vessels was examined using light and electron microscopy (Figure 2d, e), confirming their adherence to our design specifications. Moreover, we also examined the morphology of control vessels without micropores on the sidewalls (Figure 2e, f). Taken together, we designed vessels based on the cortical meshed microvasculature and fabricated them made of photoresist for organoid culture by TPP 3D printing.

Generation of 3D Printing Vascularized Brain Organoids

To obtain brain organoids suitable for coculture with 3D-printed vessels, we modified the protocol of cerebral organoid generation as previously reported (Figure 3a).17−20 We embedded hPSC-derived brain organoids in Matrigel at day 7 to promote the formation of neural epithelial cells. By day 30 of culture, we initiated the coculture of the 3D-printed vessels and control vessels with the brain organoids to facilitate the perfusion of culture medium for vascularization of organoids (Figure 3a). Here, the control (cocultured with control vessels), default (without artificial vessels) and 3D printing vascularized organoids (cocultured with 3D-printed meshed vessels) were obtained and then cultured until day 90 to achieve long-term culture.

Throughout the extended culture period, the organoids increased in size (Figure 3b). Notably, the vascularized organoids, cocultured with 3D-printed meshed vessels from day 30, demonstrated dimensional breaking growth compared to the control and default organoids (Figure 3b). Specifically, there was a significant increase in the size of vascularized organoids compared to other groups at day 60 (vascularized: 2.76 ± 0.60 mm2, control: 2.19 ± 0.51 mm2, default: 2.04 ± 0.66 mm2) (Figure 3c, d). Additionally, we quantified the surface complexity of the organoids by counting inflection points and calculating circularity at day 60 (Figure 3e).21 The vascularized organoids displayed more inflection points (vascularized: 16.92 ± 2.39, control: 10.43 ± 1.83, default: 8.83 ± 3.52) and lower circularity (vascularized: 0.80 ± 0.08, control: 0.88 ± 0.06, default: 0.88 ± 0.08) than the control and default organoids (Figure 3f, g). The higher surface complexity of vascularized organoids indicated an enhanced expansion of neural epithelial cells with the 3D-printed vessels.

We next assessed the biocompatibility of 3D-printed vessels. There was no significant difference in cell apoptosis between the vascularized organoids (25.62 ± 3.19%) and the controls (control: 31.59 ± 4.51%; default: 29.37 ± 5.87%) at day 35. However, the vascularized organoids showed significantly less cell apoptosis at day 60 (vascularized: 20.60 ± 9.69%, control: 32.49 ± 9.87%, default: 33.79 ± 3.60%). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in apoptosis between the periphery of 3D-printed vessels and the entire field of view (Figure S2a, b). Compared with the control and default organoids, there was no difference in cell viability of vascularized organoids (vascularized: 1.08 ± 0.09, control: 1.08 ± 0.08, default: 1.00 ± 0.03), as measured by the CCK-8 assay at day 35 (Figure S2c). These results suggested that 3D-printed vessels and the process of coculturing did not cause significant damage to the growth of organoids.

We further investigated whether 3D-printed meshed vessels influenced the expression of organoid biomarkers by immunofluorescence staining. The presence of FOXG1 (vascularized: 58.36 ± 3.80%, control: 58.40 ± 4.13%, default: 58.13 ± 4.64%) confirmed the forebrain identity of the vascularized organoids and the expression of PAX6 (vascularized: 55.17 ± 3.81%, control: 49.85 ± 4.81%, default: 52.09 ± 5.69%) revealed abundant dorsal telencephalic progenitors in vascularized organoids at day 60. The higher expression of KI67 (vascularized: 25.46 ± 1.60%, control: 20.74 ± 1.96%, default: 21.89 ± 2.67%) at day 60 indicated that the proliferation of vascularized organoids had been enhanced at early time points, which might be a potential mechanism for the continuous increase in size of vascularized organoids. The expression of newborn neuron marker DCX suggested ongoing neurogenesis in the vascularized organoids. Additionally, cortical deep-layer neuronal markers TBR1 (vascularized: 30.09 ± 4.61%, control: 29.76 ± 5.66%, default: 30.18 ± 5.05%) and CTIP2 (vascularized: 40.80 ± 4.16%, control: 40.32 ± 4.48%, default: 39.89 ± 3.21%) indicated the differentiation and acquisition of cortical organoids, while neuronal markers TUJ1 and NeuN (vascularized: 31.20 ± 5.22%, control: 27.53 ± 5.39%, default: 28.43 ± 4.37%) signified abundant mature neurons in the vascularized organoids at day 60 (Figure 3h, i). Histological analysis of organoids with a 3D-printed vessel was performed (Figure 3j), and the results demonstrated that cells grew well around the 3D-printed vessel, indicating that the 3D-printed vessel was biocompatible for brain organoids.

Furthermore, we detected the biocompatibility of 3D-printed vessels in long-term culture by immunofluorescence staining. The results revealed the persistence of typical rosette structures and expression of the apical surface marker PKC-λ in vascularized organoids group at day 90 (Figure 3k). Additionally, vascularized organoids expressed the higher proliferation marker KI67 (vascularized: 24.88 ± 2.26%, control: 21.10 ± 2.15%, default: 21.63 ± 3.34%) and intermediate progenitor cell marker PAX6 (vascularized: 49.74 ± 4.14%, control: 46.86 ± 3.95%, default: 47.84 ± 4.59%), indicating that vascularized organoids still have proliferative ability in long-term culture. The expression of cortical deep-layer neuronal marker TBR1 (vascularized: 29.16 ± 4.79%, control: 29.45 ± 4.24%, default: 30.70 ± 5.45%), and cortical upper-layer neuronal marker SATB2 (vascularized: 15.00 ± 2.57%, control: 14.73 ± 2.67%, default: 15.67 ± 2.61%) suggested continuous cortical differentiation of vascularized brain organoids (Figure 3k, l). Moreover, we also observed GFAP-positive astrocytes around 3D-printed vessels and in the lateral regions of vascularized brain organoids. Notably, astrocytes in vascularized organoids were observed to line up on the walls of 3D-printed vessels, and tend to have a similar arrangement to astrocytes around the native blood vessels (Figure 3m).

Taken together, we generated 3D printing vascularized brain organoids, and found that biocompatible 3D-printed vessels displayed a structure similar to the native blood vessels after long-term culture in brain organoids.

Improved Culture in Long-Term Cultured 3D Printing Vascularized Organoids

To determine whether 3D-printed vessels have enabled the culture medium to infiltrate into brain organoids, we exposed the 90-day organoids to fluorescent tracer FITC-Dextran for 2 h (Figure 4a). The results revealed that the percentage of FITC-positive areas in the total cross-sectional area of vascularized organoids was significantly higher (47.24 ± 17.10%) than that of the control (14.73 ± 4.49%) and default organoids (16.51 ± 4.36%) (Figure 4b, c). This suggested that 3D-printed vessels allowed culture media to diffuse to the core of brain organoids, which may establish a nutrient transport pathway.

Next, we detected hypoxia, proliferation, and apoptosis in vascularized organoids to determine if 3D-printed vessel could increase the size and decrease the apoptosis in vascularized brain organoids. Our results showed a significant increase in the size of 3D printing vascularized organoids (Figure 3b, d). Subsequently, we incubated the organoids for 2 h in a medium containing Hypoxyprobe-1, and we found that the average fluorescence intensity of Hypoxyprobe-1 inside the vascularized organoids (46.25 ± 13.18) was significantly lower than that of the control (57.33 ± 14.53) and default organoids (56.71 ± 12.82) (Figure 4d, e). This indicated that 3D-printed vessels could improve the oxygen supply inside brain organoids.

We then analyzed cell apoptosis within the organoids. Using TUNEL staining, we observed a significant reduction in the number of apoptotic cells per unit area in the vascularized organoids (vascularized: 395.69 ± 152.18/mm2, control: 933.72 ± 246.53/mm2, default: 1033.62 ± 261.06/mm2) (Figure 4f, g). Furthermore, we discovered the enhanced proliferation in long-term cultured vascularized organoids, as evidenced by increased expression of the proliferation marker KI67 (vascularized: 44.79 ± 8.97%, control: 29.19 ± 16.50%, default: 25.83 ± 10.86%) (Figure 4h, i). These results indicated that 3D-printed vessels improved proliferation and reduced apoptosis in brain organoids by ameliorating hypoxia.

Increased Astrocytes and Enhanced Calcium Signaling in 3D Printing Vascularized Organoids

To further explore the function of vascularized brain organoids, we employed calcium imaging to assess the brain organoids at day 90 using the calcium indicator dye Fluo-4 AM (Figure 5a).22 By loading Fluo-4 AM onto the cells, we monitored the fluctuation of intracellular calcium over time through fluorescence intensity stimulated by 67 mM KCl (Figure 5a). Our findings indicated that the peak value of Ca2+(Fmax-F0)/F0 in the vascularized organoids was higher (vascularized: 1.24 ± 0.88, control: 0.88 ± 0.45, default: 0.84 ± 0.64) (Figure 5b, c). Furthermore, we implemented patch-clamp to record the action potential (AP) under current clamp and potassium and sodium currents under voltage clamp of organoids at day 70–80 (Figure 5d). The results showed that APs were more readily evoked in the vascularized organoids than controls (vascularized, 89% cells; control, 80% cells; default, 80% cells). Moreover, the APs recorded in the vascularized organoids exhibited larger amplitudes (vascularized: 46.21 ± 6.87 mV, control: 35.11 ± 6.42 mV, default: 37.65 ± 4.58 mV) and reduced half-widths (vascularized: 5.09 ± 1.41 ms, control: 7.50 ± 2.48 ms, default: 7.31 ± 2.47 ms), revealing stronger electrical activity (Figure 5e-g). In addition, vascularized organoids exhibit increased inward sodium current (vascularized: 911.78 ± 149.67 pA, control: 735.94 ± 131.92 pA, default: 763.25 ± 126.30 pA) and decreased potassium current (vascularized: 811.50 ± 157.26 pA, control: 1024.65 ± 93.28 pA, default: 994.85 ± 109.00 pA) (Figure 5h-j). These results confirmed a higher maturity of vascularized organoids.

In order to investigate whether the transcriptome of 3D printing vascularized organoids had changed, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (Bulk-seq) analysis of both vascularized and control organoids at day 75, which is the time point that we observed the significant size changes (Figure S3). By analyzing the Bulk-seq data of vascularized organoids and control, we found downregulation of GABAergic neurons (0.07-fold decrease in GABRA1 and 0.03-fold decrease in GABRA6) and potassium ion-related pathways (0.14-fold decrease in KCNK2, 0.18-fold decrease in KCNV, 0.10-fold decrease in KCNH5, 0.19-fold decrease in KCNA3 and 0.18-fold decrease in KCNMB2), while neurogenesis (85.47-fold increase in FZD10, 129.35-fold increase in IRX6, 178.68-fold increase in WNT2, 221.09-fold increase in LHX1 and 334.16-fold increase in RUNX3), angiogenesis (154.59-fold increase in FLT1, 232.02-fold increase in BMP4, 245.23-fold increase in S100A1, 353.85-fold increase in TWIST1 and 449.72-fold increase in GATA6), and calcium signaling-related pathways (25.98-fold increase in TRPV4, 38.02-fold increase in S1PR4, 38.75-fold increase in PTGER2, 71.93-fold increase in CALCA and 391.23-fold increase in AGTR1) were upregulated using gene ontology (GO) analysis (Figure S3d, e).

Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed transcriptional activation of the WNT pathway (49.38-fold increase in WNT1, 57.70-fold increase in WNT8B, 85.47-fold increase in FZD10, 136.92-fold increase in SOST and 178.68-fold increase in WNT2) and downregulation of GABAergic synaptic-related pathways (0.01-fold decrease in GAD2, 0.02-fold decrease in SLC32A1, 0.03-fold decrease in GABRA6, 0.07-fold decrease in GABRA1 and 0.17-fold decrease in SLC6A1) in the vascularized organoids (Figure S3f, g), indicating the ventral differentiation of vascularized organoids might be inhibited.

Based on the activation of the WNT pathway and the downregulation of GABAergic neuron-related pathways in Bulk-seq data of vascularized organoids and control, we hypothesize that the cell types of vascularized brain organoids may differ from that of the control group. To further investigate their cellular composition, we examined the sequencing data for typical biomarkers associated with various cell types in brain organoids. Our analysis revealed that vascularized organoids exhibited upregulation of astrocytes (13.20-fold increase in GFAP and 2.83-fold increase in VIM), epithelial cells (17.36-fold increase in VTN, 13.28-fold increase in ITM2A and 15.41-fold increase in CLDN5), neural stem cells (2.64-fold increase in FTL), intermediate progenitor cells (2.33-fold increase in EOMES), and excitatory cortical neurons (2.81-fold increase in NEUROD6 and 10.23-fold increase in RELN) (Figure 5k). In contrast, inhibitory GABAergic interneurons were significantly reduced (0.21-fold decrease in GAD1, 0.01-fold decrease in GAD2, 0.03-fold decrease in DLX1, 0.04-fold decrease in DLX2, 0.01-fold decrease in DLX5, 0.03-fold decrease in DLX6 and 0.27-fold decrease in TAC1) (Figure 5k). The increased astrocytes suggested further maturation of the vascularized organoids, which aligns with previous reports on biological approaches for vascularized organoids.4,5,23 The increase in excitatory cortical neurons and the decrease in inhibitory GABAergic neurons might indicate inhibition of ventral differentiation in the 3D printing vascularized organoids, with a greater inclination toward the dorsal cortex in terms of cell fate determination.

Furthermore, we conducted a comparative analysis of our organoid sequencing data with the human brain post- conceptional weeks (PCW) 8–16 data from the BrainSpan database. Our findings revealed that both the differentiated vascularized and control organoids at day 75 exhibited closer resemblance to the 9 pcw human brain (Figure 5l). Building upon this, we further compared our data with various brain regions of the 9 pcw human brain and observed that the differentiated control organoids showed closer similarity to the neocortex, hippocampus, and the ganglionic eminences (GE) region, whereas the vascularized organoids were primarily similar to the neocortex and hippocampus (Figure 5m). These results suggested that the activation of the WNT pathway during the development of vascularized brain organoids might contribute to the observed suppression of ventral differentiation,24−28 thereby confirming our previous speculation regarding 3D printing vascularized brain organoids.

According to transcriptome sequencing results, an increase in astrocytes was observed in 3D printing vascularized brain organoids. To validate this finding, we performed immunofluorescence staining of the astrocyte biomarker GFAP in long-term cultured brain organoids at day 90. Our analysis revealed that the area occupied by GFAP was larger in the vascularized organoids (7.28 ± 6.71%) than controls (4.21 ± 4.53%) and default organoids (4.96 ± 3.61%) (Figure 5n, o). The increase of astrocytes suggested further maturation of vascularized brain organoids, which was in agreement with the transcriptome analysis.29

Generation of 3D Printing Vascularized Cortico-Striatal-MGE Assembloids

In order to further explore the application scope of 3D-printed meshed vessels, we attempted to apply them to multiregion assembloids, which require more nutritional supply as the larger size. To address this challenge, we developed a method that utilizes 3D-printed meshed vessels as scaffolds to generate complex assembloids that recapitulate key features of the developing forebrain (Figure 6a). During brain development, MGE-derived GABAergic interneuron progenitors tangentially migrate into the striatum and cortex, and the cortex projects to the striatum to form cortico-striatal connections.30−33 Thus, we respectively generated the cortical, striatal, and MGE organoids as previously described by our laboratory (Figure 6b),17,18,34 and 3D-printed meshed vessels were employed to guide the fusion of three brain regions organoids to generate vascularized assembloids for exploring migration and projection in brain development (Figure 6c).

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to determine the three types of organoids cultured at day 45, revealing distinct marker expression patterns. Human cortical organoids (hCOs) expressed cortical deep-layer neuronal markers (CTIP2: 40.45 ± 3.92%, TBR1: 31.00 ± 6.48% and BRN2: 23.75 ± 2.48%), striatal organoids (hSOs) expressed lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE) (Meis2: 71.87 ± 9.23% and GSH2: 48.26 ± 7.33%) and medium spiny neurons (MSN) markers (DARPP32: 65.05 ± 3.80% and CTIP2: 66.19 ± 5.34%), while MGE organoids (hMOs) expressed MGE (Nkx2.1: 65.30 ± 17.73%) and GABAergic neuronal markers (GABA: 67.53 ± 11.13% and GAD67: 64.73 ± 11.33%) (Figure S4). These quantitative data supported the distinct regional identities of the organoids, providing a foundation for the subsequent functional assays in the assembloids.

MGE generates most cortical and striatal interneurons,31,35 and these interneurons migrate from MGE to dorsal cortex and striatum tangentially.36−40 Therefore, we labeled hMOs with an adeno-associated virus (AAV) driving enhanced GFP expression from a human synapsin (hSyn) enhancer (pAAV-hSyn-EGFP-3 × FLAG-WPRE) at day 30 to investigate the migration of MGE cells in brain development. At day 37, the three types of organoids were sequentially fused using 3D-printed meshed vessels. We observed a significant cell migration from the hMO to the hSO and hCO at day 40 (Figure 6d-f). Vascularized assembloids showed increased migrated MGE cells from hMO to hSO (<50 μm distance from hMO: 92.60 ± 10.55; > 50 μm distance from hMO: 109.80 ± 18.06), compared to the control (nonvascularized assembloids) (<50 μm distance from hMO: 67.00 ± 16.84; > 50 μm distance from hMO: 68.40 ± 6.35) (Figure 6g, h).

Neural projections from the cortex to the striatum form the cortico-striatal circuits to regulate the motivated behaviors.32,41−44 To observe the projection of cortical neurons in vascularized assembloids, we labeled hCOs with an AAV driving enhanced GFP expression from a hSyn enhancer (pAAV-hSyn-EGFP-3 × FLAG-WPRE) at day 38 and the three types of organoids were fused using 3D-printed meshed vessels after 7 days. Samples were collected to observe the projection of cortical neurons at day 70, and cortical neurons projected extensively in vascularized assembloids (Figure 6i-k). Statistical results displayed that vascularized assembloids (21.16 ± 5.48%) produced more projections from hCOs to hSOs, compared to the control (nonvascularized assembloids) (12.81 ± 1.86%) (Figure 6l, m).

To further validate excitatory signaling transduction in different brain regions of the assembloids, we applied the glutamate sensor iGluSnFR (AAV-hsyn-iGluSnFR3.v857-WPRE-PA, BrainVTA) to infect the Striatal-MGE, nonvascularized and vascularized Cortico-Striatal-MGE assembloids at day 45 (Figure 7a-c).45,46 We found that glutamate signals were hardly detected in Striatal-MGE assembloids due to the absence of fused hCOs with abundant glutamatergic neurons at day 60. In contrast, abundant glutamate signals were detected in hCOs, hSOs, and hMOs in both nonvascularized and vascularized Cortico-Striatal-MGE assembloids at day 60 (Figure 7d), indicating excitatory signal transduction in the three brain regions of the assembloids. Furthermore, increased iGluSnFR coverage was found in the hSOs (vascularized: 15.13 ± 3.34%, nonvascularized: 6.46 ± 2.31%) and hMOs (vascularized: 8.79 ± 1.75%, nonvascularized: 2.53 ± 1.26%) of vascularized assembloids, suggesting that 3D-printed meshed vessels could promote excitatory signaling transduction in Cortico-Striatal-MGE assembloids (Figure 7e-h). These findings provided evidence of the interaction and communication between various brain regions during the construction of Cortico-Striatal-MGE assembloids guided by 3D-printed meshed vessels.

Together, the results suggested that 3D-printed meshed vessels could promote the interactions between different brain-regional organoids in vascularized assembloids, including cell migration, neural projection and excitatory signaling transduction, possibly due to their facilitating better access of nutrients into the organoids to regulate the metabolism of assembloids.

Discussion and Prospect

Our study used TPP 3D printing to fabricate artificial meshed vessels for promoting the diffusion of the culture medium to induce dimensional breaking growth of brain organoids and assembloids. With the aid of computer-aided design (CAD) technology, 3D printing offers the advantage of creating intricate internal structures without the need for physical molds.13,47 TPP 3D printing exploited the phenomenon of two-photon absorption to enable the achievement of exceptionally high resolution beyond the diffraction limit.10,48−50 This high-resolution capability is especially advantageous in our research, as it allows for the precise machining of the μm-scale micropores within vessel sidewalls.

Instead of directly using cells as bioinks for 3D printing,9 we used TPP 3D-printed vessels as cell scaffolds to preserve the typical rosette structure in brain organoids. Our findings showed that the vascularized organoids exhibited enhanced and accelerated growth, and reduced hypoxia and apoptosis. It is noteworthy that our 3D-printed vessels reconstructed the structure of native blood vessels with astrocytes arranged around them after long-term culture in vascularized brain organoids. These results revealed that the 3D-printed vessels partially mimicked the structure and function of native blood vessels.

Research has reported that high Young’s modulus is beneficial for the differentiation of astrocytes in gray matter.51 Therefore, we chose the high Young’s modulus material IP-S (about 10 GPa) as the 3D printing material, which could maintain a stable structure during the manual insertion operation and throughout the long-term coculture process, as well as facilitate the differentiation of astrocytes.10,48 Moreover, the nutrient transport channels established by such 3D-printed vessels within organoids may potentially contribute to creating a favorable microenvironment for astrocyte differentiation.52

Our results demonstrated increased astrocytes and calcium signaling in the vascularized organoids. Astrocytes are crucial for the transmission of calcium signals and the stability of electrophysiology.53−55 Therefore, increased astrocytes in vascularized organoids may contribute to the improved neural development and maturation. Furthermore, the vascularized organoids exhibited suppressed ventral differentiation by transcriptome sequencing analysis. Several studies have indicated that the identity of certain cell types could be selectively influenced by glucose metabolism and hypoxia,56,57 which is related to a disproportionate localization of these cell types toward the hypoxic centers within organoids.58 The metabolic modulation provides a potential explanation for the alterations in cell types observed when employing 3D-printed vessels to enhance nutrient supply in the core region of organoids.

Moreover, we utilized 3D-printed meshed vessels to guide the fusion of hCOs, hSOs and hMOs, resulting in the construction of three brain regions assembloids. Notably, we observed enhanced cell migration, projection and excitatory signaling transduction between different brain regions within the vascularized assembloids. In this study, our vascularization scheme did not contain vascular cells and could not model the interaction between vascular cells and neural lineage cells. However, we suggest that our platform could be further extended by incorporating other cell types, such as vascular endothelial cells or microglia, with 3D-printed meshed vessels. These cells might be integrated with organoids or assembloids by the 3D-printed meshed vessels, to construct more realistic vascular networks for modeling the human brain.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we utilized TPP 3D printing technology to construct artificial meshed vessels compatible with brain organoids culture. Our study demonstrates that 3D-printed vessels improve the nutrient supply to brain organoids, enhance their dimensional breaking growth and maturation and modulate their cell fate specification. More importantly we used 3D-printed meshed vessels to guide and improve the assembly of multiregion assembloids derived from the fusion of hCOs, hSOs and hMOs. These findings suggest that 3D-printed meshed vessels enable better growth and long-term culture of brain organoids, providing a platform for studying brain development, function, and diseases.

Experimental Section

TPP 3D Printing of Meshed Vessels

The 3ds MAX software was used to design a hollow cylinder of 2–3 mm in length and 200 μm in diameter, and circular micropores of 20 μm diameter were created on the tubular sidewall. The design information was saved as.stl file and imported into DeScribe software. Photoresist (IP-S) was used to fabricate the design scheme by two-photon polymerization printing in a 3D bioprinter (Nanoscribe GT2). The main monomer of the nondegradable IP-S photoresist from Nanoscribe was a carbamate and methacrylate mixture, which would lead to a solid polymer network after photopolymerization. The formed printed materials were immersed in developer 1 (poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether acrylate) overnight to remove excess photoresist. Printed materials were immersed in isopropanol overnight to eliminate developer 1 for obtaining 3D-printed vessels. Likewise, hollow cylinders of the same dimensions without micropores on the tubular sidewall were designed and 3D-printed as control vessels.

Generation of Human Brain Organoids

Brain organoids were derived from embryonic stem cells (ESCs, cell line: H9), which were maintained and passaged in Essential 8 medium (Life Technologies). When ESCs reached about 70% confluency in each well, they were detached to form embryoid bodies (EBs) by Dispase (1 U/mL, Gibco). EBs were cultured with the neural induction medium (NIM) supplemented with 2 μM SB431542 (Tocris) and 2 μM DMH1 (Tocris). NIM was composed of N2 supplement (1:100; Gibco), nonessential amino acids (MEM-NEAA, 1:100; Gibco) and DMEM/F12 (Gibco). At Day 7, EBs were embedded in Matrigel to facilitate neuroepithelial formation and the medium was changed to NIM without SB431542 and DMH1. Brain organoids were obtained at day 30 and could be cultured for at least 90 days.

Generation of 3D Printing Vascularized Brain Organoids

3D-printed meshed vessels were preincubated in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) with 10 μg/mL Fibronectin (Gibco) for 1 h. After 30 days of culture, brain organoids were moved to a 5 cm × 5 cm parafilm sheet in a dish. The 3D-printed meshed vessel was grasped with a tweezer under an optical microscope and inserted into an organoid. The 3D-printed meshed vessel had to penetrate the central region of the organoid. The 3D printing vascularized brain organoid was transferred to a new dish for further culture, and the same procedure was repeated until all 3D-printed vessels were fused with the brain organoids.

Multiphysics Simulation

COMSOL Multiphysics software was used for physical field modeling. A ball with a radius of 0.8 mm and a cylinder with a bottom radius of 3.1915 mm and a height of 3.125 mm were set up to simulate adding 100 μL of culture medium to a single organoid with a radius of 0.8 mm in a well of a 96-well plate. A cylinder with a radius of 0.1 mm and a height of 1.6 mm was set up to simulate the hollow pipeline inside the organoid. The ambient temperature was set to 310.15K to simulate the incubator temperature. Transport of dilute substances in porous media was used as a module to model the diffusion of glucose and lactate between organoids and culture media. The diffusion coefficient of the culture medium in brain tissue was set to 1.35 × 10–9 m2/s,59 the diffusion coefficient of glucose in the culture medium was set to 6.7 × 10–10 m2/s,60 the diffusion coefficient of lactate in the culture medium was set to 7.1 × 10–10 m2/s,61 the porosity of brain organoids was set to 0.3,62 the initial concentration of glucose in the culture medium was 17.49 mol/m3, and the glucose consumption of each brain neuron was 5.79 × 10–9 μmol/min.63

Immunofluorescence Staining

Brain organoids were fixed with 4% PFA (Sigma) for 2–4 h at day 60 and 90. They were washed three times with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) for 10 min each, and then dehydrated overnight with 20% and 30% sucrose solutions. The next day, the organoids were embedded in the optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) compound and sectioned to obtain 20 μm thick slices using a cryostat. The slices were washed three times with PBS for 10 min each. PBS with 1% Triton X-100 and 5% donkey serum was added to the sections to block for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were incubated in primary antibodies diluted with 0.2% Triton and 5% donkey serum, and overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the sections were washed three times with PBS for 10 min each. Then, they were exposed to secondary antibodies with 5% donkey serum and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were washed three times with PBS for 10 min each. The sections were visualized and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

CCK-8 Assay

At day 35, each single brain organoid was cultured in one well of a 96-well plate. Ten μL of cell counting kit-8 reagent (Beyotime) was added to 100 μL of NIM per well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The absorbance was measured and normalized in a 450 nm microplate reader (Infinite M Nano, TECAN).

FITC-Dextran Penetrating Assay

Brain organoids were incubated in 50 μg/mL FITC-Dextran (Sigma) for 2 h at day 90. Then they were washed three times with PBS and fixed, sectioned, and stained following the Immunofluorescence Staining protocol. FITC-Dextran was costained with nuclear dye (Hoechst) and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. The percentage of FITC+ areas within the entire field of view of each organoid class was statistically analyzed and compared.

Immunostaining for Hypoxyprobe-1

Brain organoids were exposed to 100 μM Hypoxyprobe-1 (Hypoxyprobe) for 2 h at day 90. Then they were fixed and stained according to the Immunofluorescence Staining. Fluorescence staining images were acquired using a microscope and the mean fluorescence intensity of Hypoxyprobe-1 in the central region of brain organoids was measured to assess the level of hypoxia in organoids.

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) Assay

Brain organoids were fixed and sectioned following the Immunofluorescence Staining. Brain organoid sections were rinsed twice with PBS for 10 min each. The sections were treated with TUNEL detection solution containing 10% Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase (TdT) enzyme and 90% fluorescent labeling solution (Beyotime) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. The sections were washed three times with PBS. Sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium and examined under a fluorescence microscope. The density of TUNEL+ cells in the central region of organoids was used as a measure of cell apoptosis.

Bulk RNA Sequencing Analysis

Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol assay kit (Life Technology), and mRNA was purified using Oligo (dT) beads prior to cDNA synthesis. Library construction and RNA sequencing were performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. RNA seq reads were aligned to the human reference GRCh38 genome and transcriptome using HISAT2 (v.2.1.0).64 RNA differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 software package (v.1.30.0).65 The cutoff criterion was set to an adjusted P-value <0.05 and an absolute log2 (fold change) ≥ 1. The Web sites Enrichr, STRING, and BioLadder were respectively used for GO analysis, KEGG enrichment analysis, Protein–protein Interaction (PPI) networks, and figure generation. Transcriptome data from different fetal brain regions (8 pcw, 9 pcw, 12 pcw, 13 pcw and 16 pcw) of different gestations from Allen Brain Atlas were utilized as reference materials to verify the optimal correspondence of brain regions for organoids.

Calcium Imaging

Brain organoids were planted on confocal dishes coated with Matrigel at day 87. At day 90, organoids were soaked with 1 μM Fluo-4 AM (Life Technologies) loading solution for 20 min at 37 °C and then for another 20 min at room temperature and washed with DPBS. Then, organoids were placed in live-cell imaging solution for imaging. Calcium imaging was conducted using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss LSM 800) equipped with 20x objective at 488 nm excitation. Time series images were captured at a rate of 1 frame every 3.2 s. The organoids were stimulated with a high-KCl solution (67 mM) approximately 1 min after imaging began. Single-cell calcium activities were analyzed using ImageJ software and Prism (v9, GraphPad). The peak value of [Ca2+] (Fmax–F0)/F0 for single cell was calculated by (maximum fluorescence intensity minus initial fluorescence intensity) divided by initial fluorescence intensity.

Whole-Cell Patch-Clamp Recordings

Brain organoids were planted onto coverslips coated with Matrix (Corning) at day 70. From day 70 to 80, coverslips were immersed in a bath solution containing 145 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM HEPES, and 5 mM glucose (280 mosM, pH 7.3). Organoids were observed using a 60× water-immersion objective of an upright microscope (BX51WI, Olympus). Recordings were digitized using a Digidata 1550B (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA) and collected with an Axon MultiClamp 700B differential amplifier (Molecular Devices). Data analysis was performed using Axon Clampex software (Molecular Devices). Recording pipettes with resistances of 6–10 MΩ were filled with an intracellular recording solution containing 130 mM K-gluconate, 10 mM KCl, 10 mM EGTA, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.3 mM Na-GTP, 2 mM Na-ATP, and 10 mM HEPES (280 mosM, pH 7.3). Sodium and potassium currents were recorded in voltage-clamp mode at a holding potential of −70 mV with step-voltage changes (16 steps from −80 to 60 mV). In order to trigger action potentials, cells were held at −70 mV in a current-clamp mode, and a series of current steps (10 steps of 10 pA increments) were injected. Electrodes (Sutter instrument) were pulled from borosilicate glass using a Sutter instrument puller (model P-1000), and had a resistance of 9–15 MΩ when filled with internal solution. Voltage and current signals were acquired using an Axopatch 700B amplifier (Axon) connected to a Digidata1322A interface (Axon). The data were digitized and stored using pClamp software (version 9; Axon).

Generation of 3D Printing Vascularized Cortico-Striatal-MGE Assembloids

The hCOs, hSOs, and hMOs fused into assembloids were derived from embryonic stem cells (ESCs, cell line: H9) following the protocol of Generation of Human Brain Organoids for culturing before day 7. EBs were grown overnight in NIM supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Life Technologies) to generate rosettes at day 7. The medium was replaced with NIM at day 8, and changed every 2 days. For hCOs, rosettes were detached by pipetting to form organoids at day 16, and organoids were harvested after continuous culture beyond day 30. For hSOs, 20 ng/mL sonic hedgehog (SHH, R&D) was added at day 16–25 based on hCOs differentiation, and hSOs were harvested after continuous culture beyond day 30. For hMOs, 1 μM smoothened receptor agonist (SAG, Calbiochem) was added at day 10–25 based on hCOs differentiation and hMOs were harvested after day 30. For neuronal migration modeling, 0.2 μL AAV (pAAV-hSyn-EGFP-3 × FLAG-WPRE, 1.58 × 1013 v.g./ml) was injected into each single hMO at day 30. The hCOs, hSOs and hMOs were fused into assembloids sequentially by 3D-printed vessels at day 37. Assembloids were harvested to observe neuronal migration from hMOs at day 40. Similarly, 0.2 μL AAV (pAAV-hSyn-EGFP-3 × FLAG-WPRE, 1.58 × 1013 v.g./ml) was injected into each single hCO at day 38. The assembloids were assembled sequentially by 3D-printed vessels at day 45, and harvested to observe neuronal projections from hCOs at day 70. For detecting excitatory signaling transduction, the Striatal-MGE, nonvascularized and vascularized Cortico-Striatal-MGE assembloids were generated by fusing hCOs, hSOs or hMOs at day 38, and each group of assembloids was infected with 5 μL glutamate sensor iGluSnFR (AAV-hsyn-iGluSnFR3.v857-WPRE-PA, 5.25 × 1012 v.g./ml, BrainVTA) at day 45. At day 60, real-time glutamate release was detected by using confocal microscopy.

Statistical Analysis

Immunostained cells were quantified and analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji, v1.51s). Each data set included more than three random independent experiments. Hoechst staining was used to mark and count the total number of cells. All experimental samples utilized biological replicates for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software via Student t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). *: p < 0.05, **: p < 0.01, ***: p < 0.001, or ****: p < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant.
