Deciphering the neural responses to a naturalistic persuasive message
Authors: Ioannis Ntoumanis, Julia Sheronova, Alina Davydova, Maria Dolgaleva, Iiro P. Jääskeläinen, Vladimir Kosonogov, Anna N. Shestakova, Vasily Klucharev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThroughout history, social influence has played a pivotal role in shaping human behavior. Although previous studies explored how the brain responds to persuasive messages, they used methods that oversimplified the complexity of real-world communication. In our study, participants underwent fMRI while listening to a healthy eating call that influenced their food choices. We show that while much of the brain is synchronized across all participants during listening, stratifying participants based on persuasion-induced product valuation change reveals additional brain regions with stereotyped activity only among unpersuaded individuals. Some of these regions are involved in theory of mind. Our findings provide deeper insights into how persuasive messages manifest in the brain and hold potential for reshaping public health strategies.

Persuasion is a fundamental form of social influence that is intended to shape, reinforce, or change the behavior of others (1). With the prevalence of obesity rates (2), persuasion has emerged as a crucial tool in health promotion campaigns, aiming at encouraging consumers to adopt healthier food choices (3, 4). We recently demonstrated that a healthy eating call can influence the willingness to pay (WTP) for sugar-containing food (5) and that its effectiveness can be explained by the electroencephalographic (EEG) responses of its recipients (6). Here, we employed functional MRI (fMRI) to delve deeper into the mechanisms that give rise to persuasion by a naturalistic persuasive message.

Earlier research has shown that the effectiveness of persuasive messages is associated with the neural responses these messages elicit (e.g., refs. 7–9). A consistent finding is that activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) correlates with persuasion-induced behavior change (8, 10–12). This has been attributed to the pivotal role of the vmPFC in decision-making (13) and, particularly, in self-referential processing (14), reflecting the degree of self-relevance experienced by the recipients of the persuasive message (15). This self-referential processing has also been observed more broadly in the mPFC and other default mode regions, which help people think about themselves and others. For example, Chua et al. (16) found that while viewing tailored smoking-cessation messages, activity in the dorsal mPFC was associated with subsequent cessation of smoking. Apart from the mPFC, neuronal activity in other brain regions has also been found to underlie persuasion. These regions include the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), implicated in conflict monitoring (17, 18), the precuneus, which is involved in the interpretation of persuasive messages (8, 19), as well as the temporal pole and supramarginal gyrus, which are involved in language processing (10, 20).

Furthermore, successful persuasion depends not only on local activity patterns but also on how strongly key brain regions are functionally connected to each other (21–23). For instance, successful persuasion has been associated with the functional connectivity between valuation regions, such as the vmPFC and ventral striatum (22). Interestingly, the connectivity between these regions contributes additional information compared to average activity within single brain regions (22). In addition, Huskey et al. (24) proposed that persuasion-induced behavior change results from an increased functional connectivity between structures implicated in affective and executive processing, such as the vmPFC, anterior insula, temporal pole, and supramarginal gyrus. However, executive–affective connectivity is also considered to play a role in resisting persuasive messages, reflecting the adverse effects of specific health behaviors (11, 25).

The findings summarized above mainly emerged from event-related analyses of fMRI data. Such methods are based on trial-averaging and consider fluctuations of brain activity over time as noise. However, if in real-life scenarios, the response to persuasive messages changes as a function of an evolving narrative context, then trial-averaging methods will fall short (26).

To address this limitation, alternative data analysis methods, such as intersubject correlation (ISC) and intersubject functional connectivity (ISFC), could provide complementary insights by capitalizing on the richness of naturalistic experimental paradigms (19, 27, 28). ISC measures how similarly a group of people process a stimulus (28, 29), and it underlies shared psychological perspectives toward a stimulus (19, 30, 31). This has also been demonstrated with persuasive stimuli in politics (32) and health (27, 33). Thus, ISC is a promising method for studying the neural correlates of persuasion by naturalistic stimuli. Furthermore, as outlined above, successful persuasion depends on the functional connectivity among key brain regions. However, within-subject functional connectivity is subject to physiological noise, resulting in spurious intervoxel correlations that have little to do with concerted neural activity (28, 34). ISFC can separate stimulus-evoked interregional correlations from those that arise from intrinsic neural dynamics or physiological noise (35).

The primary objective of the present study was to investigate the neural correlates of persuasion by a healthy eating call that was presented in the form of a continuous persuasive communication, i.e., a first-person narrative by a nutritionist emphasizing the health risks of sugar consumption. Utilizing fMRI, we aimed to bring to light neural synchronization and connectivity differences between participants who were persuaded by the healthy eating call and participants who were not. Building upon relevant previous findings (8, 17), we hypothesized that such effects would manifest in the vmPFC, ACC, and precuneus.

Participants performed two blocks of a bidding task, in which they had to bid on sugar-containing, sugar-free, and nonedible products, while their brain activity was recorded with fMRI. In between the two blocks, they listened to a persuasive healthy eating call that influenced their bidding behavior. We demonstrate that successful persuasion is associated with reduced intersubject neural synchronization and weaker functional connectivity between several brain regions, including the vmPFC, ACC, and precuneus. These findings challenge conventional interpretations of neural synchronization (27) and support the emerging practice of tailoring persuasive messages in health promotion campaigns (16).

Results

Behavioral Results.

We first evaluated the overall persuasive impact of the healthy eating call on participants’ bidding behavior. Specifically, we conducted a one-way repeated measures ANOVA to assess whether participants’ ΔWTP (i.e., WTP for a product in the second block of the bidding task subtracted by the WTP for the corresponding product in the first block) was different across the three conditions of the bidding task (sugar-containing, sugar-free, and nonedible products). This revealed a statistically significant effect of Condition on the ΔWTP (F(2,98) = 15.89, P < 0.0001, generalized eta-squared = 0.15; Fig. 1). Post hoc paired-samples t tests showed that participants’ ΔWTP for sugar-free products was higher than for sugar-containing products (t(49) = 4.87, P < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.689) and for nonedible products (t(49) = 4.12, P = 0.0002, Cohen’s d = 0.58). ΔWTP for sugar-containing products and nonedible products did not differ (t(49) = 1.72, P = 0.092, Cohen’s d = 0.24). This demonstrates that the healthy eating call increased participants’ WTP for sugar-free foods, but did not affect their WTP for sugar-containing foods, relative to the control condition of nonedible products. An Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was also conducted to account for the regression to the mean (RTM) effect, a statistical phenomenon that can cause natural variation in repeated measurements to appear as real change (36, 37). Our behavioral results remained statistically significant after controlling for the RTM (SI Appendix, Table S1).

Furthermore, a linear mixed effects model showed that the perceived tastiness and sweetness of sugar-free products modulated the effect of the healthy eating call on WTP decisions. Specifically, after listening to the healthy eating call, participants increased their WTP, especially for sugar-free products perceived as tasty and nonsweet (Table 1). However, such product characteristics did not modulate the effect of the healthy eating call on the WTP for sugar-containing products (Table 1). Finally, an exploratory correlation analysis showed that the efficiency of expert persuasion was not moderated by participant’s demographic characteristics or personality traits (SI Appendix, Table S2).

Listening to a Healthy Eating Call Evokes Widespread Neural Synchrony.

In order to detect brain areas that responded reliably to the healthy eating call, we estimated the ISC of participants’ brain activity. As expected from an audio narrative, the highest ISC was observed in the auditory cortex and especially in the superior and middle temporal gyrus, in the temporal pole, and in Heschl’s gyrus (Fig. 2). Moreover, a significant ISC was found in the precuneus, vmPFC, and ACC. SI Appendix, Table S3 summarizes all the brain areas with statistically significant ISC. Overall, these results show that the healthy eating call evoked stereotyped responses across participants not only in sensory areas but also in higher-order brain regions.

Persuasion Modulates Neural Responses to the Healthy Eating Call.

After signifying that the healthy eating call evoked widespread neural synchronization among all participants, we aimed to determine whether there were brain regions whose degree of ISC was associated with persuasion-induced product valuation change. To achieve this, we stratified our sample into a persuaded group (n = 24) and an unpersuaded group (n = 26), according to a median split of the ΔWTP for sugar-free food (subtracted by the ΔWTP for nonedible products; see Materials and Methods for details). Then, we conducted an ISC contrast analysis. Results are shown in Fig. 3. Several brain regions emerged as being significantly more synchronized in the unpersuaded group relative to the persuaded group, including the left precuneus (ISC difference = 0.033, BH-corrected P = 0.003), the right supramarginal gyrus (ISC difference = 0.032, P = 0.003), the left vmPFC (ISC difference = 0.026, P = 0.003), the right pregenual ACC (ISC difference = 0.026, P = 0.003) and the right rolandic operculum (ISC difference = 0.026, P = 0.003). There were no brain regions where the persuaded group exhibited significantly higher ISC than the unpersuaded group.

Unthresholded ISC contrast, revealing brain regions that were more synchronized among unpersuaded participants than among persuaded participants. The color bar denotes the magnitude of the ISC difference within each ROI. ROIs where the difference in ISC between groups was statistically significant are outlined.

In order to investigate whether there is a continuous relationship between ISC and persuasion (i.e., ΔWTP for sugar-free), we also conducted post hoc tests. First, we calculated the median ISC of each participant to all other participants for each of the ROIs that emerged in the contrast analysis. Then, we calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficient between these subject-level ISC values and the corrected ΔWTP for sugar-free products. For all ROIs, there was a negative correlation between ISC and persuasion, but this was statistically significant only for the precuneus (r = −0.394, P = 0.005), the vmPFC (r = −0.391, P = 0.005), and the supramarginal gyrus (r = −0.372, P = 0.008; SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Furthermore, we performed an intersubject representational similarity analysis (ISRSA) to examine whether our ISC results are best described by the “Anna Karenina” principle (38), that is, “all unpersuaded participants are alike, all persuaded participants are persuaded in their own way.” This was tested specifically for the brain regions revealed by the ISC contrast. Results showed a statistically significant Anna Karenina effect in the vmPFC (r = 0.096, P = 0.011), the precuneus (r = 0.087, P = 0.019), and the supramarginal gyrus (r = 0.080, P = 0.022; Fig. 4).

The Middle subpanel denotes the location of the corresponding ROI. The Right subpanel illustrates the correlation between participants’ neural similarity (ISC) and behavioral similarity measured with the Anna Karenina model. To evaluate whether the spatial response patterns within each ROI were more consistent in unpersuaded participants than in persuaded participants, we conducted also a spatial ISC analysis (28). Since the spatial configuration of each ROI’s voxels may vary for each participant (39), prior to the spatial ISC analysis, we functionally aligned each ROI into a common space by fitting a shared response model [SRM; (40)]. Next, we averaged the spatial ISC across all the time points corresponding to the healthy eating call, and compared it between the persuaded and unpersuaded participants. However, there were no significant differences between the groups in any ROI (all r < 0.004, all P > 0.170). To potentially identify key moments in the healthy eating call that might evoke a brief spatial alignment of brain activity, we also examined whether the spatial ISC differed between persuaded and unpersuaded participants at individual time points (39). This analysis revealed some statistically significant results, however, these were temporally isolated and bidirectional (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

Persuasion Modulates Functional Connectivity between Higher-Order Brain Regions.

To further explore persuasion-dependent differences in neural responses to the naturalistic healthy eating call, we conducted an ISFC analysis. This was done to examine whether persuasion differentiated the functional connectivity among the brain regions that emerged from the ISC contrast analysis. We found that, overall, the unpersuaded group exhibited stronger ISFC than the persuaded group (Fig. 5). This phenomenon was statistically significant in the functional connectivity between the following pairs of brain regions: ACC and precuneus (t(98) = 4.412, P = 0.001), ACC and supramarginal gyrus (t(98) = 3.772, P = 0.009), precuneus and supramarginal gyrus (t(98) = 5.125, P = 0.001), supramarginal gyrus and rolandic operculum (t(98) = 1.725, P = 0.021).

Functional connectivity between persuasion-related brain regions for (A) persuaded participants and (B) unpersuaded participants by the healthy eating call.

Neural Responses to Food Images Were Not Affected by Persuasion.

Moreover, we evaluated the degree to which the healthy eating call affected the neural responses of persuaded and unpersuaded individuals to food images during the bidding task. We hypothesized that participants persuaded by the healthy eating call would exhibit increased activation in reward processing and food perception brain regions, particularly the vmPFC and the NAcc (41), in response to images of sugar-free foods. To test this, we used a general linear model (GLM) analysis of the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal in the vmPFC, NAcc, and superior occipital gyrus (SOG). The latter was included as a control ROI expected to respond solely to low-level visual properties of the product images and to show no preferential activation in either group (persuaded vs. unpersuaded). The beta coefficients of the GLM emerged from contrasting the two blocks of the bidding task (i.e., after vs. before the healthy eating call). A three-way mixed ANOVA was then conducted to compare the beta coefficients between the persuaded and unpersuaded group, for each condition of the bidding task (sugar-free, sugar-containing, and nonedible) and each ROI (vmPFC, NAcc, and SOG).

The three-way interaction between Group, Condition, and ROI was not statistically significant (F(4,192) = 0.428, P = 0.788). There was a significant two-way interaction between Condition and ROI (F(4,192) = 7.245, P < 0.0001), with the beta coefficients being greater for the vmPFC and NAcc, compared to the SOG, but only for the sugar-containing and sugar-free condition (all corrected Ps < 0.031). Overall, there were no significant differences between the persuaded and the unpersuaded participants in any condition or ROI (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

We additionally performed several event-related control analyses to examine whether our data replicate the results of earlier research in the field. For instance, the beta coefficients corresponding to the control ROI (SOG) were pooled across both groups and all conditions and submitted to a one-sample t test (42). This confirmed that activity in SOG decreased after listening to the healthy eating call (t(149) = −8.9, P < 0.0001), as expected due to habituation of neurons in the primary visual cortex (43). Moreover, we tested whether WTP amounts correlated with activity in reward processing brain regions (i.e., vmPFC and NAcc) in response to product images during the bidding task, across all participants, conditions, and blocks. We found that participants’ WTP amounts correlated significantly with activity in the vmPFC (r = 0.042, P < 0.0001), but not with activity in the NAcc (r = 0.005, P = 0.594). This is in line with Plassmann et al. (44), but inconsistent with Knutson et al. (45), and is further discussed in the Discussion. Finally, we examined whether participants’ change in WTP (i.e., ΔWTP) after listening to the persuasive message correlated with activity in reward processing brain regions. However, we did not find a statistically significant correlation between ΔWTP and activity in the vmPFC (r = 0.023, P = 0.109) or NAcc (r = 0.008, P = 0.565).

Effects Are Specific to Persuasion.

Finally, we performed several control analyses to rule out the possibility that the observed group differences were driven by a factor other than persuasion (e.g., group differences related to demographics, personality traits, or quality of fMRI data). However, none of these variables differed significantly between the two groups (Table 2). Moreover, there was no significant correlation between the BMI and the ISC in any of the ROIs (all Ps > 0.659).

Discussion

The present study illustrated the distinct neural signatures of being persuaded and resisting persuasion in the context of a naturalistic healthy eating call. Resistance to persuasion was associated with increased neural synchrony in default mode network (DMN) regions, as well as with increased functional connectivity among these regions. These results contribute to the growing body of literature that seeks to unravel the neural correlates of persuasion (8, 22), making an important step forward in studying the phenomena using naturalistic stimuli. Below, we further consider our findings in more detail.

At the behavioral level, we found that the healthy eating call significantly increased the participants’ WTP for sugar-free food. This aligns with our prior research demonstrating the persuasive power of healthy eating calls on food choices (5, 6). However, we did not observe a significant decrease in participants’ WTP for sugar-containing food after listening to the healthy eating call. A possible explanation for this differential finding is that the benefits of consuming sugar-free food were mentioned toward the end of the healthy eating call, and the ending of cautionary narratives is known to influence how individuals are persuaded by them (46, 47). For instance, in order to maintain a positive emotional state, participants may have continued to reflect on the positive narrative ending (i.e., the expert approval to consume sugar-free sweets) rather than thinking about the prohibitive tone of the narrative about sugar-containing food consumption (48). In addition, the healthy eating call was particularly effective at increasing the WTP for sugar-free products, which participants perceived as tasty and nonsweet. This confirms the notion that personal beliefs and judgments impact the effectiveness of persuasive messages (49).

Moving into the neural domain, we found that participants who were persuaded by the healthy eating call exhibited lower levels of ISC during exposure. Specifically, brain areas previously found to be implicated in persuasive processing, such as the vmPFC (11), precuneus (8), ACC (17), and supramarginal gyrus (10), demonstrated reduced synchronization among individuals who were persuaded by the healthy eating call. This unexpected decrease in ISC challenges conventional interpretations of synchronized neural responses as indicative of engagement with a stimulus (27, 50, 51). Instead, it suggests that persuasion might be accompanied by a more individually tailored neural response, perhaps reflecting complex cognitive processes or selective attention to aspects of the message. This interpretation is supported by our ISRSA demonstrating that the relationship between persuasion and neural synchrony is best described by the Anna Karenina principle: all unpersuaded participants are alike, all persuaded participants are persuaded in their own way. This aligns with recent findings suggesting that divergent neural activity, rather than synchronous, may underlie good conversations (52). The hypothesis that persuasion entails self-related processing has successfully been tested before (53), with a plethora of studies implementing tailored messages in studies of persuasion (16, 54, 55).

Future studies could provide further insights into the Anna Karenina effect in persuasion by incorporating a self-report investigation following the neuroimaging session (56). In this approach, participants would rate the perceived persuasiveness of each argument presented in the persuasive message, allowing researchers to examine whether persuaded individuals are persuaded by different arguments (in line with the Anna Karenina principle discussed earlier, i.e., “all persuaded participants are persuaded in their own way”). However, it is important to acknowledge the potential limitation that self-report measures often correlate poorly with actual behavior (57, 58).

A similar study in the literature has demonstrated that effective persuasive messages increase the neural synchrony of the audience rather than decreasing it (27). These contradictory results may be attributed to several factors. First, our study utilized a long-lasting stimulus (7 min), as opposed to the short video clips used in Imhof et al. (27). Second, we compared the ISC between two groups of subjects who experienced the same persuasive message, rather than comparing the ISC of the same group of subjects between two different categories of stimuli, as done in Imhof et al. (27). Additionally, we measured the actual behavioral effect of the persuasive message, as opposed to perceived message effectiveness used in Imhof et al. (27). Last, the object of our persuasive message was related to a primary need (food) in contrast to alcohol (27). Future research controlling for these factors could further examine the circumstances under which neural synchrony underlies persuasion and those under which neural synchrony underlies resistance to persuasion.

An interpretation of our ISC results is that the healthy eating call might have been perceived as misleading from participants who resisted persuasion. Although directly testing this with postexperiment self-reports would be preferred in future studies, our speculation is supported by the emergence of precuneus and ACC in the contrast ISC analysis. Precuneus is known to be more responsive to reading inventive vs. factual text (59) and ACC is known to be recruited when there is uncertainty about the truth of a text (60). Thus, if the healthy eating call was indeed perceived as misleading, that could be reflected in a high ISC in these brain regions. Moreover, although activity in vmPFC has been consistently found to correlate with persuasion-induced behavior change (8), patients with damage to the vmPFC appear to be more susceptible to misleading advertisements compared to healthy controls (61). This highlights the flexible role of vmPFC which might be determined by the subjective perspective of the participants. Ultimately, subjective perspective is known to impact how the human brain processes naturalistic stimuli (19, 31, 62, 63). It is important to note that, although the regions in question (vmPFC, ACC, and precuneus) have been found to be involved in the abovementioned processes, they also serve multiple other functions. Thus, future research is needed to clarify the cognitive mechanisms underlying synchronized neural activity in the context of persuasion.

Several of the brain regions revealed by the ISC analysis belong to the DMN (64, 65). DMN structures, such as the vmPFC, are also recruited when maintaining a belief in the face of counterevidence (66). This might be due to resistance to evidence entailing disengagement from external reality and increased inward focus (66), which are psychological processes supported by the DMN (67, 68). In addition, emerging evidence suggests that the DMN is sensitive to intrinsic information (e.g., beliefs), which influences and interacts with the interpretation and processing of incoming external information (65). Our data support this modern view of the hybrid function of the DMN.

Our investigation into functional connectivity patterns further refines our understanding of the neural correlates of persuasion. Apart from exhibiting higher neural synchronization in the ACC, precuneus, and supramarginal gyrus, participants who were not persuaded by the healthy eating call also demonstrated stronger connectivity among these regions. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that positive connectivity between ACC, precuneus, and vmPFC, besides negative connectivity between these areas and executive neural areas, play a role in resisting persuasive messages (11, 25). It has been argued that this connectivity structure reduces the extent to which “feeling bad” brings about a change in behavior (25, 69). For example, Dinh-Williams et al. (25) found a similar connectivity structure in tobacco users when viewing antismoking images.

Moreover, previous research has suggested that the success of persuasive messages depends on how flexibly vmPFC is connected to other valuation regions (22). In our data, the connectivity between vmPFC and other regions was not significantly different between the persuaded and the unpersuaded participants. This might be due to methodological differences. Unlike Cooper et al. (22), who estimated the intrasubject functional connectivity, we estimated the ISFC. These two measures are considered to provide distinct and complementary information (70). Another interpretation of our functional connectivity results has to do with the long duration of our stimulus. It has been found that the vmPFC is not consistently strongly connected to other valuation regions, but it is rather flexibly changing its connections in response to effective persuasive messages (71).

Finally, our event-related analysis indicated no differences in neural responses to food images between persuaded and unpersuaded participants across different conditions and ROIs. This aligns with findings from our EEG study with the same experimental design (6), where we similarly observed no effect of persuasion on event-related potentials (ERPs) elicited by food stimuli. While one would expect a shift in reward processing brain regions postpersuasion, for instance, reflecting an increased valuation of sugar-free food in line with our behavioral results, this was not observed in our data. A possible explanation for this is that the healthy eating call may have initiated cognitive processes that extend beyond immediate valuation, possibly engaging regulatory mechanisms that mitigate the anticipated changes in neural activity (72). Future studies could test this speculation by implementing a more complex experimental design. Moreover, our event-related control analysis showed that activity in the vmPFC, but not NAcc, correlated with participants’ WTP amounts in the bidding task. Earlier research has shown that product valuation is associated with activity in the vmPFC or NAcc, depending on the experimental task. For example, Knutson et al. (45) found a correlation between product valuation and activity in the NAcc, but not in the vmPFC. On the contrary, Plassmann et al. (44) who used a slightly different task, found a correlation with vmPFC activity, but not with NAcc activity. Plassmann et al. (44) attributed this differential finding to subtle but important differences between the computations that the brain makes in different tasks, with the vmPFC and NAcc playing a differential role in such computations (44). The bidding task used in our study was closer to that of Plassmann et al. (44), which could explain why we observed a correlation between WTP and vmPFC activity, but not NAcc activity.

While our study provides valuable insights, it possesses several limitations. The ecological validity of laboratory settings, even when using naturalistic stimuli, might not fully capture the complexity of real-world persuasive encounters. From a methodological point of view, a limitation is the spatial scale at which we studied the processing of the healthy eating call. We divided the brain into hundreds of ROIs, thus limiting the spatial resolution of the original data, which is a common technique to reduce the dimensionality of the data (e.g., ref. 73). However, this is unlikely to have artificially enhanced our results. Previous research exploring the neural correlates of persuasion has also performed analysis at the level of ROIs (8, 22). Finally, our study examined neural responses to a single persuasive message, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies may employ a variety of persuasive messages to assess the replicability of our results.

In conclusion, our study represents an important step forward in using naturalistic methods to study the neural mechanisms of persuasion. We replicated the involvement of vmPFC, ACC, and precuneus in processing persuasive messages and demonstrated the flexible role of these regions that gives rise to persuasion resistance. Our results suggest that individuals who respond positively to healthy eating messages may engage in a more intricate neural processing of persuasive content, as indicated by lower levels of neural synchrony. Overall, our findings not only contribute to our understanding of how persuasion emerges but also have practical insights for improving the effectiveness of health promotion campaigns.

Materials and Methods

Participants.

Fifty participants (25 females and 25 males, aged 18 to 32 y, mean age = 21.4, white) were recruited via online advertisements to participate in an fMRI experiment. All of them were right-handed, healthy, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, had no history of psychiatric diagnoses or eating disorders, no neurological or metabolic illnesses, and were not taking any prescribed medication. Eating sweets was also an inclusion criterion so that we filter out potential participants who might already dislike sugar altogether. The body mass index (BMI) of the participants ranged from 16.8 to 30.6. Seven participants had BMI lower than 18.5 (underweight), 8 participants had BMI greater than 25 (overweight), and 1 participant had BMI greater than 30 (obese). The remaining 34 participants had a normal BMI (i.e., between 18.5 and 25). The BMI was calculated as a subject’s weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Informed written consent was obtained for each participant prior to data collection, and all procedures were approved by the HSE Committee on Interuniversity Surveys and Ethical Assessment of Empirical Research (Protocol No. 74).

The sample size was comparable or larger than previous fMRI studies exploring the relationship between WTP for products and fMRI indices (44, 74), as well as studies exploring the relationship between ISC and behavioral measures (42, 73). Moreover, a sample size of 50 respects the recommendations by Pajula and Tohka (75) for sufficient statistical power in ISC analyses. All participants received a flat fee of 1,000 monetary units (MU) equivalent to approximately $36.63, with the correction for purchasing power parities (76). Additionally, they received a reward based on their decisions in the experimental task (see below). The mechanism of how this reward was determined was explained to them in detail in the instructions prior to the experiment.

Procedure.

Participants were told that the goal of the experiment was to study food preferences. They were asked to not eat anything for at least 3 h prior to the experiment to cause appetite (5, 6, 44). Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants saw the real products in the cupboard to be assured about the validity of the procedure. Then, they were asked to indicate their perceived tastiness, sweetness, and familiarity of all presented foods using a 7-point Likert scale (outside the scanner). These ratings helped us to determine whether the effect of the healthy eating call varied according to such product characteristics.

Afterward, participants were placed in the MRI scanner and started the experiment. At the beginning, they performed a bidding task. Next, they listened to the healthy eating call. And finally, they performed a second block of the bidding task (Fig. 6A). On average, the experiment lasted 1 h per participant.

(A) The experimental procedure and (B) a sample trial of the bidding task. Here, the face of the doctor has been blurred.

Bidding Task.

In order to measure individual preferences for sweets, we used the Becker–DeGroot–Marschak auction (77). Specifically, we utilized a bidding task that was the same as in Ntoumanis et al. (6). At the beginning of each trial, a product was displayed for 4 s [“early evaluation stage,” (44)]. Afterward, participants had 10 s to indicate their WTP (“How much are you ready to pay for this product?”), in order to purchase this product at the end of the experiment (44, 72, 74). To that end, a slider was used with price options 0 to 150 MU, with an increment of 10. The initial position of the marker on the WTP slider was randomized across trials and participants used three buttons of an MRI-compatible response device to make WTP decisions: the left index shifted the marker to the right (to increase the price), the left middle finger shifted the marker to the left (to decrease the price), and the right index confirmed bid choices (78). No response within the time limit resulted in a WTP of 0 MU, following previous studies (5, 6, 44). Finally, a fixation cross was shown for 2 to 6 s and the next trial began. For further details of the bidding task, see Fig. 6B.

There were 90 trials in the bidding task, each presenting a different product. The products comprised 30 sugar-containing sweets, 30 sugar-free sweets (e.g., chocolate), and 30 nonedible everyday products (e.g., towels, socks, etc.). Each product was presented as a full-colored photographic picture along with a label indicating the category of the product (i.e., “sugar-containing,” “sugar-free,” or “nonedible”). This stimulus set was used in Ntoumanis et al. (6) and is publicly available at https://osf.io/6ewy5/.

The visual stimuli were projected with an LCD projector onto a rear screen. This screen was reflected by a mirror attached to the MRI head coil, subtending approximately 20 degrees of visual angle in width. The bidding task was programmed using PsychoPy [v2022.2.1; (79)].

Healthy Eating Call.

The healthy eating call was the same as in Ntoumanis et al. (5, 6): a first-person audio narrative delivered by a nutritionist emphasizing the health risks associated with sugar consumption. The narrator supported his opinion with 13 arguments (e.g., the association of sugar consumption with cavities), each elaborated further. These arguments were retrieved from scientific sources (e.g., ref. 80). The narrative also contained clear evidence of the nutritionist’s positive view toward sugar-free products. We selected a nutritionist as the narrator because communicators with high expertise have been found to be particularly persuasive (81, 82). The healthy eating call was written and presented to the participants in their native language (an English translation is available at https://osf.io/894mk/). While the narrative played, the static image of a doctor was displayed on the screen to maximize participants’ attention (Fig. 6A). The duration of the healthy eating call was 7 min and it was delivered to the participants through MRI-compatible earphones (InVivo SensaVue), while the sound was adjusted for each participant to be loud enough to be heard over the scanner noise.

Questionnaires.

Participants completed four questionnaires not earlier than 2 d prior to attending the experiment, so that we examine the influence of consumer heterogeneity on the efficacy of expert persuasion, as well as to conduct control analyses related to the fMRI results. The first questionnaire (SI Appendix, Table S4) encompassed the demographic information, including age, sex, weight, height, and the level of education (four levels: incomplete secondary education, secondary education, incomplete higher education, and higher education), as in ref. 6. The second questionnaire (SI Appendix, Table S5) was the Conformity scale [(83); the internal consistency in the current study, α = 0.85], which was found to play a moderating role in our previous study (6). The third questionnaire (SI Appendix, Table S6) was the Consumer susceptibility to interpersonal influence scale [(84), translated by us; the internal consistency in the current study, α = 0.80]. The fourth questionnaire (SI Appendix, Table S7) was a version of the Big Five Personality traits questionnaire [(85); the internal consistency in the current study, α = 0.81]. The latter was included because previous studies have revealed a relationship between personality traits and sugar consumption, as well as social conformity (86–88).

Behavioral Data Analysis.

To examine the effect of the healthy eating call on participants’ WTP for each product category, we conducted a one-way, repeated-measures ANOVA taking Condition as a within-subjects factor (three levels: sugar-containing, sugar-free, and nonedible) and the ΔWTP (i.e., the WTP for each product in the second block subtracted by the WTP for the same product in the first block) as a dependent variable, after verifying the normal distribution of the data with a Shapiro–Wilk test. A significant interaction was further assessed by post hoc t tests and P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (89). Adjusted P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Moreover, in order to investigate whether the effect of the healthy eating call depended on product characteristics, we estimated two linear-mixed effects models with subject-level random effects (90). One model aimed to predict the ΔWTP (i.e., the WTP for each product in the second block subtracted by the WTP for the same product in the first block) for sugar-free sweets, and the other aimed to predict the ΔWTP for sugar-containing sweets, both taking as regressors the participants’ ratings of each product’s perceived healthiness, tastiness, and familiarity.

Finally, in order to examine whether the efficiency of expert persuasion depended on consumer characteristics, a series of correlation analyses was conducted. Specifically, we tested whether participants’ delta of WTP for sugar-free or sugar-containing food was correlated with their personality traits, using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. This analysis was exploratory and no hypotheses were specified in advance, hence all P-values were adequately corrected for multiple comparisons.

MRI Data Acquisition.

High-resolution structural MRI data acquisition used a T1-weighted MP-RAGE sequence. Parameters were as follows: TE = 2.47 ms; flip angle = 9°; TR = 1,900 ms; slice thickness = 0.5 mm; slice matrix = 512 × 512 × 176; number of slices = 176; FoV = 256 mm; and voxel resolution = 0.508 × 0.508 × 1 mm. These data were used for anatomical localization.

FMRI data were collected using ascending interleaved slice acquisition with gradient echo T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence in a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner equipped with a 20-channel head coil (Siemens; Erlangen, Germany). Scanning protocol parameters were as follows: TE = 21 ms; flip angle = 90°; TR = 2,000 m; slice thickness = 2 mm, 0.5 mm gap (25%); slice matrix = 94 × 94; number of axial slices = 44; FoV = 192 mm; and voxel resolution = 2 × 2 × 2 mm. The number of volumes acquired was on average 1,239, corresponding to a duration of approximately 41 min.

FMRI Data Preprocessing.

First, we transformed the raw DICOM files to NIFTI files using the dcm2niix [(91); https://github.com/rordenlab/dcm2niix]. When running it, we requested the output to be in the format of Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS). Next, we preprocessed the anatomical and the functional images using fMRIprep [v22.0.2; (92)]. The T1-weighted (T1w) images were corrected for intensity nonuniformity, skull-stripped, and brain tissue was segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid on the brain-extracted T1w. Volume-based spatial normalization to a standard space (MNI 152 2 mm) was also performed through nonlinear registration. For functional MRI data, a reference volume and its skull-stripped version were first generated using a custom methodology of fMRIprep. The BOLD scan was slice-time corrected to 1 s (middle of the slice acquisition range 0 to 2 s), following head motion correction, and coregistered to MNI 152 2 mm template. Several confounding time series were calculated based on the preprocessed BOLD, including the framewise displacement (FD) and three region-wise global signals. Using the fMRIprep, we also performed independent component analysis (ICA AROMA) to remove additional noise from the data. Next, the images were spatially smoothed with an isotropic Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full-width half-minimum (FWHM).

After preprocessing with fMRIprep, we further denoised the data using nilearn (93). Specifically, we regressed out the 6 motion parameters, their squares, their derivatives, and squared derivatives (73), we removed low-frequency signal intensity drift by detrending the BOLD time course of each voxel, and we standardized the BOLD signal so that it has unit variance (94).

Technical Validation of the fMRI Data.

The dataset was validated using different metrics that quantify data quality in separate domains. First, we calculated the FD of each subject to quantify potential noise in the data caused by subjects’ motion. The FD was automatically calculated by fMRIprep as the sum of the absolute values of the six motion parameters (three translations and three rotations) at every time point (34). A subject is considered to have MR signal of poor quality due to excessive head motion if the average FD across frames exceeds the threshold of 0.5 mm (95). All participants in our dataset had a mean FD less than 0.5 mm, and the average FD across all participants was 0.12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A).

Moreover, we estimated the temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR), for each voxel separately, to make sure that all subjects had comparable levels of SNR and to highlight areas with low SNR. The tSNR was calculated for every voxel as the mean signal in the corresponding voxel divided by its SD (96). This was done in the MNI 152 2 mm template space, using subjects’ preprocessed data before standardizing (which by definition results in a time series of zero mean and unit variance). SI Appendix, Fig. S4B demonstrates the tSNR levels per participant across voxels. The mean whole-brain tSNR across subjects was 95.39, which is comparable to or better than previous fMRI datasets (e.g., refs. 96 and 97). SI Appendix, Fig. S4C shows the spatial distribution of the tSNR. As expected, tSNR was higher in dorsal areas and lower in ventral areas (96).

Temporal ISC.

An ISC analysis was conducted to examine the reliability of neural responses to the dynamic audio stimulus across participants (29). This analysis was similar to that presented in Chen et al. (73). Specifically, for each participant we first extracted the BOLD signal corresponding to the time interval of the healthy eating call. Notably, when acquiring data, we locked the narrative onset to the scanner triggers (indicating TR onsets), as it is recommended in Nastase et al. (28), so that a temporal alignment across participants is assured. The first 10 s (i.e., 5 TRs) and the last 2 s (i.e., 1 TR) of this interval were then removed, as recommended by Nastase et al. (28) prior to ISC analysis. Next, for each participant we extracted the mean BOLD signal within 166 nonoverlapping brain ROIs defined from a whole-brain anatomical parcellation [AAL3v1; (98)]. For each of these ROIs, we calculated the pairwise correlation between participants’ mean BOLD signal, producing a subject by subject correlation matrix for each ROI. Afterward, we computed the mean of the lower triangle of this subject by subject correlation matrix for each ROI to obtain an estimate of the ISC in each ROI across the whole sample. Finally, for statistical testing, we bootstrapped the subjects 10,000 times and each time computed the corresponding similarity matrix with randomly selected subjects. This bootstrapping approach is considered to be the most efficient one for making ISC inferences for one group (99). The actual ISC values were then compared to the bootstrapped ISC values to obtain a P-value for each ROI, based on a one-tailed test with the alternative hypothesis of correlations greater than 0 (73, 100). These P-values were further corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR) correction, with a critical value of 0.05.

Second-level analyses were conducted by stratifying participants according to a median split of the ΔWTP for sugar-free food (subtracted by the ΔWTP for nonedible products) since this is the condition in which we observed a behavioral effect of the healthy eating call. Specifically, participants with the corrected ΔWTP > 3.0 were thought to belong to the persuaded group (n = 24), whereas participants with corrected ΔWTP ≤ 3.0 were thought to belong to the unpersuaded group (n = 26). The correction of the ΔWTP for the control condition is essential in order to control for potential changes in WTP decisions due to other elements of the study that do not directly relate to the manipulation. For instance, it would not be meaningful for a participant who equally increased the WTP for all product categories after listening to the healthy eating call to be classified in the group of persuaded participants. The median-split approach described above is common in ISC studies (42, 101). After stratifying the participants into persuaded and unpersuaded, we calculated the ROI-level ISC between all pairs of persuaded participants and all pairs of unpersuaded participants separately. We performed the second-level ISC analysis at the dyad level rather than at the subject level following previous studies (42, 101, 102). Once the pairwise correlations were computed within each group, we calculated the average ISC per ROI, after applying the Fisher r-to-z transformation to the individual coefficients, and then converted back to correlations to minimize artificially inflating the correlation values (28). For statistical testing, we performed two-tailed subject-wise permutation testing, as recommended by Chen et al. (99) for comparing the ISC between two groups. The obtained P-values were further corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR) correction, with a critical value of 0.05 (89). The ISC analyses were conducted in Python 3, using the packages nilearn and nltools (103, 104).

ISRSA.

Moreover, we examined whether our data show an Anna Karenina principle, such that all unpersuaded participants are alike, all persuaded participants are persuaded in their own way (38, 105, 106). To that end, we performed an ISRSA for the brain regions revealed by the ISC contrast between persuaded and unpersuaded participants. First, participants’ ΔWTP for sugar-free food was used to define a pairwise “behavioral similarity” matrix. Specifically, for each pair of participants, i and j, we defined their behavioral similarity as the minimum of their ΔWTP for sugar-free food, i.e., min(ΔWTPi,ΔWTPj), as proposed by Finn et al. (38). Then, we used the pairwise ISC matrix of each ROI as a measure of “neural similarity.” Finally, a Spearman rank correlation was calculated between the behavioral and the neural similarity matrices for each ROI. For statistical testing, we generated a null distribution of correlations by permuting the rows and columns of the behavioral similarity matrix 10,000 times and recomputing the ISRSA value for each ROI. The P-values were calculated as the proportion of correlation values in the null distribution that exceeded the true correlation values (105).

Spatial ISC.

Furthermore, we conducted a spatial ISC analysis to examine whether the distributed pattern of activations within each ROI was more consistent for unpersuaded than persuaded participants. Since the spatial configuration of each ROI’s voxels may vary for each participant (39), prior to the spatial ISC analysis, we functionally aligned each ROI into a common space by fitting a shared response model [SRM; (40)]. This algorithm identifies functional response components that maximally align across participants (39, 40). We selected a 100-dimensional embedding for each ROI since the number of components cannot exceed the number of voxels within each ROI and the brain parcelation we used contains some small ROIs. We estimated the transformation matrices using data from the first half of the healthy eating call (104 TRs) and subsequently applied these transformations to data from the second half of the healthy eating call (104 TRs). Similarly, data from the first half of the healthy eating call were projected based on the transformation matrices estimated from data corresponding to the second half of the healthy eating call. Finally, the projected data were concatenated to match the entire duration of the healthy eating call. This procedure is common and minimizes the risk of overfitting (39). SI Appendix, Fig. S5 demonstrates how functional alignment improved the spatial ISC in our data. Finally, to compare the spatial ISC between persuaded and unpersuaded participants, we performed two-tailed subject-wise permutation testing using 5,000 permutations (39). The obtained P-values were further corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR) correction, with a critical value of 0.05 (89). For the dynamic spatial ISC analysis (presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S2), we followed the same procedure as described above, however, instead of averaging the spatial ISC across all time points, we performed statistical testing at each individual time point.

ISFC.

We performed an ISFC analysis to investigate whether persuasion is associated with the stronger functional connectivity between brain regions. In this analysis, we included only the ROIs that were revealed by the second-level ISC analysis to have persuasion-dependent responses, following the methodological approach of Katabi et al. (63). For each of these ROIs and for each participant, we extracted the BOLD signal while listening to the healthy eating call and correlated it with the averaged BOLD signal of the remaining participants in each ROI [(63); for a more detailed description of the ISFC methodology, see Nastase et al. (28)]. These correlation values were then averaged across participants, resulting in an asymmetric ISFC matrix (63). In order to make this matrix symmetric, we averaged its upper and the lower triangles (63).

Apart from the averaging procedure described above, which was helpful for visualization, we also used the subject-level ISFC matrices for statistics. Specifically, for every pair of ROIs, we conducted an independent-samples t test in order to compare the connectivity in the persuaded group and the unpersuaded group (63). The resulting P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (89). Adjusted P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Event-Related Analysis.

As for the event-related analysis, we estimated a GLM at the time of the early evaluation stage (Bidding Task). For each participant, we first estimated the voxel-level beta coefficients of the following contrasts: sugar-free products in block 2 vs. sugar-free products in block 1, sugar-containing products in block 2 vs. sugar-containing products in block 1, and nonedible products in block 2 vs. nonedible products in block 1. Subsequently, these beta coefficients were averaged across all voxels within predefined ROIs: vmPFC, NAcc, and SOG. We selected the vmPFC and the NAcc because of their role in reward processing and food perception (41). Activity in the vmPFC has also been found to correlate with WTP decisions (44). The SOG was included as a control ROI, as it was expected to respond solely to low-level visual properties of the product images and to show no preferential activation in either group (persuaded vs. unpersuaded). These ROIs were extracted according to the AAL3 atlas used for the ISC and ISFC analysis.

A three-way mixed-ANOVA was conducted, with beta coefficients as the dependent variable, group (persuaded and unpersuaded) as a between-subjects factor, condition (sugar-free, nonedible, and sugar-containing) as a within-subjects factor, and ROI (vmPFC, NAcc, and SOG) as a within-subjects factor (42). For the control event-related analysis, we first estimated the beta coefficients of the corresponding contrast and then pooled them across participants and conditions.
