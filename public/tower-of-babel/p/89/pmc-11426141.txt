Practice of blood pressure self-monitoring and associated factors among hypertensive patients on follow-up visits at hospitals, West Shoa Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, 2022…An institution-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Nimona Amena, Adamu Birhanu, Lammi Atomsa, Dawit Teklehymanot, Befkad Derese Tilahun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBlood pressure self-monitoring is patients' regular use of personal blood pressure measurement equipment to measure their blood pressure outside of a clinical environment. Inadequate blood pressure self-monitoring remains a significant problem faced by healthcare providers and populations in the prevention of Hypertension. This study is to assess the practice of blood pressure self-monitoring and associated factors among hypertensive patients on follow-up visits at Hospitals, West Shoa zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, 2022.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional quantitative study supported by a qualitative study was carried out on 412 hypertensive patients from September 01–30/09/2022. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to collect data, and a simple random sampling technique was employed to select the study participants. Data were entered into a computer using the Kobo toolbox and exported to Statical package of social science Version 26 for analysis. Binary logistic regression analysis was run to assess the association between the dependent and independent variables and Variables with P- value < 0.25 were entered into a multivariate logistic regression to control the effects of potential confounding factors. P-value of <0.05 was taken as a cutoff point to declare a statistically significant association between independent and dependent variables.ResultThe proportion of blood pressure self-monitoring practice among hypertensive patients on follow-up visits at hospitals in the West Shoa zone was 19.6 %. Educational level [AOR=7.49, 95 %CI (3.00, 8.67)], income [AOR=3.14, 95 %CI (1.21, 8.13)], co-morbidities [AOR=5.55, 95 %CI (2.74, 11.24)], recommendation toward self-monitoring [AOR=2.40, 95 %CI (1.15, 5.03)] and awareness of self-monitoring [AOR=8.54, 95 %CI (3.54, 10.60)] were factors significantly associated with blood pressure self-monitoring practice.Conclusion and RecommendationThe proportion of blood pressure self-monitoring among hypertensive patients on follow-up visits at hospitals in the West Shoa zone was low. The practice of blood pressure self-monitoring needs to be deliberated by health education programs and recommended to minimize hypertension complications.Keywords: Blood pressure, Hypertension, Patient, Practice, Self-monitoring

Introduction

Hypertension is defined by World health organization as a persistently elevated systolic or diastolic blood pressure equal to or >140/90 mmHg in persons aged 18 years and older, based on the average of two or more accurate blood pressure measurements taken during contacts with a healthcare provider Esh et al. (2018); Bochen Cao, Gretchen A. Stevens (2020). Globally, the projected related fatalities of people with hypertension have significantly increased over the past 25 years Forouzanfar et al. (2017). Although it was previously believed that this disease was uncommon in low and middle-income nations, the results of present studies have shown as the prevalence of hypertension among lower and middle-income countries was higher (31.5 %, 1.04 billion people) than in higher-income countries (28.5 %, 349 million people Mills, Stefanescu and He (2020).

Blood pressure self-monitoring is a term that refers to a patient's regular use of personal blood pressure measurement equipment outside of a clinical environment Pk et al. (2017). The Japanese hypertension guideline indicates that blood pressure self-monitoring has many advantages, including being highly reproducible, having higher prognostic value, being effective for evaluating drug effects and their duration, facilitating long-term blood pressure control, and detecting seasonal blood pressure variation Umemura et al. (2019). The practice of blood pressure self-monitoring can help patients stick to their treatment plans. Patients who participate in blood pressure self-monitoring management become more conscious of their blood pressure levels and take an active role in their management.

Home blood pressure monitoring is recommended as an essential method for evaluating almost all untreated and treated individuals with suspected or treated hypertension Stergiou et al. (2018). It is projected to be a standard aspect of hypertension patients' care Mcgrath (2015).Clinicians can assist patients in improving self-monitoring outcomes by teaching them how to monitor their blood pressure at home appropriately Health (2016). Blood pressure self-monitoring could be integrated into hypertensive patients’ routine care in the community to enhance hypertension control Qi, Qiu and Zhang (2017).

Home blood pressure monitoring is more accurate than office blood pressure monitoring. There is increasing evidence that suggests that the office blood pressure measurement process may produce inaccurate estimates of a patient's genuine blood pressure level. The intrinsic blood pressure fluctuation and the few readings normally collected in a doctor's office, poor technique, the influence of the white coat, and the masked effect were those shortcomings that were discovered Pickering and White (2008).

Determining the associated factors of home blood pressure monitoring practice is crucial for enhancing hypertension management and improving patient outcomes. By identifying factors healthcare providers can tailor interventions to support individual patients, ultimately leading to better adherence to monitoring practices and more accurate blood pressure control (Tzeng, J.I., Chen, Y.L. & Huang, Y.H., 2020). Furthermore, understanding these factors can facilitate early detection of hypertension, allowing for timely therapeutic adjustments and reducing the risk of complications associated with uncontrolled blood pressure (Mancia, G., Fagard, R., Narkiewicz, K., et al., 2013). Improved engagement in self-monitoring also empowers patients, providing them with a sense of control over their health and fostering adherence to prescribed treatments (Bodenheimer, T., Chen, E. & Bennett, H.D., 2002).

The significance of blood pressure self-monitoring practice to nurses and global readers lies in its potential to enhance patient outcomes, promote proactive health management, and improve the overall understanding of hypertension. For nurses, effective implementation of self-monitoring practices empowers patients to take an active role in managing their blood pressure, facilitating better communication and collaboration between patients and healthcare providers (McManus, R.J., Gavin, J. & Smith, I. (2016). For global readers, it can raise awareness about hypertension, which is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular diseases worldwide. It underscores the need for educational initiatives promoting self-monitoring to improve health literacy and encourage lifestyle changes that can reduce the burden of hypertension on healthcare systems NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (2016).

Blood pressure self-monitoring can result in a clinically significant reduction in blood pressure, but the level of practice of blood pressure self-monitoring is still very low. As previously stated, the burden of hypertension has been documented to be rising. Obtaining data on blood pressure self-monitoring practice was utilized as a part of integrated treatment strategies for hypertension control. However, few studies have looked at the practice of blood pressure self-monitoring and associated factors in hypertensive patients in Ethiopia. As a result, this study aims to determine the practice of blood pressure self-monitoring and associated factors in hypertensive patients on follow-up visits in West Shoa zone hospitals.

Methods and materials

Study area and periods

The study was conducted in selected Hospitals found in West Shoa Zone. West Shoa is one of the Oromia Regional State zones located in the western direction of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Oromia, at 114 km distance. Currently, this zone has a population of 2.5 million, with around 1.2 million men, and 1.3 women living within an area of 14,788.78 square kilometers. West Shoa has a population density of 139.21, while 342,352, or 13 %, are urban residents. It has 23 districts. Currently, there are 9 Hospitals, 91 health centers, and 482 health posts. The study was conducted from September 01/09/2022 - 30/09/2022.

Study design

Institution based cross-sectional quantitative study mixed with qualitative one (Mixed study design) was carried out to assess the practice of blood pressure self-monitoring and associated factors among Hypertensive patients on follow-ups visits at Hospitals, West Shoa zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, 2022

Source population

All hypertensive patients on follow-up visits at hospitals found in the West Shoa zone.

Study population

Randomly selected hypertensive patients on follow-up visits in selected hospitals during the data collection period.

Inclusion criteria

All adult (greater than 18 years) hypertensive patients who have been on follow-up visits during the data collection period.

Exclusion criteria

Hypertensive patients on follow-up visits who have been severely ill (Hypertensive patients who has a serious or life-threatening condition that requires urgent medical attention and due to this condition unable to respond to the interview during data collection time).

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated separately for both specific objectives. For the first objective, the sample size was calculated using single population proportions using the following assumptions. 95 % confidence level, 3 % marginal error, and using the prevalence of blood pressure self-monitoring among hypertensive patients (7.75 %) from the previous study conducted at Arsi zone Wake (2020). The sample size was 306. By adding a 10 % non-response rate, the final sample size became 337.

For the 2nd objective: double population formula using Epi Info version 7.1 for individual factors at 95 % two-sided confidence level, 80 % power, and 1:1 ratio of exposure to unexposed and by taking the duration of hypertension illness, less than or equal to six years was 57.5 % as unexposed and greater than six years was 42.5 % as exposed. Hence, the result was n= 374 (Wake, 2020). By adding a 10 % non-response rate, the final sample size was 412. Therefore, the largest sample size was taken, thus 412.

The final sample size for qualitative part was determined after saturation of idea was reached and the total sample of it was 40 participants. This means four focus group discussion from two hospital each containing 10 participants.

Sampling technique

For Quantitative data: From the nine hospitals found in the West Shoa zone, four hospitals were selected using the lottery method which includes Ambo University Referral Hospital, Ambo General Hospital, Gedo General Hospital, and Ginchi primary Hospital. The sample size was proportionally allocated for these selected four hospitals based on the number of hypertensive patients on follow-up visits per month. The study participants were selected by simple random sampling using the patient's medical record number as a sampling frame (Fig. 1).

Schematic representation for proportional allocation of sample size for selected Hospitals of West Shoa zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, 2022.

For Qualitative data: Focus group discussion was conducted to explore barriers to self-monitoring of blood pressure. The focus group discussion was separately performed in purposively selected hospitals (Bako Primary Hospital and Incinni Primary Hospital). Two focus group discussion were conducted for each selected hospital. The participants were selected purposively based on their appointment for follow-up on the data collection date by asking about their willingness to participate in focus group discussion and who can communicate in the Afan Oromo language. One focus group discussion comprised ten participants.

Variables of the study

Dependent variables

The practice of blood pressure self-monitoring

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors: Age, gender, residence, educational status, occupation and monthly income

Patients-related factors: Family history of hypertension, duration of diseases, health insurance, comorbidities, awareness of self-monitoring and regular health care visit.

Health care professional-related factors: Recommendations toward using blood pressure self-monitoring and giving awareness on hypertension complications.

Data collection techniques and tools

For Quantitative data: The interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to collect data on the practice of blood pressure self-monitoring and associated factors. Data were collected by using a mobile application called Kobo Toolbox. It includes fifteen socio-demographic questions, five health professional-related questions, and twelve practice-related questions. All questions were prepared in English, translated into Afan Oromo and Amharic by experts in both languages, and translated back to English to see their consistency. The investigator gave training for three hours about the contents of the questionnaires, ways of approaching the participants, and how to obtain consent before data collection. Four Bachelor of Science holder health professionals who speak Amharic and Afan Oromo languages were involved in the data collection, and two Master of Science holder health professionals were recruited as supervisors.

For Qualitative data: The data were collected via focus group discussion, using focus group discussion guide prepared in Afan Oromo Language. Data was recorded by tape recorder and then transcribed by the investigator.

Data quality assurance

For Quantitative data:To ensure the quality of data, the questionnaires were pretested on 5 % (21) of the sample size, among hypertensive patients in Gudar Hospital, two weeks before the actual data collection period. Based on the pretest result, all the necessary amendments were made to the instruction, contents, order, and grammatical issues. During the study period, all data were checked for completeness, accuracy, and clarity by the supervisors and investigator immediately after the data were collected. Internal consistency of the data was checked by chrome bach alpha (Annex 8). Double data entry was performed to ensure the quality of data, and data was intensively cleaned before analysis.

For Qualitative data: The focus group discussion record was transcribed immediately after the discussion was conducted. The recorded tapes were transcribed carefully after listening several times and the interrelationship, connections, and patterns that emerged in the data were noted carefully.

Data processing and analysis

For Quantitative data:After data collection, the data were coded and entered into a computer using Kobo Toolbox and exported to Statical package of social science Version 26 for further analysis. Then, the mean and proportion were calculated. A binary logistic regression model was run to assess the association between the independent and dependent variables. For those whose P value was <0.25, a multivariable logistic regression model was carried out to control the effects of potential confounding factors. Multi-collinearity between independent variables was checked in linear regression by Variance inflation factors which is 1.088. The odds ratio was calculated to determine the strength of association between independent and dependent variables, and a 95 % confidence interval was utilized to guide the interpretation of the results. The model fitness for the variables was assessed by Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test statistics at P> 0.05, which shows the fitness of the model. A p-value of <0.05 was taken as a cutoff point to declare a statistically significant association between independent and dependent variables. Finally, the result was presented by using texts, tables, and graphs.

For Qualitative data: Data from focus group discussion was transcribed, formed themes, and analyzed thematically by ATLAS.ti software. The information was translated into English, and through this process, a verbal quotation was used to illustrate responses on relevant issues. Finally, it was incorporated with quantitative findings to provide complete information.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 403 respondents were enrolled in the study, making a response rate of 97.8 %. The mean age of participants was 55 years. More than half, 242 (60 %) of the respondents were males, and the majority, 384(95.3 %) of the respondents were married. More than two-thirds, 360(89.3 %) of respondents were Oromo by ethnic background, and 182(45.2 %) were protestant in religion. Regarding educational status, 63(15.6 %) of the respondents have attended above secondary school, while about 71(17.6 %) of them had no formal education, and more than one-third, 164(40.7 %) of the respondents were farmers. More than one-third, 130(32.3 %) of the respondents had an average monthly income of ≥ 3000 Ethiopian Birr, and more than half, 234(58.1 %) of the respondents were urban residents. (Table 1).

Practice of blood pressure self-monitoring among hypertensive patients

The result of the present study among those who practice blood pressure self-monitoring (is self-measurement of blood pressure by patients or assistance (family, relatives) at home using a self-monitoring device which was assessed by asking the question, ‘Do you currently self-monitor your blood pressure (i.e., check your blood pressure by yourself by having a self-monitoring blood pressure device at home?), indicates that more than half 44(55.7 %) of the participants remembered their last blood pressure reading, and also more than half 44(55.7 %) of the participants measured their blood pressure once per day. The majority, 43(54.4 %) of the respondents had measured their blood pressure in the morning, and almost one-third 28(35.4 %) of respondents sidestepped measuring their blood pressure within 30 min of exercise, while 21(26.6 %) of them had no any precautions while measuring their blood pressure. The majority, 54(68.4 %) of the respondents, did not compare their home blood pressure reading to their office blood pressure reading. In addition to this, the majority, 59(74.7) of the respondents, do not calibrate the device and more than half 46(58.2 %) of respondents check their device battery every three months. (Table 4)

Reasons for not practice of blood pressure self-monitoring

More than two-thirds 236(72.84 %) of respondents cannot afford the price of the blood pressure self-monitoring device, almost half 150(44.75 %) of the respondents perceived that blood pressure self-monitoring is inaccurate and 145(44.89 %) of respondents said that blood pressure self-monitoring had no benefit for them. More than two-thirds 230(70.9 %) of respondents didn't understand how to operate the device, and no discouragement of using blood pressure self-monitoring at their home by health care professionals, even if their recommendation toward using blood pressure self-monitoring device was low, which 77(23.77 %) (Fig. 3).

Barriers to BP self-monitoring practice

Patient-related barriers

‘‘…Most of our community are rural residents with poor socio-economic conditions, which is very difficult to afford the device to use at home. For this, we need support from different organizations to cope with self-monitoring of blood pressure…’’. A 54- year old male participant said.

A 75- year old participant said, ‘‘…most of our communities were uneducated, and difficult for us to understand how it works and how it should be monitored. So we need strict advice and health education about blood pressure self-monitoring practice…’’.

Health professionals-related barriers

A 45 years hypertensive female patient on follow-up said, ‘‘…I have no full information about blood pressure self-monitoring device and how to use it in my home. Even the health professionals did not tell me about this means of monitoring rather than telling me about keeping my follow-up schedules…’’.

Health facilities-related barriers

‘‘…Even for those who had money to buy the device, the availability of this device on the market is uncertain. For example, last time, my friend was recommended by health professionals, but he didn't have the device from the market…’’. A 54- year old male participant said.

A 45- years hypertensive male patient on follow-up said, ‘‘…I was recommended about self-monitoring of blood pressure, but this device is not available in the health facilities because I have asked for a lot of time, it is only available on the wholesaler which is very expensive to buy. So the management of the health facilities should think about it… ’’. (Table 5).

Factors associated with blood pressure self-monitoring were classified as socio-demographic factors and health professional-related factors. In bi-variable analysis, gender, educational level, monthly income, occupation, residency, duration of hypertension, health insurance, comorbidity, regular healthcare professional visits, recommendation toward blood pressure self-monitoring and awareness of hypertension-related target organ complication, and awareness of self-monitoring were factors with p-value ≤ 0.25. However, educational level, monthly income, co-morbidities, recommendation toward blood pressure self-monitoring, and awareness of blood pressure self-monitoring were the factors that have shown an association with blood pressure self-monitoring in multivariable logistic regression.

The odds of blood pressure self-monitoring practice among participants who attended above secondary education were seven times [AOR=7.49, 95 %CI (3.00, 8.67)] higher than those with no formal education. Participants who belonged to the income category of ≥ 3000 Ethiopian Birr were three times [AOR=3.14, 95 %CI (1.21, 8.13)] more to have blood pressure self-monitoring practice when compared to those who belonged to the income category of ≤999 Ethiopian Birr.

The likelihood of having blood pressure self-monitoring practice among participants who were with comorbidities like (stroke, heart disease, kidney disease, eye disease, and diabetic mellitus) were five times [AOR=5.55, 95 %CI (2.74, 11.24)] folds more as compared to those who had no comorbidities. The odds of blood pressure self-monitoring among participants who were recommended toward using blood pressure self-monitoring were two times [AOR=2.40, 95 %CI (1.15, 5.03)] higher than participants who were not recommended to use blood pressure self-monitoring .

Moreover, the odds of blood pressure self-monitoring practice among participants who have an awareness of self-monitoring were eight times [AOR=8.54, 95 %CI (3.54, 10.60)] higher than those who were not aware of blood pressure self-monitoring. (Table 6)

Discussion

Blood pressure self-monitoring was one of the essential prevention mechanisms for hypertension complications. It offers several blood pressure readings, addresses the problem of white coat hypertension, seasonal hypertension, and masked hypertension, and performs better at predicting the risk of cardiovascular events than standard office readings (23). The proportion of blood pressure self-monitoring among hypertensive patients was 19.6 %. Among them, more than half (59.5 %) of respondents had poor practice, and (40.5 %) had good practice of blood pressure self-monitoring in their homes.

This result is lower than findings from a study in the United States, which reported 53.8 % Rn et al. (2016). The variation may stem from differences in socio-economic status and the use of an online survey, which could exclude low-income patients due to accessibility issues, potentially skewing the self-monitoring proportions. Additionally, when compared to studies in the United States and Colombia, which reported 24.7 % and 60.9 % respectively (Seidlerová et al., 2014; Ostchega et al., 2017; Fang et al., 2021), this finding remains lower. Differences in study design and setting could explain these variations, as the United State study used a correlational design among an urban community population, while the Colombian study focused on hypertensive patients at community pharmacies.

This result is lower than findings from studies in China (24.5 %), Saudi Arabia (40.3 %), and Amman, Jordan (82 %) Hu, Li, and Arao (2013); Basheti and Khdair (2015); Alqahtani et al. (2021). The higher self-monitoring rate in Jordan may be attributed to the study setting, which involved institutions in Amman and included pharmacist participation for patient education on proper blood pressure monitoring. Additionally, this finding is lower than reports from Karachi (25 %), northeastern Singapore (24 %), and Romania (52 %) (Tan, Khin, and Pagi, 2005; Zahid et al., 2017; Suciu et al., 2018), with variations likely stemming from differences in study settings, such as the focus on a tertiary hospital in Karachi.

The result of this study is also lower than the study conducted in Nigeria and Ghana, where the prevalence of blood pressure self-monitoring was 44.57 % and 31.9 %, respectively In et al. (2015); JO et al. (2021). This difference may be due to differences in the study setting and the data was self-reported by hypertensive patients, respectively. But the result of this study was to some extent higher than the study conducted in Ethiopia specifically, in the Arsi zone, which was 7, 75 % Wake (2020). The discrepancy in this result may be due to the period of the study conducted.

In this study, a significant portion of respondents showed limited awareness and perceived barriers to blood pressure self-monitoring: 70.9 % did not know how to operate the equipment and could not afford it, 66.67 % had never heard of self-monitoring, 44.75 % perceived it as inaccurate, 44.58 % deemed it unimportant, 44.8 % saw no benefits, and 76.23 % were not recommended it by health professionals. Comparatively, a study in Northern Singapore revealed lower percentages of those who didn't see the need for self-monitoring (56.1 %) or were unaware of it (38.3 %). This discrepancy may relate to socio-economic conditions and health literacy differences between regions. In contrast, a study in Ghana found that 41.7 % cited lack of information and 61.5 % reported lack of funds as barriers, while a study in Ethiopia's Asella zone revealed that 91.9 % didn't know how to operate the device, with 41.2 % unable to afford it. The differences in findings may be attributed to the varying timing and contexts of the studies.

According to the qualitative findings of this study, lack of affordability, poor recommendations by health professionals, lack of awareness about blood pressure self-monitoring practice and poor attitude toward blood pressure self-monitoring practice were some important idea raised by focus group discussion participants. This findings were almost similar with the qualitative study conducted in Ghana, the biggest hurdles to self-monitoring of blood pressure were, lack of information, lack of funds to obtain a personal blood pressure monitor, blood pressure self-monitoring is not important and beneficial In et al. (2015).

Those respondents who had attended above secondary education were seven times more to practice blood pressure self-monitoring than those with no formal education. This finding was supported by the study conducted in Karachi, southern Asia Zahid et al. (2017). This reveals that it could be because higher education apprises better information about blood pressure self-monitoring and a self-care practice used to control hypertension-related complications among hypertensive patients. This indicates that more than two third (70.9 %) of respondents didn't understand how to operate the device. This result was also supported by the qualitative results as A 75 years old male hypertensive participant said that ‘‘…most of our community were uneducated and difficult for us to understand how it works and how it should be monitored… ’’. These findings suggest a critical need for targeted educational initiatives aimed at improving health literacy regarding blood pressure self-monitoring, particularly among populations with lower educational attainment.

Participants who belonged to the monthly income category of ≥ 3000 Ethiopian Birr were three times more likely to have blood pressure self-monitoring when compared to those who belonged to the income category of ≤999 Ethiopian Birr. This result was consistent with studies conducted in northeastern Singapore, Asia Tan, Khin and Pagi (2005). The reason might be that the patient can afford the device if they have enough monthly income and could be familiar with different sources of information concerning disease and blood pressure self-monitoring practice. This implies that more than two third (72.84 %) of respondents cannot afford the price of the blood pressure self-monitoring device. This result was also supported by the qualitative results as ‘‘…Most of our community are rural residents with a poor socio-economic condition, which is very difficult to afford the device to use it at home…’’. A 54 years male hypertensive participant said. Financial assistance initiatives aimed at providing blood pressure monitoring devices to low-income individuals can greatly enhance access. This could involve partnerships with government agencies, non-profits, or healthcare organizations to create programs that distribute devices at reduced costs or for free.

The likelihood of having blood pressure self-monitoring practice among participants with comorbidities like (stroke, heart disease, kidney disease, eye disease, and diabetic mellitus) was five times folds more as compared to those who had no comorbidities. This finding was in line with the study done in Ethiopia, which was nearly four times more Wake (2020). The reason might be due to thinking that the patient is expected to find the strategy to handle hypertension complications, which is blood pressure self-monitoring practice. The healthcare providers should prioritize education and resources for blood pressure self-monitoring among patients with comorbidities, as they may be more receptive to such interventions. Next, it would be beneficial to implement targeted educational programs and support systems that empower these patients to effectively monitor their blood pressure,

The odds of blood pressure self-monitoring among participants who were recommended toward using blood pressure self-monitoring were twice higher than those who were not recommended. This study was supported by a study done in the United States Ostchega et al. (2017). The reason might be due to awareness creation regarding hypertension complications, and training on procedures by health care professionals. This indicates that only 23.7 % were recommended to use blood pressure self-monitoring in their home. This result was also supported by the qualitative results. A 45 year female hypertensive patient on follow-up said, ‘‘…The health professionals didn't tell us about this means of monitoring rather than how to keep our follow-up schedules…’’. The healthcare providers should receive training on effectively communicating the benefits of self-monitoring and regularly incorporate these discussions during patient consultations, thereby empowering patients to take an active role in managing their health.

The odds of blood pressure self-monitoring among participants who had an awareness of self-monitoring were eight times higher than those who had no awareness about self-monitoring. This study was supported by the study conducted in Ethiopia, Addis Ababa, individuals who had an awareness of self-monitoring were more likely to monitor their blood pressure Amare, Nedi and Berhe (2020). The healthcare providers and public health organizations should collaborate to create targeted awareness programs that not only educate patients about the importance of monitoring but also provide practical training and resources to facilitate self-monitoring practices, ultimately leading to better management of hypertension and improved overall health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strength: Supported by a qualitative approach

Limitation: Lack of related literatures to discuss with qualitative results, self-reported practice of blood pressure self-monitoring which could attribute to overestimation of the findings and the study design was cross sectional, where cross-sectional study design cannot create causal attribution between independent variables and dependent variables. The future researcher could use qualitative study on barriers to self-monitoring of blood pressure.

Conclusion

The proportion of blood pressure self-monitoring practice among hypertensive patients on follow-up visits at hospitals in the West Shoa zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, was low. Educational level, co-morbidities, recommendation toward blood pressure self-monitoring, and awareness of self-monitoring were factors significantly associated with blood pressure self-monitoring and poor socio-economic status, negative perception to blood pressure self-monitoring, unavailability of the device, lack of formal education and lack of awareness were barriers to blood pressure self-monitoring practice.
