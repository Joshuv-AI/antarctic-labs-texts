Introduction

Transfusion of blood components can be lifesaving and, at times, the only available treatment option. Despite substantial efforts to minimise associated risks, it should be undertaken only when no alternatives exist and when anticipated benefits clearly outweigh harms.1

Transfusion is associated with adverse reactions arising from multiple factors, including properties of the blood component, patient characteristics and comorbidities. In addition, errors may occur at any stage of the transfusion process, further increasing the risk of adverse events.

Although some reactions are self-limiting, others may lead to serious complications or even death. Reducing exposure to transfusion can improve clinical outcomes, reduce mortality and shorten hospital length of stay.2

For these reasons, the WHO has urged the implementation of Patient Blood Management (PBM).3 PBM is defined as ‘a patient-centered, systematic, evidence-based approach to improve patient outcomes by managing and preserving a patient’s own blood while promoting patient safety and empowerment’.4

PBM is ethically grounded, focusing on optimising the patient’s condition while minimising transfusion-induced complications.5 Key principles of PBM are patient education and empowerment, informed consent and shared decision-making.3 Patients’ values and preferences are also at the core of evidence-based medicine, together with the best research evidence and clinical expertise.6

It is essential that patients understand the purpose, risks, benefits and alternatives to transfusion. Patients have a fundamental right to refuse blood transfusion. In this context, obtaining consent for transfusion is critical to shared decision-making, enabling patients to make informed choices about their care.7

Through the consent process, patients or their legal representatives evaluate and authorise the use of blood components after receiving the necessary information regarding the indications for therapy and its associated risks. This approach ensures respect for patient autonomy.8

According to the WHO, unsafe healthcare harms a large number of patients each year. In 2021, at its 74th session, the World Health Assembly adopted the Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030. The plan aims to achieve the maximum possible reduction in avoidable harm resulting from unsafe healthcare globally. Its implementation is guided by seven principles, one of which is to engage patients and families as partners in safe care, including through institutional policies that support robust informed consent.9

Improved consent and shared decision-making are recognised as fundamental to transfusion safety.7 These practices are a crucial means of empowering patients to take an active role in the haemovigilance process. When patients and families understand the procedures and associated risks, they can be vigilant in monitoring the patient’s condition, helping prevent errors and enabling swifter responses to acute transfusion reactions.

In 2020, the Advisory Committee on the Safety of Blood, Tissues and Organs updated its recommendations on patient consent for blood transfusion. All patients who require blood components, or who may require transfusion during surgery, should be fully informed about the reasons for transfusion, its benefits and risks and alternatives. The consent discussion should be documented, and the consent form filed in the patient’s medical record by the responsible physician.10

Previous studies indicate that approximately 50–60% of patients recalled discussing transfusion with a clinician.11 12 A 2022 UK audit reported that only 475 of 1356 transfused patients (35%) had received both written and verbal information on the reasons for transfusion and its benefits, risks and alternatives. Although this represents an improvement on the 2021 figure (26%), the proportion remains low.13

Consent can be particularly complex in specific populations, including children, individuals who lack decision-making capacity (eg, unconscious patients) and those who refuse transfusion, for example, for religious reasons.

Transfusion is a common intervention in neonatal intensive care units.14 In the context of inherited haemoglobinopathies, blood components are frequently required throughout childhood.15 16 In paediatric populations, parents/guardians or the state may impose their worldviews on children and make decisions that patients themselves might not make later in life.17

Emergencies in which the patient is unable to express a view and no substitute decision-maker is available are commonly accepted exceptions to obtaining consent before medical intervention. When a patient is unable to provide informed consent, it is important that clear directions exist regarding the timing and process for obtaining consent as soon as practicable.18

Patients may decline blood components for various reasons, most commonly on religious grounds or owing to concerns about transfusion risks. Jehovah’s Witnesses, who number approximately 8.5 million worldwide, generally regard receipt of donor blood as incompatible with their interpretation of biblical texts.19 Failure to respect a competent adult’s refusal can raise ethical and legal issues and, in some jurisdictions, expose clinicians or institutions to proceedings.20

Healthcare professionals involved in transfusions should possess core knowledge of blood components, the indications for their use, available alternatives, the risks and benefits and the recognition and management of adverse reactions. Transfusion-specific consent is critical to enabling shared decision-making, and all patients have the right to be adequately informed so that they can make choices about their care. Healthcare institutions should be encouraged to review local practice to ensure robust consent processes that support safe care.7

Therefore, in line with the WHO Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030, there is an urgent need to strengthen and broaden the approach to obtaining consent for transfusion. Policies mandating informed consent for the administration of blood components should be implemented universally, and consent processes should align with the principle of patient autonomy and promote transfusion safety.

Despite the ethical importance of informed consent in transfusion, there is little empirical evidence on how consent processes are implemented across clinical settings. To date, only one systematic review has been published, and it explored patients’ and healthcare professionals’ perceptions of transfusion rather than the consent forms themselves or their compliance with regulatory and international standards.21 Evidence is also limited on the use of emerging technologies, such as electronic or multimedia consent platforms, to obtain consent. Likewise, the process of securing consent in special populations, such as neonates or unconscious patients, has been largely neglected and warrants focused investigation.

By systematically mapping the global use and characteristics of transfusion consent forms, this scoping review will address these gaps. It will catalogue the technologies used to obtain consent, assess whether consent documentation conforms to international recommendations, analyse the key elements presented to patients and families and describe practices in special populations. In doing so, the review will provide a baseline for standardising consent procedures and improving patient-centred care. It will also identify areas where evidence is sparse and outline priorities for future interventional studies, such as developing digital consent tools, creating guideline-aligned form templates and designing educational initiatives, to enhance transfusion safety and ethical practice worldwide.

Objectives

The primary outcome of this scoping review is to map the global use and characteristics of transfusion consent processes and forms. Secondary outcomes are: (1) to describe technologies used in the process of obtaining consent for transfusion; (2) to identify the key elements typically included in consent forms and to assess compliance with international recommendations; (3) to assess patients’ and families’ understanding of the consent process; (4) to describe practices in special populations; and (5) to highlight gaps and limitations in the current literature.

Methods

We will adopt a scoping review methodology to examine how transfusion-specific consent processes and forms are implemented in practice. This protocol is registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF Registration DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/NU69J).22 The review will be conducted from October 2025 to March 2026.

The review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis23; the methodological framework is detailed below.

Step 02: developing and aligning the inclusion criteria with the objectives and questions

We defined inclusion and exclusion criteria that map directly onto the PCC elements and the types of sources to be considered.

The eligible population includes patients for whom transfusion is indicated or contemplated and, when the unit of analysis is the transfusion consent process or form itself, family members/caregivers and healthcare professionals involved in obtaining or documenting consent. Studies that address consent not specific to transfusion, such as generic procedural consent without transfusion-specific analysis, will not be eligible.

Regarding the concept, we will include records that examine transfusion-specific consent processes and forms in sufficient detail to permit charting of who obtains consent and when, the modality or technology used (paper, verbal or digital/electronic), the validity period, documentation practices and signatories and the substantive content of the discussion or form. The scope of transfusion encompasses whole blood and blood components (eg, red cells, platelets, plasma and cryoprecipitate). Records focusing exclusively on industrial plasma-derived medicinal products (such as coagulation factors or immunoglobulins) without a transfusion-consent component will be excluded to maintain conceptual coherence with the review questions.

The context is deliberately broad: any healthcare setting and any jurisdiction worldwide are eligible, and no language or date restrictions will be applied at the selection stage. With respect to types of sources, we will consider primary empirical studies (quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods). Opinion pieces, editorials, reviews and conference abstracts lacking extractable primary data will not be included, although reference lists of relevant items may be screened for additional sources.

Step 03: describing the planned approach to evidence searching

Five databases will be searched: PubMed, Embase, LILACS, Web of Science and Scopus. Grey literature will be explored through Google Scholar and the Open Access Theses and Dissertations (OATD) platform.

A comprehensive strategy combining controlled vocabulary — Medical Subject Headings (MeSH; the National Library of Medicine’s thesaurus), Emtree (Elsevier’s life-sciences thesaurus used by Embase), and Health Sciences Descriptors (DeCS; the PAHO/WHO trilingual health-sciences vocabulary) — and free-text terms underpins the search. An initial, full and reproducible strategy has been developed for PubMed and is provided in online supplemental appendix 1; following calibration, this strategy will be adapted to the syntax and subject headings of the other databases. In addition, reference lists of included studies will be screened to identify additional sources not retrieved through electronic searches.

In Google Scholar, manual searches will be carried out using keyword combinations derived from the final search strategy, and the first 100 results ordered by relevance will be screened. In OATD, advanced searches will be conducted using the same terms applied to the title and abstract fields.

Records retrieved from all sources will be exported to EndNote V.21 for reference management and de-duplication prior to screening.

Study selection will be carried out in two stages. In the first stage, titles and abstracts will be screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (see table 1). Two independent reviewers will conduct the screening, and any disagreements will be resolved through discussion. If consensus is not reached, a third reviewer will be consulted. In the second stage, the full texts of potentially eligible records will be retrieved and assessed for eligibility using the same process.

Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be documented to ensure transparency, and the overall selection process will be summarised using a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses: extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) flow diagram.

Step 04: data charting and analytical data management

All relevant data will be extracted independently by two reviewers and recorded in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. This charting instrument will be drafted to mirror the review questions and the operational definitions previously set out, ensuring that each field maps to an a priori analytical need. The proposed variables include bibliographic information (eg, authors, year of publication, title, country, journal or source); study characteristics (design, setting, study population, sample size); objectives and methods used; type of data collected (quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods); and a detailed capture of transfusion-specific consent processes and forms. We will chart who obtains consent and at what point in the care pathway, the modality or technology used (paper, verbal or digital/electronic), the validity period, documentation practices and required signatories and the substantive content discussed or recorded, namely risks, benefits, alternatives and options aligned with PBM. We will also extract data on patients’ and families’ understanding, recall, satisfaction and experience and on practices reported for special populations. such as children, individuals lacking decision-making capacity, perioperative context, emergency care and patients who refuse transfusion.

The data extraction tool will be refined as needed during the process. Any disagreements will be discussed, and if consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted. Given the heterogeneity expected across selected studies, the instrument will combine structured categorical fields with free-text fields to retain contextual nuance.

Step 05: collating, summarising and reporting the results

The extracted data will be presented in a diagrammatic and/or tabular form, accompanied by a structured narrative synthesis. Results will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR checklist.24

Expected outputs will include, where reported: (1) a comparative overview of how transfusion consent is operationalised across settings (who obtains consent, timing, documentation, signatories), (2) a content matrix of consent forms against international recommendations, (3) frequency tables of consent modalities and technologies used (paper, verbal, electronic) and (4) a thematic synthesis of patient and family outcomes regarding understanding, recall and satisfaction. Where data allow, findings will be stratified by clinical setting (paediatrics, inpatient wards, outpatient/day transfusion, perioperative/anaesthesia and emergency). Gaps in the literature and areas with insufficient evidence regarding transfusion consent will be highlighted. No formal critical appraisal is planned, consistent with the aims of a scoping review; this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Step 06: consultation

In line with JBI guidance, we will conduct a focused stakeholder consultation after the preliminary synthesis to enhance interpretability, assess face validity and identify practice-relevant gaps. Participants will include two transfusion medicine experts and two patient representatives with prior experience of transfusion decision-making. Sessions will be held virtually as brief semi-structured interviews.

Participants will receive a succinct summary of the evidence map. The topic guide will invite participants to comment on the clarity and completeness of outputs, the suitability of stratifications, contextual factors that may refine interpretation and priorities for future research, education and policy. Their input will be used to validate and refine the interpretation of findings and inform the Discussion section of the final review.

Discussion

This scoping review aims to summarise the current evidence and identify gaps in practices for obtaining transfusion consent, and to offer recommendations for future research.

By mapping the global use and characteristics of transfusion consent processes and forms across diverse settings, this scoping review will generate a comprehensive overview of current practices. Evidence from recent studies highlights the need for standardisation and improved readability: audits of consent forms used in Spanish hospitals found inconsistent formats, overuse of capital letters and abbreviations and readability levels that exceeded patients’ comprehension.25 Similarly, electronic consent systems have been shown to improve accuracy and reduce processing time (saving approximately 26 min per case in one emergency department study).26 These findings suggest that a robust synthesis of existing forms, their alignment with international guidelines and the technologies used will inform best practices and facilitate the development of standardised templates that are accessible to patients. The review’s findings may also support regulatory and institutional policies that encourage the adoption of digital consent platforms and require clear, patient-centred language, thereby enhancing transfusion safety and patient understanding.

Beyond clinical practice and policy, the review is expected to identify gaps that warrant targeted investigation. Pilot studies could assess whether interactive electronic consent platforms improve knowledge retention, satisfaction and decisional confidence relative to traditional paper forms or explore how consent is secured by proxies in emergency settings. The review will also expose limitations in educational content delivered during consent and may guide the design of interventions, such as multimedia tools or tailored educational materials, to enhance patients’ and families’ understanding. Identifying these areas will not only lay the groundwork for future systematic reviews and intervention studies but also support iterative improvements in practice and policy to ensure that informed consent for transfusion is both ethically sound and patient centred.

Ethics and dissemination

As this study exclusively uses data from publicly accessible published and grey literature, research ethics approval is not required. The findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentation at scientific conferences.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and public representatives were not involved in the initial design of this scoping review protocol. However, they will be actively involved in the consultation phase (Step 06), planned to take place after preliminary data extraction and thematic synthesis.

Two patient representatives with prior experience in transfusion-related decision-making will be invited to contribute to the interpretation and refinement of the findings. These individuals will be selected in collaboration with patient support groups and transfusion-related associations to ensure that they provide relevant lived experience and insight.

Their input will be used to validate the interpretation of results and to help define practical implications and research gaps. They will also be consulted on strategies for disseminating the findings in accessible formats to patient communities and transfusion service users.
