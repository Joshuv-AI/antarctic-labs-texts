Robot‐Assisted Partial Nephrectomy via a Retroperitoneal Approach With Selective Arterial Clamping Guided by Blood Flow Analysis in a Horseshoe Kidney: A Case Report

Introduction

Horseshoe kidney is the most common congenital renal fusion anomaly, occurring in approximately 0.15%–0.25% of the population. It is characterized by renal malrotation, limited mobility, and highly variable vascular anatomy, which can complicate nephron‐sparing surgery [1].

Renal tumors arising in horseshoe kidneys are uncommon. With advances in minimally invasive surgery, laparoscopic and robot‐assisted partial nephrectomy have increasingly been reported [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. However, the optimal surgical approach and vascular control remain unclear because of the anatomical complexity of the horseshoe kidney.

Case Presentation

A 43‐year‐old woman presented with epigastric pain. Abdominal ultrasonography incidentally revealed a horseshoe kidney, and magnetic resonance imaging detected a right renal tumor. She had no significant medical history, and laboratory examination showed preserved renal function (serum creatinine 0.6 mg/dL; eGFR 85.18 mL/min/1.73 m2). There were no other remarkable findings in the abdomen.

Dynamic contrast‐enhanced computed tomography revealed a 16 × 16 × 11 mm hypovascular tumor located on the dorsal aspect of the right renal moiety, suspicious for renal cell carcinoma (Figure 1). The right renal moiety was smaller than the left and was extensively covered by the liver on the anterior side.

Dynamic contrast‐enhanced computed tomography showing a hypovascular tumor measuring 16 × 16 × 11 mm located on the dorsal aspect of the right renal moiety of a horseshoe kidney.

A preoperative renal biopsy was not performed. Imaging findings suggested possible malignancy, including renal cell carcinoma, although the differential diagnosis also included benign tumors such as angiomyolipoma or oncocytoma; therefore, surgical resection was selected as both a diagnostic and therapeutic approach.

Three‐dimensional computed tomography demonstrated two renal arteries supplying the right renal moiety (Figure 2A). An upper pole artery originated directly from the abdominal aorta and coursed posterior to the inferior vena cava, whereas a lower pole artery branched near the aortic bifurcation and supplied the renal isthmus. Blood flow analysis using SYNAPSE VINCENT (Fujifilm, Japan) showed that the tumor was supplied exclusively by the upper pole artery (Figure 2B). Based on these findings, selective clamping of the upper pole artery via a retroperitoneal approach was planned.

(A) Three‐dimensional computed tomography demonstrating the vascular anatomy of the right renal moiety. An upper pole artery originates directly from the abdominal aorta and courses posterior to the inferior vena cava, while a lower pole artery arises near the aortic bifurcation and supplies the renal isthmus. (B) Blood flow analysis using VINCENT showing tumor perfusion exclusively from the upper pole artery.

Surgery was performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the right lateral decubitus position. Because a conventional retroperitoneal approach from the midaxillary line was expected to result in peritoneal entry due to extensive liver coverage, the retroperitoneal space was accessed via a pararectus approach, and ports were placed accordingly. The retroperitoneal space was established via a pararectus incision at the level of the umbilicus and expanded using a balloon dissector. A robotic port was inserted through the pararectus incision, followed by placement of a second port on the midaxillary line near the tip of the 11th rib. Two additional robotic ports were then placed at 7‐cm intervals so that the four robotic ports were aligned in a straight line. A 12‐mm assistant port was inserted between the camera and left robotic ports (Figure 3).

Port placement for robot‐assisted partial nephrectomy via a retroperitoneal approach. A conventional retroperitoneal approach along the midaxillary line was not feasible because the right renal moiety was extensively covered by the liver, with a high risk of peritoneal entry. Therefore, the retroperitoneal space was established using a pararectus approach, in which access was obtained through an incision along the lateral border of the rectus abdominis muscle to allow a more anterior and direct entry into the retroperitoneum. Ports were placed accordingly.

After robotic docking (da Vinci Xi), the working space was limited, and robotic incision of the lateroconal fascia was technically difficult. Laparoscopic dissection was therefore performed initially to incise the lateroconal fascia, mobilize the dorsal kidney, and secure the renal artery. The procedure was subsequently continued using the robotic system, and partial nephrectomy was completed under selective clamping of the upper pole artery. Because the tumor was small, hemostasis of the tumor base was achieved using electrocautery without tumor bed suturing. Renorrhaphy was then performed with 0 Vicryl sutures, and renal blood flow was restored. Because preoperative blood flow analysis using VINCENT demonstrated that the tumor was completely supplied by the target artery, partial nephrectomy was performed without intraoperative ultrasonographic confirmation.

The total operative time was 162 min, estimated blood loss was 10 mL, and warm ischemia time was 10 min and 11 s. Histopathological examination revealed angiomyolipoma with negative surgical margins. The postoperative course was uneventful. The drain was removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 7. Renal function remained unchanged at 9 months after surgery, with a serum creatinine level of 0.56 mg/dL and an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 91.86 mL/min/1.73 m2. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography performed at the same time showed no abnormal findings.

Discussion

Horseshoe kidney is characterized by renal malrotation, limited mobility, and highly variable vascular anatomy, which increases the technical complexity of nephron‐sparing surgery [1]. With advances in minimally invasive surgery, laparoscopic and robot‐assisted partial nephrectomy have increasingly been reported for renal tumors arising in horseshoe kidneys [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

In most previous reports, vascular control was determined primarily by anatomical imaging, and selective clamping often required intraoperative confirmation or clamping of multiple segmental arteries. However, anatomical information alone may not accurately predict the perfusion of the tumor‐bearing region in horseshoe kidneys.

Kobari et al. [7] reported the usefulness of selective arterial clamping guided by preoperative tumor perfusion analysis using VINCENT during robot‐assisted partial nephrectomy in a horseshoe kidney, with intraoperative ultrasonography used to confirm the absence of blood flow around the tumor. Numakura et al. [8] noted that the complex vascular anatomy and limited mobility of horseshoe kidneys may restrict robotic maneuverability and described the usefulness of a hybrid laparoscopic–robotic approach. Uketa et al. [2] reported that a retroperitoneal approach may provide direct access to posterior tumors and renal arteries. These reports suggest that surgical strategies should be individualized according to tumor location and vascular anatomy.

In the present case, the tumor was located on the dorsal aspect of the right renal moiety, and a retroperitoneal approach was selected for direct access to the tumor and renal arteries. Because extensive liver coverage made a standard midaxillary retroperitoneal approach unsuitable, the retroperitoneal space was established via a pararectus approach. Preoperative blood flow analysis using VINCENT demonstrated that the tumor was supplied exclusively by the upper pole artery, allowing selective arterial clamping without intraoperative ultrasonographic confirmation. Because the operative field remained restricted, a hybrid laparoscopic–robotic approach was adopted to maintain surgical flexibility and achieve secure vascular control and precise tumor excision.

Although histopathological examination in the present case revealed angiomyolipoma, previous studies have reported that renal cell carcinoma is the most common tumor arising in horseshoe kidneys, whereas benign tumors such as angiomyolipoma are rare [9].

Although conclusions drawn from a single case are inherently limited, this experience suggests that combining functional blood flow analysis with flexible, patient‐specific surgical planning may expand the feasibility and safety of selective arterial clamping in anatomically complex horseshoe kidneys.

Conclusion

Robot‐assisted partial nephrectomy for renal tumors in a horseshoe kidney can be safely performed with careful preoperative evaluation and individualized surgical planning. Functional assessment of tumor‐specific blood supply using VINCENT may facilitate precise selective arterial clamping and minimize unnecessary renal ischemia.
