Fulminant varicella-zoster virus meningoencephalitis without rash: Aggressive perivascular necrotizing and leptomeningeal MRI findings
Authors: Rio Kou, Saya Ando, Akari Nishida, Kango Kawase, Naotoshi Hatsuda, Takashi Katakami, Takehiro Ueda, Mitsunori Kanagaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaricella-zoster virus (VZV) can cause a wide spectrum of central nervous system (CNS) complications, including meningitis, encephalitis, and vasculopathy, occasionally in the absence of characteristic cutaneous manifestations. We report a case of fulminant VZV-associated CNS infection in an immunocompetent man in his 40s without rash, who presented with rapidly progressive impaired consciousness. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed diffuse leptomeningitis, widespread intracranial vasculopathy, and extensive perivascular parenchymal lesions characterized by hemorrhage, necrotizing changes, and markedly restricted diffusion. Magnetic resonance angiography demonstrated irregular narrowing of the bilateral anterior and middle cerebral arteries, consistent with intracranial vasculopathy. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis using multiplex polymerase chain reaction detected VZV DNA, confirming the diagnosis. Despite antiviral therapy and supportive treatment, the clinical course was fatal. This case highlights that VZV CNS infection may produce aggressive perivascular destructive lesions beyond classic vasculopathy, even in patients without rash, and underscores the importance of comprehensive MRI evaluation in atypical fulminant presentations.Keywords: Varicella-zoster virus infection, Encephalitis, Meningitis, Vasculopathy

Introduction

VZV can cause a wide range of central nervous system (CNS) complications, including meningitis, encephalitis, and vasculopathy [1,2]. Neuroimaging is essential for the evaluation of VZV-related CNS disease, particularly in patients without cutaneous manifestations, in whom the diagnosis may be delayed [3,4]. Typical imaging findings of VZV vasculopathy include multifocal infarctions or hemorrhagic lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), segmental stenosis or dilatation of intracranial arteries on magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), and vessel wall enhancement on contrast-enhanced vessel wall imaging in the detection of arterial inflammation [[5], [6], [7]].

We report a rare case of fulminant VZV CNS infection without rash, presenting with rapidly progressive impaired consciousness. MRI demonstrated widespread vasculopathy, extensive leptomeningitis, and distinctive perivascular parenchymal lesions characterized by hemorrhage and necrotic tissue destruction. This case highlights the diverse and aggressive imaging spectrum of VZV CNS infection and underscores the importance of comprehensive MRI assessment in atypical presentations.

Case report

A man in his 40s with a history of right testicular seminoma presented with acute neurological deterioration. Six months before the current admission, he had undergone a high inguinal orchiectomy for stage I seminoma and was managed with active surveillance without adjuvant therapy. Several days before admission, he developed progressively worsening headache, dizziness, and vomiting. On the day before admission (day X − 1), he visited a local medical facility, where noncontrast head computed tomography (CT) reportedly revealed no apparent abnormalities, and he was discharged home.

On the morning of day X, he was found by his family with impaired consciousness and was transported to our emergency department by ambulance. Shortly after arrival, he developed generalized tonic–clonic seizures. Because of marked involuntary movements and ongoing seizures, a detailed neurological examination could not be reliably performed. A repeat head CT obtained on day X demonstrated newly developed ventricular dilatation (Fig. 1A) compared with the CT performed the previous day (not shown), raising concern for acute hydrocephalus secondary to meningitis. In addition, newly emerged extensive hypoattenuating areas were observed in the perivascular brain parenchyma along the course of the bilateral middle cerebral and anterior cerebral arteries, as well as in the brainstem and the superior surface of the left cerebellum (Fig. 1B). Based on these findings, concomitant acute encephalitis or encephalopathy was suspected, and emergent ventricular drainage was performed.

(B) Noncontrast head CT shows newly developed hypoattenuating areas in the brain parenchyma along the courses of the bilateral middle cerebral and anterior cerebral arteries, as well as in the brainstem and the superior surface of the left cerebellum.

Serological testing revealed elevated levels of VZV-specific IgG (74) and IgM (4.94), suggesting reactivation of varicella-zoster virus. Lumbar puncture demonstrated xanthochromic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). CSF analysis showed pleocytosis with 84 cells/µL, elevated protein of 112.2 mg/dL, and normal glucose. Multiplex polymerase chain reaction testing (FilmArray meningitis/encephalitis panel) detected VZV DNA, and CSF serology was positive for VZV-specific IgG. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of VZV-associated central nervous system infection was made. Information regarding the patient's history of herpes zoster, varicella, and prior varicella vaccination could not be obtained.

A contrast-enhanced brain MRI performed on day X + 14 demonstrated diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement involving the cerebral hemispheres, cerebellum, and brainstem (Fig. 2A and B). Corresponding to the hypoattenuating areas observed on the CT, extensive hyperintense lesions were identified on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) in the bilateral temporal and frontal lobes, midbrain, pons, medulla oblongata, and cerebellum (Fig. 3A-F).

Magnetic resonance imaging on day X + 14. (A) T1-weighted image demonstrates internal hyperintensity consistent with hemorrhage (arrows). (B) Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted image shows ring-like enhancement of the perivascular lesions (arrows) and extensive leptomeningeal enhancement along the cerebral hemispheres, brainstem, and cerebellum, consistent with meningitis (arrowheads). (C) T2*-weighted image shows corresponding hypointense foci, consistent with hemorrhage (arrows). (D, E) Diffusion-weighted image (B = 1000) and ADC map demonstrate restricted diffusion in the brain parenchyma surrounding the bilateral middle cerebral and anterior cerebral arteries (arrows). (F) Contrast-enhanced perfusion imaging demonstrates markedly reduced perfusion within the lesions (arrows).

T2-weighted MR imaging on day X + 14. (A-F) Axial T2-weighted MR images demonstrate extensive hyperintense lesions in the bilateral temporal and frontal lobes, midbrain, pons, medulla oblongata, and cerebellum, corresponding to the hypoattenuating areas observed on the CT.

In particular, restricted diffusion was noted in the brain parenchyma surrounding the bilateral middle cerebral arteries (MCAs) and anterior cerebral arteries (ACAs) (Fig. 2D and E). These perivascular lesions showed ring-like enhancement on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) after contrast administration and contained internal hemorrhagic components, appearing hyperintense on T1WI and hypointense on T2*-weighted imaging (T2*WI) (Fig. 2A-C). Contrast-enhanced perfusion imaging demonstrated markedly reduced or absent perfusion within these lesions (Fig. 2F). MRA revealed irregular narrowing of the bilateral M1 segments of the MCAs and the bilateral ACAs (Fig. 4). On contrast-enhanced vessel wall imaging, assessment of enhancement of the stenotic arterial walls was limited due to prominent diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement. In addition, an acute infarction was identified in the right corona radiata (not shown).

Magnetic resonance angiography. Magnetic resonance angiography demonstrates irregular narrowing of the bilateral M1 segments of the middle cerebral arteries and the bilateral anterior cerebral arteries, suggestive of vasculopathy.

Treatment with intravenous acyclovir (10 mg/kg every 8 hours) and levetiracetam (1000 mg every 12 hours) was initiated on day X. On day X + 3, high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g) was administered to reduce cerebral edema. Despite antiviral therapy, antiepileptic treatment, and supportive care, the patient’s impaired consciousness and neurological deficits showed minimal improvement throughout the clinical course. He ultimately died on day X + 57. No cutaneous lesions suggestive of herpes zoster were observed at any point during the disease course. No autopsy was performed.

Discussion

Varicella–zoster virus (VZV) establishes lifelong latency in cranial nerve and dorsal root ganglia after primary infection and may reactivate to involve the central nervous system [1,2,8]. Reactivation can result in a wide spectrum of neurological complications, including meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis, and vasculopathy [2,8,9]. VZV vasculopathy is thought to result from direct viral invasion of the arterial wall with subsequent transmural spread following viral reactivation [8,10]. Neuroimaging findings of VZV vasculopathy have typically been reported as territorial infarctions or multifocal hemorrhagic lesions, often accompanied by arterial stenosis or irregularity on angiographic studies [5−7,11].

In the present case, however, the imaging findings were not limited to vasculitic changes. In addition to widespread leptomeningitis, extensive perivascular parenchymal lesions with hemorrhage and necrotic tissue destruction were observed, raising several possibilities regarding the underlying pathophysiology.

One possible mechanism is direct extension of infection from diffuse meningitis into the adjacent brain parenchyma and/or vasculitis involving small leptomeningeal and cortical arteries. This mechanism could best explain the predominantly superficial ischemic and hemorrhagic lesions observed in the cerebral hemispheres, brainstem, and cerebellum. Previous reports have described VZV-associated meningoencephalitis presenting with band-like lesions along the surface of the brainstem, referred to as the “bloomy rind sign [12].” The presence of similar superficial brainstem involvement in our case supports this interpretation.

Another possible explanation is vasculitis predominantly affecting large intracranial arteries, such as the anterior cerebral arteries (ACAs) and middle cerebral arteries (MCAs), resulting in hemorrhagic infarctions. However, the ischemic lesions in our case did not conform to the typical territorial distribution of these large vessels and instead showed a diffuse, predominantly superficial pattern. Therefore, large-vessel–predominant vasculopathy alone was considered insufficient to account for the observed imaging findings. Taken together, the imaging features of this case suggest a complex pathological process involving diffuse leptomeningitis, vasculitis at the level of leptomeningeal and cortical arteries, and direct perivascular extension of inflammation into the brain parenchyma. This case highlights the potential for VZV CNS infection to produce destructive perivascular lesions beyond classic vasculopathy, particularly in patients without cutaneous manifestations.

Given the fulminant clinical course and destructive imaging findings, a broad differential diagnosis was initially considered, including other infectious meningoencephalitides, inflammatory demyelinating disorders, and systemic vasculitic diseases. However, these conditions were considered unlikely based on the cerebrospinal fluid findings, clinical course, and the absence of supportive systemic features. Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis or its hemorrhagic variant was also considered; however, the predominant leptomeningeal involvement and the presence of perivascular necrotizing lesions were atypical for these entities.

In the present case, the typical cutaneous manifestations of VZV reactivation were absent. However, VZV reactivation in the absence of rash, referred to as zoster sine herpete, is increasingly recognized and may make the diagnosis challenging. Several studies have reported that cutaneous eruptions are absent in a substantial proportion of patients with VZV-associated neurological disease, which can delay recognition of the infection [3,4]. Consequently, diagnosis may rely on cerebrospinal fluid virologic testing, particularly in patients presenting with unexplained meningoencephalitis or vasculitic imaging findings.

Although reactivation of varicella-zoster virus within the central nervous system is generally considered more common in immunocompromised patients, a retrospective study from Chile reported that approximately half of affected patients were immunocompetent [3]. Similarly, Takeshima et al. found that many patients with VZV meningitis had no identifiable predisposing factors [13]. These observations underscore the important clinical message that severe VZV-associated CNS infection can occur even in relatively young and apparently immunocompetent individuals, as demonstrated in the present case.

In conclusion, this case expands the recognized imaging spectrum of VZV CNS infection by demonstrating aggressive perivascular destructive lesions beyond classic vasculopathy. Recognition of this imaging pattern may facilitate earlier consideration of VZV infection and prompt cerebrospinal fluid virologic testing, even in patients without rash or apparent immunodeficiency.
