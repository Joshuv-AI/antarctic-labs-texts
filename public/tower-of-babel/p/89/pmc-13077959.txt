Deciding whether to donate fetal tissue for research
Authors: Katherine E MacDuffie, Jennifer C Dempsey, Lucinda A Cort, Dan Doherty, Ian Glass, Lyndsey Benson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman fetal tissue (HFT) has played a pivotal role in many areas of biomedical research. Since its inception, HFT research has been a topic of ethical debate and specific regulation due to its link to abortion. To date, the ethical debate surrounding HFT donation for research has proceeded with limited empirical data to guide policy decisions. Current regulations are designed to minimize the possibility that HFT donation could impact the decision of whether to have an abortion by requiring that clinical consent for abortion be obtained prior to discussing research opportunities. Despite these timing restrictions, questions continue to be raised about the validity of informed consent for HFT research. We sought to collect empirical data to inform the policy debates by directly querying the experience of pregnant women asked to donate HFT for research.MethodsWe conducted qualitative interviews with 51 women who chose (n = 40), or declined (n = 11), to donate HFT. Recruitment challenges and high attrition impacted the number of participants we were able to interview who had declined to donate.ResultsResults revealed that none of the interviewees felt pressured to donate, and no one objected to being asked to participate. The majority of those who chose to donate HFT felt invested in the research and motivated to learn results. Participant feedback about the consent process, however, converged on a desire to learn about the option of HFT donation earlier in the appointment to allow for more time to consider research participation. Limitations include that the study took place in a single US state and results may differ in other jurisdictions.ConclusionsWe conclude with recommendations for policy amendments that could improve the experience of potential participants asked to donate HFT for biomedical research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01420-8.

A great deal of biomedical research relies upon donation of biospecimens from human donors for research purposes [1]. Individuals who undergo abortion or experience miscarriage may be eligible to donate the resulting tissue for research. Human fetal tissue (HFT), defined by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) as “tissue or cells obtained from a dead human embryo or fetus after a spontaneous or induced abortion or stillbirth” [2], is a particularly important resource for biomedical research. HFT has been used in biomedical research since the 1930s, and has played a pivotal role in vaccine development and production [3]. In addition, HFT has proved valuable for studying human-specific aspects of development and disease, and as a standard against which to validate in vitro stem-cell derived models like organoids [4]. Compared to adult tissue, cells derived from HFT have been shown to have expanded proliferation and differentiation capacity, and while alternatives such as induced pluripotent stem cells, organoids, and embryoids could someday supplant the need for HFT, no currently established alternatives can replace HFT’s roles in biomedical research [5].

Most commonly, HFT is collected following induced abortion. Researchers typically obtain HFT through local arrangements or tissue banks that are responsible for collecting, processing, storing, and distributing HFT. Investigators in the United States (U.S.), following a set of requirements laid out by the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), have for decades worked with abortion clinics to obtain informed consent from individuals seeking care to collect the tissue after the procedure. These requirements state that (1) no inducements, monetary or otherwise, will be offered to terminate a pregnancy, (2) individuals engaged in research will have no part in any decisions as to the timing, method, or procedures used to terminate a pregnancy, and (3) consent for the abortion is obtained before requesting consent for the resulting tissue to be used in research [2]. Of note, on January 22, 2026 it was announced that NIH funds can no longer be used for research using HFT from elective abortions [6].

The US HFT research regulations date back to the 1988 Fetal Tissue Transplantation Panel, which was convened by the Reagan administration to evaluate the possibility that HFT donation could influence decisions to terminate pregnancies [7]. By prohibiting inducement and requiring that consent for abortion be obtained before requesting consent for HFT donation, the current regulations guard against the possibility that a termination decision could be influenced by the decision to donate tissue. Despite these safeguards, the notion that pregnant people seeking abortion may not be able to provide informed consent for HFT donation persists. Most notably, a member of a short-lived NIH Ethics Advisory Board convened in 2020 to review NIH grants proposing use of HFT questioned whether informed consent for tissue donation “could ever be valid given the vulnerability inherently present” for pregnant people seeking abortion [8].

Such comments raise the question of whether the circumstances of informed consent in the context of an abortion appointment create vulnerabilities that are unique or distinct from other medical research settings that are characterized by emotional intensity and temporal urgency (e.g., consent to cancer research after receiving a metastatic diagnosis; consent for experimental treatment for a newborn in intensive care) yet are still considered conducive to valid informed consent [9]. We sought to develop a preliminary understanding of the vulnerabilities relevant to research consent that may (or may not) be present in the setting of an abortion appointment by interviewing recently pregnant women who were asked to donate HFT prior to their abortion procedure. Our goal was to investigate the consent process in HFT research; we do not engage ethical arguments about the morality of HFT donation.

Prior studies have asked pregnant or recently pregnant women [10] and women who had obtained abortion [11, 12] about the hypothetical option to donate HFT. Participants in these studies were supportive of being offered the option to donate, emphasized the importance of robust informed consent, and felt the experience could be meaningful for those who choose to donate. However, no studies have yet directly interrogated the perspectives of those who have been asked to donate fetal tissue for research during an abortion appointment. This is the only group of individuals with access to the direct experience of informed consent for research in this context; understanding their experience and perspective should be central to the design of regulations related to HFT research participation. The goal of this study was to understand the first-hand experience of being asked to donate fetal tissue following elective abortion, with a focus on whether the context for informed consent (i.e., the setting, timing, and process) was perceived as conducive to informed decision making about research participation.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedure

This study was conducted in collaboration with an abortion clinic network in Washington State. For over 60 years, a fetal tissue bank has recruited participants from this clinic network. The typical recruitment procedure for the tissue bank is as follows: only patients who have consented to procedural abortion in the first or second trimester of pregnancy are eligible for tissue donation. The majority of patients at the clinics who choose procedural abortion are approached regarding fetal tissue donation; this occurs after they meet with a clinic counselor, have an ultrasound for pregnancy dating, and sign a clinical consent form for abortion. Eligible patients are approached by a separate study coordinator who describes the option to donate fetal tissue for research. If the patient is interested, the coordinator answers questions and reviews the HFT research informed consent form with the patient (Fig. 1).

Series of events in a typical clinical day, indicating the required timing of the discussion about donating HFT for research. Participants in this study were then asked if they wanted to participate in a qualitative interview, regardless of whether they had decided to donate HFT

The research consent form for HFT donation asks participants for permission to use the donated tissue for a broad range of future research (i.e., “broad consent” [13], an approach that is in keeping with regulations but has been debated as potentially inappropriate for stem cell research [14]). The consent form describes the overarching goal of the research as “to study birth defects and other diseases, now and in the future and describes application to a few specific diseases including cancer, Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s.” Participants are presented with check boxes to indicate their opt-in preferences for three additional consent options: (1) providing contact information to learn about future research opportunities (2) the creation of an immortalized cell line from the tissue and (3) receiving results from genetic testing of the tissue. The section about receiving results states that genetic testing of the tissue may reveal clinically significant information, and that if participants opt-in to receiving results, only clinically significant results would be shared. The consent form and consent conversation emphasized that HFT donation is optional, and the donation decision would not impact the procedure or their care in any way.

Recruitment for this qualitative interview study occurred immediately after participants were asked if they would be interested in donating tissue for research. Participants were asked by the study coordinator if they wanted to participate in a qualitative interview whether or not they decided to donate tissue. All interview participants consented to be interviewed and audio recorded; study procedures were approved by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board. Interviewees who elected to donate tissue are hereafter referred to as donors; interviewees who declined to donate tissue are referred to as decliners.

Interview procedure

Phone interviews were conducted in April 2022 through April 2023 and occurred on average 6 days (range 1–30 days) after the participant’s abortion procedure. We attempted to schedule phone interviews within one week of the procedure, but some were delayed due to rescheduling. Interviews were conducted by trained members of the research team (KM, JD, LC), followed a semi-structured interview guide (available in supplementary material), and averaged 21 min (range 11–55 min). Interviews were audio recorded with participant permission to enable transcription for data analysis. All participants who completed an interview were compensated with a $50 electronic gift card.

The semi-structured interview guide was organized into sections: reasons for seeking abortion, prior knowledge and attitudes towards HFT research, consent process for HFT donation, decision process about HFT donation, current and anticipated feelings about the HFT donation decision, and feedback on the HFT donation consent process.

Analysis

We employed qualitative content analysis, a common approach in empirical bioethics and health sciences research [15], and appropriate analysis method when the informational content of interviews is of primary interest to the research question. Transcripts of audio recordings were entered into Atlas.ti. We began with an initial code list of deductive codes (derived from interview guide questions) and supplemented this with “in-vivo” codes generated from additional concepts raised in the interviews. Four members of the author group (KM, LC, JCD, LB) served as coders, and each transcript was reviewed by at least two coders. All coded transcripts were then reviewed by the entire coding team, with coding conflicts discussed to resolve discrepancies.

During the interpretation phase, we revisited transcript segments with relevant codes and derived both quantitative and qualitative summaries. Qualitative summaries were derived via close reading of coded segments and, in some cases, secondary coding and reorganization. We present results related to each of our research questions below, with associated qualitative summaries and supporting quotes provided for each [15]. Filler words (like, um, you know) have been removed from quotes for clarity.

Results

Participants

During the recruitment period, approximately 406 patients were approached about tissue donation, and 264 consented to donate tissue. 62 donors and 36 decliners initially consented to the qualitative interview. Attrition was high across both groups, however, with 41 donors (66%) and 12 decliners (33%) actually completing the interview. Due to technical difficulties, 1 donor and 1 decliner interview were not audio recorded. Therefore, the final sample size for analysis was 40 donors and 11 decliners. Participant demographics

Reasons for seeking abortion

Many participants reported more than one reason for seeking abortion. The most commonly-cited reason was being financially unprepared (n = 17; 33%). This was followed by partner-related factors (either not wanting a child with current partner or not wanting to be a solo parent; n = 16; 31%), the need to focus on other children (n = 15; 29%), not being emotionally or mentally prepared for parenthood (n = 14; 27%), or concerns about their own health, including struggles with addiction (n = 11; 27%). Eight participants shared concerns about the baby’s safety if born, and one participant shared that her pregnancy was the result of sexual assault. Three participants reported seeking abortion due to a fetal anomaly detected via prenatal genetic testing.

Attitudes towards HFT research

The majority of interviewees (n = 30) had not previously been aware that HFT can be used for research. Donors and decliners reported a range of attitudes when asked what they thought about using HFT for research. Most commonly participants felt positively (n = 24) and/or had no concerns (n = 29) about the research. As one put it: “I think it is amazing…you know, anybody just can think what they want to think about what’s going on in the womb…and what’s going on with the fetus, but you can’t really know if you’re never, you know, able to get one [for research].” (Donor 21)

Some participants (n = 10; 5 decliners, 5 donors) felt more negatively about the research. Reasons included feeling uncomfortable with the notion of the fetus being experimented upon, uncertainty about not knowing what would happen with the tissue, and the fact that researchers could create an immortalized cell line. As one decliner described: “I had heard that human fetal tissue had been being used to make the vaccine for Johnson and Johnson…it just doesn’t seem right.” (Decliner 9) The donors who expressed negative attitudes described having an initial negative reaction when the topic of HFT research was first mentioned, but then after learning more through the consent conversation felt reassured about potential risks and benefits. As one put it: “At first I was a little scared just because of like the cloning part. But then I realized…well, then she mentioned that she wasn’t going to use it for cloning…Like I said before, I approve of [the research]…I really think it will benefit us very much later on in the future.” (Donor 13)

Motivations for donating (donors only)

The majority of donors cited wanting to help advance scientific knowledge and improve things in the future (n = 31), as well as feeling that there was no other use for the tissue (n = 16). As one donor put it: “Maybe people would see it as I lost something, [but] I feel like I’m giving back something to other people with my experience by hopefully… by the research that they’re going to do with it.” (Donor 30) Some donors spontaneously mentioned personal health experiences that led them to want to contribute to research. For example, 8 donors mentioned a personal genetic condition, prior pregnancy anomalies, or health experiences of friends or family that motivated them to participate. As one noted: “I have inherited eye problems. So, you know, maybe this can one day, this can lead to y’all figuring out different things with my eye problems.” (Donor 6)

Donors were asked to reflect upon their hopes for the research. Most (n = 19) wanted their donation to help in a general way, and a few offered specific conditions they hoped would be studied with their tissue (e.g., cancer, Down syndrome, substance use during pregnancy). Some also hoped for personal benefit, even though none was promised. 5 participants mentioned that they were hoping to learn that there were no genetic anomalies associated with their pregnancy. “The benefits, I think, that they told me was that … they can give me the results if everything looked fine.” (Donor 31) In contrast, others were clear that there was no individual benefit: “She did make it pretty clear that…there’s no financial compensation. You’re not getting anything except for a sense of helping science.” (Donor 27)

Reasons for declining (decliners only)

Participants who declined to donate tissue cited feeling uncomfortable about the research (n = 6), being preoccupied with other aspects of the appointment or their personal life (n = 5), or feeling attachment to the pregnancy (n = 1) as reasons for not donating. When asked to elaborate, participants described feeling uncomfortable due to the emotional charge of the situation, viewing the fetus as a part of themselves, and moral concern or discomfort about unknown future use of tissue, including for commercial purposes or DNA sharing. As one put it: “Even though I didn’t want to go through with the decision of having another child, I still felt like it’s my own blood and…I didn’t want my own flesh getting examined on after the fact.” (Decliner 10). Some decliners wanted the whole abortion experience to be over and felt donating would interfere with their desire to not think about it in the future. Others reported feeling preoccupied with the difficulty of the abortion decision itself and/or current life circumstances, and general anxiety about the upcoming procedure and associated risks. As one put it: “I’m trying to wrap my brain around the ‘what if’. What if I die? What if I have a stroke or heart attack?…[The research coordinator]’s talking to me, and I’m thinking about dying, you know?” (Decliner 2).

Perceptions of consent process

The decision felt personal, but not difficult

Donors and decliners were asked to reflect on the consent conversation in which they were asked to donate HFT, and how they came to their decision. The majority (22 donors, 7 decliners) reported that the decision about participating felt personal, yet was not difficult to make. As one donor noted: “that was probably the easiest part of my entire day.” (Donor 11) Many explained that the decision felt personal because participating in research is meaningful; others felt a connection to the pregnancy. As one decliner put it: “It felt personal in the sense of… I didn’t want to do it, because I felt connected to the pregnancy…But other than that, it wasn’t really a big deal. It was just kind of like, ‘Nope, all right, cool.’ It wasn’t hard or anything.” (Decliner 11)

Did not feel pressured to donate

Donors and decliners were asked whether they felt any pressure to donate HFT during the consent conversation. Of the 51 interviews, only one decliner reported feeling pressure due to being put on the spot to make a quick decision. She compared it to the feeling of unexpectedly needing to leave a voicemail message during an unanswered phone call—she described that she often needs to call back a second time to leave a voicemail after giving some thought to what she wants to say. When elaborating on why they did not feel pressured, both donors and decliners (n = 19) expressed that they felt there would be no repercussions for saying no, and some (n = 10) added that they felt safe and comfortable during the consent conversation. As one participant put it: “I definitely didn’t feel pressured at all. [The consenter] made it clear that it was totally fine if I wanted to decline, there wouldn’t be any negative repercussions or anything or I wouldn’t have to feel bad or anything like that.” (Donor 2)

Felt caught off guard by consent conversation

The most common feedback participants spontaneously offered about the consent process was a wish that they could have had advance warning of the research conversation. A substantial proportion of participants (5/11 decliners, 14/40 donors) reported feeling caught off guard by the conversation and wished that they could have known ahead of time (e.g., when making their appointment) that they would have the option to donate the tissue so they could have had more time to think about it. 2/11 decliners stated that they might have made a different decision and decided to donate if they had had more time to consider. As one decliner put it:

“I feel like that should be something that should be talked about initially when you call and make the appointment, so that way, it doesn’t throw people off guard when they get there…You’re already under so much anxiety from you actually being at this appointment… it’s like one thing, to another, and then to be asked [to donate] directly after your ultrasound? I don’t think they should do it in that way…it’s just too much.” (Decliner 5).

Donors shared this sentiment as well. As one put it:

“So I feel like by the time I got back to have these [research] conversations…I wasn’t able to really fully think clearly because I just wanted to go home at that point, I was already there for like, over four hours. And, I just was really frustrated and like sad and just hungry and just wanting to go home.” (Donor 29)

In addition to having advance notice about the research conversation, some participants suggested including a checkbox on intake paperwork indicating interest in discussing donating HFT: “just like on your ID they ask if you want to be a[n organ] donor.” (Donor 12).

Other feedback given on the consent process related to the amount and content of the information discussed during the consent conversation. Some participants (n = 7) felt that they would have liked more explanation or information about the purpose of the research, or a more gradual introduction to the topic, while a few (n = 3) felt that the conversation was too long or used overly technical language. Finally, a few (n = 3) participants noted that they would have appreciated the opportunity to discuss the decision to donate with their partner.

Interest in learning overall research results (donors only)

Donors were asked if they would be interested in learning what research discoveries/advances were enabled by their tissue donation. Almost all (n = 38) said they would; one said she didn’t care one way or the other, and one stated that she did not want to learn the results because she didn’t want to be reminded of her abortion procedure in the future. Most explained that they were interested in learning research results because they were curious: “it will help satisfy my brain knowing that I did help” (Donor 38). A few mentioned that it would be emotionally meaningful for them: “I think for me, since it was a hard decision for me to do with not keeping the baby…I would want to know that that decision is going into something … that there has been something that’s happening with it.” (Donor 20). When asked about the preferred mode of learning about research progress and results, the majority said via email (n = 33), with a few (n = 3) preferring text or phone call.

Discussion

Our results build upon a small number of prior studies focused on attitudes towards HFT donation for research, and represent the first direct reports about the experience of being asked to donate HFT during an abortion appointment. The majority of participants—both donors and decliners—felt positively or had no concerns about the use of HFT for research, similar to findings from other qualitative and survey research [10–12]. Donors reported feeling motivated to advance scientific research; some had personal connections to health issues that they specifically hoped the research would address, and many found the opportunity to donate meaningful. Decliners reported feeling preoccupied by other aspects of life or the appointment, or were uncomfortable with the idea of their tissue being used for research.

When asked to reflect on the decision, both donors and decliners described that the decision of whether to donate HFT felt personal, in line with prior work [10]. Yet most felt it was an easy decision to make, particularly in comparison to the abortion decision. We agree with the assertion made in prior work that given the profound nature of the reasons that participants gave for seeking abortion (similar to what has been reported in larger studies; [16]), it is highly unlikely that the option to donate tissue would have influenced the larger abortion decision [12].

We saw no evidence of feeling pressured by the research coordinator to donate tissue, and no one reported being upset about being asked to donate. However, a substantial proportion of both donors and decliners were caught off guard by the consent conversation and wished they had had advance warning ahead of the research conversation.

A few donors expected to benefit personally from participating in the research by learning about the health of the fetus. This reflected a minor misunderstanding of what was on the consent form, which stated that only clinically significant genetic findings might be returned, but not negative results. This expectation is an example of therapeutic misconception, a common cognitive error in which participants inaccurately assume that research procedures have therapeutic intent [17], and could be remedied by research coordinators spending more time during the consent conversation framing and emphasizing the social vs. personal benefits of the research. Finally, almost all participants wanted to learn overall results of the research, adding to the evidence suggesting that even in sensitive research contexts participants want to learn the results of research they participate in [18].

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations worth noting. First, while the number of participants is larger than typical for a qualitative investigation, all were recruited from a single network of abortion clinics in Washington State. While the demographics of the sample are representative of the patient population seen at the clinic network we recruited from, it is not representative of the overall US population. Furthermore, responses to our questions may have differed if the study were conducted in states with laws that restrict abortion access. We also experienced a fair amount of attrition between consent for the qualitative interview and interview completion. It may be the case that those who did not complete interviews felt differently about being asked to donate HFT, the consent process, or their abortion decision, compared to those who completed interviews, but we unfortunately do not have data that captures their perspectives.

Implications for regulation of HFT research

We consider these findings in light of US regulatory changes occurring in the past decade that removed pregnant women from the list of vulnerable populations in research [19]. This regulatory revision was intended to counter ideas of vulnerability and over-protection during pregnancy that have negatively impacted women’s health [20]. However, current regulations for HFT research remain grounded in a protectionist stance, assuming that pregnant women seeking abortion may be particularly susceptible to manipulation or harm [12].

We found evidence that the strictly-regulated timing of the consent conversation (occurring after consent for the abortion procedure and before surgery) may in fact be counter to the intention to protect decision making and participant autonomy. Participants in our study shared the ways in which the timing of the consent conversation negatively impacted their decision-making experience, given the highly emotionally salient nature of the period of time immediately preceding their procedure. This concern about timing of the consent conversation has been found in other studies [10, 21]; for example, one member of a focus group of women in the UK with prior abortion experience stated: “Yeah, it’s terrible [to ask] on the day. I really think that’s awful.” [21].

Critics of HFT research could interpret this finding to suggest that research conversations in the setting of an abortion appointment are inappropriate. Yet there are many highly emotional medical settings in which participants are asked to participate in research: for example, parents of hours-old newborns who are critically ill in the neonatal intensive care unit; families coping with a child’s recent cancer diagnosis, and people about to undergo brain surgery. Bioethics scholarship in each of these settings suggests that the heightened emotional salience does not necessarily prevent or invalidate informed consent for research, but rather requires the informed consent conversation be extended across time, multiple formats, and settings [22–24].

Our findings from participants with direct experience of informed consent for HFT donation align with prior work asking about hypothetical consent from women who have experienced pregnancy and/or abortion in the US and UK [10–12, 21]. Considering this accumulated evidence, we recommend that HFT research regulations be amended to allow informed consent for HFT donation to be a process, rather than a single conversation restricted to occur in the middle of an abortion appointment. As our participants suggested, patients seeking abortion could be told to expect a research conversation when making their appointment, or when they check in. They could be given handouts with information about HFT donation to read and consider prior to their appointment or while sitting in the waiting room. Future empirical work should evaluate the efficacy of such modifications. The introduction of such materials and procedures to support informed decision making would bring HFT research in line with other research conducted with decisionally-capable adults in the midst of emotional healthcare experiences.

Our results suggest that pregnant people seeking abortion appreciate the opportunity to engage in research and should be offered research opportunities with protections and support for informed consent similar to other patients receiving medical care.
