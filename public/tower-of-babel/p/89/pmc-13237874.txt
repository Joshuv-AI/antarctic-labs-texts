Beyond the gut: a case report of Shigella-associated myocarditis
Authors: Liam Maher, David Murphy, Tara Ramsbottom, Cathal O’Broin, Matthew Barrett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Shigellosis is a faecal–oral–transmitted gram-negative bacterial infection caused by Shigella species. Though it commonly presents with self-limiting diarrhoea, bloodstream infection and extra-intestinal complications, such as myocarditis, are rare. Rising incidence among gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (gbMSM) has been associated with antimicrobial resistance.Case summaryA 36-year-old gbMSM presented with a 3-day history of bloody diarrhoea, dehydration, and hypotension. Initial management included fluid resuscitation and empiric antibiotics. Blood and stool cultures later confirmed Shigella flexneri bacteraemia, prompting escalation to meropenem and intensive care unit admission for septic shock. On Day 5, the patient developed chest pain, electrocardiogram changes, and elevated troponin. Computed tomography (CT) coronary angiography excluded obstructive coronary artery disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed septal mid-wall late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), compatible with a clinical diagnosis of probable acute myocarditis. He was managed with guideline-directed medical therapy and completed 14 days of targeted antibiotics. At 6-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic. Repeat cardiac MRI showed no evidence of oedema on T2-weighted sequences and near-complete resolution of LGE on T1-weighted sequences, with improved left ventricular ejection fraction from 44.6% to 49%.DiscussionThis rare case of S. flexneri-associated myocarditis reinforces the need to consider bacterial causes of myocarditis in the context of systemic infection. Cardiac MRI was instrumental in establishing the diagnosis and monitoring recovery. In line with European Society of Cardiology and American College of Cardiology guidelines, repeat imaging provided essential prognostic data. This case highlights the potential for invasive Shigella infection in gbMSM and underscores the crucial role of cardiac MRI in myocarditis diagnosis and follow-up.

Introduction

Shigellosis is an infectious disease caused by Shigella, a gram-negative bacillus. There are four serogroups: Shigella dysenteriae, Shigella flexneri, Shigella boydii, and Shigella sonnei. Transmission occurs primarily via the faecal–oral route, and person-to-person spread is common. In recent years, sexual transmission has emerged as a significant route of infection, particularly among gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (gbMSM).1

The incidence of shigellosis has increased markedly over the past decade, with a clear predominance among adult males. The crude incidence rate rose from 1.1 per 100 000 population in 2013 to 4.4 per 100 000 in 2023. Clinically, shigellosis typically presents with bloody diarrhoea, abdominal pain, and fever and is usually self-limiting. Shigella blood stream infection and extra-intestinal complications are rare. Antibiotic therapy is generally reserved for patients with severe illness or those who are immunocompromised.

Antimicrobial resistance is an increasing concern, particularly among the gbMSM population, where transmission of clonal groups of S. flexneri and S. sonnei with high-level resistance to ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, and azithromycin has been documented. National guidelines now recommend the use of a carbapenem for seriously ill adult male patients requiring hospitalization with suspected shigellosis.2

Case presentation

A 36-year-old male presented with 3 days of frequent bloody diarrhoea, faecal urgency, anorexia, postural dizziness, and oligoanuria. His medical history included hypertension, dyslipidaemia, obesity (body mass index 42 kg/m2), anxiety/depression, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and previous treatment for primary syphilis. He had recently engaged in sexual intercourse with several male partners. This involved the use of oral gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB) and intravenous crystal methamphetamine. He denied sharing needles with others. He was a non-smoker, with no relevant family history.

On arrival, he was markedly hypotensive [blood pressure (BP) 63/33 mmHg] and dehydrated. His other vital signs were within normal limits. Initial laboratory indices (Table 1) showed an acute kidney injury (AKI) Stage 3, elevated inflammatory markers, presumed hypovolaemic hyponatraemia, and lactic acidosis. Blood and stool samples were collected for culture and polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Empiric intravenous co-amoxiclav was commenced, along with a stat dose of 3 mg/kg gentamicin to expand gram-negative cover, for suspected intra-abdominal sepsis. After fluid resuscitation, his BP improved to 90/60 mmHg and lactate normalized. He was admitted to an isolation room for further management.

Blood results

On Day 3, he developed recurrent hypotension (systolic BP 75 mmHg), persistent diarrhoea (now non-bloody), low-grade fever, and polyuria, requiring intensive care unit transfer. Noradrenaline infusion (20 mcg/min) was initiated. Stool PCR demonstrated infection with Shigella species. Blood cultures confirmed a gram-negative bacteraemia, and he was escalated to meropenem. Subsequent stool and blood culture results confirmed S. flexneri infection. Once sensitivity results were available, he was de-escalated to i.v. cefotaxime (2 g q8 h). Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated acute colitis from the rectum to the hepatic flexure.

By Day 5, vasopressor requirements and diarrhoea frequency decreased. However, he reported transient left parasternal chest pain. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with new repolarization abnormalities in the lateral precordial leads (Figure 1), along with increases in high-sensitivity troponin-T to a peak of 2719 ng/L. In the absence of ongoing gastrointestinal bleeding and given his cardiovascular risk factors, dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated.

Serial ECGs with the top panel representing the ECG on day of admission, the middle panel representing ECG on Day 5 with lateral repolarization abnormalities, and the bottom panel representing ECG on Day 7 showing resolution of previously demonstrated changes.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved chamber sizes, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) at 35%–40%, with hypokinetic apical septal and mid-anteroseptal walls, and no significant valvular disease (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). Computed tomography coronary angiography excluded obstructive coronary artery disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (1.5 T Siemens Avanto) showed base-to-apex septal signal hyperintensity on early post-contrast steady-state free precession (SSFP) T1-weighted images, septal hypokinesia, and septal mid-wall late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) (Figure 2A and B) with LVEF of 44.6%. After multidisciplinary discussion, a diagnosis of probable acute Shigella-associated myocarditis was made—a rare but previously reported entity. A repeat cardiac MRI was scheduled for 6 months after completion of appropriate antibiotic therapy.

(A) Index cardiac MRI four-chamber early post-contrast steady-state free precession-weighted image with arrows demonstrating mid-wall signal hyperintensity. Although steady-state free precession provides mixed T2/T1 weighting; post-contrast signal cannot confirm oedema and instead may appear increased due to T1 shortening from gadolinium; such findings may be compatible with inflammation in appropriate clinical contexts. (B) Index cardiac MRI short-axis post-contrast phase-sensitive inversion recovery T1-weighted fast gradient-echo sequence with arrows demonstrating mid-wall late gadolinium enhancement in the mid-inferoseptal and anteroseptal walls, extending to the sub-epicardium of the mid-anterior wall.

Following clinical stabilization, low-dose beta-blocker therapy was initiated alongside perindopril. Intravenous cefotaxime was transitioned to oral co-trimoxazole to complete a total of 14 days of targeted antibiotics. He was discharged on Day 16.

At 6-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic and compliant with medications. Repeat cardiac MRI (3.0 T Siemens Magnetom Vida) showed an absence of myocardial oedema on T2-weighted short tau inversion recovery sequences and near-complete resolution of septal LGE (Figure 3A and B), with improved LVEF to 49%, consistent with interval resolution of the previously suspected acute myocarditic process.

(A) Follow-up cardiac MRI short-axis post-contrast phase-sensitive inversion recovery T1-weighted fast gradient-echo sequence demonstrating interval improvement of the previously noted late gadolinium enhancement with a smaller region of residual fibrosis in the mid-inferoseptal and anteroseptal walls. (B) Follow-up cardiac MRI short-axis mid-ventricle T2 short tau inversion recovery image demonstrating the absence of myocardial oedema.

Discussion

We report a rare case of probable Shigella-associated myocarditis. Although Shigella-associated myocarditis has been previously reported on a handful of occasions,3–6 this case is unique in documenting both blood and stool cultures positive for S. flexneri and longitudinal cardiac MRI changes consistent with acute myocarditis. The aetiology of myocarditis is often unclear, commonly linked to viral infections (e.g. enteroviruses, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, adenovirus, influenza viruses, Ebstein-Barr Virus, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis C virus, human herpesvirus 6, and parvovirus B19),7 but may also include non-viral pathogens, toxins, drugs, or autoimmune processes. Interestingly, severe gastrointestinal inflammatory states, including acute pancreatitis, have been associated with secondary myocarditis, as highlighted in a recent systematic review.8

The traditional diagnostic criteria for acute myocarditis established by the World Health Organization Task Force on Cardiomyopathies in 1995 defined myocarditis as ‘an inflammatory disease of the myocardium that is diagnosed by assessing an endomyocardial biopsy specimen according to established histological, immunological, and immunohistochemical criteria’.9 The 2013 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Working Group on Myocardial and Pericardial Diseases proposed a new diagnostic criterion. They suggested that myocarditis should be suspected when (i) one or more pre-specified clinical presentations of myocarditis are present and (ii) one or more diagnostic investigation results prove positive (or two or more positive investigation results if the patient is asymptomatic).10 Furthermore, they recommend that all patients with clinically suspected myocarditis should be considered for selective coronary angiography and endomyocardial biopsy.

Our patient met criteria for clinically suspected myocarditis: chest pain, ECG changes, raised myocardiocytolysis markers, wall motion abnormalities, and cardiac MRI finding of mid-wall LGE. Computed tomography coronary angiography ruled out ischaemia as a mimic. Endomyocardial biopsy was deemed unnecessary in this case given our patient’s clinical improvement and positive blood cultures for the causative organism. Whilst Shigella bacteraemia is exceptionally rare, it is postulated that the patient became bacteraemic either through contamination of needle works, through gastrointestinal translocation during sexual intercourse, or as a result of translocation secondary to colitis. The inoculum size or bacterial burden required for Shigella infection is small; hence, it is highly contagious.11

The cardiac MRI was instrumental in aiding the multidisciplinary diagnostic process in this case. Recent validation work has shown that updates to the Lake Louise criteria, including incorporation of mapping techniques, substantially improve diagnostic sensitivity.12 Unfortunately, neither dedicated T2-weighted imaging nor T2 mapping was performed at the index scan, and therefore the study did not fulfil the 2018 Lake Louise criteria13 for myocardial inflammation of ‘at least one T2-based criterion (global or regional increase of myocardial T2 relaxation time or an increased signal intensity in T2-weighted images) in addition to ≥1 T1-based criterion (increased myocardial T1, LGE, or increased extra-cellular volume),’ with signs of pericarditis and ventricular systolic dysfunction, which were supportive. Similarly, the 2009 Lake Louise criteria were not strictly met, as early gadolinium enhancement sequences were not acquired and only one of the three original tissue markers (non-ischaemic LGE) was present.14 Accordingly, our CMR findings should be interpreted as supportive of, rather than definitively diagnostic for, acute myocarditis. Importantly, both the 2009 and 2018 consensus statements emphasize that myocarditis remains a clinical diagnosis, with CMR findings serving as complementary evidence alongside symptoms, biomarkers, ECG changes, and exclusion of ischaemia. In this integrated context, the presentation in our patient supports a diagnosis of probable acute myocarditis.

In accordance with the 2023 ESC Guidelines on the management of cardiomyopathies—specifically the section addressing non-dilated left ventricular cardiomyopathies7—cardiac MRI also played a central role in guiding our management. The presence of LGE provided valuable prognostic information regarding the risk of future arrhythmias and heart failure. Based on the 2024 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on Strategies and Criteria for the Diagnosis and Management of Myocarditis,15 our patient met criteria for medium-to-high-risk Stage C myocarditis. Consequently, we adhered to the recommendation for repeat cardiac imaging at 6 months to reassess left ventricular function, confirm resolution of inflammation characteristic of an acute myocarditic process, and exclude persistent mid-wall septal changes that could suggest an alternative diagnosis.

This case reinforces the importance of considering Shigella as a rare but plausible cause of myocarditis and highlights the pivotal role of cardiac MRI in diagnosis and follow-up.
