The association between maternal HIV and stillbirths in an Era of universal ART access in pregnancy in the Western Cape, South Africa, 2017 – 2021
Authors: Elizabeth Lubinda, Florence Phelanyane, Alexa Heekes, Ushma Mehta, Andrew Boulle, Salome Maswime, Emma Kalk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Maternal HIV infection has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including stillbirth. We aimed to investigate the association between maternal HIV and stillbirths in the context of universal antiretroviral therapy (ART) during pregnancy in the Western Cape, South Africa.Methods:We defined a cohort of pregnant women with documented HIV status and pregnancy outcome >20 weeks gestation enrolled in the Western Cape Pregnancy Exposure Registry from January 2017 - December 2021. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses assessed proportion of stillbirths and odds ratios by HIV and ART status, and their relationship with clinical and demographic variables.Results:Among 15,123 pregnant women, HIV prevalence was 31.6 % at pregnancy outcome (n = 4773) and 61.6 % of women living with HIV (WLHIV) had started ART prior to pregnancy. Although stillbirths were higher among WLHIV: 17/1000 births (95 %CI:13.3–20.7; n = 79) versus 14/1000 births (95 % CI:11.8–16.3; n = 147) among women without HIV, HIV was not associated with stillbirth in multivariable analysis (aOR=1.2, 95 % CI:0.9–1.5). Among WLHIV, ART duration ART≥100weeks had reduced odds of stillbirth compared to those on ART<20weeks (aOR=0.5, 95 % CI: 0.3–0.9). Pre-existing diabetes was associated with stillbirth (aOR=2.6, 95 % CI: 1.1–6.5).Conclusion:In this cohort of pregnant women we found no differences in stillbirth rates between WLHIV and women without HIV but given the sample size and number of stillbirths, the study could not exclude small increases in risk. Longer ART duration was confirmed to be protective for stillbirth among WLHIV.Keywords: Maternal HIV, Stillbirth, Antiretroviral therapy, South Africa, Antenatal care quality

Introduction

Globally, an estimated 1.9 million stillbirths occur each year, corresponding to 13.9 per 1000 total births [1]. Stillbirths, defined as fetal death at ≥28 weeks of gestation [2], represent a major epidemiological burden with broad social and psychological consequences for affected mothers and families [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears a significant burden, with a stillbirth rate of 21.7 per 1000 births using the gestational age threshold of ≥28 weeks [3], and 36.5 per 1000 births using a 20-week threshold [4]. In 2019, South Africa reported a stillbirth rate of 16.3/1000 births (gestational age ≥28 weeks) [5], despite concerted efforts to achieve United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 3.2, which aims to reduce stillbirths to 12/1000 births by 2030 [6].

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), major contributors to stillbirths include hypertension, bacterial infections, placental disorders, syphilis, HIV, and inadequate antenatal care (ANC) [7,8]. High-quality ANC has been consistently linked to lower stillbirth rates [9–10]. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes the importance of essential ANC services, including maternal and fetal clinical assessments, education on complications, and maternal vaccinations [11,12].

South Africa has 8.4 million people living with HIV, with an antenatal HIV prevalence of 30 % [13]. Studies have documented associations between stillbirth and maternal HIV status [10,14]. While antiretroviral therapy (ART) has substantially improved maternal survival rates, its impact on stillbirths varies depending on the regimen used and the timing of initiation [10,14,15]. By 2019, South Africa had achieved 97 % ART coverage at delivery for pregnant women known to be living with HIV (WLHIV). In the Western Cape the antenatal HIV prevalence was 17.9 %, with ART coverage at delivery exceeding 90 % in 2019 [16,15].

Given the high prevalence of HIV among pregnant women in South Africa and the substantial burden of stillbirths in SSA and South Africa, it is important to examine the relationship between maternal HIV and stillbirths within the context of universal ART in pregnancy. Identifying these associations may provide insights to inform interventions aimed at reducing stillbirths and improving maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Using routinely collected clinical data digitized in the Western Cape Pregnancy Exposure Registry (PER) [16], we aimed to investigate the association between maternal HIV and stillbirths in the era of universal ART access in pregnancy.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Western Cape PER between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021. The PER systematically captures pregnancy exposures and outcomes prospectively through routine sentinel site-based surveillance managed by the Western Cape Government Department of Health & Wellness (WCGHW) [16]. The PER records administrtaive, clinical, laboratory and pregnany outcome data with diagnoses documented in the clinical stationery by attending clincians.. By attending clinicians. Medication exposures, including ART and other prescribed treatments, are systematically included from electronic pharmacy dispensing data. Only two sentinel sites (at Gugulethu and Worcester) were included in this analysis, as these were the facilities where the PER has been implemented and maintained during the study period [16,17].

The PER is integrated into the Provincial Health Data Centre (PHDC), a health information exchange which uses a unique patient identifier to synchronize individual-level electronic health data across the provincial health services. This integration facilitates the creation of virtual cohorts within the PHDC, providing comprehensive datasets for analysis [17].

Study population

The cohort included all women attending antenatal care (ANC) at two public sector primary care sentinel sites: Gugulethu Midwife Obstetrics Unit (GMOU) and Worcester MOU (WMOU), during the study period. Approximately 5000 women seek ANC at GMOU annually, with half delivering at the clinic and the rest referred to a hospital. The antenatal HIV prevalence was approximately 30 % in 2019 [16]. WMOU, situated in the farming town of Worcester, handles delivery services for approximately 3600 expectant mothers annually, with an antenatal HIV prevalence of 16 % in 2019 [16]. HIV testing is routinely performed at the first ANC visit, typically within the first and second trimsters, and repeated 3-monthly. Pregnant women testing positive are initiated on ART on the same day in line with South African national guidelines for universal ART in pregnancy. During the study period, all pregnant women had access to ART, regardless of CD4 count or clinical stage [18].

Participants were ≥18 years old, with known HIV status, and had a recorded pregnancy outcome after 20 weeks of gestation (i.e., a viable pregnancy [4]). Since 2013, the Western Cape implemented WHO Option B+ for the prevention of vertical transmission of HIV (VTP) by which all pregnant WLHIV were initiated on life-long ART. The Universal Test and Treat policy recommending ART for all at HIV diagnosis had been in place since 2016 [18].

Study variables

The primary outcome was stillbirth, defined as the intrauterine death of a fetus occurring after 20 weeks of gestation or weighing >500grams [4,19]. The primary exposure variable was maternal HIV status during pregnancy, determined from HIV laboratory and treatment data in the PHDC.

ART regimens primarily included a fixed-dose combination of tenofovir, emtricitabine/lamivudine, and efavirenz. Dolutegravir (DTG) replaced efavirenz in the first-line ART regimen at the end of 2020, and only a limited number of WLHIV received DTG during the study period.

ANC quality was assessed in accordance with the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) Recommendations for a Positive Pregnancy Experience [12,20]. Indicators included: timing and frequency of ANC (initiation before 12 weeks’ gestation; number of visits); clinical assessments (weight, HIV, syphilis, urine, blood pressure); and preventive interventions (iron and folate supplementation; tetanus and influenza vaccination). To account for variability in implementation, ANC quality was categorised by visit frequency as basic (≥4 visits) or enhanced (≥8 visits). In addition, a composite ANC content score was constructed using the eleven available WHO-recommended indicators, with good-quality ANC defined as achievement of at least seven indicators (≈67 % of the WHO benchmark of 8/12 recommended interventions [20]). The available indicators may not fully capture the complexity of antenatal care, which should be considered when interpreting observed associations.

Other relevant variables in the PER included maternal age, syphilis status, comorbidities, alcohol and cigarette use, body mass index (BMI), and the nature, timing, and duration of ART in WLHIV.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using R software, version 1.2.5003. Descriptive statistics summarized maternal and pregnancy characteristics at enrolment into the PER during the first ANC visit. Categorical variables were expressed as proportions and frequencies and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous variables were summarised using medians and interquartile ranges and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Univariable logistic regression models assessed associations between independent variables and stillbirth, followed by multivariable models to adjust for potential confounders. Variables were selected a priori based on existing literature and theoretical relevance to stillbirth, including maternal age, BMI, HIV status, ANC quality, maternal comorbidities, and ART-related variables [7,8]. The prevalences of smoking and alcohol use, reported risk factors for stillbirth, were low in this cohort (smoking: 5.1 %; alcohol use: 4.2 %) with ~5 % missing data and, these variables were excluded from the multivariable models to avoid unstable estimates.

Missing data for other covariates were handled using complete case analysis, whereby only participants with complete data for all variables included in the models were analysed (n = 15,119 for all women; n = 4771 for women living with HIV). Cumulative incidence proportions of stillbirths among women living with HIV (WLHIV) and women without HIV were estimated using the epitools package in R. Annual analyses assessed temporal trends and variations.

Sensitivity analyses

Given the potential effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on stillbirths, a sensitivity analysis was conducted comparing data from pre-pandemic and pandemic periods (after March 2020) to investigate any potential impacts.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Cape Town Faculty of Health Sciences Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC REF: 689/2022 and 749/2015) and the Western Cape Department of Health (WC_2016RP6_286 and WC_202,202_022). A waiver of informed consent was approved.

Results

Of 15,123 participants, 31.6 % (4773) were women living with HIV (WLHIV), and 68.4 % (10,350) were women without HIV (Table 1; Fig. 1). All participants had a known HIV status. WLHIV were older (median age: 31 years; interquartile range [IQR]: 26–35) compared to women without HIV (median age: 28 years; IQR: 23–33; p = 0.14). Median body mass index (BMI) was comparable across the two groups, as were the proportions of participants with pre-existing and pregnancy-associated diabetes and hypertension (Tables 1 and S1).

Among women with at least 8 ANC visits, 7.1 % achieved good-quality ANC (≥7 of 11 recommended ANC components), while the remaining 92.9 % received poor-quality ANC (Tables 1 and S1). Similarly, among women with at least 4 ANC visits, only 12.4 % achieved good-quality ANC.

Stillbirth rates were 17/1000 births (95 % confidence interval [CI]: 13.34–20.66) for WLHIV and 14/1000 births (95 % CI: 11.75–16.25) for women without HIV. Over the five-year study period, cumulative incidence of stillbirth among WLHIV declined from 2.7 % in 2017 to 1.4 % in 2018 and remained stable thereafter. In contrast, women without HIV experienced a decline in stillbirth incidence to 1 % in 2020, followed by an increase to 2 % in 2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 2).

Women without HIV and a history of diabetes had higher stillbirth risks than those without diabetes (4.1 % vs 1.1 %; p = 0.01) (Table 1). Similarly, among women without HIV, a history of hypertension was associated with higher stillbirth risks (5.3 % vs 1.7 %; p = 0.02) (Table 1).

Among WLHIV, ART use during pregnancy was high: 62.6 % had initiated ART prior to conception, while 30.4 % started or restarted ART during pregnancy. A higher proportion of women with stillbirth had initiated/restarted ART during pregnancy compared to those with livebirth (70.9 % versus 30.5 %; p < 0.001). Similarly, a higher proportion of pregnancies where women were on ART < 20 weeks duration were affected by stillbirth (35.4 % with stillbirth versus 19.9 % with livebirth) (Table 2).

Multivariable analysis did not support a difference in stillbirths between WLHIV and women without HIV (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 1.2; 95 % CI: 0.9–1.52; p = 0.3) (Table 3). Poor-quality ANC was associated with a non-significant trend towards increased stillbirth risk. Using women with good-quality ANC (≥7 of 11 variables) as the reference, the aOR for stillbirth was 1.3 (95 % CI: 0.9–2.2; p = 0.21) among women with ≥4 visits and 1.6 (95 % CI: 0.9–3.1; p = 0.15) among women with ≥8 visits (Table S3).

Discussion

In our study of pregnant women in the Western Cape using routine clinical data, we explored the relationship between maternal HIV status and stillbirths. No statistically significant relationship was observed. However, the relatively small number of stillbirths limits the ability to detect modest effect sizes, and clinically meaningful increases in risk cannot be excluded. This contrasts with earlier findings that reported strong positive associations, suggesting that improvements in ART access and duration may have mitigated the previously observed risks [10,11]. Our results align with more recent studies [21,22] that considered confounders, involved large samples, and included women with improved access to and longer duration on ART.

The overall stillbirth rate in our study was 15/1000 births, slightly exceeding the WHO’s Every Newborn Action Plan global target of ≤12 stillbirths/1000 births by 2030 [6]. This is lower than findings from other South African studies, where stillbirth rates among WLHIV (25/1000) and women without HIV (22/1000) were higher than our observed rates of 17/1000 and 14/1000 births, respectively [23]. Notably, our study’s overall stillbirth rate was lower than the reported 20/1000 births in previous studies [23], potentially reflecting the impact of broader ART rollout initiatives starting in 2013.

A notable decline in stillbirths among WLHIV was observed in 2018, coinciding with an increased proportion of individuals on ART for >100 weeks. The protective effect of longer ART use has been noted in other studies [24]; our findings showed a 47 % reduction in stillbirth odds among WLHIV with ART duration ≥100 weeks compared to <20 weeks. This finding underscores the critical role of sustained ART access in managing stillbirth risk. The incidence proportion of stillbirths varied by year and HIV status. The decline observed among WLHIV from 2017 to 2018 coincided with increased ART coverage, whereas the notable decrease in overall stillbirths in 2020 despite the COVID-19 pandemic’s disruptions may reflect improved ANC quality during that period, particularly among women without HIV. On sensitivity multivariable analyses, the period of COVID-19 was not associated with changes in the odds of stillbirth (Table S5).

While DTG has shown clinical benefits in HIV treatment its impact on stillbirths could not be fully assessed in our study due to limited DTG usage among the cohort following its introduction to first-line ART regimens in late 2020.

In contrast to expectations, we found no statistically significant association between access to quality ANC and stillbirths regardless of HIV status. This may be attributed to specific components of ANC received and the generally low percentages of individuals in both ANC quality groups receiving optimal care. In contrast, other studies have shown an 18.4 % decrease in stillbirths with more ANC visits (RR 0.8, 95 % CI 0.7–0.9), highlighting the importance of comprehensive ANC in reducing adverse pregnancy outcomes [20,24].

Gestational hypertension, major risk factor for stillbirth, was not significantly associated in this cohort. This may reflect effective clinical management of comorbidities and the relatively small number of stillbirths, which may have limited statistical power to detect modest effects.

The positive association between pre-pregnancy diabetes and stillbirths observed in our study is consistent with findings from previous research, emphasizing the importance of managing comorbidities such as diabetes in addressing multiple risk factors for stillbirths [3,4,25].

Strengths and limitations

Our study leveraged routine electronic health data to investigate a large cohort of pregnant women (n = 15,123) in a setting of high antenatal HIV prevalence and universal access to ART in pregnancy. However, there are limitations inherent in the use of routine operational data and we were unable to account for missing data, including missing data on ANC quality components. This may have resulted in misclassification. Data on certain known risk factors for stillbirth were not captured in the PER, including placental pathologies, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), specific obstetric complications, and socioeconomic or environmental factors. We acknowledge that the absence of these variables may result in residual confounding, which could influence the observed associations. In addition, we could not account for potential unmeasured confounders. The sample size and number of stillbirths has meant that the study is under-powered to detect small differences in stillbirths between WLHIV and those without HIV. Limited DTG usage over the study period meant that the impact of DTG could not be assessed.

Conclusions

Within a robust HIV treatment program encompassing women enrolled in primary care, we found no differences in stillbirth prevalence or adjusted odds of stillbirth between WLHIV and women without HIV. Longer duration of ART among WLHIV was protective for stillbirth, highlighting the potentially protective effect of sustained ART access. Furthermore, while the quality of ANC was universally suboptimal, it did not show a significant association with stillbirths in adjusted analyses. Future research should focus on addressing ANC quality and other modifiable risk factors to further improve maternal and fetal outcomes in this population.
