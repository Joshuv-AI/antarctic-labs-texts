The distribution of colposcopy directed ectocervical biopsy and endocervical curettage results: a retrospective study of 47,134 cases
Authors: Ruizhe Chen, Chun Ye, Ying Li, Zhuoqun Lin, Lingfang Wang, Yunfeng Fu, Xiaodong Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the distribution of colposcopy directed ectocervical biopsy and endocervical curettage (ECC) results, and to identify patients who may benefit from ECC.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed the clinical data of 47,134 patients who underwent colposcopy directed ectocervical biopsy and ECC between January 2021 and December 2023. Risk factors were identified by univariate and multivariate logistic analyses.ResultsThe positive rate of the final histopathological diagnosis was 37.1% (17,503/47,134), with 22.8% (10,724/47,134) for low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) and 14.4% (6,779/47,134) for high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) or worse lesions (≥HSIL). At final diagnosis of LSIL, HSIL, adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), HSIL+AIS, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), adenocarcinoma (ADC), and adenosquamous carcinoma (ASC), the additional diagnostic yield of ECC were 3.1%, 4.5%, 11.2%, 6.7%, 15.6%, 3.3%, and 0.0%, respectively. Multivariate analysis identified that age over 45 years old, high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, atypical squamous cells cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H) + (including ASC-H, HSIL and SCC) or atypical glandular cells (AGC) cytology and transformation zone (TZ)3 were risk factors for additional lesions detection.ConclusionsECC is a useful procedure for detecting additional endocervical lesions, and is recommended for patients aged ≥ 45, with high-risk HPV infection, ASC-H + or AGC cytology, and TZ3, as it will help reduce the rate of missed diagnosis of cervical lesions.Keywords: Endocervical curettage, squamous intraepithelial lesion, colposcopy, transformation zone, perimenopausal diagnostics, cervical endocervical pathology

Introduction

Role of human papillomavirus (HPV) testing, Papanicolaou test or liquid-based cytology (LBC), and colposcopy have been fully affirmed in detection and treatment of cervical cancer and pre-invasive lesions. According to current guidelines, patients are referred to colposcopy for cervical histopathological evaluation based on the risk of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) grade 3 (CIN3) or more severe diagnoses determined by a combination of current cytology/HPV testing results and past history [1,2]. With the rapid development of molecular diagnostic technologies, a number of novel biomarker detection methods are gradually demonstrating significant clinical potential. HPV E6/E7 mRNA testing directly reflects the carcinogenic activity of HPV and is closely associated with the risk of disease progression [3]. P16/Ki-67 dual-stain testing improves the specificity and positive predictive value for high-grade lesions by simultaneously evaluating the abnormal expression of cell cycle proteins [4,5]. DNA methylation detection of genes such as PAX1, SOX1, CADM1 and MAL can identify host gene epigenetic changes, providing new evidence for early molecular diagnosis and risk stratification of cervical cancer [6,7]. These technologies not only enhance the accuracy of detecting cervical lesions, but may also be integrated with existing methods in the future to reduce subjective variability and unnecessary invasive procedures.

At present, endocervical curettage (ECC) remains a valuable procedure for detecting additional endocervical lesions that would be missed by ectocervical biopsy alone. Usually, the colposcopist takes biopsies from suspicious lesions. ECC is preferred for patients when colposcopy is inadequate, and for those not at lowest risk with no lesion is identified [1]. It is also acceptable when a lesion is seen [1]. Meanwhile, the United States recently provides updated guidelines for the use of ECC among patients referred for colposcopy [8]. These guidelines stated that ECC is contraindicated during pregnancy because of the risk of injury to membranes or the placenta. Due to obvious differences in sample size, population distribution, or research methodologies and designs, studies on the detection rate of cervical histopathological high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) or worse lesions (≥HSIL, including CIN2, CIN3, adenocarcinoma in situ[AIS] and cervical cancer) through ECC reported a wide range from 1.0% to 14.3% [9–12]. Previous studies have mostly focused on the discovery of additional ≥ HSIL by ECC, but detailed lesion distributions have been less described. Both ≥ HSIL and ungraded squamous intraepithelial lesion diagnosed by ECC can affect the diagnosis and subsequent treatment strategies selection. However, patients may experience some discomfort during the placement of ECC, which reduces the likelihood of participating in follow-up, and may also result in postoperative medical injuries such as cervical adhesions and stenosis [2,13]. Therefore, the clinical efficacy and value of ECC remain controversial [14–17], warranting further investigation to determine its optimal application in cervical lesion diagnosis.

We retrospectively collected basic information and results of patients undergoing colposcopy directed ectocervical biopsy and ECC, and analyzed the risk factors influencing the additional diagnostic yield of ECC. Aim to provide more evidence for reasonable selection of ECC, reduce the rate of the missed diagnosis, and simultaneously offer preliminary data support for the future integration of novel molecular markers into clinical diagnostic pathways.

Patients and methods

Participants

The present study retrospectively analyzed medical data and records of women who underwent colposcopy directed ectocervical biopsy and ECC between January 2021 and December 2023 in the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, China, and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IRB-20240058-R). The requirement for informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective observational study and data were kept anonymous. Patients were included if they had detailed medical records of HPV testing, cytologic examination, ectocervical biopsy and ECC results. Patients were excluded if they met the following exclusion criteria: (1) nondiagnostic or inadequate sampling; (2) incomplete information; (3) infected with human immunodeficiency virus. A total of 47,134 cases were eligible for study.

Clinical and examination information

Clinical data of patients, including age, high-risk HPV status, cytologic examination, transformation zone (TZ) type, ectocervical biopsy and ECC results, were collected from medical records. HPV testing included the Aptima HPV test (Hologic, Inc, San Diego, CA), Digene Hybrid Capture 2 test (HC2, Qiagen, Gaithersburg, MD) and Kaipu HPV 21 typing test (Kaipu Biochemistry Co., Ltd., Chaozhou, China). Cytology examination was performed using the liquid-based ThinPrep test and classified according to the 2001 Bethesda classification system as follows: negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM), atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US), LSIL, atypical squamous cells cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H), HSIL, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and atypical glandular cells (AGC) (including subcategories of AGC) [18]. The results of cytology and HPV testing within 3 months before biopsy were collected. TZ type was assessed under colposcopy and divided into3 types: TZ1 was fully visible and completely ectocervical; TZ2 was still fully visible but had an endocervical component; and TZ3 was not fully visible and had an endocervical component [19]. Normal saline, 5% acetic acid, and 5% iodine were administered to the cervix in order to identify abnormal colposcopy findings, and ectocervical biopsy was performed, with ECC if deemed necessary. Pathological diagnosis was reviewed by two experienced pathologists working independently, with any disagreements resolved through discussion. Final diagnosis was determined based on the results of ectocervical biopsy and ECC. For statistical analysis, the cytology of ASC-H, HSIL, and SCC were combined into a single category labeled ‘ASC-H+’. Histopathological results were categorized as (1) <HSIL: negative, LSIL, and (2) ≥HSIL: HSIL(CIN2/CIN3), AIS, HSIL+AIS, SCC, adenocarcinoma (ADC), and adenosquamous carcinoma (ASC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 20, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were presented as number (percentages) and analyzed using Chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to examine whether the variables were independent risk factors. Group distributions are represented with histograms. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Results distribution and the detecting performance of ectocervical biopsy and ECC

Table 1 shows the distribution of ectocervical biopsy and ECC results. The positive rate of the final histopathological diagnosis was 37.1% (17,503/47,134), with 22.8% (10,724/47,134) for LSIL and 14.4% (6,779/47,134) for ≥ HSIL. ≥HSIL accounted for 4.2% (2,002/47,134) of the ECC results, while ≥ HSIL accounted for 13.7%(6,468/47,134) of the ectocervical biopsy results. In the final histopathological diagnosis of LSIL, the additional diagnostic yield of ECC was 3.1%, while in the final histopathological diagnosis of ≥ HSIL, the additional diagnostic yield was 4.7% (Table 2). In the final diagnosis of HSIL, AIS, HSIL+AIS, SCC, and ADC, the additional diagnostic yield of ECC was 4.5%, 11.2%, 6.7%, 15.6%, and 3.3%, respectively. The additional diagnostic yield of ECC was 1.4% in all cases (328 LSIL + 320 ≥ HSIL/47,134). This means, 71 ECCs were needed to be performed to identify one case where lesion would not have been identified by colposcopy directed ectocervical biopsy.

A significant discrepancy was observed between additional diagnostic yield of ECC and diagnostic yield of ectocervical biopsy alone (McNemar’s text p < 0.001), with slight agreement (κ = 0.131, 67.4%[31,788/47,134] concordance rate). When compared to ectocervical biopsy alone, ECC demonstrated low sensitivity (12.8%, 95% CI: 12.3–13.3%), high specificity (97.9%, 95% CI: 97.7–98.0%), moderate positive predictive value (76.9%, 95% CI 75.3-78.4%) and limited negative predictive value (66.8%, 95% CI 66.4-67.3%).

Risk factors for additional diagnostic yield of ECC

ECC detected 646 additional cases compared to ectocervical biopsy alone. As shown in Table 3, logistic regression analysis and variable stratification showed that the risk of additional diagnostic yield of ECC was significantly higher in patients aged 45–64(OR 2.339, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.232–4.441, p = 0.009) and ≥ 65 (OR 3.778, 95% CI 1.904–7.495, p < 0.001) than in those aged < 25. There was a higher risk of HPV 16/18 type positive (OR 3.916, 95% CI 2.604–5.889, p < 0.001), HPV non 16/18 type positive (OR 2.391, 95% CI 1.606–3.559, p < 0.001) and untyped HPV positive (OR 2.486, 95% CI 1.570–3.937, p < 0.001) than of high-risk HPV negative. Patients with ASC-H+ (OR 2.691, 95% CI 2.146–3.375, p < 0.001) and AGC (OR 3.150, 95% CI 1.513–6.561, p = 0.002) cytology were at significantly higher risk of additional lesions detected by ECC than those who with NILM cytology. Patients with TZ3 (OR 3.557, 95% CI 2.162–5.854, p < 0.001) were more likely to be diagnosed with additional lesions by ECC than those with TZ1.

Ectocervical biopsy and ECC diagnosis in final histopathological diagnosis ≥ HSIL

Among 47,134 patients, 6,779 had a final histopathological diagnosis ≥ HSIL. As shown in Table 4, the final histopathological diagnosis ≥ HSIL additionally detected by ECC increased to 1.0% (185/17,636) in patients aged 45–64 and 2.2% (41/356) in patients over 65 years old. In patients with high-risk HPV infection, an additional 1.3% (140/2,911), 0.4% (104/25,027) and 0.4% (20/4,706) of histopathological diagnosis ≥ HSIL cases were detected by ECC in HPV16/18 type positive group, non 16/18 type HPV positive group, and untyped HPV positive group, respectively. The additional ≥ HSIL yield of ECC in the patients with ASC-H + and AGC cytology detected up to 2.3% (92/2,383) and 2.0% (9/81). ECC increased histopathological diagnosis of ≥ HSIL in 0.8% (310/5,133) patients with TZ3.

Discussion

Although ECC may detect additional cervical lesions, clinical utility of ECC is still controversial. In this study, 47,134 women who underwent colposcopy directed ectocervical biopsy and ECC were found to have 37.1% lesions, of which 22.8% were LSIL and 14.4% were ≥ HSIL. An additional 3.1% LSIL and 4.7% ≥HSIL were detected by ECC. The overall rate of additional diagnostic yield of ECC was 1.4% in all patients. This suggests that to detect one additional lesion, missed by ectocervical biopsy, would require screening of 71 women. Patients over 45 years old, with high-risk HPV infection, ASC-H + or AGC cytology, and TZ3 were found more likely to have additional lesions detected by ECC. In particular, a higher proportion of additional ≥ HSIL was detected in HPV 16/18 type positive patients. Therefore, ECC should be performed reasonably, considering probability of missed lesions and risk to benefit ratio of procedure along with consideration of patient ‘s comfort level.

Several studies have found that ECC can increase detection rate of endocervical lesions, with a 0.8-11.9% increased yield for ≥ HSIL [11,13,20,21]. A study involving 3,213 patients found that 9.3% of ≥ HSIL cases would have been missed if only random biopsies were performed without ECC [6]. Another retrospective study involving 3,026 women found that 4.2% of ≥ HSIL cases were additionally detected by ECC, which would have been missed by ectocervical biopsy alone [22]. In our large-sample study including 47,134 cases, ECC demonstrated an additional diagnostic yield of 4.7% among 6,779 ≥HSIL cases and 3.2% among 10,724 LSIL cases. The additional yields of ECC among AIS and SCC cases were 11.2% and 15.6%, respectively. Although the additional diagnostic of ECC was low in LSIL cases, ECC demonstrated a higher additional diagnostic yield in ≥ HSIL cases, especially in AIS and SCC cases. Thus, ECC was proven to be a clinically valuable tool for detecting these occult endocervical lesions. While ECC demonstrated high specificity (97.9%) compared to ectocervical biopsy alone, its remarkably low sensitivity (12.8%) suggests it misses most ectocervical biopsy-positive cases. The substantial number of ECC-negative/ectocervical biopsy alone-positive cases (n = 14,698) underscores the clinical importance of utilizing both techniques in high-risk patients to optimize lesion detection across different cervical anatomical sites.

Factors were stratified for multivariate logistic regression analysis, which indicated that patients over 45 years age, with high-risk HPV infection, ASC-H + or AGC cytology, and TZ3 were more likely to have additional diagnostic yield of ECC. Most of the previous studies have suggested that older women were more likely to have additional lesions detected on ECC. The latest standardizing colposcopy guidelines suggested that ECC should be preferred for all patients aged over 40 [12]. However, there is still no consensus on the cut-off age. Shepherd et al. [16] and Schneider et al. [23] found that ECC is most beneficial for women over 50 years old. There are also studies analyzing the relationship between age and ECC results by using the cut-off age of 30, 40 or 45 [11,13,24]. Meanwhile, with the decrease in age-related hormone levels and occurrence of cervical atrophy, a portion of the squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) becomes invisible and develops into TZ3. A study reported that the ≥ HSIL detection rate in 74 women with partial visible or invisible SCJ (TZ3) was 20.3%, while in 105 women with complete visible SCJ (TZ1 and TZ2) was 10.5%, indicating a significant difference [6]. Our study also found that patients with TZ3 had a higher rate of additional lesions (including LSIL and ≥ HSIL) detected by ECC than those with TZ1 and TZ2. Therefore, considering the fact that colposcopy cannot evaluate lesions hidden within the endocervix, it is reasonable to utilize ECC in elderly patients or patients with TZ3. As reported by Wright et al. in the ATHENA study, the baseline risk of ≥ HSIL was 20-25% among HPV16-positive individuals and 10-15% in HPV18-positive cases [25]. Furthermore, HPV 16/18 type infection, which accounted for 51.9% of HSIL and 69.4% of cervical cancers [26], has also been shown to be a risk factor for additional lesions on ECC [11,13,27]. Similarly, our study also found that there was a higher proportion of additional ≥ HSIL detected by ECC in HPV 16/18 type infection group. When analyzing detection rate for additional lesions, ECC had a higher detection rate in high-risk HPV positive group compared to HPV negative group. On multivariate logistics regression analysis, the additional ECC was higher in patients with ASC-H + or AGC cytology, while detection rate was lower in LSIL and ASC-US cytology. This result is consistent with the conclusion of several previous studies [9,11,19], that for patients with LSIL and ASC-US cytology, routine ECC examination is not recommend. The findings from our study are in line with the latest guidelines for ECC at colposcopy [12]. However, further studies, on a larger scale, from different ethnicities and regions can provide more reliable evidence.

The advantage of this study is the inclusion of a larger sample size, stratified analysis of utility of ECC in cervical lesion detection and its risk factors. The major limitations of this study are that it is a single center and retrospective study, and the possibility of data bias cannot be excluded. The potential biases including selection bias (due to non-random sampling), recall bias (in patient-reported data), and diagnostic review bias (in pathological assessments) could affect results. In addition, limitations in data processing, particularly regarding missing data handling, may influence the findings’ reliability. Finally, all participants’ HPV test results were retrieved from medical records. However, due to variations in HPV genotyping methods across different centers, the heterogeneity in testing may have influenced our findings. Currently, there is no universally accepted reference standard for HPV genotyping, and further research is warranted in this field. Future randomized trials based on different ethnicities and geographic regions will be necessary to provide more reliable evidence.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the utility of ECC in the diagnosis and treatment of cervical lesions. For patients with high-risk factors, such as age over 45, high-risk HPV infection, ASC-H + or AGC cytology, as well as TZ3, ECC examination may reduce the number of missed diagnoses of hidden lesions. By selectively applying ECC, women without high-risk factors could avoid unnecessary examination and discomfort.
