Efficient production of glycine from glucose via a two-stage biosynthetic strategy
Authors: Lei Chen, Zhijie Zheng, Feifei Chen, Weipeng Wang, Siqi Huo, Xiangyu Li, Alei Zhang, Kequan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-quality glycine is widely used in the cosmetics and pharmaceutical industries. Conventional chemical synthesis of glycine is disfavored because it relies on toxic reagents and complex downstream purification.ResultsHerein, we established an efficient two-step biosynthetic process for producing glycine from glucose. First, an l-threonine−overproducing strain was used to synthesis l-threonine via 50 L fed-batch fermentation, achieving a titer of 119.3 g/L. Then, a highly active l-threonine aldolase (PpLTA) was screened to convert l-threonine into glycine. To overcome thermodynamic and acetaldehyde inhibition constraints, an acetaldehyde elimination system was constructed via introducing ScADH1 and ScCR, which increased the molar conversion yield of glycine increased by 3.9-fold. Whole-cell catalysis gave a significantly higher conversion yield than crude enzyme preparations. A whole-cell co-expressing PpLTA, ScADH1, and ScCR was subsequently constructed. Finally, at 50 L scale, direct conversion of l-threonine fermentation broth afforded a glycine titer of 65.4 g/L within 24 h, with a molar conversion yield of 87.2%, In addition, 99.8% pure glycine was obtained with a yield of 76.6% via separation.ConclusionsAn efficient two-step process for converting glucose to glycine was developed and successfully validated. Evaluation at the 50 L scale demonstrated record-high glycine production performance. This work provides a promising technological route for the industrial manufacture of high-quality glycine.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-026-02966-3.

Introduction

Glycine, the simplest amino acid, has a wide range of industrial applications, including its use as a precursor for the pesticide glyphosate, as a pharmaceutical intermediate, food additive, and feed supplement [1–4]. In recent years, with the rapid development of the pharmaceutical and cosmetics industries, market demand for high-quality glycine (with a high purity and free from residual toxic reagents) has grown significantly [5]. Commercial glycine is mainly produced from chloroacetic acid using hexamethylenetetramine as the catalyst. Although the chemical route is efficient, it poses significant environmental risks. In particular, the reaction generates toxic residues such as chloroacetic acid and urotropine, as well as several byproducts (glycine anhydride, diglycine, and diglycine glycine) [6–8]. Consequently, to satisfy the requirements of high-end applications, stringent separation and purification steps are required, which greatly increase production costs [9].

Currently, glycine biosynthesis has emerged as a highly attractive strategy because it can produce glycine under green and mild conditions and without toxic residues. For instance, Wu et al. constructed a multi-enzyme electrocatalytic system that converts CO₂ and ammonia into glycine using electrical energy [10]. Kim et al. developed a coupled electrochemical-biochemical process to synthesize glycine via oxalate-mediated C-N reduction, using CO₂ and waste nitrate as carbon and nitrogen sources [11]. However, these systems suffer from relatively low yields, which limits their practical application. In addition to these biocatalytic approaches, the conversion of l-threonine to glycine using l-threonine aldolase (LTA) holds significant potential because of its high catalytic activity [12]. Nie et al. reported that 12.1 g/L glycine can be obtained from l-threonine within 12 h via shake-flask fermentation [13]. However, this approach still faces several challenges. The catalytic reaction of LTA is reversible and the equilibrium constant favors the condensation direction, resulting in low glycine yields [14]. Meanwhile, the accumulation of byproduct acetaldehyde further decreases catalytic efficiency [15]. In addition, the cost of the l-threonine substrate is relatively high. With the rapid development of industrial biotechnology, l-threonine fermentation using glucose as a carbon source has been industrialized achieving production titers exceeding 150 g/L [16–18]. Therefore, if the aforementioned issues can be resolved, it may be possible to achieve efficient biosynthesis of glycine.

In this study, we developed a two-step biosynthesis process for the efficient production of glycine from glucose. First, an l-threonine overproducing strain was used to synthesize l-threonine from glucose. Subsequently, an efficient LTA was screened and employed to catalyze the conversion of l-threonine into glycine. Meanwhile, an acetaldehyde elimination system was constructed by introducing alcohol dehydrogenase and isopropanol dehydrogenase. Finally, whole-cell co-expressed the three enzymes was used to catalyze l-threonine into glycine. In addition, the glycine production process was implemented in a 50 L fermenter, and the purification of glycine was also investigated.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

l-threonine (purity: ≥ 99%) and glycine (purity: ≥ 99%) were purchased from Aladdin Bio Inc. (Shanghai, China). Pyridoxal-5’phosphate (PLP) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) were purchased from Macklin Bio Inc. (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals and reagents were purchased from local suppliers (Nanjing, China) and were analytical grade.

Strains, plasmids and primers

The l-threonine-producing Escherichia coli strain maintained in our laboratory was used in this study. E. coli DH5α was employed as the cloning host for plasmid construction. E. coli BL21 (DE3) was used for gene expression. Details of plasmid construction are provided in the Supporting Information. The plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Table S1 and Table S2.

Protein expression

E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells were inoculated into 5 mL of LB medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/mL), chloramphenicol (34 µg/mL), or both, and grown at 37 °C and 220 rpm for 15 h. The preculture was transferred into 100 mL TB medium in 250 mL shaking flasks and cultivated at 37 °C and 220 rpm for 2–3 h until the cell density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6 − 0.8. Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, and further incubated at 18 °C for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min, washed with 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 8.0), and used as whole-cell biocatalysts. The protein expression was analyzed using 10% SDS-PAGE.

Construction of acetaldehyde elimination system

An acetaldehyde elimination system was constructed by co-expressing alcohol dehydrogenase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (ScADH1, NP_014555.1) and isopropanol dehydrogenase from Streptomyces (ScCR, WP_003971780.1). The DNA sequences encoding ScADH1 and ScCR were synthesized by Anhui General Biologics Co., Ltd. (China) and cloned into plasmid pRSFDuet (Novagen) to create pRSFDuet-ScADH1/ScCR.

Activity assay

The activities of PpLTA was determined by NADH coupled with ADH assay as previous described [19]. Under measurement conditions, one unit of enzyme activity is defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the oxidation of 1 µmol NADH per minute.

For ScADH1 and ScCR activity assays, 480 µL of HEPES buffer (20 mM, pH 8.0) containing 0.25 mM NADH or NAD+ and varying concentrations of acetaldehyde (20 mM − 50 mM) and isopropanol (10 mM − 20 mM) were incubated at 37 °C for 1 min. Reactions were initiated by adding 20 µL of crude enzyme preparations, and the absorbance at 340 nm was monitored for 2 min [20].

Denatured enzyme samples were used as negative controls. One unit (U) of activity was defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the conversion of 1 µmol of NADH or NAD+ per min [21]. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Fed-batch fermentation of l-threonine in the 50 L fermenter

The seed and fermentation media are described in Table S3 and Table S4.

The fermentation was carried out at 37 °C with pH maintained at 7.0 by automated addition of 25% (v/v) NH₃·H₂O. Dissolved oxygen was controlled at > 30% by automatically regulating the agitation speed between 300 and 800 rpm. A nutrient feed solution (peptone 40 g/L, glycerol 300 g/L, yeast extract 20 g/L) and a concentrated glucose solution (500 g/L) were continuously fed into the bioreactor for 32 h at flow rates ranging from 9 to 24 mL/h. Samples were periodically withdrawn and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Biosynthesis of glycine

Glycine biosynthesis using crude enzyme preparations was carried out at 30 °C for 1 h in 1 mL of HEPES buffer (20 mM, pH 8.0) containing 100 mM l-threonine, 1 mM NAD+, 0.5 mM PLP, 2 equivalents of isopropanol, 100 µL each of LTAs, ScADH1 and ScCR.

Glycine biosynthesis using whole-cell biocatalysts was carried out at 25–47 °C for 3–36 h in 1 mL of HEPES buffer (20 mM, pH 7 − 9) containing 100 mM − 1200 mM l-threonine, 2 equivalents of isopropanol and 10 − 70 mg/mL wet cells.

Scaled-up glycine biosynthesis was performed at 37 °C and pH 8.0 for 9 h in a 50 L fermenter containing 30 L of l-threonine fermentation broth, 2 equivalents of isopropanol and 50 mg/mL wet cells.

Separation and purification of glycine

The reaction mixture (initial glycine concentration of approximately 50 g/L) was heated at 100 °C for 10 min to denature proteins and then passed through a microfiltration membrane. The resulting filtrate was concentrated by rotary evaporation at 60 °C until the glycine concentration was approximately 150 g/L. Subsequently, an equal volume of ethanol was added and stirred at 4 °C for 12 h to induce glycine crystallization. Glycine crystals were collected by suction filtration. The mother liquor was subjected to ethanol recovery by rotary evaporation at 40 °C, followed by additional crystallization cycles. The obtained crystals were redissolved, purified using a 150 Da nanofiltration membrane (molecular weight cutoff, MWCO), and recrystallized.

Analytical methods

l-threonine and glycine were analyzed by HPLC (Shimadzu system). Prior to HPLC analysis, samples were derivatized using the Fmoc-Cl method: a 50 µL aliquot of sample solution (concentration ≤ 10 mM) was mixed with 250 µL of 100 mM borate buffer (pH 9.0), followed by addition of 300 µL of 10 mM Fmoc-Cl solution in acetone. The mixture was immediately vortexed for 10 s to ensure thorough homogenization and allowed to react for 30 min at ambient temperature in the dark. The reaction was quenched by adding 600 µL of 250 mM sodium borate solution containing 25% acetonitrile. The derivatized sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min to remove precipitates, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to injection.

Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent HC-C18 reversed-phase column (5 μm, 250 mm × 4.6 mm) maintained at 50 °C, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and an injection volume of 10 µL. The mobile phase consisted of mobile phase A (ultrapure water containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) and B (acetonitrile supplemented with 0.1% TFA). The following gradient elution program was employed: 0–5 min, linear gradient from 50% to 90% B: 5–9 min, isocratic elution at 90% B; 9–12 min, linear gradient from 90% to 50% B; 12–15 min, column re-equilibration at 50% B. Detection was performed at a wavelength of 254 nm.

Results and discussion

l-threonine production from glucose in a 50 L bioreactor

Previous studies have demonstrated that l-threonine can be produced from glucose via fermentation, achieving titers exceeding 150 g/L [16–18]. Our lab has constructed an l-threonine-producing strain, and the engineered pathway is shown in Fig. 1a. Consequently, a fed-batch fermentation of l-threonine was conducted in a 50 L bioreactor. As shown in Fig. 1b, the l-threonine titer increased with cell growth and reached 119.3 g/L within 32 h, corresponding to a glucose-to-l-threonine yield of 0.46 g/g and an average volumetric productivity of 3.73 g/L/h. The strain reached a high cell density (final OD600 = 94.4 at 32 h), and residual glucose was maintained at ~ 1 g/L throughout the production phase, thereby minimizing glucose accumulation. To further contextualize this performance, we benchmarked representative state-of-the-art l-threonine production processes using titer, yield, and productivity as key metrics (Table 1). Although the final titer was lower than best-in-class reports, the productivity achieved at 50 L scale within a relatively short fermentation time falls within the upper range of reported values. Overall, the upstream fermentation provided a stable and sufficient l-threonine supply to support downstream glycine bioconversion.

Biocatalytic synthesis of glycine

l-threonine aldolase (LTA) can cleave l-threonine into glycine and acetaldehyde, represents a promising biocatalytic pathway for glycine synthesis (Fig. 2a) [26, 27]. To obtain efficient LTA, we systematically evaluated eight natural LTAs, including EcLTA from E. coli (WP_000566376.1), PaLTA from Pseudomonas aeruginosa (WP_003105702.1), ScLTA from S. cerevisiae (NP_010868.1), PpLTA from P. putida (WP_065760197.1), StLTA from Actinomycetes (WP_011027566.1), CpLTA from Cellulolytic bacterium (WP_069998496.1), FbLTA from Firmicutes (OQB25421.1), and PsLTTA from P. salmasensis (WP_186607900.1). In addition, the potential aldol condensation activity of serine hydroxymethyltransferase (KpSHMT) from Komagataella phaffii (XP_002494026.1) were also evaluated because of its evolutionary and structural similarity with LTA [28–30]. The genes encoding these enzymes were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3), and the recombinant proteins were predominantly soluble form. Soluble expression analysis indicated comparable expression levels among the aldolases tested. (Fig. 2b). The molecular weights of recombinant EcLTA (36.3 kDa), PaLTA (38.2 kDa), PpLTA (37.9 kDa), StLTA (39.1 kDa), CpLTA (38.0 kDa), FbLTA (38.5 kDa), PsLTTA(50.0 kDa) and KpSHMT (54.7 kDa) were consistent with the values calculated from their amino acid sequences. The catalytic activities of these recombinant proteins toward l-threonine were then examined. As shown in Fig. 2c and d, all enzymes were able to catalyze l-threonine to glycine. Among them, PpLTA exhibited the highest activity, giving a glycine titer of 1.92 g/L and a molar conversion rated of 25.73%. The results are consistent with the report by Nie et al., in which PpLTA was identified the most active l-threonine aldolase toward l-threonine [13]. We further assayed the activity of the key enzyme PpLTA. Using l-threonine as the substrate, PpLTA exhibited an activity of 2937 ± 156.67 U/mL in the clarified sonication supernatant (Table S5). This observation is consistent with previous reports. It further supports the high catalytic activity of PpLTA toward l-threonine [31]. Therefore, PpLTA was selected for subsequent experiments.

Construction of an acetaldehyde elimination system for enhancing glycine production

Previous studies have revealed that LTA-catalyzed reactions are reversible, with an equilibrium constant that favors the condensation reaction, and that the byproduct acetaldehyde inhibits LTA activity, resulting in low glycine yields [32].

To overcome this limitation, we constructed an acetaldehyde elimination system by introducing alcohol dehydrogenase from S. cerevisiae (ScADH1) and isopropanol dehydrogenase from Streptomyces (ScCR) to convert acetaldehyde to ethanol, thereby driving the reaction equilibrium toward glycine formation while simultaneously regenerating NADH (Fig. 3a). As depicted in Fig. 3b, this strategy resulted in a 3.9-fold increase in both glycine titer and molar conversion yield. Xu et al. also reported that the production of β-hydroxy-α-amino acids was enhanced through ScADH1-mediated acetaldehyde consumption in l-threonine transaldolase-catalyzed reactions [33], which is consistent with our findings.

To reduce enzyme costs, PpLTA, ScADH1 and ScCR genes were co-expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) (Fig. 3c). SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that all three enzymes were expressed in soluble form, which is similar to that of single expression (Fig. 3d). Activity assays showed that both ScADH1 and ScCR were catalytically active (Table S5). The high activity of ScADH1 is essential to ensure efficient elimination of acetaldehyde.

In addition, the crude enzyme preparations and whole-cell biocatalysts were compared. As shown in Fig. 3e, whole-cell catalysis produced 7.04 g/L glycine with a molar conversion yield of 93.8%, which was 18.9% higher than that of obtained with crude enzyme preparations. To further examine potential by-product effects, we quantified acetone and ethanol in addition to acetaldehyde. Supplementation with acetone or ethanol (0.5 or 1.0 eq) did not significantly affect the glycine titer, indicating that the formation of these by-products did not significantly affect glycine production (Fig. 3f).

Optimization of reaction conditions for whole-cell glycine synthesis

The reaction conditions of whole-cell glycine synthesis were systematically optimized, as shown in Fig. 4(a). The glycine titer increased with increasing l-threonine concentrations from 100 to 1200 mM and then reached a plateau between 1000 and 1200 mM. Therefore, 1000 mM was selected for subsequent experiments.

The influence of temperature on glycine production is presented in Fig. 4b. The glycine titer increased as the temperature was raised from 25 to 37 °C, then decreased with further increase. Thus, 37 °C was the optimal temperature.

As shown in Fig. 4c, increasing the cell concentration from 10 to 70 mg/mL (wet cell weight) enhanced glycine production, with a marked increase observed at 50 mg/mL; higher cell loadings did not lead to further improvements. In addition, the glycine titer increased over the first 24 h of reaction and then leveled off (Fig. 4d).

The effect of pH on glycine production was also investigated. As illustrated in Fig. 4e, the optimal pH for glycine production was 8.0, whereas both lower and higher pH values adversely affected the glycine titer.

Finally, under the optimized reaction conditions, glycine production was substantially improved, reaching a titer of 69.0 g/L with a molar conversion yield of 92.0%, representing a 36.3-fold increase in yield compared with the initial reaction conditions (Fig. 4f).

Furthermore, the feasibility of cells directly utilizing l-threonine fermentation broth as the reaction medium was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4g, there was no significant difference in glycine titer between pure water and buffer solution. The l-threonine fermentation broth resulted in a slight decrease in glycine titer. Given the economic viability of the process, the feasibility of directly utilizing the fermentation broth is promising.

Scale up production of glycine from l-threonine fermentation broth

The scale-up production of glycine from l-threonine fermentation broth was evaluated in a 50 L bioreactor (Fig. 5a). As shown in Fig. 5b, the glycine titer reached 65.4 g/L within 24 h, corresponding to a molar conversion yield of 87.2%. This performance was comparable to that in small-scale reactions and was only slightly lower than that using pure l-threonine as the substrate (molar conversion yield of 92.0%). These results demonstrate that the whole-cell system can be effectively scaled up to convert l-threonine fermentation broth directly into glycine.

Separation and purification of the glycine

The glycine produced was separated and purified via concentration, nanofiltration, and crystallization as shown in Fig. 6a. A total of 1.48 kg of glycine was obtained, with overall recovery yield of 76.6%. Purity and whiteness are critical parameters for cosmetic applications (purity and transmittance values > 99%). As shown in Fig. 6b, the purified glycine was a white crystalline solid. HPLC and spectrophotometer analyses showed that the obtained glycine had a purity of 99.8% (Fig. 6c) and a whiteness of 99.3%, (Table S6), which meet the market requirements for cosmetic-grade glycine. The structural identity and high purity of the product were further confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Fig. 6d and e).

Conclusion

In this study, we developed an integrated two-step biosynthetic process for producing high-purity glycine from glucose. An l-threonine–overproducing E. coli strain achieved an l-threonine titer of 119.3 g/L in 50 L fed-batch fermentation. A highly active l-threonine aldolase from P. putida (PpLTA) was identified, and an acetaldehyde elimination module comprising ScADH1 and ScCR was constructed to relieve thermodynamic and byproduct inhibition. Under the optimal condition, a glycine titer of 69.0 g/L and a molar conversion yield of 92.0% were obtained using whole-cell as catalyst. Furthermore, direct conversion of l-threonine fermentation broth at 50 L scale afforded 65.4 g/L glycine within 24 h with a molar conversion yield of 87.2.0%. Finally, 99.8% pure glycine was obtained with a recovery yield of 76.6%. Overall, this work offers a practical route for the cost-effective industrial production of high-quality glycine from renewable carbon sources.
