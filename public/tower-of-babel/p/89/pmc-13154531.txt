Case report: delayed profound hypomagnesemia from carboplatin- associated renal magnesium wasting
Authors: Louaa Chebbo, Elias Karam, Gaelle Salame
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypomagnesemia is a well-known side effect of certain chemotherapy drugs, especially platinum-based agents. Renal magnesium wasting is more frequent and better described with cisplatin. Carboplatin induced hypomagnesemia is common, generally mild, occurs during the treatment or shortly thereafter, and typically stabilizes or normalizes during follow up.Case presentationWe report the case of a 58-year-old female patient with metastatic breast cancer, who presented for generalized pain and palpitations. Introduction

Magnesium is the second most abundant intracellular cation after potassium and the fourth most abundant cation in the body. It is predominantly stored in bone (60%), of which nearly one-third is readily exchangeable and plays an important role in serum magnesium buffering, followed by skeletal muscle (20%), other soft tissues (19%), and a minor extracellular fraction (1%) [1].

Magnesium homeostasis is maintained by intestinal absorption, skeletal storage, and renal excretion. Intestinal magnesium uptake depends essentially on magnesium intake and occurs via paracellular transport in the small intestine responsible for bulk absorption, and to a much lesser extent via transcellular transport in the colon and cecum. This renders the kidneys the key actor in magnesium handling. At the nephron level, 70% of the serum magnesium is filtered by the glomeruli. Paracellular magnesium reabsorption occurs in the proximal convoluted tubule and the thick ascending limb of the Henle’s loop, where 10 to 25% and 50 to 70% of filtered magnesium are reabsorbed respectively [2]. Fine tuning of tubular magnesium occurs via transcellular transport in the distal convoluted tubule, which reabsorbs 5 to 10% [3].

The normal serum magnesium level in adults is 1.7 to 2.4 mg/dL [4]. Serum magnesium levels define hypomagnesemia severity as follows: grade 1, 1.7–1.2 mg/dL; grade 2, 1.2–0.9 mg/dL; grade 3, 0.9–0.7 mg/dL and grade 4 for levels below 0.7 mg/dL [5]. Hypomagnesemia is considered severe when serum magnesium levels are less than 1.2 mg/dL. Etiologies of hypomagnesemia

Hypomagnesemia frequently causes hypokalemia by reducing magnesium’s inhibition of renal outer medullary K (ROMK) channels, which increases potassium excretion. It can also lead to hypocalcemia by lowering PTH levels, reducing vitamin D activation, and impairing intestinal calcium absorption.

A common measure of renal magnesium handling is the fractional excretion of magnesium. In hypomagnesemia cases, healthy kidneys should reduce their magnesium excretion to less than 2%. Higher levels are considered an indicator of renal magnesium wasting [3].

Platinum-based chemotherapy, including cisplatin, carboplatin and oxaliplatin, remains a cornerstone of treatment for multiple solid malignancies [6]. Hypomagnesemia is a well-recognized adverse effect of this drug class, occurring more frequently and severely with cisplatin due to direct tubular injury affecting magnesium reabsorption in the ascending limb of Henle’s loop and the distal convoluted tubule [7]. The underlying mechanisms are similar with carboplatin [8]. Carboplatin-associated hypomagnesemia is generally milder, typically occurs during or shortly after treatment, and decreases with severity over time. Here, we report a case of profound hypomagnesemia from renal magnesium wasting one year after cessation of carboplatin therapy, highlighting a rare and delayed manifestation of platinum-associated nephrotoxicity.

Case description

A 58-year-old woman with a history of metastatic breast cancer involving the bone, lungs, liver, and brain presented for diffuse pain. Her oncologic history began in 2019 with a diagnosis of HER2-positive localized breast cancer, for which she underwent surgical resection. Disease recurrence occurred in 2023 as locally advanced HER2-positive breast cancer, and she was subsequently treated with multiple systemic chemotherapy regimens that were sequentially modified based on disease progression (Supplementary Fig. 1). Two days prior to admission, she had completed a 10-day course of radiation therapy consisting of volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) combined with image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT), targeting metastatic lesions involving L4–S3 and the right hip.

Her past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, chronic gastroesophageal reflux disease, and hyperthyroidism. Surgical history included right mastectomy, cholecystectomy, left oophorectomy, bilateral cataract surgery, right carpal tunnel release, and prior orthopedic repair of a left distal fibular fracture. Her home medications included sitagliptin, metformin, bisoprolol, esomeprazole (40 mg twice daily since > 10 years), morphine, sertraline, alprazolam, and gabapentin.

Her presenting symptoms included asthenia, non-specific diffuse pain and palpitations.

Diagnostic, treatment and outcomes

Diagnostic assessment

Fractional excretion analysis initially demonstrated a markedly low fractional excretion of magnesium (FEMg) of 0.72%. This value may have been influenced by the non-simultaneous collection of serum and urine samples, as well as by a low serum creatinine (0.27 mg/dL), reflecting the patient’s very low muscle mass from her ongoing malignancy, in combination with a relatively concentrated spot urine sample (urine creatinine 46.46 mg/dL), potentially underestimating the true fractional excretion. Despite this, the serum magnesium was profoundly low (0.45 mg/dL), with an inappropriately elevated urinary magnesium concentration (0.56 mg/dL), supporting ongoing renal magnesium loss. Additionally, the patient had been on long-term proton pump inhibitor therapy, which may have contributed to intestinal magnesium malabsorption, suggesting a mixed mechanism of hypomagnesemia and complicating the initial biochemical interpretation. Fractional excretion of calcium was markedly reduced (0.03%) in the context of severe hypocalcemia (corrected calcium 6.36 mg/dL), consistent with functional hypoparathyroidism secondary to severe hypomagnesemia. Following partial magnesium repletion, with serum magnesium increasing to 1.23 mg/dL, repeat FEMg rose to 6.06%, further confirming inappropriate renal magnesium wasting.

The diagnosis of magnesium wasting had in fact been established during a prior hospitalization approximately 6 weeks earlier, when the patient presented with generalized pain as well.

Laboratory evaluation at that time revealed severe hypomagnesemia, with a nadir serum magnesium level of 0.74 mg/dL, associated with inappropriate renal magnesium wasting, as reflected by a fractional excretion of magnesium of 9.47% following intravenous magnesium repletion and 5.35% after discontinuation of intravenous supplementation. Concomitant laboratory findings included hypocalcemia with a low fractional excretion of calcium (0.36%), consistent with secondary hypocalcemia. Review of her home medications did not identify agents known to cause renal magnesium wasting. Although high-dose proton pump inhibitor therapy can contribute to hypomagnesemia, its impact is mainly through reduced intestinal absorption rather than increased renal magnesium loss, and it is not the primary cause of hypomagnesemia in our patient. Carboplatin therapy was administered from 2 February 2023 to 25 May 2023 and was subsequently re-introduced from 6 June 2024 to 26 December 2024. In the absence of alternative etiologies, the patient’s renal magnesium wasting was attributed to cumulative prior carboplatin exposure. The patient was discharged on oral supplementation with calcium carbonate 1,800 mg daily and magnesium lactate 1,410 mg daily, while continuing her twice-daily proton pump inhibitor, resulting in normalization of serum magnesium and calcium levels on follow-up. Outpatient evaluation demonstrated a parathyroid hormone level of 47 pg/mL, inappropriately normal in the setting of hypocalcemia, and a sufficient 25-hydroxyvitamin D level of 48 ng/mL. Notably, the patient discontinued both oral supplements approximately two weeks prior to the current admission due to painful oral aphthae.

Therapeutic interventions

Based on the magnesium and calcium results of the actual admission, the patient received 4 g of intravenous magnesium sulfate and 2 g of intravenous calcium gluconate, with subsequent boluses guided by follow-up laboratory measurements, allowing partial correction of magnesium and calcium levels above critical thresholds. Due to difficulties maintaining magnesium above 1.2 mg/dL, a daily continuous magnesium infusion of 4 g over 24 h was initiated. Pantoprazole 40 mg (started per hospital protocol in place of her home esomeprazole 40 mg twice daily) was later switched to famotidine 40 mg daily.

Follow-up and outcomes

Following these interventions, magnesium levels remained consistently above 1.3 mg/dL, reaching normal values for the first time during her hospitalization.

The patient later developed urinary symptoms, including dysuria, and urine culture confirmed an E. coli infection. An abdominal and pelvic CT scan demonstrated new bladder wall thickening, as well as diffuse colonic wall thickening with mucosal enhancement, submucosal edema, and several intestinal loops with wall thickening and fluid content. She had already experienced 2–3 episodes of diarrhea earlier during the hospitalization, which resolved spontaneously; stool culture, microscopic examination, and Clostridium difficile testing were all negative. The patient was subsequently started on tigecycline to treat her cystitis and cover possible pancolitis.

Unfortunately, her malignancy continued to progress despite recent chemotherapy modifications. She required escalating doses of morphine and ultimately passed away peacefully from her disease, remaining comfortable and free of pain in her final stages, which was the most important aspect of her care.

Discussion and conclusions

Mechanism of platinum-induced magnesium wasting

Platinum agents exert renal toxicity by binding to mitochondrial and nuclear DNA-an interaction that also underlies their therapeutic activity-within renal tubular cells. This binding leads to the formation of platinum-DNA adducts, which leads to DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress through increased levels of free radicals. All these events ultimately trigger cellular apoptosis [9]. It was also demonstrated that carboplatin causes time-dependent oxidative renal injury in rat models, starting day 3–5 post exposure, marked by platinum accumulation, increased lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion, and reduced antioxidant enzyme activity and expression [10]. Carboplatin produces the same types of DNA adducts as cisplatin, but in smaller amounts due to its greater molecular stability. Furthermore, carboplatin-DNA adducts persist for only days, whereas cisplatin-DNA adducts may remain for weeks [9].

Evidence from the literature

Carboplatin is a second-generation platinum agent that was developed to overcome the side effects seen with cisplatin such as nephrotoxicity [8]. However, it has also been associated with magnesium-wasting syndromes, albeit with lower frequency and severity. In a retrospective single-center review of ovarian cancer patients receiving carboplatin-based chemotherapy between 2012 and 2018, hypomagnesemia occurred in 72% of cases, of which only 11% were grade 3/4 [11]. Our patient had multiple episodes of grade 4 hypomagnesemia. The same review showed that premorbid hypomagnesemia is associated with a significantly longer duration and higher grade of hypomagnesemia [11]. In our case, baseline serum magnesium was within normal limits at 2.0 mg/dL in October 2022, prior to initiation of platinum-based chemotherapy. The patient received carboplatin from February to May 2023, during which time serum magnesium declined to 1.3 mg/dL by May 2023, consistent with carboplatin-associated renal magnesium wasting, although no urinary magnesium was measured at that time. Following discontinuation of carboplatin, partial recovery was observed, with magnesium increasing to 1.5 mg/dL in September 2023. Carboplatin therapy was re-initiated in June 2024 and continued through December 2024. Subsequent monitoring demonstrated recurrent hypomagnesemia, with serum magnesium measuring 1.3 mg/dL in April 2025. Levels initially remained relatively stable but later demonstrated progressive decline, reaching 1.0 mg/dL and subsequently 0.74 mg/dL by October 2025, indicating worsening hypomagnesemia following cumulative platinum exposure (Fig. 1).

Serum magnesium levels over time with carboplatin treatment

A report of chemotherapeutic agents’ nephrotoxicity in 651 sarcoma patients showed that the prevalence of hypomagnesemia decreased and the mean serum magnesium concentration increased during follow up of patients treated with platinum-based agents [12]. Regarding the recent decline in magnesium levels, proton pump inhibitors are unlikely to be the cause, as PPI-induced hypomagnesemia results from impaired intestinal magnesium absorption and is typically associated with a low fractional excretion of magnesium [13].

Carboplatin-induced magnesium wasting appears to be dose dependent, with severity correlating with cumulative exposure [8]. In the DCCSS-LATER 2 Renal Study which evaluated 1024 childhood cancer survivors (at least five years after cancer diagnosis), carboplatin cumulative dose > 2800 mg/m2 was associated with potassium loss, but long-term tubular dysfunction was infrequent [14]. In our case, the patient received an approximate cumulative carboplatin dose of 5,500 mg, a cumulative exposure that could plausibly predispose to subclinical renal tubular injury.

Carboplatin-associated nephrotoxicity typically manifests during treatment or shortly after completion and may be prolonged; however, serum magnesium levels generally improve over time with continued follow-up. A prospective single-center longitudinal cohort study evaluating long-term renal outcomes over a 10-year follow-up included 63 pediatric patients treated with platinum-based chemotherapy, of whom 24 received carboplatin alone. Within this subgroup, two patients required magnesium supplementation one month after treatment completion, only one at one year, and none at ten years of follow-up [15]. A case report published in 2017 described a middle-aged woman with stage IIIa chronic kidney disease who presented with a femoral neck fracture following recurrent syncopal episodes. She had severe hypomagnesemia (serum magnesium 0.6 mg/dL) and had received carboplatin therapy four months earlier, with persistently low magnesium levels since. Her fractional excretion of magnesium was 16%, indicating renal magnesium wasting [16].

Uniqueness of the delayed presentation

This case is strengthened by its novelty, describing a rare and markedly delayed severe hypomagnesemia caused by renal magnesium wasting, occurring up to one year after carboplatin cessation. The diagnosis was supported by detailed biochemical evaluation, including repeated fractional excretion of magnesium measurements obtained both at presentation and after partial magnesium correction, demonstrating inappropriate renal magnesium losses. There were no lifelong electrolytes abnormalities and magnesium levels were normal prior to carboplatin initiation making Gitelman syndrome less likely. Alternative causes of hypomagnesemia were systematically evaluated and reasonably excluded, reinforcing the association with prior carboplatin exposure. Magnesium levels in our patient fell substantially at ten months then at one year after the last carboplatin dose, following an initial period of stability at acceptable levels without supplementation. The exact mechanism behind this delayed decline is unclear, but we hypothesize that the patient’s recent poor oral intake due to disease progression may have contributed to renal hypoperfusion, exacerbating tubular injury.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. A 24-hour urinary magnesium collection could not be performed, as withholding magnesium supplementation was deemed unsafe due to the risk of worsening hypomagnesemia and potential cardiac arrhythmias. In addition, causality cannot be definitively established in a single case report; however, the absence of other identifiable causes of renal magnesium wasting supports a likely link to prior carboplatin therapy.

Prevention and treatment of platinum-agents induced hypomagnesemia

A meta-analysis published in October 2023 showed that prophylactic Mg supplementation prior to cisplatin treatment significantly reduces the risk of hypomagnesemia as compared with no prophylaxis [17]. Magnesium may help prevent platinum buildup in kidney tubular cells, likely by regulating the renal transporters that control drug uptake and elimination [18]. There are no well-established guidelines for the management of platinum-salts induced hypomagnesemia. The mainstay of treatment is magnesium supplementation. Mild hypomagnesemia is typically treated with oral magnesium preparations. Organic magnesium salts -such as magnesium citrate, aspartate, glycinate, gluconate, and lactate- are generally better absorbed than inorganic salts, including magnesium chloride, carbonate, and oxide [4]. Intravenous magnesium therapy is recommended for patients whose hypomagnesemia does not respond to oral supplementation. This includes individuals with short-bowel syndrome, those experiencing tetany or seizures, and patients who are hemodynamically unstable, particularly if accompanied by arrhythmias, hypokalemia, or hypocalcemia [19]. Amiloride is a potassium sparing diuretic that inhibits the epithelial sodium channel in the distal convoluted tubule and the collecting duct. In addition to his natriuretic and potassium sparing effects, it increases the reabsorption of magnesium in the distal nephron. A clinical trial in 18 patients with Gitelman syndrome showed that amiloride (titrated to 20 mg/day) increased serum magnesium by 0.43 mg/dL and significantly reduced urinary magnesium loss [20]. Recently, SGLT2 inhibitors have been recognized as a promising class of drugs for managing hypomagnesemia. Their effect is believed to result from multiple mechanisms: increased paracellular magnesium reabsorption in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, enhanced TRPM6-mediated magnesium transport in distal convoluted tubule cells, improved intestinal magnesium absorption, and a shift of magnesium from intracellular to extracellular compartments through reduced insulin levels [21].

Clinical implications

This case highlights the importance of prolonged monitoring of magnesium levels in patients treated with carboplatin, as normal findings early after therapy do not rule out the possibility of delayed, severe depletion, even up to one year after treatment cessation. Since routine magnesium assessment is often not performed during late follow-up after carboplatin therapy, we hope this case will help inform clinical practice and encourage the inclusion of magnesium as a routine parameter to monitor in patients with a history of carboplatin exposure.

Patient perspective

The patient experienced significant fatigue and diffuse pain related to both her underlying malignancy and complications of hypomagnesemia. She was unable to tolerate oral supplementation due to painful oral ulcers. Throughout her hospitalization, she was cooperative and demonstrated a clear understanding of her disease and associated nephropathy. Her primary goal was to remain free of pain, and this was achieved by adjusting her continuous intravenous morphine infusion, allowing her to be comfortable during her final moments. This focus on comfort was highly valued by both the patient and her family. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s brother for the publication of this case report. Oral consent was obtained from the patient before her passing.
