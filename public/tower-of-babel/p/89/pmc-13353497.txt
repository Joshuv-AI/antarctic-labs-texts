Nurses’ interest in the care of patients who have attempted suicide in hospital-based emergency care settings: a grounded theory study
Authors: Kumiko Hashiratani, Hiroko Azechi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency nurses caring for patients who have attempted suicide face ethical dilemmas and negative emotions that hinder empathetic care. Existing studies have mainly reported emotional burden and mixed attitudes, with limited research examining nurses’ internal interest processes. Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the structure and processes underlying nurses’ interest in caring for these patients in hospital-based emergency care settings, using a grounded theory approach.MethodData were collected through semi-structured interviews with 25 nurses experienced in emergency care in Japan who had provided care to patients who had attempted suicide. Interviews were conducted from August 2022 to December 2023 and analyzed using constant comparative analysis.ResultsSix major categories emerged: “Attention towards care,” “Being influenced by one’s inner self,” “Welling up emotions,” “Focusing on attention,” “Deciding the direction of care,” and “Taking a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation.” Nurses’ beliefs and values were profoundly challenged by the gravity of suicide attempts, eliciting complex emotional responses combining a sense of moral responsibility, discomfort, and conflicting feelings.ConclusionsEmergency nurses maintained an interest in patient care despite emotional conflict and a destabilized sense of self. Self-awareness and situational awareness, particularly taking a meta-perspective, supported professional caring and decision-making.

Background

Enhancing well-being in Japan requires prioritizing suicide prevention as a critical public health issue. In Health Japan 21, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare set a target of reducing the suicide mortality rate to 13.0 or fewer per 100,000 people by 2026. However, despite enhanced legal measures and local policies, suicide continues to disproportionately affect certain demographic groups, including children, adolescents, and women (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, 2022).

Emergency department (ED) encounters following a suicide attempt are crucial for secondary prevention. The ACTION-J trial demonstrated that a multifaceted intervention significantly reduced the rate of repeat suicide attempts (Kawanishi et al., 2014). However, challenges remain, including poor integration between emergency and psychiatric services and limited psychiatric follow-up after discharge (Ohbe et al., 2021). Consequently, ED nurses play a vital role in delivering essential psychological support, effectively addressing the gaps in care continuity. In this context, nurses’ interest in and attitudes toward patients who have attempted suicide may critically influence care engagement and the quality of support.

Interest in patients significantly influences the quality of nursing care; however, greater interest does not necessarily result in better care. Interest should be understood as a multidimensional phenomenon involving affective, attentional, and cognitive engagement (Hidi et al., 2004; Renninger & Hidi, 2021). The way these dimensions are structured and interact shapes how nurses engage with patients and, ultimately, affects care quality. Therefore, interest is not simply a matter of intensity but rather a structured and dynamic internal process.

Although the concept of interest has been widely used in both psychology and nursing, its operational definition remains inconsistent. In psychology, interest has been theorized as a multidimensional construct integrating emotional engagement, selective attention, and cognitive processing (Kumata, 2012; Sakurai, 2020). By contrast, nursing has tended to approach interest in two limited ways. First, in Benner and Wrubel’s (1989) theory, a concept similar to interest is expressed as concern, defined as care, attentiveness, and consideration manifested in caregiving behaviors, and indirectly interpreted through the degree of engagement in practice. Second, previous studies have measured what individuals are interested in (Chonan, 2018; Irie et al., 2020; Kubik & Lee, 2014; Maruo & Kono, 2016), as well as the presence and degree of such interest (Coleman-Minahan et al., 2020; Morténius et al., 2016; Otsuka et al., 2019; Toyoda et al., 2019; Yamada et al., 2019; Zahner & Henriques, 2013; Zhang et al., 2017), primarily through questionnaires and similar instruments. However, these studies have not sufficiently examined the internal structure or developmental process of interest and are limited to static and descriptive understandings. In other words, interest as a dynamic process integrating cognition, emotion, and decision-making has not been adequately defined in nursing science.

The present study focuses on interest because of persistent practical challenges in caring for individuals who have attempted suicide in emergency care settings. Previous studies have shown that emergency nurses often experience difficulty, conflict, and negative emotions when caring for suicide attempt survivors; simultaneously, they perceive limitations in their ability to provide appropriate support to these individuals (Anderson et al., 2003; Aoki & Katayama, 2017; Conlon & O’Tuathail, 2012; Fontão et al., 2018; Hashiratani & Tomikawa, 2023; Urizaki & Kuwana, 2009). However, the underlying mechanisms explaining these difficulties, as well as concrete strategies for improvement, remain unclear. This study proposes that one important background factor is the internal process through which nurses direct their interest toward such patients. Specifically, what nurses attend to, how they interpret situations, and how they determine the direction of care may strongly influence both the selection and quality of nursing actions.

This assumption is supported by several theoretical traditions. From a philosophical perspective, Kant and Heidegger regarded “interest” as a fundamental driving force that directs cognition, action, and will, thereby shaping the orientation of human behavior (Heidegger, 1927/2013; Hiromatsu et al., 1998). In cognitive psychology, interest functions as a form of selective attention and influences subsequent information processing and decision-making. In nursing, as reflected in Nightingale’s (1893) triadic concept of interest, involving knowledge, heart, and skill, and Benner and Wrubel’s (1989) theory of practice, interest has been understood as a foundational element that guides the content and quality of care.

Based on these perspectives, this study reconceptualizes interest as an integrative process rather than a static attribute. This redefinition offers several contributions. First, it reveals nurses’ internal processes: what they notice, how they assign meaning, and how they make judgments. Second, it models nursing behavior as a sequence linking antecedents such as experience, values, knowledge, and skills, with outcomes such as caring actions, avoidance, or silence. Third, it includes forms of interest that do not lead directly to action, thereby helping explain insufficient care or disengagement. Finally, it may inform educational and clinical support that enables nurses to reflect on and regulate their own interest, thereby contributing to improved quality of care. In summary, this study seeks to reconstruct interest as a concept capable of explaining and enhancing nursing practice and to clarify its theoretical and practical significance.

Methods

Design

This qualitative study employed the Saiki–Craighill version of the grounded theory approach (GTA), a systematic methodology grounded in symbolic interactionism. The GTA explores how individuals attribute meaning to everyday events and formulate theories about human actions and social processes.

The GTA has undergone multiple iterations, starting with the original Glaser and Strauss version (1967/1996). Subsequent versions included those developed by Glaser and Strauss, as well as the Saiki–Craighill version, which further evolved the Strauss and Strauss–Corbin models by emphasizing further analyses involving properties and dimensions. Additionally, it incorporates category relationship diagrams based on properties and dimensions to investigate phenomena. This approach is characterized by the iterative and concurrent progression of data collection and analysis, with concepts progressively refined through repeated cycles of analysis (analytic circularity). This study employed a method designed for constructing an explanatory theory grounded in data (Saiki, 2014, 2016) to examine nurses' perspectives on caring for patients who had attempted suicide in emergency settings.

Data analysis

The analysis was conducted in a series of structured steps, following the methodological guidance outlined by Saiki (2014, 2016, 2021).

(1)Simultaneous data collection and analysis

(1)Simultaneous data collection and analysis

Simultaneous data collection and analysis

Following interviews with participants and observational data collection, verbatim transcripts were prepared, and initial coding was conducted concurrently. Subsequent sampling decisions were informed by emerging analysis. Nonverbal and observational data collected during the interviews were recorded as field notes, which served as additional context for understanding the transcripts and interpreting participants’ narratives. Based on coding results, theoretical comparisons were made to generate possible properties, and minor refinements were made to subsequent interview questions. Thus, the data collection and analysis procedure was iterative and cyclical.

(2)Theoretical sampling

(2)Theoretical sampling

Theoretical sampling

Sampling decisions were informed by previous analysis and analytic memos. Preliminary analysis of the initial 17 interviews suggested that nurses’ ability to view themselves and the situation from a meta-perspective was important for explaining differences in care engagement. Therefore, we conducted additional theoretical sampling by recruiting four nurses with varying years of emergency care experience to examine this emerging category in greater depth. We then interviewed one advanced nurse practitioner to explore whether and how this process appeared among nurses with more specialized clinical expertise. Finally, interviews 23–25 were conducted to confirm the relationships among categories and assess theoretical saturation. Following Saiki–Craighill’s version of the GTA, a tentative category relationship diagram and an integrated relationship diagram were created. These diagrams helped examine candidate properties and determine the target participants and required data or subsequent theoretical sampling.

(3)Continuous comparative analysis

(3)Continuous comparative analysis

Continuous comparative analysis

Comparisons were made across data levels, codes, and categories, refining concepts through the identification of similarities and differences. Additionally, new data were continuously compared with existing codes and categories, leading to the reconsideration of attributes, conditions, and variations. Further properties were generated by identifying differences and variations. The analysis utilized open, axial, and selective coding. In axial coding, category relationship diagrams visualized relationships among categories for each phenomenon. Selective coding was used to examine relationships among the major categories using properties and dimensions, and to integrate these relationships to develop the theoretical structure and storyline. This process was intended to develop an integrated explanatory model, rather than to designate one category as hierarchically superior to the others. Based on these data, integrated category relationship diagrams were constructed to clarify the structural relationships among categories. These diagrams were illustrative and served as analytic tools for theoretical integration and development of storylines, as well as final theoretical propositions.

(4)Note-taking

(4)Note-taking

Note-taking

Analytical insights, coding decisions, and theoretical relevance were continuously documented (113 notes in total), forming the foundation for theory development. Notes encompassed descriptions of concepts, hypothetical relationships among categories, deviant cases, and the researcher’s theoretical interpretations. Codes and categories were also repeatedly visualized through notes and diagrams, facilitating the development of category relationship diagrams and an integrative relational diagram.

(5)Theoretical saturation

(5)Theoretical saturation

Theoretical saturation

By the 23rd interview, no new insights emerged regarding the properties or relationships among the main categories. Saturation was confirmed between the 23rd and 25th interviews, when no new insights emerged regarding the properties of the main categories or the relationships among them.

Sample

Data were collected from August 2022 to December 2023 through one-on-one semi-structured interviews with registered nurses (RNs) in Japan who had at least one year of emergency care experience and had cared for patients who had attempted suicide. A single face-to-face interview was conducted with each participant, and those without experience in this field were excluded. The inclusion criterion of a minimum of one year of emergency nursing experience ensured that participants had adequate exposure to emergency care processes and team-based practices. Theoretical sampling was employed to recruit participants; interviews continued until theoretical saturation was reached, yielding a final sample of 25 nurses. Participants were recruited from emergency medical facilities across Japan, including those providing secondary- and tertiary-level emergency care.

In the initial phase, the director of nursing at the participants’ hospital was contacted, requesting cooperation for nurses’ participation in this research. Upon obtaining consent, we visited the facility, explained the research purpose, and sought collaboration. For those preferring written communication, the research protocol and a request form were mailed, followed by follow-ups via phone to confirm responses. Subsequently, the director of nursing or head nurse was requested to refer eligible nurses based on the GTA’s theoretical sampling method. The researchers explained the study details directly and individually to the candidates referred and requested their participation, assuring them that opting out would not lead to any negative consequences. Finally, nurses who agreed were regarded as the study participants.

Generating the interview guide

A prior conceptual analysis of “interest” identified five attributes: “attention,” “solicitude,” “own emotions,” “making sense of a situation,” and “directional decision making.” These attributes were used as a sensitizing framework for developing the initial interview guide. They were not treated as predetermined analytical categories; rather, they were used as broad guiding domains for constructing questions to explore nurses’ experiences of caring for patients who had attempted suicide. The research team reviewed and revised the initial interview guide prior to data collection to enhance clarity and validity. Early interview questions prompted participants to recall specific care situations, describing their focus of attention, interpretation of the situation, thoughts about the patient, emotions experienced, and subsequent decision-making and actions implemented.

Consistent with grounded theory methodology, data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively. Concepts that emerged in the early interviews were used to refine subsequent interview questions and guide theoretical sampling. Through this iterative process, the later interviews explored not only the initial question domains derived from the concept analysis but also newly generated concepts arising from the ongoing analysis.

Data processing and management

Interviews were conducted in quiet, private rooms to ensure confidentiality. Each interview lasted approximately 50 minutes, allowing sufficient time to gather basic attributes and detailed recollections. With written consent, interviews were audio-recorded; nonverbal communication was documented. All recordings were transcribed by the primary researcher. During transcription, personally identifiable information was removed, and each participant was assigned a research code. Audio files, transcription data, and analysis materials were stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team. The interviews were conducted in Japanese, and any quotations included in the manuscript were translated into English after analysis, with careful attention to preserving their contextual meaning. The translated quotations were checked against the original Japanese transcripts by the authors to ensure semantic accuracy and to preserve participants’ intended meanings. They were also reviewed during professional English editing to improve readability.

Researcher reflexivity

The interviews were carried out by KH (the first author of this paper), a certified nurse specialist in psychiatric and mental health nursing with experience in emergency care. Despite having professional expertise in psychiatric mental health nursing and emergency care, the interviewer did not supervise the participants. This professional background enabled the interviewer to be familiar with the clinical context, allowing the use of professional terminology with the participants and thereby ensuring the collection of rich and nuanced data. Meanwhile, this expertise may have also raised concerns about potential bias in both the interview process and data interpretation. Specifically, the interviewer’s clinical familiarity might have led to assumptions being made about certain elements that were not adequately explored. Furthermore, the shared professional background between the interviewer and participants may have resulted in participants relying on a tacit mutual understanding or framing their experiences in professionally desirable ways.

To address these potential influences, reflexive examination was conducted throughout data collection and analysis. After each interview, the first author (KH) wrote analytic memos documenting intuitive impressions, emotional responses, and assumptions, together with their possible influence on interpretation. These memos were revisited during coding and category development to critically examine how KH’s professional standpoint may have shaped the analysis. The second author (HA), who had expertise in qualitative research, independently reviewed the ongoing analysis on 23 occasions (once or twice monthly) to assess interpretive credibility and explore alternative explanations. After each review, both authors discussed their interpretations, and when discrepancies arose, they returned repeatedly to the raw data to resolve them, thereby strengthening the credibility and transparency of the findings.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the University of Kochi Research Ethics Committee on June 1, 2022 (Approval No. Nursing Research Ethics 22-04). All participants provided both written and verbal informed consent, which detailed the study’s purpose, confidentiality measures, the voluntary nature of participation, the right to withdraw, the disclosure of results, and the exclusive use of data for research purposes, along with secure data storage protocols. Written informed consent was also obtained from all participating facilities. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Attention towards care

This element emphasized the importance of gathering relevant information to understand patients during the care of patients who have attempted suicide. It highlighted that psychological distance was not prioritized, leading to distributed attention among the various aspects of patient care. The element comprised seven categories, including a subcategory focused on “Information that reveals the person’s individuality” (Category: “Understanding the patient’s way of being”). It emphasized nurses’ ability to readily comprehend the patient’s image while providing care. This understanding is developed throughout the engagement process, and the critical aspect identified was the degree of ease with which a nurse could grasp a patient’s image.

One nurse said,

“I try to see things as a life story—to see the person. I wonder why this person before me is experiencing this phenomenon now.” (V-18)

She sought to understand patients through details related to their personality, family relationships, and life history.

Being influenced by one’s inner self

This element captured how nurses’ beliefs and values were swayed by a broad range of information gathered to provide care, including the gravity and delicacy of a patient’s decision to end their life, thereby unsettling nurses’ inner being. Simultaneously, nurses experienced a complex mix of feelings, including both pleasure and discomfort. This element was often unconscious and influenced by their personal experiences. It contained four categories.

For instance, in the subcategory “Overlaying with one's own experiences” within the category of “Overlaying experiences,” nurses attempted to understand the patient’s circumstances by juxtaposing their own experiences with what the patient had been through. This property was identified as the degree of overlay experience. One nurse said,

“She (a senior nurse who died by suicide) took her life in a way that clearly showed she was determined to die. So, I realized she truly wanted to die, that she must have been suffering a lot. When I thought about it... I realized how easily death can come.” (K-31)

Reflecting on the death of a colleague who died by suicide, she sought to empathize with the survivor of the suicide attempt and the pain they exhibited.

Welling up emotions

“Welling up emotions” describes the complex feelings that nurses experience as they assess their situation and that of their patients. These emotions include a sense that nurses experience a complex mix of feelings, encompassing a feeling of doing good, alongside pleasure and discomfort, reflecting an intricate interaction with their inner selves (element: Being influenced by one’s inner self). This element comprises five categories.

For instance, “Worries about emotional fluctuations” is a subcategory (Category: “Confusion about being drawn into”) that refers to nurses’ anxieties about losing control over their own emotions—whether it is anger toward a patient’s reactions or empathy for their suffering. This property was identified as the degree of confusion regarding emotional involvement in patient care; for instance, a nurse who reported feeling distressed while empathizing with a patient and stated,

“It may sound strange, but I try to act as if I’m not too affected… (omitted) … If I listen too deeply, I feel like I might become weighed down myself” (F-24).

Conversely, the subcategory “Immersing oneself in the patient” (category: “Enthusiasm”) represents a positive emotion wherein the nurse actively attempts to comprehend the patient’s situation by intentionally placing herself in the patient’s experience. This category revealed the nurses’ proactive attitude toward vicariously experiencing the patient's situation. One nurse said,

“I let myself be absorbed in my own emotions and then in the patient’s emotions—on purpose. But I don’t get covered in them. I don’t, but I do. And then I check how it felt to be immersed.” (V-57)

Focusing on attention

“Focusing on attention” involves nurses recognizing their situations, being aware of their emotions, and consciously making decisions in care. They refine their focus by filtering information to deliver appropriate care, guided by their “Being influenced by one’s inner self” and “Welling up emotions.” This element comprises four categories.

One example is the category “Focusing on the present,” where nurses concentrate on current events in the immediate setting. The subcategory “Focusing attention on ensuring safety” exhibits a specific focus on safety, demonstrating an explicit degree of present concentration. One nurse described this prioritization of immediate safety for a violent patient:

"They kept trying to get up, something happened, and they were really thrashing around... [omitted]... (sedating the violent patient), and, well, it felt like restraint—safety was the top priority in how we responded.” (M-8)

Deciding the direction of care

“Deciding the direction of care” is a process wherein nurses assess situations, narrow their focus to prioritize appropriate care, and determine actions. It comprises three categories. For instance, the subcategory “Expecting others to become involved” (Category: “Considering delegating to others”) indicates that nurses foresee the impact of others’ involvement and their own apprehensions, which influences their level of engagement based on others’ responses. Consequently, the degree of activity in the intervention toward the patient becomes evident.

One nurse described her expectation that others would intervene first, given the delicacy specific to suicide cases:

“When the doctor says something like, ‘Why did you take the medication?’ and the patient answers, ‘Because I wanted to die,’ then maybe we could talk more. But no one asked that, and from my side, I couldn’t just suddenly ask, ‘Why did you take the medication?’” (P-35)

Taking a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation

“Taking a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation” (subcategories: 1. Analyzing oneself from a detached perspective; 2. A bird’s-eye view on matters) refers to a state in which another aspect of the self observes the entire situation and recognizes the structure or context in which one is currently situated. This element was referenced by a limited number of nurses, typically those with greater clinical experience or specialist certifications, although even these nurses seldom made a deliberate effort to adopt a comprehensive viewpoint.

For instance, in the subcategory “Analyzing oneself from a detached perspective,” nurses engaged in objective self-observation and analysis during patient interactions. By recognizing their emotional involvement and the influence of patients’ emotions, as well as their individual defense mechanisms, they effectively managed themselves accordingly.

As one nurse explained,

“I also step back and observe myself—myself as a person—from a certain distance ...[omitted]... So, when things are hard, sometimes I let it out on my own, I express what I am feeling, and I think maybe it is better to let it out. Like, ‘Let it out, let it out.’ So, that is my way of managing myself.” (V-53)

This statement illustrates the nurse’s understanding of herself through deliberate self-analysis from an objective perspective.

In another subcategory, “A bird’s-eye view on matters,” nurses reported perceiving their surroundings within the phenomena that emerged from interactions with patients, akin to an observer looking down from above. Consequently, the degree to which nurses adopted this elevated and detached perspective became apparent.

One nurse described her perception of the situation from such a detached, overarching perspective:

“I must take a very bird's-eye view of things. In my mind, it’s like there’s a helicopter hovering above the hospital. Within the culture of that community, there’s this acute hospital, and inside it, the emergency unit where I’m standing now. Who is around me, and what kind of person is lying there in front of me —I’m constantly seeing the whole scene from above.” (V-40)

A theoretical model of nurses’ interest in the care of patients who have attempted suicide in hospital-based emergency care settings

Nurses’ interest, as a process, was shaped by their lived experiences, beliefs, and values, swayed by the information they received while co-occurring emotional responses welling up from within. Moreover, they devoted attention to making decisions related to nursing care, sometimes adopting a bird’s-eye perspective (Figure 1).

Relationships among the six elements in the structure and process of nurses' interest in caring for patients who have attempted suicide in hospital-based emergency care settings.

The six major categories represented the components of nurses’ interest, whereas the five patterns described how these components were combined and unfolded according to nurses’ individual characteristics and the clinical context. In other words, the five patterns were not additional categories; rather, they showed different ways in which nurses’ interest was directed and regulated during care. Although “Taking a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation” was identified as one of the six major categories, it also appeared to function as a reflective and situationally aware moderating process that influenced how nurses regulated their emotions, attention, and care decisions. The prominence of this process varied across the five patterns and was particularly evident in the future-oriented care process.

The process of recognizing patient aggression (Figure 2)

The process of recognizing patient aggression is often observed in situations characterized by the patient’s unstable mental state, uncontrollable agitation, and verbal and physical violence.

Facing patients who have attempted suicide, nurses experienced fear (aggression directed at nurses) and (unpredictability), turning attention to the immediate situation (focusing on the present). Their primary goal was to calm patients and stabilize circumstances (considering ways to stabilize the situation).

A distinctive feature of this pattern was nurses’ fear of aggression (“welling up emotions”) arising directly from the patients’ aggression and unpredictability (“attention towards care”), rather than from the element of “Being influenced by one’s inner self.”

Caring for patients who have attempted suicide from a future-oriented perspective (Figure 3)

The process of caring for patients who have attempted suicide with a future-oriented perspective was observed when nurses had confidence based on prior experience and knowledge (confidence in care), a strong sense of responsibility to assess suicidal ideation (awareness of responsibility), and close psychological distance from patients (the barrier created by thoughts of death). This process was influenced less by years of clinical experience and more by individual responsibility, confidence, and reduced psychological barriers.

In such cases, nurses connected with the patient by sharing their experiences with those of the patient (overlaying experiences) to foster understanding. Their goal was to alleviate suffering while also envisioning patients’ future, selecting appropriate interventions implementable within emergency settings through future-oriented involvement (considering future-oriented involvement).

The challenging process of providing care for patients who have attempted suicide: Feeling responsible yet lacking confidence (Figure 4)

The process of caring for patients who have attempted suicide is fraught with challenges for nurses, who often feel a strong sense of responsibility (awareness of responsibility) yet lack the confidence to provide effective care (confidence in care).

Driven by their sense of responsibility, nurses endeavored to approach patients by overlapping individual perspectives with those of the patients (overlaying experiences). Simultaneously, they prioritized understanding the patients (focusing on the individual’s way of being) and (focusing on the surroundings of the patient). Although lacking confidence in their caregiving abilities, nurses considered the direction of care from the perspective of what actions they could take within the limits of their own competence (considering future-oriented involvement).

This struggle arose particularly when nurses experienced challenges in forming a coherent image of the patient and felt uncertain in caring for patients who have attempted suicide.

The process of turning inward behind the strong barrier formed by thoughts of death (Figure 5)

The nurses maintained a significant psychological distance from patients who had attempted suicide (the barrier created by thoughts of death)—a process marked by introspection influenced by the daunting thoughts of death. In parallel, nurses exhibited a duality of awareness of professional responsibility (awareness of responsibility) and a lack of confidence in delivering care (confidence in care).

In the observed process, the nurses frequently shifted their focus from the patient’s perspective to their own intentions and desires (focusing on myself). Within “Deciding the direction of care,” they had considerations, such as (considering ways to stabilize the situation) or (considering delegating to others).

This process was common among nurses who, despite some degree of composure, maintained a strong psychological distance from patients who have attempted suicide.

The entrusting process to others: stemming from a sense of diminished responsibility and lack of confidence (Figure 6)

The process of delegation among nurses is influenced by feelings of limited responsibility (awareness of responsibility) and a lack of confidence in their ability to provide care (confidence in care).

In such circumstances, nurses tended to rely on others or expect others to become involved, rather than engaging with the patient themselves (considering delegating to others).

Discussion

This study examined the structure and process of nurses’ interest in caring for patients who have attempted suicide in emergency settings. The analysis outlined a process of interest that begins with attention to care, evolves through emotional engagement, focuses on directing attention, and concludes with decision-making regarding care direction. During this course of action, emergency nurses experienced a destabilization of their own beliefs and values—that is, their sense of self—while encountering complex emotions involving both positive and negative feelings. Despite these internal fluctuations, nurses managed to concentrate on care and make decisions. A key aspect was the ability to “take a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation,” which relativized these processes and enabled nurses to acknowledge their cognition and emotions.

These findings not only illustrate the directionality of attention but also indicate that nurses continuously direct their interest process to patient care while regulating both their own sense of self and emotions, despite the time constraints and urgency inherent in emergency contexts. The category “Being influenced by one’s inner self” aligns with previous research addressing psychological distance, self-confidence issues, and role-related responsibility (Abe & Ueno, 2014; Aoki & Katayama, 2017; Fontão et al., 2018; Hashiratani & Tomikawa, 2023; Nakamura & Matsushita, 2021; Sugimoto & Kageyama, 2013; Vedana et al., 2017). Similarly, “Welling up emotions” aligns with prior reports identifying emotions such as anger and confusion (Abe & Ueno, 2014; Aoki & Katayama, 2017; Bohan & Doyle, 2008; İnan et al., 2025; Nakamura & Matsushita, 2021; Sugimoto & Kageyama, 2013; Urizaki & Kuwana, 2009; Vedana et al., 2017). This study explored the interaction between two dimensions that collectively shape nurses’ approach to care, thereby consolidating and expanding upon previously fragmented findings in this area.

Furthermore, among the patterns of interest identified in this study, the “future-oriented process in caring for individuals who have attempted suicide” appears to reflect a form of interest that is more likely to lead to proactive care. On the other hand, even in other processes, nurses continued to maintain a focus on patient care and showed sustained concern for patients’ well-being. Previous studies have reported that emergency nurses’ attitudes toward self-harm tend to reflect a lack of empathy or negative tendencies (Rayner et al., 2019). However, the results of this study suggest that even in situations where such attitudes are present, nurses’ attention may still be directed toward patient care. Therefore, these processes, which do not necessarily lead directly to proactive care, should also be understood as one aspect of nursing practice.

Simultaneously, this study highlights that processes in which nurses’ interest does not directly result in proactive care should not be interpreted merely as a lack of engagement. Rather, such processes may reflect complex internal conflicts, whereby personal values regarding death, fear, a lack of confidence, and a sense of professional responsibility unsettle nurses’ sense of self. Understanding this complexity may provide a crucial foundation for considering the kinds of support that are required to help nurses reorient such experiences toward more proactive patient care. Therefore, a capacity to reflect on oneself and the situation may be particularly vital.

In this regard, “Taking a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation” appears to be an important reflective and situationally aware process within nurses’ interest. This perspective has not been sufficiently clarified in previous research, which emerged through a focus on the internal processes of emergency nurses. When confronting the serious and sensitive issue of suicide, both self-awareness and situational awareness may be involved in nurses’ ability to make decisions while understanding their own internal state.

The subcategory “Analyzing oneself from a detached perspective” pertains to self-awareness, supporting Montgomery’s (1993) assertion that the essence of professional care lies in self-awareness and intentional self-use. Additionally, this detached and overarching perspective relates to situational awareness, which encompasses understanding the broader context in nursing. Fore and Sculli (2013) defined situational awareness as the ability to perceive environmental elements, interpret their significance, and anticipate their future states, highlighting its importance for safe and high-quality care.

In summary, the ability of “Taking a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation” may play a certain role in providing safe and high-quality care to patients who have attempted suicide in emergency settings. However, only a subset of participants exhibited this skill in this study. This suggests that it may be developed through experience and reflection and highlights the need to clarify its developmental process and identify appropriate forms of support. Future research should, therefore, examine the factors that facilitate the development of this capability and explore educational strategies to support it.

Limitations

First, the theory generated in this study was grounded in the care of patients who have attempted suicide in Japanese emergency settings and thus cannot be directly generalized to other contexts. However, it may offer a valuable perspective for understanding the realities of nursing care for patients who have attempted suicide in emergency care. Future research should examine nurses’ interest among those other than emergency nurses, as such interest may also arise in clinical settings outside the ED where nurses engage with the sensitive issue of suicide.

Second, because this study was based on participants’ retrospective accounts, the findings depended on their memories and interpretations. As the narratives were not produced in the actual care situation, they may not have fully corresponded to what occurred in practice. Future studies should, therefore, incorporate participant observation.

Third, the study’s strength lies in its adequate sample size and theoretical sampling, which effectively captured emergency nurses’ internal processes when caring for patients who have attempted suicide. However, because the participants were recruited through their institutions, the sample may have been biased towards nurses with a strong sense of responsibility. Therefore, further quantitative research is needed to capture more broadly the interest of nurses across emergency care settings.

Conclusions

Emergency nurses consistently directed their interest toward patient care, even while experiencing complex emotions and inner conflict. In particular, “Being influenced by one’s inner self” and “Welling up emotions” did not directly generate negative attitudes; rather, they functioned as a foundation that supported care orientation through their dynamic interaction. Additionally, “Taking a meta-perspective on oneself within the situation” emerged as an important perspective that supported care-related decision-making through self-awareness and situational awareness. These findings are significant for reconsidering the expertise required in nursing care for patients who have attempted suicide in emergency settings, and they suggest the need for educational support that sustains nurses’ internal processes.
