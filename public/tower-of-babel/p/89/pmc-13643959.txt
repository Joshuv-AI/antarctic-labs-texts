Psychiatric factors associated with early-onset and poly-drug use: insights from a large-scale addiction registry
Authors: Zohreh Manoochehri, Samaneh Nakhaee, Borhan Mansouri, Adeleh Elahi, Omran Davarinejad, Sana Karimi, Zana Ramazani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsEarly initiation of substance use and concurrent use of multiple drugs are critical factors linked to adverse health outcomes. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the demographic, behavioral, and psychiatric associates of early-onset and poly-drug use among individuals with substance use disorders in Western Iran.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, data were collected from the Addiction Disorders Registry of inpatients admitted to Farabi Hospital in Kermanshah, Iran. A total of 1,030 individuals diagnosed with substance use disorders were included in the analysis. Data were gathered through structured clinical interviews and reviews of medical records. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and logistic regression models.ResultsParticipants who began using substances before the age of 18 had a significantly lower mean age. Poly-drug users also tended to be younger. Logistic regression analysis indicated that male gender, being single, curiosity-driven initiation of use, and the presence of bipolar or personality disorders were significantly associated with early-onset substance use. Additionally, poly-drug use was associated with younger age, male gender, divorced marital status, and the presence of depressive disorders.ConclusionDemographic and psychiatric characteristics linked to early initiation and poly-drug use. These findings underscore the need for targeted prevention and intervention strategies that focus on vulnerable subgroups.

Introduction

Substance use disorder as a chronic and recurrent disease, is one of the most pressing concerns [1] and is closely related to cultural, social, religious, economic, psychological, and historical aspects of a community [2, 3]. Substance use causes serious cultural and social issues including robbery, suicide, murder, unemployment, violence, and child abuse. It also results in economic, political, and health-related harm, including hepatitis and HIV transmission through shared syringes or sexual behavior) [4–6]. Due to certain conditions and proximity to major sites of production, Iran has the highest per capita opiate addiction in the world [7–9]. However, Iran has seen significant changes over the last few decades in terms of substance use patterns [8, 10]. Opium has previously been relatively widespread as an analgesic [11]; but young people in particular are more interested in other substances like heroin, hashish, cannabis, and cocaine today [12]. Iranian “crystal” is one of the newest drugs used among people who use drugs and has become extremely popular among youth and adults [10, 13]. The abuse of new types of drugs in Iran has now become a prominent health issue, especially for youth [10, 13]. The previous studies have tried to find major factors associated with the use of drugs. One previous study in the Khorasan Province found that the type of abused drug is associated with place of residence, age, occupation, marital status, and education levels [11]. Since addiction is highly associated with the social, cultural, religious beliefs and the socio-historical context of the countries, referencing foreign studies will not be particularly beneficial; therefore, domestic studies are needed in this regard [10, 14]. In this study, we attempt to identify factors associated with drug use in a large population sample based from Addiction Disorders Registry at the west of Iran.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted using data extracted from the Addiction Disorders Registry of inpatients admitted to Farabi Hospital, affiliated with Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences in western Iran. Trained interviewers collected data using the same structured protocol and methodology employed in the earlier cross-sectional study by Farnia et al. [17]. The registry includes individuals diagnosed with substance use disorders (SUDs). A total of 1,030 eligible patients were selected through census sampling based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were: (1) documented registration in the addiction registry; (2) diagnosis of SUDs confirmed by a trained psychiatrist using DSM-5 criteria; (3) ability and willingness to participate in the study; and (4) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included severe psychiatric comorbidities that would impair informed consent or reliable interview completion: intellectual disability (IQ < 80), active psychosis preventing communication, and acute mania or severe intoxication at the time of assessment.

Data collection

Psychiatric disorders were diagnosed by trained psychiatrists using DSM-5 criteria based on structured clinical interviews and review of medical records. Only current, non-substance-induced diagnoses were included. Data were collected through structured clinical interviews conducted by experienced psychologists, complemented by a review of medical records. The dataset included key demographic variables such as age, gender, marital status, education level, and employment status. Substance use history was documented through variables including age at onset of drug use, type of first substance used, and reasons for initiation (e.g., curiosity, peer encouragement, pain relief, and increased concentration). Concurrent use of multiple substances was also recorded. Poly-drug use (concurrent use of multiple substances) was defined as regular use of two or more distinct psychoactive substances (excluding nicotine and caffeine) within the same 30-day period before admission, as reported during clinical interview. Psychiatric health status was assessed based on the presence or absence of specific disorders, including depression, psychotic disorder, bipolar disorder, and personality disorder. These variables were analyzed in relation to both ages at onset of substance use and poly-drug use status. All data were coded and entered into the registry system for statistical analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.KUMS.MED.REC.1404.015). Before participation, all individuals provided written informed consent. Confidentiality of patient information was strictly maintained throughout the study, and no personal identifiers were recorded. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

In this study, after excluding incomplete data, all variables were described according to their measurement scale, classified as either quantitative or qualitative. For quantitative variables, the mean and standard deviation were reported, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of their distribution. If the data were normally distributed, the Student’s t-test was applied to compare means between two groups; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. Qualitative variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Differences in their distribution across response groups were examined using the chi-square test. Two response variables were considered: (1) age of onset of substance use (< 18 years vs. ≥18 years), and (2) poly-drug use (yes vs. no). Current age was included as a covariate in the logistic regression models. It is important to note that current age serves as a proxy for historical context rather than the duration of substance use. To evaluate the impact of psychiatric disorders on these outcomes, two separate logistic regression models were fitted. Variable selection in each model was performed using the backward elimination method. Only variables that temporally precede or are fixed (e.g., gender, age) were considered primary predictors. Variables that could be consequences of substance use (e.g., unemployment, divorce) are interpreted with caution regarding directionality. All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of 0.05. Data analysis was performed using R software, version 4.0.3.

Results

Population characteristics

In this study of 1,030 participants, the mean age was 40.84 ± 10.72 years. Table 1 outlines characteristics based on age of onset of substance use: those who started before 18 years had a lower mean age (36.87 ± 9.96) compared to those starting at 18 or older (43.37 ± 10.42; p < 0.001). This younger group included more males (98% vs. 92.5%) and a higher percentage of singles (53.2% vs. 30.3%), with significant differences in marital status (p < 0.001). Curiosity was a more common reason for starting use in the younger group (24.6% vs. 14.8%), but peer encouragement rates were similar (37.6% vs. 37.7%). Table 1 also compares participants based on concurrent substance use. Those with poly-drug use had a lower mean age (37.98 ± 10.09) than those using one (41.63 ± 10.76; p < 0.001). The poly-drug users had more males (98.7%) and a larger proportion of singles (43.5%), while nearly half of single-substance users were married (50.3%; p = 0.005). Peer encouragement (37.7%) and curiosity (about 18%) were common reasons in both groups, but 12.1% of the poly-drug users cited improved concentration for their initial use, compared to 7.4% in the single-substance group (p = 0.002).

Figure 1 presents the results of examining the frequency percentages of response groups (age at onset and poly-drug use) by the type of first drug used. According to the results of this figure (above), among people whose first drug use was methadone, 87.5% had started using the substances after the age of 18. This rate was 82.1% and 75% for various other drugs and methamphetamine, respectively. In contrast, among those whose first drug use was cannabis, 66.1% began using the drug before the age of 18. In the lower part of Fig. 1, the highest prevalence of poly-drug use was observed among individuals whose first drug use was opium extract (85.1%), opium (83.1%), alcohol (81.6%), and methamphetamine (80.4%). The distribution of the percentage frequency of response groups among the type of first substance used was also statistically significant (p-value < 0.001).

Further examination of psychiatric health across different age-of-onset groups revealed that among the 1,030 participants, 39% initiated substance use before the age of 18, while 61% began at age 18 or older (Table 2). Overall, 79% of individuals reported no psychiatric disorders. The most common condition was psychotic disorder, affecting 10.5% of the participants, followed by depressive disorder at 3.5%, bipolar disorder at 2.9%, and personality disorder at 2.2%. Although bipolar and personality disorders were slightly more prevalent among early-onset users (4% and 3.2%, respectively) compared to those who started later (2.2% and 1.6%), the overall distribution of psychiatric disorders between the two groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.187). The results also illustrated the distribution of psychiatric disorders based on poly-drug use (Table 3). Among the 1,030 participants, 807 individuals (78.3%) reported poly-drug use simultaneously, while 223 (21.7%) reported using only one substance. Although the prevalence of depressive and psychotic disorders was slightly higher among those who were poly-drug users (3.5% and 11.2%, respectively) compared to single-drug users (1.3% and 8.1%, respectively), the overall distribution of psychiatric disorders between these two groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.365).

Logistic regression models

Following univariate analysis, two logistic regression models were employed to control for potential confounding effects of the explanatory variables on the outcomes (age of onset of substance use and poly-drug use). The results are shown in Tables 4 and 5 and illustrated in Figs. 2 and 3. Table 4; Fig. 2 show that males are significantly more likely than females to start using substances at an earlier age, with an odds ratio (OR) of 3.31 (95% CI: 1.51–8.14). This indicates that men have over three times the odds of beginning substance use before age 18. Marital status also plays a role; married individuals have a lower likelihood of early substance use (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.45–0.86). Participants who cited curiosity as their reason for first-time use had higher odds of early initiation (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.00–2.54). Those with bipolar disorder (OR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.05–3.28) and personality disorders (OR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.70–2.63) were also more likely to initiate substance use earlier. In Table 5; Fig. 3, the model assessing concurrent substance use indicates that each year of age reduces the likelihood of poly-drug use (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.98). Males have significantly higher odds of poly-drug use compared to females (OR = 5.55, 95% CI: 1.98–23.2). Divorced individuals are more likely than singles to engage in concurrent use (OR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.16–2.96). Lastly, participants with depressive disorders have 2.78 times higher odds of poly-drug use (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.91–4.02), highlighting a strong link between depression and poly-drug use.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that male gender, single marital status, curiosity-driven initiation of use, and the presence of bipolar or personality disorders play a significant role in early-onset substance use. Multiple epidemiologic studies have found that early drug exposure is determined by individual, familial, and environmental factors. A recent qualitative study among Turkish adolescents underscored that mental health concerns, peer pressure, traumatic experiences, childhood maltreatment and family dysfunction are important factors in adolescent substance use [15]. The same study reported that lack of parental supervision, high impulsivity, early romantic relationships, and co-occurring psychopathology, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or depression, also increase the chances that adolescents engage with alcohol and drug use [15]. Our finding that curiosity was associated with early use is consistent with these factors because sensation-seeking and impulsivity are often associated with curiosity and experimentation in adolescents.

In our analysis, we also found that male gender was a characteristic linked to early initiation. International evidence indicates that males are more likely to use substances earlier than females, in part due to gender norms that reinforce risk-taking and greater access to drugs [16]. Some work has also started to examine differences in the effects of early initiation across gender. A Spanish cohort demonstrated, for example, that early alcohol use predicted later problems more strongly in men, while early tobacco use was of greater concern for women [17]. There were few females in our sample, and we could not assess sex interactions, but this is consistent with the male predominance of early initiation. Married participants were less likely to use substances earlier than their single peers. Population-based samples indicate that marriage is associated with a lower likelihood of first onset for most mental disorders, including SUD, for both sexes [18]. In contrast, being divorced is an associated factor for substance use disorder [18]. Our finding that divorced participants were more likely to engage in poly-drug use is also in agreement with this evidence. Among a hospital-based sample from Sierra Leone, male gender, younger age (14–35 years), and marital status (being unmarried) were significantly associated with SUDs [19].

Collectively, the presented findings imply that marital stability may be associated with social supports and structured roles, which in turn are linked to later drug initiation and a lower likelihood of concurrent substance use. There were increased odds of early initiation for participants living with bipolar disorder or personality disorders, while those with depressive disorders endorsed increased rates of poly-drug use. These findings are consistent with previous findings, which have indicated, for example, that SUDs impact up to 50% of individuals living with bipolar I disorder, given the unique co-occurring neurobiological and genetic factors that make these individuals particularly vulnerable to substance misuse [20]. Similarly, another review emphasized that individuals with personality disorders, particularly the borderline and antisocial, frequently present with co-occurring SUDs, which may be associated with poorer eventual outcomes [21]. Individuals living with bipolar disorder consistently show lower rates of relationship stability, which is often accompanied by higher rates of substance use as a coping mechanism during a manic or depressive state [22, 23]. These results underscore the importance of social support systems in addressing the initiation of substance use among individuals living with mental health conditions.

The Sierra Leone-based study further supported that depressive and psychotic disorders were prevalent in hospitalized SUD patients [19]. Hence, the results of our study fit within a body of literature suggesting that mood, psychosis, bipolar, and personality disorders are associated with substance use and ultimately both the timing of initiation into substance use and the development of poly-drug use.

Additionally, in our study poly-drug use was associated with younger age, male gender, divorced marital status, and presence of depressive disorders. Studies conducted in high-income countries have shown that poly-drug use is more common among young adults, and is concurrently associated with mental health symptoms, including depression, anxiety, and PTSD [24]. Literature demonstrates that younger individuals are more susceptible to poly-drug use behavior due to a variety of factors, including seeking novel experiences, peer pressure and, experimentation [25, 26].

Cruz et al.‘s study revealed that most young adult crack users had a pattern of poly-drug use and that this usage pattern was observed mostly among men [27]. In addition, differences in substance usage patterns by gender may reflect broader societal issues regarding attitudes towards drug use, as males may be less deterred by the risks associated with drug use than females [25, 28]. We noted a strong connection between depressive disorders and poly-drug use in our analysis, which is consistent with findings obtained from a psychiatric cohort in Sierra Leone, where mood disorders were a common disorder among patients with SUD [19] and latent class analyses that also found that severe poly-drug users had greater odds of having depression and PTSD than non-users [24]. Jongenelis et al. showed that major depressive disorder was significantly associated with poly-drug use among adolescents and that youth may use products such as alcohol and tobacco, and other illicit drugs, to self-medicate depressive symptoms [29]. The self-medication hypothesis proposes that the use of substances to alleviate emotional pain due to depression may be used to greater dependence on drugs, and this pattern is associated with a worsening of depressive symptoms [29].

A recent NIH-supported MRI investigation noted that adolescents who began substance use before age 15 exhibited differences in brain structure compared to their non-using counterparts [30]. In fact, many of these neuroanatomical differences existed before drug use, suggesting the presence of potential neurodevelopmental factors that may be associated with some youths to experiment with substances at an earlier age. Sociocultural factors in Iran, such as greater freedom of movement for men and reduced social control for unmarried individuals, may also facilitate earlier substance use and poly-drug use [22, 31]. The notions of curiosity and sensation seeking that were noted in our results are behavioral manifestations of underlying impulsivity that could lead to experimentation and, in turn, habituation. Psychiatric disorders may have an effect through similar neurobiological mechanisms. Mood, bipolar, and personality disorders may involve emotion dysregulation and impulsivity, which could increase vulnerability to substance use as a maladaptive coping strategy. Given that our cross-sectional design does not allow us to determine temporal order, the psychopathology involved in substance use and psychiatric disorder; however, our findings highlight the importance of mental health consideration in prevention.

Strengths and limitations

The study utilized a large sample derived from a regional addiction registry and incorporated the use of structured interviews by trained clinicians, which improved diagnostic accuracy. The addition of multiple socio-demographic and psychiatric factors also enabled multivariable modeling of factors associated with early and poly-drug use. However, the study has several limitations. First, the sample is restricted to inpatients at a single tertiary hospital in Western Iran, likely overrepresenting severe, complicated cases with higher psychiatric comorbidity. Findings may not generalize to outpatients, community samples, or other regions. Second, the percentage of females in the study cohort comprised only a small proportion, inhibiting the possibilities of examining gender interactions or drawing conclusions about women. Third, due to the cross-sectional design, we cannot determine whether psychiatric disorders preceded substance use, emerged as a consequence, or co-occurred through shared vulnerabilities. Temporal order remains unknown. Fourth, with respect to variables related to reasons for first use and psychiatric diagnosis, they rely on self-reports from clients or clinician records, which may be susceptible to recall bias as well as diagnostic and classification bias. It is also important to reiterate that the current age was included in the logistic regression models solely as a proxy for historical or cohort effects (e.g., changes in drug availability or social norms over time), not as an indicator of the duration of substance use. Therefore, the inverse association between current age and poly-drug use should be interpreted as a cohort phenomenon rather than an individual-level protective effect of aging. Additionally, key potential confounders were not captured, including socioeconomic status, genetic factors, family structure (e.g., family type, birth order), urban/rural locality, tobacco use history, childhood disorders (especially ADHD), and early life trauma. Their absence may result in residual confounding. We did not capture early life trauma or information about family functioning, socioeconomic status, and genetic factors, all of which are known to play a role in substance use. The limitations associated with the cross-sectional nature of the present study should be considered when interpreting the findings. In particular, causal direction between demographic or clinical characteristics and substance use outcomes cannot be firmly determined. Therefore, longitudinal research is needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships between these factors and the initiation of substance use. Finally, the study used logistic regression to control for some independent covariates, but there remains the potential for unmeasured confounding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this hospital-based registry study indicates that early-onset and poly-drug use are associated with male gender, marital status, curiosity-driven motives, and psychiatric comorbidities. This is in line with evidence from the literature globally demonstrating that neurodevelopmental, psychosocial, and mental health factors all work together in determining an individual`s substance use trajectory. Interventions that focus on sensation seeking behaviors, social support and mental health care may be associated with delayed the initiation of use and a decreased the likelihood of progression to poly-drug use. Providing these interventions to high-risk groups, such as young men, people who are unmarried, and people experiencing mood, bipolar, or personality disorders, may decrease the burden of SUDs in Iran or similar settings.
