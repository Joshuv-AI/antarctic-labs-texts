Anti-androgen therapy in polyendocrine metabolic ovary syndrome (PMOS, formerly PCOS): efficacy, safety, and phenotype-oriented clinical decision-making
Authors: Hongfa Peng, Jingjing Jiang, Zhu Wang, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS, formerly polycystic ovary syndrome [PCOS]), hereinafter referred to as PMOS/PCOS, is the most prevalent endocrine metabolic disorder among women of reproductive age. It is characterized by a self-perpetuating cycle in which hyperandrogenemia (central in several, but not all, phenotypes) acts as the primary driver, exacerbating reproductive dysfunction, metabolic disturbances, and psychological distress. Anti-androgen therapy remains an important treatment for alleviating hyperandrogenism-related clinical signs, including hirsutism, acne, and female-pattern hair loss. In this review, we provided a comprehensive molecular analysis of hyperandrogenism in PMOS/PCOS, systematically evaluated the efficacy of key anti-androgen agents, including combined oral contraceptives, spironolactone, cyproterone acetate, finasteride, and emerging therapies, and assessed their safety profiles concerning metabolic, reproductive, and general adverse effects. We further examine integrated management strategies combining pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modifications, insulin sensitizers such as metformin, and emerging integrative approaches. Special considerations for adolescents, women planning pregnancy, and individuals with obesity are highlighted. This review also addressed current controversies, including uncertainties regarding long-term cardiovascular safety, optimal treatment duration, and the lack of predictive biomarkers, outlining future research priorities. Finally, we proposed a practical phenotype-guided framework for the stratified clinical management of hyperandrogenic PMOS/PCOS.

Introduction

Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), formerly known as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), hereinafter referred to as PMOS/PCOS, is a complex, heterogeneous disorder affecting 5–33% of reproductive-aged women worldwide and constitutes a major public health concern [1–3]. Unlike its former name—which emphasizes only ovarian cysts—PMOS/PCOS reflects the full clinical spectrum, which extends beyond anovulation and ovarian dysfunction to include significantly increased lifetime risks of obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD]), and adverse mental health outcomes [4]. This redesignation is a major advance because it appropriately acknowledges the polyendocrine and metabolic nature of the condition. However, PMOS/PCOS remains ovarian centered. In hyperandrogenic and insulin resistant phenotypes, the ovary is more accurately viewed as a target organ of systemic endocrine–metabolic dysfunction rather than the primary cause of the disease. For this reason, the expert group on inositol in basic and clinical research, and on PMOS/PCOS, proposes the term endocrine metabolic syndrome (EMS), which merits discussion. According to this model, hyperandrogenic metabolic phenotypes correspond to EMS, whereas the normoandrogenic phenotype D (polycystic ovarian morphology + ovulatory dysfunction) may represent a distinct ovarian disorder—such as multifollicular ovarian disorder (MFOD) [5, 6]. This distinction has important therapeutic implications, particularly for anti-androgen therapy, which is mechanistically justified only in the presence of androgen excess or increased androgenic tissue activity. Hyperandrogenemia—including excess biochemicals and clinical features—is a central pathophysiological axis and a key diagnostic feature of hyperandrogenic PMOS/PCOS [2, 3, 7, 8]. It disrupts folliculogenesis, contributes to ovarian stromal expansion, and promotes adipose tissue dysfunction and chronic low-grade inflammation, aggravating IR and metabolic dysregulation and creating a vicious, self-reinforcing cycle.

Therefore, the clinical management of hyperandrogenism is not only cosmetic but fundamentally therapeutic, aimed at interrupting this pathological cycle. Evidence suggests that timely control of hyperandrogenism may have favorable implications for long-term reproductive health and metabolic outcomes [9]. Contemporary international guidelines consistently recommend combined oral contraceptives (COCPs) as the first-line pharmacological therapy for hyperandrogenism, relegating specific anti-androgen drugs (e.g., the androgen receptor antagonist spironolactone) to second-line status [8]. However, the landscape of anti-androgen therapy is nuanced and influenced by patient phenotypes, comorbidities, reproductive goals, and regional drug availability. In this review, we synthesize and expand current evidence on the efficacy and safety of anti-androgen therapies for PMOS/PCOS, explore rational combination strategies, delineate unresolved controversies, and inform evidence-based, individualized clinical decision-making through a detailed analysis of underlying pathophysiology and clinical trial data. This review uses the 2023 International Evidence-Based Guideline [10] as the benchmark to summarize and evaluate the evidence on the degree of conformity of current treatment options with the guideline’s recommendations, differences in efficacy, and safety aspects, as well as to identify current knowledge gaps and future research directions.

Pathogenesis of hyperandrogenemia: a multifaceted network

Hyperandrogenemia in PMOS/PCOS arises from a dysregulated interplay between ovarian steroidogenesis, adrenal function, hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis activity, peripheral hormone metabolism, and systemic metabolic signals (Fig. 1) [11]. Understanding this network is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms of action of various therapies.

Ovarian dysfunction: the primary source

Enhanced androgen biosynthesis

Ovarian theca cells in PMOS/PCOS exhibit intrinsic and stimulated overproduction of androgens, primarily driven by upregulated expression and activity of key steroidogenic enzymes, such as 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase (CYP17A1) [12, 13]. This intrinsic dysregulation is potentiated by hyperinsulinemia and elevated luteinizing hormone (LH), which synergistically amplify cytochrome P450 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase (P450c17) activity and steroidogenic acute regulatory (StAR) protein expression, leading to increased conversion of cholesterol to androstenedione and testosterone [14, 15]. This theca cell hyperactivity appears to be a fundamental, genetically determined defect and an underlying cause of PMOS/PCOS. Recent studies using novel mouse models, such as the TC17 (Transgenic CYP17A1) mouse with theca-cell-specific cytochrome P450 family 17 (Cyp17) overexpression, have directly demonstrated that isolated theca-cell androgen excess is sufficient to reproduce key PMOS/PCOS features, including anovulation, polycystic ovarian morphology, and metabolic disturbances, highlighting its central role [14].

Granulosa cell impairment and altered follicular environment

Hyperandrogenism disrupts the function of granulosa cells, which are essential for follicular maturation and aromatization of androgens to estrogen. This disruption occurs through multiple pathways, including (i) androgen receptor (AR) overactivation, (ii) metabolic stress, (iii) inflammatory activation, and (iv) endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS). In AR overactivation, sustained AR signaling, potentially modulated by epigenetic regulators such as bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4), disrupts normal follicular maturation, promotes the expression of fibrotic factors (e.g., collagen), and contributes to ovarian stromal expansion and fibrosis [16]; furthermore, BRD4, by activating AR transcription, provides a molecular link between chromatin remodeling, androgen action, and ovarian tissue remodeling in PMOS/PCOS. In metabolic stress, androgen-driven metabolic reprogramming, including upregulation of the glutamine transporter, solute carrier family 1 member 5 (SLC1A5), redirects glutamine metabolism to support excessive ovarian cell proliferation and steroidogenesis, creating a metabolically stressful microenvironment [17]. Concurrent activation of the polyol pathway, in which aldose reductase converts glucose to sorbitol, under hyperandrogenic conditions leads to the depletion of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, excessive oxidative stress, impaired granulosa cell function, and oocyte competence [11]. In inflammatory activation, androgen-stimulated granulosa cells release pro-inflammatory signals, including specific microRNA-containing exosomes (e.g., microR-379-5p), which promote macrophage polarization towards the M1 phenotype, sustain a local inflammatory milieu detrimental to folliculogenesis, and promote a vicious cycle of dysfunction [18, 19]. This creates a state of “sterile inflammation” within the ovaries. In ERS, chronic androgen exposure induces ERS in granulosa cells, triggering an unfolded protein response that may lead to apoptosis and further disrupt steroidogenic and metabolic homeostasis [20]. Protective pathways, such as the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/AKT kinase (PI3K/AKT) signaling, may be downregulated, as evidenced by studies showing the protective effect of curcumin via this pathway [20].

Systemic contributors and vicious cycles

Hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis dysregulation

Women with PMOS/PCOS often exhibit increased gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse frequency, leading to an elevated LH-to-follicle-stimulating-hormone (FSH) ratio [21]. This pattern preferentially stimulates theca cell androgen production over follicular development [22]. Hyperandrogenemia can further perturb GnRH pulsatility, potentially through androgen aromatization to estrogens in the brain or through direct effects, creating a self-sustaining loop that disrupts normal cyclicity [23]. The reduced progesterone-mediated negative feedback owing to anovulation further exacerbates this high-frequency GnRH pulse pattern.

IR and hyperinsulinemia

This constitutes the core loop in the pathogenesis of PMOS/PCOS, particularly in individuals with obesity [24]. Insulin acts directly on ovarian theca cells via its receptor to synergistically enhance androgen synthesis with LH [25]. It also suppresses the hepatic production of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), increasing the fraction of free biologically active testosterone [26, 27]. Conversely, androgens promote visceral adiposity and adipose tissue dysfunction, characterized by increased lipolysis, macrophage infiltration, altered adipokine secretion (e.g., reduced adiponectin and increased leptin), and enhanced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [28, 29]. Dysfunctional adipose tissue is a major contributor to systemic IR and hyperinsulinemia, which can close the abovementioned cycle. Studies have shown that mediators such as High Mobility Group Box 1 and its receptors are upregulated in PMOS/PCOS and may link hyperandrogenism to inflammatory states in the reproductive tissues [28].

Adrenal androgen excess

Approximately 20–30% of patients exhibit elevated dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), indicating hyperactivity of the adrenal reticularis zone [30]. This may be driven by factors such as dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, hyperinsulinemia, or altered adrenal sensitivity to adrenocorticotropic hormone [31, 32]. The prevalence of elevated DHEAS was higher in women with Rotterdam phenotypes B (hyperandrogenism + ovulatory dysfunction) and C (hyperandrogenism + polycystic ovarian morphology), suggesting distinct pathophysiological subtypes [30]. The liver-ovary axis has also been implicated, with hepatic factors, such as Krüppel-like Factor 9 deficiency being shown to modulate DHEA-induced PMOS/PCOS features in animal models, indicating extra-gonadal regulation of adrenal-androgen-driven pathology [33].

Altered peripheral metabolism

Increased activity of 5α-reductase (types 1 and 2) in skin, hair follicles, and adipose tissue amplifies the conversion of testosterone to the more potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT), driving hirsutism and acne [34]. Conversely, reduced aromatase (Cytochrome P450 Family 19 Subfamily A Member 1[CYP19A1]) activity in adipose tissue may lower the peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogens, although this finding is inconsistent [35]. The androsterone glucuronide to DHEAS ratio has been proposed as a discriminatory marker for women with obesity and PMOS/PCOS, reflecting altered peripheral androgen metabolism [36].

Emerging contributors

Recent research implicates dysregulated adipose tissue-derived factors (adipokines), chronic low-grade inflammation, and gut microbiota alterations in modulating insulin sensitivity and steroidogenesis, further complicating the hyperandrogenism network [37–39]. For example, chemerin acts via CMKLR1 to impair insulin signaling, mediate androgen effects, suppress progesterone, and promote granulosa cell autophagy/inflammation, contributing to PMOS/PCOS ovarian dysfunction [39]. The cross-talk between adipocytes and steroidogenic cells via adipokines and free fatty acids can directly influence ovarian function [37].

Indications for anti-androgen therapy

Given the clinical heterogeneity of PMOS/PCOS, anti-androgen therapy should not be applied as a one-size-fits-all strategy but rather should be strictly restricted to phenotypes with clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism [8, 40–42]. According to the Rotterdam criteria, PMOS/PCOS can be classified into four phenotypes, among which phenotype D lacks the pathological basis for androgen excess. Therefore, the use of anti-androgen agents should be explicitly limited to hyperandrogenic phenotypes [8, 40–42]. For the phenotype D, routine anti-androgen therapy is not recommended unless the patient has clearly documented clinical signs of androgen excess, such as hirsutism, acne, or androgenetic alopecia [8, 42]. Goals of anti-androgen therapy

Anti-androgen therapy exerts its effects mainly by suppressing ovarian or adrenal androgen synthesis, increasing sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) levels, or blocking androgen binding to its receptor. It should be clearly noted that such treatment does not directly correct the upstream endocrine-metabolic drivers of PMOS/PCOS, including insulin resistance, hyperinsulinaemia, visceral adiposity, low-grade chronic inflammation, dysregulated SHBG production, or increased cardiometabolic risk [10, 43, 44]. The therapeutic role encompasses the following three aspects.

Symptom control

Significant improvement of androgen-mediated clinical manifestations such as hirsutism, acne, androgenetic alopecia, and menstrual cycle irregularities.

Auxiliary role in metabolic risk management

Anti-androgen therapy itself does not directly improve insulin resistance, but when combined with lifestyle intervention and insulin sensitizers, it may synergistically improve metabolic parameters. It should not be mistaken as first-line treatment for metabolic abnormalities [41].

Intervention on pathological links in hyperandrogenic phenotypes

In patients with hyperandrogenic phenotypes, anti-androgen therapy can partially block the adverse effects of androgens on follicular development, insulin sensitivity, and lipid metabolism. However, this represents a phenotype-specific pathological intervention, not a universal etiological treatment for all PMOS/PCOS patients.

Efficacy of anti-androgen pharmacotherapy

COCPs: first-line standard

COCPs are the cornerstone of pharmacological management for hyperandrogenism in PMOS/PCOS (Table 3) [8, 41, 46]. Before prescribing combined oral contraceptives, an individualized assessment of cardiometabolic and thromboembolic risks must be performed, taking into account factors such as obesity, age, smoking, hypertension, migraine with aura, previous venous thromboembolism, thrombophilia, dyslipidemia, diabetes risk, family history, and prolonged immobilization [41, 47–49]. Based on current guidelines and consensus, we propose the following risk stratification: low-risk patients (without major risk factors) may use COCPs; intermediate-risk patients (e.g., mild obesity, controlled hypertension, smokers aged < 35 years, dyslipidemia, or diabetes risk) should use COCPs with caution, prioritizing lower-risk formulations (e.g., lower estrogen dose, non-androgenic progestins) and monitoring metabolic parameters; high-risk patients (e.g., previous venous thromboembolism, known thrombophilia, heavy smokers aged > 35 years, migraine with aura, uncontrolled hypertension, or multiple metabolic risk factors) should avoid any estrogen-containing contraceptive method and instead consider cyclic progestins for endometrial protection, dermatological treatment, metformin, lifestyle intervention, or specialist-guided anti-androgens combined with reliable non-hormonal contraception. The anti-androgenic efficacy of COCPs is multimodal and well established.

Statins

(e.g., Atorvastatin)

1. Indirect Anti-Androgen Effect: Inhibits cholesterol synthesis, reducing substrate for androgen production.

2. Primary Role: Management of concomitant dyslipidemia; androgen reduction is a secondary benefit.

Positioning: Not a primary anti-androgen therapy.

Recommendation: Indicated for managing dyslipidemia per cardiovascular risk guidelines; anti-androgen effect is an adjunct benefit.

Suppression of ovarian androgen production

The estrogen component (typically ethinylestradiol) provides negative feedback on the pituitary gland [50], suppressing LH secretion and thereby reducing LH-induced theca cell androgen synthesis [51].

Increased SHBG

Estrogens stimulate the hepatic synthesis of SHBG, which binds to circulating testosterone and reduces the free bioavailable fraction [52]. A meta-analysis confirmed that COCPs increase SHBG by 100–200% and reduce free testosterone levels by 40–70%, depending on the formulation [53]. An increase in SHBG levels is the primary mechanism underlying its anti-androgenic effect.

Suppression of adrenal androgens

Some data suggest a mild suppressive effect on adrenal androgen production, although less pronounced than ovarian suppression [41].

Direct anti-androgenic progestins

Certain progestins, such as cyproterone acetate (CPA), drospirenone, and dienogest, have inherent anti-androgenic properties [54]. CPA is a potent AR antagonist with anti-mineralocorticoid and anti-androgenic activities, whereas dienogest also possesses anti-androgenic effects through receptor antagonism [54]. This provides additional benefits beyond elevating SHBG levels.

High-quality evidence, including a systematic review and meta-analysis by Alesi et al. (2023) encompassing numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs), confirmed that COCPs effectively improve clinical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism and acne) and regulate menstrual cycles [41]. Evidence shows no superior efficacy of specific anti-androgen drugs over COCPs for general hyperandrogenemia management, reinforcing their role as primary agents. The choice of progestin can be tailored; COCPs containing CPA (2 mg) or drospirenone (3 mg) may offer slightly greater anti-androgenic effects on hirsutism than levonorgestrel [55]. COCPs suppress ovarian androgens but also reduce endogenous estradiol production and replace it with ethinylestradiol, which has different metabolic and hepatic effects [56].

Spironolactone: second-line androgen receptor antagonism

Spironolactone, a potassium-sparing diuretic, is a competitive antagonist of the AR and weakly inhibits ovarian and adrenal steroidogenesis [57]. It is a well-established second-line agent, particularly for hirsutism [41, 58].

Efficacy for hirsutism

Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses have demonstrated that spironolactone (typically at doses of 50–200 mg/day) significantly reduces Ferriman–Gallwey (FG) hirsutism scores compared with a placebo [59]. A systematic review by Bashir et al. (2023) highlighted its pleiotropic effects, noting improvements beyond just androgenic symptoms [58]. Its effect is often comparable or slightly superior to that of metformin alone and synergistic when combined with lifestyle interventions [41, 58]. The reduction in FG scores typically ranges from 20 to 40% after monotherapy.

Efficacy for acne

Spironolactone is also effective in treating moderate-to-severe acne vulgaris in women with PMOS/PCOS and idiopathic acne, with studies showing significant reductions in inflammatory and non-inflammatory lesion counts [60]. The doses for acne (50–100 mg/day) are often lower than those used for hirsutism [61, 62].

Efficacy for female patterned hair loss (FPHL)

Spironolactone has demonstrated favorable efficacy in the treatment of FPHL [63]. Some studies have reported a relatively high response rate, with approximately 66.1% of patients achieving a complete response; this effect was observed across hair loss subtypes of varying severity [64]. However, specific data on populations with PMOS/PCOS are limited.

Onset and duration of effect

Clinical improvement in hirsutism is typically observed after 3–6 months of therapy, with maximal effects requiring up to 12–18 months or longer owing to the long hair growth cycle [65]. Hair growth returned to baseline levels upon discontinuation of treatment, necessitating long-term maintenance therapy for symptom control [66]. This underscores the importance of chronic PMOS/PCOS management. For acne, spironolactone usually begins to work within 3 months and achieves optimal results by 5 months [67], with benefits continuing for a few months post-treatment [68].

Therapeutic niche

Its established role is in patients with contraindications, intolerance, or an inadequate response to COCPs, particularly in moderate-to-severe hirsutism, where a direct AR blockade at the pilosebaceous unit is especially valuable [69]. It is also the preferred option for women who cannot take or prefer not to take estrogen-containing pills.

Other anti-androgen agents

CPA

CPA is a potent progestin with strong AR antagonistic properties. It is commonly used in a reverse sequential regimen (e.g., CPA 50–100 mg/day for the first 10–15 days of a cycle combined with ethinyl estradiol for 21 days) or in low-dose (2 mg/day) in combination with COCPs [54]. It is highly effective for severe hirsutism but is often considered second-line owing to its less favorable metabolic profile and limited availability in some countries, such as the USA [65]. High-dose CPA (50–100 mg/day) is generally reserved for short-term use (e.g., three to six cycles) owing to its side effects.

5α-reductase inhibitors (Finasteride and dutasteride)

These agents block the conversion of testosterone to DHT. Finasteride (type II inhibitor, 2.5–5 mg/day) shows efficacy similar to spironolactone and CPA for hirsutism [70]. Dutasteride (type I and II inhibitor) may be more potent. However, owing to the significant risk of teratogenicity (potential for male fetal genital abnormalities if exposed during pregnancy) and the lack of large-scale RCTs demonstrating superiority over older agents, they are generally reserved as third-line options for women with PMOS/PCOS adhering to strict contraception [71, 72]. They may be effective in treating FPHL [73], which is common in PMOS/PCOS. Evidence supports that topical combination therapy with 0.25% finasteride and 3% minoxidil demonstrates favorable efficacy and safety in the treatment of FPHL [74].

Flutamide

A nonsteroidal AR antagonist. Although effective for hirsutism, its association with rare, severe, and sometimes fatal hepatotoxicity has drastically limited its use in PMOS/PCOS, and it is not recommended in most contemporary guidelines [75]. Its risk-benefit profile is considered unfavorable for chronic, non-life-threatening conditions such as PMOS/PCOS.

Statins: indirect modulation via lipid pathways

By inhibiting 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase, statins reduce the availability of cholesterol, a substrate for steroidogenesis. In women with PMOS/PCOS, atorvastatin (20 mg/day) has been shown in RCTs and a Cochrane review to be associated with a secondary reduction in total testosterone and the free androgen index independently of lipid changes, an effect that may be particularly relevant for those with concurrent dyslipidemia [76]. This finding underscores the intimate link between lipid metabolism and androgen production [27, 33], potentially involving reduced cholesterol substrate for StAR protein-mediated mitochondrial transport [77]. However, statins are not indicated as primary anti-androgen therapy; they are primarily lipid-lowering agents reserved for managing dyslipidemia according to cardiovascular risk guidelines. Their role in PMOS/PCOS is therefore primarily metabolic, with any beneficial effect on androgen levels being secondary and possible [76].

Safety and tolerability profile

A nuanced understanding of the safety profiles of these agents is critical for risk-benefit assessments and patient counseling. The choice of therapy must balance efficacy with potential metabolic, reproductive, and general health risks (Table 4).

Metabolic effects

COCPs

The metabolic impact of COCPs varies with estrogen dose and progestin type. Ethinylestradiol can increase the hepatic production of triglycerides and angiotensinogen, while decreasing insulin sensitivity [78]. The progestin type determines effects; androgenic progestins (e.g., levonorgestrel and norethindrone) may worsen lipid profiles (decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], increased low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]) and IR, whereas anti-androgenic (CPA and drospirenone) or neutral progestins (desogestrel and norgestimate) have neutral or beneficial effects [49, 79]. A recent study by Luque-Ramirez et al. (2025) using advanced lipidomics demonstrated that although women with PMOS/PCOS have an atherogenic lipid profile, common antiandrogenic treatments such as COCPs and spironolactone can worsen specific lipoprotein subclasses, highlighting the complexity of assessing metabolic impact [79]. Overall, the absolute increase in venous thromboembolism (VTE) exists but the risk is small (approximately 2–9 per 10,000 women) [80]. The long-term impact of diabetes risk in PMOS/PCOS remains controversial, with studies reporting conflicting results. The benefits of cycle control, endometrial protection, and hyperandrogenism management generally outweigh these risks in healthy, nonsmoking women aged < 35 years [81].

Spironolactone

It generally has a neutral or potentially beneficial effect on glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity, owing to its mild diuretic effect and impact on mineralocorticoid receptors [62]. Its effects on lipids are mixed, with minor increases in high-density lipoprotein or triglycerides reported, but these changes are often clinically insignificant [62]. Its primary metabolic concern is its anti-mineralocorticoid properties, which can cause hyperkalemia. Periodic monitoring of serum potassium (e.g., at baseline, 1 month after initiation or dose change, and annually) and renal function (e.g., serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate) is recommended for spironolactone, particularly in patients with renal impairment, diabetes, heart failure, or those on other medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [28, 58]. For agents with hepatotoxicity concerns, regular monitoring of liver function is advised [47, 58].

CPA (high-dose)

Associated with more significant adverse lipid changes (marked increase in triglycerides and LDL-C, and decrease in HDL-C) and a greater negative impact on glucose metabolism than other anti-androgens or low-dose COCPs, limiting its long-term use for metabolic reasons [79, 82]. This has been observed in both PMOS/PCOS and transgender medicine contexts [83].

Combination mitigation

Some evidence from studies on transgender women suggests that combining spironolactone with a COCPs may mitigate the unfavorable triglyceride elevations sometimes observed with CPA monotherapy, presenting a rational strategy for selected patients [84].

Reproductive and endometrial safety

Endometrial protection

COCPs provide excellent endometrial protection by inducing regular, progestin-mediated secretory transformation and withdrawal bleeding, thereby mitigating the long-term risk of endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma associated with chronic anovulation and unopposed estrogen [85, 86]. This is a critical non-contraceptive benefit for amenorrheic women with amenorrhea and PMOS/PCOS.

Spironolactone and endometrium

Spironolactone alone does not induce ovulation or promote regular endometrial shedding. By disrupting the HPO axis through its anti-androgenic effects [87], it may cause menstrual irregularity and, in anovulatory patients, potentially increase the risk of endometrial hyperplasia. There is no evidence that anti-androgen therapy raises the risk of endometrial cancer; indeed, it may lower this risk by supporting endometrial protection [47].

General adverse effects and contraindications

COCPs

Common side effects include breakthrough bleeding (particularly in the first few months of use), nausea, breast tenderness, and mood changes. Absolute contraindications include a history of VTE, high-risk thrombophilia (e.g., Factor V Leiden, Protein C deficiency. and/or Protein S deficiency), migraine with aura, active liver disease, and uncontrolled hypertension. Relative contraindications include smoking in women aged > 35 years, obesity (body mass index [BMI] > 35), hypertriglyceridemia, and prolonged immobilization [41, 48, 49]. A meta-analysis by Gariani et al. (2020) confirmed that PMOS/PCOS is associated with a 1.5–2-fold increased risk of VTE, which must be considered when prescribing COCPs [48].

Spironolactone

Common side effects include menstrual irregularity, breast tenderness, fatigue, headache, and diuresis (particularly early in therapy) [58]. The drug is contraindicated in pregnancy (Pregnancy Category C/D depending on the region, owing to anti-androgen effects on male fetal development, specifically the risk of feminization) and in patients with renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min), hyperkalemia, or Addison’s disease [88]. Therefore, effective contraception is essential. Interactions between this drug and other potassium-elevating agents must be reviewed [58].

Therapeutic choice

The decision between a COCP and spironolactone often depends on the predominant symptoms (COCPs are more effective for acne and cycle regulation, whereas spironolactone may be better for moderate-to-severe hirsutism), the need for contraception, patient comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, migraine), and individual tolerance [69]. Shared decision-making is paramount.

Integrated management strategies

PMOS/PCOS treatment rarely involves the use of a single drug. A comprehensive, multimodal approach targeting multiple pathophysiological pathways yields the best outcomes. This approach is particularly effective given the chronic nature of the condition.

Guideline-supported therapies

Regardless of pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modification, including a structured diet with moderate caloric restriction and improved nutritional quality, combined with regular physical activity ≥ 150 min per week at moderate intensity, is the cornerstone of first-line management [46, 89]. Lifestyle modification has been proven to improve IR and aid weight loss, with even a modest 5–10% loss in weight being able to restore ovulation, reduce androgen levels, and improve cardiometabolic risk factors [90–92]. A Cochrane review confirmed the benefits of lifestyle changes for patients with PMOS/PCOS [92]. Lifestyle interventions synergize with metformin and anti-androgen treatments to optimize metabolic and reproductive parameters [93, 94]. Behavioral modification programs have shown significant success in improving menstrual function [91].

Off-label but clinically used therapies

Metformin + Anti-androgen

This is a rational, evidence-based combination regimen for patients with the metabolic phenotype of hirsutism. Insulin resistance represents an upstream driver of hyperandrogenemia. Therefore, in patients with PMOS/PCOS exhibiting both hyperandrogenic and metabolic phenotypes, antiandrogen therapy—while an important symptomatic measure—cannot replace systematic intervention targeting the upstream metabolic disturbances [43, 44]. The two modalities should not be viewed as being in simple “alternative” or “primary adjunctive” relationship; rather, they should be structured into a well-stratified, synergistic, and complementary integrated treatment pathway [95]. This approach is also an inherent requirement for achieving long-term dual improvements in both metabolic health and reproductive function in PMOS/PCOS patients [96]. Evidence supports the combination of metformin with spironolactone for superior androgen-lowering and menstrual outcomes compared to monotherapy [41, 62, 96]. This regimen is suitable for patients not requiring contraception (with separate endometrial protection), and metformin has been ranked as the most effective oral agent for endocrine–metabolic improvement in PMOS/PCOS [97].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs)

GLP-1 RAs, primarily approved for type 2 diabetes and obesity, are increasingly used off-label in clinical practice for PMOS/PCOS patients, particularly those with obesity [98]. They have been reported to significantly reduce androgen levels and improve ovulation rates, providing a potent metabolic and anti-androgenic strategy for obese phenotypes [99]. While promising, their use in PMOS/PCOS remains off-label, and long-term safety/efficacy data specific to PMOS/PCOS are still accumulating.

Investigational or preclinical approaches

The following approaches are at various stages of investigation. Their inclusion in this manuscript is for illustrative purposes regarding potential future directions. They should not be interpreted as current clinical recommendations.

Nutraceuticals and phytochemicals

Co-administration of metformin and curcumin showed additive benefits on metabolic parameters (Homeostatic Model Assessment of IR [HOMA-IR] and lipids) and hormonal parameters (testosterone) in a factorial RCT, suggesting potential complementary mechanisms such as anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [100]. This aligns with the concept of using natural compounds to modulate pathways such as PI3K/AKT to protect granulosa cells from hyperandrogen-induced stress [20]. However, these findings are preliminary, and curcumin is not currently recommended for routine clinical use in PMOS/PCOS.

Novel pharmacological targets

Artemisinin derivatives, particularly artemisinin, have demonstrated significant promise in preclinical models by targeting the mitochondrial protease Lon protease 1 (LonP1), leading to the degradation of cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1; a key enzyme in steroidogenesis) and a substantial reduction in androgen production [101]. This represents a novel mechanism of action. No clinical data are yet available to support its use in patients.

Integrative approaches: traditional chinese medicine (TCM) and herb-drug combinations

Growing research interest exists in integrating TCM principles with conventional anti-androgen therapy, but this approach remains exploratory and lacks guideline support. TCM views PMOS/PCOS through patterns such as “Liver Qi Stagnation,” “Phlegm-Dampness,” and “Kidney Deficiency”; however, the evidence for many herbal interventions remains limited owing to small sample sizes, lack of standardization, and an absence of high-quality randomized controlled trials [102, 103]. Importantly, potential herb-drug interactions may occur in patients using combined oral contraceptives, spironolactone, finasteride, metformin, GLP-1 receptor agonists, or fertility medications [102]. Thus, with safety being paramount, patients must be counseled against the unsupervised use of herbal remedies, especially because of the teratogenic risk of some anti-androgens and the unknown effects of many herbs on drug metabolism and pregnancy.

Caveat

Robust evidence from large-scale, long-term randomized controlled trials is required before any of the above investigational approaches can be widely adopted in clinical practice [104]. Herbal medicines often lack standardized and rigorous safety data [104].

Management across the lifespan and phenotypes

Adolescence

The diagnosis of PMOS/PCOS in adolescents should adopt stricter criteria than those used in adults. It must meet the following requirements: persistent menstrual irregularities (with definitions specific to years since menarche) and ovulatory dysfunction; clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism; and exclusion of other disorders with similar presentations [81, 105, 106]. Pelvic ultrasound and anti-Müllerian hormone should not be used for diagnosis in adolescents.

Regarding anti-androgen therapy, treatment should be symptom-driven and avoid premature disease labeling [81]. Given that the overall certainty of evidence for treatment in adolescents is low to moderate, all treatment recommendations should be developed through adequate communication and shared decision-making with the patients and their guardians, and the benefits, potential risks, and adverse effects should be fully explained before treatment initiation [107]. Indications for treatment include severe persistent acne, progressive hirsutism, and other distressing hyperandrogenic symptoms. Combined oral contraceptives are the first-line choice for managing irregular menstrual cycles and clinical hyperandrogenic manifestations (e.g., acne, hirsutism) [108]. Low-dose formulations (containing 20 µg to 30 µg ethinylestradiol) are generally preferred, whereas metformin is better reserved for those with significant metabolic disturbances (e.g., obesity, insulin resistance, or dysglycemia) [95, 109]. For moderate-to-severe, refractory, or standard topical treatment-resistant acne, as well as progressive hirsutism, combination therapy with COCPs and/or topical agents is a reasonable first-line option [95, 109]. Cosmetic procedures such as laser hair removal may be used to improve appearance and quality of life under the guidance of qualified and experienced professionals [95, 109]. The treatment goals should not only focus on symptom relief but also address diagnostic uncertainty, psychological well-being, and long-term metabolic follow-up [110].

Reproductive age (Without immediate desire for pregnancy)

The goals include symptom control, cycle regulation, and the prevention of long-term sequelae, including endometrial hyperplasia and metabolic deterioration. Therefore, COCPs are ideal for contraceptive use. For women who do not require contraception (e.g., those using barrier methods), anti-androgens, such as spironolactone, must be paired with reliable contraception and a plan for endometrial protection. A planned approach is required when transitioning to fertility-focused therapy, such as discontinuing spironolactone 2–3 months before attempting conception because of its teratogenic risk [2]. Preconception counseling that focuses on weight optimization and metabolic health is crucial.

Preconceptional women

Hyperandrogenemia in women with PMSO/PCOS is associated with potential adverse effects on oocyte quality, endometrial receptivity, and pregnancy complications. However, this effect is not independent; its magnitude and mechanistic pathways are significantly modulated by multiple factors, including PMSO/PCOS phenotype, obesity, IR, and the type of fertility treatment [111–114]. Based on this, relevant international guidelines suggest that for anovulatory infertile PMSO/PCOS women with overt biochemical hyperandrogenemia or significant clinical hyperandrogenism, a 3- to 6-month course of a COCP may be considered before initiating ovulation induction, with the aim of improving the endocrine environment [8]. It must be emphasized that this is a conditional recommendation, primarily intended to correct the endocrine disturbances associated with hyperandrogenism. Currently, there is a lack of sufficient high-quality direct evidence to demonstrate that preconception COCPs pretreatment can definitively increase the clinical pregnancy rate, reduce the miscarriage rate, or decrease the risk of pregnancy complications. The key to improving these long-term outcomes remains sustained combined lifestyle modifications and metabolic intervention throughout the entire care process. Furthermore, anti-androgen medications are absolutely contraindicated during pregnancy due to their potential teratogenic risk.

Obesity and metabolic dysregulation

Obesity, which affects 40–80% of women with PMOS/PCOS, amplifies both IR and hyperandrogenism [115]. Visceral adiposity is detrimental to human health. Management must be aggressive and stepwise: (1) Intensive lifestyle intervention is a non-negotiable foundation and often requires structured programs to achieve success. (2) Pharmacotherapy should favor or include insulin sensitizers (e.g., metformin) because of their comprehensive metabolic benefits. GLP-1 RAs are powerful options for significant weight loss and metabolic improvement and are often superior to metformin alone for weight reduction [116]. (3) Anti-androgen selection should consider the metabolic profile; spironolactone may be metabolically preferable to high-dose CPA. Close monitoring of weight, glycemic parameters (fasting glucose and HbA1c levels), and lipid levels is essential [36]. The pathogenesis of obesity-related PMOS/PCOS involves adipose tissue hypoxia, inflammation, and altered adipokine secretion, all of which represent therapeutic targets [115].

Unresolved questions and future directions

Long-term cardiovascular safety

While PMOS/PCOS is associated with an elevated cardiometabolic risk profile, including endothelial dysfunction, increased carotid intima-media thickness, and a higher incidence of coronary artery disease [117, 118], the independent contribution of anti-androgen therapies to long-term cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk remains unclear and is a major knowledge gap. Data extrapolated from androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) for prostate cancer suggest potential adverse effects, including increased risks of metabolic syndrome, IR, and possibly cardiovascular events [119]. However, the context differs; ADT causes profound androgen deficiency in men, whereas anti-androgen therapy in PMOS/PCOS aims to normalize, not ablate, androgen levels in women. Furthermore, the baseline cardiometabolic risk of PMOS/PCOS is high. Does long-term use of COCPs or AR antagonists have a net protective effect––by improving metabolic parameters through weight and androgen control––or detrimental effects––through prothrombotic or proinflammatory effects––on atherosclerosis and CVD events? Large, long-term prospective cohort studies and registry data linking treatment histories to hard CVD endpoints are urgently required. The androgen excess and PMOS/PCOS society consensus statement highlighted this need over a decade ago, and it remains unmet.

Duration of therapy and post-treatment course

No consensus exists on the optimal treatment duration. Most RCTs provided short-term evidence (< 12 months); however, PMOS/PCOS is a lifelong condition. Key unanswered questions include the following: What is the rate and severity of symptom recurrence (hirsutism and acne) after discontinuation? Is there a “remission” period after long-term therapy? What is the appropriate maintenance dose for long-term therapy? What is the long-term risk-benefit ratio of prolonged treatment spanning decades, particularly concerning bone health in adolescents (with anti-androgens) and metabolic effects or breast cancer risk in older women (with COCPs)? Clinical practice is often guided by patient symptoms and preferences; however, evidence-based guidelines for treatment de-escalation or discontinuation are lacking.

The quest for precision medicine

The heterogeneity of PMOS/PCOS presents a major therapeutic barrier. The correlation between biochemical androgen levels and clinical features is imperfect; some women with marked hirsutism have normal serum androgen levels, whereas others with high levels have few symptoms [120, 121]. This suggests that differences exist in tissue-specific AR sensitivity and peripheral metabolism. A significant challenge is the absence of validated biomarkers for predicting individual responses to specific anti-androgen therapies [122]. Why do some women respond well to spironolactone while others do not? Advances in omics technologies (genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics) and deep phenotyping are promising approaches for future patient stratification, identification of therapeutic targets, and personalized therapy selection [33, 43]. Furthermore, the role of novel agents, such as topical AR antagonists (for localized hirsutism or acne), more selective androgen signaling modulators (selective androgen receptor modulators in reverse), and agents targeting specific inflammatory pathways in PMOS/PCOS, warrants exploration.

Clinical recommendations: a stratified, phenotype-guided framework

Effective management requires moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach. The following framework integrates current evidence and acknowledges the need for individualized care, stratified by distinct clinical phenotypes.

Hyperandrogenic PMOS/PCOS (without major metabolic disturbances)

In reproductive-aged women with clinically relevant hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, or androgenetic alopecia) and no significant metabolic abnormalities, anti-androgen therapy is central [41]. First-line therapy is a COCP containing low-androgenic or anti-androgenic progestins (e.g., drospirenone, dienogest, cyproterone acetate [2 mg], or desogestrel), preferred for managing both hyperandrogenism and cycle control [123]. VTE risk should be assessed before prescription. For persistent hirsutism or acne unresponsive to COCPs after 6–9 months, or when COCPs are contraindicated, second-line therapy with spironolactone should be added starting at 50–100 mg/day and titrated to 100–200 mg/day as needed and tolerated, along with effective contraception. In anovulatory women, endometrial protection should be provided using cyclic progestin therapy.

Metabolic and hyperandrogenic PMOS/PCOS

In patients who are overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 25), have significant insulin resistance (e.g., HOMA-IR > 2.5), or metabolic syndrome, anti-androgen therapy should be combined with lifestyle intervention, insulin-sensitizing therapy, and cardiometabolic prevention [45]. Core measures include intensive lifestyle modification together with metformin (500–2000 mg/day). For patients with obesity (BMI ≥ 30, or ≥ 27 with comorbidity) struggling with weight loss, adding a GLP-1 receptor agonist should be considered [45]. Spironolactone may provide additional symptomatic and hormonal benefits but should be used as an adjunct to––not a replacement for––metabolic-focused interventions [124]. If contraception or cycle control is desired, a low- or anti-androgenic COCP can be used, with careful monitoring of metabolic and VTE risk.

Phenotype D / MFOD (normoandrogenic, without significant dermatologic manifestations)

In women with normal androgen levels and without relevant hirsutism or acne, anti-androgens have limited rationale. Management focuses on cycle regulation and metabolic health. Cycle regulation can be achieved with cyclic progestin or a low-dose COCP (for those needing contraception). Metabolic health is addressed through lifestyle advice and metformin if metabolic abnormalities are present. Anti-androgens (e.g., spironolactone) are only indicated when specific androgen-related dermatological symptoms (e.g., refractory acne or seborrhea) are documented, despite normoandrogenemia [5, 43, 125].

Integrative and adjunctive considerations (All phenotypes)

Evidence-based nutraceuticals (e.g., myo-inositol) may be considered as adjuncts, particularly for metabolic support [126]. When patients express interest in complementary approaches such as traditional Chinese medicine, it is important to consult a qualified practitioner, for patients to disclose all conventional medications to avoid interactions, and to prioritize evidence-based conventional therapy as the foundation. Adjunctive therapies should not replace first- or second-line pharmacological treatments without robust evidence or specialist guidance.

Special populations

For adolescents, lifestyle and low-dose COCPs should be prioritized; anti-androgens such as spironolactone may be used with caution when clinically indicated (typically in hyperandrogenic or metabolic and hyperandrogenic phenotypes with severe symptoms), and patient education should be emphasized [81]. For women planning pregnancy, teratogenic anti-androgens (spironolactone and finasteride) should be discontinued 2–3 months before conception; COCPs can be used for cycle control until readiness, then transition to ovulation induction agents (e.g., letrozole); in selected cases, metformin may be continued into pregnancy (e.g., for diabetes prevention) [124]. For normoandrogenic women or those with mild hyperandrogenism not meeting phenotype I or II criteria, management follows the Phenotype D framework, and anti-androgens are not routinely used unless specific dermatologic symptoms are present.

Monitoring and follow-up

A structured monitoring plan should be established [8]. Metabolic parameters (fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipid profile, blood pressure, and weight/BMI) should be measured at baseline and at least annually. Hormonal and clinical response (FG score, acne assessment, and menstrual diary) should be assessed every 3–6 months initially. Drug-specific safety checks include serum potassium at baseline and 1 month after spironolactone initiation or dose change, then annually, as well as regular reassessment of VTE risk factors in COCP users. Psychological well-being should be assessed routinely.

Multidisciplinary care

Effective PMOS/PCOS management requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach involving endocrinologists, gynecologists, dermatologists, dietitians, exercise physiologists, and mental health professionals to address the multifaceted nature of PMOS/PCOS and provide sustained, patient-centered care across the lifespan [8].

Conclusion

In hyperandrogenic phenotypes of PMOS/PCOS, hyperandrogenemia drives the expression of reproductive, metabolic, and psychological features. Lowering androgen levels helps break the vicious cycle involving ovarian dysfunction, insulin resistance, and chronic inflammation. For non-pregnant patients without contraindications, COCPs are first line anti androgen therapy. For moderate to severe hirsutism, or when COCPs are not tolerated or contraindicated, spironolactone can be used as second line, with attention to contraception and electrolyte monitoring.

PMOS/PCOS is clinically heterogeneous. Its management should be patient-centered, phenotype driven, and dynamically adjusted according to life stage. In non-hyperandrogenic phenotypes, routine anti androgen therapy is not indicated; management should focus on phenotype specific metabolic and reproductive strategies. For patients with obesity and insulin resistance, regardless of androgen status, lifestyle intervention combined with metformin forms the foundation of metabolic management. Anti androgens are added only when clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism is present. Anti-androgen therapy plays an important role in the comprehensive management of hyperandrogenic PMOS/PCOS phenotypes. It improves symptoms such as hirsutism and acne as well as potential metabolic risks, while attention should be paid to adverse effects and teratogenicity. Therefore, clinical indications should be strictly followed, with appropriate drug selection and regular monitoring. In contrast, other phenotypes require tailored metabolic and reproductive interventions. The combination of pharmacotherapy with lifestyle modification, multidisciplinary care, and individualized follow up comprehensively improves patients’ health status and quality of life. Future research should address the long-term cardiovascular safety of anti-androgen therapy, optimal treatment duration, and biomarker guided precision strategies. Novel drug targets and integrated care models are promising.
