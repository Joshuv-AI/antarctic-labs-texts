The lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio predicts frequent exacerbations in COPD patients with coronary heart disease
Authors: HongXu Zhang, MengYu Lian, Jie Song, JingJing Yang, RuiYan Lin, JinXiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and coronary heart disease (CHD) frequently exhibit heightened systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and cardiopulmonary dysfunction, contributing to an increased risk of recurrent exacerbations. The lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio (LAR) is a readily available biomarker reflecting inflammatory and metabolic stress; however, its association with the frequent-exacerbator phenotype in patients with COPD and CHD remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between admission LAR levels and frequent exacerbations in this high-risk population.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 489 hospitalized patients with acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) and comorbid CHD. Patients with two or more exacerbations were classified as frequent exacerbators. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and lab parameters were collected. Multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to evaluate the association between LAR and the frequent-exacerbator phenotype.ResultsA total of 489 patients with AECOPD and CHD were included, with a mean age of 76 years, and 66.9% were male. Higher admission LAR levels were significantly associated with the frequent-exacerbator phenotype in the unadjusted analysis (Odds Ratio [OR] 1.23; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.12 ~ 1.35; p < 0.001). The association remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders (adjusted OR 1.34; 95% CI 1.19 ~ 1.51; p < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a significant nonlinear association (p = 0.004), with an inflection point at a LAR value of approximately 5.1 (OR 4.04, p = 0.001), whereas a more modest increase was observed above this threshold (OR 1.45, p = 0.009).ConclusionHigher admission LAR levels were independently associated with the frequent-exacerbator phenotype among patients with AECOPD and CHD. This readily available biomarker may improve risk stratification and personalized management in this high-morbidity population.Keywords: AECOPD, Coronary heart disease, Lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio, Frequent exacerbations, Biomarker, Inflammation

Introduction

Acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) are pivotal in COPD management and represent a major contributor to the global clinical and economic burden of the disease [1]. AECOPD substantially impairs patients' quality of life, accelerates disease progression, causes an often irreversible decline in lung function [2–4], increases hospitalization and readmission rates [5], and is associated with significantly increased mortality [6]. Patients experiencing frequent exacerbations (≥ 2/year) represent a distinct clinical phenotype characterized by greater healthcare utilization, increased comorbidity burden, and poorer prognosis [7, 8]. Therefore, early identification of individuals at high risk for recurrent exacerbations remains an important clinical objective.

Individuals with COPD experience a high incidence of death due to cardiovascular issues [9]. Among cardiovascular comorbidities, coronary heart disease (CHD) is particularly prevalent in patients with COPD, with affected individuals exhibiting a substantially higher risk than the general population [10, 11]. Increasing evidence suggests that COPD and CHD are linked through shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, hypoxemia, endothelial dysfunction, genetic susceptibility, and physical inactivity [12, 13]. Chronic pulmonary inflammation induced by cigarette smoke exposure, air pollution, and recurrent infections may extend beyond the lungs, resulting in systemic vascular injury and accelerated atherosclerosis. Simultaneously, cardiovascular dysfunction may aggravate tissue hypoperfusion, impair oxygen delivery, and further worsen respiratory symptoms and exacerbation risk. This bidirectional interaction, often referred to as the cardiovascular–pulmonary axis, is increasingly recognized as an important mechanistic and therapeutic target in patients with COPD and cardiovascular comorbidities. Consistent with this concept, the risk of severe cardiovascular events or death increases following the onset of moderate or severe COPD exacerbation [14].

Given the central role of systemic inflammation, hypoxemia, and tissue injury in the cardiovascular–pulmonary axis, biomarkers capable of integrating these pathophysiological processes may provide valuable prognostic information in patients with COPD and CHD. The lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio (LAR) integrates two complementary physiological signals: elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which reflects tissue injury and metabolic stress during hypoxic or inflammatory states [15, 16], and reduced albumin, which indicates systemic inflammatory activation and diminished physiological reserve [17]. As a composite biomarker, LAR may provide a more comprehensive assessment of inflammatory and metabolic stress than either parameter alone.

Recent studies have identified LAR as a prognostic biomarker in several acute illnesses, including sepsis [15], COVID-19 infection [18], and acute pulmonary embolism [19]. However, its potential utility in predicting the risk of frequent exacerbations in patients with concurrent COPD and CHD has not been investigated. Given the heightened inflammatory–metabolic vulnerability in this population, we hypothesized that LAR may serve as a more informative prognostic indicator than LDH or albumin alone for identifying patients at increased risk of recurrent exacerbations.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between admission LAR levels and the frequent-exacerbator phenotype in COPD patients with CHD, with the goal of informing improved clinical risk stratification in this high-morbidity group.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a study involving 489 participants who experienced acute exacerbations of COPD with CHD between June 2016 and February 2023. The inclusion criteria for patients were: a) a documented diagnosis of COPD in the medical records, confirmed by available historical post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) measurement below 0.7 during a clinically stable period; b) a primary diagnosis of severe AECOPD on admission, characterized by worsening dyspnea and/or cough and sputum occurring within 14 days [20]; c) a documented history of CHD. Patients were excluded if they: a) had recurrent admissions; b) had a hospital stay shorter than 24 h or died. Finally, 489 participants were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Those who had two or more exacerbations were considered frequent exacerbators [7]. Because LAR was obtained at admission during an AECOPD episode, this study evaluates the relationship between admission LAR levels and the established frequent-exacerbator phenotype. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Luhe Hospital (2020-LHKY-014–03), and informed consent was waived by the ethical review board. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data and sample collection

The following variables were collected from all participants: (1) demographic characteristics (gender, age, body mass index [BMI], smoking status); (2) hospitalization-related variables (history of pneumonia, length of stay [LOS]); (3) respiratory support and treatment variables, including long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT), home non-invasive ventilation (NIV), history of tracheostomy related to AECOPD, inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), long-acting β2 agonist (LABA), and long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA); (4) comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, CHD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), bronchiectasis, asthma, respiratory failure, pulmonary encephalopathy, lung cancer, cor pulmonale, reflux esophagitis; (5) laboratory parameters, including white blood cell (WBC) count, eosinophil (EOS) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), platelet (PLT) count, total protein (TP), albumin (Alb), creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), LDH, arterial blood pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), and lactic acid (LAC). The LAR was subsequently calculated as LDH divided by albumin for each participant.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas skewed distributed variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were shown as quantities and percentages. For continuous variables, the t-test and Mann–Whitney U-test were utilized to compare the two groups, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test and Fisher's exact test.

The relationship between LAR and frequent exacerbations was analyzed using a multivariable logistic regression model with sequential adjustments: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for gender, age, BMI; Model 3 was further adjusted for major comorbidities (pneumonia, hypertension, CHF, diabetes, CKD, bronchiectasis, asthma, RF, pulmonary encephalopathy, lung cancer, cor pulmonale, reflux esophagitis) and treatment-related variables (home NIV, ICS + LABA, and ICS + LABA + LAMA); Model 4 was additionally adjusted for laboratory variables including WBC, ALT, PH, and LAC.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed with knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of LAR to examine potential nonlinearity and assess the dose–response relationship between LAR and frequent exacerbators, while adjusting for variables in Model 4. Piecewise regression was used to identify inflection points.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to gender and key comorbidities, including hypertension, CHF, diabetes, RF, cor pulmonale, and reflux esophagitis. All statistical analyses were performed using R 3.3.2 (The R Foundation, Shanghai, China) and Free Statistics software version 1.5 [21]. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and general characteristics

Among the 489 patients with AECOPD and CHD included in the study, the mean age was 76 years, and 66.9% were male. Of these, 158 patients (32.3%) were classified as frequent exacerbators, while 331 patients (67.7%) were non-frequent exacerbators. The baseline clinical characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in demographic characteristics, most comorbidities, and laboratory parameters. However, frequent exacerbators had a higher proportion of LTOT (19 [12%] vs 21 [6.3%]; p = 0.032), Home NIV(17 [10.8%] vs 5 [1.5%]; p < 0.001), ICS + LABA(74 [46.8%] vs 100 [30.2%]; p < 0.001), and ICS + LABA + LAMA (55 [34.8%] vs 81 [24.5%]; p = 0.017) usage than the non-frequent exacerbators. In addition, frequent exacerbators had lower BMI (23.3 ± 3.7 vs 24.1 ± 3.6; p = 0.022) and ALT levels (13.0 [9.0, 19.8] vs 16.0 [11.0, 24.5]; p = 0.002), but higher levels of LDH (228.6 ± 84.6 vs 193.2 ± 64.1; p < 0.001), LAC (2.1 ± 1.1 vs 1.8 ± 1.3; p = 0.021), and LAR (6.4 ± 3.0 vs 5.3 ± 2.1; p < 0.001) compared to the non-frequent exacerbators.

The relationship between LAR and the frequent-exacerbator phenotype

Multivariate logistic regression analyses demonstrated a consistent association between higher LAR levels and the frequent-exacerbator phenotype (Table 2). For each unit increase in LAR, the odds ratios (ORs) for frequent exacerbations were 1.23 (95% CI 1.12–1.35, p < 0.001) in Model 1, 1.22 (95% CI 1.11–1.35, p < 0.001) in Model 2, 1.26 (95% CI 1.13–1.4, p < 0.001) in Model 3, and 1.34 (95% CI 1.19–1.51, p < 0.001) in Model 4, respectively.

RCS analysis

The association between LAR and frequent exacerbations displayed a nonlinear curve (p = 0.004) in the RCS (Fig. 2). In the threshold analysis, participants with LAR < 5.1 had a markedly higher risk of being frequent exacerbators (OR 4.04, 95% CI 1.68–9.72, p = 0.001), whereas those with LAR ≥ 5.1 showed a more modest increase in risk (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.09–1.92, p = 0.009) (Table 3). The slope of the association became less steep at higher LAR values, indicating that the incremental risk of frequent exacerbations diminishes above the threshold.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed according to gender and major comorbidities, including hypertension, CHF, RF, diabetes, cor pulmonale, and reflux esophagitis (Fig. 3). Overall, the association between LAR and the frequent-exacerbator phenotype remained generally consistent across most subgroups. However, significant interaction effects were observed for CHF and cor pulmonale (p < 0.05), suggesting that the strength of the association between LAR and the frequent-exacerbator phenotype may differ according to underlying cardiopulmonary conditions.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that LAR is independently associated with frequent exacerbations in COPD patients with CHD. Because LAR integrates metabolic stress and systemic inflammation, its elevation at hospital admission during an acute exacerbation may reflect a state of heightened physiological stress in individuals who subsequently demonstrate a higher risk of recurrent events.

COPD and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) often coexist due to shared risk factors, systemic inflammation, genetic susceptibility, and physical inactivity [9, 22]. Among cardiovascular comorbidities, CHD, stroke, and CHF contribute substantially to hospitalization and mortality in patients with COPD [23]. Increasing evidence suggests that the cardiovascular–pulmonary axis represents a key mechanistic and therapeutic target in COPD with cardiovascular comorbidities, whereby chronic pulmonary inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired gas exchange may contribute to systemic vascular injury and cardiovascular dysfunction, while cardiovascular impairment may further aggravate pulmonary symptoms and exacerbation risk [13, 23]. Persistent airway and systemic inflammation is a hallmark of COPD and is further amplified during acute exacerbations [22, 24]. Even after smoking cessation, airway biopsies show ongoing inflammation, with elevated sputum inflammatory markers persisting for months [24]. Compared with stable COPD, AECOPD is characterized by markedly increased inflammatory indices such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, high-sensitivity CRP, and procalcitonin [25]. Within this pathophysiological context, biomarkers integrating inflammatory and metabolic stress may provide additional insight into the mechanisms linking pulmonary and cardiovascular dysfunction and may help identify patients at increased risk of recurrent exacerbations [26].

LDH is widely expressed in human tissues and increases in response to ischemia, hypoxia, and inflammatory activation, serving as an indicator of tissue injury and metabolic stress [15, 16]. Consequently, elevated LDH has been linked to adverse prognosis in pneumonia [27], sepsis [28], severe COVID-19 [29], hematologic diseases [30], and malignancies [31]. Albumin, the major plasma protein, reflects nutritional status, systemic inflammation, and hepatic synthetic capacity [17]. Hypoalbuminemia may occur in chronic inflammatory states, malabsorption, nephrotic syndrome, or severe infection [15], and has been associated with poor outcomes in sepsis and acute myocardial infarction [32–34].

By integrating LDH and albumin, LAR provides a composite measure reflecting inflammatory and metabolic stress at the time of acute exacerbation. Because LAR was measured during hospital admission for AECOPD, elevated values may partly reflect acute physiological stress rather than long-term vulnerability. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as demonstrating an association between higher admission LAR levels and an increased likelihood of the frequent-exacerbator phenotype, rather than establishing a causal relationship or indicating a chronic physiological state.

The nonlinear relationship observed in the RCS analysis suggests that the association between LAR and frequent exacerbations is not constant across the entire range of values. At lower LAR values (< 5.1), relatively small increases in LAR were associated with disproportionately greater increases in exacerbation risk, whereas the risk curve became less steep beyond this threshold. One possible explanation is that once substantial inflammatory and metabolic stress has developed, additional increases in LAR contribute less incrementally to overall risk. From a clinical perspective, a LAR value of 5.1 could arise from an LDH concentration of approximately 230 U/L with an albumin level of 45 g/L, or from an LDH level of 180 U/L with an albumin level of 35 g/L. Thus, values exceeding this threshold do not necessarily require extreme abnormalities in either parameter alone but may reflect the combined effect of moderate LDH elevation and relative hypoalbuminemia. Such patients may warrant closer clinical monitoring because of their higher likelihood of belonging to the frequent-exacerbator phenotype.

The association observed in our study aligns with growing evidence that LAR serves as a prognostic indicator across diverse disease states. Prior studies have demonstrated that elevated LAR predicts short-term mortality in AECOPD [35], all-cause mortality in sepsis [15], worse outcomes in COVID-19 [18], and increased 30-day mortality in acute pulmonary embolism [19]. LAR has also been identified as a predictor of mortality in lower respiratory tract infections [36]. Collectively, these findings support the utility of LAR as a prognostic marker in acute inflammatory and cardiopulmonary conditions. Our results extend these observations by demonstrating an association between elevated admission LAR and the frequent-exacerbator phenotype in patients with COPD–CHD overlap.

This study has several limitations. First, LAR was measured only once at hospital admission during an acute exacerbation. Therefore, temporal or causal inferences cannot be established, elevated LAR levels may partly reflect acute disease severity rather than underlying long-term biological vulnerability. Whether LAR measured during clinically stable periods independently predicts future exacerbation risk remains uncertain and warrants prospective validation. Second, although COPD diagnosis was confirmed based on available historical pulmonary function records, detailed severity-related indices such as FEV1% predicted and GOLD stage were not consistently available, leading to potential residual confounding related to underlying disease severity. Third, although CHF was adjusted for as a comorbidity, the absence of detailed indicators of cardiac function severity may have introduced residual confounding because cardiac dysfunction can influence LDH levels through tissue hypoperfusion and myocardial injury. Finally, this was a retrospective, single-center design study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating serial LAR measurements during both stable and exacerbation phases are needed to clarify its prognostic value and clinical applicability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, higher admission LAR was independently associated with the frequent-exacerbator phenotype among patients with AECOPD and comorbid CHD. A nonlinear relationship was observed, with exacerbation risk increasing more steeply below a LAR value of approximately 5.1. Given its availability from routine laboratory testing, LAR may serve as a simple and accessible marker for identifying patients at increased risk of frequent exacerbations. However, because LAR was measured during an acute exacerbation, prospective studies incorporating serial assessments during stable and exacerbation phases are required to validate its predictive value and determine its role in clinical risk-stratification strategies.
