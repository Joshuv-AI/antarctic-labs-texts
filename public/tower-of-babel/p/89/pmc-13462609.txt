Improving seismic fault detection through fault-balanced patch extraction and deep learning networks (UNet, Efficient-UNet, and VGG19-UNet)
Authors: Keyvan Najafzadeh, Mohammad Emami Niri, Abbas Bahroudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate seismic fault detection is essential for reliable structural interpretation, reservoir characterization, and drilling risk mitigation. Conventional attribute-based methods often suffer from noise sensitivity, fragmented fault continuity, and strong dependence on interpreter experience, particularly in structurally complex settings. This study evaluates the performance of three convolutional neural network–based segmentation models, Standard UNet, Efficient-UNet, and VGG19-UNet, for automatic fault detection using 2D seismic patches extracted from a real 3D seismic volume. To address the severe class imbalance inherent in fault interpretation, a fault-aware dataset preparation strategy was implemented. Training samples were extracted from fault-rich regions using a minimum fault-pixel threshold, combined with controlled fault-mask dilation and data augmentation. All models were trained using a unified Dice + Binary Cross-Entropy loss function and evaluated on 128 × 128 patches using both overlap-based metrics (Dice and IoU) and distance-tolerant measures to assess spatial robustness. The results show that the Standard UNet achieves the highest overall performance, with a mean Dice of 0.91 and IoU of 0.85, demonstrating strong balance between precision and recall. The VGG19-UNet provides competitive performance (Dice = 0.88, IoU = 0.81) with relatively consistent fault continuity, while the Efficient-UNet exhibits lower accuracy (Dice = 0.73, IoU = 0.61), particularly in detecting thin or low-contrast faults. Distance-tolerant evaluation further confirms the robustness of the Standard UNet, with F1 scores exceeding 0.98 at small spatial tolerances. These results highlight the importance of both dataset design and architectural choice in deep learning–based seismic fault detection. The proposed framework demonstrates strong performance on the studied dataset, while its broader applicability to other geological settings remains an important direction for future work.

Introduction

The detection and interpretation of faults in seismic studies are essential for successful hydrocarbon exploration and production. Faults can act as sealing barriers that compartmentalize reservoirs or as conduits that enhance hydrocarbon migration and accumulation1,2. Consequently, accurate fault interpretation plays a pivotal role in drilling decisions, sweet spot identification, and reservoir volume estimation. Misinterpretation may lead to drilling hazards, wellbore instability, or inefficient reservoir management strategies3.

Traditionally, fault interpretation has been performed manually by geoscientists. This process is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and inherently subject to interpreter bias and inconsistency4. With the increasing size and complexity of modern seismic datasets, manual picking becomes increasingly impractical, introducing uncertainties that affect structural mapping and decision-making. Automation of fault detection has therefore emerged as a critical need, aiming to accelerate interpretation workflows, reduce subjectivity, and improve reliability5.

Conventional automated fault detection methods are primarily based on seismic attributes such as amplitude, coherence, curvature, and phase6,7,1. These attributes enhance structural discontinuities that may correspond to faults. Despite their widespread use, attribute-based approaches exhibit notable limitations. Their performance is sensitive to seismic noise, acquisition footprints, and parameter selection, while interpretation of attribute maps often remains subjective and dependent on interpreter expertise1,4. In structurally complex geological settings, fault signatures may be subtle or obscured, leading to incomplete or inaccurate delineation. These limitations have motivated the development of more objective and data-driven methodologies.

In recent years, Machine Learning (ML), and particularly Deep Learning (DL), has become increasingly prominent in seismic interpretation tasks. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and related architectures have been applied to automate fault detection, semantic segmentation, and structural interpretation workflows8–10. Unlike conventional model-driven techniques, DL approaches learn hierarchical feature representations directly from seismic data, enabling improved handling of structural complexity. DL has demonstrated success in facies classification11, horizon tracking12, and fault detection13,14. However, the performance of DL models strongly depends on the quality, representativeness, and balance of training datasets, and their generalization may degrade when training samples do not adequately capture geological variability10.

Within DL approaches, CNN-based segmentation architectures such as UNet and its variants have shown particular promise for seismic fault delineation due to their ability to capture both local discontinuities and broader structural patterns15,8. Modified encoder-enhanced architectures, incorporating deeper backbone networks (e.g., VGG-type encoders) or compound-scaled designs such as Efficient-Net, have been explored to improve feature representation and robustness, particularly for thin and subtle discontinuities16–18.

More recent studies (post-2022) have further advanced DL-based seismic fault detection through attention mechanisms, multi-scale feature aggregation, and hybrid CNN-transformer architectures capable of capturing long-range spatial dependencies within seismic volumes19,20. These approaches have demonstrated improved continuity and delineation of subtle fault structures[,24. Recent developments have also emphasized strategies for addressing class imbalance and enhancing sensitivity to thin fault features through tailored loss functions, data augmentation, and optimized training protocols23. Despite these advances, controlled architectural comparisons under consistent optimization settings remain limited, and the influence of dataset preparation strategies on segmentation performance has not been thoroughly examined.

Earlier CNN-based studies established the feasibility of automated seismic fault detection. Araya-Polo et al.25 trained deep neural networks on synthetic pre-stack seismic data for fault identification. An et al.26 predicted fault probabilities from2D seismic slices. Xiong et al.27 applied CNNs directly to seismic images to predict fault probabilities, demonstrating the effectiveness of data-driven fault detection on real seismic datasets. Subsequent studies trained models on labeled 2D seismic slices and reconstructed 3D fault volumes28,29, significantly reducing labeling effort while maintaining competitive accuracy. However, many of these approaches rely on randomly sampled patches or uniformly spaced slices, which can result in class imbalance and insufficient representation of thin or complex fault structures. In addition, slice-wise training strategies may introduce discontinuities when predictions are analyzed across spatial directions.

Although recent studies report promising performance using advanced architectures, several methodological limitations remain. In particular, the effects of dataset sampling strategies and training configurations are often not isolated, making it difficult to distinguish the impact of architecture from that of data preparation. Moreover, the role of targeted dataset design in improving segmentation stability and robustness has received limited attention.

In this work, we address these challenges by introducing a fault-aware dataset preparation strategy in which training patches are extracted from fault-rich regions and filtered using a minimum fault-pixel threshold. This approach aims to improve the representation of fault structures within the training data while mitigating class imbalance. In addition, we conduct a controlled comparison of three UNet-based architectures—Standard UNet, Efficient-UNet, and VGG19-UNet—under a unified training framework using a consistent loss function. By systematically evaluating segmentation performance on real 3D seismic data, this study provides insight into the combined influence of dataset design and architectural choice on deep learning–based fault detection.

Theoretical framework

The theoretical framework of this study encompasses the essential concepts underlying seismic fault detection through both conventional attribute analysis and DL methods. It is divided into three main components: (1) Data Conditioning, (2) Seismic Attributes for Fault Detection, and (3) Convolutional Neural Network Architectures.

Data conditioning

Prior to model training, the seismic volume was conditioned to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio while preserving structural discontinuities associated with faults. A Fault Enhancement Filter (FEF) was applied, combining a Dip-Steered Median Filter and an anisotropic Diffusion Filter. The dip-steered filtering aligns smoothing along local reflector orientations, suppressing random noise while maintaining fault-related discontinuities30,31. The subsequent diffusion-based filtering further reduces residual noise and enhances fault contrast by promoting smoothing in homogeneous regions while preserving high-gradient structural edges, following the anisotropic diffusion framework introduced by Perona and Malik32, further developed in image processing33, and adapted to seismic data by Fehmers and Höcker34 and Hale35. The combination of these filters improves fault visibility and provides a conditioned seismic volume suitable for attribute computation and CNN-based fault detection. Detailed mathematical formulations and implementation parameters are provided in Appendix A.

Seismic attributes for fault detection

Seismic attributes were computed to enhance structural discontinuities prior to model training. For fault detection, discontinuity and curvature-sensitive attributes are particularly effective because they emphasize reflector terminations, lateral amplitude variations, and geometric irregularities. In this study, five complementary attributes were employed: Similarity, Variance, Chaos, Ant Tracking, and Thin Fault Likelihood (TFL). Similarity measures lateral trace coherence and highlights discontinuities through reduced waveform correlation. Variance captures local amplitude fluctuations associated with abrupt reflector terminations. Chaos quantifies structural disorder by evaluating local dip variability, enabling detection of small-scale geometric disruptions. Ant Tracking enhances fault planes by propagating edge information along discontinuities, facilitating the delineation of complex fault networks. Thin Fault Likelihood (TFL) integrates multiple structural indicators to emphasize narrow and subtle fault planes while reducing false positives. Together, these attributes provide complementary structural information and improve the visibility of fault-related features. Detailed mathematical formulations and implementation parameters are provided in Appendix B.

Convolutional neural network architectures

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have emerged as powerful tools for seismic image segmentation, capable of automatically learning hierarchical representations of geological structures. Their encoder-decoder architectures are particularly effective in capturing both global context and fine-scale details of faults. In this study, three CNN-based segmentation networks were adopted: Standard UNet, Efficient-UNet and VGG19-UNet. All models follow the general UNet concept but employ different encoder backbones to optimize feature extraction.

Study area

This study utilizes a 3D marine seismic dataset acquired from a field in the Persian Gulf region, characterized by complex salt tectonics. The area lies on the northeastern margin of the Arabian Plate, where extensive salt tectonics have shaped the subsurface. Salt structures, including diapirs and associated collapse zones, have led to the development of complex fault networks. These faults are typically normal and listric in geometry, forming above and around the salt domes due to differential movement and gravitational collapse. Faults show variable orientations, displacements, and spacing, reflecting the interplay of salt movement, regional extensional stresses, and local sediment loading. The region is characterized by layered carbonate and clastic sequences, often disrupted by salt-induced deformation, providing a structurally complex environment suitable for detailed fault analysis. The structural complexity of the area, with faults of varying geometries and scales, presents a challenging but ideal setting for automated fault detection. This heterogeneity allows ML models to learn and identify a wide range of fault patterns, making the dataset particularly suitable for training, testing, and evaluating the performance of ML-based fault segmentation techniques.

Applied workflow

The methodology of this study integrates conventional seismic attribute analysis and deep learning–based segmentation within a unified workflow for fault detection and comparative evaluation. The overall process consists of four main stages: (1) seismic data pre-processing and conditioning, (2) attribute-based fault interpretation for benchmarking and mask preparation, (3) construction of a fault-balanced training dataset, and (4) training and evaluation of UNet-based convolutional neural networks. Detailed mathematical formulations and parameter settings are provided in Appendix A, B and C, while this section outlines the principal methodological steps.

The workflow begins with the pre-processing of input seismic data to enhance signal quality, suppress random and coherent noise, and ensure optimal conditioning for interpretation. This step improves the signal-to-noise ratio while preserving structural and stratigraphic features critical to fault identification.

Conventional fault detection is then performed through a detailed seismic attribute analysis. Attributes such as variance, chaos, similarity, ant tracking, and thin fault likelihood are computed to highlight geological discontinuities that may correspond to fault structures. Beyond serving as a baseline reference for performance comparison, this attribute study also assists manual fault interpretation, which is used in this work to prepare mask data samples for training and validating the DL models.

In the advanced stage, fault detection is carried out using DL methods based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). To improve the robustness and generalization of these models, data augmentation strategies are applied to expand dataset diversity and reduce the risk of overfitting. The dataset was divided into training and validation subsets to monitor model performance during training.

Finally, the results obtained from the DL approaches are compared with those from conventional seismic attribute analysis. This comparison is conducted qualitatively to assess the spatial continuity and interpretability of detected faults. In addition, quantitative evaluation of the CNN-based networks is performed using the Dice-score and Intersection over Union (IoU), providing objective measures of their detection accuracy and overlap with reference fault interpretations. To further assess boundary localization performance, distance-tolerant metrics were also computed, allowing small lateral deviations between predicted and annotated fault traces to be evaluated in a geologically meaningful manner. Data pre-processing

Prior to attribute computation and ML analyses, the 3D seismic volume was structurally conditioned using a Fault Enhancement Filtering workflow. The process began with the computation of a steering cube using the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) method to estimate local reflector dips with high stability. This steering cube provided the dip and azimuth information required for subsequent structurally guided filtering steps.

A Similarity attribute was then calculated using a time gate of − 28 to 28 ms and a step-out of 1 inline × 1 crossline to quantify local seismic coherency. Based on this similarity volume, two dip-steered filters were sequentially applied: Dip-Steered Median Filter (DSMF) for general noise suppression and reflector smoothing, followed by the Dip-Steered Diffusion Filter (DSDF) for fault enhancement. To ensure that structural discontinuities such as faults were preserved, a similarity threshold of 0.7 was applied so that the DSDF operated selectively near faulted zones.

This integrated approach effectively attenuates random noise and acquisition footprints while preserving and enhancing fault-related discontinuities, thereby improving the interpretability and subsequent fault segmentation performance of the ML models. The fault-enhanced seismic cube resulting from this step was then used as input for both attribute-based fault analysis and DL-based fault detection workflows. Seismic attribute analysis

To provide a benchmark for evaluating the CNN-based segmentation results, the conditioned seismic volume was analyzed using five discontinuity-sensitive attributes: Similarity, Variance, Chaos, Ant Tracking, and Thin Fault Likelihood (TFL). These attributes enhance reflector terminations and structural irregularities and are widely employed in conventional seismic fault interpretation workflows.

The attribute volumes successfully highlight major fault corridors and structural discontinuities, particularly in zones with strong reflector offsets. However, their responses vary depending on local structural complexity and noise conditions. Similarity and Variance emphasize general discontinuities but may produce fragmented responses in areas of subtle faulting. Chaos enhances structurally disordered zones but can introduce ambiguity in highly heterogeneous intervals. Ant Tracking improves the continuity of fault corridors but remains sensitive to parameter selection and attribute quality. Thin Fault Likelihood provides higher-resolution fault indications, particularly for narrow or steeply dipping structures, yet isolated false positives may still occur in noisy regions.

Compared to the attribute-based interpretation, the CNN-based segmentation results demonstrate improved spatial continuity and structural coherence across the seismic cube. The networks integrate contextual information beyond local amplitude or geometric variations, enabling more consistent delineation of thin and weak fault segments. This contrast highlights the advantages of the proposed DL framework in reducing interpreter dependency and enhancing fault detection consistency in complex geological settings.

Dataset preparation

Training DL models for seismic fault detection is inherently challenging due to the limited availability of labeled data and, more critically, the severe class imbalance between fault and non-fault pixels. In typical marine seismic datasets, fault structures occupy only a small fraction of the subsurface, while most samples correspond to continuous, un-faulted reflectors.

This imbalance is a well-documented issue in seismic interpretation4,8, and when used directly for supervised learning, it causes networks to be biased toward predicting the dominant non-fault class, resulting in poor generalization and missed faults. Therefore, constructing a balanced and geologically meaningful training dataset is a crucial step in the overall methodology and has a direct impact on the performance of any DL model. Patch extraction is a strategy adopted in prior seismic DL studies to increase sampling efficiency38,39.

Fault-balanced patch extraction

A structured patch extraction workflow was implemented to construct the training dataset. The pre-conditioned seismic cube and its corresponding interpreted fault mask were first loaded from the processed SEGY volumes. Because fault occurrences are not uniformly distributed throughout the survey, patch extraction was restricted to crossline intervals identified as fault-rich zones. This constraint ensured efficient sampling of structurally relevant regions while avoiding redundant background-dominated areas.

Square patches of 128 × 128 pixels were extracted from the selected intervals. This patch size was chosen to provide sufficient local structural context while maintaining computational efficiency during training. A minimum fault-pixel threshold of 2% was applied during patch selection to ensure that retained samples contain sufficient fault information and to prevent the dataset from being dominated by background pixels. This value was selected to balance the inclusion of meaningful fault features while preserving variability in fault geometry. Lower thresholds tend to introduce a high proportion of low-information patches, whereas higher thresholds significantly reduce the number of available training samples.

To account for the inherently thin nature of interpreted fault traces and potential spatial uncertainty in manual annotations, a morphological dilation operation was applied to the fault masks prior to patch filtering. This dilation slightly expands the fault labels in the horizontal direction, improving spatial continuity and ensuring that narrow fault planes are sufficiently represented within the extracted patches without significantly altering their geometric characteristics. This combined strategy, targeted sampling from fault-rich intervals, threshold-based filtering, and label dilation, constitutes the fault-balanced patch extraction framework adopted in this study.

Data augmentation

Augmentation operations were designed to improve model performance while maintaining consistency with the input data. To further enhance model generalization, a data augmentation strategy was implemented. A total of 3000 training patches were constructed, with 20% generated through augmentation and the remaining 80% retained in their original form. Augmentation operations were designed to improve model performance while preserving the structural characteristics of the seismic data.

The applied transformations included random horizontal flipping, small controlled rotations (± 10°), pixel-level translational shifts (5 samples), and Gaussian noise injection (applied only to seismic images). These transformations were limited to small perturbations to preserve the structural characteristics of seismic reflections and fault geometries. In particular, the use of small-angle rotations is assumed to maintain realistic fault orientations without introducing significant distortion in structural dips.

These augmentations increase the diversity of fault geometries and orientations encountered during training while minimizing disruption to stratigraphic continuity, thereby improving model generalization.

DL architectures and training strategy

DL-based semantic segmentation models have become increasingly effective for seismic fault detection due to their ability to learn hierarchical spatial features directly from data. In this study, three architectures, UNet, Efficient-UNet and VGG19-UNet, were employed to predict fine-scale fault patterns from noisy and structurally complex seismic images. These architectures offer several advantages over conventional attribute-based interpretation, including improved representation of subtle discontinuities, robustness to noise, and the capability to learn multi-scale geological expressions of faulting. UNet configuration

The UNet model serves as the baseline encoder-decoder architecture for seismic fault segmentation. It consists of a five-level contracting-expanding structure with skip connections that preserve spatial detail. Each encoder stage contains two 3 × 3 convolutional layers followed by max-pooling, while the decoder mirrors this structure using transposed convolutions for up-sampling and concatenation with encoder features. L2 regularization (1 × 10−5) was applied to all convolutional kernels to stabilize training and reduce overfitting.

To address the strong class imbalance between fault and background pixels, a hybrid Dice + Binary Cross-Entropy (Dice + BCE) loss function was employed. This formulation combines the region-overlap sensitivity of Dice loss with the pixel-wise stability of binary cross-entropy, enabling robust detection of thin and low-contrast fault segments that occupy only a small fraction of each patch.

Global min–max normalization was applied across the entire training volume, and the same normalization parameters were preserved and reused during inference to ensure consistency between training and prediction stages. The UNet was trained under the following configuration:

Efficient-UNet configuration

The Efficient-UNet architecture integrates an EfficientNet-B1 encoder with a symmetric UNet-style decoder. The model was trained from scratch (no ImageNet weights) to ensure full adaptation to seismic textures. A 1 × 1 convolution was used to expand the grayscale seismic input to 3 channels for compatibility with Efficient-Net. Skip connections were extracted from four key expansion layers of the encoder and merged into the decoder through transposed convolutions and convolutional refinement blocks.

Training configuration and implementation details

To ensure a fair comparison between the evaluated network architectures, all models were trained using a unified loss function. A hybrid Dice + Binary Cross-Entropy (Dice + BCE) loss was selected based on its stable convergence behavior and consistent performance in handling class imbalance.

Preliminary experiments indicated that, although the Focal Tversky loss is well suited for imbalanced segmentation tasks, it resulted in less stable training for deeper architectures such as VGG19-UNet. In contrast, Dice + BCE provided more consistent optimization across all models. This unified training setup ensures that performance differences among Standard UNet, Efficient-UNet, and VGG19-UNet are primarily attributable to architectural design rather than variations in the loss function.

A fixed patch size of 128 × 128 was used for all models to ensure consistency across experiments. This size reflects a practical balance between capturing local fault geometry and maintaining manageable computational cost. While larger patches may better preserve long-range fault continuity, they significantly increase memory requirements and training time. To mitigate potential fragmentation effects, overlapping patches and fault-aware sampling were employed, allowing the models to learn fault continuity across multiple spatial contexts. Nevertheless, the limited global context inherent to patch-based training is acknowledged as a limitation and may be addressed in future work through larger input windows or 3D approaches.

All encoder backbones, including those based on EfficientNet-B1 and VGG19, were trained from scratch without using pretrained weights. This design ensures a fair and controlled comparison between architectures under identical training conditions, allowing performance differences to be attributed primarily to architectural characteristics. In addition, pretrained models (e.g., trained on natural image datasets) may not be directly suitable for seismic data due to the significant domain differences between visual textures and geophysical signals.

Since the encoder backbones were trained from scratch (i.e., without pretrained ImageNet weights), no assumptions related to natural image RGB statistics are imposed. This avoids potential bias associated with RGB-based inductive priors and enables the networks to learn domain-specific feature representations directly from seismic data, better capturing geological structures and discontinuities. However, it is acknowledged that pretrained encoders may further enhance performance, particularly when larger labeled datasets are available. Exploring transfer learning strategies for seismic fault detection is therefore considered an important direction for future work.

To adapt the single-channel seismic input to the three-channel input requirement of EfficientNet-B1 and VGG19, a 1 × 1 convolution was applied as a learnable channel expansion layer. Unlike simple replication into pseudo-RGB channels, this operation performs a trainable linear projection of the input, allowing the network to generate three distinct feature representations from the original seismic data. Results and discussion

This section presents the results of the proposed workflow for seismic fault detection. The performance of the different approaches is evaluated using quantitative metrics and visual comparisons.

Input data preparation

Good results in seismic fault detection fundamentally depend on the quality of the input data, making the pre-processing stage essential for achieving reliable interpretations. In this study, the seismic volume first underwent a comprehensive pre-processing workflow in which a steering cube was generated using PCA-based dip estimation. This steering information guided the application of the Fault Enhancement Filter (FEF), which integrates Dip-Steered Median Filtering (DSMF) and Dip-Steered Diffusion Filtering (DSDF). The diffusion step was selectively constrained using a similarity threshold of 0.7 to ensure that reflector continuity was enhanced while preserving the sharp discontinuities associated with fault zones. Visual inspection confirms that this filtering stage improved the structural clarity of the seismic image, reduced random noise, and increased the lateral coherence of reflectors, thereby providing a cleaner and more reliable input for both attribute computation and subsequent DL analysis. Figure 5 displays a raw inline seismic section prior to filtering, and the corresponding section after applying the FEF. The filtered result demonstrates noticeable noise suppression, enhanced reflector continuity, and clearer visibility of fault planes, achieved without degrading fine structural details.

A suite of widely used structural seismic attributes, including Similarity, Variance, Chaos, Ant Tracking, and Thin Fault Likelihood (TFL), was computed to enhance fault visibility and support both qualitative interpretation and quantitative assessment of structural complexity. However, consistent with previous studies, they primarily highlight discontinuities rather than providing complete fault segmentation, motivating the subsequent application of DL models for improved fault delineation.

ML application results

Building on the limitations observed in attribute-based fault delineation, this study applies a standard UNet together with two encoder-enhanced variants, Efficient-UNet and VGG19-UNet, to achieve more robust and high-resolution fault segmentation. All three models are based on the UNet framework15; however, the encoder-enhanced architectures incorporate deep convolutional backbones, EfficientNet36 and VGG1937, to explore the impact of different feature extractors on seismic fault detection performance.

All networks were trained on carefully curated and augmented seismic-fault patch pairs using loss functions tailored for highly imbalanced segmentation tasks40,41. Compared to attribute-based results, all CNN models produce prediction maps with substantially improved spatial continuity, structural completeness, and fault-plane sharpness. Among the three architectures, the Standard UNet achieved the highest overall performance, followed by VGG19-UNet, while Efficient-UNet showed comparatively lower accuracy. These results indicate that, under the adopted training setup, the Standard UNet provides the most reliable fault delineation, particularly in capturing both continuous and subtle discontinuities in seismic data.

Training dataset construction

Accurate training data are essential for developing reliable machine-learning models in seismic fault detection. In this study, ground-truth labels were created through manual interpretation of 20 seismic inlines extracted from the 3D volume. Seismic fault interpretation was implemented using both the original seismic amplitude and a suite of auxiliary structural attributes, to improve confidence in identifying fault breaks and subtle discontinuities. Final fault interpretations were always made on the original amplitude data to ensure spatial accuracy and consistency across all interpreted sections.

Each manually interpreted fault trace was converted into a binary mask, assigning a value of one to fault pixels and zero elsewhere. The masks were then quality-checked to ensure geological plausibility, continuity of interpreted fault segments, and consistency with reflector terminations. Figure 7 shows an example of an interpreted seismic inline alongside its corresponding binary mask, illustrating how discrete fault breaks are translated into training labels.

Because manually interpreted samples are limited relative to the size and structural complexity of the 3D volume, data augmentation was performed to enhance model generalization and reduce overfitting. A combined dataset of 3,000 seismic-mask pairs was generated through a mix of original and augmented samples. Approximately 80% of the dataset consisted of original patches extracted from the 20 interpreted inlines, while the remaining 20% were synthetically generated using controlled augmentation. This included horizontal and vertical flipping, small rotations (− 10° to + 10°), pixel-wise translations, and Gaussian noise added only to the seismic images. These augmentation strategies introduce variations in fault orientation, dip angle, and reflector continuity, enabling the network to learn more robust structural patterns rather than memorizing the training examples. Model training and segmentation

All models were trained using consistent configurations and a unified Dice + Binary Cross-Entropy loss function, as described in the Methodology. Training was conducted for a fixed number of epochs, and convergence was verified through loss behavior.

To further assess the optimization behavior of the evaluated architectures, the evolution of training loss is shown in Fig. 9. All models demonstrate stable convergence under the unified loss function. However, differences in convergence rate and smoothness are observed. The Standard UNet converges more rapidly and exhibits a smoother loss trajectory, indicating stable learning dynamics. In contrast, Efficient-UNet shows slower convergence and higher fluctuations, suggesting reduced training efficiency under the given data conditions. The VGG19-UNet also converges steadily, but with slightly less stability compared to the Standard UNet. These observations are consistent with the quantitative performance results presented earlier. Following training, all models were applied to the unseen test set and subsequently to the full seismic volume.

During inference, the trained 2D networks were applied to the full 3D seismic volume in a slice-wise manner along the inline direction. Each inline section was processed using a sliding-window approach with patch sizes of 128 × 128 pixels. To minimize edge artifacts and improve spatial consistency, overlapping patches were used, and predictions in overlapping regions were combined through averaging.

Due to computational constraints, inference was performed on every fifth inline, producing a subsampled 3D fault probability volume. The original spatial spacing between inlines was preserved during reconstruction to ensure geometric consistency. This approach enables efficient application of 2D-trained models while maintaining meaningful 3D structural continuity.

In contrast, Efficient-UNet successfully detected major fault features but showed reduced sensitivity to subtle fault segments and produced comparatively more fragmented predictions.

Performance evaluation

Quantitative evaluation of the segmentation results was carried out using multiple complementary metrics designed to assess both pixel-level accuracy and boundary-level localization performance on the test data including 300 number of seismic image patches and their corresponding labels. Two primary overlap-based measures were the Dice Similarity Coefficient and the Intersection-over-Union (IoU), both widely used in seismic fault detection and more general image segmentation tasks15,17,42. Dice is particularly sensitive to the balance between false positives and false negatives, making it useful for evaluating thin geological structures that occupy a small proportion of the image. IoU provides a stricter measure of overlap by penalizing mismatched regions more heavily, and is frequently reported as a benchmark metric for fault segmentation models4,43. Together, these metrics offer a robust assessment of segmentation quality, especially in datasets where faults represent sparse, high-contrast discontinuities relative to the background geology.

To further evaluate the models’ ability to accurately localize thin and spatially discontinuous fault traces, distance-tolerant metrics were computed for tolerance radius of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 5 pixels. These metrics relax the exact-pixel match requirement and instead consider a prediction correct if it falls within a specified neighborhood of the ground truth, following distance-based evaluation approaches commonly used in edge and boundary detection research44,45. Such measures are particularly relevant for seismic fault identification, where slight lateral misalignment of annotated fault traces is unavoidable and should not be penalized as segmentation errors4,39. The distance-tolerant F1 score therefore provides a more geologically meaningful assessment of fault extraction performance, especially for thin, sparsely distributed, and laterally shifting structures43,46.

The mean Dice and IoU values for the three architectures are summarized in Table 2, while Fig. 11 shows the distributions of these overlap-based metrics across all validation patches, highlighting the relative variability and central tendency of each model’s performance. The distance-tolerant precision, recall, and F1 metrics are reported separately in Table 3, capturing boundary-level localization performance under different tolerance radius.

The quantitative evaluation reveals a clear and consistent performance hierarchy among the three networks. The Standard UNet achieved the highest segmentation accuracy, with a mean Dice score of 0.91 and a mean IoU of 0.85, outperforming both VGG19-UNet and Efficient-UNet. The VGG19-UNet demonstrated strong performance, yielding a mean Dice of 0.88 and mean IoU of 0.81, while Efficient-UNet produced comparatively lower accuracy, with a mean Dice of 0.73 and mean IoU of 0.61. Distance-tolerant evaluation further reinforces these trends. At zero tolerance (d = 0), the Standard UNet demonstrates the highest fault localization accuracy, with an F1 score of 0.91, followed by VGG19-UNet (0.88) and Efficient-UNet (0.73). As the tolerance radius increases, all models exhibit improved recall and F1 scores; however, the Standard UNet consistently maintains the best performance across all distances. At a 5-pixel tolerance, its F1 score reaches 0.99, indicating highly accurate geometric alignment and strong fault continuity. VGG19-UNet also shows high performance (F1 ≈ 0.98), while Efficient-UNet improves to a lower level (F1 ≈ 0.92), reflecting its reduced sensitivity to subtle and discontinuous fault segments.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the Standard UNet provides the most reliable and consistent fault delineation, outperforming both VGG19-UNet and Efficient-UNet in pixel-level metrics as well as in distance-tolerant evaluations. The strong performance of the Standard UNet suggests that, under the adopted training conditions and dataset size, a simpler architecture can effectively capture both local discontinuities and broader structural patterns in seismic data.

While VGG19-UNet also achieves high accuracy and produces coherent fault predictions, its increased architectural complexity does not lead to superior performance in this case. Efficient-UNet, although computationally efficient, shows comparatively lower sensitivity to subtle fault features and produces more fragmented results.

Discussion

Model performance and segmentation behavior

The results demonstrate a clear performance hierarchy among the evaluated models, with the Standard UNet achieving the highest accuracy, followed by VGG19-UNet, while Efficient-UNet shows comparatively lower performance (Fig. 10). This indicates that, under the given dataset and training conditions, increased architectural complexity does not necessarily translate into improved segmentation performance. The distance-tolerant metrics further support these observations (Table 3). All models show improved performance with increasing tolerance, but the Standard UNet consistently maintains the highest F1-scores, indicating superior spatial consistency and reduced fragmentation. The Efficient-UNet, in contrast, shows a larger gap between precision and recall, confirming its tendency toward under-segmentation.

Architectural design and model complexity

From a segmentation perspective, these results reveal an important trade-off between model complexity and generalization capability. While deeper and more efficient architectures are designed to improve feature representation, they may require larger and more diverse datasets to reach their full potential. In this study, the Standard UNet appears better aligned with the dataset size and fault characteristics, enabling more stable and effective learning.

The strong performance of the Standard UNet can be attributed to its balanced encoder–decoder design, which effectively captures both local discontinuities and broader structural context while preserving spatial resolution. With moderate down-sampling and symmetric skip connections, the architecture maintains sensitivity to thin and low-contrast fault features, enabling accurate and continuous fault delineation.

The performance of the VGG19-UNet may be associated with its deeper encoder structure, which enables multi-scale feature representation. This is consistent with its observed segmentation behavior, including relatively improved continuity compared to lighter architectures. Such characteristics support the capture of complex structural patterns and fine-scale variations in seismic data. However, under the adopted training conditions, this increased architectural complexity did not translate into improved performance compared to the Standard UNet, likely due to limitations in training data size that restrict effective generalization. In contrast, the Efficient-UNet shows comparatively lower performance, reflected in reduced recall and increased fragmentation in the predicted fault structures. This behavior may be associated with its lightweight encoder design and compound scaling strategy, which prioritize computational efficiency. While this design reduces computational cost, it may also limit the network’s ability to capture fine-scale structural variations, particularly for subtle or low-contrast fault features.

It is recognized that max-pooling operations in UNet architectures may lead to the loss of fine-scale features, particularly for thin and low-contrast faults. In this study, several design choices help mitigate this limitation. Skip connections enable the recovery of high-resolution spatial information during decoding, while the relatively shallow network depth reduces excessive resolution loss. In addition, the fault-balanced dataset preparation strategy and mask dilation enhance the representation of thin fault structures during training, helping compensate for potential information loss associated with down-sampling.

Training strategy and data-related effects

The fault-balanced dataset preparation strategy plays a critical role in enabling all models to detect thin fault structures. By enforcing a minimum fault-pixel threshold and focusing on fault-rich regions, the dataset improves model sensitivity and reduces class imbalance effects. However, the results suggest that simpler architectures may benefit more directly from this strategy, while more complex models may require additional data or regularization to fully leverage their capacity.

The results also demonstrate that loss function selection can significantly influence the relative performance of different architectures. When inconsistent loss functions are used, performance differences may be misattributed to architectural design. Under the controlled setup adopted in this study, the use of a unified Dice + BCE loss function ensures that the observed performance differences primarily reflect architectural characteristics rather than optimization bias.

Training deep architectures such as VGG19 and EfficientNet-B1 from scratch on relatively limited seismic datasets increases the risk of overfitting due to their large number of parameters. To mitigate this effect, several strategies were employed, including L2 regularization, fault-balanced dataset construction, and targeted data augmentation, while model performance was monitored using a validation set to ensure stable convergence. Despite these measures, the results indicate that simpler architectures such as the Standard UNet achieved better generalization performance, suggesting that model complexity should be carefully matched to dataset size.

This observation is further supported by the fact that the labeled dataset is derived from a limited subset of the seismic volume. Although targeted patch extraction and augmentation improve training efficiency and robustness, they cannot fully substitute for large-scale and diverse geological data. Consequently, deeper architectures such as VGG19-UNet and Efficient-UNet may not fully exploit their representational capacity under these conditions.

The relatively lower performance of Efficient-UNet may also be attributed to the training-from-scratch strategy adopted in this study. More complex encoders such as EfficientNet typically benefit from large-scale pretraining, and their performance may be limited when trained on relatively small, domain-specific datasets. This further emphasizes the importance of aligning model complexity with data availability.

Data augmentation also contributes to improved model generalization by exposing the networks to a wider range of fault geometries and orientations. The stable convergence behavior observed during training, along with the spatial consistency of predicted fault structures, suggests that the applied augmentation strategy enhances robustness. However, a dedicated ablation study would be required to quantify its exact contribution, which is left for future work.

Practical considerations and limitations

A practical consideration of the proposed workflow is the balance between manual data preparation and computational efficiency. Although the fault-balanced dataset relies on manually interpreted seismic sections, the number of required annotations is limited compared to fully labeling 3D volumes. In this study, a relatively small set of interpreted inlines was sufficient to generate a large and diverse training dataset through targeted patch extraction and augmentation. While the initial labeling requires expert time and effort, this cost is incurred only once, whereas the trained models can be applied efficiently to large seismic volumes with minimal additional computational expense. This trade-off reflects a common characteristic of supervised deep learning approaches, where upfront data preparation is offset by significant gains in interpretation speed and consistency during deployment. Consequently, the proposed framework remains practical and scalable for real-world seismic interpretation tasks.

In addition to segmentation accuracy, computational efficiency represents an important practical trade-off. While deeper or more complex architectures may offer enhanced feature representation, they also require increased training time and computational resources. The Efficient-UNet benefits from a lightweight encoder design, resulting in faster inference and reduced computational cost. In contrast, the VGG19-UNet and Standard UNet demand greater computational resources due to their increased architectural complexity. However, this added complexity does not necessarily translate into proportional performance gains. In this study, the Standard UNet provides the most favorable balance between accuracy and efficiency, achieving strong segmentation performance with moderate computational demand. A more detailed quantitative analysis of computational cost, including memory usage and processing time, is left for future work.

Despite the overall effectiveness of the models, some failure cases are observed across all architectures. These include difficulty in detecting very thin or low-contrast faults, as well as regions where seismic discontinuities are weak or ambiguous. In such cases, the models may produce fragmented predictions or miss portions of fault planes. These limitations highlight the inherent challenges of fully automated fault detection and emphasize the continued importance of data quality, geological variability, and interpretation context.

Limitations and future work

While the present study compares deep learning models with conventional seismic attribute-based methods, it does not include benchmarking against recent deep learning-based fault detection approaches. Such comparisons would provide further insight into the relative performance of the proposed framework. However, ensuring a fair evaluation across different architectures and training configurations requires careful standardization, which is beyond the scope of this study. Future work will therefore focus on extending the comparison to additional state-of-the-art deep learning models.

Although the proposed fault-aware sampling strategy is designed to address class imbalance and improve fault representation, its isolated impact has not been evaluated through a dedicated ablation study. A comparative analysis between fault-balanced and randomly sampled datasets would provide further quantitative insight into its contribution. In addition, the increased proportion of fault pixels in the training data may introduce bias toward overprediction in fault-sparse regions. While the reported precision values suggest that false positives are reasonably controlled, a dedicated evaluation on fault-sparse areas would provide a more comprehensive assessment. These aspects are identified as important directions for future work.

It is important to note that the proposed workflow does not explicitly enforce structural continuity or orientation consistency at the model level. Although preprocessing steps enhance fault visibility, the networks are trained using pixel-wise loss functions that do not directly encode topological or geometrical constraints. In this study, fault continuity is learned implicitly through the convolutional architecture, receptive field expansion, and the fault-aware dataset preparation strategy. While this results in improved spatial coherence, no explicit constraint is imposed to enforce structurally consistent fault surfaces. Future work may incorporate topology-aware loss functions or structure-based regularization techniques to address this limitation.

Similarly, while overlap-based and distance-tolerant metrics provide useful quantitative evaluation, they do not explicitly capture topological properties such as connectivity and structural continuity. Incorporating topology-aware or graph-based evaluation metrics would enable a more comprehensive assessment of fault network coherence. Nevertheless, the combination of distance-tolerant metrics and 3D visualization in this study provides a meaningful approximation of spatial continuity.

Finally, several broader limitations should be acknowledged. The study is based on a single 3D seismic dataset, and therefore the generalizability of the findings to other geological settings and acquisition conditions requires further validation. In addition, the fault labels are derived from manual interpretation, which may introduce uncertainty and interpreter bias. Although the applied preprocessing and dataset design aim to mitigate these effects, the quality and diversity of training data remain critical factors influencing model performance. Future work will focus on evaluating the proposed framework across multiple datasets, including publicly available benchmarks such as the North Sea F3 block, to assess robustness across diverse geological conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that deep learning-based segmentation provides a robust and effective approach for seismic fault detection, outperforming conventional attribute-based methods in terms of accuracy, continuity, and spatial consistency. Among the evaluated architectures, the Standard UNet achieved the best overall performance, delivering the most balanced results, while VGG19-UNet showed competitive performance and Efficient-UNet exhibited reduced sensitivity to subtle fault features.

A key outcome of this work is the demonstrated importance of dataset design. The proposed fault-balanced sampling strategy significantly improves the representation of sparse fault features and contributes directly to model performance. The results further indicate that segmentation accuracy depends not only on architectural complexity but also on the alignment between model design and dataset characteristics.

Overall, the study provides a practical and scalable framework for automated seismic fault interpretation. It highlights that, under limited and domain-specific data conditions, well-structured training data and appropriately selected architectures can achieve reliable and geologically meaningful fault detection.
