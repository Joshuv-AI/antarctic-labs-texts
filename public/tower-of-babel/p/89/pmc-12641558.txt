Massive immune-related myositis and cytokine release syndrome during dual immunotherapy plus chemotherapy for recurrent lung adenocarcinoma: case report
Authors: Shogo Nakai, Yusuke Inoue, Masato Karayama, Hideki Yasui, Yuzo Suzuki, Hironao Hozumi, Kazuki Furuhashi, Noriyuki Enomoto, Tomoyuki Fujisawa, Naoki Inui, Takafumi Suda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Immune-related myositis (irMyositis) is a rare but potentially life-threatening immune-related adverse event (irAE) associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). We report a case of irMyositis presenting with massive unilateral enlargement of the periarticular hip muscles in a patient with recurrent lung adenocarcinoma after two courses of combination therapy with tremelimumab, durvalumab, and chemotherapy. Computed tomography scans revealed marked unilateral swelling of the gluteal and thigh muscles without evidence of contrast enhancement. Laboratory tests revealed elevated creatine kinase and inflammatory marker levels, and magnetic resonance imaging confirmed muscle inflammation. The patient was diagnosed with irMyositis accompanied by cytokine release syndrome and immune-related pneumonitis. Prompt initiation of high-dose corticosteroids, followed by tocilizumab therapy, has resulted in rapid clinical and radiographic improvements. This case illustrated an uncommon radiological manifestation of irMyositis. It underscores the importance of early recognition and timely immunosuppressive therapy for managing life-threatening irAEs.

Introduction

Immune-related myositis (irMyositis) is a rare but potentially life-threatening immune-related adverse event (irAE) with a prevalence of 1 % among patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [1]. The prompt recognition and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy are critical for improving patient outcomes. Although diffuse muscle involvement is a typical manifestation, irMyositis can occasionally present with atypical radiological features, complicating the diagnosis.

Here, we describe a case of irMyositis characterized by localized muscle swelling mimicking skeletal muscle metastasis in a patient treated with dual ICI therapy targeting programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) in combination with chemotherapy.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old Asian male with a 45-pack-year smoking history was diagnosed with unresectable lung adenocarcinoma (cT0N2M0, Stage IIIA). The Oncomine Dx Target Test identified no targetable oncogenic driver mutations. PD-L1 expression, assessed using the PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx assay, showed a tumor proportion score of <1 %.

Following the completion of concurrent chemoradiation therapy and 1 year of durvalumab (an anti-PD-L1 antibody) consolidation therapy, disease recurrence was observed after 21 months, presenting as lung metastasis and pleural dissemination. A combination therapy with tremelimumab (an anti-CTLA-4 antibody), durvalumab, carboplatin, and pemetrexed was initiated.

The patient presented to the emergency department with a high-grade fever (39.7 °C), dyspnea, and right thigh myalgia on day 35 after treatment initiation (cycle 2, day 11). Computed tomography (CT) revealed marked enlargement of the right gluteus medius, obturator internus, and quadratus femoris muscles without abnormal contrast enhancement (Fig. 1A and B; yellow arrows), in addition to new pulmonary consolidations (Fig. 2A). Laboratory tests showed elevated serum levels of creatine kinase (15,004 U/L), aldolase (31.0 U/L), myoglobin (4342 ng/mL), IL-6 (1350 pg/mL), and ferritin (1235 ng/mL). Sputum and blood cultures were negative. These findings were consistent with those of myositis (grade 2 according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0) and cytokine release syndrome (CRS) (grade 3). Although the KL-6 level was not elevated (258 U/mL), the patient was diagnosed with immune-related pneumonitis (grade 3). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with a fat-suppressed T2-weighted sequence demonstrated a high signal intensity in the right gluteus medius, obturator internus, quadratus femoris, adductor, and gracilis muscles (Fig. 1C; yellow arrows), supporting the diagnosis of myositis rather than muscle metastasis or abscess. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit and treated with high-dose methylprednisolone (1000 mg for 3 days), followed by oral prednisolone (0.8 mg/kg/day). Meropenem was administered for 10 days. Although the clinical symptoms and C-reactive protein and creatine kinase levels improved, fever recurred on day 3. Tocilizumab (8 mg/kg) was administered, resulting in a rapid resolution of fever and inflammatory markers (Fig. 3). Follow-up CT on day 14 showed resolution of the muscle swelling (Fig. 1D and E), and MRI on day 16 confirmed improvement in the previously inflamed muscles (Fig. 1F; yellow arrows). The pneumonitis also showed radiographic improvement (Fig. 2B). Although the KL-6 level increased to 634 U/mL on day 14, it eventually decreased on day 39. Discussion

Here, we report a rare case of irMyositis that presented with massive unilateral enlargement of the periarticular hip muscles, including the abductor, adductor, and external rotator muscles. To the best of our knowledge, no previous reports have described irMyositis manifesting as a localized muscle swelling mimicking skeletal muscle metastasis on CT imaging.

Marked muscle enlargement necessitated a differential diagnosis, including skeletal muscle metastasis and abscess formation. In addition, it was unclear whether myositis was immune-related or associated with CRS. In the present case, the myositis improved rapidly with steroid pulse therapy, whereas fever recurred and resolved only after tocilizumab administration. This clinical course suggests that myositis and CRS were distinct entities. Although myalgia is a recognized feature of CRS, localized muscle swelling attributable to CRS has not been previously reported to the best of our knowledge. Previous studies have shown that most cases of irMyositis (81.5 %) occur within the first three months of ICI therapy, with a median onset of 31 days [2], and often coexist with other extra-muscular irAEs [3]. In our patient, the onset on day 35 after initiating dual ICI therapy with chemotherapy, markedly elevated creatine kinase levels, absence of contrast enhancement on CT, and rapid resolution of muscle enlargement within 14 days of steroid treatment collectively supported the diagnosis of irMyositis.

MRI is crucial for detecting myositis. Although irMyositis lacks pathognomonic imaging findings, typical features include low to isointense signals on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted sequences [4]. Although bilateral symmetric edema of the proximal muscles and fascia has been reported in patients with irMyositis [5], the degree of massive unilateral swelling in the present case was unusual. CRS-related inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 might increase capillary permeability and fluid accumulation, contributing to extensive muscle swelling.

Because sepsis and bacterial pneumonia could not be initially ruled out, empiric meropenem therapy was initiated. However, the elevated KL-6 levels and radiological findings, together with the absence of bacterial growth in blood and sputum cultures, suggested that immune-related pneumonitis was more likely than bacterial pneumonia.

The risk of irMyositis is higher when anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies are used in combination with anti-CTLA-4 antibodies than when a monotherapy is used [6]. As ICI use continues to expand, clinicians are likely to encounter an increasing number of irMyositis cases. Although potentially fatal, irMyositis generally responds to timely immunosuppressive treatment [7]. In the present case, early administration of corticosteroids and tocilizumab led to rapid clinical improvement, highlighting the importance of prompt recognition and management of this serious irAE.

Conclusion

This case highlights that irMyositis can mimic skeletal muscle metastasis by presenting as localized muscle swelling. Recognizing this rare imaging pattern is critical for early diagnosis and appropriate management, and contributes to favorable outcomes. Clinicians should be vigilant about atypical irAE presentations during ICI therapy.
