Does Burnout Lead to Poor Organizational Commitment among Prison Officers in Sri Lanka?: Findings from a Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Nimali F. Wijegoonewardene, Janaki P. Vidanapathirana
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Burnout is a recognized risk factor for poor organizational commitment across occupational groups. This has not been explored among Sri Lankan prison officers previously. The aim of this article is to identify whether burnout was an associated risk factor for poor organizational commitment in this occupational group. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 1803 prison correctional and rehabilitation officers in 32 prison institutions across the country. Multistage sampling was used. The “Organizational Commitments Questionnaire” was used following judgmental validation. Risk factors identified through literature were analyzed using bivariate analysis, followed by logistic regression analysis. Three-hundred and sixty-six participants (20.3%) had poor organizational commitment. Burnout was found significant in the bivariate analysis, and in the logistic regression analysis at P < 0.001 (odds ratio: 2.2, 95% confidence interval: 1.7–2.8). Burnout was an associated risk factor for poor organizational commitment in Sri Lankan prison officers. Interventions for employee burnout would contribute to improved organizational commitment.

INTRODUCTION

Burnout can be defined as “a state of physical, emotional and mental exhaustion that results from long-term involvement in work situations that are emotionally demanding.”[1] Burnout has been identified in literature across many occupations to be a predictor for many organizational outcomes including absenteeism, intent for turnover, poor performance, and poor organizational commitment.[234] Organizational commitment is defined as “the relative strength of an individual’s identification with and involvement in a particular organization.”[5]

This study component was carried out to identify whether burnout was an associated risk factor for poor organizational commitment among Sri Lankan prison officers, as it has not been previously studied.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional study was carried out as a component of a larger study on burnout of prison officers in 2017, across 32 prison institutions across the country including closed prisons, remand prisons, work camps, open-prison camps, facilities for youth offenders, and drug abuser inmates and lock-ups. Some of the institutions were selected purposively considering the importance, while the others were selected randomly.

All correctional officers in the rank of prison guards, and rehabilitation officers, working in Sri Lankan prisons were considered the study population. They were the “ground-level” categories of staff with regular and close contact with the inmates. The largest minimum sample size of 1708 for correctional officers was obtained for assessing the prevalence of burnout in prison officers (a previous component), and the same sample size was used for this component. The total number of prison rehabilitation officers (n = 120) was added to this sample, making the total sample size 1828. Officers who had worked in the Sri Lankan prison setting for more than 6 months were included. Those who did not have contact with the inmates for at least 4 days a week, were on maternity or other long-term leave, had a diagnosis of a mental disorder, or could not read and write in Sinhala language were excluded. Correctional officers from each prison institution were selected through multistage sampling, using probability proportionate to sample size and random sampling.

The Sinhala-translated and validated Maslach Burnout Inventory—Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) was used for assessing burnout.[6] The judgmentally validated and Sinhala-translated Organizational Commitments Questionnaire by Mowday and colleagues[57] was used for assessing organizational commitment. A panel of experts assessed the face validity, content validity, and consensual validity of the questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of 15 questions, both positively and negatively worded, with a 7-point Likert scale (1—strongly disagree to 7—strongly agree) and obtainable scores from 15 to 105. The translated Sinhala version was pretested prior to administration.

Ethics clearance for the study was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee, University of Kelaniya. Necessary administrative permissions were obtained. Informed written consent was obtained from the participants after explaining the study. Confidentiality of the collected data was strictly maintained. The questionnaire was administered as a self-administered questionnaire by visiting each of the prison institutions selected.

Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 version. Prevalence of burnout and organizational commitment were calculated. The possible risk factors for organizational commitment were identified through literature,[8910] since one specific model or group of risk factors was not directly related to the work setting of the prison officers. Hence, the identified risk factors were age, gender, level of education, job satisfaction, satisfaction with growth opportunities, job security, burnout, work–family culture, autonomy in the job, variety in the job, and group cohesiveness. Some of those data obtained in the previous study components were useful in gathering the necessary information on these identified risk factors. Accordingly, job satisfaction and job security were directly assessed, satisfaction with growth opportunities was assessed by career prospects, work–family culture was assessed by work–family conflict, autonomy in the job was assessed by ability to take decisions in one’s own job, variety in the job was assessed by the variety at work score and group cohesiveness was assessed with the responses for “job expects individual responsibility rather than group responsibility.”

These identified risk factors were analyzed through bivariate analysis, using Chi-square test and independent samples t-test (for variety at work). The significant risk factors were then entered into a logistic regression (LR) model to identify the independent association of burnout with organizational commitment, in relation to all other confounding factors. In the LR model, the identified risk factors were included as the independent variables, while organizational commitment was added as the dependent variables in a binary form (high and poor organizational commitment).

RESULTS

A total of 1803 prison officers participated in the study (1683 correctional officers and 120 rehabilitation officers), with a response rate of 98.6%. Around two-thirds (64.2%) were between 25 and 44 years. Majority were males (88%) in accordance with the gender distribution of staff working in the prison settings. Many of the participants had passed the advanced-level examination.

According to the cut-off value obtained through validation of MBI-HSS for Sri Lankan prison officers, the true prevalence of burnout was 31.1% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 22.1–40.1). Previous research has used cut-offs for the organizational commitment questionnaire categorizing the scores into three categories, namely, uncommitted (15–52 marks), neutral (53–67 marks), and committed (68–105 marks).[11] For the current study, it was decided to categorize the participants into two categories of “high organizational commitment” and “poor organizational commitment,” with the sores in the neutral group incorporated into those two categories. Hence, the participants were categorized into two categories of “high organizational commitment” and “poor organizational commitment.” Scores from 15 to 59 indicated “low organizational commitment,” while scores from 60 to 105 indicated “high organizational commitment.” Accordingly, 20.3% (n = 366) had poor organizational commitment. The cut-off score for deciding whether the person had high or low organizational commitment was 59.

Age, sex, highest educational qualification, group cohesiveness, and job security were not significantly associated with organizational commitment in the bivariate analysis (P > 0.05) [see Table 1]. The significantly associated variables with organizational commitment in the bivariate analysis were overall job satisfaction, burnout, variety in the work, autonomy at one’s own job, work–family conflict, and career prospects (P < 0.05) [see Table 2]. The association between organizational commitment and variety at work assessed through the t-test showed a significance of P < 0.001 (t = −7.8).

DISCUSSION

Organizational commitment can be defined as “the psychological state of the employee which express that he or she is dedicated to organizational goals and shows high performance to stay within the organization.”[12] It is known that employees who remain in the job with burnout tend to be less productive, less effective, and less committed to the organization, and they can have more conflicts with colleagues and disruption of job tasks.[13] Organizational commitment is an emotional affection to an organization. It signifies the loyalty toward the organization one is working.[14] By measuring organizational commitment, what employees think and feel about their workplace can be identified.[15] One of the most important work-related psychological problems of employees is burnout.[3] Burnout syndrome can significantly reduce the performance of workers.[16] According to Yener and colleagues, the most important problem employees encounter as a result of burnout is a drop in job satisfaction and also a significant decrease in organizational commitment.[3] The emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and the diminishing of personal accomplishment aspects of burnout would be having varying effects on reducing the organizational commitment of employees. Organizational commitment has an important implication for correctional management and organization,[17] since it is related to human services.

The most widely used instrument to measure organizational commitment throughout the world is Mowday, Steers, and Porter’s Organizational Commitments Questionnaire.[1418] The scale has satisfactory predictive, convergent, and discriminant validity, as well as test–retest reliability and internal consistency reliability. This scale was selected to be used in the current study considering these facts.

According to the decided cut-off, a considerable percentage (20.3%) was found to have poor organizational commitment. This needs to be brought to the focus of the authorities. Interventions to improve organizational commitment among the prison employees who are working in close contact with the inmates will benefit the service rendered to the inmates.

Burnout was found to be a significant predictor of poor organizational commitment in the current study. Studies in other professions[315] have found significant associations between burnout and organizational commitment in line with this finding. A study conducted among public and private school teachers in Pakistan has shown that burnout negatively affects their organizational commitment.[15] Further, depersonalization (β = −0.258, P = 0.021) and reduced personal accomplishment (β = −0.255, P = 0.007) were significantly negatively correlated with organizational commitment.[15] Yener and colleagues[3] found that there were significant negative relationships between emotional exhaustion (Pearson’s correlation = −0.494, P < 0.001) and depersonalization (Pearson’s correlation = −0.21, P < 0.001) components of burnout with organizational commitment. This evidence confirms burnout as a predictor of organizational commitment. However, though in some studies the effect of different dimensions of burnout on organizational commitment was specifically shown, in the current study, burnout was assessed as one entity, and thus the significant effect of each dimension could not be unveiled.

Considering the current study findings, organizational changes are thought to be necessary in improving the organizational commitment in the prison employees through reduction of their burnout, and other identified interventions. It would be highly beneficial to the organization, service recipient inmates, and the employees themselves.

The strengths of this study lie in the sample size and the diversity of the study settings used. Hence, the findings could be reasonably generalized to the prison officers working in close contact with inmates across the country. It should be noted that this study was a cross-sectional study, which restricts the ability to comment on causality. However, going in line with the well-identified association of burnout as a risk factor of poor organizational commitment in literature, this study has attempted to prove this association among Sri Lankan prison officers. The study used the possible risk factors identified through literature in carrying out the analyses, and therefore, some of the possible risk factors not identified through literature may have been missed in the model. Future research should focus on identifying the causality of this association through a longitudinal study design and should expand the study to identify the association of different dimensions of burnout with organizational commitment.
