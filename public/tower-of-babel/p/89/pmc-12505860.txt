Fatal recurrent Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) in an Ethiopian male: a case report
Authors: Eden Tesfaye Beyene, Yonas Berhane Tesfay, Yared Getachew Tadesse, Estifanos Bekele Fole
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGuillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is a disease affecting the peripheral nerves, leading to a constellation of motor, sensory, and autonomic symptoms. It typically has a monophasic course, and recurrence is the exception rather than the case, so much is not known about it. We discuss the risk factors, presentation, and course of patients with recurrent Guillain–Barré syndrome.Case presentationA 28-year-old Ethiopian male presented with progressive ascending weakness that started from the lower extremities but spread to involve his respiratory muscles and resulted in respiratory failure. He had a similar but milder presentation 3 years prior and achieved a complete recovery. He was admitted to the intensive care unit where he passed away after 4 days.ConclusionGuillain–Barré syndrome can recur after years of complete recovery, and subsequent episodes tend to be more severe than the initial attack. Young age and lack of comorbidities are not determinants of clinical outcome in patients with recurrent Guillain–Barré syndrome.

Introduction

Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is a neurological disorder where a person’s immune system mistakenly attacks the peripheral nerves with a possible mechanism of molecular mimicry, where the immune system, triggered by an infection, cross-reacts with parts of a nerve [1, 2]. It is among the rare causes of acute paralysis, and even though its cause is not known, it is mostly provoked by a preceding infection [3]. It is more common in adults; however, people of any age can be affected by GBS [1, 3].

Patients with GBS can present with sensations of pins and needles, progressive and symmetric weakness, bowel–bladder dysfunction, and dysautonomia [1, 3, 4]. It can have a mild course or be life-threatening due to the involvement of respiratory muscles [1]. GBS symptoms progress over 2 weeks, with more than 90% of patients reaching a nadir of the disease by 4 weeks after onset [5]. There is no cure for GBS, but supportive care and immunotherapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasma exchange are effective treatments [6]. Functional recovery after GBS is highly variable and depends on individual risk factors. Close to 80% of patients can walk unaided, and more than half will have a complete recovery in the first year [7].

GBS is typically monophasic (a single episode), and recurrence is extremely unusual [7, 8]. Little is known about the presentation and course of recurrent GBS, and management of such patients is a challenge as they are rarely seen. Herein, we report a case of a 28-year-old male from Ethiopia with recurrent GBS.

Patient information

A 28-year-old right-handed Ethiopian male presented to the emergency department with a complaint of progressive ascending weakness that initially started from the lower extremities and involved the upper extremities within 4 days. Two weeks before the current presentation, he had a history of vomiting and watery diarrhea that resolved on its own. Otherwise, he denied recent vaccinations, headache, abnormal body movement, visual disturbances or hearing loss, fever, urinary complaints, shortness of breath, neurological or other comorbidities, and a family history of similar illness or comorbidities.

Three years prior he had a similar presentation of ascending weakness that followed a diarrheal illness. Physical examination then revealed a power of 3/5 in all extremities, areflexia, intact sensation, and negative meningeal signs. He was diagnosed with Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS), acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) type. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis during the first attack showed albuminocytologic dissociation with no growth on culture. Stool culture was negative. Antibody testing was not done for financial reasons. Even though the degree of weakness was at that time similar to the current presentation, the progression was slower in the initial attack. Due to financial reasons, he did not get definitive treatment but was discharged after a 4-day stay with some improvement. He continued follow-up at the neurology clinic and had physiotherapy. Within 6 months, he had a complete recovery and was back to doing his daily activities. He worked in a cement factory as a site engineer and lived with his parents. His parents did not have any medical illnesses. He was an only child.

Clinical findings

Upon arrival at the hospital, his vitals were stable. The Glasgow Coma Scale score was 15, and pupils were mid-sized and reactive. Cranial nerves were intact. Power was 3/5 and 4/5 in the proximal and distal muscle groups of the lower and upper extremities, respectively. He had absent deep tendon reflexes and was hypotonic in the lower extremities. Superficial reflexes were normal. The plantar reflex was downward. Sensation was intact, and meningeal signs were negative. The single breath count ranged between 21 and 28. Other physical examinations were unremarkable, and the patient was immediately transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). As depicted in Fig. 1, 36 hours after ICU admission, he started having dysautonomia with fluctuating blood pressure, pulse rate, and diaphoresis. Power became 1/5 in all extremities. The single breath count dropped to 6, and the patient was intubated. Diagnostic assessment

Lumbar puncture showed a cell count of 6/µL, glucose of 4 mmol/L, and protein of 0.9 g/L. CSF culture was negative. Other laboratory tests, such as renal function tests, liver function tests, and lipid panels, were within normal ranges.

The nerve conduction study (NCS) (Tables 1, 2, and 3) showed moderate-to-severely reduced compound motor action potential (CMAP) with greater decrement in the lower extremity, with moderately prolonged motor distal latencies and slowed conduction velocities. Sural sensory response was normal. F and H waves were absent. These findings were consistent with AMAN-type GBS with secondary demyelinating features. His previous NCS study (Tables 4, 5, and 6), during the first attack, also showed similar findings to the current one, except the distal latencies and conduction velocity were preserved. Four days after ICU admission, the patient developed high-grade fever and leukocytosis. He also required increased mechanical ventilator support. With the assumption of ventilator-associated pneumonia, he was started on broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics but did not respond. His Glasgow Coma Scale score became 3 T (E1V1M1) without sedation. He died 1 week after admission to the ICU.

Discussion and conclusion

Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is a heterogeneous disorder that comprises several phenotypes, electrophysiological features, and outcomes. More than 95% of patients with GBS experience a monophasic course, and less than 5% of cases have a documented recurrence [8]. It is suggested that patients with recurrent GBS may have related clinical symptoms in subsequent episodes while having similar or different triggering events, but the pathogenesis of recurrent GBS remains largely unknown. [9]. There have been only a few published cases of recurrent GBS in people aged less than 30 years and even fewer in adults over the age of 30 years [7, 9].

A recurrence is defined as two or more episodes that fulfill the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke (NINCDS) criteria for GBS, with a minimum time between episodes of 2 months (when fully recovered in between) or 4 months (when only partially recovered). It is important to distinguish between recurrent GBS and GBS with treatment-related fluctuations (GBS-TRF) or chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy with acute onset (A-CIDP), especially because the treatment regimen for CIDP is different. GBS-TRF is described as clinical deterioration after initial improvement following treatment within 2 months of presentation (which was not the case in our patient) and with A-CIDP, though the clinical course, NCS, and CSF findings point toward GBS recurrence, it is very difficult to accurately differentiate between the two [10]. Recurrences occur more frequently in young adults with mild symptoms and in those patients with the Miller Fischer variant [8].

Our patient was a 28-year-old male who, after his first attack, had spontaneous recovery within 6 months, and following an interval of 3 years, had a second attack of GBS and took IVIG, the mechanism of which is not well understood, but it serves as an immune modulator, including neutralization of antibodies, inhibition of complement, and saturation of Fc receptors [11]. The inter-episode interval in our patient was comparable to the mean of previous studies in which the lag time between two episodes was 4 months to 10 years [7, 12]. There is no strong evidence indicating that not getting treatment for the first GBS episode is directly related to recurrence. His first GBS phenotype was found to be the AMAN variant, which developed after a diarrheal episode, and so was the second attack. In line with our patient, prior gastrointestinal or respiratory infection in the first episode and the AMAN variant are associated with recurrence [12]. Most studies show that recurrence is common in patients under 30 years of age and tends to be severe in subsequent episodes, especially in the elderly [8, 13, 14].

The clinical characteristics of the recurrent episodes are usually similar, although some cases showed differences in relation to the severity of the episode [9]. Our patient also had a severe kind of clinical presentation with a fast progression of weakness as opposed to his previous attack. In relation to cerebrospinal fluid analysis, some authors have shown that there is an increase in protein with normal cell counts in the first episode and recurrent episodes; nevertheless, the protein increase is higher in recurrent episodes compared with the first, and some authors have reported cell count increases during recurrent episodes [15, 16]. Studies have shown that neurophysiologic abnormalities could be more intense in recurrent episodes [17, 18] but our patient had a similar NCS finding (AMAN variant) with the same degree of severity and pattern of affection (lower > upper), albeit with a fast clinical progression.

Even though this is a single report with diagnostic and management challenges such as lack of antibody testing and treatment with IVIG, this case illustrates that GBS recurrence can be fatal even in young, healthy patients, and though it is a rare phenomenon, both clinicians and patients should be aware that GBS can happen again even after years of full recovery.
