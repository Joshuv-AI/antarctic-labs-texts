Sotagliflozin improves cognitive deficits and attenuates neuroinflammation of Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Yuye Ning, Meilin Chen, Hao Yang, Jianping Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Effective treatments for Alzheimer's disease (AD) are limited. Due to shared pathological mechanisms between AD and diabetes, antidiabetic drugs like sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) are potential therapeutic options. Although SGLT2is have shown cognitive benefits in diabetes models, their effects in AD models are not fully established. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic effects of sotagliflozin (Sota), an SGLT2i, in both in vivo and in vitro AD models. The network pharmacology analysis was used to predict the potential targets and pathways of Sota. And we selected 6-month-old APP/PS1 transgenic mice to investigate the effects of Sota. Cognitive function was assessed using the Morris water maze test. Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence were employed to quantify amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaque deposition in the hippocampal or cortex and analyze neuronal loss. Additionally, amyloid β oligomers induction and microglial cells were used to evaluate the effects of Sota on the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and to investigate the underlying mechanisms. In vivo, Sota treatment improved cognitive impairments, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines, inhibited microglial activation, and promoted neuronal survival. In vitro, Sota mitigated Aß oligomer-induced toxicity in microglial cells by decreasing reactive oxygen species and pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Mechanistically, Sota treatment was associated with suppression of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling. Our findings suggest that Sota improved cognitive impairment and attenuates neuroinflammation in AD. Sota may be a promising candidate for the treatment of AD.

Introduction

As the global population ages, Alzheimer's disease (AD) is increasing at an unprecedented rate. Currently, it is estimated that approximately 50 million people worldwide are living with AD. This number is projected to triple, reaching 150 million by 2050 [1]. AD is a common progressive neurodegenerative disorder. Its neuropathological features include intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein and extracellular amyloid plaques (Aβ) [2]. Other pathological changes in AD include neuronal loss, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation [3]. The disease has a significant personal, familial, and societal impact, both emotionally and economically. Currently, there is no cure for AD, and available medications only offer limited symptom control. As a result, there is increasing interest in repurposing existing medications, including anti-diabetic drugs, to alleviate the early risk of AD [4].

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a common risk factor for AD in adults [5], and anti-diabetic treatments have been shown to improve the condition of AD patients. This highlights the connection between AD and T2DM and underscores the potential of repurposing anti-diabetic drugs. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) are a new class of anti-diabetic drugs that inhibit glucose reabsorption in the proximal renal tubules, inducing glucosuria and thereby reducing hyperglycemia [6]. SGLT2i can reduce the burden of cardiovascular risk factors, including T2DM and hypertension, which may have a positive impact on neurodegenerative diseases [7]. Several preclinical studies have shown that SGLT2i have potential benefits in alleviating AD-related neuropathophysiology and improving cognitive dysfunction [[8], [9], [10]]. In an AD and T2DM mixed mice model, studies on empagliflozin demonstrated that treatment significantly reduced Aβ levels, lowered the density of amyloid plaques in the cortex and hypothalamus, reduced neuronal loss, and improved cognitive function [9]. Clinical evidence also suggests that SGLT2i may offer potential benefits in mitigating cognitive decline and reducing dementia risk [[11], [12], [13]]. A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the impact of novel anti-diabetic drugs on dementia risk found that SGLT2i users had a lower risk of all-cause dementia compared to non-users [14]. While SGLT2i may be beneficial for neurodegenerative diseases in T2DM patients, there is currently no theoretical basis for their direct neuroprotective effects. A recent mendelian randomization study using genetic proxies for SGLT2i, defined by SLC5A2 expression and HbA1c-lowering effects, reported that genetically proxied SGLT2i was associated with a lower risk of AD [15]. This finding provides complementary genetic evidence supporting further investigation of SGLT2i in AD-related models.

Sotagliflozin (Sota) is a novel SGLT2i that received FDA approval for market use on May 26, 2023. It is indicated for reducing the risk of cardiovascular death, hospitalization due to heart failure, and emergency visits in heart failure patients, as well as for the prevention of T2DM and chronic kidney disease [16]. Currently, there are no studies investigating the potential neuroprotective mechanisms of Sota in AD. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of Sota on learning and memory functions in APP/PS1 mice, as well as its effects on cerebral Aβ deposition. Additionally, it sought to investigate the potential neuroprotective role of Sota in mitigating Aβ oligomer-induced toxicity in BV2 microglial cells. The findings of this study may provide critical scientific insights to support the development of novel therapeutic strategies for the prevention and treatment of AD.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Sotagliflozin (LX-4211, purity >99%) was procured from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA), and Aβ 1–42 oligomer powder was sourced from China Peptides Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Key experimental kits were supplied by Beyotime (Shanghai, China), including the BCA Protein Assay Kit and Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay Kit was ordered form NCM Biotech (Suzhou, China). Mice ELISA kits for tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) were obtained from Shanghai Jianglai Industry Co., Ltd. Reagents for immunofluorescence, such as blocking goat serum, Triton X-100, DAPI nuclear stain, and anti-quenching sealer, were provided by Solarbio (Beijing, China). Primary antibodies for glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK), and P38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (P38 MAPK) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China), while phospho-specific antibodies for JNK (P-JNK), ERK (P-ERK), and P38 (P–P38) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA). Ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1) antibody was sourced from Invitrogen (CA, USA), and antibodies for Aβ 1–42 and NeuN were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Secondary antibodies for western blotting and immunohistochemistry were also obtained from Abcam, while those for immunofluorescence were provided by Proteintech.

Animals and treatment

Six-month-old male APP/PS1 and wild-type (WT) littermate mice (weighing 28–32 g) were obtained from Viewsolid Biotechnology (Certificate Number: SCXK 2021-0008). All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Publication No. 85-23, revised 1985) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval Number: VS212601352). APP/PS1 and WT littermate mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room with a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. The mice were divided into four groups (n = 8 per group): (a) Wild-type (WT) group, (b) APP/PS1 group, (c) APP/PS1 + Sota (10 mg/kg) group, and (d) APP/PS1 + Sota (30 mg/kg) group. Sota was prepared in a suspension containing 10% DMSO in corn oil and administered orally at 10 mg/kg or 30 mg/kg once daily for two months. The WT and APP/PS1 control groups received an equal volume of the vehicle solution. After behavioral testing, the mice were euthanized. Their brains were carefully removed, rinsed with cold saline (0.9% NaCl), and immediately bisected. Morris water maze (MWM)

The experimental protocol for the MWM test was based on previously published methods [17]. The MWM consisted of a circular tank with a diameter of 100 cm and a height of 60 cm. A submerged platform (10 cm in diameter) was placed in one quadrant (W, defined as the second quadrant). Four starting points (E, W, N, S) were evenly distributed around the tank's edge, marked with distinct visual cues, including a star, triangle, square, and circle. The tank was divided into four equal quadrants, and the water level was adjusted to 0.5 cm above the platform to ensure it was not visible to the mice. Water temperature was maintained at 20 ± 1 °C, and room temperature was kept at approximately 22 ± 1 °C. To acclimate the mice to the environment, they were transferred to the behavioral testing room one week before the experiment. Platform learning trials were conducted over five consecutive days. Each mouse was individually placed into the water from one of the designated starting points, and its swimming behavior was recorded using a camera connected to a tracking system. The time taken for the mouse to locate and climb onto the platform was recorded as the escape latency, and swimming paths were tracked. Each training day included four trials per mouse. Mice unable to find the platform within 60 s were assigned a default escape latency of 60 s, and the system automatically stopped recording. In such cases, the mice were guided to the platform by the experimenter and allowed to remain on the platform for 20 s before resting for at least 1 min prior to the next trial. On the sixth day, the platform was removed for a 60-s probe trial to evaluate spatial memory. The time spent in the target quadrant (where the platform was previously located) and the number of times the mouse crossed the platform's previous location were recorded as measures of spatial memory performance. Behavioral data, including swimming paths and escape latency, were analyzed using the SMART3 video-tracking software (Panlab, Harvard, USA).

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Three mice from each group were selected for brain tissue analysis. The brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by a gradient dehydration process before paraffin embedding. Coronal sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared, deparaffinized, hydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using 0.01 M citrate buffer. After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated in 3% H2O2 for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. To block nonspecific binding, the sections were incubated with 5% goat serum for 1 h. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (anti-Aβ 1–42, Abcam, ab201060). After three PBS washes, the sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h and then visualized using a DAB kit. Images were captured using a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Three random non-overlapping fields of view per section were selected, and the number of senile plaques in each field was quantified using ImageJ software (Image Pro Plus, USA).

For tissue immunofluorescence, tissue sections were washed three times with 1 × PBS to remove OCT and blocked for 60 min at room temperature with 10% BSA in PBS to prevent nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies (Iba-1, Invitrogen, MA527726, and NeuN, abcam, ab177487) were added, and the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the sections were washed three times with 1 × PBS, followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. After three additional PBS washes, the sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Network pharmacology

The chemical structure of Sota was obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and potential targets of Sota were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction (http://SwissTargetPrediction.ch/). Information about AD-related target genes was collected from the online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://omim.org/) and GeneCards databases (https://www.genecards.org/). A Venn diagram was generated to identify overlapping genes. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database and subsequently imported into Cytoscape 3.10.1 for further analysis and visualization. The Centiscape 2.2 plugin was used to identify key targets based on three topological features (degree, betweenness, and closeness). The network was arranged and optimized based on degree values. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database to elucidate the core mechanisms and pathways of Sota's effects against AD. Finally, molecular docking of candidate proteins with Sota was performed using AutoDock Vina software, and docking results were visualized using PyMOL software.

Cell culture

BV2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (100 mg/mL). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM every two days. When the cells reached 80–90% confluency, they were detached using 0.25% trypsin, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS. The resuspended cells were then seeded into new culture dishes. For subsequent experiments, the cells were pretreated with the culture medium for 2 h before drug treatment.

Cell viability assay

The Aβ42 oligomer powder was dissolved in DMSO and stored at −80 °C until use. BV2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well and cultured overnight. The cells were then incubated for 24 h in medium containing various concentrations of Sota or Aβ42 oligomers. To assess the protective effect of Sota against Aβ42 oligomer-induced cytotoxicity, cells were pretreated with Sota for 2 h, followed by co-incubation with Aβ42 oligomers for 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh culture medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Finland) to evaluate cell viability. Live cell activity was determined by comparing absorbance values to those of the control group.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) staining

The effect of Sota on intracellular ROS levels was assessed using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA, following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, BV2 cells were treated as specified and then incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with 1 × PBS. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. The intensity of the green fluorescence was directly proportional to the intracellular ROS levels.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Mice's blood was collected via orbital extraction and centrifuged to obtain the serum supernatant. BV2 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 3 × 106 cells per well in culture medium. The cells were pretreated with Sota for 2 h, followed by stimulation with Aβ42 oligomers for 24 h. The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in mice serum and BV2 cell supernatants were measured using ELISA kits, following the standard protocols provided by the manufacturer.

Quantitative Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using a total RNA isolation kit (Tiangen) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial kit (Takara). Amplification of cDNA was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Relative mRNA levels were normalized to GAPDH expression in the same samples. Data analysis was performed using the 2ˆ-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference gene.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from brain tissue and BV2 cells. Tissue and cells were lysed in RIPA buffer to isolate total protein. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Protein samples were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in 1 × Tris-buffered saline with Tween (TBST) at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including GAPDH, JNK, P-JNK, ERK, P-ERK, P38, and P–P38. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Protein abundance was detected using an ECL detection system. Band intensities were analyzed with ImageJ software, and protein expression levels were normalized to the corresponding GAPDH expression in each sample.

Statistical analysis

All values are presented as mean ± SEM. Group differences in target distance during the Morris water maze test were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with repeated measures, with treatment and training days as factors. Other data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by post hoc Bonferroni tests to identify group differences. All experimental results were derived from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Prism Software, CA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sota improved cognitive deficits

To evaluate the effects of Sota on learning and memory in APP/PS1 model mice, we conducted the MWM test. After the 5-day training period, all mice successfully learned the task. Representative swimming paths are shown in Fig. 1 b. Compared to vehicle-treated APP/PS1 mice, Sota-treated APP/PS1 mice demonstrated improved cognitive performance. During the platform learning trials, the distance to the target platform and escape latency significantly decreased over time in the Sota-treated group (Fig. 1c and d), indicating a faster learning rate compared to untreated model mice. In the spatial probe trial phase, APP/PS1 mice spent less time in the target quadrant and showed fewer platform site crossings than control mice. However, Sota treatment increased the number of platform crossings (Fig. 1e) and the time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 1f) compared to the AD model group, suggesting that Sota treatment alleviated cognitive deficits in APP/PS1 mice.

Sota reduced AD burden

To investigate the effects of Sota treatment on Aβ deposition in APP/PS1 mice [18], immunohistochemical staining was performed using a specific antibody against Aβ1-42. The results showed a significant increase in plaque formation in the hippocampus and cortex of APP/PS1 mice compared to their WT littermates. Notably, Sota treatment reduced Aβ deposition compared to untreated APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that Sota effectively alleviates Aβ burden in APP/PS1 mice, potentially contributing to its protective effects against AD pathology. To assess the effects of Sota on neuronal loss in the APP/PS1 mice, immunofluorescence staining was performed (Fig. 3). The results showed that Sota treatment led to a significant increase in NeuN-positive cells, indicating improved neuronal survival. Sota inhibited inflammation response in mice model

ELISA results demonstrated an increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the serum of AD model mice. However, Sota treatment significantly suppressed the expression of these cytokines. Specifically, TNF-α levels decreased by 39% in the 10 mg/kg Sota-treated group and by 44% in the 30 mg/kg Sota-treated group. Similarly, IL-1β expression was reduced by 39% in the 10 mg/kg group and by 60% in the 30 mg/kg group. The pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 showed a 39% reduction in the 10 mg/kg group and a 60% reduction in the 30 mg/kg group compared to the AD model mice (Fig. 4 a-c). Additionally, Iba1 plays a crucial role in regulating microglial immune function and serves as a specific marker for microglia. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed an increased number of Iba1-positive cells in the hippocampus and cortex of AD model mice, indicating microglial activation. However, Sota treatment significantly reduced the number of Iba1-positive cells (Fig. 4d). Next, BV2 cells were pretreated with various concentrations of Sota for 2 h, followed by co-incubation with 10 μM Aβ42 for 24 h. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that 20 μM Sota effectively reversed Aβ42-induced cytotoxicity in BV2 cells (Fig. 5c). Based on these findings, we selected 20 μM Sota and 10 μM Aβ1-42 for subsequent studies in BV2 cells. Furthermore, we evaluated the effects of Aβ42-induced oxidative stress by measuring intracellular ROS levels using fluorescent staining. The results demonstrated that Sota pretreatment significantly reduced ROS levels in BV2 cells (Fig. 5 d). Additionally, in BV2 microglial cells, pretreatment with Sota (10 μM) for 2 h before Aβ42 stimulation resulted in significantly lower levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β compared to the Aβ42 stimulation group (Fig. 5 e-g). Network pharmacology showed the potential targets of sota

This study employed network pharmacology and molecular docking to predict and validate the potential targets of Sota. Using the SwissTargetPrediction platform, we identified 99 potential targets of Sota. We then retrieved and merged AD-related targets from the GeneCards and OMIM databases, yielding a total of 2962 targets. After cross-referencing the datasets, we identified 60 common targets shared between AD and Sota (Fig. 6 a). These common targets were further analyzed using PPI analysis (Fig. 6b). The GO enrichment analysis identified the top 10 biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) associated with these targets (Fig. 6c). The findings suggest that the relevant gene products play a key role in regulating cell signaling, phosphorylation, and membrane-related functions. These gene products are involved in the cellular response to oxidative stress, promoting self-phosphorylation, regulating ERK1/ERK2 cascade reactions, and controlling critical pathways for cell proliferation and survival. The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 6d), sorted by p-value and target count, highlighted the MAPK pathway as a critical pathway potentially regulated by Sota in AD. Molecular docking results indicated that Sota may bind to MAPK3 (also known as ERK1), a key member of the MAPK family, which, together with MAPK1 (ERK2), is widely involved in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and stress responses [19]. Sota treatment was associated with suppression of ERK signaling

Network pharmacology analysis indicated a strong association between the MAPK pathway and Sota treatment in AD. Based on this finding, we further explored the potential mechanisms using Western blotting in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Among the major MAPK, three primary subfamilies are widely recognized: JNK, P38, and ERK [20]. To further investigate the effects of Sota on these kinases, we performed Western blot analysis to detect changes in their expression. The results revealed that in BV2 cells, Aβ42 oligomer stimulation led to significant activation of BV2 cells, marked by an increase in P-ERK expression. However, Sota pretreatment effectively inhibited ERK signaling activation. Furthermore, although Aβ42 oligomers activate the JNK and p38 pathways to promote inflammation, Sota did not inhibit the P-JNK or P–P38 in the MAPK pathway (Fig. 7a). Discussion

In this study, Sota significantly alleviated cognitive dysfunction in APP/PS1 mice and reduced Aβ deposition. Additionally, Sota pretreatment significantly reduced cell damage in BV2 cells, decreasing intracellular ROS production and pro-inflammation markers. Based on the network pharmacology results, Sota may improve neuronal inflammation by inhibiting ERK activation, while showing no significant effect on the JNK and P38 MAPK signaling pathways. Overall, these findings suggest that Sota holds promise as an effective drug candidate for the treatment of AD.

SGLT2 is a glucose transporter primarily expressed in the proximal renal tubules [21]. However, it is also widely distributed in the central nervous system, particularly in the hippocampus and cerebellum, where it is involved in learning, appetite regulation, energy balance, and autonomic cardiovascular control [22]. Research suggests that SGLT2is promote neuronal survival through multiple mechanisms. One key mechanism is the inhibition of neuroinflammation, which is closely linked to cognitive impairment. A study by Kim et al. demonstrated that a 30-day SGLT2i treatment in patients with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk significantly suppressed macrophage NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This, in turn, inhibited the NLRP3/IL-1/TNF-α/miR-501-3p/ZO-1 axis, thereby reducing neuroinflammation, beyond its glucose-lowering effects [23]. Although Aβ deposition is a hallmark event in AD pathology, chronic neuroinflammation is also a key pathological feature and is strongly associated with Aβ accumulation in the AD brain [24]. In fact, neuroinflammatory markers can often be detected before the onset of neurodegenerative biomarkers and clinical symptoms, indicating that neuroinflammation plays a driving role in AD pathogenesis rather than being a secondary bystander response [25]. Alongside Aβ plaques and intracellular NFTs, neuroinflammation has been recognized as the third core feature of AD pathology, potentially linking these two pathological processes [26,27]. During the early stages of AD, immune cells release anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, IL-4, IL-13) to repair tissue and regulate inflammation. However, as the disease progresses, a state of chronic inflammation emerges, characterized by the transformation of microglia into an activated phenotype, which leads to increased ROS production and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, and TNF-α [28]. Neuroinflammation is primarily driven by abnormal activation of glial cells. Aβ aggregates stimulate surrounding microglia to secrete neurotoxic mediators and inflammatory cytokines, which further activate more microglia and astrocytes, exacerbating neurotoxicity [29]. This compromises their ability to phagocytose and degrade Aβ, thereby accelerating Aβ accumulation, promoting tau pathology, and ultimately leading to neuronal death, both in vivo and in vitro. Consequently, inhibiting glial activation has been considered a key therapeutic strategy in AD treatment [[30], [31], [32]]. Our results show that Sota treatment reduced Iba1 expression in the hippocampus and cortex, indicating that Sota suppresses glial activation and mitigates neuroinflammation. In our study, Sota not only decreased activated microglia and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) in mouse serum, but also lowered Aβ levels in APP/PS1 mice. This suggests that Sota may reduce neuroinflammation, subsequently leading to a reduction in Aβ production. Previous evidence has shown that activated microglia surround Aβ plaques in AD brains, suggesting that microglia may initially exert a neuroprotective role through Aβ phagocytosis [33]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that in 8-month-old APP/PS1 mice, a significant number of amyloid plaques were already present, particularly in the cortex, much more than that in the hippocampus. These plaques were smaller in size compared to those in older AD mice, but were accompanied by greater microglial activation and fewer surviving neurons. Sota treatment reduced Aβ plaque deposition, provided neuroprotection, and attenuated microglial activation in both the cortex and hippocampus. These findings suggest that Sota may alleviate Aβ burden in APP/PS1 mice by promoting glial-mediated Aβ clearance.

This study builds on network pharmacology findings to further explore the therapeutic potential and mechanisms of Sota in AD. Network pharmacology analysis indicated a strong association between the MAPK signaling pathway and Sota's effects in AD treatment. Based on this, we discovered that Sota significantly reversed ERK phosphorylation in the MAPK pathway within the brain tissue of APP/PS1 mice. However, in vitro experiments revealed that Sota did not affect JNK or p38 expression in the MAPK pathway. The MAPK signaling pathway is a crucial intracellular network present in all eukaryotic organisms, responding to various physical and chemical stimuli to regulate key cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, migration, survival, stress response, inflammation, and apoptosis. Due to its central role in cellular regulation, MAPK signaling is a critical therapeutic target in disease states [34]. Research has shown that MAPK signaling mediates neuroinflammatory responses and is closely linked to AD pathogenesis. Aβ accumulation promotes the M1 phenotype pro-inflammatory phenotype of microglia while reducing the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby activating ERK, JNK, and p38 pathways to amplify inflammation [35]. In 5xFAD mouse models, acutely isolated microglia exhibit significant ERK phosphorylation, which is strongly associated with AD-related gene expression and several AD risk genes. Furthermore, proteomic analyses of postmortem human brain tissues have demonstrated that increased ERK1 and ERK2 expression correlates with neuropathological grading, providing evidence of ERK pathway activation in AD. These findings highlight ERK phosphorylation in microglia as a key regulator to modulate microglial inflammation and contributes to disease progression [36]. ERK1/2 phosphorylation induces c-FOS synthesis, which dimerizes with c-Jun N-terminal kinase to form Activator Protein-1, a key transcription factor in the inflammatory response. Both ERK1/2 and c-FOS activation promote the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that inhibiting ERK1/2 can attenuate inflammation [37,38]. Our study demonstrated that Sota treatment significantly suppressed ERK1/2 pathway activation, thereby reducing ERK1/2-mediated inflammatory responses. To further investigate its anti-inflammatory effects, we established an Aβ oligomer (AβOs)-induced BV2 microglial model, a well-established in vitro model for evaluating drug effects on neuroinflammation. Activated microglia, characterized by branched processes and enlarged cell morphology, release various pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6, ultimately leading to neuronal damage in AD brains. Our results revealed that Sota significantly inhibited AβOs-induced BV2 microglial activation, preserving their resting morphology. Additionally, qPCR and ELISA analyses showed a marked reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, further supporting Sota's anti-inflammatory effects. In vivo, Sota treatment also reduced inflammation and microglial activation in a dose-dependent manner.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although Sota treatment reduced ERK phosphorylation in vivo and in vitro, the current study did not include gain-of-function or loss-of-function experiments to establish a causal role of ERK signaling. Therefore, the mechanistic involvement of ERK should be interpreted cautiously. Second, BV2 cells are widely used for mechanistic screening of microglial inflammatory responses. However, as an immortalized cell line, they do not fully recapitulate the molecular and functional characteristics of primary microglia. In addition, the concentrations of Sota used in vitro, particularly 20 μM, may be higher than clinically relevant plasma exposure levels. Therefore, the in vitro findings should be interpreted as preliminary mechanistic evidence and require further validation in primary microglia and pharmacologically relevant exposure settings. Third, although Sota reduced Aβ plaque burden in APP/PS1 mice, the current study did not distinguish whether this effect resulted from altered Aβ production, aggregation, or clearance. Future studies examining APP processing, Aβ degradation pathways, and microglial phagocytosis are needed. Fourth, only male APP/PS1 mice were included in the current study, and sex-specific effects were not evaluated. Fifth, pharmacokinetic analyses were not performed, and brain penetration of Sota was not directly assessed. Therefore, whether the observed central effects result from direct CNS exposure or indirect peripheral mechanisms remains to be clarified. Sixth, although Sota is a dual SGLT1/2 inhibitor, the present study does not establish whether its protective effects in AD-related models are strictly SGLT2-dependent. Therefore, the observed effects may involve SGLT2-dependent mechanisms, SGLT1-related effects, off-target actions, or indirect systemic regulation of inflammatory and metabolic pathways. Seventh, although body weight was monitored during treatment, blood glucose levels were not measured before or after sotagliflozin administration. Moreover, high-dose Sota was associated with lower body weight at selected time points (Fig. S1 in the supplementary appendix). Therefore, the contribution of glycemic regulation or other systemic metabolic effects cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the present study assessed Iba1-positive microglial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokines. However, these markers do not fully define the spectrum of microglial phenotypes in AD. Therefore, whether Sota merely suppresses pro-inflammatory microglial activation or also promotes reparative microglial phenotypes remains unclear. To contextualize the animal dosing regimen, the mouse doses of 10 and 30 mg/kg correspond to approximate human equivalent doses of 0.81 and 2.43 mg/kg, respectively, based on body surface area conversion. For a 60-kg adult, these values correspond to approximately 49 and 146 mg/day. Although these estimates are below or near the lower range of clinically used sotagliflozin doses, direct clinical extrapolation should be made cautiously because pharmacokinetic exposure, comorbidities, renal function, and drug tolerability may differ substantially between young adult mice and elderly patients with AD.

Conclusion

Sota exhibits neuroprotective properties by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS. In APP/PS1 mice, Sota effectively mitigated AD pathology, including Aβ accumulation and neuronal loss. These findings suggest that Sota holds promise as a potential therapeutic strategy for AD.
