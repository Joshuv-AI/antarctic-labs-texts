Persistent hiccups following endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration
Authors: Monika Saran, Mithlesh Kushwah, Girish Sindhwani, Prasan Kumar Panda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is a minimally invasive diagnostic procedure widely used in modern pulmonary medicine. Major complications are rare and usually hemorrhagic or infectious, while neurological sequelae are exceptionally uncommon. We report a rare case of persistent hiccups following EBUS-TBNA in a middle-aged man evaluated for fever and necrotic mediastinal lymphadenopathy with a probable diagnosis of tuberculosis or invasive fungal diseases. Despite extensive investigations, including cross-sectional imaging and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, no structural abnormalities such as mediastinitis or pleuritis were identified. The patient's hiccups were severe, interfered with sleep, and persistent despite oral intake of baclofen for 5 days; however, resolved within 2 days of combination therapy with baclofen and chlorpromazine. This case highlights that hiccups, although uncommon, may occur as a direct and debilitating iatrogenic complication of EBUS-TBNA, likely due to mechanical stimulation of mediastinal neural structures. Clinicians should be aware of this potential complication, particularly when hiccups occur immediately after the procedure.Keywords: Transbronchial needle aspiration, Persistent hiccups, Vagus nerve irritation, Neurogenic complication, Mediastinal lymphadenopathy

Background

Hiccups are generally benign and self-limiting, but when they persist for more than 48 hours, they are defined as persistent hiccups [1]. Intractable hiccups, lasting longer than one month, may lead to exhaustion, insomnia, malnutrition, and significant impairment in quality of life [2].

Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is a well-established diagnostic tool for mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy. It allows real-time ultrasound guidance to accurately sample lesions for cytological and histological diagnosis. It is highly effective and carries a favorable safety profile, making it the preferred option over more invasive surgical procedures [3]. Large-scale audits and meta-analysis, including a nationwide Japanese survey, confirm its safety, with reported complications limited to infection, hemorrhage, and pneumomediastinum [4,5].

Hiccups result from involuntary contractions of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, followed by abrupt glottic closure, producing the characteristic sound of singultus. The hiccup reflex arc involves afferent pathways (vagus and phrenic nerves, sympathetic chains), central processing centers (brainstem and medulla), and efferent outputs (phrenic and accessory nerves to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles). Irritation at any level of this circuit can trigger hiccups [6]. While the precise pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, both peripheral and central mechanisms have been implicated [5]. Procedure-related hiccups are rare but have been described following endotracheal intubation, gastroscopy, and sedative use, particularly midazolam [7]. Persistent hiccups following EBUS-TBNA are exceptionally uncommon, with no previous reports in the literature, to best of our knowledge.

Here, we report a rare case of persistent hiccups occurring immediately after EBUS-TBNA, outlining the clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, and successful management.

Case report

A man in his late 30s was transferred from the psychiatry department to internal medicine for evaluation of persistent high-grade fever. He had initially been admitted under a provisional diagnosis of schizophrenia with catatonia, presenting with a 3-month history of psychomotor retardation, delusion of persecution followed by mutism. His psychiatric symptoms improved significantly following a Lorazepam Challenge Test and subsequently, he was maintained on oral clobazam for agitation. However, no anti-psychotic medications such as aripiprazole were used.

The patient reported nocturnal fever for 2 months, associated with chills, without localizing symptoms but a significant family history for pulmonary tuberculosis. Initially, urine and blood cultures were negative, and a tropical fever panel was unremarkable. Empirical antibiotics (ceftriaxone) were administered and modified subsequently, but symptoms persisted.

Thoracic Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomography (CECT) revealed multiple enlarged, conglomerated mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes with central necrosis, along with tree-in-bud opacities in the lung parenchyma (Fig. 1A–B).

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was done under local anaesthesia with possible differentials of fungal and tubercular etiology, which demonstrated an elevated galactomannan level (2.41 Optical Density index, considered positive if > 0.5), consistent with invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. Cartridge-Based Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (CBNAAT) for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was weakly positive. Other BAL studies were negative for bacterial or viral pathogens. EBUS-TBNA was performed targeting right sided subcarinal necrotic lymph nodes for histological confirmation of suspected co-infections and to exclude lymphoma (Fig. 2A–C). The procedure was uneventful.

Within 1 hour of EBUS-TBNA, the patient developed severe, continuous hiccups that interfered with sleep and oral intake, persisted beyond 48 hours. Initial therapy with oral baclofen (10 mg three times daily) and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for 5 days was ineffective. Serum electrolytes were within normal limits (Table 1).

For psychiatric illness: oral clobazam for agitation was continued along with treatment for schizophrenia with catatonia as per psychiatry team.

The patient's hospital course was notable for hemodynamic stability and the resolution of hiccups and fever. He was discharged in stable condition on post-procedure day 15. At the one-month outpatient follow-up, he was afebrile, demonstrated adequate weight gain, and his psychosis was stable. His medications, including voriconazole and anti-tubercular therapy with follow up serum voriconazole levels of 1.53 mcg/ml (as voriconazole-rifampicin has significant drug-drug interaction, serum voriconazole levels were measured during follow up at regular intervals [8]), were continued, with ongoing follow-up coordinated through the psychiatry department.

Discussion

EBUS-TBNA enables precise sampling of mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes under real-time ultrasound guidance, facilitating both cytological and histological diagnosis [3]. While generally safe, with rare potential adverse effects such as pneumomediastinum, lymph node abscess, massive hemoptysis, severe cough, and pneumothorax, along with rare neurological sequelae may occur [4]. Persistent hiccups, though often considered benign, can significantly impair quality of life when unrelenting, causing sleep disturbance, nutritional deficits, and psychological distress [5].

A crucial step in management is the exclusion of other post-procedural complications, particularly mediastinitis, pneumomediastinum, esophageal perforation, or pleural inflammation, all of which are well-documented but uncommon complications of EBUS-TBNA [3,7]. In this case, extensive imaging and endoscopy ruled out these structural abnormalities, suggesting a neurogenic mechanism as the most likely cause.

The hiccup reflex arc involves afferent pathways, including the vagus and phrenic nerves, central processing centers in the brainstem and medulla, and efferent outputs through the diaphragm and intercostal muscles [5]. Direct iatrogenic irritation of this arc during needle aspiration provides a plausible pathophysiological explanation. Specifically, the vagus nerve and esophageal plexus course near the subcarinal lymph node station, one of the most commonly sampled sites during EBUS-TBNA. Mechanical stimulation from the needle, or local perineural irritation caused by aspiration of a necrotic lymph node, could transiently alter/lower the firing threshold of the nerve without producing detectable inflammatory or hemorrhagic changes [9].

Previous reports support the notion of neurogenic complications after EBUS-TBNA. Niwas et al. described vasovagal shock following EUS-B-NA, illustrating that periprocedural neural stimulation can lead to clinically significant events [7]. Similarly, rare cases of hiccups have been described in sarcoidosis or other mediastinal pathologies, indicating that both disease- and procedure-related irritation of mediastinal nerves can manifest as intractable hiccups [10]. Prospective studies of EBUS-TBNA complications further confirm that neurological sequelae, while uncommon, are a recognized risk [11,12].

Management of persistent hiccups is challenging due to the paucity of high-quality evidence. Pharmacological therapy targeting the reflex arc has proven effective in case reports. Baclofen, a GABA-B receptor agonist, modulates central reflex excitability, while chlorpromazine, a dopamine D2 receptor antagonist, exerts additional neuromodulatory effects [1,5]. In this patient, baclofen alone was insufficient, but combination therapy with chlorpromazine led to rapid resolution, supporting a neurogenic etiology.

This case also highlights the need to identify and manage coexisting infections. The patient had probable invasive pulmonary aspergillosis and confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis, treated with voriconazole and standard four-drug anti-tubercular therapy. Prompt recognition and management of these comorbidities were crucial for the overall favorable outcome.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that EBUS-TBNA, while generally safe, can rarely induce persistent hiccups through direct mechanical or perineural irritation of the vagus nerve or associated neural structures. Direct irritation of the hiccup reflex arc (vagus or phrenic nerves) may underlie immediate post-procedural hiccups. Combination therapy with baclofen and chlorpromazine can be effective in resolving persistent hiccups. Reporting such cases increases awareness, informs post-procedural monitoring, and improves management of this rare complication.
