The “perfect storm” in bladder oncology: a case of diverticular carcinoma with plasmacytoid features and its clinical ramifications
Authors: Qiuhong Fan, Zhanpei Fu, Xiamo Zheng, Gangping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarcinoma arising within a bladder diverticulum is a rare and diagnostically challenging entity. Its management is further complicated by aggressive histological variants such as plasmacytoid urothelial carcinoma (PUC). The anatomical lack of muscularis propria in diverticula predisposes to early extra‑diverticular spread and impedes accurate clinical staging. The coexistence of a diverticular location and plasmacytoid histology creates a high‑risk clinical scenario with limited evidence to guide therapy.Case presentationA 74‑year‑old male with a history of high‑grade urothelial carcinoma, previously treated with transurethral resection and two cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (gemcitabine plus cisplatin), was found on imaging to have a persistent tumor within a right‑sided bladder diverticulum. He underwent robot‑assisted laparoscopic radical cystectomy. Histopathological examination confirmed high‑grade PUC with 40% squamous differentiation. The tumor invaded peridiverticular adipose tissue (pT3a) and exhibited extensive lymphovascular invasion (> 10 vessels). Immunohistochemistry was positive for GATA3 (weak), p63, CD138 (partial), and CK7 (weak positivity), with PD‑L1 expression (Combined Positive Score = 5), Focal weak membranous E-cadherin staining in approximately 30% of tumor cells, HER2 equivocal (2+). Surgical margins and lymph nodes were negative. The patient was disease‑free at the 3‑month postoperative follow‑up. However, 5 months after surgery, pelvic MRI revealed metastatic lesions, consistent with stage IV disease (rT0N+M1). He subsequently received palliative radiotherapy to the pelvis (200 cGy×25f) and combined targeted therapy (disitamab vedotin 120 mg) plus immunotherapy (toripalimab 240 mg). At the last follow‑up in May 2026 (approximately 7 months postoperatively), he was clinically stable without severe symptoms and continued outpatient radiotherapy.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic complexities of managing high‑grade PUC within a bladder diverticulum. It underscores the essential role of high‑quality cross‑sectional imaging for assessing extra‑diverticular extension, the need for meticulous pathological evaluation to identify this aggressive variant, and the centrality of radical surgery. The presence of lymphovascular invasion and PD‑L1 expression supports the consideration of multimodal strategies and close surveillance. Given the rarity of this dual‑pathology entity, collaborative reporting is crucial to optimize management and improve outcomes.

Background

Bladder carcinoma arising within a diverticulum constitutes a rare and clinically challenging subset of urothelial malignancies, accounting for approximately 1–10% of all bladder cancers [1–6]. The management of diverticular carcinoma is inherently complicated by the unique anatomical structure of the diverticulum, which lacks a well-developed muscularis propria layer [3, 5, 7]. This anatomical deficiency not only facilitates early transmural invasion into perivesical fat but also undermines the accuracy of clinical staging [8]. In particular, the conventional TNM staging criterion for pT2 disease—defined by detrusor muscle invasion—is not applicable in this setting, often resulting in understaging during initial transurethral resection and subsequent upstaging at radical cystectomy [9, 10]. Furthermore, diagnostic sensitivity is frequently limited by a narrow diverticular orifice, which may hinder cystoscopic visualization and diminish the diagnostic yield of urine cytology [11]. Consequently, tumors within diverticula often present at an advanced stage and are associated with a heightened risk of extravesical extension at diagnosis [4].

Among the histological variants that may arise in this context, plasmacytoid urothelial carcinoma (PUC) is recognized as one of the most aggressive forms of high-grade urothelial carcinoma [12, 13]. Histologically characterized by discohesive tumor cells with eccentric nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm—morphologically resembling plasma cells—PUC is notable for its diffuse and infiltrative growth pattern, high propensity for lymphovascular invasion, and a predilection for peritoneal carcinomatosis [10, 14]. Clinically, PUC often presents with non-specific symptoms such as hematuria, and cystoscopic findings may be subtle, manifesting as mucosal edema or thickening without a prominent exophytic mass [14, 15]. This can lead to diagnostic delays or misinterpretation as inflammatory conditions [16]. Immunophenotypically, PUC typically expresses CD138 and frequently shows loss of E-cadherin, aiding in its distinction from other malignancies and reactive processes [17, 18]. Prognosis is generally poor, with higher rates of positive surgical margins, local recurrence, and distant metastasis compared to conventional urothelial carcinoma [9].

Literature review of histologic variants in bladder diverticular carcinoma: A systematic literature search (PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI, up to December 2025) using terms including “bladder diverticulum carcinoma”, “intradiverticular tumor”, and “histologic variant” revealed that previously reported diverticular carcinomas have encompassed various histologies. These include sarcomatoid carcinoma/carcinosarcoma [19], squamous cell carcinoma [20–22], adenocarcinoma [23, 24], Clear cell adenocarcinoma [25], and even metastatic adenocarcinoma from gastric cancer [26]. Notably, to our knowledge, no previously reported case was identified after systematic literature review.

The convergence of a diverticular location and the plasmacytoid phenotype creates what may be termed a “perfect storm” in urologic oncology—compounding diagnostic ambiguity, staging uncertainty, and therapeutic complexity. Existing literature on this specific clinicopathological combination remains virtually nonexistent. Thus, there is a pressing need to elucidate the clinical behavior, multidisciplinary decision-making, and pathological nuances of such cases to improve patient outcomes.

This case report details the management of a 74-year-old male with high-grade plasmacytoid urothelial carcinoma originating in a bladder diverticulum, with a focus on its distinctive squamous differentiation. To our knowledge, this is the first description of diverticular PUC, with the additional unique features of substantial squamous differentiation (40% of tumor area), PD-L1 expression (Combined Positive Score = 5), and partial E-cadherin retention (∼30% weak positivity). We aim to underscore the critical role of advanced cross-sectional imaging, meticulous histopathological evaluation, and the strategic use of radical surgery in addressing this high-risk clinical scenario. Furthermore, we discuss the prognostic significance of aggressive histological features such as lymphovascular invasion and PD-L1 expression, and their potential influence on adjuvant therapy considerations.

Case presentation

Clinical history and initial management

A 74-year-old male non-smoker with no significant history of alcohol use or occupational chemical exposure presented to our urology clinic with persistent gross hematuria and mild dysuria for three months. He had no relevant family history of malignancy. On initial admission (June 12, 2025), contrast-enhanced abdominal CT already revealed a soft-tissue density lesion within a right-sided bladder diverticulum, suspicious for malignancy (see Preoperative Imaging Evaluation). Subsequently, on June 17, 2025, he underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) and transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP). Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed high-grade urothelial carcinoma without definite plasmacytoid morphology (reviewed retrospectively). Given the high-grade histology and the high-risk diverticular location, a multidisciplinary decision was made to proceed with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical surgery. The patient received two cycles (instead of the planned four) of neoadjuvant chemotherapy with gemcitabine and cisplatin (GC regimen) in July and August 2025 due to poor tolerance (myelosuppression and fatigue).

Current presentation and examination

Upon presentation to our institution, the patient reported suprapubic fullness and mild tenderness. Physical examination revealed a palpable, slightly tender suprapubic mass. Vital signs were stable, and routine laboratory investigations were within normal limits.

Preoperative imaging evaluation

Staging workup included pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) urography. MRI identified a right lateral bladder wall diverticulum containing a soft‑tissue nodule with irregular wall thickening, measuring approximately 44 × 18 mm. On T1‑weighted images, the nodule exhibited slightly low signal intensity (Fig. 1A). It showed high signal intensity on T2‑weighted (Fig. 1B) and diffusion‑weighted images (Fig. 1C), with corresponding low signal on the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map (Fig. 1D), indicating restricted diffusion. Following contrast administration, the lesion demonstrated heterogeneous progressive enhancement, features consistent with malignancy. No definite radiologic evidence of direct invasion into adjacent organs was seen, although surrounding fat stranding raised suspicion for extra‑diverticular extension. No pelvic lymphadenopathy was detected.

The imaging findings (Figs. 1 and 2) unequivocally localized the tumor within the diverticulum, confirming its diverticular origin.

Cytological findings

Liquid‑based cytology of urine exfoliated cells revealed scattered and clustered tumor cells against a background of hemorrhage and neutrophils. The tumor cells exhibited an increased nuclear‑to‑cytoplasmic ratio, nuclear eccentricity, plasmacytoid morphology, irregular nuclear membranes, pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, coarse chromatin, and occasional prominent nucleoli (Fig. 3A).

Treatment decision and surgical intervention

Following multidisciplinary tumor board review, which considered the tumor’s location within a diverticulum and its aggressive histological subtype (suggested by cytology and prior TURBT), radical surgery was recommended. In October 2025, the patient underwent robotic‑assisted laparoscopic radical cystectomy (RARC) with bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection and bilateral cutaneous ureterostomy (both ureters were brought out via separate skin incisions; the left ureter was not suitable for transureteroureterostomy due to dense adhesions). Intraoperatively, significant adhesions were noted between the bladder diverticulum and the adjacent sigmoid colon, which were meticulously lysed.

Pathological findings

Gross examination

The cystectomy specimen contained a well‑demarcated, grayish‑red, friable tumor within a right‑sided bladder diverticulum. Although a gross photograph was not obtained, the diverticular origin of the tumor was unequivocally demonstrated by preoperative MRI and CT (Figs. 1 and 2) as well as intraoperative findings (see below).

Histology

The tumor was a high-grade carcinoma with predominant plasmacytoid morphology (approximately 85% of the invasive tumor component) and a minor conventional urothelial carcinoma component (15% of the invasive tumor component). The plasmacytoid cells were discohesive, with eccentric, hyperchromatic nuclei, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and occasional binucleation, imparting a plasma-cell-like appearance (Fig. 3B). Squamous differentiation was observed in approximately 40% of the overall tumor area, predominantly within the plasmacytoid component, characterized by intercellular bridges, keratin pearl formation, and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm (Fig. 3B, arrow). The squamous and plasmacytoid components were intermixed without a clear separation, favoring divergent differentiation rather than a collision tumor. Foci of tumor necrosis were present (Fig. 3C). The tumor invaded through the diverticular wall into the peridiverticular adipose tissue (pT3a), and prominent lymphovascular invasion (involving > 10 vascular spaces) was identified. All surgical margins were free of tumor.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor cells were diffusely positive for pancytokeratin (AE1/AE3) (Fig. 3D), showed weak positivity for GATA3 (Fig. 3E), and diffusely strong nuclear p63 (Fig. 3F), and exhibited a mutant-pattern p53 expression (strong nuclear positivity in > 80% of cells) (Fig. 3G). There was focal weak membranous E-cadherin staining in approximately 30% of tumor cells and partial positivity for CD138 (approximately 40% of tumor cells) (Fig. 3H, I). The Ki‑67 proliferation index was approximately 50%. PD‑L1 expression was observed (combined positive score = 5), and strong, diffuse EGFR positivity was noted. HER2 showed equivocal (2+). Additional markers: CK7 was weakly positive, CK20 showed partial positivity, and uroplakin II was negative. To exclude histologic mimics, additional immunohistochemical stains were performed: the tumor cells were negative for CD45, CDX2, ER, PR, and membranous β-catenin, which effectively ruled out lymphoma, metastatic gastrointestinal signet-ring cell carcinoma, and lobular breast carcinoma, respectively. The overall immunoprofile supported the diagnosis of plasmacytoid urothelial carcinoma with squamous differentiation.

Immunohistochemical differential diagnosis

To exclude mimics, we used a panel: (1) Plasmacytoma – ruled out by diffuse pan‑cytokeratin (AE1/AE3) positivity and CD45 negativity; (2) Metastatic signet‑ring cell carcinoma (e.g., gastric) – excluded by negative CDX2, positive GATA3 and p63; (3) Lobular breast carcinoma – excluded by negative ER, PR, and membranous β‑catenin; (4) Lymphoma – excluded by negative CD45, positive CD138 but also positive cytokeratin. The focal weak E-cadherin staining (30% of tumor cells) is consistent with plasmacytoid differentiation but also allows for a hybrid phenotype, as discussed in Table 1.

Final staging, postoperative course, and follow-up

The final pathological staging after radical cystectomy was pT3a N0 (0/16 lymph nodes involved) M0. The patient recovered well postoperatively and was discharged on postoperative day 7.

Post-operative follow-up and disease progression: At the 3-month follow-up (January 2026), the patient remained asymptomatic with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0, and surveillance imaging showed no evidence of recurrence. However, approximately 5 months after surgery, he developed new pelvic symptoms. Pelvic contrast-enhanced MRI revealed multiple enhancing nodular lesions along the bilateral iliac vessels and the course of the surgical tract, consistent with metastatic disease (rT0N+M1, stage IV). In response, the patient received palliative radiotherapy to the pelvic metastatic lesions and regional lymphatics (prescription dose: PTV 200 cGy×25 fractions, GTV 220 cGy×25 fractions). Concurrently, he started first-cycle targeted therapy plus immunotherapy with disitamab vedotin 120 mg combined with toripalimab 240 mg. On May 5, 2026, he underwent percutaneous bilateral ureteral stent replacement without complications. At the most recent follow-up (May 2026), he reported no fever, no gross hematuria, and tolerable treatment-related side effects. His general condition was stable, and he continued outpatient radiotherapy and systemic therapy. His ECOG performance status was 1 due to metastatic disease. Nutritional screening (NRS 2002) scored 3, prompting regular nutritional follow-up (Table 2).

Discussion

This case presents a formidable clinical challenge arising from the convergence of two significant risk factors: a tumor situated within a bladder diverticulum and a plasmacytoid histological variant. This dual pathology tests the limits of diagnostic accuracy, staging precision, and therapeutic decision-making, underscoring several critical considerations in the management of such rare and aggressive neoplasms.

Diagnostic and staging dilemmas in diverticular tumors

The anatomical peculiarity of a bladder diverticulum—specifically the absence of a true muscularis propria—profoundly complicates both diagnosis and staging [2, 15]. Standard cystoscopy often fails to adequately visualize tumors concealed within a diverticulum, particularly those with a narrow neck, potentially leading to false-negative evaluations [15]. In the present case, while initial TURBT established the diagnosis of carcinoma, cross-sectional imaging was indispensable for delineating the tumor’s location within the diverticulum and assessing its extra‑diverticular extent [27].

More fundamentally, the TNM staging system encounters a conceptual dilemma in this setting. The pT2 category, defined by invasion of the detrusor muscle, is not applicable because the diverticular wall lacks this anatomical layer. Consequently, tumors are frequently under‑staged on initial resection (as pTa or pT1) and subsequently up‑staged to pT3 (invasion into perivesical fat) upon examination of the cystectomy specimen. This staging shift, observed in our patient, carries direct implications for prognosis and adjuvant treatment planning.

Imaging studies are critical for early detection and accurate staging, which significantly influence prognosis and adjuvant therapy selection. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), due to its superior soft‑tissue resolution, allows more accurate assessment of local tumor invasion and staging [28, 29]. Furthermore, radiogenomic analysis integrating MRI with RNA sequencing may provide more precise staging information, which holds significant importance in bladder cancer management [30]. Multiparametric MRI (mp‑MRI) has proven highly effective in T‑staging bladder cancer, and the application of the Vesical Imaging‑Reporting and Data System (VI‑RADS) has improved diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility [31, 32]. While CT scanning remains valuable for evaluating lymph nodes and distant metastases, its accuracy in differentiating non‑muscle‑invasive from muscle‑invasive bladder cancer is limited [29]. Therefore, a multimodal imaging approach combining MRI and CT can offer more comprehensive diagnostic information, facilitating the development of precise treatment plans [32]. Our experience reinforces the necessity of high‑quality preoperative imaging (MRI and CT) to evaluate extra‑diverticular extension and to counsel patients regarding the likelihood of pathological upstaging [30, 33].

Aggressive biology of the plasmacytoid variant

Plasmacytoid urothelial carcinoma (PUC) is a rare and highly aggressive subtype, characterized by diffuse infiltration, a high frequency of lymphovascular invasion (LVI), and a propensity for peritoneal dissemination [9, 34, 35]. This cancer is typically diagnosed at an advanced stage with a poor prognosis, warranting radical surgery even when the disease appears localized preoperatively.

The aggressive and highly metastatic nature of PUC poses challenges in clinical management. Its discohesive growth pattern and cytomorphological resemblance to plasma cells can be misleading in limited biopsy specimens, potentially mimicking a chronic inflammatory infiltrate [35]. In our case, definitive diagnosis relied on thorough examination of the cystectomy specimen and a supportive immunoprofile (CD138+, GATA3+, with loss of E‑cadherin). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy in PUC yields pathological complete response and downstaging rates of only approximately 12% and 21%, respectively, and these pathological responses do not consistently translate into improved clinical outcomes [7]. Although chemotherapy may provide disease control in some cases, its efficacy appears limited, and surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment [36]. The high invasiveness of PUC is further evidenced by its potential to cause colonic obstruction and duodenal metastasis [34], and by the frequent loss of E‑cadherin expression, which is associated with impaired intercellular adhesion and enhanced invasiveness [37, 38].

In the present case, the tumor was composed predominantly of plasmacytoid morphology with a minor conventional urothelial carcinoma component. Squamous differentiation was present in 40% of the overall tumor area, predominantly within the plasmacytoid component. This combination of histologic features may contribute to the aggressive clinical course. Diverticular carcinoma exhibiting both plasmacytoid and squamous differentiation (as in our case, with 40% squamous differentiation) is associated with higher malignancy, advanced pathological staging, and poorer survival [39, 40]. The combination of squamous and plasmacytoid features may increase metastatic risk and worsen prognosis, underscoring the necessity of early diagnosis and aggressive treatment [40]. In summary, PUC and its variant forms demonstrate high invasiveness and metastatic potential, typically necessitating radical surgery to improve survival. While chemotherapy and immunotherapy may be beneficial in selected cases, surgery remains the mainstay of treatment, particularly for localized disease [9, 40, 41]. The presence of extensive LVI (involving more than 10 vascular spaces) is a hallmark of PUC and a well-established independent predictor of metastatic recurrence and poor survival, justifying a radical surgical approach even when preoperative staging suggests localized disease.

However, several aspects deserve emphasis regarding the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). The patient received only 2 cycles of GC due to poor tolerance. Post‑NAC pathology showed ypT3a (no downstaging, no pathological complete response). This suggests chemoresistance, which is consistent with published PUC behavior (pCR rate only 12% in PUC vs. 30‑40% in conventional UC) [42, 43]. We hypothesize that the emergence of prominent plasmacytoid morphology after NAC (which was not present in the initial TURBT) might represent clonal selection of a more aggressive subclone, although this remains speculative.

Clinical course after radical cystectomy: Despite complete resection (negative margins, node‑negative) and standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy, our patient developed pelvic metastases only 5 months after surgery. This early recurrence underscores the aggressive natural history of plasmacytoid urothelial carcinoma, even when apparently localized at the time of cystectomy. The pattern of spread (along the surgical tract and regional lymphatics) is consistent with the known propensity of PUC for peritoneal and soft‑tissue dissemination. This case reinforces the need for intensive postoperative surveillance (e.g., cross‑sectional imaging every 3 months) and consideration of adjuvant clinical trials in high‑risk variant histologies.

Therapeutic strategy and the role of multimodality treatment

Radical cystectomy remains the cornerstone of curative treatment for muscle‑invasive bladder cancer and for carcinoma arising within a diverticulum. A point raised by reviewers is why immediate radical cystectomy was not performed instead of NAC. In retrospect, immediate RC might have been preferable given the high risk of understaging and chemoresistance in diverticular tumors. However, the patient was referred to our center after already receiving 2 cycles of NAC at an outside hospital. We elected to proceed directly to RC without further NAC because of poor tolerance and lack of clear benefit. In this technically complex case, robotic‑assisted radical cystectomy (RARC) demonstrated significant advantages, particularly in enhancing visualization and precision, thereby facilitating safe and effective dissection in a challenging surgical field. Studies indicate that RARC allows for safe separation of diverticular‑colonic adhesions under magnified view, promoting oncologically complete resection and reducing intraoperative complications [44, 45]. Furthermore, RARC maintains a low complication rate even in complex anatomical settings, such as in patients with prior pelvic radiotherapy, highlighting its applicability in complex cases [46, 47].

The decision to administer NAC was based on the high‑grade histology identified on initial TURBT without recognition of the diverticular location. Although the benefit of NAC in pure PUC has not been definitively established due to the variant’s rarity, it is often considered given the poor prognosis associated with this histology. Studies have demonstrated that NAC significantly improves survival and reduces the risk of distant recurrence in muscle‑invasive bladder cancer [48, 49]. The finding of residual viable tumor (ypT3a) after NAC in our patient aligns with anecdotal reports suggesting a degree of chemoresistance in some PUC cases.

However, after diagnostic TURBT confirmed high-grade UC, a multidisciplinary decision was made to pursue NAC followed by RC, aiming to reduce tumor burden and improve margin status. In retrospect, we agree that for high-grade diverticular tumors, especially when imaging has clearly indicated a diverticular carcinoma, primary radical cystectomy without NAC might have been a preferable option, as it would have avoided the delay and potential chemoresistance associated with NAC. This remains an important learning point from this case.

A notable finding in our case was PD‑L1 expression (CPS = 5). While not yet a standard predictive biomarker in non‑metastatic bladder cancer, this raises a compelling question regarding the potential role of adjuvant or salvage immunotherapy in high‑risk, localized PUC, particularly in cases with residual disease after cystectomy. Such biomarker findings should be integrated into personalized postoperative surveillance and therapeutic planning. Evidence suggests that immune checkpoint inhibitors such as nivolumab can reduce recurrence risk in the adjuvant setting, particularly in patients with residual nodal disease after NAC [50, 51].

Comparison with conventional UC (Urothelial carcinoma): Unlike conventional UC, PUC has higher positive margin rates (20‑30% vs. 5‑10%), higher peritoneal recurrence, and less benefit from NAC. Therefore, meticulous surgical technique (wide excision, consideration of peritoneal sampling) and closer surveillance (including cross‑sectional imaging every 3 months) are recommended. Our patient had intraoperative adhesions requiring lysis, reflecting the infiltrative nature.

Limitations

Our report is inherently limited by its nature as a single-center case study. Although the current follow-up of 7 months has documented an early metastatic recurrence, longer observation is still required to fully assess long-term oncologic outcomes and response to salvage therapies. In addition, no gross photograph of the surgical specimen was taken, which is a limitation in visually demonstrating the diverticular origin; however, preoperative imaging (MRI/CT) and intraoperative findings clearly confirmed this relationship. The patient received only 2 cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy instead of the planned 4 cycles due to poor tolerance (myelosuppression and fatigue), which may have affected treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the emergence of plasmacytoid morphology after chemotherapy could represent clonal selection, but this hypothesis remains speculative without molecular confirmation. Conclusions regarding optimal management strategies (including the role of primary radical cystectomy without NAC, and the use of targeted/immunotherapy in metastatic disease) should be interpreted with caution, and broader validation through multi-institutional collaboration is needed.

Conclusions and clinical implications

This case underscores the distinct clinical challenges posed by carcinoma arising within a bladder diverticulum, particularly when compounded by an aggressive plasmacytoid variant. The anatomical absence of muscularis propria in diverticula not only facilitates early extra-diverticular spread but also complicates accurate staging, often leading to pathological upstaging after radical surgery. For bladder diverticular carcinoma in general—and especially when high-risk histological features are present—a heightened index of suspicion, precise staging, and coordinated multidisciplinary management are imperative. Key clinical implications include:

Staging precision in diverticular tumors

Preoperative cross-sectional imaging, especially multiparametric MRI, is essential to evaluate perivesical fat invasion and anticipate pathological upstaging, which is common in diverticular carcinomas due to the lack of a true muscular layer.

Histopathological recognition

Pathologists should maintain vigilance for both diverticular origin and aggressive variants such as plasmacytoid morphology. In biopsies with discohesive growth, confirmatory immunohistochemistry (e.g., CD138, GATA3, E-cadherin) is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis.

Surgical approach

Radical cystectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy remains the cornerstone of treatment for diverticular carcinoma, given its propensity for extravesical extension. Minimally invasive techniques may offer technical advantages in navigating complex diverticular anatomy.

Integrated therapeutic strategy

For high-risk diverticular tumors exhibiting features such as lymphovascular invasion or plasmacytoid differentiation, neoadjuvant chemotherapy should be considered cautiously given potential chemoresistance. Primary radical cystectomy may be a reasonable alternative. Biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression may further guide adjuvant immunotherapy in selected cases.

Uniqueness of this case report

(1) First case of PUC in a bladder diverticulum with 40% squamous differentiation; (2) First report of PD-L1 expression (CPS = 5) in this dual-pathology setting; (3) Detailed imaging-pathology correlation using both MRI (VI-RADS) and CT; (4) Partial E-cadherin retention – a variant not previously described in diverticular PUC.

Important lessons from post-operative course

Despite achieving a negative-margin, node-negative resection (pT3aN0) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radical cystectomy, our patient developed pelvic metastases only 5 months after surgery. This early recurrence highlights the aggressive natural history of plasmacytoid urothelial carcinoma, even when apparently localized at the time of cystectomy. Therefore, intensive postoperative surveillance (e.g., cross-sectional imaging every 3 months) and a low threshold for initiating palliative systemic therapy are warranted. This case also illustrates the potential use of combination regimens such as disitamab vedotin (an HER2-directed antibody-drug conjugate) plus toripalimab (an anti-PD-1 antibody) in metastatic PUC, although further studies are needed.

Given the rarity and aggressive nature of diverticular carcinoma with plasmacytoid features, systematic documentation and collaborative research are vital to refine staging paradigms and therapeutic algorithms. Prospective, multi-institutional studies are needed to establish evidence-based management strategies for this challenging dual-pathology entity.
