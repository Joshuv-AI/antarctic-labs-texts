Restricted antibiotic use in the clinical center of montenegro
Authors: Dzana Lukac, Tanja Vojinovic, Pinaki Dutta, Hasib Lukac, Sanja Medenica, Arshiya Dutta, Mitar Delevic, Sanela Abazovic Hot, Dzihan Abazovic, Jovana Vukovic, Branislava Miljkovic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:In this century, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) presents a major public health problem, because it is a leading cause of death worldwide. Antimicrobial resistance is caused by the overprescription of antibiotics as well as self-medication. In 2010, antibiotics were the most commonly prescribed medications according to the Montenegrin Institute of Public Health. To reduce side effects, improve treatment efficacy, and prevent AMR, it is necessary to have greater control and help formulating national and international level policies over antibiotic prescription and their use.AIMS:This research focuses on the analysis of requests for the dispensing of reserve antibiotics and the justification of their prescription and use.MATERIALS AND METHODS:In the Clinical Center of Montenegro, 174 requests for restricted antibiotics were retrospectively analyzed.RESULTS:Data analysis of 174 requests for restricted antibiotics determined that an antibiogram has been performed in only 21.84% of cases, which means that there were 78.16% of cases of empirical antibiotic use. In 72.25% of cases, restricted antibiotics were included in therapy without prior use of unrestricted antimicrobial therapy.CONCLUSIONS:Based on data analytics, it can be concluded that using restricted antibiotics in therapy is not completely in line with rational antibiotics implementation. Restricted antibiotics are used without culture sensitivity tests in more than 2/3rd of cases. Similarly, restricted antibiotics were used as first-line therapy.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the principal public health problems of the 21st century that threatens the effective prevention and treatment of an ever-increasing range of infections caused by bacteria, parasites, viruses, and fungi, which are no longer susceptible to the common antimicrobial agents.[1] Lower respiratory infections accounted for more than 1.5 million deaths associated with resistance, making it the most burdensome infectious syndrome. AMR is a leading cause of death around the world, with the highest burdens in low-resource settings.[2] To counter the growing problem of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), many hospitals have adopted antimicrobial stewardship programs that aim to optimize the beneficial effects of antibiotics.[3] Antimicrobial resistance occurs due to the uncontrolled and inappropriate prescription of antibiotics as well as their use in self-medication.[4] Controlled use of antibiotics will not only improve the effectiveness of treatment but also reduce costs and prevent AMR.[5] Responsibility for health remains predominantly at the national level, but collective action at the international level is needed to combat AMR.[6] Montenegro is one of the European countries with the highest rate of antibiotic usage. In 2011, the Ministry of Health formed the National Interdisciplinary Commission for AMR, which coordinates activities for controlling AMR. Since January 2016, all of the microbiological laboratories in Montenegro have collected information about AMR using the Central Asian and Eastern European Surveillance of Antimicrobial Resistance method. In 2010, 59% of isolated Staphylococcus aureus in the Clinical Center of Montenegro were Methicillin-resistant. 28% of Escherichia coli in 2006 were resistant to third-generation cephalosporins. In 2014, the Ministry of Health formed the National Committee for Antibiograms, which collects information about new methods for microbiological analysis and analyzes their implementation in Montenegrin laboratories. According to data published by the Institute for Public Health, antibiotics were the most prescribed medicines in 2010.[7]

To prevent severe and potentially fatal infections, in 1990, the WHO defined the concept of “restricted antibiotics.” Restricted antibiotics include the ones that are not used as first-line antibiotics since they are preserved for the treatment of infections that cannot be cured with common antibiotics.[8]

Rational use of antibiotics includes choosing the antibiotic according to the sensitivity of the causative agent to antibiotics, pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics of the antibiotic, and dosing based on the patient’s kidney and liver elimination capacity. The possibility of interactions with other medicines must be as low as possible. If possible, the cheapest of equally effective therapy options is chosen, so that expenses can be reduced. Most doctors disapprove of programs for controlled antibiotic usage. Although there is no strong evidence that rationalization of antibiotic usage has a positive effect on the outcome of therapy, current data gives strong support to the implementation of different programs for antibiotic usage rationalization.

In 2016, the Clinical Center of Montenegro filed a restriction on the implementation of amikacin, tobramycin, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, cefixime, cefepime, colistin, linezolid, moxifloxacin, ofloxacin, levofloxacin, meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin, ertapenem, piperacillin/tazobactam, tigecycline, vancomycin, and teicoplanin.

Objectives

This study focuses on the evaluation of the rational implementation of restricted antibiotics in the Clinical Center of Montenegro. In addition, the aim of this study is the assessment of bacterial resistance to restricted antibiotics.

Materials and Methods

This descriptive observational study is conducted in the Clinical Center of Montenegro. One hundred and seventy-four requests for restricted antibiotics were retrospectively analyzed. Requests were retrieved from the database of the Department for medical suppliance of the Clinical Center of Montenegro. Requests were retrieved from the surgical, medical, and intensive care units. Each request contains the name of the patient, diagnosis, site of infection, causative agent, the name and dosing regimen of the restricted antibiotic used in therapy, and information about previous antimicrobial therapy. Data were analyzed using the method of descriptive and inferential statistics using the SPSS program (IBM SPSS Statistics 22, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

By data analysis, it was determined that the antibiogram has been done only in 21.84% of cases. In 72.25% of cases restricted, antibiotic was included in therapy without prior antimicrobial therapy using an unrestricted antibiotic. Monotherapy was included in the largest number of cases (75.28%), two antibiotics were used together in 19.54% of cases, a combination of three in 4.02% of cases, and there were 1.15% of cases of using 4 restricted antibiotics in therapy. The average therapy duration was 8.84 days, though there was a large range of shortest (3 days) and longest (40 days) therapy duration. The most commonly used antibiotics were meropenem (53 cases – 30.29%), amikacin (36 cases – 20.57%), and vancomycin (33 cases – 18.86%). The combination of imipenem and cilastatin was used in 13 cases (7.43%).

The most common sites of infection were the chest, head, lower limbs, abdomen, and lesser pelvis. The sensitivity of the most commonly isolated causative agents on antibiotics was also analyzed. Therapy was started with meropenem, although the bacteria were sensitive to some of the first-line antibiotics. P. mirabilis was isolated from a skin swab.

C.freundii was isolated from blood samples. Therapy was started with Vancomycin after the culture report. E. coli was isolated from urine samples, and it showed resistance to amikacin, ampicillin, and ciprofloxacin.

S. aureus has been isolated from skin samples and wound swabs.

Klebsiella pneumoniae has shown resistance to ampicillin, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefotaxime, and gentamicin. In 2 of 3 cases, K. pneumoniae has shown resistance to amikacin and ciprofloxacin, and in 1 of 3 cases to imipenem and meropenem. It has been isolated from the thoracal drain and urine samples.

Corynebacterium spp. has shown resistance to benzylpenicillin, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, clindamycin, and tetracycline. It has shown sensitivity to vancomycin. It has been isolated from the Central venous catheter.

Acinetobacter spp. and Serratia spp. infections were usually followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection.

Acinetobacter spp. has shown sensitivity to Colistin and resistance to all the other antibiotics. It has been isolated from wound swabs, blood, bronchoalveolar lavage, and central venous catheter.

In 62.5% cases, Pseudomonas aeruginosa has shown resistance to piperacillin, ceftazidime, cefepime, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, and aztreonam, and in 50% of cases, it has shown resistance to Imipenem, and in 25% of cases, it has shown sensitivity to amikacin. It has been isolated from the thoracic drain, blood, and wound swabs.

Discussion

Restricted antibiotics were used in more than 2/3rd of cases empirically. Similarly, restricted antibiotics were used as first-line therapy again in 72% of cases. S. aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter were the most common isolates. It is alarming to see the poor antimicrobial sensitivity of piperacillin/tazobactam among of the organisms isolated in this study. Piperacillin/tazobactam being the most widely used empirical antibiotic in many centers, this study highlights the need of paradigm shift in our approach to empirical antibiotic therapy. Meanwhile, the sensitivity of Carbapenems is relatively preserved, and no resistance to Colistin was demonstrated in vivo in this study. Although some authors claim that the concept of restricted antibiotics leads to higher AMR[9] or redistribution of costs towards older antibiotics,[10] this concept has been accepted with many facts that confirm its significance in the rationalization of antibiotic usage.[11121314] As the restricted antibiotics concept has not been accepted in some hospitals, especially in the USA, an antibiotic replacement method has been established – an antibiotic is replaced with another that is cheaper and has a more narrow spectrum of action. Furthermore, parenteral therapy is changed to oral as soon as possible. According to some data, this is a more efficient way to rationalize antimicrobial therapy.[10]

According to an observational study in the University Hospital Centre of Zagreb, 38% of hospitalized patients had been on antimicrobial therapy, and 24.63% of used antibiotics were restricted. The most common diagnoses were sepsis (64%), Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) (24%), abdominal infections (21%), and lung infections (7%). The average duration of therapy was 4 days, which is shorter than in the Clinical Center of Montenegro.[15]

Research in Henri-Mondor University Hospital in France, which included 188 patients, has shown that in 2/3rd of cases, antibiotic therapy was empirical. However, in 95% of cases, empirical therapy has been justified. The most common isolated causative agents were S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, which are also isolated in CCM.[16]

The limitation of antimicrobial usage in Turkey has significantly reduced usage of meropenem, imipenem, vancomycin, and piperacillin/tazobactam in 2004 compared to 2001. The most common isolated causative agents in that study were E. coli, Klebsiella spp., Acinetobacter spp., P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus, similar to our study, although in a smaller number of samples.[17]

Research in Croatia, which examined the effect of microbiologists’ and clinical pharmacists’ supervision on therapy, showed that hospitalization was reduced from 28 days to 17 days.[18]

The role of pharmacists in antimicrobial therapy has been analyzed in another research in 2015. In 23% of cases, the antibiotic dosage has been changed, and in 21% of cases, the antibiotic has been changed, thus reducing the incidence of C. difficile-associated pseudomembranous colitis? and Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae.[19]

In 2018, 7.2% of isolated K. pneumoniae were resistant to Carbapenems in Croatia, Estonia, Iceland, Slovenia, and Greece.[20] One-third of isolated K. pneumoniae in CCM were resistant to Carbapenems. 14.9% of isolated E. coli have been resistant to 3rd generation Cephalosporins (from 5.9% in Norway to 41.3% in Bulgaria),[20] which was similar to our small study.

Conclusions

To conclude, the use of restricted antibiotics in therapy is not completely in line with rational antibiotics implementation. Developing a national strategy and program for antibiotic prescribing based on antibiograms, coordinated decisions in the use of antibiotics for patient treatment, and control of its implementation are key components that should be developed in every hospital to prevent misuse of these drugs. Clinical audits followed by feedback can influence the improvement and rational prescribing of antibiotics as well as reserve antibiotics. Global cooperation is needed to preserve the few effective antibiotics for future generations and can help formulating national and international antibiotic policy for future generation.

Limitations

The limitations of our study are small sample size, nonavailability of antibiogram in majority, cross-sectional and retrospective nature of data collection. Additional information regarding antibiotic sensitivity/resistance profile prospectively collected could have provided more meaningful information for Policy-making. The disk diffusion method was used for the Culture and Sensitivity. There was no mention of prior antibiotic usage. The most important limitation is that the definition of “Reserve Antibiotics” is highly subjective and variable, as per the locoregional microbial flora and previous antibiogram data of the hospital, but for our country, this is a maiden attempt to formulate a future antibiotic policy for the Republic of Montenegro.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Clinical Center of Montenegro no 03/01-22236/1 date September 21, 2021, as per the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective study, informed consent was not taken. However patient’s identity was anonymized.
