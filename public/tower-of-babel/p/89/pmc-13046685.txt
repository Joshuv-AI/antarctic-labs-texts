Species diversity, distribution and habitat suitability modeling of ixodid ticks infesting cattle in selected districts of the North Wollo Zone, northeast Ethiopia
Authors: Assaye Wollelie Fentie, Moges Maru, Teketay Bayleyegn Derso, Gebyaw Menge Getnet, Animaw Andargie Worku, Assaye Desta Amare, Ashenafi Wondye Dagne, Zerihun Getie Wassie, Abebech Atinafu Yehulashet, Ayenalem Shibabaw Atenaf, Yihenew Getahun Ambaw, Zewdu Seyoum Tarekegn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ixodid ticks, as vectors for numerous pathogens, pose a significant threat to Ethiopia’s livestock industry. However, data on their species diversity and distribution in the North Wollo zone are scarce. This cross-sectional study aimed to identify ticks infesting cattle, associated risk factors, and their habitat suitability in the region. From March to December 2024, a multistage cluster sampling technique was used to examine 340 tick infested cattle across four districts. Collected ticks were morphologically identified. Data analysis involved descriptive statistics and logistic regression, to identify risk factors, while the maximum entropy was employed to predict habitat suitability for the five dominant species. A total of 2,258 ticks, comprising three genera and twelve species were collected. The five most abundant species namely; Amblyomma variegatum (26.9%), Amblyomma cohaerens (14.2%), Rhipicephalus decoloratus (18.5%), Rhipicephalus pulchellus (17.1%), and Rhipicephalus evertsi evertsi (13.1%) together accounted for more than 90% of the total collection. The primary attachment site was the ano-vulval region (35.2%), followed by the udder/scrotum (22.4%). Logistic regression revealed that altitude, body condition, herd size, and animal movement were significant risk factors for tick infestation. Furthermore, habitat suitability modeling identified elevation, vegetation type, and land cover as key environmental predictors for tick distribution. This study provided the first comprehensive data on tick diversity and distribution in North Wollo zone. By identifying both host-related risk factors and geographically suitable habitats, these findings are crucial for developing targeted and effective tick control strategies, ultimately safeguarding livestock health and productivity in the region.

Introduction

Ticks are obligate hematophagous ectoparasites of terrestrial vertebrates (Wegi 2023)and are recognized as the second most significant arthropod vectors, next to mosquitoes, in transmitting pathogens affecting both animal and human health. They transmit a wide array of viruses, bacteria, and protozoa, causing numerous diseases of veterinary and medical importance (Magesa et al. 2023). The economic burden of tick infestations and the diseases they carry is immense, particularly in the livestock sector. Globally, approximately 80% of the world’s cattle population is at risk, contributing to annual economic losses estimated between $22 and $30 billion (Sachin-Kumar et al., 2023).

In Ethiopia, these challenges are amplified by diverse agroecological zones that provide favorable environmental conditions for a wide variety of tick species(Kumsa et al. 2012; Kaba 2022; Wolde and Mekuria 2022). The primary genera infesting cattle in the country include Ambylomma, Hyalomma, and Rhipicephalus (Solomon and Tanga 2020). Despite the recognized national threat, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding tick distribution and epidemiology, particularly in specific regions. The North Wollo zone, located in northeastern Ethiopia, represents a critical area where such information is profoundly scarce. This lack of localized data is a major impediment to effective disease control, as previous studies have highlighted significant variations in species composition across different Ethiopian regions (Kaba, 2022). A comprehensive understanding of local tick diversity is essential (Okely and Al-Khalaf 2022; Tagwireyi et al. 2022), as different species possess varying vectorial capacities for specific pathogens (Estrada-Pena et al., 2018), directly influencing the risk of tick-borne disease (TBD) outbreaks (Kopsco et al. 2022; Uusitalo et al. 2022).

The geographic distribution of ticks is not static; it is expanding in response to global drivers such as climate change, international trade, and increasing livestock density and mobility (Musa et al. 2020). This dynamic situation necessitates the use of modern epidemiological tools to anticipate and manage emerging threats (Zannou et al. 2021; Perez-Otanez et al. 2024). Species Distribution Models (SDMs) have become indispensable for mapping current and potential future habitats of vectors. By integrating species occurrence data with environmental variables like climate and land cover, these models can identify areas of high suitability for tick survival and propagation (Estrada-Pena and Venzal 2007).

Among these models, the Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) algorithm has emerged as a particularly robust tool for predicting species distributions, renowned for its high predictive accuracy even with limited “presence-only” data, which is typical of field surveys (Phillips et al. 2006b; Bellamy et al. 2013; Ma et al. 2024). Mapping habitat suitability through MaxEnt not only enhances our understanding of tick biogeography but also provides a crucial evidence base for designing targeted, geographically-focused surveillance and control strategies (Aktas et al., 2023).

Therefore, this study was aimed to address the critical knowledge gaps in the North Wollo zone. Our objectives were to: (1) asssess the species diversity, burden, and distribution of ixodid ticks infesting cattle; (2) evaluate the host and environmental risk factors associated with infestation by the most common tick species; and (3) predict the habitat suitability for the dominant tick species using the MaxEnt model and identify key environmental predictors. The findings provide baseline data for future research and policy-making, contributing to the development of cost-effective, evidence-based management programs to mitigate the impact of ticks and TBDs on livestock health and productivity in the study area.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in the North Wollo zone of northeastern Ethiopia (11°–13°N, 38°–40°E). The region is characterized by a dramatic topography, with altitudes ranging from 600 to 4,284 m above sea level (m.a.s.l.). This gradient creates four distinct agro-ecological zones: lowland (500–1500 m), midland (1500–2300 m), highland (2300–3200 m), and Wurch (> 3200 m). Consequently, the climate varies significantly, with mean annual rainfall from 500 to 1300 mm and temperatures from 10 to 27 °C.

Agriculture is the primary economic activity, with mixed crop-livestock farming dominating the landscape. Cattle are integral to the local economy and food security, with an estimated population of 924,892 in the zone (Bahiru and Zewdu 2021). From the zone’s 14 districts, four were purposefully selected for this study: Woldia, Habru, Guba-lafto, and Angot. These districts were chosen as they collectively represent the full spectrum of agro-ecological conditions found in North Wollo zone (Fig. 1), ensuring a comprehensive regional assessment.

Study Animals

The study included cattle of all ages, sexes, and breeds (indigenous and cross) from different production systems in the four selected districts. Age was determined by dental examination (Karisch 2022) and owner reports, then categorized as young (< 3 years), adult (3–6 years), or old (≥ 6 years) as per Tarekegn et al. (2025). Body Condition Score (BCS) was assessed and categorized as poor, medium, or good based on the criteria of Nicholson and Butterworth (1986) and Kemal et al. (2016).

Study Design and Period

A cross-sectional study was conducted from March to December 2024 to investigate the species diversity, distribution, and habitat suitability of ixodid ticks infesting cattle across four districts of the North Wollo Zone. Tick collection was conducted between May and July 2024, corresponding to the early rainy season.

An intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) was assumed to be 0.6 to reflect the strong similarity in tick infestation within-villages due to shared grazing area, acaricide use, and microclimate, consistent with high ICC values reported in clustered parasitological and epidemiological outcomes (Ouyang et al. 2023; Mbekwe et al. 2021). For this study, we decided to include 5 animals per cluster to balance operational feasibility and statistical precision. The D for a cluster-sample survey with 5 animals per cluster was computed to be 3.4 (Eq. (2). Accordingly, 340 cattle from 68 villages were sampled for the study.

Sampling Technique

A multistage cluster sampling design was employed because ecological conditions, cattle management practices, and tick control strategies are highly shared within villages, leading to clustering of tick infestation outcomes. A method also selected due to the absence of a comprehensive individual animal sampling frame (Dohoo et al. 2003). First, four districts (Woldia, Habru, Angot, and Guba-lafto) were purposively selected to represent the zone’s three of the four agro-ecological zones (lowland, midland and highland), accessibility, and cattle production systems. Second, 34 kebeles (the smallest administrative unit) (3 from Woldia, 12 from Habru, 5 from Angot, and 14 from Guba lafto) were randomly selected proportionally to district’s number of kebeles to ensure geographic and ecological representation. Third, two villages (subdivisions of kebele) were randomly selected from each kebele, resulting in 68 villages. Within each village (cluster), five cattle-owning households were purposively selected based on owner willingness and informed verbal consent. Finally, one tick-infested animal was randomly selected from each participating household, yielding a total of 340 cattle. Lists of kebeles and villages were obtained from districts’ animal health offices. All procedures were conducted following ethical standards and received approval from the College of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Sciences Research Ethics and Review Committee (CVMAS-RERC) at the University of Gondar (Ref.: CVMAS/UoG/RERC/19/04/2024).

Data Collection Methods

Tick collection, identification and data recording

Each selected animal was restrained either in a crush (cattle chute) or with the assistance of handlers holding the head and halter rope without sedation to ensure minimal stress, and safe tick collection in compliance with animal welfare guidelines. All visible ticks present on one side of the each animal body were counted and multiplied by two to estimate the tick burden (Admassu et al. 2015). A representative subsample of ticks were collected from both sides of animal across major predilection sites using forceps. Samples from each site were preserved in separate, labeled vials containing 80% ethanol until examination (Walker, 2014). For each animal, sex, age, breed, body condition and geographical coordinates (altitude, latitude, longitude) from a GPS device were recorded. Additionally, using semi-structured questionnaire owners were interviewed face-to-face to gather information on herd size, management system, and history of cattle mobilization (Thrusfield 2007). All samples were transported to the Woldia University Veterinary Parasitology Laboratory. Ticks were identified to the species level under a stereomicroscope at a total magnification of 40× (objective 4× × eyepiece 10×) using the morphological keys described by Walker et al. (2014). Diagnostic morphological characters such as scutum ornamentation, shape of basis capituli, leg coloration, festoons, and mouthpart length were used to differentiate tick genera and species. All specimens were photographed at this magnification, and representative images of each species were collaged for presentation (see Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5).

Environmental data

The environmental predictor variables (Table 1), identified as influential for tick survival from existing literature (Vajana et al. 2018), were compiled for the habitat suitability analysis. The study area shapefile for 2021 was obtained from the EthioGIS map server website (https://www.ethiogis-mapserver.org/). Climatic data, including temperature (min, max and average), precipitation (Prep), solar radiation (SRAD), wind speed (Wind), and water vapor pressure (Wvap) along with a Digital Elevation Model (DEM), were sourced from the WorldClim v2.1 database at a 30 arc-second (~ 1 km) resolution (http://worldclim.org) (Fick and Hijmans 2017). Land Use/Land Cover (LC) data were derived from 10-m resolution Ethiopian Sentinel-2 imagery via the Africa Geoportal (www.africageoportal.com). Monthly Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) data for 2024 were acquired from the Copernicus Global Land Service (https://land.copernicus.eu/global) (Okely et al. 2020). Prior to modeling, all monthly raster layers were averaged in QGIS. They were clipped to the study area extent and resampled to a uniform spatial resolution and coordinate reference system to ensure spatial alignment.

Data Management and Analysis

Data were entered and managed in Microsoft Excel, and subsequently imported into SPSS software package (Version 20) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize survey data and calculate species-specific infestation prevalence. The level of tick infestation was categorised as low (1–25 ticks per infested animal), moderate (26–50 ticks per infested animal) or high (more than 50 ticks per infested animal) (Tarekegn et al. 2025). Categorical variables were dummy-coded, and the reference levels were identified as those with the lowest probability of reporting tick infestations. A Chi-square test was employed to compare the difference in proportions of tick infestation prevalence between the districts and agroecological zones. Binary logistic regression analysis was employed to identify risk factors associated with infestation by the five most dominant tick species (A. variegatum, A. cohaerens, R. decoloratus, R. pulchellus, and R. evertsi). Infrequently observed species were excluded from this analysis because their low counts would lead to unstable regression estimates, inflated standard errors, and wide confidence intervals, limiting interpretability. During analysis, species-specific infestation status (yes/no) was used as dependent variable while factors such as district, altitude, latitude, longitude, herd size, sex, age, breed, body condition score, management, and cattle movement were used as explanatory variables. Initially, all predictor variables were first tested individually in univariable analysis. Variables were screened based on level of significance and multicollinearity check. Then, predictors with high p-value threshold (> 0.25) or high multicollinearity were excluded from analysis while others remained. After running backward stepwise selection procedure, only significant predictors (p < 0.05) retained in the final regression model for each species. The final models determined the odds ratios (ORs) at 95% confidence intervals of the explanatory variables associated with the outcome variables (Namgyal et al. 2021). Furthermore, maps were prepared with the quantum GIS software version 3.34. The base map was obtained from an administrative shapefile of Ethiopia.

Habitat Suitability Modeling

Habitat suitability was modeled using the Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) algorithm (v3.4.4) (Phillips et al., 2006a). To ensure robust modeling and avoid overestimation, only species with at least 20 unique occurrence records were included (Doi et al. 2021). This resulted in the selection of five dominant tick species: A. variegatum (n = 59), R. decoloratus (n = 56), R. evertsi evertsi (n = 47), A. cohaerens (n = 27), and R. pulchellus (n = 24). Geographic coordinates for these occurrences and the environmental predictor layers were prepared in QGIS and formatted for use in MaxEnt.

To mitigate model overfitting and multicollinearity, a two-step variable selection process was implemented. First, predictors with a contribution of less than 1% in an initial MaxEnt run were excluded. Second, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed on the remaining variables; for any pair with a correlation coefficient |r| > 0.9, the variable with the lower contribution rate was removed to finalize the predictor set (Fei et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2023).

The final model was run using 80% of presence points for training and 20% for testing. Key settings included 10 bootstrap replicates, 5,000 iterations, and 10,000 background points, with other parameters at their default values. The Jackknife test was used to evaluate the relative importance of each environmental predictor (Yang et al. 2021). Model performance was validated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). Predictive accuracy was categorized as excellent (AUC > 0.9), good (0.8–0.9), adequate (0.7–0.8), or poor (< 0.7) (Uusitalo et al. 2022). The final habitat suitability maps, representing the average output of the 10 replicates, were generated. In QGIS, these continuous probability surfaces were reclassified into five suitability categories: very low (0–0.2), low (0.2–0.4), moderate (0.4–0.6), high (0.6–0.8), and very high (0.8–1.0) (Namgyal et al. 2021).

Results

Tick Species Identified and Infestation Prevalence

A total of 7,458 ticks were counted from the body of 340 cattle, from which a representative subsample of 2,258 ticks were collected from all predilection sites for identification. The morphological identification revealed 12 distnict tick species (Table 2; Figs. 2, 3 and 4, and 5) belonging to three genera: Amblyomma, Hyalomma and Rhipicephalus. The five most dominant tick species were A. variegatum (26.9%), R. decoloratus (18.5%), R. pulchellus (17.1%), A. cohaerens (14.2%), and R. evertsi evertsi(13.1%) collectively accounted for over 90% of all identified specimens. The remaining seven species, including H. rufipes and A. lepidum, were present at much lower abundances (< 6% each). Additionally, a small number of unidentified immature Amblyomma species were collected.

The Distribution of Ixodid Ticks

Tables 3 and 4 below presents the occurrence and distribution of ixodid tick species across the study districts and agro-ecological zones. Among the twelve identified species, A. variegatum was the most prevalent species (57.6%), with highest prevalence (80%) in Habru district, followed by Woldia at 70% and Guba-lafto at 49.3%. It had showed strong preferences in warm climates, with significantly higher infestation rates (p < 0.001) in the lowland (77.6%) and midland (70.4%) zones. In contrast, R. decoloratus, the second most prevalent species (45.0%), was predominantly found in cooler, higher-altitude areas, with its highest prevalence in the highland areas (67.7%) and Angot district (80.0%) (p < 0.001).

Other dominant species also exhibited distinct ecological niches. R. evertsi evertsi (31.5%) and R. pulchellus (22.9%) were most abundant in lowland areas, particularly in the Habru district. A. cohaerens (20.9%) was prevalent in midland zone and Habru district. The rarest species were R. muhsamae and R. pravus, each found on less than 1% of cattle. In terms of species richness (Table 2), the Guba-lafto district harbored the highest diversity (11 species), followed by the Habru district (10 species), highlighting these as potential hotspots for tick-borne diseases. Attachment Site Preferences

The distribution of ticks across the host body was non-uniform, with distinct predilection sites identified. Overall, the highest concentration of ticks was recovered from the ano-vulval region (35.2%), followed by the udder/scrotum (22.4%), brisket (15.0%), and dewlap (14.6%). Other sites, including the head, back, legs, and belly, were less frequently infested (< 10% each). Significant attachment site preferences were observed at the genus and subgenus level (Fig. 7). Both Hyalomma and Rhipicephalus genera showed a strong affinity for the ano-vulval region, which accounted for 96.9% of all Hyalomma and 78.8% of all Rhipicephalus specimens, respectively. In contrast, Amblyomma species were predominantly found on the udder/scrotum (49.2%) and brisket (33.0%). Notably, ticks of the subgenus Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) exhibited a unique preference, with the majority (66.7%) collected from the dewlap.

Risk Factors Associated with Major Tick Infestations in Cattle

The potential risk factors including district, altitude, latitude, longitude, herd size, sex, age, breed, body condition score, management system, and cattle movement were initially tested for associatation with tick infestation in the univariable analysis for each species. Following the exclusion of variables based on significance level (p ≤ 0.25) and multicollinearity, the final regression models revealed agroecology, host body condition, herd size, animal movement, and latitude were as significant (p < 0.05) predictors associated with tick infestation.

Agroecology was a primary driver for Amblyomma variegatum and Rhipicephalus decoloratus, butwith opposing effects. The odds of A. variegatum infestation were significantly higher in lowland (AOR = 2.37) and midland (AOR = 2.86) zones compared to the highlands. Conversely, the odds of R. decoloratus infestation were significantly lower in lowland (AOR = 0.14) and midland (AOR = 0.39) zones relative to the highlands (as shown in Table 5).

Host-related factors were also critical. Poor body condition significantly increased the odds of infestation by A. variegatum (AOR = 2.98) and Amblyomma cohaerens (AOR = 3.59) when compared to animals in good condition. For Rhipicephalus evertsi, both large herd size (AOR = 3.04) and medium body condition (AOR = 2.69) were significant risk factors.

Furthermore, management and geographical location played key roles. Animal movement was the strongest predictor for Rhipicephalus pulchellus, increasing the odds of infestation by nearly fivefold (AOR = 4.93). For A. cohaerens, increasing latitude was highly protective, reducing the odds of infestation by 97.7% for each one-degree increase (AOR = 0.023). Habitat Suitability

The MaxEnt models generated habitat suitability maps that project the potential geographic distribution for each of the five dominant tick species (Fig. 10). These projections revealed distinct spatial patterns that were largely consistent with the field survey observations, highlighting key areas of high and low suitability across the study zone.

A broad area of high suitability for A. variegatum, R. pulchellus, and R. evertsi evertsi was predicted across the central and eastern parts of the study area. These suitable zones primarily encompassed the districts of Woldia and Habru, along with central Guba-lafto, corresponding largely to the lowland and midland agro-ecologies (Fig. 10B, C, E).

In contrast, the model for R. decoloratus identified highly suitable habitats primarily in the cooler, higher-altitude districts, including Angot and the western portions of Guba-lafto (Fig. 10D). The suitable habitat for A. cohaerens was more restricted, concentrated in specific areas of Woldia, eastern Guba-lafto, and western Habru (Fig. 10A).

A critical finding from this spatial analysis was the significant overlap in suitable habitats, indicating areas where multiple tick species can coexist. The models identified Woldia district and northern Habru as major hotspots for co-occurrence, predicting high suitability for all five dominant species. This prediction aligns with our field survey data (Table 4) and corresponds to the midland agro-ecological zone, which is characterized by moderate environmental conditions conducive to a high diversity of tick species.

Discussion

This study provided the first comprehensive assessment of ixodid tick diversity, distribution, and habitat suitability in the North Wollo zone of Ethiopia, an area characterized by diverse agroclimates ideal for supporting a wide range of tick vector species (Bahiru and Zewdu 2021). By integrating extensive field surveys with robust statistical and spatial modeling, our findings revealed a complex interplay of ecological, host-related, and environmental factors that govern tick populations (Namgyal et al. 2021; Paucar et al. 2022). This integrated approach not only identifies the dominant tick species and their associated risk factors but also delineates geographic hotspots of potential infestation, providing a critical evidence base for targeted control strategies (Khwarahm 2023; Tian et al. 2024).

Tick Species Diversity and Distribution

Confirming the high biodiversity of Ethiopia’s ixodid fauna (Kaba 2022; Tarekegn et al. 2025), this study identified twelve tick species, many of which are known vectors of significant veterinary diseases (Ayana et al. 2021; Etiang et al. 2024). The number of tick species recorded here is higher than the six species reported by Hadgu et al. (2018) in northern Ethiopia, reflecting the unique ecological heterogeneity of North Wollo zone.

A. variegatum, the most prevalent species, was most abundant in the warm, humid lowland and midland zones. This pattern is consistent with Silatsa et al. (2019) in Cameroon and Kaba (2022) in other parts of Ethiopia, both of whom reported its similar ecological preferences. In contrast, R. decoloratus predominated in the cooler, high‑rainfall highlands, aligning with Ayalew et al. (2014) in central Ethiopia but differing from Tesfaye et al. (2023), who found higher abundance in mid‑altitude areas. This discrepancy may be possibly due to differences in rainfall intensity and host movement patterns between regions.

Rhipicephalus pulchellus showed strong affinity for the arid and semi-arid lowlands bordering the Afar region, corroborating Hadgu et al. (2018) and Tesfaye et al. (2023), who also reported its dominance in dry environments. This confirms its well-established niche in areas with low annual rainfall (Walker et al. 2014). A. cohaerens and R. evertsi evertsi were abundant in midland and lowland zones. The widespread presence of R. evertsi evertsi supports (Ayalew et al. 2014), who described it as a generalist species with a broad environmental tolerance, but our finding of high prevalence in larger herds adds new evidence on the role of host density. The remaining species were encountered infrequently, likely reflecting their more specialized habitat requirements or seasonal population dynamics not fully captured by our sampling period (Pegram et al. 1981).

Risk Factors Associated with Tick Infestation

Our logistic regression analysis identified host, management and environmental risk factors associated with tick infestation. Poor body condition identified as a significant risk factor for Amblyomma infestation, consistent with Hadgu et al. (2018), who linked compromised immunity to higher tick loads. Management practices also played a crucial role. However, our observation that animal movement strongly predicted R. pulchellus infestation contrasts with Tesfaye et al. (2023), who emphasized herd size as the main driver. This difference likely reflects the transhumance practices in North Wollo, where seasonal migration is more pronounced to access pasture and water resources.

Similarly, larger herd sizes increased the risk of R. evertsi evertsi infestation, in agreement with Etiang et al. (2024) in Kenya, likely due to higher host density supporting a more stable tick population. The strong association between tick presence and geographic variables such as altitude and latitude reinforces Alemayehu et al. (2020), but our results highlight altitude as a more dominant factor than rainfall, suggesting that temperature constraints are particularly important in this region.

Attachment site preferences (Amblyomma on udder/scrotum, Rhipicephalus on ano‑vulval region) were consistent with Ahmed (2016) and Lemu et al. (2023), and is likely driven by a combination of host skin thickness, microclimate, and tick mouthpart morphology (Abdela 2016).

Tick Burden and Intensity in Correlation with Potential Factors

The mean tick load noted in this study was higher than previously reported in northwest Ethiopia (Tarekegn et al. 2025), with most cattle exhibiting low infestation levels while a notable proportion experienced heavy burdens. This is consistent with recent Ethiopian studies (Tarekegn et al. 2025; Kidanemariam et al. 2023) that reported variable tick intensity per risk factors. The variations in tick intensity may be due to influence of host management practices, regional differences in tick ecology, and acaricide application on infestation pressure. While earlier studies focused on easily identifiable adult ticks, our approach included a more comprehensive count, potentially capturing a wider spectrum of tick burden.

Altitude indicated as a potential risk factor, with cattle in lowland regions harboring higher tick loads compared to those in highland areas. This pattern is inline with Ethiopian studies conducted in Ambo and Jimma, where warm and humid climates support tick survival and reproduction (Tadese and Gadise 2022; Esayas 2024) while cooler highland conditions suppress tick development (Mapholi et al. 2022; Esayas 2024). Similar ecological relationship have been noted throughout Africa, where tropical lowlands consistently support higher tick populations (Makwarela et al. 2025).

Additionally, host factors were crucial. According to findings from Arsi-Negelle (Seyoum and Abate 2020) and Bedele district (Lemu et al. 2023), older cattle had greater infestations than adult and young animals. This was probably caused by extended exposure, decreased grooming effectiveness, or age-related immunological decline. Females were marginally more infested than males, but there were no significant variations between the sexes. Physiological stress in females may increase vulnerability, according to certain studies (Heylen et al. 2023; Lemu et al. 2023), while other studies find increased male infection due to roaming behaviour. There were clear breed differences, with crossbred cattle carrying noticeably more ticks than indigenous breeds. This suggests that local breeds are more resistant to ectoparasites (Munaja and Yonas 2020; Nwachukwu et al. 2021). Tick load and body condition score were inversely correlated, with cattle in poor health having higher tick infestations. This corroborates research from East and West Africa that emphasises the contribution of immunity and nutrition on ectoparasite resistance (Heylen et al. 2023).

Management system and herd size further impacted tick infestation. Extensive management systems and large herds were linked to higher tick burdens, perhaps as a result of increased exposure and reduced control efforts, consistent with Ethiopian and African studies (Chepkwony et al. 2021; Kidanemariam et al. 2023; Tarekegn et al. 2025). Movement patterns also contributed, with cattle moving over long distances acquiring greater infestations, underscoring the importance of mobility in tick transmission dynamics. Similar associations have been seen throughout the world, with tropical and subtropical areas having more tick problems because of their advantageous conditions and management strategies (Mollong et al. 2025).

Habitat Suitability and Key Environmental Drivers

MaxEnt modeling yielded robust predictive performance (AUC > 0.83) for all five dominant species. To our knowledge, this is the first study to model the potential distribution of ixodid ticks in northeastern Ethiopia, extending predictive approaches beyond descriptive surveys and providing spatially explicit risk maps for integrated tick control. The models identified NDVI and elevation (DEM) as the most important predictors. The importance of NDVI in our models aligns with Khwarahm (2023) and Zannou et al. (2022), who also identified vegetation greenness as a key driver of tick survival. However, unlike Fei et al. (2024), who found precipitation seasonality as the strongest predictor in Asian contexts, our models highlight elevation as more influential, underscoring the role of Ethiopia’s altitudinal gradients in shaping tick ecology.

NDVI, a measure of vegetation greenness, was the primary driver for the three most widespread species: A. variegatum, R. decoloratus, and R. evertsi evertsi. This is logical, as dense vegetation provides a humid microclimate crucial for off-host tick survival and offers cover from desiccation and predation (Zannou et al. 2022). Furthermore, high NDVI indicates better forage, which attracts host animals and concentrates tick populations (Khwarahm 2023). Elevation, which is strongly correlated with temperature and humidity, was the main predictor for A. cohaerens and R. pulchellus. The negative relationship between tick suitability and high altitudes observed in our models aligns with the known physiological constraints that low temperatures place on tick development and questing activity (Gilbert 2010; Tagwireyi et al. 2022).

A critical strength of this study is the synthesis of field survey data with predictive modeling. The risk factors identified through logistic regression (e.g., agroecology) were spatially validated and explained by the habitat suitability maps. For instance, the high prevalence of A. variegatum in lowland and midland zones was mirrored by the model’s prediction of high habitat suitability in those same areas.

Crucially, the models revealed significant geographic overlap in suitable habitats, particularly in the Woldia and northern Habru districts. These areas, characterized by midland agroecology, represent high-risk “hotspots” where cattle are likely to be co-infested with multiple tick species. This co-infestation increases the risk of complex, multi-pathogen transmission scenarios, making these hotspots priority areas for enhanced surveillance and integrated control efforts.

Limitations and Future Directions

While this study provided valuable insights, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design offers only a snapshot in time and may not capture the full extent of seasonal variations in tick populations. Furthermore, while MaxEnt is a powerful tool, our models did not incorporate potentially important variables like soil type or detailed host movement patterns, which could refine future predictions. Future longitudinal studies, as recommended by Etiang et al. (2024), would clarify seasonal dynamics. Our call for molecular screening to confirm species identification and for tick-borne pathogens adds novelty, as few Ethiopian tick studies (e.g., Ayalew et al. 2014) have incorporated pathogen detection. This would provide a more complete picture of the epidemiological risk of TBDs in the region.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study confirmed North Wollo as a region of high ixodid tick diversity, identifying twelve species whose distributions are strongly partitioned by agro-ecological conditions. The findings showed that tick infestation in cattle is influenced by a complex interplay of host, management, and ecological factors. The dominant species, including A. variegatum and R. decoloratus, exhibited distinct environmental preferences. Our robust MaxEnt models corroborated these field observations, identifying NDVI, elevation, and land cover as the primary drivers of habitat suitability.

Crucially, the models pinpoint the eastern midland and lowland plains of North Wollo, particularly in the Habru, eastern Guba-lafto and Woldia districts, as high-risk hotspots where multiple tick species are likely to coexist. These findings provide a clear directive for public health and veterinary authorities: control efforts should be strategically focused on these identified high‑risk areas and groups such as crossbreeds, lowland cattle, poorly conditioned animals, and those extensively managed or moved over long distances. Furthermore, this research highlights the need for subsequent studies on the prevalence of tick-borne pathogens and the efficacy of current acaricide treatments. Future work employing integrated modeling to project tick distributions under climate change is also warranted to enhance long-term prevention strategies.
