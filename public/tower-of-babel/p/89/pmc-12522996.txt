Severe central airway obstruction caused by primary endobronchial B-cell lymphoma: three case reports and literature review
Authors: Cai-li Li, Wei Zhou, Mo Chen, Shuo Li, Bao-yuan Chen, Yu-bao Wang, Hai-yan Zhao, Jing Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral airway obstruction is associated with severe clinical manifestations and a high mortality rate. To our knowledge, there have been very few published reports describing isolated central airway lesions caused by primary lymphoma without extrathoracic or extranodal involvement. This is the first case of malignant tracheal obstruction caused by primary B-cell lymphoma without involvement of the main stem bronchi, lobar bronchi, or mediastinal structures. Case Presentation Three patients were admitted to our hospital with complaints of progressively worsening cough accompanied by wheezing. Chest computed tomography (CT) scans revealed soft-tissue-density masses located in the central trachea. Pathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of lymphoma. Importantly, no significant enlargement of mediastinal or cervical lymph nodes was identified. Conclusion Endobronchial lymphoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of central airway masses, particularly when clinical and imaging findings are inconclusive.Keywords: Primary b-cell lymphoma, Central airway obstruction, Extranodal;tracheal stenosis

Introduction

Central airway obstruction (CAO), defined as an occlusion exceeding 50% of the trachea, mainstem bronchi, or lobar bronchi, is associated with severe clinical manifestations and a high mortality rate. It may result from extrinsic compression by surrounding structures, direct invasion from adjacent tumors, metastatic lesions, or, less frequently, primary intrinsic endobronchial malignancies. Although rare, primary endobronchial lymphoma presenting as extranodal lymphoma can also lead to airway obstruction [1]. This condition may result in respiratory failure [2], life-threatening airway compromise, and contribute to a poorer prognosis. Herein, we report three cases of primary endobronchial B-cell lymphoma, in which the patients experienced the rare occurrence of central airway obstruction.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 78-year-old female patient was referred to our hospital due to persistent cough with sputum production for over a year, which had recently worsened by wheezing over the past three months. She reported no history of disease and denied the use of medications or prior surgeries. She has a history of passive smoking exposure. Lung function testing was performed, revealing extremely severe obstructive ventilatory dysfunction, a mild reduction in diffusion capacity, and a normal residual volume/total lung capacity ratio (Fig. 1A). The chest computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated a soft tissue-density nodule within the trachea, along with tracheal wall thickening. The nodule was adherent to the left posterior wall, resulting in airway lumen narrowing. Notably, secretions and mucus were observed within the tracheal lumen and bilateral main bronchi (Fig. 1B and C). Physical examination and laboratory tests were normal. A rigid bronchoscopy was conducted and identified a substantial soft tissue mass originating from the upper segment of the main bronchus. Following the displacement of the lesion, ventilation of the main bronchus was restored, and endoscopic resection was performed. Laser tumor ablation was utilized to achieve enucleation of the mass (Fig. 1D-F). The operating time lasted an hour and a half. Pathological examination revealed a mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma of extranodal marginal zone B cells (Fig. 1G). Due to financial limitations, the patient declined further PET-CT evaluation and subsequent treatment. But she reminded well a year after discharge from hospital.

(B, C) CT of the chest revealed a soft tissue-density nodule within the trachea and thickened trachea wall(B, arrow shows the central bronchial stem), without enlarged lymph nodes compressing the trachea and main bronchus (C, arrow shows none enlarged lymph nodes); (D) bronchoscopic view of the trachea and carina showed significant soft mass that originated in the upper segment of the main bronchus; (E)Laser tumor ablation was used to achieve enucleation of the mass; (F) after interventional bronchoscopies, the main bronchi were rechanneled and some mucosal lesions remained; (G) pathological examination of the tumor showed lymphocyte proliferation was significant in mucosal interstitium; (H) a diffuse infiltrate of large pleomorphic lymphoid cells stained positively for CD20, indicating high grade B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (magnification, ×400); (G) the Ki67 proliferation fraction was 15% (magnification, ×400)

Case2

A 69-year-old female patient was referred to our hospital due to intermittent cough and sputum production over the past month. The patient had no significant medical history or previous surgeries. Pulmonary function tests were within normal limits. A chest computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated a soft tissue-density nodule located in the posterior tracheal wall, resulting in airway lumen narrowing. The lesion presented as heterogeneous, with a maximum cross-sectional area measuring 14 mm × 9 mm and an indistinct boundary with the esophagus (Fig. 2A). Physical examination and laboratory tests were normal. Rigid bronchoscopy demonstrated a prominent soft tissue mass located in the upper trachea near the glottis, leading to central airway obstruction. Laser ablation and biopsy forceps were utilized to resect the tumor, resulting in a significantly clearer tracheal lumen (Fig. 2). The operating time was two hours. Pathological analysis indicated the diagnosis of MALT lymphoma. The patient was then transferred to the hematology department for further chemotherapy and was advised accordingly.

(A) CT of the chest revealed a soft tissue-density nodule in the posterior tracheal lumen; (B) bronchoscopic view of the trachea showed a significant soft mass at the upper trachea near the glottis, causing central airway obstruction. (C)The tumor was eliminated by laser ablation and biopsy forceps; (D) after interventional bronchoscopies, the main bronchi were rechanneled and much more clear than before; (G) pathological examination showed that mucosal interstitial lymphocyte proliferation was significant; (H) a diffuse infiltrate of large pleomorphic lymphoid cells stained positively for CD20, indicating B-cell lymphoma (magnification, ×400); (G) the Ki67 proliferation fraction was 15% (magnification, ×400)

Case 3

A 47-year-old man was referred to our hospital for evaluation of a two-month history of cough with sputum production. He denied using medications, having prior surgeries, or having a smoking history. Two months prior to admission, he began experiencing progressive dyspnea on exertion. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated a plateau-like pattern during the expiratory phase, along with mild impairment of diffusion capacity (Fig. 3A). A chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed thickening of the tracheal wall in the right anterior region, accompanied by ring-like enhancement (Fig. 3B). Physical examination and laboratory tests were normal. Subsequently, a rigid bronchoscopy was performed, which identified a significant soft tissue mass originating from the upper segment of the main bronchus. The lesion was found to be obstructing the lumen of the main bronchus, likely contributing to the severe ventilatory dysfunction observed in the lung function tests. A biopsy was conducted during the bronchoscopy (Fig. 3D and E). The operation lasted one hour. Pathological examination confirmed B-cell lymphoma (Fig. 3F), with CD20 positivity indicating a patchy distribution of B cells. The patient was then transferred to the hematology department for further treatment and responded well to chemotherapy.

Discussion

Primary tracheal non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma constitutes merely 0.2–3% of all tracheal tumors [3, 4]. While extranodal lymphomas are not uncommon, involvement of the tracheobronchial tree by extranodal lymphoma is relatively rare, accounting for only 3.6% of cases [5]. Among the various histological subtypes of primary tracheal lymphomas, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma [4] and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [6] are the most prevalent. MALT lymphomas, characterized as low-grade malignancies, tend to remain localized to the primary site and exhibit favorable responses to localized treatments such as surgery or radiotherapy, resulting in more positive outcomes and prognoses compared to other extranodal lymphomas reported in the literature [7]. Although B-cell lymphomas have been documented, they are less frequent, accounting for approximately 20–25% of primary pulmonary lymphomas [8]. As exemplified in our case series, two cases were identified as MALT lymphoma, and one case was classified as B-cell lymphoma.

Endobronchial lymphoma is categorized into two types based on the pattern of involvement. Type I involves diffuse submucosal infiltration secondary to systemic lymphoma, which may result from hematogenous or lymphatic dissemination. Type II, as seen in our patients, encompasses airway involvement due to local tumors originating either from adjacent lymph nodes or directly from bronchial-associated lymphoid tissue. In all cases of Type II lesions, respiratory obstruction symptoms such as cough or wheezing were present, as exemplified by our patient cohort [9].

The most frequently reported symptoms include dyspnea, cough, and wheezing, which are non-specific in nature. Due to the presence of these symptoms, the condition was initially misdiagnosed as bronchial asthma. However, clinical manifestations vary depending on the severity of central airway obstruction. Patients with mild tracheal stenosis (stenosis less than 50%) typically remain asymptomatic. Moderate obstructions, resulting in an airway lumen reduction between 51% and 70%, may present with symptoms such as cough, hemoptysis, shortness of breath, chest pain, wheezing, and recurrent pneumonia or atelectasis. Severe stenosis (> 70%) should be suspected in cases of acute respiratory failure, necessitating immediate intervention to alleviate airway obstruction and ensure adequate ventilation.

CT with tracheal protocols is the preferred method for obtaining precise measurements of lesion length, the extent of airway narrowing, distal airway patency, and anatomical relationships with structures surrounding the airways. Thin-slice imaging can facilitate three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction, which is particularly advantageous for preoperative assessment of airway anatomy in relation to surrounding structures and for virtual bronchoscopic evaluation. Dynamic CT has proven efficacy in diagnosing tracheobronchomalacia (TBM). While MRI can be utilized to visualize the mediastinum, particularly vascular structures, its utility in this context is generally limited.

Both flexible and rigid bronchoscopy, as diagnostic tools, provide real-time assessment of central airway obstruction and are considered the gold standard for evaluating the morphology and extent of such obstructions. Performing an endobronchial biopsy to determine the pathological type is crucial, as this information significantly influences therapeutic decision-making. The clinical heterogeneity observed in malignant central airway obstruction provides diverse opportunities to adapt and utilize endoscopic techniques and instruments. Rapid methods for restoring airway patency include mechanical debridement, thermal ablation, cryotherapy, and airway stenting. Delayed bronchoscopic interventions, such as photodynamic therapy (PDT) and brachytherapy, can also be employed to establish airway patency in non-emergent scenarios. Retrospective analyses of therapeutic success and complications indicate that symptoms of malignant central airway obstruction can often be effectively palliated. Furthermore, a thorough evaluation of risks and benefits tailored to each individual patient is fundamental during the decision-making process.

Given the rarity of this diagnosis, there are currently no established standard treatment protocols for patients with primary tracheal lymphoma. The choice of treatment strategy is determined based on histological findings, clinical manifestations, and the severity of airway obstruction. Surgical intervention, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy are commonly employed either as monotherapies or in combination. For patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, a regimen combining intensive chemotherapy (similar to the CHOP protocol) with the anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody rituximab is recommended [10]. In cases of bilateral or extrapulmonary lesions, recurrence, or progression of pulmonary MALT lymphoma, chemotherapy alone may be considered a viable therapeutic option. However, the efficacy of multi-agent regimens such as CHOP has not been shown to be superior to that of single-agent chemotherapy using cladribine or fludarabine [6, 11]. Anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, particularly rituximab, have demonstrated significant effectiveness, achieving remission rates of approximately 70% in MALT lymphoma regardless of the disease location [12]. In cases of bulky disease (defined as a tumor mass greater than 10 cm in the largest diameter), radiotherapy can serve as an adjunct to chemotherapy. Temporary tracheal stenting is recommended for patients with tracheal lymphoma when acute airway stabilization and urgent palliation of symptoms are required, followed by chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, given the high sensitivity of primary tracheal lymphoma to these modalities [13]. Debulking surgery remains the mainstay of initial treatment in cases of severe upper airway obstruction caused by endobronchial lymphoma to promptly mitigate the risk of asphyxia. A watch-and-wait strategy may be appropriate for asymptomatic patients with MALT lymphoma [8].

Although primary lymphomas originating from the trachea are generally associated with a favorable prognosis, this prognosis appears to depend on the histological type. Specifically, the prognosis for patients with endobronchial lymphoma is significantly better compared with that of patients with aggressive lymphoma, showing a complete response rate of 79% and a partial response rate of 21% [6].

Conclusion

Despite its rarity, a high index of suspicion for endobronchial lymphoma is necessary when evaluating potential causes of central airway obstruction. This condition can present as an isolated central airway lesion caused by lymphoma, without involvement of extrathoracic or extranodal sites. Patients diagnosed with isolated endobronchial lymphoma typically have a more favorable prognosis compared to those with more aggressive forms of lymphoma. Bronchoscopic evaluation with histopathological biopsy is essential for identifying the specific lymphoma subtype. Both flexible and rigid bronchoscopy may be employed not only for diagnostic purposes but also to perform therapeutic interventions, including mechanical debridement, thermal ablation, cryotherapy, and airway stenting.
