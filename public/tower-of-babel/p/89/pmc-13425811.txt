Enzymatically triggered glucose-releasing scaffolds as nutritional supports for millimeter-scale tissue engineering
Authors: Panitporn Laowpanitchakorn, Marie Piantino, Tomoya Matsuo, Michiya Matsusaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConstruction of millimeter-sized tissues to mimic real human tissue or organ is still challenging, as oxygen and nutrients have a diffusion limit of only 100–200 μm from blood vessels. Almost all mammalian cells except red blood cells and platelets need a glucose supply to support their metabolic activities and functions. Inspired by the properties of blood vessels, we developed an alginate scaffold that is capable to release glucose by hydrolysis of glycogen via enzymes such as amyloglucosidase in this study. The scaffold can be flexibly shaped into glucose-releasing capsules (Glc-RCs) and glucose-releasing fibers (Glc-RFs). It was demonstrated that Glc-RCs can provide sustained release of glucose for up to 4 days and have no negative effect on C2C12 mouse myoblast cell and human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell proliferation. Glc-RCs could also support differentiation of C2C12 into myotubes after 7 days of application without the need for media replenishment. Glc-RFs were incorporated into millimeter-scale 3D tissue-engineered constructs, where they reduced the apoptotic activity of normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs) located within approximately 1000 μm from the fibers. In particular, the relationship between glucose diffusion distance and resulting cellular responses like apoptosis has not previously been documented in other glucose-releasing material studies. Thus, the developed glucose-releasing scaffold has the potential to be shaped into different sizes by 3D extrusion printing to serve as a localized glucose delivery system to further study glucose diffusion profiles as well as supplying glucose for supporting mm-sized tissue construction.Keywords: Large-sized tissue engineering, Glucose-releasing hydrogel, Sustained release, Spatial controlled release, Glucose supply, 3D printing

Introduction

In the discipline of tissue engineering, scaffolds, signaling molecules, and cells have been incorporated and combined with each other to serve as a placement for damaged tissue [1]. However, it is still challenging to construct over 1 mm-sized (mm-sized) tissues for medical applications such as clinical implantation. Several studies have reported that oxygen and small nutrient molecules including glucose cannot reach the inner regions of tissues due to the diffusion limitation of only around 200 μm from the periphery [[2], [3], [4]]. To solve this problem, incorporation of vasculatures has been demonstrated to decrease cellular hypoxia and apoptotic activities, as well as allow construction of human organoids with larger diameters of up to around 5 mm. However, it is still difficult to completely suppress hypoxic and apoptotic activities of cells located in the center of organoids [5]. There are also other available strategies, such as cell sheet assembly into myocardial tissues with a thickness of approximately 80 μm, as introduced by Shimizu et al. Nevertheless, this method is still limited with respect to resulting tissue thickness, as stacking more than three layers of cell sheets resulted in necrotic tissue. Repeated implantation of triple-layered cell sheets allowed infiltration of vessels and construction of 1 mm-thick tissue, but the surgical procedures are still complicated and require a long waiting time needed to construct the tissue [6].

Blood vessels play a major role in maintaining cell viability and the function of mm-sized tissues. However, the biofabrication of homogeneously-distributed engineered blood vessels and capillaries to transport nutrients and oxygen in mm-sized tissue remains challenging [7]. Biofabrication of larger blood vessels (100–1000 μm) mostly relies on complex perfusion systems and still faces the issue of collapsing because of surrounding tissue pressure, such as in sacrificial printing [8]. On the other hand, smaller blood vessels, such as capillaries (<100 μm), are mostly fabricated by the self-organization of endothelial cells. However, it is still difficult to control orientation and needs complex co-culture conditions such as co-culture with fibroblasts [9,10]. More simple and feasible fabrication methods are still under development to achieve multiscale engineered blood vessels. For example, additive manufacturing of tubular engineered blood vessels without endothelial cells (ECs) and perfusion of this structure were demonstrated to support surrounding cell survival and proliferation [11].

Furthermore, there have been several scaffolds with properties inspired by blood vessels in development, which function without the need of ECs to release oxygen and nutrients. For example, oxygen-releasing scaffolds using calcium peroxide (CaO2) have attracted much attention recently. We demonstrated that CaO2 microparticles coated with a hydroxyapatite layer, then embedded in gelatin hydrogels, could release oxygen to support cell proliferation for up to 10 days in a hypoxic environment [12]. Other than oxygen-releasing materials, there are also currently a few studies on glucose-releasing materials. Polysaccharides are generally present as the glucose source, then degraded by an enzyme to release glucose to the cells encapsulated inside the glucose-releasing scaffold system itself. For example, in a study by Zargarzadeh et al., glucose supply relied on the self-degradation of laminaran scaffolds mediated by amyloglucosidase (AMG). However, this approach led to significant leakage of AMG from the scaffolding material, raising concerns about the short duration of glucose availability [13,14]. In other studies, fibrin hydrogel or sodium alginate (SA) hydrogels were chosen to encapsulate starch, which AMG degraded to produce glucose [15,16]. However, starch is non-soluble in neutral water at 20 °C due to its crystalline structure and higher temperatures and chemical modifications are required to increase the solubility [17,18]. Therefore, only up to 20 mg/mL of starch could be dissolved in an aqueous solution [15]. This caused limited glucose production of only up to 8 mM, where burst release of glucose could also be observed only after 400 to 600 s of enzymatic reaction. Hence, this study aims to explore other polysaccharide options for development of an EC-free scaffold with “blood vessel-inspired” properties that can provide sustainable release of glucose to the surrounding tissues.

SA is a natural polysaccharide widely used for tissue engineering applications due to its fast gelation by coordination bonding with calcium ions [19,20], not covalent bonding which often causes cytotoxicity due to the use of photoinitiators or/and crosslinkers. Accordingly, SA has been chosen as the stable main material for the encapsulation of candidate polysaccharides as a glucose source because it is not involved in the glucose production process, since AMG specifically cleaves α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic linkages at the non-reducing ends of polysaccharides and degrades them into glucose [21].

In this study, three candidate polysaccharides of different molecular weights, namely maltodextrin, dextran, and glycogen (GLY) were used as potential sources of glucose for comparing their time-dependent glucose release profile, encapsulation, retention efficiency inside the scaffold, and their enzymatic degradation by AMG (Scheme 1). Furthermore, the application of the developed glucose-releasing scaffolding material to support cell metabolic activities was also investigated. Since muscular cells, including cardiomyocytes, have been shown to have a higher glucose consumption rate, the C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line was chosen for this study [22]. The metabolic activities of C2C12, especially differentiation, have been reported to be dependent on glucose as has the survival of primary cells, such as ADSCs [23,24]. We investigated the contribution of glucose as well as released glucose from the developed scaffold on C2C12 and ADSCs survival and proliferation, and myogenic differentiation of C2C12. Moreover, the scaffold's ability to support 3D cell culture by releasing glucose to the surrounding cells within a tissue was also demonstrated.

Optimization of short-term glucose production by AMG in solution

30 mg/mL polysaccharide solutions were prepared by dissolving maltodextrin, dextran, and GLY in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4). 1 mg/mL AMG stock solution was prepared by dissolving AMG powder in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4). After that, 40 μL of stock AMG solution was added to the polysaccharide solution to a final concentration of 20 μg/mL. The reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 1 h. After the reaction, 1 mL of supernatant was taken in an Eppendorf tube and heated in a heat block (Eppendorf, ThermoStat plus, Hamburg, Germany) at 99 °C for 20 min to deactivate AMG [31]. The amount of glucose produced was measured by the addition of 50 μL deactivated supernatant in each well of a 24-well plate (IWAKI, 3820-024, Tokyo, Japan), followed by the addition of 1 mL of glucose assay reagent. The assay was in reaction for 15 min at 25 °C, then the absorbance was detected at the wavelength of 340 nm using a microplate absorption spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad, xMark, California, USA). At the same time, 50 μL of standard glucose solution with 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mg/mL concentrations were mixed with 1 mL of glucose assay reagent to construct a standard curve.

For optimization of buffer type, GLY was dissolved in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 50 mM acetate (pH 7.4), and 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) to reach a final concentration of 30 mg/mL. After that, 20 μL of AMG stock solution was added to a final concentration of 20 μg/mL. The reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 1 h. After that, the detection of produced glucose was carried out by glucose assay as described above. In the case of the reaction pH and temperature optimization, GLY was dissolved in 50 mM acetate buffer (pH 4.3) and 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4) to obtain a final concentration of 30 mg/mL. After that, 40 μL of AMG stock solution was added to a final AMG concentration of 20 μg/mL. The reaction was carried out at 60 °C in acetate buffer, or at 37 °C in HEPES buffer for 1 h.

Preparation and characterization of Glc-RCs

To determine the encapsulation efficiency and remaining ratio, AMG and GLY were labelled with fluorescein-4-isothiocyanate (FITC) and rhodamine B isothiocyanate (RBITC), respectively. F-AMG was prepared by the addition of 10 equivalents of FITC to 1 equivalent of AMG dissolved in 100 mM carbonate buffer (pH 9). Then, FITC was added in increments every 10 min to the AMG solution stirred at 250 rpm. The reaction was left on stirring at 4 °C for 8–12 h. After that, the resulting solution was dialyzed (MWCO 15 kDa) at 4 °C for 15 h in double-distilled water, with the water changed every 4 h. The F-AMG obtained was freeze-dried for 2–3 days. To prepare R-GLY, 1 equivalent of GLY was added to dissolve in 0.35 equivalents of TEA in DMSO solution. After that, 0.01 equivalents of RBITC and 0.06 equivalents of DBTDL were added to the solution, and the reaction was left on stirring at 95 °C in a silicone oil bath for 24 h. Then, the resulting solution was dialyzed (MWCO 15 kDa) at 25 °C in methanol and then in double-distilled water for a total of 2–3 days. The R-GLY obtained was freeze-dried for 2–3 days.

For the fabrication of Glc-RCs, the solution of 200 μg/mL F-AMG, 30 mg/mL R-GLY, and 80 mg/mL SA was dropped at a dispensing speed of 1 mL/min into 10 mL of 200 mg/mL CaCl2 solution while stirring at 600 rpm. The dropping setup was composed of a syringe pump (KD Scientific, KDS210, Massachusetts, USA), 5 mL syringe with concentric Luer Lock (Terumo, SS-05LZ, Tokyo, Japan), and a 25G 90° angle nozzle (San-ei tech, SH25-B-90, Chiba, Japan). Fabricated Glc-RCs were collected immediately after fabrication, transferred to a cell strainer with a 500 μm mesh size (PluriSelect Life Science, 43-50500-01, Leipzig, Germany), then washed with cold 50 mM HEPES buffer solution (pH 7.4) 3 times before the fluorescence images of encapsulated F-AMG and R-GLY were taken using a confocal laser microscope (Olympus Life Science, FV3000, Tokyo, Japan). The same image acquisition settings including laser power (2.0%) and sensitivity (500 V) were maintained for observation of both F-AMG and R-GLY. 30 Glc-RCs were then incubated in 2 mL of HEPES buffer solution at 37 °C for 1, 4, 7, and 10 days. After that, the capsules were dissolved in 100 mM EDTA-4Na solution for 2 h at 4 °C. The amount of F-AMG and R-GLY retained in the alginate capsules was quantified by measurement of fluorescence intensity (FI) using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (JASCO, FP-8500, Tokyo, Japan). The FI values of F-AMG were obtained at an excitation wavelength of 495 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm, while the values of R-GLY were obtained at an excitation wavelength of 543 nm and an emission wavelength of 580 nm. Optimization of AMG concentration for short-term glucose release by Glc-RCs

The Glc-RCs were fabricated by dropping the solution of 0, 20, 100, 200, and 1000 μg/mL AMG, 30 mg/mL GLY, and 80 mg/mL Alg at a dispensing speed of 1 mL/min into 10 mL of 200 mg/mL CaCl2 solution while stirring at 600 rpm. Fabricated Glc-RCs were collected immediately after fabrication, transferred to a cell strainer with a 500 μm mesh size (PluriSelect Life Science, 43-50500-01, Leipzig, Germany), then washed with cold 50 mM HEPES buffer solution (pH 7.4) 3 times. 30 Glc-RCs were incubated in 2 mL of HEPES buffer solution at 37 °C for 1 h. After that, 200 μL of HEPES supernatant was collected, and the released glucose amount was measured by glucose assay using the method previously described.

Long-term glucose production and release by Glc-RCs

To investigate long-term glucose production and release profile, the Glc-RCs were fabricated by dropping the solution of 30 mg/mL GLY, 20 μg/mL or 200 μg/mL AMG, and 80 mg/mL Alg at a dispensing speed of 1 mL/min into 10 mL of 200 mg/mL CaCl2 solution while stirring at 600 rpm. Fabricated Glc-RCs were collected immediately after fabrication, transferred to a cell strainer with a 500 μm mesh size (PluriSelect Life Science, 43-50500-01, Leipzig, Germany), then washed with cold 50 mM HEPES buffer solution (pH 7.4) 3 times. 30 Glc-RCs were incubated in 2 mL of HEPES buffer solution at 37 °C for 6 h, 1, 4, 7, and 10 days. After that, 200 μL of HEPES supernatant was collected and the released glucose amount was measured by glucose assay using the method previously described in 4.2.

Contribution of Glc-RCs to C2C12 and ADSCs proliferation, and C2C12 differentiation

The commercial C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line was purchased from ATCC (CRL-1772). C2C12 cells were cultured under standard normoxic cell culture conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2, and 21% O2 in high glucose DMEM culture medium (Nacalai Tesque, 08458-16, Kyoto, Japan) with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Cells of passages 5–7 were used for the experiments. To evaluate glucose-dependent C2C12 proliferation, 0.6 × 105 C2C12 cells were seeded in each well of a 24-well plate (IWAKI, 3820-024, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were left to adhere on the well plate surface in the presence of DMEM with glucose (10% FBS, 1% antibiotics) for 24 h. After that, the culture media were changed to culture media with low FBS concentration (DMEM with 2% FBS and 1% antibiotics) without glucose (Nacalai Tesque, 09891-25, Kyoto, Japan) or with 5 or 25 mM glucose concentrations. Reagents for Glc-RCs fabrication including 50 mM HEPES buffer solution with 1% antibiotics, CaCl2 solution, and GLY solution, were filter-sterilized using syringe filter units with a 0.22 μm pore size (MilliporeSigma, SLGPR33RS, Massachusetts, USA). Glc-RCs were prepared by the previously described procedure under sterile conditions. In brief, 30 mg/mL of sterile-filtered GLY solution was added to SA powder to reach a final SA concentration of 80 mg/mL, then mixed with AMG solution to a final concentration of 200 μg/mL. After fabrication, 24-well inserts with a 0.4 μm (Greiner Bio-One, 662641, Kremsmünster, Austria) or 8.0 μm pore size (Corning, 3464, New York, USA) containing 30 Glc-RCs per well were added to C2C12 cells and cultured in glucose-free DMEM media. As a positive control, C2C12 were cultured in glucose-free conditions, with 5 and 25 mM of glucose, and with Glc-RCs until day 3. Cells were harvested and then cell viability was evaluated by cell counting with Trypan blue (2420718, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using an automated cell counter (Countess II, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA).

The ADSCs used in this study were purchased from Lonza BioScience (PT-5006). ADSCs were cultured under standard normoxic cell culture conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2, and 21% O2 in high glucose DMEM culture medium (Nacalai Tesque, 08458-16, Kyoto, Japan) with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Cells of passages 6–7 were used for the experiments. For evaluation of glucose-dependent ADSCs proliferation, 0.1 × 104 ADSCs were seeded in each well of a 24-well plate (IWAKI, 3820-024, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were left to adhere on the 24-well plate surface in the presence of DMEM with glucose (10% FBS, 1% antibiotics) for 24 h. After that, the culture media were changed to culture media (DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics) without glucose (Nacalai Tesque, 09891-25, Kyoto, Japan) or with 5 or 25 mM glucose concentrations. Glc-RCs were applied to ADSCs in glucose-free culture media as previously described before cell counting by Trypan blue (2420718, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) on day 8.

To evaluate glucose-dependent C2C12 differentiation, C2C12 cells were seeded in each well of a 24-well plate, changed into differentiation media (2% FBS and 1% antibiotics) without glucose or with 5 or 25 mM glucose concentrations, then applied with Glc-RCs in glucose-free differentiation media as previously described. C2C12 cells were kept on culture until day 8 to induce their differentiation into myotubes. After that, cell differentiation was evaluated based on immunofluorescence (IF) staining for cell nuclei using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, dihydrochloride (DAPI; Thermo Fisher, D1306, Massachusetts, USA), and IF staining for Myosin 4 (MYH4, cell myotube/differentiation marker; eBioscience, 14-6503-82, California, USA). IF images were obtained using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer (CellVoyager CQ1 System Highlights, Yokogawa, Tokyo, Japan). After that, the IF images were processed using a custom-made macro under ImageJ/Fiji (version 1.54p) for automated counting of the total cell nuclei and nuclei within MYH4 negative areas. Briefly, each TIFF image was converted to 8-bit and split into separate “DAPI” for nuclei and “myo” channels for MYH4. Next, the “myo” channel was smoothed using a Gaussian blur (sigma = 2) to reduce noise, followed by automatic thresholding using the Huang algorithm, assuming a dark background, then median filtering (radius = 2), conversion to a binary mask, and hole filling to generate a clean myosin-positive region mask. The threshold for “DAPI” channel was established using the default algorithm (dark background), followed by median filtering, mask conversion, hole filling, and watershed segmentation to separate clustered nuclei. Particle analysis was performed on the DAPI mask to count total nuclei, then image subtraction (“DAPI” minus “myo”) was used to identify nuclei without overlapping “myo” signal and analyzed again to yield myosin-negative nuclear counts. Parameters (sigma = 2, threshold methods, median radius 2, watershed) were empirically chosen after comparing against manual counts to ensure accuracy and minimize noise or oversegmentation errors. Preparation and characterization of Glc-RFs

To prepare Glc-RFs, the hydrogel precursor solution consisted of 80 mg/mL SA, 30 mg/mL GLY, and 200 μg/mL of AMG were loaded into a syringe with 18G (San-ei tech, SH18-B, Chiba, Japan) and manually extruded into a 200 mg/mL CaCl2 solution. The fabricated Glc-RFs were immediately collected and cut to an approximate length of 5 mm, then washed with cold 50 mM HEPES buffer solution (pH 7.4) 3 times. Each Glc-RF was placed in a 24-well plate (IWAKI, 3820-024, Tokyo, Japan) and incubated in 1 mL of DMEM without glucose (Nacalai Tesque, 09891-25, Kyoto, Japan) (10% FBS, 1% antibiotics) at 37 °C for 1, 4, 7, and 10 days. At each timepoint, 0.5 mL of DMEM supernatant was collected and then 0.5 mL of fresh DMEM without glucose (Nacalai Tesque, 09891-25, Kyoto, Japan) (10% FBS, 1% antibiotics) was added to each well plate. The amount of glucose released from Glc-RFs was measured by using a glucose assay reagent as previously described.

Evaluation of Glc-RFs’ effect on local NHDFs morphology and apoptotic activity within the 3D tissue-engineered construct

Normal Human Dermal Fibroblasts (NHDFs) were purchased from Lonza Bioscience (CC-2509). NHDFs were cultured under standard normoxic cell culture conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2, and 21% O2. Cells of passage 12 were used for experiments. 3D tissue engineered constructs were fabricated by the addition of NHDFs to 12 U/mL thrombin, followed by 80 mg/mL fibrinogen solution in DMEM without glucose (Nacalai Tesque, 09891-25, Kyoto, Japan) (10% FBS, 1% antibiotics). The fibrin gel precursor solution with a final fibrinogen concentration of 20 mg/mL, thrombin concentration of 3 U/mL, and NHDFs concentration of 6.67 × 106 cells/mL was gently mixed before 150 μL of solution was deposited into 24-well inserts with a 0.4 μm pore size (Greiner Bio-One, 662641, Kremsmünster, Austria). Then, the 24-well inserts were pre-treated with plasma treatment using desktop vacuum plasma treatment equipment (Sakigake Semiconductor, YHS-R, Kyoto, Japan) with operational power of single phase 100 V, 50/60 Hz, 5.0 A for 2 min. Gelation of hydrogel with 1 × 105 NHDFs in each construct was carried out by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. In the experiment conditions of blank alginate fibers (SA-fibers), alginate fibers containing only GLY (GLY-fibers), alginate fibers containing only AMG (AMG-fibers), and Glc-RFs, the fabricated hydrogel fibers were placed in the 24-well inserts with a 0.4 μm pore size (Greiner Bio-One, 662641, Kremsmünster, Austria) before the addition of fibrin gel precursor solution with NHDFs.

Fabricated NHDF-laden 3D fibrin gel constructs were then cultured in 2 mL of DMEM with (Nacalai Tesque, 08458-16, Kyoto, Japan) or without glucose (Nacalai Tesque, 09891-25, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% antibiotics, and 20 μg/mL of aprotinin for 3 days before fixation with 4% PFA. Each of the fixed fibrin gel constructs were then cut into halves by transversal sections, followed by evaluation of NHDFs morphology and apoptosis inside the hydrogel constructs by immunofluorescent (IF) staining for cell nucleus, F-actin, and cleaved Caspase-3. After that, acquisition of fluorescence Z-stack images at the transversal section of NHDF-laden fibrin gel constructs (at z-position of 0–500 μm, 25 μm step size, 21 slices) was carried out using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer (Yokogawa, Tokyo, Japan). Cellular morphological parameters including cell area by F-actin, circularity, and cell aspect ratio were analyzed using Fiji software (Fig. S11). Proof of concept for glucose diffusion gradient from a glucose-generating hydrogel material

To prepare SA hydrogel fibers containing 2-NBDG (2-NBDG fibers), the hydrogel precursor solution consisted of 80 mg/mL and 100 μM 2-NBDG were loaded into a syringe with 18G (San-ei tech, SH18-B, Chiba, Japan) and manually extruded into a 200 mg/mL CaCl2 solution. The fabricated 2-NBDG fibers were immediately collected and cut to an approximate length of 5 mm, then washed with cold 50 mM HEPES buffer solution (pH 7.4) 3 times. 2-NBDG fibers were placed in the 24-well inserts with a 0.4 μm pore size (Greiner Bio-One, 662641, Kremsmünster, Austria) before the addition of fibrin gel precursor solution in PBS containing NHDFs prepared as previously described. The diffused glucose gradients were observed at 3, 5, 10, 15, and 30 min after addition of fibrin precursor solution while the gelation was taking place at 37 °C. After gelation, 100 μL of PBS were added to the top of the 3D tissue engineered constructs and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Image acquisition was carried out by using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer at the z position of 750 μm. After that, the glucose gradient profiles were quantified from the obtained confocal fluorescence images using Fiji software.

Myoblast culture and differentiation on protein-coated Glc-RFs

The SA-fibers and Glc-RFs were fabricated as previously described. After that, surface plasma treatment of Glc-RFs was carried out by using desktop vacuum plasma treatment equipment (Sakigake Semiconductor, YHS-R, Kyoto, Japan) with operational power of single-phase 100 V, 50/60 Hz, 5.0 A for 2 min. Then the Glc-RFs were immersed in a coating solution consisting of 0.85 mg/mL FN, 0.5 M CaCl2, and 3 μM of MnCl2 at 4 °C or 25 °C for 90 min.

For optimization of FN coating time, 0.765 mg/mL FN and 0.085 mg/mL FN were mixed to obtain a final FN concentration of 0.85 mg/mL in the coating solution. Plasma-treated SA-fibers were coated with Rho-FN at 25 °C for 90 min, where sequential fluorescence images of adhered Rho-FN on the surface of fibers were taken at 5, 15, 45, and 90 min after the beginning of immersion using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer (Yokogawa, Tokyo, Japan). The mean fluorescence intensity per area of SA-fibers was measured using Fiji software. For layer-by-layer (LbL) coating, the LbL coating protocol was adapted from Ref. [72]. Briefly, SA-fibers were immersed in 0.85 mg/mL FN solution with 0.5 M CaCl2 and 3 μM MnCl2 at 25 °C for 1 min. After that, SA-fibers were washed by immersion in 50 mM HEPES buffer solution (pH 7.4) containing 1% antibiotics at 25 °C for 1 min before the fibers were immersed in 1 mg/mL gelatin solution containing 0.5 M CaCl2 at 25 °C for 1 min. After washing with HEPES solution (1% antibiotics) again, the SA-fibers were repeatedly immersed in FN and gelatin solution for 10 cycles to obtain FN and gelatin LbL coating on the surface.

In the next step, 1.5 × 105 C2C12 cells in 50 μL of DMEM with (Nacalai Tesque, 08458-16, Kyoto, Japan) or without glucose (Nacalai Tesque, 09891-25, Kyoto, Japan) (without FBS, with 1% antibiotics) were seeded on the fiber surface, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h to allow cell attachment. After that, 0.5 mL of DMEM with or without glucose (10% FBS, 1% antibiotics) was added into each well containing hydrogel fibers. C2C12 cells were left to proliferate on the surface of SA-fibers, AMG-fibers, or Glc-RFs for 3 days before fixation and immunostaining for F-actin and cell nuclei. Fluorescence images of C2C12 cells proliferated on the surface of hydrogel fibers were taken using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer. To induce differentiation of C2C12 cells on the hydrogel fiber surface, proliferation media (DMEM with or without glucose, 10% FBS, 1% antibiotics) were replaced with differentiation media (DMEM with or without glucose, 2% FBS, 1% antibiotics). The C2C12 cells were differentiated for 4 days before fixation and stained for F-actin and cell nuclei. Fluorescence images of C2C12 cells proliferated on the surface of hydrogel fibers were taken using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer (Yokogawa, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

For C2C12 cells cultured on 24-well plates, the cells were washed with 1× PBS solution once before fixation with 4% PFA at 25 °C for 30 min. After washing fixed cells with 1× PBS solution 3 times, permeabilization was carried out by incubation in 0.2% Triton X-100 at 25 °C for 15 min. Next, C2C12 cells were washed with 1× PBS solution 3 times before incubation in 1% BSA solution for 1 h to block nonspecific binding of antibodies. After that, C2C12 cells were incubated with a 1/500 dilution of mouse anti-Myosin 4 Monoclonal Antibody (eBioscience, 14-6503-82, California, USA) in 1% BSA solution at 4 °C overnight for primary antibody staining. In the next step, C2C12 cells were rinsed with 1× PBS solution 3 times (5 min each time) before addition of 1/200 dilution of Alexa Fluor® 647 goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Thermo Fisher, A21235, Massachusetts, USA), 1/800 dilution of Alexa Fluor® 488 Phalloidin (Cell Signaling Technology, 8878, Boston, USA), and 1/1000 dilution of DAPI in 1% BSA solution for secondary antibody staining. After incubating at 25 °C for 2 h in the dark, C2C12 cells were washed with 1× PBS, then observation of fluorescence images was carried out using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer.

In the case of C2C12 cells proliferated on the hydrogel fiber surface and NHDFs cultured inside 3D fibrin gel constructs, the samples were first washed with 50 mM HEPES buffer solution (pH 7.4) containing 20 mM of CaCl2 once. After that, C2C12 cells were fixed in 4% PFA at 25 °C for 15 min, while NHDFs were fixed in 4% PFA at 25 °C for 20 min. Cells were washed with HEPES (20 mM CaCl2) 3 times before immunostaining with 1/800 dilution of Alexa Fluor® 488 Phalloidin (Cell Signaling Technology, 8878, Boston, USA) and 1/1000 dilution of DAPI in 1% BSA solution at 4 °C overnight. After that, the samples were rinsed with HEPES containing 20 mM CaCl2 before fluorescence image acquisition using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer.

For apoptotic activity evaluation of NHDFs inside fibrin hydrogel samples, washing, fixation, permeabilization, and blocking were conducted as previously described before incubation with a 1/400 dilution of cleaved Caspase-3 Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 9661, Boston, USA) in 1% BSA solution at 4 °C overnight for primary antibody staining. After the rinsing step, the samples were incubated in a 1/200 dilution of Alexa Fluor® 647 goat anti-Rabbit IgG antibody (Thermo Fisher, A27040, Massachusetts, USA) in 1% BSA solution for secondary antibody staining. After incubating at 25 °C for 2 h in the dark, C2C12 cells were washed with 1× PBS, then observation of fluorescence images was carried out using a benchtop CQ1 confocal quantitative image cytometer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with Graphpad Prism (version 8.0.2). The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. For the comparisons of two related groups, a two-tailed paired t-test was used. For the comparison of more than two independent groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey's test, and one-way repeated measures ANOVA were used. In the case of NHDFs' morphological parameters and apoptotic activity analysis, two-way ANOVA was used. Differences between experimental groups were statistically significant at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001 where N.S. refers to no significant difference.

Results and discussion

Enzymatic degradation studies of GLY solution

Different types of polysaccharides possess different branching structures, which consist of glucose units linked by different glycosidic bonds. Maltodextrin, dextran, and GLY were chosen because they have α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic linkages which can be cleaved by AMG (Fig. 1A). The results of glucose releasing properties by AMG degradation shown in Fig. 1B suggested that less glucose was produced by the enzymatic degradation of dextran, possibly because dextran does not consist of α-1,4 glycosidic linkages, which are hydrolyzed by AMG with higher reaction rates [25]. On the other hand, maltodextrin and GLY with α-1,4 glycosidic linkages resulted in significantly more glucose production. Even though enzymatic degradation of both maltodextrin and GLY could produce similar amounts of glucose, GLY was chosen as a glucose source in the glucose-releasing material in terms of molecular weight. The weight-average molecular weight (MW) of maltodextrin, dextran, and GLY are 1k Da, 500k Da, and 270k ∼ 3,500k Da, respectively, and thus GLY was expected to be better for retaining glucose releasing ability than smaller MW ones within the alginate encapsulating material. It is also important to mention that GLY is abundant in the human body, including muscle tissue, and is directly involved in cellular metabolism [26,27].

The effect of buffer solution on AMG activity was also investigated. Tris-HCl, which is a buffer widely used with proteins, hinders glucose production by 8-9 times when compared to acetate and HEPES buffer as shown in Fig. 1C. It was previously reported that aminoalcohols like Tris showed competitive inhibition to AMG degradation activity [28]. Indeed, Tris can bind to the active-site pocket of the AMG catalytic domain by hydrogen bonding [29]. Even though enzymatic glucose production in acetate buffer is only 0.1% lower than in HEPES, the buffering capacity of acetate buffer (pH 3.6 – 5.6) is not suitable for mammalian cell culture. HEPES was therefore selected as the solvent for the preparation of the glucose-releasing material precursor solution because of its buffering capacity at pH 7.4, and its compatibility with AMG as well as SA. Although the optimal conditions for AMG enzymatic reaction have been reported at a temperature of 60 °C and a pH of 4.3 [30,31], HEPES buffer with physiological pH and temperature conditions was selected because the purpose of the glucose-releasing scaffold material is to support cell metabolic activities and function in vitro, where the temperature and pH conditions are normally 37 °C and pH 7.4, respectively. The results in Fig. 1D demonstrated that enzymatic degradation of 30 mg/mL GLY solution by 20 μg/mL AMG activity could produce 25 μmol of glucose after 1 h at physiological conditions, which is two times lower than at optimal conditions. It has been reported that 1 g of AMG could produce 1.0 × 106 μmol of glucose after 10 min of enzymatic degradation of starch at 65 °C and a pH of 4.3 [30]. In this study, 40 μg of AMG could produce 50 μmol glucose after 1 h of enzymatic degradation of GLY, which is within the same range as previously reported [30]. Nevertheless, glucose produced at physiological conditions shows potential to maintain the culture of up to 1.25 × 108 cardiac cells for 1 h, as 0.20–1.2 μmol/h of glucose are necessary to culture 106 cardiac cells [22].

Optimization of GLY and AMG encapsulation system for development of Glc-RCs

After the optimization of polysaccharide type and buffer solvent in solution, we investigated the production of glucose by enzymatic degradation of GLY by AMG under physiological conditions when encapsulated in Glc-RCs using SA. To achieve sustained release of glucose from the developed Glc-RCs, crosslinking of encapsulating SA by calcium ions should also be optimized. First, SA with concentrations of 16 and 80 mg/mL were crosslinked with 20 and 200 mg/mL of CaCl2, respectively, while containing 200 μg/mL fluorescein-4-isothiocyanate-labelled AMG (F-AMG) and 30 mg/mL rhodamine-labelled GLY (R-GLY). The result as shown in Fig. 2A suggested that preparation of Glc-RCs by using 80 mg/mL of SA and 200 mg/mL CaCl2 resulted in optimal encapsulation efficiency of F-AMG and R-GLY of 79 and 90% when compared to the original amount in the hydrogel precursor solution, respectively. It was shown that higher concentrations of SA and CaCl2 resulted in higher retention of encapsulated AMG and GLY, which could also allow slower release of produced glucose from the hydrogel. This result is in accordance with a study by Campbell et al., where the mesh size of SA hydrogels was predicted to be inversely correlated to Ca2+ crosslinking density and had better retention of AMG and GLY [32]. When 200 μg/mL F-AMG and 30 mg/mL R-GLY were encapsulated inside the SA capsules and undergone enzymatic reaction for 10 days, almost all GLY was degraded and released by day 10, with only 5% of R-GLY remaining inside the capsules (Fig. 2B). As the mesh size of SA hydrogels was reported to be 50–200 nm, the produced glucose with a smaller hydrodynamic radius of only 0.36 nm can diffuse through this hydrogel [32,33]. Fig. S1A showed the nanometer-sized pore and relatively higher wall thickness at 48-172 μm of the capsules observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which contributed to the sustained release of produced glucose. Even though the hydrodynamic radius of AMG (7.23 nm, Fig. S1B) is half of the radius of GLY (15.8 nm, Fig. S1B), a higher percentage (20%) of F-AMG remained inside Glc-RCs at day 10 (Fig. 2B). Moreover, while the amount of both encapsulated F-AMG and R-GLY continued to decrease from day 1 to day 10 (Fig. 2B), only slight, non-significant amount of F-AMG leaked out from the capsule (Fig. S2A). Previously reported electrostatic interaction between carbohydrate recognition sites of AMG and the SA polymer chain is possibly contributing to retention of the enzyme inside the hydrogel matrix [34].

We then examined the glucose release from Glc-RCs after 1 h incubation in HEPES under physiological conditions by varying the AMG concentrations from 20 to 1000 μg/mL. The results shown in Fig. 2C demonstrated that the amount of released glucose can be controlled by variation of AMG concentrations. For the investigation of long-term glucose production and release from Glc-RCs under physiological conditions, 20 and 200 μg/mL AMG concentrations were selected, but not 1000 μg/mL due to avoiding burst release of glucose. As shown in Fig. 2D, a significant amount of glucose was produced until day 4, before a slight increase in glucose production until day 10. Different concentrations of AMG resulted in different amounts of glucose production, where 200 μg/mL AMG converted all of GLY to glucose, but 20 μg/mL AMG converted only 88% of GLY to glucose. When AMG and GLY were encapsulated inside SA hydrogels, a significant amount of glucose was also released until day 4 and 30–34 μmol of glucose was released from Glc-RCs on day 10 depending on AMG concentration, where 90-100% GLY was degraded and released in the form of glucose (Fig. 2E). Dependency of glucose release rate on AMG concentration could also be observed in Fig. S2, where detection of fluorescently labelled F-AMG and R-GLY release from Glc-RCs suggested that 20 μg/mL AMG allowed slower glucose release as illustrated in Fig. S2C. At the same time, the diameter of Glc-RCs prepared with higher AMG concentration decreased significantly (Fig. S2D). On the other hand, in the study by Zargarzadeh et al., the conversion of laminaran to glucose units by AMG was reduced in encapsulated conditions when compared to the solution condition [14]. It was stated that the active sites of AMG were not accessible to laminaran due to the conformational change of the enzyme molecules during the immobilization inside the laminaran hydrogel itself [14,35]. In our study however, encapsulation of AMG and GLY in SA hydrogel capsules did not decrease the enzymatic activity and glucose conversion of GLY, might be due to the presence of SA as a capsule component, not as a substrate of AMG.

Effect of Glc-RCs and different glucose amounts on cell proliferation and differentiation

After finding optimal fabrication conditions of the Glc-RCs for glucose production, we next evaluated their potential effects on cell behavior. Glucose is directly involved in multiple cellular metabolic pathways and has been reported in several publications to support cell viability and functions [23,36,37]. Muscle cells in particular, such as cardiomyocytes, have been reported to have a higher glucose consumption rate to support their high energy-demanding metabolism [22]. C2C12, which is a skeletal mouse myoblast cell line, showed a hindered proliferation rate and more doubling time when cultured in DMEM without glucose for 3 days [24]. Because of this, after the characterization of 10 days glucose release from Glc-RCs, the effect of released glucose on C2C12 cell proliferation rate was investigated as well as the effect of different glucose concentrations. The C2C12 cells were seeded on a 24-well plate and incubated in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with glucose for 1 day to ensure attachment, and then this medium was replaced by DMEM without glucose, or with 5 mM or 25 mM of glucose dissolved in the DMEM (Fig. 3A). The fabricated 30 Glc-RCs were placed in one of the of 24-well inserts with a 0.4 μm or 8.0 μm pore size, and added to the adhered C2C12 cells cultured in glucose-free DMEM media. The C2C12 proliferation rate was then calculated based on Trypan blue cell counting on day 3. The experimental conditions of 5 mM and 25 mM glucose were chosen according to the typical concentration in low and high glucose culture media respectively. The results in Fig. 3B and C suggested that there was no significant C2C12 proliferation rate difference in the presence of 5 mM (425%) compared to 25 mM (406%) of glucose-containing DMEM, however there was a significant decrease in C2C12 viable cell percentage to only 85% after culture in DMEM without glucose when compared to the initial seeding number. When 30 Glc-RCs were added to C2C12 in DMEM without glucose, the cells could still proliferate to 172% of the cell number. The results suggested that Glc-RCs have the ability to support C2C12 viability and proliferation, where a significant difference in viable cell number percentage between the cells cultured in the absence of glucose and cells cultured with Glc-RCs could be observed.

It has previously been reported that glucose starvation causes the impairment of C2C12 differentiation into myotubes, which is their key characteristic as a model of skeletal muscle [24,37]. Hence, the effect of Glc-RCs and different glucose amounts on C2C12 cell myotube formation during their differentiation was investigated by immunostaining of C2C12 according to the phenotypical expression of the differentiation marker Myosin-4 (MYH4). As shown in the MYH4 staining immunofluorescent (IF) images in Fig. 3D, C2C12 failed to differentiate in the absence of glucose and had already consumed all the glucose in DMEM culture media after 7 days of differentiation in the 5 mM DMEM condition (Fig. 3F and Fig. S3A). The detection of glucose remaining in DMEM over time helped in estimating the glucose consumption rate of C2C12, where 2–4 mM of glucose was consumed per 60,000 cells per day (Fig. 3F). Similar analysis was conducted by O'Neill et al., where C2C12 cells at passage 17 had a glucose consumption rate of approximately 1.6 mM per day [38]. The slightly lower glucose consumption rate in their study was mainly caused by lower seeding density (10,417 cells/cm2) compared to this study (31,579 cells/cm2). Although currently not yet investigated, a higher C2C12 passage was assumed to affect cellular nutrient consumption as well [38]. Furthermore, quantification of IF images in Fig. 3E showed that there was only a slight but not significant decrease in fusion index of myotubes formed after culture in 30 Glc-RCs with glucose-free DMEM (12.9%) compared to 25 mM DMEM (15.2%) on day 8. The slight decrease in C2C12 fusion index was caused by lower cell confluency according to cell nuclei quantification in the 30 Glc-RCs (66.4%) condition than in the 25 mM glucose in DMEM condition (100%) (Fig. S3B). Indeed, initial cell confluency before the differentiation induction phase by serum deprivation is known to impact C2C12 myoblast ability to efficiently differentiate and fusion [39,40]. The cause of hindrance in C2C12 proliferation is possibly due to formation of calcium aggregate, obstructing the areas for cells to proliferate and to be reached by nutrients. As Ca2+ diffused out of Glc-RCs, they were chelated by the phosphate groups presented in DMEM, forming calcium aggregates [41]. Taken in account of the aggregates formation, 24-well inserts with suitable pore size should be applied to the glucose supply system to effectively deliver the released glucose from Glc-RCs to the C2C12 cells at the bottom chamber. It was discovered that 24-well inserts with a 0.4 μm pore size could not allow diffusion of glucose, while inserts of 8 μm pore size allowed diffusion of released glucose towards the cells at the bottom compartment (Fig. S4B). This is caused by blocking of smaller transwell membrane pores by the formed calcium aggregates (1–2 μm in diameter, Fig. S4C). On the other hand, when transwell inserts with larger pore size were applied, the amount of glucose transported to the C2C12 cells at different time points is similar to when the transwell inserts were absent. Consequentially, the transwell pore size has significant effect on the proliferation of C2C12 cells (Fig. S4D and S4E). Nevertheless, the results suggest that Glc-RCs could successfully support differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts for 7 days, as similarly found for the standard culture condition of 25 mM glucose-containing DMEM medium.

Furthermore, in addition to C2C12 cells, we aimed to extend the applicability of our Glc-RCs to other cell types. To this end, we demonstrated the capability of Glc-RCs to support the proliferation of primary human adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs). ADSCs are widely used in cellular therapy strategies due to their ability to differentiate into several lineages and their source abundance where primary human ADSCs can be obtained from lipoaspirates of patients collected during surgical liposuction surgeries. Other than oxygen, glucose is also a key factor contributing to glycolysis, ATP synthesis, and activation of certain transcription factors that induce ADSCs differentiation such as into osteogenic and adipogenic lineages [42,43]. Moreover, survival of ADSCs in 3D culture was demonstrated to be heavily dependent on the availability of glucose, where the ADSCs were capable of anaerobic glycolysis [23]. As shown in Fig. S5A, ADSCs were seeded on 24-well plates and cultured in DMEM with glucose to allow attachment for 24 h. After that, DMEM without glucose, or containing 5 mM, or 25 mM of glucose, as well as 30 Glc-RCs, were added to the cells. After 7 days of culture under different experimental conditions, there was no significant difference in ADSCs proliferation when cultured in DMEM with low (5 mM) glucose (330%), compared to when cultured in DMEM with high (25 mM) glucose (423%) (Fig. S5B and S5C). This is in agreement with the research findings of Liang et al., where ADSCs cultured in DMEM with low glucose could still maintain their viability, proliferation, and biosynthesis at the relatively normal level compared to when cultured in DMEM with high glucose [44]. When 30 Glc-RCs were added to DMEM without glucose, they could support ADSCs proliferation (382%) at a similar level to the condition of 25 mM glucose in DMEM (423%) (Fig. S5B and S5C). Interestingly, Glc-RCs contributed to a 298% increase in ADSCs proliferation rate when compared to the glucose-free condition, but only an 87% increase in C2C12 proliferation was achieved using the same experimental settings. This was possibly caused by the enhanced metabolism capability of C2C12 in the absence of glucose as a result of their genetic modifications during the cell line establishment [39,45].

Overall, Glc-RCs can support the survival and proliferation of both cell lines (C2C12) and primary cells (ADSCs). Furthermore, they can also support the differentiation of C2C12, which was reported to be highly dependent on glucose. These findings highlight the potential of the proposed glucose-releasing scaffold for further tissue engineering applications using different types of cells.

Effect of glucose availability and Glc-RFs proximity on NHDF morphology and apoptosis in 3D tissue-engineered constructs

In previously reported glucose-releasing materials, the contribution of glucose to cell viability and function was studied for cells cultured in both 2D and 3D. However, most of them were focused on hMSCs due to increasing glucose consumption rate of hMSCs in a hypoxic environment, where the need for a glucose-releasing material could be emphasized to support cellular metabolic activity [43]. Furthermore, the contribution of glucose-releasing materials to hMSCs culture was only analyzed in terms of cell viability, cell proliferation, cellular metabolic activity, and proangiogenic potential of ADSCs [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. However, the diffusion distance of released glucose from the glucose-releasing material towards cells inside 3D tissues has yet to be investigated. Hence, we investigated normal human dermal fibroblasts’ (NHDFs) cell behavior in 3D culture following the distance from the glucose supply source. This cell type also relies on glucose for its collagen synthesis and ECM homeostasis, which are its key functions [46,47]. Instead of capsules (Glc-RCs), the glucose-releasing scaffolding material was shaped into fibers (Glc-RFs, Fig. 4A) with a diameter of approximately 0.9 mm. The Glc-RFs were prepared by manual extrusion of hydrogel precursor solutions consisting of SA, GLY, and AMG into the CaCl2 solution, where concentrations of all components were kept the same as previously used in Glc-RCs fabrication.

After 10 days of reaction under physiological conditions, the glucose release from Glc-RFs was investigated. The results in Fig. 4B have shown that only 46% of GLY was degraded and released from Glc-RFs after 10 days. A smaller percentage of glucose based on total glucose units within GLY was released from Glc-RFs when compared to Glc-RCs (90–100%, Fig. 2E). Even though both Glc-RCs and Glc-RFs were prepared with same crosslinking density, in particular same concentration of SA crosslinked with same concentration of CaCl2, the sustained glucose release profile of Glc-RFs is likely due to their lower surface area to volume ratio when compared to the Glc-RCs. The influence of hydrogel geometric parameters on drug release was also reported, where hydrogel constructs with higher surface area to volume ratio exhibited faster release rate [48]. Since the amount of glucose was still significantly increasing and not all GLY was converted into glucose, it is possible that Glc-RFs can provide a glucose supply for more than 10 days (Fig. 4B).

Then, the use of the Glc-RFs was investigated in 4 mm-thick 3D tissue-engineered constructs consisting of NHDFs using different experimental configurations. As illustrated in Fig. 4C, the NHDF-laden fibrin gels with an initial thickness of 4 mm were cultured with embedded glucose-releasing alginate fibers containing both GLY and AMG (Glc-RFs), with negative controls of blank alginate fibers (SA-fibers), alginate fibers containing only GLY (GLY-fibers), and alginate fibers containing only AMG (AMG-fibers) in glucose-free DMEM for 3 days. Control samples without embedded fibers were prepared to isolate the effect of homogeneous glucose availability from that of localized glucose release. NHDF-laden fibrin gels were therefore cultured either in glucose-free DMEM (0 mM, w/o Glc) or in conventional high-glucose DMEM (25 mM, w/Glc) for 3 days (Fig. S6A). These controls represent the two extreme conditions of complete glucose deprivation and uniform glucose availability throughout the culture medium, allowing comparison with the localized glucose gradients generated by Glc-RFs and other fibers. The samples were stained for cytoskeleton marker F-actin to investigate the differences in NHDF morphology parameters due to glucose spatial distribution (Fig. 4D and 5B to 5D, and Fig. S6B–S6E). IF staining for cleaved Caspase-3 was also conducted to investigate apoptosis of NHDFs according to a similar spatial effect (Fig. 4D and 5E, S6B, and S6F) [49,50]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate how the distance from a glucose-releasing material influences cellular responses within a millimeter-scale engineered tissue.

The morphology of encapsulated NHDFs within the fibrin gel construct was analyzed at areas near the fiber (<500 μm from the fiber) and at areas far from the fiber (>500 μm from the fiber). Since the height of the magnified image (Fig. 4D, red squares) used for cell morphological parameter calculation is 570 μm, the distance of 500 μm from fibers was selected as a cut-off value between areas “near” and “far” from the fiber surface to prevent overlapping of each observation area. In the case of NHDFs-laden fibrin gel without fibers, the images used for analysis were taken at the top (∼800 μm from the fibrin gel boundary), center (∼1600 μm from the fibrin gel boundary), and bottom of the construct (∼400 from the fibrin gel boundary) (Fig. S6B, red squares). Combination of different NHDFs’ morphological parameters including cell area, circularity, and aspect ratio allow comparison of cell spreading among the experimental groups as all of them are associated to the organization of F-actin (Fig. 5A). Among the mentioned parameters, cell area mentioned in Fig. 5B is quantified according to the spreading area of F-actin. Cell circularity results in Fig. 5C allow quantification of F-actin protrusion, where circularity values approaching one refers to round cell shape while the values approaching zero refers to more protrusive structures (Fig. 5A). On the other hand, cell aspect ratio values approaching one refer to round cell shape while the higher values refer to elongated cell shape (Fig. 5A and D).

The results in Fig. 5B showed a global trend where NHDFs located close to SA-fibers, GLY-fibers, AMG-fibers, and Glc-RFs (<500 μm from the fiber) exhibited slightly less spreading area when compared to cells located far (>500 μm) from the fibers. As further highlighted by the difference between cell aspect ratios at the regions near (2.95, <500 μm) and far (3.40, >500 μm) from the SA-fibers, it was suggested that the diffused Ca2+ from fibers influenced the morphological response of NHDFs to some extent by induction of transient loss and reorganization of the cellular F-actin stress fibers [51,52]. Among the experimental groups, AMG-fibers showed the most evident influence in cell area, circularity, and aspect ratio of NHDFs (Fig. 5B-D). As AMG specifically hydrolyzes α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic linkages in polysaccharides, which are absent in mammalian cell membranes and the extracellular matrix, they would have no bioactive effects on NHDFs [25]. However, diffusion of AMG from the fibers could generate an osmotic flux that drives NHDFs’ F-actin reorganization that results in a round cell morphology [53]. With a smaller hydrodynamic size of 7.23 nm (Fig. S1B), the encapsulated AMG could gradually diffuse through the SA hydrogel meshes, which were reported to be around 50-200 nm in size [32].

Even though diffusion of Ca2+ and AMG from the fibers was shown to influence NHDFs’ morphology, analysis of control groups without embedded fibers suggested that glucose availability alone influences cell morphology independently of the presence of hydrogel fibers. As shown in Fig. S6C–S6E, NHDFs cultured under homogeneous high-glucose conditions (25 mM DMEM) exhibited reduced spreading compared with glucose-free conditions (0 mM DMEM). On the other hand, less cell aspect ratio could be observed in the regions close to Glc-RFs (2.39, <500 μm from the fiber), especially when compared with the regions close to the negative control group of SA-fibers (2.95, <500 μm from the fiber) (Fig. 5D). There was also similarly less NHDF aspect ratio at the regions far from Glc-RFs (2.39, >500 μm from the fiber) as well (Fig. 5D). Even though there were no significant differences between NHDFs area and circularity in the regions close to Glc-RFs (<500 μm from the fiber) when compared to the regions near other negative control fibers (Fig. 5B and C), less cell aspect ratio suggested less cell spreading in the regions close to Glc-RFs (<500 μm from the fiber). The result of overall less NHDFs spreading in presence of glucose is also in correspondence with the IF staining images in Fig. 4D as well. Reduced aspect ratio indicates a less elongated phenotype, which has previously been associated with reduced fibroblast migration. Although some literature has reported impaired migration of human gingival and dermal fibroblasts in the presence of higher glucose concentrations (25-30 mM) [54,55], migration of NHDFs was not directly evaluated in this study.

Glucose deprivation can contribute to apoptosis, which is an early cell death activity, in several cell types including slight apoptotic activities in fibroblast cells [[56], [57], [58]]. Hence, the effect of cell spatial position away from the Glc-RF was also investigated on NHDFs apoptosis. To observe apoptotic activity of NHDFs, the obtained images were analyzed at areas near the fiber (<1000 μm from the fiber) and at areas far from the fiber (>1000 μm from the fiber). In the case of NHDFs-laden fibrin gel without fibers, the images used for analysis were taken at the top (∼800 μm from the fibrin gel boundary), center (∼1600 μm from the fibrin gel boundary), and bottom of the construct (∼400 from the fibrin gel boundary) (Fig. S6B, red squares). The distance of 1000 μm from fibers was selected as a cut-off value between areas “near” and “far” from the fiber surface because of its correspondence to the position between top or bottom to center area, relative to the hydrogel boundaries of control fibrin gels without fiber. Caspase-3 contributes to the cleavage of key cellular proteins during apoptosis, where the activation of Caspase-3 requires cleavage of its own structure [49,50]. Hence, detection of phenotypic cellular expression of cleaved Caspase-3 by IF staining was carried out for analysis of apoptotic activity of NHDF. The results shown in Fig. 5E and S6F were obtained by IF image quantification according to number of cellular nuclei with expression of phenotypical cleaved Caspase-3 due to localization of Caspase-3 within the cytoplasm and the nucleus [59,60].

It was suggested in Fig. 5E that diffusion of Ca2+ from the fibers has no cytotoxic effects according to low apoptotic activity of NHDFs located at both areas close to SA-fibers (9.83%, <1000 μm from the fiber) and areas far from SA-fibers (10.2%, <1000 μm from the fiber). In contrast, higher apoptotic activities of NHDFs could be observed at the areas far (>1000 μm) from both GLY-fibers (31.5%) and AMG-fibers (30.5%) (Fig. 5E). As Both GLY and AMG have smaller hydrodynamic sizes of 15.8 nm and 7.23 nm, respectively (Fig. S1B), they could gradually diffuse out of the SA fibers with mesh sizes of around 50-200 nm [32] and generate osmotic pressure on NHDFs throughout much of the fibrin gel construct. The observed apoptotic activities would be unlikely caused by direct biological effects of AMG due to its specific hydrolysis capability on α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic linkages in polysaccharides, which are absent in mammalian cell membranes and the extracellular matrix [25]. Furthermore, AMG from Aspergillus niger is generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for food and industrial applications [61]. Even though GLY could not be readily transported across the cell membrane, extracellular GLY could be digested into glucose to support cellular metabolic activities according to recent findings by Gurian et al. [62,63]. Hence, the reduced apoptotic activity in the regions close to the GLY-fibers (12.9%, <1000 μm from the fiber) when compared to regions far from the GLY-fibers (31.5%, >1000 μm from the fiber) might possibly resulted from extracellular glycogenolysis (Fig. 5E).

However, the spatial difference in NHDFs' phenotypical apoptotic activity is the most evident in Glc-RFs among the experimental groups with hydrogel fibers. The observed apoptotic activity was remarkably low in the regions close to Glc-RFs (2.83%, <1000 μm from the fiber) when compared to the regions far from Glc-RFs (30.47%, >1000 μm from the fiber) (Fig. 5E). Diffusion of encapsulated AMG from the fibers might have caused the higher apoptotic activity at regions far (>1000 μm) from Glc-RFs, which is previously demonstrated diffusion of encapsulated F-AMG from Glc-RCs consisted of the same components as Glc-RFs (Fig. S2A). The apoptotic activity of NHDFs in regions close to Glc-RFs (2.83%, <1000 μm from the fiber) is also lower than in regions close to other negative control fibers, with the most evident difference when compared to regions close to AMG-fibers (25.2%, <1000 μm from the fiber) (Fig. 5E). In addition, the fiber-free control groups were established to determine whether the observed NHDFs' apoptotic activities resulted from global glucose availability or from spatially heterogeneous glucose delivery. As shown in Fig. S6F, there was significantly higher cell apoptosis at the bottom of the hydrogel when compared to the center region, especially after cultured in the presence of 25 mM glucose. This phenomenon may be caused by the limited diffusion of DMEM to the bottom of the hydrogel positioned at the membrane of the transwell inserts with a smaller pore size (0.4 μm), as the effect of transwell membrane pore size on diffusion of glucose was previously discussed in section 2.3. Nevertheless, the availability of globally high glucose concentration (25 mM) within the fibrin gel constructs without fibers did not significantly influence overall NHDF's apoptotic activity (Fig. S6F).

The less spreading morphology of NHDFs throughout the fibrin gel with embedded Glc-RFs (Fig. 4D and 5D) is in the same tendency of the w/Glc control group in the absence of hydrogel fibers (Fig. S6B–S6E). These observations suggest that glucose released from Glc-RFs may have been sufficient to influence NHDFs' morphology throughout much of the construct. However, higher local glucose concentration at the regions near Glc-RFs may be required to block Caspase-3 activation and protect cells from apoptosis, as shown in Fig. 5E. Glucose-mediated cell spreading are known to occur through bFGF-induced JNK pathways or focal adhesion signaling [55,64]. The change in cellular morphology and migration could already be achieved with smaller increase of glucose concentration in culture media from 5 to 25 mM [54,65]. On the other hand, cellular apoptotic activities associated with glucotoxicity are more evident when glucose concentrations were increased to 35 mM or more, which is regulated by the downstream Caspase-3/PARP signaling pathway [66,67]. For glucose deprivation, the apoptotic activity was rather mediated by the upstream AMPK/PERK-CHOP pathway [68]. Overall, it could be observed that different cellular processes exhibit different glucose sensitivity thresholds, where lower glucose concentrations are needed to influence NHDFs' morphology, whereas higher glucose concentrations are needed for suppression of NHDFs’ apoptotic activity.

To provide experimental evidence that Glc-RFs generate local diffusion gradients rather than uniform glucose distribution, we performed a proof-of-concept experiment using the fluorescent glucose analogue 2-(N-(7-Nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl) Amino)-2-Deoxyglucose (2-NBDG). SA hydrogel fibers encapsulating 2-NBDG were fabricated with the same concentrations of SA and CaCl2 as previously used in Glc-RFs fabrication and embedded in NHDFs-laden fibrin gel. Then, the diffused 2-NBDG gradient from SA hydrogel fibers towards the surrounding NHDFs could be observed by confocal fluorescent microscopy (Fig. S7). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Glc-RFs establish biologically relevant glucose gradients capable of modulating cellular responses over defined spatial distances within millimeter-scale tissue constructs. In particular, the generated glucose gradient could reduce the apoptotic activity of NHDFs from 30.47% at areas near the Glc-RFs (<1000 μm from fiber) to 2.83% at areas far from the Glc-RFs (>1000 μm from fiber). This emphasizes the advantage of the shape flexibility of the developed glucose-released scaffolding material, which allows control over the glucose-releasing profile for different scenarios of tissue engineering applications.

Conclusion & future perspectives

A glucose-releasing material relying on the enzymatic degradation of GLY by AMG was successfully developed. Compared to other types of polysaccharides, GLY is the most suitable glucose source due to its higher water solubility and higher retention capability inside the SA encapsulating material. It was shown as well that increased crosslinking density of the main SA matrix allowed higher retention of both AMG and GLY. The glucose-releasing material was then fabricated as Glc-RCs, where the encapsulation of AMG and GLY did not hinder long-term glucose production and release. In this study, Glc-RCs allowed releasing of up to around 30 μmol of glucose, where 4 days of glucose supply was available instead of only several hours [15], due to the dense crosslinking structure and high capsule thickness. To further improve the glucose releasing duration, it would be worth trying to increase encapsulation efficiency of AMG and GLY by exploring the highest possible SA concentration available to be crosslinked with Ca2+ for fabrication of glucose-releasing hydrogels with higher crosslinking density. As immobilized AMG was reported to have higher stability than solubilized form at physiological pH of 7.4 [31], chemical modification of AMG on the SA matrix could also allow more prolonged glucose release.

Furthermore, Glc-RCs could support proliferation and differentiation of C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line and support the proliferation of primary human cells including ADSCs. Apart from the Glc-RCs, the material could be shaped into Glc-RFs, where Glc-RCs produced and released more percentage of glucose units from GLY (90-100%) than Glc-RFs (46%) after 10 days, suggesting that different shapes of glucose-releasing hydrogels exhibited different glucose-releasing kinetics. A novel perspective on the effect of glucose availability and Glc-RFs proximity on biological responses of NHDFs in 3D tissue-engineered constructs was also demonstrated, where there was a significant difference in NHDFs’ apoptotic activities at areas near the Glc-RFs (<1000 μm from fiber) compared to areas far from the Glc-RFs (>1000 μm from fiber).

Despite the low bioactivity of SA, the hydrogel fiber surface can be treated by plasma to enhance protein attachment, before coating with fibronectin (FN) and/or gelatin (Gel) [[69], [70], [71]]. Even though Layer-by-layer (LbL) strategy of FN and Gel coating allowed some cell attachment, only one layer of FN coating resulted in more homogeneous distribution of the attached cells (Fig. S8). FN-coated Glc-RFs could provide glucose to support C2C12 cells attachment on the hydrogel fiber surface to some extent when compared to the Ctrl-fibers cultured in glucose-free DMEM (Fig. S9). To further improve cell attachment, immersion time of plasma-treated SA fibers in FN solution could be increased from 5 min to 90 min (Figs. S10A–S10C). According to Fig. S10D, plasma treatment and FN coating on Glc-RFs did not hinder their glucose-releasing capability. This protein coating strategy on Glc-RFs allows further expansion of application aspects such as utilization of coaxial printing, where multi-compartmental structures containing different cell types at the inner shell surface and outer shell surface could be constructed. After the improvement of cell attachment on the glucose-releasing scaffold, it would be worth conducting in vivo experiments to prove its applicability in a model with more similarities to the real human body.

As SA, GLY, and AMG are edible materials, the proposed glucose-releasing material is also appropriate for food applications. Maintaining co-cultures including muscle and fat components within cultured meat has remained a challenge due to the different nutrient requirements of each cell type. Glc-RCs were demonstrated to support both skeletal myoblast and ADSCs proliferation, indicating their potential to maintain cultured meat without the need for optimization for proper culture medium components. Hence, the potential application scope could be expanded to areas other than biomedical tissue engineering. In conclusion, this study shows the potential to uncover new aspects of blood vessel tissue engineering, the contribution of glucose to cell activities, as well as production of large-sized tissues in the future.
