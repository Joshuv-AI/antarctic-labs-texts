Evaluation of optimal pooling strategies for Mpox virus genomic surveillance using the Illumina Viral Surveillance Panel on the iSeq100 platform in outbreak settings
Authors: Julien A Nguinkal, Aryse M Melo, Néhémie Nzoyikorera, Jürgen May, Muna Affara, Florian Gehre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGenomic surveillance is critical for outbreak control and pathogen intelligence, yet protocols such as the Illumina Viral Surveillance Panel (VSP) remain under-evaluated in low-resource and mobile laboratory settings. A key operational question is whether sample pooling can increase throughput without compromising enrichment yield, genome coverage, variant resolution or assembly quality.MethodsWe conducted an empirical evaluation using Mpox virus (MPXV)–positive clinical specimens collected during the 2024 outbreak in Burundi. Hybrid-capture reactions were performed with pooled sets of 3, 6, 9 and 15 samples, sequenced on the Illumina iSeq100 platform. We assessed viral enrichment yield, genome coverage, sequencing depth, variant resolution, detection sensitivity and assembly quality across pooling levels.ResultsThe standard three-sample protocol yielded optimal performance across most parameters. Pools of six samples maintained high genome coverage (≥90% at 10×), robust enrichment and reliable variant resolution, indicating that pooling up to six samples preserves data quality for genomic surveillance. Pools of nine or more showed marked performance losses, particularly for high-Ct specimens, with reduced enrichment yield, genome completeness and variant sensitivity. Nonetheless, viral identification remained feasible even at pooling levels up to fifteen.ConclusionsMultiplexing beyond three samples can balance cost-efficiency and genomic fidelity in resource-limited settings using the VSP/iSeq100 workflow. These findings support a tiered pooling strategy and provide a practical basis for adaptive genomic surveillance tailored to outbreak response needs.Keywords: Disease Outbreaks, Public Health Practice, Public Health, Zoonoses

Introduction

The resurgence of Mpox virus (MPXV) in multiple regions across Africa and its global spread during the 2022–2024 outbreaks has underscored the urgent need for scalable genomic surveillance systems capable of rapid deployment in outbreak settings.1 2 While whole-genome sequencing (WGS) plays a critical role in monitoring viral evolution and informing public health responses, implementing such systems in low-resource environments is often hindered by logistical, financial and technical constraints.3,5 These challenges are particularly pronounced in field-based or mobile laboratory configurations, where instruments, workflows and turnaround times must be streamlined and robust against power, space and reagent constraints. Unlike shotgun metagenomic sequencing, which is untargeted and resource-intensive, the Illumina Viral Surveillance Panel (VSP)6 7 is a recently developed, targeted hybrid-capture enrichment platform designed to enable sensitive detection of a wide range of viruses, including orthopoxviruses like MPXV, directly from clinical samples. However, the iSeq 100’s default recommendation to limit pooling to three samples per sequencing run using paired-end reads (yielding approximately eight million reads) may restrict its cost-effectiveness and scalability during high-volume outbreak scenarios.7 8 Although digital PCR provides cost-efficient and precise viral quantification, it cannot recover genome-wide variation or support lineage assignment, making whole-genome sequencing indispensable for genomic surveillance.

Sample pooling, the process of combining multiple individual libraries for parallel sequencing, is a well-established strategy in genomic surveillance and pathogen genomics to optimise utilisation of consumables, reagents and instrument to reduce turnaround time and increase throughput. Yet, optimal pooling levels must balance efficiency with data quality9. The performance trade-off, especially in the context of highly multiplexed, potentially low-viral-load samples, has not been systematically assessed for the VSP panel.6 8 10

The East African Community (EAC) mobile laboratory platform, deployed to support response activities during the 2024 Mpox outbreak in Burundi, is a fully equipped BSL-2+/BSL-3-capable unit designed to support molecular workflows under outbreak conditions. This platform includes an Illumina iSeq100 sequencer and has been used for on-site sequencing during previous deployments, as described by Gehre et al.11 12 For the present study, stabilised samples from the outbreak were subsequently transferred to the national reference laboratory to enable a controlled evaluation of pooling feasibility and reagent efficiency on the same platform. Both the mobile laboratory and the reference laboratory operate under substantial resource and supply-chain constraints characteristic of low-resource outbreak environments, which motivated the assessment of scalable and resource-efficient sequencing strategies.

This study evaluates how different levels of sample pooling (3, 6, 9 and 15 samples) affect sequencing performance using real-world MPXV-positive samples from the 2024 outbreak in Burundi. We aim to establish practical pooling thresholds that optimise resource use and scalability of VSP/iSeq100 sequencing workflows for MPXV, thereby supporting both viral identification and genomic surveillance in mobile outbreak deployments and resource-limited public health laboratories.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

This study assessed the effects of expanded sample multiplexing on the performance of the VSP for MPXV detection and genomic characterisation in resource-limited outbreak settings. Twenty-two MPXV-positive clinical samples were randomly selected for sequencing from the national surveillance database collected during the September 2024 outbreak response in Burundi.12 13 DNA was extracted from vesicular lesion swabs using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). MPXV positivity was confirmed using the FlexStar Monkeypox Virus PCR Detection Mix 1.5 kit (Altona Diagnostics, Hamburg, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cycle threshold (Ct) values ranged from 17.1 to 24.1, indicating a wide spectrum of viral loads, all within the range suitable for high-quality next-generation sequencing.

Library preparation and standardisation

To assess the feasibility of multiplexing beyond the manufacturer’s recommendations, samples were randomly assigned to four pooling configurations: three samples per pool (RUN-3, Illumina standard), six samples (RUN-6), nine samples (RUN-9), and fifteen samples (RUN-15). Sample IDs, Ct values and pooling assignments (RUN-3, RUN-6, RUN-9, RUN-15) are provided in online supplemental file 1. Several samples were sequenced in more than one pooling level to enable reproducibility assessment using identical clinical material. Library preparation was performed using the Illumina DNA Prep with Enrichment kit in combination with the MPXV-targeted Viral Surveillance Panel v2 (VSP2). All libraries were prepared strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including all enzymatic steps, hybrid-capture conditions and incubation times.

To ensure comparability across pooling levels, the following parameters were standardised for every sample: (i) input DNA amount as measured by fluorometric quantification; (ii) final amplified library concentration before pooling; (iii) library fragment size distribution as assessed by electrophoretic profiling; and (iv) equimolar pooling ratios prior to loading onto the iSeq100 cartridge. The only intentional deviation from the manufacturer’s recommendations concerned the number of individual samples combined per iSeq100 run (3, 6, 9 or 15 samples), which represents the experimental variable evaluated in this study. Sequencing was performed on the benchtop Illumina iSeq100 platform, which has an average output of eight million paired-end reads per run. The expected read yield per sample therefore decreased proportionally with increasing pool size: roughly 1.6 million reads per sample in RUN-3, 0.8 million in RUN-6, and correspondingly lower yields per sample in RUN-9 and RUN-15.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw paired-end Illumina reads were processed using a modular pipeline (available at https://github.com/bbalog87/mpxv-pooling-fieldstudy). Reads were assessed with FastQC, and adapters were trimmed with fastp (v0.23.4) using a minimum base quality of Q20 and read length of 50 bp. Host sequences were removed by classification with Kraken2 (v2.1.2) against the GRCh38 human database, retaining unclassified reads with SeqKit (v2.4.0). Remaining reads were aligned to the MPXV reference genome (GenBank NC_003310.1) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17), with duplicates marked in Picard (v2.27.5). Mapping depth and genome coverage were calculated in Samtools (v1.19.2), including mean depth and the proportion of the genome covered at ≥10×.

Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) were called with iVar (v1.4.2) using thresholds of mapping quality ≥20, base quality ≥20 and minimum coverage of 10×. Variants were filtered and summarised in bcftools (v1.17), including transition/transversion ratios and variant quality distributions, and final consensus genomes were generated with iVar consensus. Sample-specific MPXV enrichment was assessed by taxonomic profiling with Kraken2, KrakenUniq (v1.0.8), and Centrifuge (v1.0.4), applied to both raw reads and assembled contigs. De novo assemblies were generated with SPAdes (v3.15.5), and assembly quality metrics (N50, contig counts, contigs≥10 kb, genome coverage and completeness) were evaluated using QUAST (v5.2.0) and assembly-stats. Statistical summaries and visualisations were produced in R (v4.5.0) and Python (v3.12.0).

Results

MPXV enrichment is consistently high up to nine-sample pools

Across taxonomic classifiers (Kraken2, KrakenUniq, Centrifuge), the proportion of reads assigned to MPXV remained uniformly high in RUN-3, RUN-6 and RUN-9, with all samples exceeding 95% MPXV reads enrichment. In contrast, enrichment fidelity in the 15-plex run showed increased variability, with some samples yielding <85% MPXV-assigned reads, suggesting reduced capture specificity and higher proportion of non-target reads in these samples (figure 1). This reduction in enrichment quality was most evident in samples with higher Ct values, indicative of lower viral loads.

Sample-specific effects on MPXV abundance

Reduced MPXV abundance in certain specimens was not consistently explained by pooling level alone. For example, MPXV-S02 in RUN-3 achieved <76% genome coverage, but the same sample yielded stronger metrics in other pools, suggesting variability may reflect sample quality or technical artefacts. In the 15-plex run, two samples (MPXV-S15 and MPXV-S18) contained a substantial proportion of reads assigned to varicella-zoster virus (15–25%), which reduced the relative abundance of MPXV reads. Other low-abundance specimens in this run corresponded to higher Ct values (>23). In addition, several samples were taxonomically classified as MPXV (100% reads assignment), despite very low sequencing depth (<10×), underscoring that perfect taxonomic assignment does not guarantee sufficient depth for reliable variant detection. Overall, these results highlight that sample-specific characteristics and technical variability can strongly influence enrichment outcomes, independent of pooling levels.

Genome coverage and depth decline with increasing pool size

Genome recovery and sequencing depth declined progressively with increasing pool size, but the impact remained limited up to nine samples per pool. Pools of 3, 6 and 9 maintained high mean genome coverage at ≥10× (91.7%, 91.1% and 90.4%, respectively), with correspondingly high mean depths (1118×, 898× and 574×). In contrast, the 15-plex run showed a marked deterioration: mean genome coverage ≥10× dropped to 25.2% (SD 32; IQR 23) and mean sequencing depth to 44× (SD 115; IQR 6). Several samples in this batch recovered <5% of the genome, rendering them unusable for reliable variant detection or downstream genomic epidemiology. Taken together, these results indicate a clear inflection between nine and fifteen samples per pool, beyond which per-sample read allocation becomes insufficient to support robust genome recovery (table 1, figure 2).

Technical reproducibility in multiplexed runs

Several specimens were sequenced in more than one pooling configuration whenever sufficient input material was available. This allowed us to distinguish true dilution effects from inherent sample-level variability. For samples included in both RUN-3 and RUN-6, genome coverage at ≥10× differed by less than 1%, and all detected variants were identical across runs. Comparable agreement was observed between RUN-6 and RUN-9, with coverage profiles remaining nearly superimposable despite the expected reduction in absolute depth. Pronounced discrepancies emerged only at the highest pooling level, where RUN-15 performance was limited by insufficient per-sample read depth rather than by differences in sample quality (online supplemental figure S1). Read quality score remained uniformly high for pools of three to nine samples, with mean and median scores consistently above Q35 and Q20/Q30 fractions exceeding 95%. In RUN-15, several samples showed substantial reductions in Q20 and Q30 proportions and slightly lower mean/median quality, reflecting the reduced per-sample depth associated with high multiplexing.

Read quality, variant detection and coverage profiles

Read quality remained uniformly high in pools of three, six and nine samples, with mean and median Phred scores consistently above Q32 and >95% of bases exceeding Q20 and Q30 thresholds. In RUN-15, several samples showed modest reductions in Q20/Q30 fractions and slightly lower mean and median quality scores, consistent with the reduced per-sample depth associated with very high multiplexing. Overall, read quality remained within recommended thresholds (>Q30) for all pooling levels except in a few samples within the 15-sample pool.

The sensitivity and quality of variant detection decreased with higher pooling levels. High-confidence variants (QUAL ≥30) were consistently recovered in RUN-3 (median=280 SNVs) and RUN-6 (median=220 SNVs), but declined in RUN-9 (median=150 SNVs) and were markedly reduced in RUN-15, where most samples yielded fewer than 100 high-quality SNVs. In the largest pool, high-Ct specimens frequently produced no variants above the quality threshold, indicating that limited per-sample read depth disproportionately affected low-titre samples (online supplemental figure S1). For two representative samples sequenced in multiple pooling configurations (MPXV-S05: RUN-3, RUN-6, RUN-9 and MPXV-S06: RUN-6, RUN-9), genome-wide coverage profiles were computed in 10-kb windows. Mean depth decreased proportionally with pooling level, while the coverage shape remained stable, indicating that reduced variant detection sensitivity at higher pooling primarily reflects read dilution rather than differences in sample quality or capture efficiency. These findings corroborate the observed depth-dilution patterns and confirm that hybrid-capture efficiency itself remained stable across pools (online supplemental figures S2,S3).

De novo assembly performance across pooling levels

De novo assembly using SPAdes produced high-contiguity genomes (N50>100 kb) in pools of three and six samples. However, in RUN-9 and RUN-15, assemblies became highly fragmented and incomplete. Several samples recovered less than 70% of the ∼200 kb MPXV genome, highlighting the unsuitability of high-pooling strategies for assembly-dependent applications such as phylogenetics or novel variant detection. Assembly continuity mirrored the expected depth dilution associated with increasing pool size. In RUN-3 and RUN-6, assemblies were near-complete, with total assembly lengths of 190–194 kb, N50 values of 35–45 kb, and 90% of samples yielding assemblies >100 kb. Importantly, none of the samples in these pools produced assemblies<50 kb, indicating that full-genome reconstruction remains consistently achievable when multiplexing up to six specimens. In RUN-9, assembly quality remained stable despite reduced per-sample depth. Median assembly length was 191 kb, and 89% (8/9) of samples still generated assemblies >100 kb. Although contig counts increased modestly and N50 values were lower than in smaller pools, genome continuity remained sufficient for downstream phylogenetic placement and variant calling.

A pronounced decline occurred in RUN-15. Mean assembly length dropped to <99 kb, median N50 fell below 1 kb and only 64% of samples exceeded 100 kb as the total assembly length. In addition, over 30% of RUN-15 assemblies were <50 kb in length, indicating low-quality assemblies despite detectable MPXV reads (table 2). Largest contigs were also markedly reduced (median 4.9 kb), demonstrating that multiplexing beyond nine samples severely limited the recovery of contiguous MPXV genomes. These results show that de novo assembly is robust up to nine samples per pool but collapses at larger pool sizes. While RUN-15 still generated enough MPXV-specific reads to confirm taxonomic identity, the assemblies lacked the continuity required for high confidence variant resolution, lineage assignment or phylogenetic inference—applications central to genomic surveillance and outbreak investigation.

Discussion

This study addressed a key operational question for genomic surveillance in mobile and resource-limited laboratories: how far can sample pooling be extended in target enrichment sequencing without undermining performance? Using the Illumina Viral Surveillance Panel v2 on the iSeq100 platform with Mpox virus as a test case, we demonstrated that pooling up to six samples per hybrid-capture reaction is feasible, with minimal loss in sequencing quality, genome coverage or variant sensitivity compared with the standard three-sample protocol. Pools beyond six reduced the ability to recover complete genomes and resolve lineage-defining mutations but still produced sufficient reads for basic viral identification and confirmation. These findings have practical implications for tailoring VSP deployment across diverse laboratory contexts.

Implications for VSP deployment in outbreak scenarios

Our evaluation indicates that pooling up to six samples per reaction offers clear efficiency gains, halving reagent use and turnaround time compared with the standard three-sample workflow. When sample quality is consistently high (Ct<22), pooling nine samples may further reduce the number of reactions, though with trade-offs in genome completeness and variant sensitivity. Importantly, viral detection remained reliable, supporting presence/absence confirmation even in higher-plex runs. This optimisation is directly relevant in resource-limited outbreak settings, where sequencing capacity and reagent availability are constrained. Mobile and fixed laboratories can leverage six to nine sample pooling to scale up Mpox genomic surveillance while maintaining the minimum data quality needed for epidemiological response.

Cost and turnaround time considerations

As the iSeq100 cartridge cost is fixed per run, increasing pooling depth directly lowers per-sample reagent costs (table 3). In East African pricing, three samples per run cost about 300–350 USD each, dropping to 150–180 USD with six samples, 100–120 USD with nine, and 60–80 USD with 15—corresponding to price reductions of 50% up to 80%. Pooling also shortens reporting timelines, as a 30-sample batch would require ten runs under the standard workflow but only five with six-plex pooling and three to four with nine-plex. These combined gains in cost, throughput and turnaround highlight the practical advantage of adopting moderate pooling levels for routine MPXV genomic surveillance, particularly in laboratories with restricted reagent supply, limited run capacity or acute outbreak demand.

Although this study focuses on optimising VSP2 performance on the Illumina iSeq100, portable Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) MinION devices have also been used for real-time pathogen genomics in African settings.14,16 In practice, however, reagent availability is the main constraint in some African public health laboratories. ONT implementation is often limited by long procurement times for flowcells and library kits, whereas Illumina reagents are regionally stocked and can be replenished within days. During the outbreak, where timelines and readiness are critical, this difference critically affects operational feasibility. Optimising the VSP workflow therefore provides immediate efficiency gains within platforms already deployed in the EAC’s mobile laboratory network, while the portable MinION platform remains a useful complementary option.

Consideration for variant calling and genomic epidemiology

Pooling up to nine samples per reaction represents a practical upper limit for reliable variant calling, particularly when sample quality is high. At this level, genome completeness and assembly contiguity still met surveillance thresholds, though with greater variability between samples. Pooling beyond nine, especially at 15, led to significant losses in depth and variant recovery, rendering many samples unsuitable for detailed genomic analysis. From a public health perspective, our findings support a tiered pooling strategy: nine samples or more may be appropriate for rapid confirmation or presence/absence testing when Ct values are low, while three to six samples are recommended when high-resolution genomic data are required for transmission mapping, mutation surveillance or phylogenetics. Alternative approaches, such as amplicon-based sequencing, can improve sensitivity in very low-titre specimens but sacrifice whole-genome breadth. Hybrid-capture enrichment therefore remains preferable for comprehensive surveillance, particularly when genomic epidemiology and variant tracking are central to outbreak response.

The efficiency gains from pooling must be interpreted alongside technical limitations. High multiplexing reduces per-sample depth, compromises genome completeness in low-quality specimens and introduces variability in hybrid-capture performance. Pooling also cannot compensate for poor-quality input material or upstream laboratory shortcomings. Thus, while multiplexing optimises reagent use and turnaround time, it must be balanced against minimum depth thresholds needed for accurate lineage resolution and reliable genome reconstruction. Pooling additional samples per run, while maintaining sequencing accuracy and genome recovery, increases effective capacity in both mobile outbreak units and reference laboratories and promotes efficient use of infrastructure and resources.

Methodological limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, the sample size was modest and restricted to a single outbreak in Burundi, which may limit generalisability to other contexts. Second, the evaluation was conducted on one sequencing platform (Illumina iSeq100 with VSP2), so results may not directly translate to other technologies or enrichment methods. Finally, laboratory operations were performed under fixed laboratory conditions. Operational variability during mobile deployments—such as reagent handling, power stability or sample transport—could further affect performance. These constraints should be considered when applying our recommendations and highlight the need for validation across larger, more heterogeneous datasets and diverse outbreak settings.

Conclusion

Our evaluation of pooling strategies for Mpox genomic surveillance on the Illumina VSP2/iSeq100 platform shows that moderate multiplexing can substantially improve efficiency without undermining core surveillance outputs, while reducing turnaround time, reagent use and per-sample costs by half . Pools of up to six samples consistently preserved genome coverage, variant detection and enrichment fidelity, making them optimal for high-resolution surveillance. Larger pools of nine samples remained sufficient for broad viral identification, though with reduced resolution in low-titre specimens. In contrast, pooling fifteen samples compromised genome completeness and variant sensitivity, limiting their utility for epidemiological inference. These thresholds provide platform-specific guidance for laboratories operating under resource and supply-chain constraints, enabling them to balance throughput, cost-effectiveness and genomic fidelity during outbreak response. Optimising existing infrastructure and resources will strengthen sustainable, efficient and context-appropriate pathogen genomics capacity across resource-limited public health settings.
