Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a common virus that can cause serious infections in newborns and infants, especially those who are born prematurely or have chronic cardiac or respiratory conditions. Until recently, the only intervention available to prevent RSV in infants was palivizumab, an antibody preparation given during the winter season. Palivizumab is safe and effective, but is recommended only in infants younger than 2 years who are at high risk of severe RSV infection.1 Given that palivizumab is provided to only a small number of infants,2 its effect on pediatric hospital admissions for RSV has been small. Recently, 2 interventions to prevent RSV in infants have received approval from Health Canada — a vaccine for pregnant people and the monoclonal antibody nirsevimab. Here, we focus on nirsevimab; information about the RSV vaccination for pregnant people can be found elsewhere.3

What is nirsevimab?

Nirsevimab is a long-acting monoclonal antibody that prevents RSV infection by blocking the prefusion F surface protein and neutralizing viral entry. Nirsevimab is not a vaccine, but a preparation of antibodies against RSV, with 50 times more neutralizing activity against the virus in vitro than palivizumab. Nirsevimab has a half-life of 63–73 days, meaning that a single intramuscular dose protects against RSV for an entire RSV season in most regions of Canada.4 Nirsevimab will likely replace palivizumab for high-risk infants once programs are rolled out in Canada.

Why is it important to prevent RSV infections in young children?

In Canada, RSV circulates predictably every year mainly between October and April. It is the leading cause of acute respiratory infection in infants younger than 6 months.5 Infection early in life has been associated with other morbidities in later childhood, including long-term wheezing.6 In Canada, RSV accounts for around 4% of all-cause pediatric admissions to hospitals,5 and 1%–2% of infants require hospital admission for RSV-associated illness in their first year of life.7 The virus poses a particular burden among children in remote regions of Canada, with admission rates reported to be as high as 166 per 1000 live birth-years;8 affected children often require hospitalization long distances from their home communities. Although risk factors, such as prematurity or cardiac or respiratory conditions, increase an infant’s risk of hospital admission for RSV, 75% of those admitted for RSV are healthy, with no known predisposing risk factors.9,10

How effective is nirsevimab?

In a pooled analysis of 3 randomized controlled trials, including 2350 infants (1564 in nirsevimab group and 786 in placebo group), a single dose of nirsevimab was 79.5% (95% confidence interval [CI] 65.9%–87.7%) effective in decreasing the risk of acute respiratory infection from RSV that required medical consultation.11 Nirsevimab was also 77.3% (95% CI 50.3%–89.7%) effective in preventing hospital admission for RSV infection, and 86.0% (95% CI 62.5%–94.8%) effective in preventing very severe RSV infection that required hospital admission with supplemental oxygen or intravenous fluids. Protective levels of antibody were maintained for at least 150 days.11 Those who received nirsevimab also had fewer antibiotic prescriptions (relative risk reduction 23.6%, 95% CI 3.8%–39.3%) and outpatient visits (relative risk reduction 41.9%, 95% CI 25.7%–54.6%) for lower respiratory tract infection. 11 The HARMONIE study, in which 4037 infants were randomized to receive nirsevimab, compared with 4021 control infants who did not receive the intervention, showed 83.2% efficacy (95% CI 67.8%–92.0%) in reducing hospital admission for RSV and 75.7% efficacy (95% CI 32.8%–92.9%) in reducing very severe RSV infection.12 Nirsevimab programs were implemented during the 2023–2024 winter season in the United States, Spain, and France; data from these programs showed that nirsevimab was 74%–89% effective in preventing hospital admissions for RSV.13

What are the harms of nirsevimab?

Safety monitoring of more than 7500 infants who received nirsevimab showed that the incidence of treatment-related adverse events was 1.3%–2.0% or less across all included trials.12,14 Injection site reactions (i.e., injection site pain, induration, and swelling) occurred in fewer than 1% of nirsevimab recipients.14 Other adverse effects included pyrexia (usually within 24–48 h), in fewer than 1% of infants, and skin rashes (within minutes to hours) in around 0.7% infants receiving nirsevimab. Anaphylaxis, a theoretical concern with administration of monoclonal antibodies, has not been reported. Nirsevimab is also safe when a second dose is given in the subsequent RSV season.14,15

For whom is nirsevimab recommended?

In April 2023, Health Canada approved nirsevimab for prevention of RSV infection in children up to 24 months of age.4 It can be given at birth. In May 2024, the National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) recommended that infants entering their first RSV season receive nirsevimab, with priority for those at increased risk of severe RSV disease, as well as infants entering their second RSV season at ongoing increased risk of severe RSV disease (Table 1).16

Currently, NACI’s recommendation states that nirsevimab in infants in preference to the RSVpreF vaccine in birthing parents during pregnancy, based on evidence of nirsevimab’s higher efficacy, possible longer duration of protection, and safety.16 However, this preferential recommendation may change as additional data accumulate. Most infants whose birthing parent received the RSVpreF vaccine in pregnancy do not need nirsevimab.3 Indeed, in most healthy, term infants, susceptibility peaks between 1 to 3 months of age and decreases thereafter; in these infants, RSVpreF provides adequate protection.3 Conversely, many infants with chronic illnesses remain at high risk of severe RSV infection beyond the 6-month protection afforded by RSVpreF, and nirsevimab would be recommended even if they received RSVpreF.

Nirsevimab is recommended over palivizumab, although palivizumab can still be used in high-risk children when nirsevimab is unavailable or in limited supply.

Who can administer nirsevimab?

Nirsevimab can be administered by any licensed health care provider trained to give intramuscular injections. It should be administered as a single dose before or during the RSV season, which is from October to April in most of Canada, in a hospital or ambulatory setting that is equipped to deliver vaccines and respond to adverse events such as anaphylaxis. Nirsevimab comes in pre-filled syringes as either 50 mg/0.5 mL or 100 mg/1 mL. For infants who are immunized in the first year of life, the amount of nirsevimab given is based on their weight (50 mg for infants weighing < 5 kg, 100 mg for those weighing ≥ 5 kg). For children who require nirsevimab in the second year of life, 200 mg (2 × 100 mg/1 mL), divided between 2 injection sites is recommended by the manufacturer.16

What are the resource implications?

In Canada, palivizumab has been given to a small population of high-risk infants since 2002. Universal immunization of infants with a long-acting monoclonal antibody requires developing new programs in each province and territory. Key considerations for program planners are product supply, the setting where immunizations are given, the personnel who will administer the immunization, and the education needed for health care providers and the public about RSV and immunization. Options for setting include maternity wards for infants born during the RSV season, and primary care, pharmacies, or vaccination clinics for those born outside the RSV season, as done for routine vaccinations.

Spain was the first country with a universal nirsevimab program to report their experience. Nirsevimab was offered in hospital at birth to all infants born during the RSV season and, for infants younger than 6 months and those aged 6–24 months with high-risk factors, by electronic appointments to attend a public hospital or health care centre for nirsevimab administration. Their nirsevimab program included educational and training for health care professionals and material disseminated to the general population (e.g., websites); the program achieved 85%–94% coverage among eligible infant risk groups.17

Quebec and Ontario have recently announced publicly funded programs for all infants, and Ontario has also announced a publicly funded program for the RSVpreF vaccine in pregnancy.18,19 Nunavut and Yukon are also working toward implementing all-infant programs for the 2024–2025 winter season. Public health authorities in other provinces are evaluating programmatic considerations, budget impact, product supply, and the cost-effectiveness of seasonal nirsevimab immunization in all infants versus RSVpreF vaccination for all pregnancies plus nirsevimab in select high-risk groups, in line with NACI’s recommendation.

What can be expected in the foreseeable future?

At present, nirsevimab is not available for individual physician access or patient purchase in Canada. Many provinces and territories other than Quebec and Ontario do not have access to nirsevimab, and details for other immunization programs are not available. Health care providers caring for infants should be alert to communications from their health authorities about new nirsevimab programs in their jurisdictions. The RSVpreF pregnancy vaccine is also effective protecting against RSV in infants up to 6 months and is available in all provinces.
