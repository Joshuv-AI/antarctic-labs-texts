Somatostatin Ameliorates Corneal Nerve Abnormalities and Inflammation Through Regulation of Macrophage Activities in Dry Eye Disease
Authors: Xingyu Zhu, Han Ye, Yiteng Lu, Qingye Zhang, Hong Zhang, Yuqing Wu, Yirou Zhang, Yawei Chai, Yuyu Cao, Jiaxu Hong, Xujiao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of pasireotide (SOM230) on corneal nerve abnormalities in a mouse model of dry eye disease (DED) and to elucidate its potential mechanisms of action.MethodsMale C57BL/6J mice underwent lacrimal gland excision (LGE) to induce DED and were treated with SOM230 or PBS. Corneal epithelial integrity, pain-related behaviors, and corneal sensitivity were evaluated. RNA sequencing was performed to identify potential pathways modulated by SOM230. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence quantified macrophage polarization states.ResultsSOM230 treatment significantly improved corneal epithelial integrity, reduced pain behaviors, and restored corneal sensitivity compared to PBS. RNA sequencing revealed that SOM230 downregulated key inflammatory pathways, including TNF-α and NF-κB. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence showed that SOM230 modulated macrophage polarization by decreasing pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and enhancing anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, contributing to reduced corneal inflammation and improved healing.ConclusionsSOM230 alleviates nerve abnormalities in DED by suppressing inflammatory pathways (TNF-α, NF-κB) and promoting a shift in macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the reparative M2 phenotype. These findings suggest that modulation of macrophage polarization is a crucial mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of SOM230, highlighting its potential as a treatment strategy for nerve damage in DED.

Dry eye disease (DED) ranks as the second most common condition in ophthalmology outpatient clinics, with patients typically presenting with symptoms of ocular dryness, itching, foreign body sensation, and burning.1 These symptoms are attributed to inflammatory responses in the cornea, leading to ocular pain and sensory disturbances. Persistent inflammation may also result in corneal nerve remodeling and the facilitation of central pain pathways, making even mild stimuli capable of triggering ocular pain, burning sensations, photophobia, and excessive tearing—key nerve abnormalities seen in patients with DED.2 However, treatments targeting neurosensory abnormalities in DED remain limited, necessitating innovative approach to address this issue and significantly improve the quality of life for affected patients.

Recent advancements in understanding corneal nerve function, particularly the role of neuropeptides in modulating pain and inflammation, have inspired researchers to explore novel therapeutic approaches for nerve abnormalities associated with DED.3,4 Somatostatin (SST) is a key neuropeptide that exerts its effects by binding to somatostatin receptors (SSTRs). It has garnered significant attention due to its anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties within the endocrine, immune, and nervous systems.5 For example, SST has been shown to effectively modulate the activity of immune cells, such as macrophages and microglia,6 thereby exhibiting considerable anti-inflammatory effects.7,8 Furthermore, SST and its analogs have demonstrated unique analgesic properties in managing cancer-related pain and postoperative discomfort where traditional opioid therapies prove inadequate.9

Building on these findings, specific SSTR agonists have shown potential in alleviating pain and inflammation across various animal models. Previous studies have demonstrated that SSTR agonists, such as TT-232, can reduce pain and inflammation, particularly in arthritis models,10 suggesting their potential as innovative candidates for treating neuropathic pain and neurogenic inflammation. Preliminary evidence suggests that the local administration of exogenous SST can significantly enhance epithelial and nerve regeneration in diabetic corneas through its anti-inflammatory actions.11 However, the potential analgesic effects of SST and its analogs in the cornea—mediated by neuro-immune interactions—remain to be fully investigated.

Given this background, pasireotide (SOM230),12 a novel multi-SSTR agonist approved for treating Cushing's disease, has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in retinal ischemia models, underscoring its potential in ocular disorders.13 We propose that pasireotide is a promising candidate for treating nerve abnormalities in DED, with the expectation that it can effectively address existing gaps in managing chronic inflammation and neuropathic pain. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of pasireotide in ameliorating corneal nerve abnormalities in DED, utilizing a lacrimal gland excision (LGE)-induced model and in vitro cell experiments. Additionally, we sought to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the interactions between corneal nerves and immunomodulation mediated by pasireotide. Our findings may provide novel insights into the treatment of neurotrophic deficits in DED, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for clinical translation.

Methods

Animals

Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Sibeifu Company (Suzhou, China). Experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Fudan University Ethics Committee (EENTIRB-2018-03-01), as well as the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Mice were housed at 25°C with 60% humidity and maintained on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water.

Lacrimal Gland Excision Model (Chronic Dry Eye–Induced Corneal Hypersensitivity, Neuroinflammatory Responses, and Synaptic Plasticity in the Mouse Trigeminal Brainstem)

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of Avertin (1.25% tribromoethanol, 16 mL/kg body weight). Surgical excision of the lacrimal gland was performed, followed by wound closure with sutures. In the SHAM group, incisions were made, and the lacrimal glands were partially exposed but not removed. Surgeries were carried out under sterile conditions, and the mice were kept warm until they regained consciousness and were returned to their cages.

Treatment

Fourteen days after lacrimal gland excision, mice that underwent lacrimal gland excision were randomly assigned to the following groups: LGE+PBS, LGE+SOM230 (HY-16381, 0.5 mM; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), and cyclosomatostatin (CycloSST; HY-P1201, 0.5 mM; MedChemExpress); 5 µL of each drug was topically administered to the eye twice daily, for a duration of 14 days. Based on dose–concentration gradient experiments, we ultimately selected a concentration of 0.5 mM for subsequent administration (Supplementary Fig. S1). The ocular safety was also confirmed in healthy control mice (Supplementary Fig. S2). For the SHAM group, PBS was administered for 14 days as control. For macrophage depletion, on the 14th day after lacrimal gland excision, subconjunctival injections of clodronate liposomes (1 µg/5 µL; MedChemExpress) were administered every 2 days. Successful depletion of macrophages was confirmed by immunohistochemical staining for F4/80 in corneal sections (Supplementary Fig. S3A).14,15 Mouse health was assessed by their weight (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Cornea samples were collected 28 days after lacrimal gland excision for subsequent experiments.

Corneal Fluorescein Sodium Staining Score

Mice were anesthetized with Avertin (1.25%, 16 mL/kg body weight), and 3 µL of 0.5% sodium fluorescein (F6377; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was instilled into the lateral conjunctival sac. Corneal epithelial integrity was evaluated under a slit-lamp microscope with a cobalt blue filter. Each cornea was divided into four quadrants and scored individually on a scale of 0 to 4 based on the degree of staining: 0, no staining; 1, <30 stained dots; 2, >30 non-diffuse stained dots; 3, severe diffuse staining but no plaque staining; and 4, positive fluorescein plaque. The scores from each quadrant were summed to obtain the final score (0–16).16

Tear Secretion Test

Mice were anesthetized with Avertin (1.25%, 16 mL/kg body weight). A phenol red cotton thread (Jingming New Technology Development Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) was inserted into the conjunctival sac for 30 seconds. The length of the red-stained portion was measured as an indicator of tear secretion.

Corneal Sensitivity Measurement

Corneal sensitivity of the operated eye was measured using a Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometer (Luneau Technology Group, Chartres, France). Mice were gently restrained, and the esthesiometer filament was vertically applied to the central cornea. A positive response was recorded if the filament elicited a blink reflex in the mouse on at least three out of six applications. The length of the filament that produced positive responses was recorded as the corneal sensitivity threshold.

Eye Closure Rate Measurement

Mice were placed in a 10 × 10 × 10-cm chamber with cameras positioned on both sides at a height parallel to the mice's eyes. Mice were allowed to freely explore the chamber for 5 minutes to acclimate to the environment. Subsequently, a 5-minute video was recorded, and clear images of the mice's eyes, parallel to the angle of the cameras, were extracted from the footage. Using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), the horizontal axis (distance between inner and outer canthus of the eye) and vertical axis (distance from upper to lower eyelid edge passing through the center of the cornea perpendicular to the horizontal axis) of the mouse's eye fissure were measured (Supplementary Fig. S4), and the ratio of the vertical axis to the horizontal axis was calculated. For each mouse, three clear photographs were taken to calculate the eye closure rate, and the final average value was obtained.

Open Field Test

The open field test was conducted to assess anxiety-like behavior induced by corneal pain and discomfort in mice. Mice were placed in a 50 × 50-cm arena with a digital camera positioned above to cover the entire field of view. The arena was illuminated with uniform artificial lighting, and environmental noise was maintained below 65 dB throughout the experiment to avoid light and sound interference. After each mouse experiment, the arena was wiped with alcohol to remove any odors. Supermaze software (Xinsu Information Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used to record and analyze the trajectory of mice for 10 minutes after they entered the open field. The 50 × 50-cm arena was divided into a 4 × 4 grid, with the central four squares designated as the central zone and the surrounding 12 squares as the peripheral zone. The distance and time traveled were recorded and calculated, respectively.

Corneal Whole-Mount and Immunofluorescence

Eyeballs were dissected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 minutes. The cornea was excised along the corneal edge and trimmed into a “cloverleaf” shape, then fixed again for another 30 minutes. The tissue was then incubated in PBS solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with β-III tubulin antibody (TUBB3, MAB1195, 10 µg/mL; Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA), CD86 (TD6332, 1:50; Abmart, Shanghai, China) or CD206 (TU313804, 1:100; Abmart). After primary antibody incubation, the tissue was incubated with the corresponding Invitrogen secondary antibody (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at room temperature for 1 hour, then laid flat on a glass slide with the epithelial side facing up and sealed with an anti-fade mounting medium. Local images of the corneal nerve fiber layer were captured at 40× magnification using a confocal microscope (Leica Camera, Wetzlar, Germany) and stitched to generate complete corneal images. For cell immunofluorescence, staining was performed using standard protocol. Briefly, cells were fixed and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and then blocked with 1% BSA for 1 hour. Next, cells were incubated with TUBB3 antibody (20 µg/mL), followed by further incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies in the dark for 1 hour and sealed with an anti-fade mounting medium. The staining results were captured at 63× magnification using a Leica confocal microscope.

Corneal Nerve Image Analysis

We followed methods validated previously by others.17,18 Confocal Z-projections were generated before analysis. Sholl analysis was applied using the corresponding ImageJ plugin to quantify the subbasal nerve in the central corneal. Then, 50-pixel rolling ball background correction was applied, and the number of intersections between nerve fibers and 10 concentric circles (with radii increasing by 10 µm each) was counted. The Sum Length function of the NeuronJ plugin and Cell Counter plugin for ImageJ were applied separately for peripheral subbasal nerve and intraepithelial nerve terminals quantification.

Cell Culture and Treatment

RAW264.7 cells were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in complete medium (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium [DMEM] + 10% fetal bovine serum [FBS] + 1% penicillin/streptomycin) provided by the company. Logarithmically growing cells were seeded in culture plates and cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After adhesion, cells were stimulated with 1 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; MedChemExpress) for 12 hours. For treatment groups, cells received either 20-µM SOM230 or CycloSST along with LPS for 12 hours. Control wells received no specific treatment.

Trigeminal Neuron and Macrophage Coculture

Trigeminal neurons were prepared from freshly isolated trigeminal ganglia of adult male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old; The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA). The ganglia were placed in a 15-mL conical tube containing Gibco Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and papain solution (20 U/mL in HBSS; Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ, USA), and incubated in a 37°C water bath for 20 minutes with gentle agitation by flicking the tube at the 10-minute mark. Collagenase/dispase solution (1 mg/mL each in HBSS; Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) was then added under a Class II biosafety cabinet, followed by gentle flicking to disperse the ganglia, and incubated in a 37°C water bath for an additional 20 minutes, flicking the tube again at 10 minutes.

A Percoll (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) density gradient was prepared in Gibco L15 medium by gently layering 12.5% Percoll over 28% Percoll. The cell suspension (0.5 mL) obtained from tissue digestion was carefully layered on top of the gradient without disturbing the underlying layers. The gradient was centrifuged at 1300g for 10 minutes (Centrifuge 5810R; Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), after which the top 4.5 mL (including the debris-containing interface) was discarded. The remaining fraction was diluted with 4 mL of complete L15 medium and centrifuged at 1000g for 6 minutes. The resulting cell pellet was visible at the bottom of the tube. Detailed procedures were performed as previously described in this study.19

The isolated cells were washed in Gibco Neurobasal Medium containing B27 supplement and penicillin/streptomycin, then plated (1–1.5 × 104 cells/well) onto wells precoated with poly-d-lysine (10 µg/mL) and laminin (5 µg/mL; both Sigma-Aldrich) in Neurobasal Medium containing B27 supplement and penicillin/streptomycin, supplemented with 25 ng/mL recombinant mouse NGF (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

For coculture experiments, on day 2 of neuronal culture, when neuronal adherence and stabilization had been confirmed, RAW264.7 macrophages (5 × 104 cells/well; American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) were seeded into the upper chambers of 24-well Transwell inserts with 0.4-µm pore polycarbonate membranes (Corning, Inc., Corning, NY, USA) in B27-containing Neurobasal Medium. After a 6-hour incubation to allow macrophage adhesion, LPS (100 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the upper chamber to stimulate macrophage activation. In designated treatment groups, SOM230 (Novartis, Basel, Switzerland) or specific inhibitors were also added to the upper chamber. Coculture was maintained for a total of 48 hours before downstream analyses.20 Nerve length was measured to assess neurite growth, as previously reported.21

Flow Cytometric Analysis

After stimulation and treatment, the cells were collected and a single-cell suspension was prepared. The cells were blocked with rat anti-mouse CD16/CD32 (553141, 1:100; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), then incubated with BV421 anti-mouse CD86 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). After incubation, cells were washed with fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer and centrifuged. For intracellular staining, cells were permeabilized with membrane reagent (BD Biosciences), then stained with PE anti-mouse CD206 (BioLegend). The cells were then washed with FACS buffer, fixed with 4% PFA, resuspended, and filtered into flow cytometry tubes for testing.

For conjunctival single-cell preparation, conjunctival tissue was rinsed with PBS after removal, cut into pieces, and incubated in 1 mL digestive solution (RPMI 1640 containing 1 mg/mL collagenase IV and 0.5 mg/mL DNase I) at 37°C for 30 minutes. Subsequently, the digested tissue was filtered through a 70-µm mesh to obtain a single-cell suspension. The cells were blocked with rat anti-mouse CD16/CD32 and stained with Alexa Fluor 700 anti-mouse/human CD45 (BioLegend). The rest of the steps for flow cytometry staining were the same as above, except for the additional incubation of APC anti-mouse F4/80 (BioLegend) and FITC anti-mouse/human CD11b (BioLegend). Fluorescence minus one (FMO) and isotype controls were included to reduce interference (400528, 400511, 400507, 400505, 400535; 1:100; BioLegend). Gating strategies are depicted in Supplementary Figures S5 and S6. Isotype controls and FMO controls were included to ensure the accuracy of the gating strategy (Supplementary Figs. S7, S8).

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted using a universal RNA purification kit (EZB-RN4; EZBioscience, Houston, TX, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol, and a NanoDrop One (840-317400; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to measure the concentration of the extracted RNA. The RNA went through reverse transcription using a color reverse transcription kit (A0010CGQ; EZBioscience) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (A28567; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (A0012-R1; EZBioscience). Target gene expression was normalized to the expression of β-actin. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA Sequencing

SHAM, LGE+PBS, and LGE+SOM230 groups each consisted of two samples, with each sample pooled from three corneas. Invitrogen TRIzol was employed for the initial RNA extraction, with subsequent mRNA enrichment, purification via Invitrogen Dynabeads Oligo(dT)25 magnetic beads, and chemical fragmentation through fragmentation buffer. Subsequently, high-quality RNA samples were utilized for transcriptome library construction, followed by PCR amplification and product purification. Sequencing analysis of target DNA fragments was conducted on the NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Relative gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential gene expression was identified and visualized using the package DESeq2 for R 3.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) by inputting raw count data. Specifically, DESeq2 normalized the counts, calculated the base mean (average expression within groups), and computed log2 fold changes (FCs) using default parameters; the reported FC values were derived from 2 log2FC. Statistically significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined and selected based on a FC >2 and P < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed using the KEGG pathway database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html) to functionally analyze the selected DEGs. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed based on the KEGG pathway database and the Gene Ontology (GO) knowledge base (https://www.geneontology.org/).

Cell Immunofluorescence

After stimulation, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA and blocked with 5% BSA. The cells were then incubated with the previously mentioned primary antibodies (CD86, 1:50; CD206, 1:100) and secondary antibody(Alexa Fluor 555, 1:500) sequentially. After a washing with PBS, the nucleus were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The cells were mounted with 50% glycerol and imaged under a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction was performed to compare the differences among multiple groups. Significance was set at P < 0·05, and two-tailed tests were used in all experiments. The significance levels were denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001; n.s. indicates non-significant differences. Statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro.

Results

Therapeutic Effects of SOM230 on DED and Nerve Abnormalities in LGE Mouse Model

We first evaluated the therapeutic effects of SOM230 on DED and associated nerve abnormalities using a LGE model in mice. The excision of extraorbital and Harderian glands significantly impaired tear secretion, leading to corneal epithelial damage, inflammation, and nerve injury, mimicking dry eye–induced nerve abnormalities. Corneal health was assessed using fluorescein sodium staining. Mice in the LGE+PBS group displayed extensive corneal epithelial damage compared to the SHAM group, whereas the LGE+SOM230 group exhibited moderate improvements, with a significant reduction in corneal injury scores (Figs. 1A, 1B). As expected, the phenol red cotton thread test revealed minimal tear production in all LGE groups, confirming the efficacy of the model. SOM230 treatment did not restore tear secretion, aligning with the nature of the model (Figs. 1C, 1D). In the open field test, mice in the LGE+PBS group displayed anxiety-like behavior, characterized by reduced activity and less time spent in the center of the open field (Figs. 1E–G), reflecting pain-related anxiety. SOM230 treatment not only increased overall activity but also promoted exploration of the center area, reflecting a reduction in pain-related anxiety (Figs. 1E–G). Moreover, immunofluorescence and Sholl analysis demonstrated that SOM230 effectively restored subbasal nerve fibers in the central and peripheral cornea and corrected the abnormal nerve trajectories observed in LGE+PBS mice, as well as corneal intraepithelial nerves (Figs. 1H, 1K–M). This restoration of corneal nerve structure was accompanied by a significant improvement in corneal sensitivity, as measured by aesthesiometry (Fig. 1I). Finally, the improved eye closure rate in SOM230-treated mice further supported its role in reducing ocular pain (Fig. 1J). In contrast, treatment with CycloSST, a somatostatin receptor antagonist, showed no significant improvements compared to the LGE+PBS group. These findings indicate that SOM230 can effectively restore corneal sensitivity, reduce corneal pain, and improve corneal nerve integrity in a lacrimal gland excision model.

SOM230 Modulates Inflammatory Pathways and Macrophage-Related Gene Expression in LGE-Induced Nerve Abnormalities in DED

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which SOM230 alleviates LGE-induced nerve abnormalities in DED, RNA sequencing analyses were performed among the SHAM, LGE+PBS, and LGE+SOM230 groups. Volcano-plot comparisons revealed that lacrimal gland excision triggered a broad transcriptional response, with 182 DEGs in LGE+PBS versus SHAM (Fig. 2A), whereas SOM230 treatment resulted in 242 DEGs relative to the LGE+PBS group (Fig. 2B). The Venn diagram illustrates the distribution of DEGs across the experimental groups, with 61 genes shared by the two LGE-containing comparisons (Fig. 2C). Hierarchical clustering further illustrated that genes related to chemokine production and stress responses were strongly upregulated by LGE but normalized after SOM230 treatment (Figs. 2D, 2E). Several inflammatory pathways, including the TNF signaling pathway and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathways, showed high enrichment scores, underscoring their roles in mediating inflammatory responses in LGE (Supplementary Fig. S9). Subsequently, SOM230 treatment demonstrated significant modifications within these inflammatory pathways (Fig. 2F). GSEA further identified significant upregulation of TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 2G), which was markedly downregulated after SOM230 treatment (Fig. 2H), indicating a potential modulatory effect of SOM230 on inflammation. Consistent with these pathway shifts, a heatmap of the 61 overlapping DEGs between LGE+PBS-versus-SHAM and LGE+SOM230-versus-LGE+PBS revealed that LGE markedly induced genes related to inflammatory responses, whereas SOM230 broadly attenuated the expression of these genes (Fig. 2I).

Interestingly, we observed that a substantial portion of these inflammation-related genes was associated with macrophage activities. Given the broad expression of SSTRs on macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S10), we performed macrophage-focused GSEA using the GO database. GSEA revealed significant downregulation of key macrophage functional pathways following SOM230 treatment, including macrophage activation involved in immune response (normalized enrichment score [NES] = –1.65), macrophage differentiation (NES = −1.68), macrophage cytokine production (NES = –1.51), and positive regulation of macrophage chemotaxis (NES = −1.70) (Fig. 2J). Together, these integrated transcriptomic data indicate that SOM230 counteracts LGE-induced inflammation potentially by regulating macrophage activities.

Modulation of Macrophage Polarization by SOM230 Through Somatostatin Receptor Interaction In Vitro

To further investigate the effect of SOM230 on macrophage polarization in vitro, we established cellular models using RAW264.7 cells. These cells were polarized from the M0 state to the M1 phenotype through treatment with LPS, which resulted in a significant increase in CD86-positive cells, a marker of M1 macrophages (Fig. 3A). Following treatment with SOM230, a notable decrease in CD86 expression was observed, along with an increase in CD206 expression, indicative of a shift toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Figs. 3A, 3B). This demonstrates the capacity of SOM230 to modulate macrophage polarization by enhancing M2 characteristics while reducing M1 features. Conversely, treatment with CycloSST resulted in a tendency to shift macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, contrasting with the effects of SOM230 (Figs. 3A, 3B). Flow cytometry further quantified these effects, showing a reduction in the percentage of M1 macrophages and an increase in M2 macrophages with SOM230 treatment, whereas the opposite effects were observed with CycloSST (Figs. 3C, 3D). Additionally, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed these findings, revealing that SOM230 treatment led to decreased expression of M1-associated cytokines, including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and increased expression of M2 markers, including IL-10, mannose receptor C-type 1 (CD206), and arginase-1 (ARG-1) (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these results illustrate the effectiveness of SOM230 in modulating immune responses toward a resolution phenotype through a receptor-mediated mechanism in macrophage polarization.

In Vivo Validation of SOM230 Modulatory Effects on Macrophage Polarization in LGE Mouse Model

To investigate the effects of SOM230 on macrophage polarization in a physiological context, macrophage activities were investigated in vivo in LGE-induced DED models. Following the treatment period, immunofluorescence staining for CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) revealed significant changes in macrophage polarization (Fig. 4A). In the SHAM group, minimal levels of CD86 and CD206 were observed. The LGE+PBS group exhibited a marked increase in CD86 expression, indicating a predominance of the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (Fig. 4A). Conversely, the administration of SOM230 led to a notable decrease in CD86 expression and an increase in CD206 expression, suggesting a shift toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This was in contrast to the effects observed with CycloSST. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed these findings, demonstrating a significant reduction in the proportion of M1 macrophages and an increase in M2 macrophages in the SOM230-treated group compared to the LGE+PBS group (Figs. 4B, 4C). The LGE+CycloSST group exhibited a pattern opposite that of SOM230, underscoring the specific receptor-mediated actions of SOM230. Gene expression analysis corroborated these observations, revealing decreased levels of M1-associated cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, iNOS, TNF-α) and increased expression of M2 markers (IL-10, CD206) with SOM230 treatment (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these in vivo results validate our in vitro data and further confirm that SOM230 promotes macrophage polarization toward a resolution-driven M2 phenotype through somatostatin receptor engagement.

Dependency of SOM230 Therapeutic Effects on Macrophage Presence in LGE-Induced Nerve Abnormalities in DED

To investigate whether SOM230-protective effects on corneal nerve abnormalities are mediated by macrophages, we administered clodronate liposomes to deplete macrophages in LGE mice. Behavioral and corneal nerve outcomes were compared between macrophage-depleted (M) and non-depleted groups. In open field tests, mice treated with LGE+SOM230 displayed increased distance traveled and more time spent in the center area, indicating reduced anxiety-like behavior associated with pain. However, these improvements were absent in the macrophage-depleted LGE+SOM230-M group (Figs. 5A–C), suggesting that the effects of SOM230 require macrophage presence. Corneal sensitivity significantly improved in the LGE+SOM230 group but not in the macrophage-depleted LGE+SOM230-M group (Fig. 5D). Similarly, the spontaneous eye closure rate, which reflects ocular pain, decreased in the LGE+SOM230 group compared to LGE+PBS, but this reduction was lost following macrophage depletion (Fig. 5E). Whole-mount staining revealed that LGE+SOM230 treatment resulted in higher nerve fiber density and straighter trajectories, as indicated by βIII-tubulin staining (Figs. 5F–H, Supplementary Fig. S11). In contrast, lower nerve fiber density with tortuous trajectories was observed in the LGE+PBS, LGE+PBS-M, and LGE+SOM230-M groups, demonstrating the loss of SOM230 protective effects in the absence of macrophages. Furthermore, our in vitro co-culture experiments using primary trigeminal neurons and macrophages revealed that the M2 macrophage-dominated inflammatory microenvironment induced by SOM230 promotes neurite outgrowth and elongation of trigeminal neurons, consistent with the observed regeneration of corneal nerve terminals (Supplementary Fig. S12).

These results indicate that SOM230 alleviates corneal nerve dysfunction and structural abnormalities through macrophage modulation. The protective effects of SOM230 are negated by macrophage depletion, emphasizing their critical role in mediating the therapeutic benefits on corneal nerve structure and function. However, macrophages alone do not account for the full spectrum of corneal pathology induced by LGE.

Discussion

DED is a complex chronic corneal disease that negatively impacts the quality of life of patients,2 particularly when nerve abnormalities are involved. However, the underlying mechanisms driving the development and progression of DED-associated nerve abnormalities are still not well understood. Using a DED mouse model induced by LGE, this study provides insights into the role of SOM230-mediated macrophage polarization in the pathogenesis of DED and associated nerve abnormalities.

Our study illuminated the role of SOM230 in modulating macrophage activity, a key player in the inflammatory response associated with DED. By engaging with SST receptor subsets on macrophages,8 SOM230 not only exhibited its well-known anti-inflammatory properties12 but also demonstrated the ability to influence macrophage polarization. Shifting the inflammatory milieu from a pro-inflammatory state toward a more anti-inflammatory or resolution phase not only alleviated the symptoms of DED but also promoted nerve terminal regeneration and enhanced the intrinsic regenerative capacity of the corneal epithelium (Supplementary Fig. S13). Considering the multifaceted nature of DED,22 where pain perception is heightened and altered due to nerve injury or dysfunction, targeting the immune response through SOM230 presents a promising avenue. By modulating the immune environment, SOM230 may not only mitigate the underlying inflammation but also impact the neural pathways involved in pain sensation.

The therapeutic potential of SOM230 in treating DED-associated nerve abnormalities also offers novel perspectives on managing a complex condition that intertwines with neuropathic corneal pain (NCP). The relationship between NCP and DED is intricate, as both conditions share overlapping symptoms yet stem from distinct pathophysiological mechanisms.23,24 Our results indicate that SOM230 not only modulates pain perception and reduces inflammatory markers but also improves corneal epithelial health.

Interestingly, our study introduces a novel observation in the context of LGE models for DED, showing for the first time, to our knowledge, that macrophages undergo polarization toward an M1 phenotype in this setting. This shift is crucial, as M1 macrophages release a vast array of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, amplifying the sensitization of nociceptive pathways and intensifying the perception of pain.25 Although existing research has highlighted the pivotal role of macrophage polarization in neuropathic pain regulation,26 the specific polarization dynamics of macrophages in LGE contexts remain unexplored. The transition toward an M1 state indicates an escalated inflammatory response, potentially playing a central role in the pathophysiology of LGE. Consequently, targeting macrophage polarization presents a promising therapeutic avenue, with the aim of transitioning from a pro-inflammatory M1 state to a reparative M2 phenotype, thereby mitigating pain and promoting corneal healing.

Despite these promising functional findings, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of SOM230 remain to be fully elucidated. Future investigations should focus on delineating the distinct roles of SSTR subtypes, particularly SSTR2, SSTR4, and SSTR5, in immune and neural cells within the corneal microenvironment.

SSTR2 has been shown to regulate immune responses in macrophages by promoting antimicrobial pathways, such as ferroptosis induction in bacterial infections, and is associated with increased M2 macrophage polarization in tumor settings, suggesting its pivotal role in shaping macrophage phenotypes.27,28 SSTR5, through interactions with transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1)-positive sensory neurons and receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1), selectively suppresses neutrophil and γδ T-cell recruitment while enhancing the presence of chemokine receptor CCR2– anti-inflammatory macrophages in the cornea, thereby mediating neuro-immune crosstalk crucial for inflammation resolution and tissue healing.29 Although direct evidence in ocular tissues is lacking, SSTR4 has been implicated in promoting neuronal clearance of pathological proteins in neurodegenerative conditions, indicating a potential role in corneal nerve repair.30

At the signaling level, SST analogs including SOM230 modulate several intracellular pathways such as inhibition of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) production and activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt signaling cascades, which govern cellular survival, inflammation, and neuronal regeneration.31 Clarifying whether SOM230 mediates its macrophage-polarizing and neuroprotective effects through these signaling axes will be instrumental in understanding its therapeutic mechanism.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted in a murine model, and the translational relevance of our findings to human DED requires further validation. Moreover, although SOM230 exhibited significant therapeutic efficacy, the precise roles of its receptor subtype-specific actions and downstream signaling pathways in corneal immune and neural cells remain to be fully elucidated and warrant further investigation.

Furthermore, it is important to consider the long-term implications of SOM230 treatment. DED is a chronic condition that requires sustained attention and long-term management. Although our results demonstrate that a 14-day course of SOM230 confers notable neuroregenerative benefits to the ocular surface, corneal nerve damage may re-emerge following treatment withdrawal. Therefore, the feasibility of long-term application in clinical settings must be carefully evaluated, particularly with respect to potential complications and the risk of tachyphylaxis. Notably, systemic administration of SOM230 has been associated with adverse effects such as nausea, diarrhea, headache, fatigue, and injection-site reactions. These side effects are typically mild to moderate and tend to diminish over time. As such, we postulate that tolerance development, rather than cumulative toxicity, may pose a greater limitation during prolonged use. However, the current evidence base is insufficient to determine whether such tolerance or loss of responsiveness occurs with long-term SOM230 treatment, underscoring the need for extended pharmacodynamic and clinical studies.

This study identified SOM230 as a promising candidate for DED treatment, with potential implications for improving the quality of life for patients suffering from this debilitating condition. Further research is warranted to translate these findings into clinical practice, offering hope for new, effective interventions in the management of neuropathic corneal pain.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that SOM230 effectively mitigates DED and associated corneal nerve dysfunction by promoting macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, suggesting its potential as a targeted therapy for neuroinflammatory pain. Macrophage depletion experiments confirmed that the therapeutic effects of SOM230 are macrophage dependent, emphasizing the pivotal role of macrophage-mediated immunomodulation in its mechanism of action. These findings support further investigation into the clinical translation of SOM230 for DED management.
