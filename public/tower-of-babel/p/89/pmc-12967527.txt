Zooplankton-associated Bacterial Communities are Dominated by Host-Specific Rather than Environmentally Random Taxa
Authors: Yuan Li, Dongyi Chen, Xia Liu, Yun Li, Feizhou Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zooplankton-associated bacteria (ZA) are critical to freshwater ecosystems and host health, but their assembly mechanisms and core microbiota remain poorly understood. We surveyed lakes across different altitudes in China and collected Calanoida, applying gut-emptying treatments to obtain ZA and fecal bacteria (FB). In parallel, free-living (FL) and particle-attached bacteria (PA) were sampled from waters. All bacterial communities were analyzed using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing to compare community structures and infer potential sources of ZA. Our results showed that ZA showed lower diversity and a distinct structure compared with FB, FL, and PA, while the latter three were relatively similar. Moreover, deterministic processes dominated ZA assembly, with their influence strengthening at higher altitudes, as reflected by decreasing MST values. Core microbiota analysis revealed 31 genera consistently present in ZA. Six of these (Pseudoalteromonas, Aeromonas, Streptococcus, Vibrio, Lactococcus, and Shewanella) were predominantly enriched, suggesting a stable and host-associated core microbiota, with Pseudoalteromonas and Vibrio showing marked enrichment in high-altitude lakes. Source tracking further revealed that an average of over 70% of ZA could not be assigned to FL or PA, suggesting that most ZA members did not originate directly from environmental bacterial pools. These findings indicate that ZA are not randomly assembled from environmental bacteria but include a consistent core shared by most hosts. Our study demonstrates that zooplankton maintain specific and stable bacterial communities and, by comparing ZA, FB, FL, and PA across an altitudinal gradient, provides new insights into their assembly mechanisms and ecological roles in freshwater ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02702-9.

Introduction

Zooplankton are a core component of freshwater ecosystems, serving as key links in energy transfer and food web structures [1, 2]. Through grazing and nutrient recycling, they play vital roles in ecosystem functioning and biogeochemical cycling. Beyond their ecological importance, zooplankton also harbor diverse microbial communities, which may influence both host adaptation and ecosystem processes [3, 4].

An increasing body of evidence shows that zooplankton harbor highly specialized and functionally diverse associated bacterial communities [5]. Zooplankton-associated bacteria (ZA) play important roles in both ecosystem functioning and host health [1, 6, 7]. They are involved in host nutritional metabolism [8], immune regulation [9], as well as energy flow and nutrient cycling [10]. However, studies on ZA have mainly focused on community-level characterization [11, 12], often without distinguishing between surface-attached and gut bacteria. Although fecal bacteria (FB) may better represent the gut microbiota of zooplankton [13], relevant studies remain scarce, leaving our understanding of ZA composition and ecological roles incomplete.

Activities such as grazing, molting, and excretion in zooplankton are accompanied by microbial attachment and transfer [12]. During grazing, zooplankton ingest both free-living bacteria (FL) and particle-attached bacteria (PA) together with particulate matter, while their exoskeleton provides additional surfaces for bacterial colonization [14, 15]. Furthermore, fecal material released by zooplankton carries FB into the surrounding environment [12]. Despite these multiple connections, previous studies have revealed significant compositional differences between ZA and the FL, PA, and FB communities [14, 16], suggesting that ZA may exhibit host-specific microbial assemblages.

A major unresolved issue concerns the assembly mechanisms of ZA. Some studies suggest that ZA are highly flexible and shaped primarily by environmental conditions [17], while others argue for the existence of a stable core microbiota maintained through host filtering or selection [18, 19]. Defining core taxa is thus critical for disentangling host–environment interactions. Core microbiota typically refer to taxa that occur with high frequency and stable abundance at a given ecological scale, often linked to host physiology or key ecological processes [20, 21]. In practice, thresholds for defining core taxa are usually based on occurrence frequency, ranging from 60% to 90%, depending on study aims [20, 21]. Moreover, core microbial taxa in zooplankton have been shown to exhibit a certain degree of consistency across species and geographic regions [22].

Copepods exhibit strong adaptive capacities and are widely distributed across aquatic environments [23]. However, research on copepod-associated bacteria has largely focused on marine systems (Tang, 2005; De Corte et al., 2014). As microbial “hotspots,” copepods harbor bacterial communities that are distinct from surrounding seawater, functionally specialized, and relatively stable [24]. The stability and composition of these communities are jointly shaped by host feeding behavior, phytoplankton seasonal dynamics, and environmental conditions [25].Environmental gradients such as temperature, dissolved oxygen, nutrients, and organic matter supply strongly influence freshwater microbial communities [26–28]. Altitude integrates many of these gradients, often reshaping microbial diversity and composition while enhancing the role of environmental filtering [29–31]. How such environmental variation shapes ZA communities, and to what extent host factors maintain core microbiota across gradients, remains poorly understood.

In this context, we surveyed lakes across altitudinal gradients in China and conducted 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of ZA, FB, FL, and PA. We hypothesized that ZA are not randomly assembled from environmental bacterial pools but instead harbor a stable set of core taxa maintained by host selection. By comparing ZA with environmental bacteria and identifying core microbiota across altitudinal gradients, this study aims to elucidate the assembly mechanisms of ZA, advance understanding of host–microbe–environment interactions, and provide new perspectives on microbial diversity and ecosystem functioning in freshwater systems.

Materials and methods

Study Area and Sample Collection

We conducted field investigations in a total of 10 lakes across China during 2023 and 2024. Based on the mean altitude of the sampling sites, all waterbodies were categorized into three groups. Low-altitude sites (average 155 m) included Botanical Garden Pond (BGP), Lake Huashen (HS), and Tangshan Pond (TSP) in Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, as well as Lake Wulungu (WLG) in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region. Middle-altitude sites (average 1808 m) comprised Lake Sailimu (SLM) in Xinjiang, together with Hushan Reservoir (HSR), Luxishao Reservoir (LXR), and Xidahe Reservoir (XDR) in Kunming, Yunnan Province. High-altitude sites (average 3750 m) included a pond on Cangshan Mountain (CSP) in Dali Bai Autonomous Prefecture and Lake Zihu (ZH) on Laojun Mountain in Jianchuan County, both located in Yunnan Province (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Characteristics of the studied lakes

At each lake, three sampling sites were established. At each site, water samples were collected by mixing surface, middle, and bottom layers to obtain a representative water column sample. Zooplankton were collected at each site using a 64 μm mesh plankton net via vertical tows. Because this study focused primarily on zooplankton-associated bacteria (ZA) rather than zooplankton species diversity, zooplankton from different sampling sites were pooled into sample bottles to ensure sufficient numbers for each replicate. Air was left in the bottles to keep the zooplankton alive during transport. In the laboratory, zooplankton were sorted under a dissecting microscope. Zooplankton in CSP and ZH lakes were dominated by a single species, Eudiaptomus sp., while for lakes at other altitudes, only Calanoida were selected. Specifically, Arctodiaptomus salinus was collected from WLG and SLM, Phyllodiaptomus tunguidus from HSR, XDR, and LXR, Ligulodiaptomus affinis from TSP and BGP, and Neodiaptomus schmackeri from HS (Table 1).

Existing research has employed various approaches to differentiate zooplankton gut and surface-associated bacteria. Some analyzed whole individuals after rinsing with sterile or filtered water [14, 32, 33], while others compared individuals with and without gut clearance [15]. Prolonged washing with distilled water has also been applied to induce gut clearance [17]. In this study, however, due to the small body size and anatomical constraints of Calanoida, reliable gut dissection was not feasible. We therefore introduced fecal bacteria (FB) as a proxy for intestinal bacteria, reasoning that feces mainly derive from gut contents and thus allow inference of gut microbiota composition and host selection effects while minimizing the influence of surface-attached bacteria.

For ZA, individual zooplankton were picked under a dissecting microscope and transferred into 0.2 μm–filtered lake water for approximately 2 h to allow gut evacuation [15, 34]. Three replicates were set up, with 30 healthy adult individuals selected for each replicate. The zooplankton were then rinsed three times with 0.2 μm–filtered water and collected into cryovials. The water remaining after gut evacuation was filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane to obtain FB, which primarily represent fecal material but may also contain a small proportion of bacteria shed from the zooplankton carapace. Lake water was first filtered through a 64 μm mesh to remove large zooplankton, and then sequentially through 5 μm and 0.2 μm polycarbonate membranes to obtain particle-attached bacteria (PA) and free-living bacteria (FL), respectively. Three replicates were set up for each, with 1 L of water filtered for each PA replicate and 200 mL for each FL replicate. The membranes were placed into 2 mL cryovials and immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen for subsequent DNA extraction. Samples that did not pass quality control were not included in further analyses.

Bacterial DNA Extraction and Sequencing Data Analysis

DNA was extracted from 110 samples (29 ZA, 25 FB, 28 FL, and 28 PA) using the MagaBio Soil/Feces Genomic DNA Purification Kit, which has been shown to efficiently recover bacterial DNA from complex matrices while minimizing inhibitors [35]. The V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) [36], a commonly used region for microbial ecology studies that balances taxonomic resolution and sequence length for Illumina sequencing. DNA integrity and purity were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer. Genomic DNA was used as the template for PCR amplification, and PCR products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The concentration of PCR products was determined using GeneTools Analysis Software (version 4.03.05.0, SynGene), and equimolar amounts from each sample were pooled to ensure balanced sequencing depth. Pooled amplicons were purified using the E.Z.N.A.® Gel Extraction Kit and eluted with TE buffer. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform (PE250), providing paired-end reads suitable for high-resolution community analysis.

Raw paired-end reads were first quality-filtered using fastp (version 0.14.1) [37] with a sliding window size of 4 and a minimum mean quality score of Q20. Adapter and primer sequences were trimmed with cutadapt [38]. The resulting clean paired-end reads were then merged into tags based on sequence overlap using USEARCH (version 10) [38] with default parameters (minimum overlap length of 16 bp and maximum mismatch of 5 bp). Finally, the merged raw tags were further filtered with fastp to obtain the clean tags for downstream analysis.

Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred using the DADA2 pipeline [39], which provides single-nucleotide resolution and reduces the impact of sequencing errors compared to traditional OTU clustering. ASVs assigned to mitochondria, chloroplast, or archaea, as well as those with total abundance ≤ 10 across all samples, were removed to reduce noise while retaining biologically meaningful variants. This approach allows robust comparison of bacterial community composition across ZA, FB, FL, and PA samples, facilitating evaluation of the effects of different microhabitats and host associations on bacterial diversity.

Data Analysis

A total of 17,439,802 high-quality reads were obtained from 110 samples, which were classified into 17,383 ASVs. To assess sequencing depth, α-diversity was first examined through rarefaction curve analysis, and the curves tended to plateau after approximately 30,000 reads, indicating that most of the bacterial diversity had been captured (Fig. S1).

Bacterial α-diversity analyses were performed both at the overall level and across different altitude levels. At the overall level, the ASV table was rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth using the R package “vegan” (version 2.6–10) [40], and α-diversity indices, including Shannon and richness, were calculated based on the rarefied ASV table. Rarefaction curves were also plotted to evaluate sequencing depth and coverage. At the group level, the ASV table was subset according to altitude, and within each altitude group, rarefaction was performed, followed by calculation of α-diversity indices. Differences among bacterial groups were tested using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests implemented in the R package “ggpubr” (version 0.6.0) [41], with p-values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method [42].

Based on community composition differences, the ASV table was Hellinger-transformed using the R package “vegan,” and Bray–Curtis distance matrices were calculated. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS, k = 2) was then conducted, and the stress value was used to evaluate the adequacy of the ordination. To assess differences in community composition among samples from different altitudes, the ASV table was converted to relative abundance and grouped according to low, mid, and high altitude. For each altitude group, Bray–Curtis distance matrices were calculated, and multi-group pairwise Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) tests were performed using the “pairwiseAdonis” package (version 0.4.1) [43] with 999 permutations and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment to evaluate differences in community composition among bacterial types.

Taxonomic differences among bacterial groups were examined by calculating relative abundances at the phylum and genus levels. The top 10 most abundant phyla were selected for visualization, with the remaining grouped as “Others.” The top 15 most abundant genera were selected, with the remainder similarly grouped as “Others.” Based on bacterial group information, boxplots were plotted for the relative abundances of the major phyla and genera, and rank-sum tests were used to compare differences among groups.

Significantly different taxa among bacterial groups were identified using LEfSe analysis implemented in the R package “microeco” (version 1.5.0) [44]. The ASV table, taxonomic annotation table, and sample grouping information were integrated into an R6 object, and relative abundances were calculated at the genus level. Using bacterial group as the grouping variable, a significance threshold of α = 0.01 was set, and FDR correction was applied to multiple comparisons to identify significantly different taxa. Results were visualized as LDA score bar plots and cladograms showing the top 20 differential genera, providing an overview of their phylogenetic distribution.

Diversity and community structure differences among samples under different environmental conditions were assessed by analyzing ZA, FL, and PA samples separately, including α-diversity, NMDS, and modified stochasticity ratio (MST). Specifically, α-diversity indices (Shannon and richness) were calculated using the R package “vegan”, community assembly was quantified using the MST calculated with the R package NST (version 3.1.10) [45], community composition differences were visualized using NMDS based on Hellinger-transformed Bray–Curtis distances. The differences among groups were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with multiple testing correction. Finally, PERMANOVA was performed on the Bray–Curtis distance matrices for both overall and pairwise comparisons, with multiple testing correction to ensure robust statistical inference.

The core microbiota of ZA was identified at the genus level based on an occurrence threshold of 80% [46]. Relative abundances of core taxa were compared among altitude groups (low, mid, and high) to assess distribution patterns and group differences. To infer whether core bacteria originated from environmental sources, we further compared the relative abundances of core ASVs in FL and PA across altitude gradients. Statistical analyses were used to test group differences.

Fast Expectation-Maximization for Microbial Source Tracking (FEAST) was used to estimate the contributions of FL and PA communities to ZA communities. Each ZA sample was treated as a mixture of FL and PA communities, and the proportional contribution of each source was estimated based on taxonomic similarity. Reads that could not be confidently assigned to either FL or PA were grouped into an “Unknown” fraction. Since, under natural conditions, the sources of ZA bacteria are limited to environmental sources and the host itself, we considered the “Unknown” fraction to represent host-specific bacteria. Accordingly, this fraction was labeled as Host-specific in our analyses. All analyses were performed using the default parameters of the R package FEAST [47].

All analyses and visualizations were performed in R (version 4.3.3) and executed using RStudio (version 2024.04.2). Visualizations were created using the R package “ggplot2” [48].

Result

Bacterial α- and β-Diversity

The overall trend across all lakes showed that the α-diversity indices (Shannon and Richness) of ZA were significantly lower than those of FB, FL, and PA. Furthermore, PA exhibited significantly higher Richness than FL, and no significant differences were observed between FB and FL, FB and PA (Fig. 2a, b). In low-altitude lakes (Fig. S2a, b), both ZA and FB exhibited significantly lower Shannon and Richness than FL and PA. Moreover, ZA had significantly lower Richness than FB, but they did not differ in Shannon; PA showed higher Richness than FL, while their Shannon did not differ significantly. In mid- and high-altitude lakes (Fig. S2c–f), the results were consistent with the overall trend.

NMDS analysis showed that for each altitude, ZA samples were clearly distinct from FL, PA, and FB samples (Fig. 2c). In low-altitude lakes, ZA samples were relatively closer to FB, while in mid- and high-altitude lakes, ZA samples were more distinct from FB. PERMANOVA tests for β-diversity further revealed that within each altitude, ZA differed significantly from FL, PA, and FB (Table S1). Notably, high-altitude lakes exhibited the most pronounced differences among bacterial types, with R² values up to 0.94 for FL vs. ZA and PA vs. ZA comparisons, whereas low-altitude lakes showed smaller within-group variation. Mid-altitude lakes displayed intermediate differences, with significant distinctions between ZA and other bacterial types but smaller effect sizes than high-altitude lakes.

Bacterial Community Composition

A total of 51 phyla were detected. Proteobacteria and Deinococcota exhibited the highest relative abundances in both ZA and FB, with significantly greater levels compared to the FL and PA. In contrast, Cyanobacteria, Planctomycetota, and Verrucomicrobiota were dominant in the FL and PA, showing significantly higher relative abundances than in ZA and FB (Fig. S3).

A total of 728 genera were detected. Among them, Pseudoalteromonas and Vibrio were significantly enriched in high-altitude ZA and were also present in other ZA. They were found in low abundance in some FB, exhibited very low relative abundances in high-altitude FL and PA, and were not detected in the remaining FL and PA (Fig. 3). The genus Cyanobium PCC-6307 was significantly less abundant in ZA and FB compared to FL and PA, with ZA showing lower relative abundance than FB. The relative abundance of Ralstonia was significantly higher in ZA and FB compared to FL and PA, while no significant difference was observed between ZA and FB. Streptococcus was significantly enriched in the ZA group (Fig. S4).

The diversity and community assembly of ZA, FL, and PA showed distinct patterns along the altitudinal gradient. For ZA, Shannon and Richness indices were significantly lower in high-altitude lakes than in low- and mid-altitude lakes (Fig. S5), while community structure also differed significantly among altitudinal groups (NMDS and PERMANOVA, p < 0.01), with greater Bray–Curtis distances between high- and lower-altitude communities (Fig. 5). In contrast, FL and PA showed higher diversity in low-altitude lakes than in high-altitude lakes, with stochastic processes (MST > 0.5) becoming increasingly important at higher altitudes. Their communities were clearly separated between high and lower altitudes, whereas low- and mid-altitude groups largely overlapped (Fig. S6). Across all ZA samples, 31 core genera were consistently detected, demonstrating high stability. Heatmaps further showed that Pseudoalteromonas and Vibrio were enriched at high altitudes (Fig. S7), while six genera (Pseudoalteromonas, Aeromonas, Streptococcus, Vibrio, Lactococcus, and Shewanella) were significantly more abundant in ZA than in FL, PA, or FB, highlighting their ZA-specific enrichment (Fig. 6).

Source Contributions To Zooplankton-associated Bacteria

FEAST analysis revealed that ZA bacteria were largely assigned to the Host-specific fraction, with an overall average of 76.0% (Fig. 7, Table S2). The median Host-specific contribution was 85.44%, with the highest value observed in CSP (97.05%) and the lowest in WLG (41.51%). Contributions from FL and PA were 7.5% and 16.5% on average, respectively. The median FL contribution was 1.78%, with WLG showing the highest value (54.34%), followed by TSP (5.51%) and HS (4.15%), while most other sites had values below 3%. PA contributions varied among sites, with BGP showing the highest value (44.16%) and HSR at 35.07%, while other sites ranged from 1.31% to 40.50%. In most lakes, PA contributions were higher than FL, except for WLG and TSP, where FL contributions exceeded those of PA.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that ZA harbor a stable core community that is conserved across geographic regions and host species. Moreover, the host exerts a certain degree of selective filtering on the associated bacteria, and this selection becomes stronger with increasing elevation. By distinguishing ZA, FB, FL, and PA in natural environments, we found that FB exhibited a closer phylogenetic relationship to environmental bacteria (FL and PA), indicating that most gut bacteria in zooplankton originate from the surrounding habitat and are expelled from the host during defecation.

Some studies suggest that ZA are highly flexible and shaped primarily by environmental conditions (e.g. Eckert et al., 2021), while others argue for the existence of a stable core microbiota maintained through host filtering or selection [18, 19]. This discrepancy is most likely attributable to methodological differences. In our study, a 2-hour gut-emptying treatment was applied prior to ZA analysis, which effectively minimized the environmental signal. Without such a procedure or with only superficial washing, bacteria from the ambient environment may persist within the intestines, potentially leading to the misinterpretation that ZA is heavily influenced by environmental factors. After gut evacuation, the Shannon and richness of ZA were significantly lower than those of the other three groups, and the community structure of ZA was also clearly distinct from them. In contrast, no significant differences in α-diversity or community composition were observed between FB and the environmental bacteria (FL and PA). This pattern indicates that the bacteria strongly influenced by the ambient environment tend to be expelled from the host, whereas zooplankton retain a subset of stable core bacterial taxa that are not affected by environmental fluctuations.

Across all elevations, the assembly of ZA communities was dominated by deterministic processes (MST < 0.5), indicating that their community assembly was shaped primarily by environmental filtering and host selection. Moreover, MST values declined significantly with increasing elevation, suggesting that the strength of environmental filtering and host selection became greater at higher elevations. In contrast, the patterns observed for FL and PA were the opposite: their MST values gradually increased with elevation, indicating a weakening influence of environmental filtering. These opposite trends demonstrate that the decline in ZA MST is not driven by FL or PA, but rather reflects an enhanced host-selection effect on ZA under high‐elevation conditions. For larger organisms (animals and plants), species richness generally decreases with increasing elevation, whereas the proportion of endemic species increases [49–51]. Elevation has also been shown to reduce the richness of FL in lakes [52, 53]. In this study, the α-diversity and MST of ZA decreased with increasing elevation, consistent with previous conclusions. However, because different zooplankton species were collected at different elevations and the sample size was relatively limited, the present data are insufficient to clearly explain the elevation effects on ZA. Future studies with larger sample sizes and broader host taxa are needed to further disentangle the relative contributions of environmental gradients in shaping ZA community structure.

The zooplankton gut, as a unique microenvironment, harbors a bacterial community with marked host specificity [12, 54, 55]. In this study, Pseudoalteromonas, Aeromonas, Streptococcus, Vibrio, Lactococcus, and Shewanella were identified as core taxa in ZA, with Pseudoalteromonas and Vibrio being particularly notable because of their very low abundance or complete absence in FL, PA and FB. FEAST analysis further showed that average about 76% of ZA were differentiated from FL and PA, suggesting that they were not directly derived from the water but may instead reflect stable symbiotic relationships with the host. In addition, PA contributed relatively more to ZA than FL, likely due to their adhesive traits, higher metabolic activity, and adaptability to low-oxygen environments [56–58].

Although different calanoid species were collected across lakes, the core taxa identified in this study were consistently detected across host species. This consistency is likely because the calanoid investigated here share broadly similar feeding habits [59–62], which may lead to convergence on a common set of core bacteria. Such consistency further suggests that these bacterial groups may fulfill conserved ecological functions in the zooplankton microbiome and are likely maintained through host–microbe interactions beyond species-specific differences. For example, Pseudoalteromonas has been repeatedly detected as a stable member of copepod-associated communities despite temporal and spatial variation [63]. Aeromonas and Vibrio are frequently associated with copepods in coastal marine environments, often showing ecological interactions that link them closely to the zooplankton host [64]. In addition, Shewanella and Vibrio were among the common taxa in copepod microbiomes from the North Sea [65]. Beyond copepods, even in freshwater crustaceans such as prawns, Lactococcus was found to play an important role in shaping the gut bacterial community [66]. Collectively, these observations reinforce the notion that certain bacterial groups are stably maintained across diverse zooplankton hosts.

The bacteria retained by the host are likely to play important roles in host physiology or ecological adaptation. For instance, Pseudoalteromonas identified in this study has been reported to exhibit probiotic potential for aquatic hosts such as rotifers, enhancing algal food growth and increasing host yield [67]. The relative abundance of Pseudoalteromonas in ZA was highest in high-altitude lakes but lower in mid- and low-altitude lakes. This pattern aligns with the psychrophilic characteristics of this genus, enabling its survival in cold and extreme environments [68]. Streptococcus and Lactococcus, as members of lactic acid bacteria, may also contribute to maintaining gut microbial homeostasis and nutritional metabolism [66, 69]. In addition, Shewanella has been reported to exhibit algicidal effects on toxic algae [70]. By contrast, some taxa detected in our study—such as Aeromonas and Vibrio—are more frequently reported to colonize zooplankton surfaces [64]. Aeromonas exhibits high chitinase activity, which may facilitate its colonization of zooplankton exoskeletons composed of chitin [71]. Notably, FEAST source-tracking analysis revealed that most ZA bacteria are host-specific. These bacteria likely evolved unique functions through adaptation to the host’s internal environment, warranting further investigation into their potential probiotic or adaptive mechanisms in future studies. Importantly, numerous studies have demonstrated that animal guts are not passive recipients of exogenous microbes but instead are selectively colonized by specific bacterial communities, which can profoundly influence host adaptation through nutrient supplementation, immune regulation, and environmental tolerance [7, 72–74].

In conclusion, this study revealed the presence of a stable, host-specific bacterial consortium within ZA, which may play important roles in the ecological functioning of aquatic hosts. Future research should combine metagenomic sequencing and functional experiments to elucidate the metabolic potential and probiotic effects of these bacteria, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of zooplankton–microbe interactions and their ecological significance in freshwater ecosystems.
