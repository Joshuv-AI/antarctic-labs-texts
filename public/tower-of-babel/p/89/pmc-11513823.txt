Closed-loop neural interfaces for pain: Where do we stand?
Authors: Jing Wang, Zhe Sage Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Advances in closed-loop neural interfaces and neuromodulation have offered a potentially effective and non-addictive treatment for chronic pain. These interfaces link neural sensors with device outputs to provide temporally precise stimulation. We discuss challenges and trends of state-of-the-art neural interfaces for treating pain in animal models and human pilot trials.

Main text

A neural interface refers to a device that interacts with the nervous system, either the central nervous system (CNS) or the peripheral nervous system (PNS), to record, to decode, and to provide feedback stimulation. The purpose of this interface is to restore, repair, and augment brain functions or to treat or prevent malfunctions. In the context of pain, a neural interface may not only help dissect neural mechanisms in pain research, but also help doctors to diagnose and treat chronic pain in patients. Neural interfaces implanted inside the brain or body are known as internal and invasive technologies, as opposed to the external, non-invasive, or wearable devices. A large proportion of neural interface research focuses on the CNS target, including the brain or spinal cord, and interfaces that target the brain in particular are also known as brain-machine interface (BMI) or brain-computer interface (BCI). The combination of continued technological improvement and its vast therapeutic potential has allowed BMI or BCI to attract attention from both the scientific communities and the public in recent years.

Pain is a double-edged sword: we can’t live without it, but too much or the wrong kind (e.g., chronic pain) can make lives miserable. Pain is heterogeneous in nature and is often categorized into nociceptive (due to tissue injury), neuropathic (due to nerve injury or damage), inflammatory (due to localized or systemic inflammation), or nociplastic (due to a sensitized central nervous system). While acute pain protects us from harm, chronic pain, defined as pain lasting greater than three months and which affects more than 30% of adult population worldwide, has contributed to an opioid crisis in addition to creating a large financial burden on health care systems. There remains no highly effective treatment for many chronic pain conditions, raising the need for novel therapeutic options. Development of closed-loop neural interfaces can pave a way to treat a wide range of acute and chronic pain. Compared to the open-loop systems of neurostimulation employed by current spinal and peripheral stimulation treatments, a closed-loop system is adaptive and customizable, and its parameters can be adjusted to enhance therapeutic efficacy and/or to minimize side effects. Most significantly, chronic pain is defined by discrete episodes that are either evoked by noxious stimuli or occur spontaneously, and a closed-loop neural interface has the potential to detect distinct pain episodes with minimal delay and treat these symptomatic episodes with temporally precise stimulation. Such neural interfaces may even become essential in certain clinical conditions such as the diagnosis and treatment of pain for unconscious and noncommunicative patients or for patients at extremes of age.

The concept of a closed-loop neural interface for pain consists of two arms: a detection arm and a treatment arm (Figure 1A). These arms can be joined in a single device operating in the same anatomic location or separately at multiple locations (e.g., two different areas in the brain or a region in the brain and a separate area in the spinal cord or periphery structures). First, the detection arm consists of neural signal acquisition and algorithmic implementation for pain detection or classification. In data acquisition, advances in neuroimaging technologies have enabled us to record neural activities from the brain or body, ranging from cortical and subcortical brain structures to the spinal cord and peripheral nerves (Figure 1B). Invasive electrophysiology and intracranial electroencephalograph (EEG) have high temporal resolution in characterizing dynamic transient nociceptive responses at specific local functional neural circuits, whereas non-invasive functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) or EEG may be used to characterize the whole brain responses.1 Meanwhile, the treatment arm of a closed-loop interface provides a neurostimulation or neuromodulation strategy that targets either CNS or PNS, and this treatment arm operates in response to neurofeedback received from the detection arm. A broad spectrum of neurostimulation technologies have emerged to target pain symptoms. Invasive options include optogenetic stimulation that requires genetic modification to introduce opsins, electrical deep brain stimulation (DBS), and electrical spinal cord stimulation (SCS). Noninvasive strategies include focused ultrasound stimulation (FUS), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), transcranial direct/alternating current stimulation (tDCS/tACS), cranial electrotherapy stimulation (CES), reduced impedance non-invasive cortical electrostimulation (RINCE), and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS).2 The operational strategy of neurostimulation can be fixed or adaptive, depending on the clinical need and safety considerations. Preclinical studies

A majority of pain studies in animal models involve rodents, including a wide variety of genetically modified strains in the laboratory mice as well as rats. Rodent BMI systems that combine multiple brain targets for electrophysiological recording and neurostimulation represent the state-of-the-art to treat acute and chronic pain in real time with high temporal and anatomic resolution.3,4 Pain is processed distributively in the brain, and a number of individual brain areas have been reported to encode pain. The most frequently used regions for detecting pain signals are the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), primary somatosensory cortex (S1), prefrontal cortex (PFC), insular cortex, and the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. Different brain regions play complementary roles in representing affective, sensory, and cognitive components of pain. In preclinical studies, electrophysiological signals such as spikes from individual neurons and local field potentials (LFPs, which represent summed synaptic activity of a group of neurons in a local region) can provide specific readout of population codes of “pain” in one or multiple brain regions, and these neural signals can be used for pain detection. Detected pain events based on such neural codes can then be used to trigger neurostimulation such as optogenetic activation and DBS.3,4 Using neural signals from multiple regions simultaneously can potentially improve pain detection accuracy and specificity and reduce false detection rates. At the same time, ultra-low frequency stimulation has offered promise for adjusting sensory responses to modulate cortical outputs to treat pain and other associated neuropsychiatric conditions.5 Commonly considered areas for targeted stimulations in rodents have included the prelimbic (PL)-PFC, ACC, motor cortex, thalamus, and dorsal horn of spinal cord. Research is ongoing to optimize decoding accuracy, stimulation parameters, target engagement, and long-term stability of the BCI or BMI system. At the basic level, although mechanisms of cortico-cortical, subcortical-cortical, or cortico-subcortical modulation have been widely studied, a complete dissection of the causal interactions among different functional anatomic elements within the CNS pain network remains a work in progress. As a result, a systematic comparison between different neurostimulation sites and parameters would be important for identifying the optimal neurostimulation strategy. The recent study that couples pain detection based on neural signals from the ACC and the S1 with DBS of the PFC shows feasibility of the BMI approach to treat pain with high sensitivity and specificity in preclinical models.4

Other areas of development in rodent studies include investigations into novel noninvasive stimulation strategies such as transcranial FUS (tFUS). Ongoing studies are focused on optimizing stimulation parameters for targeting specific brain regions such as the ACC to deliver analgesia.6

Robust behavioral assays are required to test the efficacy of the closed-loop neural interface device. Conditioned place aversion (CPA) or conditioned place preference (CPP) is a standard paradigm to test pain aversion (for evoked response to noxious stimuli and for spontaneous pain), and these assays can be complemented by tests for sensory withdrawal such as Hargreaves’ test.7 Meanwhile, acute pain assays are often complemented by assays in chronic pain models. Newer assays have focused on automated animal behavioral characterizations in native environments, especially in chronic pain models.8 Closed-loop neural interfaces can be tested in a variety of these behavioral assays.

Human studies

Compared to the preclinical animal models, the majority of existing neuromodulation devices in human pain studies are open-loop systems. Safety and efficacy are the major concerns for clinical research into neurostimulation. Cortical and subcortical targets for neurostimulation treatment of pain, such as the motor cortex and thalamus, have been considered in a wide range of human pain studies. Epidural motor cortex stimulation (MCS), first introduced in 1985, has been tested extensively for different types of refractory neuropathic pain syndromes, with similar efficacy as subdural MCS and repetitive TMS (rTMS).9 Meanwhile, a recent first-in-human study has shown that invasive intracranial EEG recordings have the potential to provide reliable prediction of chronic pain states and may be used to drive closed-loop stimulation.10

While invasive BMI designs have the advantages of recording neural signals from defined areas of brain to facilitate pain decoding and providing targeted stimulation therapy, noninvasive approaches provide better safety parameters. An area of focus in current development of noninvasive BMI for pain is pairing pain signal detection using EEG recordings with either behavioral therapy or transcranial stimulation. In a recently published longitudinal, non-randomized, open-label pilot study, a novel neurofeedback-controlled vibrotactile BMI system based on frontal EEG theta power (4–7 Hz) was developed to alleviate chronic pain in patients.11 Another non-invasive BMI design that combines wireless EEG recording and multimodal virtual reality (VR)-TENS platform has been developed to deliver individualized multisensory intervention for neuropathic pain treatment.12 fMRI has also been used, despite its technical complexity, to support real-time closed-loop stimulation. In a recent study, the feedback input of real-time decoded fMRI-BOLD (blood-oxygen-level-dependent) responses from the insular cortex was fed into an EEG-based closed-loop control system for experimentally induced pain, providing co-adaptive neural and behavioral responses to pain.13

In addition to behavioral therapy, transcranial stimulations can also be paired with EEG guided closed-loop treatments. The most commonly used transcranial stimulation methods include tDCS/tACS and TMS. For example, rTMS of the primary motor cortex has been tested in treating chronic neuropathic pain; however, its clinical efficacy has shown some degree of heterogeneity across multiple studies and thus requires further investigation.

Outside the brain, SCS has been a mainstay treatment for refractory neuropathic pain over decades. Recent developments include high frequency (>10 kHz) and ultra-low frequency (<5 Hz) stimulations. Closed-loop SCS with adaptive stimulation parameters can potentially reduce the variability in dose delivery and the volume of tissue activation, with potential to enhance efficacy to side effects ratio. The evoked compound action potential (ECAP), a biopotential generated by the synchronous activation of dorsal column fibers, provides a control signal.14 By receiving signal feedback such as ECAP to adjust its output as needed, a closed-loop device can prevent over-stimulation when neural response increases during specific behavioral conditions (e.g., coughing, sneezing, and bending). Stimulation of dorsal root ganglion (DRG) that emerges from the dorsal root of the spinal nerves has received also attention as a type of neurostimulation therapy to manage difficult-to-treat chronic pain in specific areas of extremities. In limited number of studies, DRG stimulation has shown similar treatment effects as traditional dorsal column stimulation (DCS). In addition to DRG stimulation, recent developments of biocompatible peripheral stimulation devices promise to improve peripheral nerve stimulation, especially in the context of postoperative pain.

Outlook and challenges

Closed-loop neural interfaces can be used not only for clinical or preclinical applications, but also for mechanistic inquiries related to neuroplasticity associated with acute and chronic pain. These interfaces are particularly suitable for dissecting neural circuits at millisecond timescale and can be used to uncover causal relationships that have eluded prior studies. An example is the successful use of the BMI to identify distinct episodes of spontaneous pain in preclinical models and to examine how prefrontal stimulations influence the nociceptive information flow between the ACC and the S1.15 Multimodal readouts that combine neural interfaces with AI-driven behavioral characterizations can further dissect pain behaviors. Design and applications of closed-loop interfaces to address knowledge gaps in nociceptive mechanisms in preclinical as well as human studies represent an important future direction for pain research.

At the same time, several technical challenges remain in translating preclinical closed-loop interfaces fully to clinical applications. First, pain may arise from a variety of etiologies in heterogeneous chronic pain patient populations; animal models of chronic pain, however, tends to be homogeneous and oversimplified, failing to fully capture the complexity of pain etiology or cultural influences. Second, the neuromodulation strategy that works for certain preclinical models may not directly translate to humans because of differences in brain anatomy and size.

Some technical development is already under way. First, as pain is encoded across many distributed brain areas, large-scale multisite neural recordings or multi-site imaging experiments would provide a more complete picture on how neural activities across multiple brain areas and the spinal cord are coordinated during pain perception and behaviors. Utilization of multi-site neural recordings can also potentially improve the decoding specificity and reduce the false alarm rate in tonic spontaneous pain, as shown in a recent study.4 In the area of neurotechnology, development of lightweight, wearable, and wireless neural interface devices is ongoing, including the development of a new generation of biocompatible or bioabsorbable devices can enhance the safety of peripheral nerve and brain stimulation. Finally, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are playing an increasingly important role in the development of closed-loop neural interfaces by enhancing neural decoding accuracy.

The future of BMI development is bright for the study and treatment of pain. The next generation of closed-loop neural interfaces that combine multisensory decoding powered by AI, with safe brain/peripheral stimulation and/or behavioral therapeutic modalities, can improve our mechanistic understanding of pain and soon fulfill a role in the clinical management of refractory pain conditions.
