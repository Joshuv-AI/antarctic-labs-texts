Thermally Robust Plasmonic Nanorings from Titanium Nitride
Authors: Xavier Baami González, Paul Maurice Leidinger, Bruno Rente, Ryan Bower, Jeppe V. Lauritsen, Peter K. Petrov, Duncan S. Sutherland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plasmonic nanostructures are widely utilized in fields such as chemical sensing, photothermal therapy, and optoelectronics due to their ability to confine and enhance electromagnetic fields at the nanoscale. Among these, nanorings offer unique advantages owing to their hollow-core geometry, which supports multiple plasmonic modes and enables efficient analyte access. However, conventional plasmonic materials such as gold, silver, or copper suffer from poor thermal stability due to either oxidation or morphological degradation, rendering them unsuitable for high temperatures or chemically harsh environments. In this study, we investigate titanium nitride (TiN) as a robust alternative for the fabrication of thermally stable plasmonic nanorings. TiN combines metallic optical behavior in the visible to near-infrared range with excellent thermal, mechanical, and chemical stability, making it particularly suitable for harsh-environment sensing applications. Using Hole-mask Colloidal Lithography (HCL), a scalable and cost-effective bottom-up technique, we successfully fabricate well-defined TiN nanorings over large substrate areas. We further evaluated their structural and spectral resilience through annealing experiments conducted up to 400 °C in air. The results demonstrate that TiN nanorings maintain their morphology and localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) characteristics under elevated temperatures, in stark contrast to their noble-metal counterparts. This work establishes a reproducible, scalable route for producing refractory plasmonic nanostructures and highlights the potential of TiN nanorings for robust operation in high-temperature sensing platforms.

Introduction

Plasmonic nanostructures have emerged as key building blocks in a wide range of applications, including chemical and biological sensing, , photothermal therapy, , photocatalysis, , and advanced optoelectronic devices. , These structures, which exhibit localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPR), enable the confinement and enhancement of electromagnetic fields at subwavelength scales, allowing for exceptional sensitivity to variations in the surrounding refractive index. Among the different plasmonic geometries, nanorings present a particularly attractive architecture due to their unique hollow-core topology, which supports multiple plasmonic modes and facilitates strong light-matter interactions. This geometry also enhances tunability across a broad spectral range and provides a natural platform for functionalization and analyte access in sensing applications.

Noble metals such as gold (Au) and silver (Ag) have traditionally dominated the field of plasmonics due to their favorable dielectric functions in the visible and near-infrared (NIR) regions. − However, their practical implementation in high-temperature or chemically aggressive environments is severely restricted. Both Au and Ag suffer from thermal instability, morphological deformation, and, in the case of Ag and copper (Cu), susceptibility to oxidation. − These limitations critically undermine the long-term reliability and performance of noble-metal-based plasmonic sensors when exposed to elevated temperatures or reactive gases. For diverse applications, this has forced to combine noble metals with several protective protocols. −

To overcome these drawbacks, the research community has increasingly turned toward alternative plasmonic materials that combine optical activity with high thermal, mechanical, and chemical stability. In this context, titanium nitride (TiN) has emerged as a promising refractory plasmonic material. TiN exhibits metallic optical properties in the visible to NIR range while simultaneously offering significant thermal robustness, mechanical hardness, and chemical inertness. These attributes render TiN particularly well-suited for high-temperature plasmonic applications, such as thermoplasmonics, photovoltaics, and harsh-environment sensing. , This includes, among others, refractometric sensing, , surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), , and surface-enhanced infrared (SEIRA) spectroscopy. Moreover, TiN is compatible with standard complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) fabrication processes, making it highly attractive for scalable and integrable device manufacturing. ,

Despite the growing interest in TiN-based plasmonics, the fabrication of TiN nanostructures with well-defined geometries and reproducible features remains a significant challenge. Conventional top-down methods, such as electron beam lithography (EBL) and focused ion beam (FIB) milling, while precise, are intrinsically limited by high cost, low throughput, and time-consuming processing. − These limitations hinder their deployment in large-scale applications or studies requiring statistical robustness. To achieve progress in this field, there is a pressing need for scalable, cost-effective, and versatile fabrication methods that can reliably produce TiN nanostructures with controlled dimensions, spacing, and morphologies.

Hole-mask Colloidal Lithography (HCL) has recently emerged as a powerful bottom-up technique that satisfies these criteria. HCL combines colloidal self-assembly with thin-film deposition and lift-off processes to yield periodic or quasi-random nanostructures over large areas, with tunable dimensions and interparticle spacing. This method enables the fabrication of various geometries, including nanodisks, nanoholes, and nanorings, with relatively low cost and minimal instrumentation requirements. − Our previous work has demonstrated the effectiveness of HCL in producing plasmonic nanorings from noble metals such as Au and Ag, highlighting its flexibility and scalability for nanofabrication purposes.

Herein, in this study, we extend the utility of HCL to the fabrication of TiN nanorings, with the aim of creating thermally stable plasmonic structures for high-temperature sensing applications. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first systematic implementation of HCL for the scalable fabrication of TiN nanorings. Beyond fabrication, we rigorously assess the thermal stability of the TiN nanorings in terms of both morphological preservation and optical performance. Heating experiments conducted up to 400 °C in air revealed that TiN nanorings retain their structural integrity and plasmonic functionality, in stark contrast to previously mentioned metals, which undergo grain coarsening, deformation, and oxidation under similar conditions.

Experimental Section

Materials and Reagents

Raw materials and reagents were used to prepare the required samples and develop the different tests as received. Silicon wafers (ConScience), Glass Substrates, size No. 2 (VWR), Ethanol 96% (EtOH, Technic France), Acetone (Technic France), Isopropanol (Technic France), Poly­(methyl methacrylate) in Anisole (PMMA A4, 4% in Anisole, M w = 495 kDa, Sigma), Polyethylenimine (PEI, Sigma), Polystyrene sulfonate (PSS, Sigma), Poly­(diallyldimethylammonium) (PDDA, Sigma), Sulfate-modified polystyrene (PS) nanobeads (200, 300, 500, 800, Thermo Fisher), Titanium (Ti), Gold (Au), Silver (Ag), Copper (Cu), PVD (Lesker), Cr etchant (Thermo Fisher), and Nitto Deko SWT10+ Tape (Nitto Scandinavia AB).

Fabrication of Gold, Silver, and Copper Nanorings

The fabrication of the metallic nanorings was completed following the same route that we reported in our previous work. This is also explained below, following steps 2.4 to 2.6.

Fabrication of TiN NanoringsDeposition of TiN Thin Film

The TiN films were deposited using reactive RF-magnetron sputtering from a 3 in. Ti target. A mixture of a 1:8 ratio of N2 to Ar was maintained at a process pressure of 7 × 10–4 mBar. Throughout the deposition, the plasma was sustained using 200 W of RF power, and the sample was not subjected to any external heating.

Fabrication of TiN NanoringsSpin Coating Step

Silicon or glass substrates were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath in acetone for 5 min, rinsed with ethanol or isopropanol, and dried with a N2 gas flow. This was followed by submitting the substrates to a reactive ion etching process with O2 plasma (3 min. 50 W, 100 mTorr, 100 SCCM O2) to remove any residues that were persistent on the surface.

Once the substrates were cleaned, a 25 nm PMMA layer was spun-coated onto the substrates by first depositing a layer of 495 PMMA A1 (diluted from the stock 495 PMMA A4 solution with pure anisole at a ratio of 1:4 by weight) solution onto the surface and then spinning the sample in the corresponding spin coating machine for 60 s (3000 rpm, 1000 rpm/s acceleration speed). The thin film was cured by hot baking at 180 °C for 2 min. When completed, the substrates were dried using a N2 gas flow.

Fabrication of TiN NanoringsColloidal Step

The PMMA spin-coated samples were submitted to reactive ion etching with an O2 plasma (15s, 50 W, 25 mTorr, 100 SCCM O2) to turn the surface hydrophilic before the polyelectrolyte’s deposition.

Three polyelectrolyte layers were deposited on top of the PMMA thin film: First, a 2 wt % PEI solution was deposited onto the sample (allowed to sit for at least 30s, rinsed with water for 30s, and dried with a N2 gas flow). This was repeated with a 2 wt % PSS solution, and the last layer of the polyelectrolytes was deposited using a PDDA solution (0.1–0.5 wt %, allowed to sit for at least 1 min, rinsed for 1 min, and dried with a N2 gas flow).

After depositing the three polyelectrolyte layers, a solution of 0.2 wt % polystyrene nanoparticles (PS NP) was deposited, allowed to sit for a range of time depending on the NP size (5–30 min), rinsed for 1 min, placed into boiling water for 1 min, and finally dried with a N2 gas flow.

Fabrication of TiN NanoringsCr Deposition Step

The colloidal layer was used to create the Cr nanorings by electron beam physical vacuum deposition (PVD) in the Cryofox Explorer 500 GLAD machine. First, a mask layer of Ti was deposited (10 nm Ti, 1Å/s, 0̊, 3 rpm). This was followed by submitting the substrates to reactive ion etching with O2 plasma (100W, 4–10 min, 25 mTorr, 100 SCMM O2) to create a hole mask.

With the generated mask to fabricate the rings of interest, a Ti adhesion layer and Cr were deposited at a 40̊ offset angle from the normal, reaching the substrate under the colloidal mask particles. First, 2 nm of Ti (0.2 Å/s, 40̊, 3 rpm) was deposited and then the desired amount of the metal of interest was deposited (1 Å/s, 40̊, 3 rpm).

After the deposition of the metals, the PS NPs were removed from the surface by tape stripping. Nitto Deko SWT10+ tape was placed on top of the substates’ surfaces, and then it was carefully peeled off to remove the tape and bring with it the PS NPs. The excess of Cr was then removed by liftoff of the PMMA layer, and the substrates were placed in an ultrasonic bath with acetone until the metal layer was no longer visually detected. The last step of the nanoring fabrication was rinsing the substrates with ethanol or isopropanol, followed by water and drying with a N2 gas flow.

Fabrication of TiN NanoringsEtching Step

The fabricated Cr nanorings defined the nanostructure hard mask pattern that was transferred into the TiN layer.

To etch the TiN, a chemically assisted ion beam etching (CAIBE) process was employed using a scia Mill 150 (scia Systems GmbH). During this process, the ion beam source operated at a microwave power of 350 W, and the argon (Ar) ions with an energy of 400 eV were directed perpendicularly onto the sample surface. The beam current was maintained at 150 mA, corresponding to an average ion current density of approximately 20 μA/cm2. Additionally, 15 SCCM of Cl2 gas was introduced via a gas shower positioned near the sample to chemically enhance the etching process. The achieved milling rate was about 8 nm/min.

TiN nanorings were revealed after submerging the substrates in a solution of Cr etchant for 5 min. This was followed by rinsing them with acetone, 2-propanol, and water before drying with a N2 gas flow.

Morphological Characterization and Performance Analysis of the Fabricated Nanorings

The fabricated nanorings’ morphology was characterized by imaging the surface of the glass and silicon substrates using both Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM). Additionally, the optical response was examined by using ultraviolet-Visible (UV–vis)-NIR spectrophotometry.

For SEM imaging, an FEI Magellan 400 SEM machine was used. To reduce charging, all glass samples had 10 nm of Ti deposited before SEM imaging. The samples were imaged by using a beam current of 50 pA and an accelerating voltage of 5.00 kV.

For AFM imaging, a Bruker Dimension Edge AFM was used. The samples were imaged using tapping mode using an RTESP-300 tip from Bruker. Each image was taken by measuring 256 lines in the y-direction, with 256 samples per line, creating a 256 × 256 pixel image that was later leveled in postprocessing if needed.

The optical spectra to examine the optical response of the fabricated nanorings were recorded by using a Shimadzu 300 UV–vis-NIR spectrophotometer. A double-beam setup has been used to measure the extinction spectra, where one beam is shot through a cover glass without any nanostructures as a control, while the other beam is shot through a sample containing the nanostructures of interest in a glass substrate. The samples were placed so that the side with the deposited metal nanoring was facing toward the direction of the light.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a lab-based setup equipped with a nonmonochromatized Al K α (1486.7 eV) source (Specs XR 50). Photoelectrons were analyzed and detected by using a Specs Phoibos 100 MCD hemispherical analyzer. To prevent sample charging, the glass samples were irradiated with electrons from a flood gun (Specs PU-eq 22). Spectra analysis was performed by using a Python script written in-house. All spectra were calibrated to the Si 2s peak fixed at 154.4 eV. Core-level peaks were fitted with Voigt-type curves and by using Shirley-type background subtraction. Metallic Au(111) and Ag(111) reference samples were measured by using the same setup.

Annealing Treatment and Thermal Stability Test

Samples were annealed in air atmosphere using the furnace Carbolite Eha 12/150b at different temperatures (100–500 °C) for periods of 60 min. The effect of these annealing processes and the thermal stability of the diverse nanorings was evaluated by comparing their optical response, morphology, and XPS spectra before and after.

Results and Discussion

TiN Nanoring Fabrication

Our objective was to develop a scalable nanofabrication strategy capable of producing large-area arrays of titanium nitride (TiN) nanorings with precise control over their geometrical parameters, such as diameter, wall thickness, and spacing. To achieve this, we selected hole-mask colloidal lithography (HCL) as the fabrication method. This technique offers the advantages of high-throughput and parallel processing inherent to colloidal lithography while eliminating the need for a lithographically defined master moldsuch as those required in nanoimprint lithography or photolithography, thereby reducing both complexity and cost. HCL is a well-established approach in the field of nanofabrication. ,,,, In brief, it relies on the deposition and immobilization of colloidal particles onto a thin polymer resist layer. These particles act as a sacrificial mask during subsequent metal deposition, resulting in a perforated metal film with circular apertures that define the pattern for subsequent material deposition or etching.

In our previous work, we presented a modified version of the traditional HCL that allows for the fabrication of different material nanorings by coupling colloidal lithography with E-beam physical vapor deposition (PVD). Here, we aim to use an array of chromium (Cr) nanorings fabricated by the mentioned approach as a fabrication pattern for TiN nanorings. As can be seen in Figure , the idea was that Cr nanorings act as a hard mask layer of the TiN located underneath while etching the rest of the nitride layer, followed by the exposure of TiN nanorings by etching away the Cr.

Diagram of the steps in the new fabrication of TiN nanorings based on Hole-mask Colloidal Lithography.

The first step to complete the fabrication of the desired nanostructures involved the deposition of a continuous thin film of TiN on top of the silicon and glass substrates. A 50 nm film was deposited by using reactive RF-magnetron sputtering (0.8 nm/min). These substrates were then used to deposit the array of Cr nanorings on top.

Cr nanorings were deposited following our versatile protocol that employed angle deposition (E-beam PVD). This protocol was completely optimized, including the thickness of the PMMA layer, the polystyrene nanoparticle (PS NP) distribution, the mask thickness, the etching of the resist, and the material deposition (amount and angle included). As a result, this allowed us to have full control of the fabricated nanostructures. In this case, Cr nanorings with a height of 20 nm and a deposition angle of 40̊ for the Cr deposition were fabricated following steps 2–11 in Figure . An example of an array of Cr nanorings can be found in Figure A, as it can be seen that the array of rings presents a short-range order; this is interesting in order to avoid plasmonic coupling and other effects from the final structures.

SEM images showing (A) 500 nm Cr nanorings on top of a thin layer of TiN and (B) 500 nm TiN nanorings on top of a glass substrate.

The Cr rings’ array was fabricated to protect the TiN underneath. The unprotected nitride layer was removed by using the CAIBE technique described in section .

After the nitride layer was etched, in order to expose the final TiN nanostructures, the Cr array was etched, placing the substrates into a commercial solution of Cr etchant that allowed the removal of the metal and the Ti used as the adhesion layer without damaging the desired structures (Figure B).

TiN Nanorings’ Morphology and Uniformity

TiN nanorings were successfully fabricated by a method that allows large-area nanofabrication, producing short-order arrays of nanostructures with tunable geometries. This enables a high degree of structural control and reproducibility over large areas, which are critical for both optical consistency and device integration. The morphological features of the fabricated TiN nanorings were characterized using SEM, as shown in Figure A,B.

SEM and AFM Images for TiN nanoring array. (A), (C): 300 nm and (B), (D): 500 nm.

The nanorings exhibit a well-defined ring-like morphology, characterized by a hollow center surrounded by a continuous TiN rim. SEM imaging revealed a uniform distribution of nanorings over the substrate, confirming the high fidelity of the HCL process. For example, in the case of 300 nm rings (Figure A), the structures present an outer diameter of approximately 300 nm and an inner diameter of 150 nm. The center-to-center pitch, determined by the colloidal mask size, is approximately 500 nm, ensuring minimal inter-ring coupling in the far-field regime while still enabling dense array formation. A deeper study of the radial distribution function of the arrays can be found in our previous work. There we observed that no matter the size of the nanoparticles employed, the distance between the different nanorings was on average equivalent to the size of 1.5 PS NP.

To qualify for structural uniformity, image analysis was performed over multiple SEM frames, and the outer diameter was constant across the sample, indicating excellent control over ring size. Similar consistency was observed in inner diameters, pointing to uniform etching and deposition during the ring definition and lift-off steps. The minimal presence of defects, such as merged or broken rings, can be due to small aggregations, an aggressive lift-off process, or overetching due to small differences in the thickness of the resist or original TiN.

AFM was employed to complement SEM data and extract accurate height profiles of the nanorings. AFM measurements, shown in Figure C and D, confirm the ring height to be within ± 2 nm of the nominal 50 nm, highlighting the planar uniformity and successful etch depth control. This is also in accordance with the initial thickness of the deposited TiN thin layer. Additionally, AFM phase images indicate constant height through the different nanostructures.

As mentioned, the coverage of the area by the nanorings is consistent across the substrate; this high and uniform coverage supports the applicability of TiN nanorings in metasurface fabrication and coating technologies where regular patterning is essential for predictable optical or thermal response. Importantly, TiN offers a high degree of mechanical and chemical robustness, enabling postfabrication processing or integration into functional platforms without compromising morphology.

In summary, the TiN nanorings produced via HCL exhibit a high morphological uniformity, precise control over dimensions, and low structural variability across large substrate areas. The combination of structural integrity and scalability positions this fabrication route as a strong candidate for next-generation plasmonic systems.

Optical Properties and Plasmonic Response

The optical behavior of the TiN nanorings was investigated to elucidate their plasmonic properties and suitability for thermoplasmonic applications across the near-infrared (NIR) and visible regions. The ring-shaped morphology of the fabricated nanostructures introduces unique plasmonic modes not accessible in simple solid geometries, making comprehensive optical analysis essential for understanding and optimizing their performance.

The optical characteristics of the fabricated TiN nanorings were investigated by measuring their extinction spectra across the near-infrared (NIR) and visible regions. Figure A illustrates the extinction profiles for TiN nanorings with outer diameters of 200, 300, and 500 nm. The data reveal a distinct red shift in the LSPR peak as the nanoring size increases, demonstrating the tunability of the optical response through geometric parameters. The 200 nm rings exhibited a prominent extinction peak near 850 nm, whereas the 500 nm rings displayed a broader peak centered at around 1700 nm. The increase in absolute extinction when studying larger ring sizes can be explained by an increasing amount of TiN, leading to higher absorption and extinction.

Extinction (1-transmission) spectra showing the optical response of the fabricated nanorings. (A) Comparison of different sizes of TiN structures. Experimental vs Simulated (normalized) for TiN structures: (B) 200 nm; (C) 300 nm; and (D) 500 nm.

To evaluate the accuracy of the experimental findings, we further compared the extinction spectra with numerical simulations based on finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) calculations. As presented in Figure B–D, the simulations show agreement on the peak position for the 200 and 300 nm with the experimental results and red-shifted up to some degree for the 500 nm nanorings. The deviations observed in the broadness and position might be attributed to differences in the exact geometry and cross section of the rings in the simulations, differing from the experimental samples. The LSPR features are captured in both data sets, confirming that the fabricated nanorings support robust plasmonic behavior and are suitable for integration in devices requiring spectral selectivity in the NIR range. In several works, we have described and studied both experimentally and theoretically the resonance modes on nanorings. ,,,

The results showed that TiN nanorings reveal broad and strong LSPR features centered in the NIR region. Their tunability, polarization insensitivity, and compatibility with thermally robust TiN materials make them ideal candidates for thermoplasmonic applications.

Thermal Stability and Annealing Effect on Plasmonic Response

The thermal robustness of TiN nanorings was systematically evaluated through a series of annealing experiments with the goal of assessing their suitability for high-temperature sensing applications. A set of extinction spectra was recorded at incremental temperature steps from room temperature to 500 °C. Figure provides a comprehensive view of the optical evolution of TiN nanorings under thermal stress and a direct comparison with conventional plasmonic materials: Cu, Ag, and Au.

Extinction (1-transmission) spectra showing the optical response of the fabricated 200 nm nanorings at different temperatures. (A) Cu; (B) Ag; (C) Au; and (D) TiN.

For the metallic nanorings, significant spectral changes are observed as the annealing temperature increases already from 200 °C. Notably, the LSPR peaks shift and broaden, and these changes are attributed mainly to changes in nanoring morphology. While XPS spectra of Au and Ag before and after annealing (Figures S4 and S5) indicate that no bulk oxidation reaction takes place, this degradation is assigned to the interfacial stress between the nanorings and the substrate. The thermal expansion coefficients of metallic Au, Ag, and Cu are significantly larger than those of the substrate material (Table S1). Several literature sources state that the fracture toughness of thin metal films is significantly lower than that of the respective bulk materials, which leads to a film/nanoring rupture already at low thermally induced strain. The blue-shift of the LSPR peaks indicates a change in the effective dielectric environment or geometry of the nanorings, while the broadening reflects a loss of coherence in the plasmonic oscillations. This is particularly noticeable beyond 200 °C. These thermally induced degradations compromise the spectral definition, posing limitations for applications at elevated temperatures.

In contrast, TiN nanorings exhibited remarkable spectral stability up to 400 °C. As presented in Figure D, the extinction profiles remained almost consistent across the entire temperature range with only minor intensity variations and negligible peak shifts. This observation highlights the excellent thermal resilience of TiN, which retains its morphological integrity and plasmonic functionality even under prolonged thermal stress. , The superior performance of TiN compared with noble metals underscores its potential as a robust alternative for plasmonic applications in harsh thermal environments. TiN possesses a lower thermal expansion coefficient of 9.35 × 10–6 K–1, which reduces the interfacial stress acting on the nanorings.

To correlate the optical changes with morphological transformations, SEM was employed to examine the nanorings before and after thermal treatment (Figure ). In the case of Cu (A,B), upon annealing, slight edge roughening and minor dimensional changes are observed. These alterations are consistent with the initial spectral broadening seen in the extinction data. Moreover, for Ag (C,D) and for Au (E,F), secondary structureslike crescentsstart appearing when heated to high temperatures, which explains the presence of new plasmonic modes on the spectrum. We thus assign the failure cause to the nanoring oxidation in the case of Cu at low temperatures, while the failure of Au and Ag is caused by morphological deformation at higher temperatures.

SEM images showing the fabricated nanorings before being exposed to any heating: (A) Cu; (C) Ag; (E) Au; and (G) TiN; and after they have been exposed to a certain temperature for 1 h: (B) Cu at 200 °C; (D) Ag at 400 °C; (F) Au at 500 °C; and (H) TiN at 500 °C.

However, even at the highest temperatures for TiN, despite optical changes, the nanorings retain their overall ring-like geometry, with no evidence of structural collapse or restructuring. This morphological stability underscores the intrinsic robustness of TiN as a plasmonic material.

XPS analysis was carried out in order to understand the chemical processes leading to TiN stability and failure at 500 °C. Figure compares the Ti 2p and N 1s spectra for TiN nanorings as synthesized and after annealing at 500 °C. Due to the complex XPS structure of the Ti 2p signal and several present intermediate species, the spectra will be discussed only qualitatively. A more detailed discussion together with the respective O 1s spectra, fitting parameters, and their literature sources can be found in Text S1 and Table S2. The bare glass substrate contains a certain amount of Ti in a SiO2 matrix, which leads to a continuous background signal in the Ti 2p spectrum (gray). For the as-synthesized TiN sample, we observe signal contributions from TiN (purple), TiO2 (green), intermediate TiO x N y and (Ti-)­N–O species (blue and teal), as well as two further nitrogen species (brown). Several literature sources state that TiN forms a natural oxidic passivating shell, which then protects the TiN core. − This is also observed here with both TiO2 and intermediate oxynitride species. The additional observed nitrogen species (brown) are assigned by Saha et al. to degraded nitrogen species after leaving the TiN lattice due to oxidation. After annealing at 500 °C, all nitrogen leaves the nanoring surface, and purely TiO2 remains within the XPS probing depth (roughly 5–10 nm). Figure c shows a sketch of the nanorings after synthesis with a passivating oxidic layer, while after annealing, the bulk oxidizes completely, and all decay products desorb.

(a) Ti 2p and (b) N 1s signal of (i) the bare glass substrate, (ii) TiN nanorings as synthesized, and (iii) TiN nanorings after annealing. The XPS analysis of the thermally treated TiN rings shows that the failure mechanism clearly distinguishes them from those of Au and Ag nanorings. While these decay through mechanical deformation, TiN nanorings lose their optical response between 400 and 500 °C because of complete oxidation. This coincides with the very stable optical response of TiN up to 400 °C and its sudden drop as soon as oxidation through the TiO2 passivation layer becomes possible (Figure D). It is thus expected that TiN nanorings could be used up to even higher temperatures in an oxygen-free or vacuum environment. The presented results are in accordance with the ones presented by Wells et al. for thin films.

Heat treatment of the TiN nanorings to 500 °C showed a red-shift of the TiN plasmon peak (Figure D). We used FDTD to explore the expected result of oxidation of the TiN rings observed in XPS. Figure presents simulated extinction spectra illustrating the influence of progressively increasing the TiO2 layer thickness on the optical response of 200 nm TiN nanorings. The results reveal a pronounced red-shift of the LSPR peak as the TiO2 overlayer thickness increases from 2 to 20 nm, accompanied by a gradual reduction in peak intensity. This spectral displacement is consistent with the well-established sensitivity of plasmonic resonances to changes in the surrounding dielectric environment, where the higher refractive index of TiO2 (n ≈ 2.4) relative to air modifies the effective optical path, combined with any changes in the shape of the TiN core, which overall lowers the resonance energy. The shift in the peak position matching that in the experiment suggests a surface oxide thickness of around 6–8 nm. The observed trend directly aligns with the XPS results in Figure , which confirm the progressive oxidation of TiN into TiO2 upon high-temperature annealing, thereby increasing the effective dielectric constant in the vicinity of the nanorings.

Simulated spectra showing the evolution of the optical response of 200 nm TiN nanorings with systematic replacement of a TiN surface layer of different thickness with TiO2.

To contextualize the thermal performance of TiN nanorings, Figure compares the extinction spectra of 200 nm Ag nanorings and 200 nm TiN nanorings after exposure to 200 °C for 6 h. The Ag nanorings exhibit a pronounced degradation in their plasmonic response, characterized by a significant reduction in the peak intensity and a broadening of the extinction profile. This behavior is consistent with the known thermal instability of Ag, which tends to undergo surface diffusion and morphological changes, even at relatively low temperatures. The discussed appearance of new structures, like crescents, can be associated with the new modes that appeared.

Extinction (1-transmission) spectra showing the thermal stability of 200 nm Ag nanorings (A) vs 200 nm TiN nanorings (B) after being exposed to 200 °C for 6 h.

In contrast, the TiN nanorings maintain a well-defined extinction peak with minimal broadening or intensity loss. This stark difference highlights the superior thermal stability of TiN, even under prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures. The retention of plasmonic features in TiN nanorings after 6 h at 200 °C demonstrates their potential for long-term operation in thermally demanding environments, where conventional plasmonic materials like Ag would fail.

The observed thermal stability of TiN nanorings can be attributed to several key material properties. TiN possesses a high melting point (∼2950 °C), which provides a wide thermal operating window. Its strong lattice structure, characterized by covalent bonding between titanium and nitrogen atoms, resists atomic diffusion and bond breakage under thermal stress. This structural rigidity minimizes the extent of thermally induced deformation, preserving the nanoring geometry and, consequently, the plasmonic response.

Additionally, TiN exhibits a relatively low thermal expansion coefficient compared to metals like Ag and Au, reducing the mechanical stresses that typically lead to cracking or delamination in nanostructures. The combination of these properties results in a material that not only withstands high temperatures but also maintains functional plasmonic behavior, as evidenced by the minimal spectral degradation observed in both Figures and.

The results presented here demonstrate that TiN nanorings can serve as reliable plasmonic transducers in such environments, offering stable LSPR signals even under prolonged thermal exposure. The minimal spectral drift at high temperatures ensures consistent sensor calibration, while the preserved morphology guarantees long-term durability.

Conclusions

The findings of this study have significant implications for the field of plasmonics. The successful fabrication of TiN nanorings through a scalable, cost-effective approach, coupled with their exceptional thermal resilience, broadens the horizon for plasmonic sensor deployment in environments that are unsuitable for traditional noble-metal nanostructures. Potential applications include real-time sensing in high-temperature environments, accurate monitoring of exhaust gases, and integration into thermally demanding optoelectronic devices. Furthermore, the retention of robust plasmonic activity at elevated temperatures positions TiN nanorings as promising candidates for thermoplasmonic applications, facilitating the precise control of light-to-heat conversion essential for advanced thermal management, nanofluidics, and photothermal modulation.

In summary, this work introduces a reliable and accessible route for fabricating thermally stable TiN nanorings via hole-mask colloidal lithography. By effectively combining material durability and manufacturing scalability, the presented methodology marks a significant advancement in the development of plasmonic nanostructures suited for high-temperature applications. This research underscores the considerable yet underexplored potential of TiN-based plasmonic sensing and solidifies the role of HCL as a versatile and powerful platform capable of fabricating sophisticated nanostructures from diverse functional materials.
