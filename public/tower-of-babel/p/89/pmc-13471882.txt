Reply to “Sural/Radial Amplitude Ratio in Non‐Length‐Dependent Neuropathy (NLDN): A Call for More Standardized Methodology and Further Evaluation of Distal NLDN”
Authors: Antoine Pegat, Guillaume Fargeot, Françoise Bouhour
Published: 2026

We read with interest the article by Shirilla et al. [1] entitled “Sural/Radial Amplitude Ratio in Non‐Length‐Dependent Neuropathy (NLDN): a call for more standardized methodology and further evaluation of distal NLDN,” in response to our study [2].

This response aims to provide more details to describe our methodology and answer the points requested. The same methodology as reported previously [3] was uniformly implemented in the current study. In addition, sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP), like radial SNAP, was recorded using antidromic stimulation. As widely observed in French practice, no fixed distance between the stimulation point and the recording electrodes was used. Instead, the stimulator was moved over the skin until the largest reproducible SNAP amplitude was obtained without stimulation artifact; an acceptable waveform included clearly identifiable polarity, configuration, onset, peaks as well as return to baseline as recommended [4]. The duration of stimulation was classically at 0.1 ms for radial nerve and 0.2 ms for sural nerve, and responses collected were averaged 5 to 10 times.

In the original study by Rutkove et al. [5], fixed conduction distances were used between the stimulating and recording electrodes, specifically 13 cm for the sural nerve and 10 cm for the radial nerve. We agree that SNAP amplitude decreases as conduction distance increases. Our distance between electrodes is often shorter, resulting in larger SNAP amplitudes. The same methodology was consistently applied by all examiners for all nerves and all participants, minimizing both inter‐subject and intra‐subject variability for the sural/radial amplitude ratio (SRAR).

Shirilla et al. cited the protocol of Rutkove et al. [5] as using negative peak amplitude for SNAP amplitudes, but we disagree because that article states “from positive to negative peaks,” corresponding to peak‐to‐peak amplitudes; this is the same methodology used by Overbeek et al. [6] and in our laboratory and accepted in current recommendations [4]. Nevertheless, we fully agree with Shirilla et al. that the method used is a critical factor and directly influences SRAR value.

The reproducibility of the SRAR was questioned, which is an important issue given that our original study [2] was conducted at a single center. A separate multicenter French study [Fargeot et al., in press], investigating principally nerve ultrasonography in sensory neuronopathy (SNN), showed SRAR values remarkably similar, supporting the reproducibility of our SRAR methodology.

We previously described an alternative technique to record radial SNAP [3], typically yielding larger amplitudes. However, this alternative radial SNAP technique was not used in our study. The conventional recording method was used as described in the original manuscript [2].

We used lowest/lowest radial and sural SNAP amplitudes rather than the highest/highest values because SNN and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) frequently exhibit an asymmetric distribution of abnormalities. Using highest/highest values, in acquired immune‐mediated SNN for example, could artificially yield a low SRAR value because the radial SNAP amplitude from the symptomatic limb (low value) was not used. In our original study [2], bilateral radial and sural nerve conduction studies were available in only 19 of 90 patients. In response to the comment by Shirilla et al., we recalculated the median (IQR) SRAR values using the highest/highest method. The results were very similar to those obtained with our original approach: 0.65 (0.50–1.00) for SNN, 0.63 (0.41–1.10) for CIDP, and 0.21 (0.15–0.35) for length‐dependent neuropathy (LDN). Several methods can be used to calculate SRAR with no significant difference [6], but in the context of asymmetrical distribution, the lowest/lowest method seems more effective.

As noted by Shirilla et al., we acknowledge that SRAR cannot reliably discriminate LDN from distal‐predominant NLDN; therefore, we agree caution is warranted when extrapolating our findings to all forms of NLDN. Furthermore, in our experience, SRAR > 0.33 may also be observed in other neuropathies, including hereditary transthyretin (TTR) amyloid neuropathy, certain forms of axonal Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (especially those associated with LRSAM1 or MME variants), and multiple mononeuropathy due to vasculitis.

In conclusion, our original study [2] suggests that a SRAR > 0.33 may be a useful indicator of NLDN, particularly in SNN and CIDP. However, this cutoff may not be applicable to all forms of NLDN, especially distal‐predominant NLDN, which was not represented in our original cohort. Furthermore, the proposed threshold should be interpreted in the context of the methodology used to measure SNAP amplitudes, as our SRAR values were derived from peak‐to‐peak amplitude measurements. Therefore, SRAR should be interpreted within the broader clinical, electrophysiological, and etiological context rather than as an isolated diagnostic marker.
