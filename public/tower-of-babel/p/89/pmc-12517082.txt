Prevalence of pathogenic bacteria and their antimicrobial patterns analysis of clinical samples from free-range chickens raised in forest farms in Zhouqu county of Gansu Province, China
Authors: Hanyurwumutima Egide, Jingyan Zhang, Lei Wang, Guowei Xu, Kang Zhang, Yanping Yang, Guolin Zhang, Niyokwishimira Alfred, Gufe Claudious, Xueyan Gu, Zhiting Guo, Pan Zhang, Jianxi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global concern, and resistant pathogens such as Escherichia coli (E. coli), Salmonella enterica, and Shigella flexneri are acquired by humans through the food chain. AMR in poultry pathogens threatens both animal and public health, yet data on AMR in free-range systems, particularly forest-farmed chickens in understudied regions like Zhouqu County, China, remain scarce, poorly understood, and hinder targeted interventions. This study investigated bacterial and AMR prevalence in such a chicken-raising system to guide sustainable farming strategies. A total of 1,315 clinical samples, including oropharyngeal swabs (n=280), cloacal swabs (n=355), fresh feces (n=535), and intestinal contents (n=145), were collected from seven free-range farms experiencing severe health issues. Bacterial prevalence and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were assessed using standardized bacterial culture, the VITEK 2 system, Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion tests, and PCR assays. The most prevalent isolates were E. coli (14.52%), followed by Shigella flexneri (6.54%) and Salmonella enterica (4.94%). Antibiogram analysis revealed that E. coli exhibited (100%) resistance to oxytetracycline, ceftiofur, and ampicillin, with high resistance rates to doxycycline (94.76%), neomycin (85.34%), and tylosin (61.78%). Shigella flexneri showed high resistance to neomycin (66.28%), oxytetracycline (61.63%), and penicillin (58.14%), while Salmonella enterica displayed resistance to neomycin (66.15%) and oxytetracycline (61.54%). Among the isolates, multidrug resistance (MDR) accounted for 27.27% of the total isolates. These findings highlight the high prevalence of AMR among E. coli and Shigella flexneri in free-range poultry farms, posing a critical concern for poultry health, productivity, and food safety. The study underscores the urgent need for stakeholder awareness and intervention strategies to mitigate infectious diseases and curb the emergence and spread of AMR in poultry farming systems.Keywords: Free-range chicken, Pathogenic bacteria, Antimicrobial resistance, Prevalence, forest-farm

Introduction

Since the world's population and urbanization are growing, the demand for animal-derived food such as protein required to feed humanity is increasing (Han et al., 2024a). Additionally, chicken is one of the important foods for smallholders and poor rural, and urban communities, mainly produced in large-scale and intensive operations, making it one of the fastest-growing (Mottet and Tempio, 2017). Therefore, the poultry industry is forced to boost production at the global level and reduce the risk of transmission of infectious pathogens among birds (M.-F. Tan et al., 2023a; Astill et al., 2018). Some pathological bacteria colonized in chickens without detectable symptoms constitute a health risk to animals and humans (Caffrey et al., 2021a). Hence, biosecurity measures, disease history, and rapid disease diagnosis technology remain indispensable to reduce the risks associated with intensified poultry production systems.

Antimicrobials have been crucial in treating infectious diseases since their discovery (Caffrey et al., 2021b), and around 73% of antimicrobials sold worldwide are employed in food-producing animals (Tiseo et al., 2020; Van Boeckel et al., 2019; C. Zhao et al., 2024). However, overuse and abuse are the main contributors to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) (Agyare et al., 2018; Byrne et al., 2019; Mandal et al., 2022). AMR was linked to the deaths of nearly 5 million people in 2019 and threatens the world economy (Murray et al., 2022). Furthermore, if the current trend of inappropriate and excessive use of antimicrobials continues, AMR will affect healthcare production and trade between countries and will cause 10 million deaths worldwide by the year 2050. South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa have the world's highest rates of mortality associated with resistant pathogenic germs (GBD, 2019; Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, 2022). Likewise, there is growing alarm about the propagation of AMR genes across poultry flocks and possible transmission through the food chain. Given the increasing demand for poultry products, the chicken population has expanded, resulting in an overuse of antimicrobial feed additives for prophylaxis and growth enhancement. Antimicrobials are commonly utilized as feed additives in South Asia and Africa, which has led to antimicrobial resistance. However, AMR data from various chicken production systems are scarce. The Escherichia, Shigella, and Salmonella species are prevalent pathogens of several diseases that result in high morbidity and mortality in chicken farms (Abdelrahman et al., 2022; Keerthirathne et al., 2022a). They are the main causative agents of food-borne illnesses and contribute to AMR (Aguilera et al., 2022a; Obi and Ike, 2018a; Abda, Haile, and Abera, 2021). The real crucial problem is that chickens can produce infected eggs, and the infected hen may contaminate the embryo during egg formation (Kursa et al., 2022). Studies showed poultry and poultry products among the reservoirs for harmful microorganisms (Jibril et al., 2020). In addition, these principal pathogenic bacteria can also transmit their antimicrobial-resistant genes to other strains of the same or different bacteria through their mobile genetic elements (Al Azad et al., 2019; Keerthirathne et al., 2022b). Furthermore, contaminated poultry and poultry products may infect consumers via the food value chain (Aquilanti et al., 2007). Birds and farms may become infected with Escherichia, Shigella, and Salmonella species through contaminated poultry products, water, feed, workers, automobiles, winds, and other domestic and wild animals. In addition, poor biosecurity control measures, inadequate microbiological monitoring, and poor microbiological quality analysis within the food chain may give rise to farm contamination (Aguilera et al., 2022b; Tan et al., 2023b). Therefore, it is necessary to monitor the prevalence of the main pathogenic bacteria in poultry farms to enhance the effectiveness of antimicrobial therapy and track the advancement of bacterial drug resistance.

Free-range chickens are usually reared in the forest farm at an altitude of 1,200–2,900 meters in Zhouqu County of Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, in the southwest of Gansu Province, China. The feed for the birds comes mostly from natural resources, including blades of grass, seeds, insects, and others. The chickens spend most of the day foraging outdoors and only return to the coop in the evening. Therefore, it is difficult to prevent and control the transmission of pathogens in these open farms. Biosecurity is always a concern in this type of environment. It is very important to investigate the bacteria prevalence in this area and their antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. This study intended to assess the major pathogenic bacteria and their antimicrobial resistance patterns against eleven antimicrobials used in free-range chickens raised in some forest farms with pressure of hygiene and diseases in Zhouqu County of Gansu Province, China. The gathered data will serve as evidence-based recommendations for establishing strategic initiatives that involve all stakeholders to tackle the identified issues.

Materials and methodology

Materials and sample collections

A total of one thousand three hundred and fifteen clinical samples were collected from free-range chickens that showed various health problems, including watery, greenish, whitish, and occasionally bloody diarrhea observed during farm inspections, owner interviews, and necropsy. Other symptoms included drooping wings, high body temperature, loss of appetite, weight loss, decreased egg production, swollen face and eyes, distended abdomen, lethargy, depression, lameness, weakness, pale combs, anorexia, sudden death, peritonitis, and oophoritis. Samples were collected as follows: fresh fecal samples [n=535 (n1=267 from healthy birds, n2=134 from sick birds, and n3=134 from the anus of dead birds)], oropharyngeal samples [n=280 (n1=140 from healthy birds, n2=70 from sick birds, and n3=70 from dead birds)], cloacal samples [n=355 (n1=177 from healthy birds, n2=89 from sick birds, and n3=89 from dead birds)], and intestinal contents (n=145) from dead birds. All samples were collected aseptically using sterile cotton swabs moistened with sterile saline. The swabs were then placed in sterile tubes of 1 mL (CY-F002-01, Shenzhen Hua Chenyang Technology Co., Ltd., China) and 5 mL centrifuge tubes (biosharp® Life Science, China). The tubes were immediately placed in an ice container and transferred to the institute’s laboratory on the same day. Upon arrival at the laboratory, the samples were processed in a second-level biosafety cabinet and stored at -4°C for 10 hours. To prevent cross-contamination between farms, the sampling procedure was conducted as follows: two farms were sampled per week, with a one-day interval between farms. Personnel used personal protective equipment (PPE) and sample protection materials, including disposable surgical gowns (Nanchang Yuhua Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China), disinfectants (Dezhou Yishenkang Sterilizing Products Co., Ltd., China), single-use blue plastic boots, latex gloves, head covers (biosharp® Life Science, China), and masks (Jiangsu Kangyou Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Animal care and experimental procedures were approved and supervised by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Lanzhou Institute of Husbandry and Pharmaceutical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (NO. 2025-13), following animal research guidelines.

Isolation and identification of bacterial strains

Samples were pre-enriched in buffered peptone water and incubated at 37°C for 18 hours. A loopful of each culture was streaked onto nutrient agar (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., China) and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours for general bacterial isolation. After incubation, growth, and Gram stain results were observed. A single colony from each visible gram-negative culture was inoculated onto MacConkey agar (Zhongshan Bai Microbial Technology Co., China) for selective isolation. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. The suspected E. coli isolate was subcultured from MacConkey agar onto a second MacConkey agar plate for purity and subsequently plated onto eosin methylene blue (EMB) for selection. Suspected Salmonella enterica and Shigella flexneri were also plated from MacConkey agar to an EMB agar, then onto Salmonella-Shigella (SS) agar, and HardyCHROM™ Salmonella agar (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd, China). Pure E. coli, Shigella flexneri, and Salmonella enterica colonies were separately plated onto triple sugar iron agar for differentiation based on sugar fermentation, H₂S production, bacterial growth, motility, and changes in the color of the media. In addition, E. coli, Shigella flexneri, and Salmonella enterica isolates were also screened using standard biochemical tests (QingDao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd, China) which including indole (IND), lysine decarboxylase (LDC), ornithine decarboxylase (ODC), urea (URE), ortho-nitrophenyl-ß-galactoside (ONPG), citrate (CIT), hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), potassium cyanide (KCN), voges-proskauer (VP), mannitol (MAN), motility (MOT), glucose (GLU), dulcitol (DUL), salicin(SAL), arginine (ARG), guanine (GUA), lactose (LAC), gelatin (GEL), inositol (INO), sorbitol (SOR), rhamnose (RHA), sucrose (SAC), melibiose (MEL), bitter almond kernel (AMY), arabinose (ARA), and xylose (XYL). E. coli ATCC25922, Salmonella enterica LT2 (ATCC 19585), and Shigella flexneri (ATCC 12022) were used as control strains. Final confirmation was performed via the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, USA; GN card) and PCR assays.

Bacterial DNA extraction and indentification with 16S rDNA

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 mL of the bacterial solution was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of buffer BP and 50 μL of lysozyme (20 mg/mL). The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 60 minutes. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, 180 μL of LS-2 buffer, 20 μL of proteinase K (20 mg/mL), and 10 μL of RNase A (10 mg/mL) were added. The mixture was incubated at 56°C for 10 minutes until it was transparent. Then, 200 μL of BS-2 buffer and 200 μL of 100% ethanol were added, and the solution was transferred to a DNA mini-column. After 1 minute at room temperature, it was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 minute, and the supernatant was discarded. The column was washed with 500 μL of buffer WA and 750 μL of WB buffer (twice), with centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 1 minute each time. The column was dried by centrifugated at 12,000 rpm for 2 minutes. DNA was eluted by adding 50 μL of elution buffer, incubated for 1 minute, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 minutes. To a higher yield, the elution step was repeated.

PCR amplification was performed using the primers 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-CTACGGCTACCTTGTTACGA-3′). The PCR reactions were prepared in a volume of 20 µL volume containing 1.0 µL of template DNA (50 ng/µL), 10.0 µL of 2x Taq Master Mix, 1.0 µL of 27F primer (10 µM), 1.0 µL of 1492R primer (10 µM), and 7.0 µL of nuclease-free ddH₂O. The reaction mixture was briefly centrifuged to collect droplets from the tube walls before being placed in the thermal cycler. The PCR amplification protocol included the following steps: initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 seconds, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 seconds, annealing at 55°C for 30 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 2 minutes. A final extension step was performed at 72°C for 5 minutes. After amplification, the PCR products were purified using an agarose gel electrophoresis kit (Sciences & Technology Co., Ltd., China). The purified PCR product was sequenced by Yangling Tianrun Oko Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. The sequence data were compared with the NCBI 16S rDNA database for identification, then FASTA, MEGA 12, and iTOL software, and presented as a phylogenetic tree.

Antimicrobial sensitivity

Using the disk diffusion method, eleven antimicrobials (Table 1) were subjected to the antimicrobial susceptibility test against E. coli, Shigella flexneri, and Salmonella enterica (Bantawa et al., 2019). Briefly, 100 µL of a bacterial suspension adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard using a sterile saline solution (Beijing Solarbio Sciences & Technology Co., Ltd., China) was inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton agar plates (Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China). Four different antimicrobial discs were then placed on the surface of each plate. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 18–24 hours in an inverted position. After incubation, the plates were examined, and the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured using a sliding caliper. The zone margin was defined as the area showing no visible bacterial growth detectable by the unaided eye. The results were interpreted as sensitive (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines (Giske et al., 2022; Humphries et al., 2018). In addition, an automated drug susceptibility test was performed using the VITEK®2 System (BioMérieux, Inc., North Carolina, USA) with the VITEK®2 AST-GN96 test card (BioMérieux, Inc., North Carolina, USA), following the standard protocol for antibiogram testing of gram-negative bacteria (Garcia-Garrote, Cercenado, and Bouza, 2000; Yoo et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA through statistical software (GraphPad. Prism.v5.0., San 172 Diego, CA) and IBM SPSS Statistics 19. Group differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Bacteria differentiation

The isolates showed distinct morphological characteristics across different culture media (Fig. 1). On MacConkey agar, large pink colonies suggested E. coli. Smooth, transparent, non-lactose-fermenting colonies without blackening suggested Salmonella enterica. Smooth, circular, pale, and smaller than Salmonella enterica colonies indicated Shigella flexneri. Isolates were further differentiated on SS agar. Among them, E. coli, a lactose fermenter, grew as pink to red colonies, Salmonella enterica was identified by its colorless to transparent appearance with a black center due to H2S production, while Shigella flexneri formed colorless and transparent colonies. Salmonella enterica showed pink to magenta colonies on MacConkey agar and mauve to purple colonies with a halo on HardyCHROM Salmonella agar, confirming their identity. E. coli grew as a green metallic sheen on eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar, while Shigella flexneri grew as colorless to pale pink. Biochemical profiling, including TSI agar tests, E coli produced a yellow slant and yellow butt with vigorous gas production (cracking) due to glucose, lactose, and sucrose fermentation but no H₂S; Salmonella enterica showed a red slant and yellow butt with blackening (H₂S production) and weak gas production, fermenting only glucose; while Shigella flexneri displayed a red slant and yellow butt without gas or H₂S, fermenting glucose only (Fig. 5). Pathogenic bacteria isolated from clinical samples

Prevalence of bacterial pathogens is presented in Fig. 3, Fig. 4. Using bacterial culture, biochemical, 16S rDNA sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis, a total of 456 samples were isolated and identified bacterial pathogens from 1,315 clinical samples (584 samples from apparently healthy birds, 293 samples from sick birds, and 438 samples from dead birds). E. coli was the most prevalent bacteria (18/584, 3.08% prevalence in apparently healthy chickens, 33/293, 11.26% in sick chickens, and 140/438, 31.96% in dead chickens), followed by Shigella flexneri (22/584, 3.77% in apparently healthy chickens, 20/293, 6,83% in sick birds, and 44/438, 10.05% in dead birds), Salmonella enterica (12/584, 2.05% in apparently healthy chickens, 8/293, 2.73% in sick birds, and 45/438, 10.27% of dead birds).

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of E. coli, Shigella flexneri, and Salmonella enterica isolates

In this study, chicken samples were obtained from 7 free-range farms. Among 1315 raw chicken samples, E. coli was isolated from 191 samples, Shigella flexneri from 86 samples, and 65 samples were positive for Salmonella enterica (Table 3). In addition, isolates underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) for 11 commonly used antimicrobials (Table 3). The 11 antimicrobials tested belonged to 7 classes of antimicrobials. Among isolates, E. coli isolates showed the highest resistance to ceftiofur, oxytetracycline, and ampicillin (191/191; 100%), followed by doxycycline (181/191; 94.76%), neomycin (163/191; 85.34%), and tylosin (118/191; 61.78%). Almost all E. coli isolates were susceptible to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (182/191; 95.29%), cephalexin (103/191; 53.93%), and enrofloxacin (100/191; 52.36%). Among Shigella flexneri isolates displayed resistance to neomycin (57/86; 66.28%), followed by oxytetracycline (53/86; 61.63%), and penicillin (50/86; 58.14%). While highly susceptible to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and Ceftiofur (70/86; 81.40%). In Salmonella enterica isolates, neomycin resistance was the highest (43/65; 66.15%), followed by oxytetracycline (40/65; 61.54%). Multidrug and multiple antimicrobial resistance profiles in E. coli, Shigella flexneri, and Salmonella enterica

The results revealed concerning antimicrobial resistance (AMR) patterns among the tested pathogens (Table 5). AMR patterns were determined following the previously established guidelines (Magiorakos et al. 2012). In this study, only resistant isolates were considered, excluding those categorized as "intermediate." An isolate was classified as MDR if it demonstrated resistance to at least one antimicrobial agent from three or more distinct antimicrobial classes. Multiple antimicrobial resistance (MAR) refers to resistance to two or more antimicrobial agents, even if they belong to the same class. The results showed that E. coli exhibited extensive drug resistance (XDR), with resistance to 5 out of 7 antimicrobial classes (penicillins, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, macrolides, and cephalosporins), and a high MAR index (0.54). Shigella flexneri qualified as multidrug-resistant (MDR), showing resistance to 3 out of 7 antimicrobial classes (penicillins, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides) and a moderate MAR index (0.27). In contrast, Salmonella enterica displays non-MDR status and the lowest MAR index (0.18), suggesting comparatively better treatment options.

Discussion

The widespread application of antimicrobials in free-range chicken farming has likely contributed to the development of AMR among pathogenic bacteria, complicating disease management. Notably, the emergence of AMR in primarily commensal bacteria, such as E. coli, represents a major challenge in poultry health systems. The present study investigated bacterial prevalence and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in clinical samples collected from free-range chickens raised in seven forest farms in Zhouqu County, Gansu Province, China. The most frequently detected bacterial genera were Escherichia, Shigella, and Salmonella. These pathogenic intestinal bacteria pose a significant zoonotic risk and can be transmitted to humans through direct contact or via food. Antibiogram analysis revealed that E. coli, Shigella flexneri, and Salmonella enterica exhibited resistance to commonly used antimicrobials, underscoring a critical threat to poultry health. In this study, E. coli exhibited a prevalence rate of 14.52%. A similar study in Shanghai (Que et al., 2025) reported the prevalence rate of E. coli at 15.67%, with 95.45% of MDR. Also, a relatively higher prevalence rate of 48.49% was documented in healthy chicken farms in China (Zou et al., 2021a). Additionally, the prevalence rate of E. coli at 91% was reported across 300 poultry farms in various regions of Nepal (Bhattarai et al., 2024). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of E. coli showed complete resistance (100%) to ampicillin, ceftiofur, and oxytetracycline. High resistance rates were observed against doxycycline (94.76%) and neomycin (85.34%), while moderate resistance was observed with tylosin (61.91%). The lowest resistance rate was observed for enrofloxacin (4.77%). The relatively lower MDR rate among E. coli isolates of 54.54%, with a MAR index of 0.54, compared to the 99.21% reported by Tan et al. (2023c), suggests possible regional differences in the development and spread of multidrug-resistant E. coli (Tan et al., 2023c). Another study analyzed prevalence and AMR in healthy chickens from farms and live poultry markets in China and reported 100% resistance to ampicillin and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, 95.5% to tetracycline, and 90.9% to doxycycline ( Zou et al., 2021b). Notably, their study reported 100% resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; no resistance to this antimicrobial was observed in our study. A similar previous study assessed the antimicrobial resistance of E. coli in clinical cases of colibacillosis in the commercial broiler, layer, and breeder chickens of Chitwan, Dang, Rupandehi, Makawanpur, Palpa, Nawalpur, and Arghakhachi districts that are known poultry pockets of Nepal and revealed a 91% prevalence of E. coli in poultry from Nepal, with resistance rates of 99.4% to ampicillin, 92% to enrofloxacin, 79.4% to tetracycline, and 81.1% to doxycycline, alongside an MDR rate of 39.8% (Rk et al., 2024).

Likewise, the emergence and spread of Shigella in livestock populations pose significant concerns for animal health, food safety, and public health due to its zoonotic potential. Our study found a Shigella flexneri prevalence rate of 6.54%. Similarly, the prevalence rate of Shigella flexneri at 6.7% in free-range reared chickens was reported in Nsukka, Nigeria (Obi and Ike, 2018). The prevalence rate of Shigella spp. (Sh. dysenteriae, S. flexneri, Sh. boydii and Sh. Sonnei) at 28.3–33.7% was documented from a serum epidemiological survey of poultry flocks in China (Shi et al., 2014a). Similarly, one clinical isolate of Shigella flexneri was reported from a diarrheal chicken farm in Zhengzhou (Henan Province) in 2004 (Hu et al., 2008). The prevalence rate of Shigella flexneri at 10% was reported in chicken meat in Tamil Nadu, India (Rabins, 2021). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of Shigella flexneri showed high resistance rates to oxytetracycline (62.00%), penicillin (58.6%), and neomycin (66.67%), and a multidrug resistance (MDR) rate of 27.27% with an AMR index of 0.27. These findings align with global reports highlighting Shigella as a major pathogen in livestock, contributing to economic losses in poultry production and public health risks (Wang et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2014b). In contrast to Northwest China, a higher prevalence (28.3%) and extreme resistance rates of 100% to penicillin were reported (Zhu et al., 2017). In this study, the lower prevalence and resistance rates may reflect regional differences in antimicrobial usage, farm management practices, or stricter biosecurity measures. The lower MDR rate in our study (27.27%) suggests the potential effectiveness of antimicrobial stewardship programs in this region. These findings underscore the need for continuous surveillance of Shigella in livestock to monitor prevalence and resistance trends. While the observed lower resistance rates may indicate progress in responsible antimicrobial use, the persistent resistance to critically important antimicrobials, such as oxytetracycline and penicillin, highlights the necessity for ongoing vigilance and alternative strategies to combat the spread of resistant Shigella strains in livestock systems.

The emergence and spread of Salmonella strains in the poultry industry is a growing problem for both animal health and food safety. A critical challenge is whether the use of antimicrobials in agriculture is driving the selection of antimicrobial-resistant Salmonella. Additionally, the role of extensive reservoirs of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) in animal manures in facilitating the spread of resistance remains an important area of research (Bythwood et al., 2019). In this study, the detected prevalence rate of Salmonella enterica was 4.94%, which is lower thanthe 6.44% reported from farm produce markets in Gansu, China (Jing et al., 2024). Similar higher Salmonella enterica prevalence rates were documented in different chicken farms in separate provinces of China. Specifically, 5.66% in Anhui (Shen et al., 2023), 7.94% Jiangxi Province between 2022 and 2023 (Jiang et al., 2025), 8.36% in Shandong, Anhui, Jiangsu, Xinjiang, and Zhejiang provinces (Wang et al., 2024), and 8.46% in Guangdong Province (Ren et al., 2016). The highest prevalence of Salmonella enterica, at 30.3% chicken fecal and cecal samples in Shandong Province, was reported between 2013 and 2018 (Yu et al., 2021). However, a lowest prevalence rate of 2.5% was reported from Jiangsu province (Meng et al., 2025). Antimicrobial sensitivity showed that Salmonella enterica exhibited a resistance of 61.54% to oxytetracycline, 66.15% to neomycin, 42.86% to tetracycline, 28.57% to doxycycline, and 5.62% to ampicillin, but no resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. MAR rates of 18.18%, but no MDR was observed in Salmonella isolates. A similar study reported a higher MAR of 44.04% in Sichuan Province, China (MaSuzhen et al., 2017), with a high prevalence rate of 26.27% and AMR against ampicillin (36.27%), doxycycline (34.75%), and trimethoprim-sulfamide (33.68%). An almost identical study (Li et al., 2013) reported a low prevalence rate of 0% and high AMR against tetracycline (77%), ampicillin (25%), and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (43%) in Sichuan Province, China. Similarly, in Shanghai, China, it was reported that more than half of the isolates (50.70%) were resistant to ampicillin, 49.32% to sulfisoxazole, 17.12% to tetracycline, and 15.75% to doxycycline (Zhou et al., 2018). Reduction in prevalence, AMR, MAR, and MDR rates could be due to the governmental policies regarding the application of antimicrobials in animal food origin (Charani et al., 2023). The relatively low isolation rates of Shigella flexneri and Salmonella enterica isolates suggest that these pathogens were not the primary cause of chicken mortality but may have contributed to secondary infections. These findings underscore the importance of continuous monitoring of antimicrobial resistance patterns at both local and regional levels to inform effective treatment strategies and antimicrobial use policies. The significant variation in resistance profiles between studies highlights the need for further investigation into the underlying factors driving these differences, including potential variations in antimicrobial selection pressure, biosafety measures, and bacterial transmission dynamics. To address AMR and improve poultry disease management, several strategies have been proposed, including the use of plant extracts and bioactive compounds from medicinal plants, such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and coumarins, which exhibit antimicrobial, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and growth-promoting properties (Daniel et al., 2025; Han et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2024), as well as transdisciplinary policymaking on AMR (Toghroli et al., 2024). Meanwhile, competitive exclusion methods (Christensen, Bachmeier, and Bisgaard, 2021), alternative therapies, and the development of effective bacterial vaccines (Guo et al., 2020; Charkhkar et al., 2024; Goswami et al., 2020), sustaining the host genetic resistance (Monson and Lamont, 2021) were recommended. Sustaining host genetic resistance (Monson and Lamont, 2021) and integrating these measures into poultry husbandry practices could provide sustainable solutions for managing infections while reducing reliance on conventional antimicrobials (Tian et al., 2023; Mountzouris et al., 2010). Further research is needed to explore genetic resistance mechanisms and the efficacy of alternative feed additives in reducing antimicrobial dependency while maintaining poultry health and productivity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies Escherichia, Shigella, and Salmonella strains as the predominant pathogenic bacteria compromising the health and productivity of free-range chickens in forest farms of Zhouqu County, Gansu Province, China. Our findings revealed a concerning antimicrobial resistance profile, with these pathogens demonstrating susceptibility to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and enrofloxacin while exhibiting significant resistance to oxytetracycline and neomycin. The emergence of multidrug-resistant foodborne pathogens underscores the urgent need for a multifaceted approach to antimicrobial resistance mitigation. The findings contribute to the growing body of knowledge on antimicrobial resistance in free-range poultry systems and provide critical insights for developing targeted interventions to safeguard both animal and human health.
