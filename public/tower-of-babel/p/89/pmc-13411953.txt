Distal ureterectomy versus radical nephroureterectomy for nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma: a GRADE-assessed systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies
Authors: Wajahat Mirza, Khalid Hussain, Maria Khalid, Rao Nouman Ali, Muhammad Bilal Moeen-Ud-Din, Abdalla M. Hadhoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe oncologic adequacy of distal ureterectomy (DU) versus radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) for nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma remains debated, particularly in patients for whom renal preservation is clinically desirable.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies was performed. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to February 27, 2026. A total of 229 records were identified, 14 full-text articles were assessed, and 6 studies were included. The primary outcome was a positive surgical margin. Secondary outcomes included intravesical recurrence, cancer-specific survival, lymph node metastasis, muscle-invasive disease (≥ pT2), and high-grade pathologic disease burden as a marker of baseline oncologic risk and treatment-selection balance. Random-effects models were used. Risk of bias was assessed with ROBINS-I, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE.ResultsA total of 6 comparative studies comprising 1,247 patients were included, of whom 588 underwent distal ureterectomy, and 659 underwent radical nephroureterectomy. DU was not associated with a significant difference in positive surgical margin compared with RNU (RR 1.24, 95% CI 0.64–2.43). Cancer-specific survival was also comparable (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.72–1.29). No significant differences were observed for lymph node metastasis (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.40–1.80), muscle-invasive disease (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.86–1.08), or high-grade disease (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.86–1.09). Intravesical recurrence was not significantly different in the primary pooled analysis (HR 1.36, 95% CI 0.89–2.07), although sensitivity analysis suggested a higher recurrence hazard after DU. Most outcomes were supported by very low to low certainty evidence.ConclusionsIn selected patients with nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma, DU appears to preserve oncologic adequacy relative to RNU while offering a kidney-sparing surgical alternative. The main trade-off may be a higher risk of intravesical recurrence, underscoring the need for careful surveillance and individualized surgical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04438-9.

Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma is an uncommon yet clinically aggressive malignancy. Tumors arising in the distal ureter present a particularly consequential management dilemma, as they combine substantial oncologic risk with the technical feasibility of renal preservation. Radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) remains the historical reference standard for high-risk disease; however, it sacrifices a functioning renal unit and may exacerbate chronic kidney disease, restrict cisplatin eligibility, and amplify long-term competing morbidity. By contrast, distal ureterectomy (DU) achieves complete distal ureteral excision with ureteral reimplantation while preserving ipsilateral renal parenchyma, rendering it both biologically and clinically attractive in carefully selected patients. Early distal-ureter-specific comparative series suggested that distal ureterectomy may preserve renal function without meaningfully compromising local control or survival; however, these studies were limited in sample size and retrospective in design, thereby constraining confidence in routine adoption [1, 2]. The central controversy is therefore not whether kidney-sparing surgery is technically feasible, but whether oncologic equivalence can be sustained when nephron preservation is prioritized in the management of nonmetastatic distal ureteral tumors.

More contemporary institutional data have intensified this debate. A large propensity score-matched Chinese cohort demonstrated comparable cancer-specific and overall survival between segmental distal ureteral resection and radical nephroureterectomy, yet identified higher risks of bladder and locoregional recurrence in the kidney-sparing arm [3]. A modern elective distal ureterectomy series similarly identified no statistically significant differences in progression-free, overall, cancer-specific, or intravesical recurrence-free survival, while demonstrating superior postoperative renal functional preservation with the kidney-sparing approach [4]. A multicenter European comparative study further suggested favorable oncologic outcomes for distal ureterectomy in clinically organ-confined distal ureteral disease, whereas a more recent two-center Chinese investigation conducted in patients with high-risk distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma demonstrated significantly worse intravesical recurrence, albeit with preserved overall and cancer-specific survival following propensity matching [5, 6]. Collectively, these data indicate that recurrence phenotype, patient selection criteria, and underlying tumor biology may be more determinative of outcomes than the choice of extirpative surgical template alone.

Existing syntheses have not fully resolved this question, largely because they have addressed broader kidney-sparing strategies or anatomically heterogeneous upper tract populations. Updated meta-analytic work examining endoscopic management versus radical nephroureterectomy focused predominantly on endoscopic rather than open distal ureteral resection [7]. Contemporary reviews of kidney-sparing surgery acknowledge that distal ureteral tumors may constitute a distinct clinical entity, yet consistently highlight persistent selection bias, inconsistent endpoint definitions, and the absence of location-specific high-level evidence [8]. A multicenter study dedicated to high-risk distal ureterectomy reinforced the technical feasibility of this approach, but its single-arm design precludes direct comparative inference [9]. Equally, the earlier meta-analysis of segmental ureterectomy versus radical nephroureterectomy pooled tumors across the entire upper tract and across multiple ureteral segments, limiting its direct applicability to nonmetastatic distal ureteral disease [10].

Against this background, the present study was designed to provide a focused, clinically relevant, and methodologically rigorous synthesis of the evidence specifically comparing distal ureterectomy versus radical nephroureterectomy for nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma. To our knowledge, this represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis devoted exclusively to this anatomical and therapeutic question, rather than to kidney-sparing surgery in the broader sense. By concentrating on distal-ureter-specific comparative cohorts, this review seeks to determine whether renal preservation can be achieved without unacceptable oncologic compromise. Its methodological rigor is further reinforced by planned sensitivity analyses, prespecified subgroup analyses, and a formal Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) certainty-of-evidence assessment, collectively providing a more robust framework for surgical decision-making in a field where clinical practice remains largely guided by retrospective evidence and institutional preference.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies evaluating distal ureterectomy (DU) versus radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) for nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma. The review question was structured according to a prespecified PICO framework: adult patients with nonmetastatic, distal ureter-confined urothelial carcinoma; intervention of DU, including distal segmental ureterectomy or distal ureteral resection with ureteral reimplantation; comparator of RNU; and outcomes including oncologic, perioperative, and renal functional endpoints. Only comparative clinical studies were considered eligible. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration ID: CRD420261328524). The review was conducted in accordance with standard systematic review methodology, and the certainty of evidence for key outcomes was planned to be assessed using the GRADE framework.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive electronic search was performed from database inception to February 27, 2026, across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to distal ureterectomy, segmental ureterectomy, ureteral reimplantation, radical nephroureterectomy, distal ureter, and upper tract urothelial carcinoma. Search syntax was adapted to the indexing structure of each database, and no initial restriction was placed on year of publication. In addition, the reference lists of all eligible studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify potentially missed records. The detailed database-specific search strings and the number of records retrieved from each source are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they enrolled adult patients with nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma and directly compared DU (including distal segmental ureterectomy or distal ureteral resection with ureteral reimplantation) with RNU. Only studies in which the lesion was confined to the distal ureter, or in which distal ureter-specific comparative data were separately extractable, were included. Retrospective or prospective comparative cohort studies were considered eligible if they reported at least one clinically relevant endpoint, including overall survival, cancer-specific survival, recurrence-related outcomes, perioperative outcomes, margin status, or renal functional outcomes. Where both unmatched and propensity score–matched cohorts were presented, the matched dataset was preferentially used for the principal comparative synthesis.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they were single-arm cohorts without a direct DU-versus-RNU comparator, review articles, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable data, case reports, technical notes, animal studies, or duplicate publications. Studies including anatomically mixed upper tract populations without separable distal ureter data were excluded, as were studies focused exclusively on endoscopic management, metastatic disease, non-urothelial histology, or purely salvage-only non-comparative settings. For multi-arm studies, only the directly relevant DU and RNU arms were included in the quantitative synthesis, while non-comparable third arms were excluded from pooled pairwise analyses.

Data extraction and outcome measures

Two reviewers (W.M. and R.N.A.) independently screened all titles, abstracts, and full-text records for eligibility. The same reviewers then independently extracted study-level data using a standardized extraction sheet, and disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third author (K.H.). Extracted variables included study design, country, cohort size, patient demographics, clinicopathological characteristics, matching strategy, perioperative chemotherapy exposure where reported, and all relevant comparative outcome data. The primary outcome was a positive surgical margin. The secondary outcomes were intravesical recurrence-free survival, cancer-specific survival, lymph node metastasis, muscle-invasive disease (≥ pT2), and high-grade pathologic disease burden. High-grade disease was not interpreted as a postoperative treatment effect, but as a comparative marker of baseline/pathologic tumor aggressiveness and treatment-selection balance between DU and RNU cohorts.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed using the ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions) tool, which is the appropriate instrument for observational comparative studies. Each included study was evaluated across the ROBINS-I domains, including bias due to confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Particular attention was given to imbalance in tumor stage, grade, tumor size, baseline renal function, and treatment-selection bias, as these were clinically important confounders in non-randomized surgical cohorts. For studies using propensity score matching, the adequacy of matching and residual post-matching imbalance were specifically considered during the ROBINS-I assessment.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) software. Random-effects models were used throughout to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included retrospective comparative cohorts. For dichotomous outcomes, pooled effect estimates were calculated as Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were used when outcomes were reported on a common scale; studies reporting only medians and interquartile ranges were synthesized narratively when reliable statistical conversion was not feasible. For time-to-event outcomes, hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were preferentially extracted, and adjusted HRs were prioritized over unadjusted Kaplan–Meier comparisons whenever both were available. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic, and the pooled findings were interpreted in light of differences in cohort selection, tumor risk profile, endpoint definitions, and use of propensity score matching.

Sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis, and GRADE certainty of evidence

Sensitivity analysis was prespecified using a leave-one-out approach only, whereby pooled estimates for each meta-analyzed endpoint were recalculated after sequential omission of one study at a time to assess the robustness of the findings. Subgroup analyses were prespecified on a clinical basis using two categories: the Predominantly localized subgroup (Adhikari [1], Dalpiaz [2], and Seisen [5]) and the Predominantly invasive subgroup (Guan [3], Lee [4], and Xie [6]). The Predominantly localized subgroup comprised cohorts in which distal ureteral tumors were mainly organ-confined, lower-stage, or elective distal ureter cases without explicit enrichment for advanced pathological burden. In contrast, the Predominantly invasive subgroup comprised cohorts with greater representation of muscle-invasive disease, high-grade tumors, or explicitly high-risk distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma. This subgrouping was selected to evaluate whether the comparative effect of distal ureterectomy versus radical nephroureterectomy varied according to baseline disease extent and pathological aggressiveness. Finally, the certainty of evidence for each major outcome was assessed using the GRADE framework across the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, with certainty rated as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Results

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 229 records were identified, including 225 through database searching and 4 through citation searching. After the removal of 95 duplicate records, 134 studies underwent title and abstract screening, of which 120 were excluded at the initial screening stage. Fourteen full-text articles were sought, and all were successfully retrieved, with no reports unavailable for assessment. Following full-text review, 8 studies were excluded because of wrong outcomes, wrong comparators, or inappropriate study design, leaving 6 comparative studies for inclusion in the final systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

All included studies were retrospective comparative cohorts evaluating DU against RNU in distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma. The older studies primarily focused on survival and recurrence outcomes, whereas the newer studies also incorporated renal functional preservation and propensity score matching. As shown in Table 2, the baseline populations were broadly comparable across studies, although important imbalances were present in several unmatched cohorts. In particular, the DU groups in Lee [4] and Xie [6] tended to have smaller tumors and better baseline renal function, while the matched analyses in Guan [3] and Xie [6] substantially improved covariate balance (Table 2). Where reported, high-grade and muscle-invasive disease remained common in both groups, indicating that most cohorts still represented clinically meaningful oncologic-risk populations. Baseline renal function varied across studies and did not uniformly follow the expected clinical pattern of DU being reserved for patients with impaired renal reserve. In several cohorts, the DU group had numerically better preoperative eGFR than the RNU group, suggesting that elective DU may also have been selected for patients with more favorable tumor features and preserved renal function rather than only for imperative renal-preservation indications. This imbalance reinforces the likelihood of treatment-selection bias and should be considered when interpreting both oncologic and renal functional outcomes (Table 2).

Methodological quality and study integrity

The overall risk of bias across the included studies was predominantly low to moderate at the domain level, with the most consistent concerns arising from bias due to confounding and bias in the selection of participants, which reached serious risk in two studies and moderate risk in the remainder. In contrast, bias in the classification of interventions and bias due to deviations from intended interventions were uniformly judged as low risk across all six studies. The remaining domains, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result were consistently rated as moderate risk, reflecting the inherent limitations of retrospective comparative designs. Overall, four studies were judged to have a moderate overall risk of bias, while two studies were rated as serious overall risk, indicating that the evidence base is methodologically acceptable but remains vulnerable to residual confounding and non-randomized treatment selection (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Primary outcome

Positive surgical margin

For the primary outcome of positive surgical margin, the pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between distal ureterectomy (DU) and radical nephroureterectomy (RNU), with a pooled risk ratio (RR) of 1.24 (95% CI 0.64–2.43; P = 0.53). Heterogeneity was negligible (I²=0%), indicating high consistency across the included studies. Although the point estimate numerically favored neither group, the confidence interval crossed unity, confirming the absence of a significant between-group effect (Fig. 2).

Across all pooled analyses, none of the evaluated outcomes reached statistical significance. Specifically, positive surgical margin, intravesical recurrence, cancer-specific survival, lymph node metastasis, muscle-invasive disease (≥ pT2), and high-grade disease all had confidence intervals that crossed the null effect. Most analyses demonstrated low or absent heterogeneity, except for intravesical recurrence, which showed substantial between-study variability. Overall, the pooled evidence suggests that DU and RNU had comparable pathologic and oncologic outcomes across the analyzed cohorts, with no statistically significant advantage for either approach across the assessed endpoints.

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis had its greatest impact on intravesical recurrence, where exclusion of Seisen [5] reduced heterogeneity from 75% to 0% and changed the pooled result from non-significant to statistically significant, indicating a significantly higher intravesical recurrence risk after DU (Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, for high-grade disease, removal of Lee 2024 eliminated the small residual heterogeneity (I² 13% to 0%) but did not alter the overall interpretation, as the pooled result remained non-significant. Thus, the intravesical recurrence outcome was sensitive to study-level effect variation, whereas the high-grade disease finding remained stable and robust after leave-one-out analysis. Overall, these results suggest that the main pathologic findings were consistent, while recurrence outcomes were more susceptible to heterogeneity from individual studies.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis of the primary outcome demonstrated no statistically significant difference in positive surgical margin rates between distal ureterectomy (DU) and radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) in either subgroup. In the predominantly localized subgroup, the pooled effect was RR 1.09 (95% CI 0.47–2.51; P = 0.85; I²=0%), while in the predominantly invasive subgroup, the pooled effect was RR 1.52 (95% CI 0.34–6.83; P = 0.58; I²=37%). The overall pooled estimate also remained non-significant (RR 1.24, 95% CI 0.64–2.43; P = 0.53), and there was no significant subgroup interaction (P = 0.70), indicating that baseline disease extent did not materially modify the comparative effect on surgical margin positivity (Fig. 8).

GRADE certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was predominantly very low to low across the evaluated outcomes. The strongest downgrading occurred for intravesical recurrence, where serious concerns about risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision led to a very low-certainty rating. Positive surgical margin, cancer-specific survival, and lymph node metastasis were also graded as very low certainty, largely because of retrospective study design and wide confidence intervals. In contrast, the certainty for muscle-invasive disease and high-grade disease was rated as low, as these pathologic endpoints were more objective, more consistently reported, and showed minimal heterogeneity (Supplementary Table 3). Overall, downgrading was performed domain-by-domain based on ROBINS-I judgments, observed heterogeneity, directness of evidence, precision of pooled estimates, and the absence of an evident publication-bias signal.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis focused specifically on distal ureterectomy (DU) versus radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) for nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma, an anatomically distinct clinical setting in which renal preservation is technically feasible yet oncologic adequacy remains the central concern. The principal finding was that DU was not associated with a higher risk of positive surgical margins, which was selected as the primary outcome because it represented the only directly extractable endpoint reported across all included studies. The pooled estimate demonstrated no statistically significant difference between approaches, with negligible interstudy heterogeneity, suggesting that when DU is performed in appropriately selected patients with distal ureteral tumors, margin control can be comparable to that achieved with RNU. This finding carries considerable clinical importance, as concerns regarding compromised distal ureteral excision or inadequate oncologic clearance have historically restricted acceptance of kidney-sparing surgery to imperative rather than elective indications [1–6].

The secondary outcomes broadly reinforced this pattern of oncologic comparability. Cancer-specific survival did not differ significantly between DU and RNU, with a pooled hazard ratio close to unity and no meaningful interstudy heterogeneity. Similarly, lymph node metastasis, muscle-invasive disease (≥ pT2), and high-grade pathologic disease burden did not differ significantly between groups, indicating that the comparative cohorts were not substantially separated by differences in core pathologic aggressiveness within the pooled analyses. These findings are consistent with the broader observational evidence suggesting that carefully selected kidney-sparing strategies can achieve acceptable oncologic outcomes in anatomically favorable ureteral tumors, particularly distal lesions in which en bloc distal ureteral excision with ureteral reimplantation remains an oncologically principled operative approach. This interpretation is further corroborated by prior comparative syntheses evaluating segmental ureterectomy or broader kidney-sparing strategies for upper tract urothelial carcinoma, although those earlier analyses were not restricted to distal ureter-confined disease and therefore encompassed greater anatomical and clinical heterogeneity than the present review [7–10].

From a clinical standpoint, the most nuanced finding concerned intravesical recurrence. In the primary pooled analysis, intravesical recurrence was not significantly different between DU and RNU; however, interstudy heterogeneity was substantial. Following leave-one-out sensitivity analysis with exclusion of the study contributing most to between-study inconsistency, heterogeneity resolved, and the pooled estimate shifted to a statistically significantly higher hazard of intravesical recurrence after DU. This represents a clinically relevant but sensitivity-dependent signal rather than definitive evidence of higher recurrence risk. Bladder recurrence following treatment for upper tract urothelial carcinoma is biologically plausible given the principles of field cancerization, intraluminal tumor seeding, and shared urothelial carcinogenic exposure, and may be further influenced by operative handling, distal ureteral manipulation, ureteroscopic instrumentation, or the inclusion of patients with antecedent bladder urothelial disease [11–13]. Progression after DU also deserves separate consideration. In the elective DU cohort by Lee et al., progression occurred in both surgical groups without a statistically significant difference in progression pattern, and upper-tract recurrence after DU was managed with salvage RNU in all affected patients. This suggests that DU may remain a reasonable kidney-sparing option in selected patients when rigorous surveillance and timely salvage treatment are feasible, but it also reinforces that kidney-sparing surgery is not a low-surveillance strategy [14, 15]. Simultaneously, the sensitivity of this endpoint to individual study effects indicates that recurrence outcomes are susceptible to variation in surveillance intensity, prior bladder cancer prevalence, perioperative intravesical instillation practices, and the precise recurrence definition applied across studies. Accordingly, while the signal toward higher intravesical recurrence with DU warrants clinical attention, it should be interpreted as hypothesis-strengthening rather than definitively practice-changing evidence [16–19].

Bladder cuff excision is also highly relevant to interpreting intravesical recurrence and survival after surgery for UTUC. In RNU, complete distal ureter and bladder cuff management is considered an oncologic principle because residual distal ureter or intramural ureter can serve as a site for recurrence. In DU, the operation inherently involves distal ureteral excision and ureteral reimplantation, but the extent and technique of bladder cuff excision or intramural ureter management were not consistently reported across the included studies. This limits our ability to determine whether intravesical recurrence differences were related to the choice of DU versus RNU itself, prior bladder cancer biology, ureteroscopic manipulation, perioperative intravesical chemotherapy, or differences in distal ureter/bladder cuff handling. Recent evidence suggests that bladder cuff excision and its technique may influence oncologic outcomes after RNU, including bladder recurrence and survival [20–22], further supporting the need for standardized reporting of BCE in future DU-versus-RNU comparative studies.

The absence of a discernible cancer-specific survival disadvantage despite this recurrence signal is clinically reassuring. It suggests that even when intravesical recurrence is somewhat more frequent following kidney-sparing management in selected patients, this does not necessarily translate into worse disease-specific mortality within the currently available follow-up horizons. This distinction carries practical importance in real-world surgical decision-making. The primary rationale for DU is preservation of renal function and avoidance of the nephron loss inherent to RNU, particularly in elderly patients, those with pre-existing chronic kidney disease, patients at risk of future contralateral disease, and those in whom preservation of renal reserve may determine eligibility for systemic therapy or influence long-term competing morbidity. Although renal functional outcomes could not be robustly pooled across all conceptually relevant studies owing to incomplete numeric reporting in some cohorts, the direction of evidence in the extractable studies consistently favored DU. This supports the established clinical rationale that nephron-sparing surgery may confer a tangible functional advantage when oncologic principles are not compromised [23–27].

The baseline renal function profile also requires careful interpretation. Although DU is often clinically justified by the desire to preserve renal function, several included cohorts reported better preoperative renal function in the DU group than in the RNU group. This counterintuitive pattern likely reflects elective use of DU in anatomically favorable tumors and patients with better overall baseline status, rather than purely imperative nephron-sparing indications. Consequently, postoperative renal preservation should not be attributed only to the surgical procedure without considering baseline renal reserve, tumor characteristics, and treatment-selection factors.

These findings should further be interpreted within the context of evolving contemporary management paradigms. Current reviews, surveillance frameworks, and guideline statements increasingly recognize that upper tract urothelial carcinoma does not represent a single surgical entity, and that distal ureteral tumors may be uniquely amenable to organ-preserving treatment when resection margins are achievable and oncologic risk can be appropriately stratified [28–33]. The prognostic literature has consistently demonstrated that pathologic stage, tumor grade, nodal involvement, lymphovascular invasion, and tumor architecture exert more powerful influence over long-term outcomes than the extirpative surgical template alone, which accounts for the observed balance between groups in the pooled analyses for muscle-invasive and high-grade disease [28, 30, 34, 35]. In this context, the present findings support a more individualized surgical framework: the pertinent clinical question is not whether DU should supplant RNU universally, but rather whether, in carefully selected patients with distal ureteral tumors, DU can preserve ipsilateral renal unit function without introducing an unacceptable oncologic trade-off. The present evidence suggests that the answer is affirmative with respect to margin status and cancer-specific survival, while necessitating greater caution and vigilance regarding intravesical recurrence.

Recent kidney-sparing surgery literature also places DU within a broader but heterogeneous organ-preserving treatment landscape that includes segmental ureterectomy, endoscopic ablation, chemoablation, and other endoscopic kidney-sparing strategies. This distinction is important because DU is an excisional operation with distal ureteral removal and ureteral reimplantation, whereas endoscopic kidney-sparing surgery has different recurrence, progression, and surveillance considerations. Emerging data, including studies of endoscopic kidney-sparing surgery in selected high-grade UTUC patients, show increasing interest in organ preservation beyond traditional low-risk indications; however, these findings should not be directly extrapolated to DU-versus-RNU comparisons [36, 37]. Therefore, the present analysis should be interpreted as specific to distal ureterectomy rather than as a general endorsement of all kidney-sparing modalities.

The certainty of evidence, however, tempers the strength of any derived recommendation. Applying the GRADE framework, most outcomes were adjudicated as providing very low to low certainty evidence. This downgrading was driven primarily by the nonrandomized design of included studies, residual confounding from unmeasured covariates, and imprecision in several pooled estimates. The ROBINS-I assessment further identified confounding and participant selection as the most consequential domains of bias, a finding that is entirely expected in retrospective surgical cohorts where treatment allocation is inherently governed by tumor location, patient comorbidity profile, baseline renal reserve, surgeon preference, and institutional practice patterns. These findings are therefore best interpreted as supportive of DU in appropriately selected patients rather than as definitive evidence of oncologic equivalence across the full spectrum of distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma.

A central limitation of the available evidence is treatment-selection bias. In routine practice, candidacy for kidney-sparing surgery is influenced by preoperative tumor size, radiographic extent, hydronephrosis, biopsy grade, urinary cytology, multifocality, renal reserve, and perceived technical resectability. Patients with larger tumors, high-grade biopsy, positive cytology, invasive features, or adverse imaging findings may be preferentially directed toward radical nephroureterectomy, whereas distal ureterectomy may be selected for patients with more favorable tumor biology or stronger renal-preservation priorities [38]. Because the included studies did not uniformly report these variables in a way that permitted subgroup meta-analysis, we could not formally stratify outcomes by tumor size, biopsy grade, cytology, hydronephrosis, renal functional indication, or perioperative chemotherapy exposure. This is important because perioperative chemotherapy use may reflect tumor aggressiveness, renal eligibility for cisplatin, and institutional treatment strategy, all of which can confound recurrence and survival outcomes. Therefore, the apparent oncologic comparability between DU and RNU should be interpreted with caution and should not be assumed to represent equivalence across all risk groups.

Preoperative diagnostic uncertainty is another important limitation. Ureteroscopic biopsy and urinary cytology often guide risk stratification and treatment selection [14], but biopsy grade, cytology status, biopsy adequacy, and biopsy-to-final pathology concordance were not uniformly reported across the included studies. As a result, we could not evaluate whether patients selected for DU had systematically more favorable preoperative biopsy or cytology profiles than those selected for RNU. This is clinically relevant because preoperative biopsy and cytology can influence whether a patient is offered kidney-sparing surgery, particularly when high-grade disease is suspected.

Based on the available evidence, DU appears most appropriate for carefully selected patients with nonmetastatic tumors confined to the distal ureter when complete distal ureteral excision with negative margins and ureteral reimplantation is technically feasible. Favorable candidates may include patients with unifocal, anatomically resectable distal ureteral tumors, no radiographic nodal or metastatic disease, and either low-risk tumor features or a strong renal-preservation rationale. DU may also be considered in selected higher-risk distal ureteral tumors when renal preservation is clinically important, provided that patients are counseled about recurrence risk and can comply with strict cystoscopic and upper-tract surveillance. Conversely, RNU remains more appropriate when there is multifocal disease, extensive or proximal ureteral involvement, suspicious nodal disease, metastatic disease, inability to achieve negative margins, or concern that kidney-sparing treatment would compromise oncologic control.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its narrow clinical focus on nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma, which substantially improves anatomic and therapeutic specificity compared with prior broader upper tract urothelial carcinoma syntheses. The review also integrated multiple clinically relevant endpoints spanning pathologic adequacy, recurrence, survival, and functional considerations, and it incorporated leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, clinically stratified subgroup analysis, ROBINS-I assessment, and GRADE certainty evaluation to provide a more rigorous and transparent interpretation of the available evidence.

The principal limitation is that all included studies were retrospective comparative cohorts, making the pooled evidence inherently vulnerable to residual confounding and treatment-selection bias. In particular, tumor size, grade, hydronephrosis, baseline renal function, and perioperative chemotherapy exposure may have influenced treatment allocation and oncologic outcomes but were not consistently reported in a way that allowed adjusted subgroup analysis. Outcome definitions were not uniform across studies, especially for recurrence and renal function endpoints, which restricted quantitative synthesis for some clinically important variables. Several secondary analyses were based on a small number of studies, and some outcomes were sensitive to single-study effects, which limit precision and reduce confidence in definitive comparative conclusions. In addition, follow-up duration and surveillance intensity likely differed across cohorts, particularly for recurrence detection.

Conclusions

In patients with nonmetastatic distal ureteral urothelial carcinoma, distal ureterectomy appears to provide oncologic outcomes broadly comparable to radical nephroureterectomy for positive surgical margin status and cancer-specific survival in appropriately selected cases. The principal area of caution is intravesical recurrence, which may be higher after distal ureterectomy in some cohorts. Taken together, these data support distal ureterectomy as a reasonable kidney-sparing option when complete distal ureteral excision is feasible and postoperative surveillance is rigorous. Clinical decision-making should therefore balance tumor biology, technical resectability, baseline renal reserve, and the patient’s long-term functional priorities.
