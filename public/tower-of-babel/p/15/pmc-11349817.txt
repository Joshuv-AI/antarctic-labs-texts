Atypical presentation of polymicrobial cervicofacial necrotizing fasciitis: An extraordinary odontogenic infection in a healthy female patient
Authors: Basel Khalil, Ibrahim Borghol, Akhilanand Chaurasia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Clinical MessageCervicofacial necrotizing fasciitis (CNF) can occur as a dental complication in young, healthy individuals. Early diagnosis, treatment, and awareness of severe acute cervicofacial pain, rapid swelling, and radiological gas formation are crucial. Broad molecular profiling is recommended for understanding the organisms involved in such infections in future research.Keywords: cervicofacial necrotizing fasciitis, contrast‐enhanced computed tomography, odontogenic infections, polymicrobial

INTRODUCTION

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF), a rapidly progressing soft tissue infection is characterized by extensive tissue necrosis and represent a critical and potentially life‐threatening condition. 1 Necrotizing fasciitis in the head and neck region can be categorized into two categories: craniofacial NF, which occurs above the lower border of the mandible, and cervical NF (CNF), which occurs below lower border of the mandible. 2 , 3

NF infections is usually caused by multibacterial sources. 4 , 5 For instance, in the majority of wound cultures, group A Streptococcus was recognized as the predominant causative agent. 6 In addition, the recent surge in nosocomial infections has changed the microbial picture involved in NF infections. As a result, the prevalence of antibiotic‐resistant bacteria, including Vancomycin‐Resistant Enterococci (VRE), Carbapenem‐Resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), and Methicillin‐Resistant Staphylococcus Aureus (MRSA), has also escalated. Therefore, the risk of severe infections has heightened accordingly. 7 , 8 Promptly identifying necrotizing fasciitis can be especially challenging due to the presence of ambiguous symptoms during the initial stages, which subsequently evolve rapidly into an acute systemic infection. 9 The occurrence of CNF in healthy individuals is not frequently documented in the literature, and its diagnosis is often delayed because its signs and symptoms can be easily attributed to mimic other soft tissue infections. In other words, the understanding of NF in the context of head and neck regions, such as CNF, is not as extensive as the general understanding of NF. This could be due to the rarity of CNF cases or the lack of comprehensive research in this specific area. As a result, there may be gaps in knowledge about the disease progression, risk factors, and treatment approaches for NF when it affects the head and neck regions. 10 Accurate diagnosis of CNF necessitates the combination of clinical signs, symptoms, radiological and histopathological findings. This infection can escalate swiftly, posing severe complications if not diagnosed and treated promptly. Although, individuals with compromised immune systems, such as those with conditions like diabetes mellitus, cancer, alcoholism, vascular insufficiencies, organ transplants, HIV, or neutropenia, are more susceptible to this particular type of infection. 2 , 3 , 11 , 12 We are presenting a case of CNF in a healthy patient originating from dental foci, shedding light on its clinical presentation, diagnostic obstacles, and the critical need for prompt and thorough intervention. Additionally, we did basic microbial analysis in present case, which may aid future research in establishing a reliable and specific microbial picture of CNF in healthy patients, addressing the existing research gap in this area.

CASE HISTORY/EXAMINATION

A 45‐year‐old healthy female patient, having no history of medications or allergies, reported to Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at Falun Hospital, nested in Dalarna region of Sweden. She complains of swelling on the right side of her face. She had undergone surgical extraction of the right lower third molar 4 days before, in addition to the extraction of the left lower third molar several weeks earlier. The postextraction healing process for the left lower molar was smooth and uneventful, thus prompting her to visit Figure 1. The patient had further gone for extraction of the right molar. Following the right lower third molar extraction, the patient encountered a rapid progressive swelling on the right side of her face, with pronounced orofacial cervical pain and erythematous appearance on the cheek skin. Simultaneously, the patient had additional signs and symptoms, of fever, trismus, difficulty breathing, and altered voice. The symptoms accompanying the right molar extraction were considerably more intense, and debilitating.

METHODS (DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS, INVESTIGATIONS, AND TREATMENT)

Owing to the escalation of symptoms and the stark contrast in postextraction experiences between the left and right sides, the patient revisited the same clinic for consultation and assessment of her condition. At the emergency room, blood test was conducted to appraise the C‐reactive protein level, which was found to be 254 mg/L, B‐leukocytes 11.4 mg/L and P‐Keratein 59 mg/L. The patient was mildly febrile (39°C). To track the progression of the swelling, a demarcation was sketched around the impacted area with a delible marker Figure 2.

Photograph illustrating the erythematous appearance on the external cheek and extraoral incision.

A fiberoptic scope was used to examine the patient's airway, which appeared unobstructed during that assessment. During the intraoperative procedure, rapid on‐site cultural analysis revealed the presence of Streptococcus anginosus, Prevotella species, and Bacteroides species in the cultures. These bacteria are known to be part of the normal human flora but can cause opportunistic infections. 13 Infections caused by these microbiotas are polymicrobial in nature.

Intravenous antibiotics (benzylpenicillin 3 g and metronidazole 500 mg, administered three times daily each for 20 days) were prescribed, and the patient was meticulously monitored. Ludwig's angina was ruled out as a diagnosis since there was no involvement of the floor of the mouth. Erysipelas and cellulitis were also excluded due to the rapid and severe progression of clinical symptoms and the presence of gas formation, as observed in the radiological examination. Moreover, a contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the head and neck region revealed the following: swelling extending to the subcutaneous region, thickening of the subcutaneous fat, enhancement and thickening of the masseteric fascia, accompanied by the formation of air pockets in the soft tissues Figure 3.

CT scan of the patient's head and neck. (A). An axial view reveals subcutaneous air pockets, resembling an abscess, extending beyond the dorsal part of the mandible, and causing deviation of the trachea. (B). A transverse view shows the abscess surrounding the outer and inner borders of the mandible base. In addition, it demonstrates the swelling of the subcutaneous tissues around the masseter muscle, indicating ongoing fasciitis.

An hour following the initial assessment, a minor increase in the size of the swelling was observed, indicating ongoing infection. The diagnosis of fasciitis was confirmed based on several factors: rapid progression, a crackling sensation (crepitus) when palpating the affected area, the necrotic appearance of the underlying soft tissues, and radiological findings. The decision was made to perform surgery under general anesthesia. An extraoral incision was made in the preauricular area, extending to the retromandibular and then to the submandibular region through the swollen area. The procedure involved the removal of infected tissue and the placement of a drainage system Figure 4. Following the surgery, the patient was intubated and admitted to the intensive care unit.

The photograph illustrates the surgical intervention, which entails making an extraoral incision to remove the infected tissue and obtain a swab for microbial analysis. Additionally, a drainage system is put in place to aid in the elimination of accumulated infective fluid and encourage the healing process.

CONCLUSION AND RESULTS (OUTCOME AND FOLLOW‐UP)

There was evidence of air pocket formation in the soft tissues, violated fascial planes, and extensive necrotic tissue consistent with NF. These affected tissues were excised as previously described. A second look was performed 12 h later, revealing no further progression of the infection. The patient was then extubated and transferred back to the general ward for continued care. The patient's total hospital stays lasted 15 days, with four of those days spent in intensive care.

DISCUSSION

CNF is extraordinarily uncommon (occurring in only 5.3% of cases) due to the presence of an extensive vascular network in the neck region, which enables a rapid immune response to infectious challenges. 14 In addition, it is even more unusual to be observed in healthy individuals. 15 , 16 CNF most frequently arises from odontogenic infections but can also be linked to dental procedures, significant penetrating injuries, or any disruption to the oropharyngeal mucosal barrier. 3 , 17 , 18 Diagnosing CNF can be challenging, specifically in healthy individuals, due to its rarity and potential confusion with other infections. 19 Additionally, the symptoms of CNF may initially be nonspecific, varying depending on the location and stage of the disease. Furthermore, if the infection is not detected and treated, it may lead to sepsis, with mortality rates reaching up to 34%. 20

CNF can be further divided into two subtypes based on the microbial culture. Type‐I represents polymicrobial involvement of both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. 21 , 22 Existing research recognizes polymicrobial involvement in medically immunocompromised patients. On the contrary, type‐II refers to a monomicrobial source, most often involving aerobic bacteria such as Group A Streptococcus, other beta‐hemolytic streptococci, and Staphylococcus aureus. 16 Interestingly, our microbial culture presented a multibacterial origin consisting of both aerobic and anaerobic species. These findings are quite uncommon, and to the best of our knowledge, there are no existing case reports in the literature addressing this clinical event. However, many case reports do not mention any relevant information regarding the bacterial background of NF. Although the polymicrobial bacteria found in present case are the same who are involved in severe odontogenic infections. In CNF cases, specifically in individuals with a normal immune system, molecular diagnostics are highly recommended. 22

Diagnostic imaging such as CT scanning can be valuable in identifying CNF, particularly when subcutaneous gas is present. 19 However, the most reliable method for diagnosis of CNF is an intravenous contrast CT scan, which can differentiate between abscesses, erysipelas, and cellulitis. CT scan findings in CNF are highly suggestive of abnormal gas collections within the cervical tissues (approximately 54.5%–64% of cases). 19 , 23 Furthermore, other significant CT features that are consistent with CNF include subcutaneous fat thickening, diffuse enhancement of the cervical fascia, musculature enhancement and thickening, as well as fluid collection within the neck compartments. 19 These specific CT scan findings collectively contribute to the diagnosis of CNF and aid in distinguishing it from other conditions.

It is crucial to note that radiological tests should never delay surgery if there are visible signs of crepitus during examination or indications of an advancing soft tissue infection. 24 In such cases, prompt surgical intervention is necessary to prevent further damage and mitigate the risk of complications. In some cases, CNF can initially be presented as a typical abscess. As a result, it is crucial for clinicians to maintain a high level of suspicion when encountering a patient with a rapidly spreading, red, swollen area accompanied by fever.

Despite the implementation of rigorous sterilization procedures in hospitals, there remains a possibility for surgical sites to become infected. In some cases, these infections may lead to CNF if they spread to the fascia, which is a thin layer of connective tissue that envelops our muscles and internal organs. Therefore, it is crucial for both general and specialist practices to consistently implement comprehensive infection control measures in every case they handle. These measures help prevent the spread of infections, protect the well‐being of patients and healthcare providers, and maintain a safe and hygienic environment within healthcare facilities. Given the potential severity of such infections, our findings emphasize the importance of adopting a more patient‐centered approach in documenting and managing prior cases of serious infections. Additionally, our findings underscore the necessity for detailed case reports that include comprehensive medical histories and thorough microbial analyses. This information can contribute to a better understanding of CNF and help improve the prevention and treatment of this potentially life‐threatening condition.

To sum up, CNF is a severe complication that can result from odontogenic infections. Our case highlights the fact that polymicrobial CNF may develop even in young and generally healthy individuals, despite adherence to appropriate infection control measures. Swift and early diagnosis is crucial, as the infection can rapidly progress and pose a life‐threatening risk. Furthermore, the clinician's awareness should be raised when acute orofacial/cervical pain is presented, followed by progressive swelling. For cases of CNF in healthy individuals, future research should emphasize the importance of obtaining a thorough medical history, maintaining meticulous documentation, and utilizing molecular diagnostic techniques to better understand and manage this potentially fatal condition.
