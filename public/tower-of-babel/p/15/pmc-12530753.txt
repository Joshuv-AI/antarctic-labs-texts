Metastatic follicular thyroid carcinoma to the brain during pregnancy: A neurosurgical and ethical challenge
Authors: Kevin S. Toache, Guadalupe Carolina Olvera-Sánchez, Ana S. Lugo-Lévaro, Victor Ramzes Chavez-Herrera, Isauro Lozano-Guzmán, Marco A. Rodriguez-Florido, Rafael Rafael Roman-Cuellar, Bayron A. Sandoval-Bonilla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Intracranial metastases originating from differentiated thyroid carcinoma are rare, with follicular subtypes representing a minority of cases. Their manifestation during pregnancy is exceedingly rare and presents considerable neurosurgical, obstetric, and ethical challenges. This report describes a case of symptomatic brain metastasis from follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) in a pregnant patient who was successfully managed with surgical resection during her second trimester.Case Description: A 28-year-old woman presented with headache, vomiting, and visual disturbances at 19 weeks’ gestation. Her medical history included papillary thyroid carcinoma, which was previously treated with total thyroidectomy, I-131 ablation (cumulative dose, 800 mCi), and external neck radiotherapy. Surveillance imaging performed during early pregnancy revealed pulmonary and submandibular lymph node metastases. The patient declined further oncological treatment due to her pregnancy. On admission, brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed a heterogeneous enhancing lesion in the right parieto-occipital region, accompanied by surrounding edema and midline shift, and two extracranial scalp lesions. Following a multidisciplinary evaluation, neurosurgical resection was performed under general anesthesia with continuous fetal monitoring. Gross total resection of both intracranial and extracranial tumors was achieved. Histopathological examination confirmed metastatic FTC. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient successfully completed her pregnancy. The patient succumbed to systemic oncologic progression in 2021.Conclusion: This case underscores the feasibility and safety of neurosurgical intervention for brain metastases during the second trimester of pregnancy, the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, and the urgent need for evidence-based guidelines to manage intracranial malignancies in pregnant patients.

INTRODUCTION

Intracranial neoplasms during pregnancy are rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 15/100,000 pregnancies, most of which are benign tumors.[7] The coexistence of pregnancy and intracranial metastases presents unique clinical, surgical, and ethical challenges, particularly when urgent neurosurgical interventions are indicated. Although brain metastases from breast cancer are among the most frequently reported during pregnancy, metastases originating from thyroid carcinomas are rare.[17]

Thyroid cancer is the most common endocrine malignancy and the ninth leading cause of cancer in the United States.[4] Differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), including papillary and follicular subtypes, has a favorable prognosis with appropriate treatment. However, distant metastases, particularly to the brain, are rare and associated with poor outcomes.[2,5,9] Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) accounts for 10–15% of DTCs[1] and may demonstrate hematogenous spread, occasionally reaching the central nervous system (CNS).[8] Genetic mutations, such as RAS or PAX8/PPARg rearrangements, have been implicated in the metastatic potential of FTC, with significant geographic variation in mutation prevalence.[12]

Brain metastases from thyroid carcinoma typically localize in the supratentorial compartment and are often associated with neurological symptoms, such as headache, seizures, or visual disturbances.[5,8] The median survival after the diagnosis of brain metastases from DTC has been reported to be 19 months, although prognostic outcomes vary widely based on performance status, number of lesions, and treatment modality.[2]

Pregnancy induces a series of physiological changes, including increased blood volume, hormonal fluctuations, and altered immune responses, which may contribute to tumor growth and vascularization.[13,14] Surgical management during pregnancy, particularly for cranial lesions, is highly individualized and must consider gestational age, maternal-fetal prognosis, and the nature of the lesion.[15] Neurosurgical intervention during the second trimester is generally considered safe and offers a favorable risk-benefit profile.[13,15,18]

We report the case of a 28-year-old pregnant woman with metastatic FTC in the brain who presented in the second trimester and successfully underwent neurosurgical resection. This case underscores the importance of multidisciplinary planning in balancing maternal and fetal risks and highlights the need for more evidence-based guidelines for the management of neuro-oncologic emergencies during pregnancy.

CASE REPORT

A 28-year-old woman, 19 weeks pregnant, had a medical history of FTC with cervical lymph node involvement. In response to this diagnosis, she underwent total thyroidectomy and radical neck dissection in 2013. Post-surgery, she received adjuvant radioactive iodine (I-131) therapy on three occasions (2013, 2014, and 2016), accumulating a total dose of 800mCi. In 2016, she underwent external beam radiotherapy to the neck, receiving 30 Gy in 10 fractions. As a consequence of the surgical intervention, the patient was placed on lifelong hormone replacement therapy to manage hypothyroidism and hypoparathyroidism.

In January 2017, the patient observed progressive enlargement of the left parietal scalp. By July, there was evidence of tumor recurrence in the submandibular lymph nodes and of pulmonary metastases. During staging workup, an unplanned pregnancy was identified in the first trimester. The patient opted to forgo further oncological treatment to continue her pregnancy. One month later, she presented to our neurosurgery department with new-onset headache, vomiting, and blurred vision. On examination, the patient was alert and oriented, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. Brain magnetic resonance imaging [Figure 1] revealed a heterogeneously enhancing lesion in the right parieto-occipital lobe, accompanied by significant perilesional edema and a midline shift. Extracranial lesions were identified in the parietal and midline regions of the scalp. The lesions were soft, depressible, and nontender on palpation. Following a multidisciplinary evaluation involving neurosurgery, anesthesiology, and maternal-fetal medicine, surgical resection was recommended. Under general anesthesia and continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring, craniotomy was performed with gross total resection of both the intracranial mass and extracranial scalp lesions. Intraoperative findings revealed a highly vascular, malodorous tumor with caseous necrotic areas consistent with metastatic thyroid carcinoma. The intracranial lesion was extra-axial, extending from the occipital to parietal region, with adhesions involving the transverse sinus and tentorium. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of metastatic FTC [Figure 2].

Radiographic and neuroimaging findings. (a and b) Chest radiographs (anteroposterior and lateral views) demonstrated multiple bilateral pulmonary opacities consistent with metastatic disease. (c and d) Preoperative brain magnetic resonance imaging: axial T2-weighted sequences revealed two parieto-occipital extracranial metastatic lesions (arrows) and a large right parieto-occipital intracranial mass associated with extensive vasogenic edema and extracranial extension (arrow), respectively. (e) Postoperative axial non-contrast computed tomography scan demonstrated gross total resection of the right parieto-occipital mass with resolution of the midline shift.

Histopathological and Immunohistochemical Findings. (a and b) Hematoxylin and eosin staining at (a) 40× and (b) 100× magnification demonstrated neoplastic epithelial cells arranged in follicular and trabecular patterns (black arrows). The tumor cells exhibited round to oval nuclei with finely dispersed chromatin and moderate nuclear atypia embedded within a richly vascularized stroma containing focal areas of hemorrhage. These histopathological features were consistent with metastatic follicular thyroid carcinoma. (c and d) Immunohistochemical analysis revealed strong cytoplasmic immunoreactivity for (c) thyroglobulin (red arrow) and intense nuclear positivity for (d) thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) (purple arrow), confirming the thyroid origin of the metastatic brain lesion (2c: 100x, Thyroglobulin, 2d: 100x, Thyroid transcription factor -1).

The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged 3 days later under outpatient surveillance. The patient continued her pregnancy without further complications and successfully delivered at full term. Unfortunately, despite surgical intervention, the patient succumbed to extensive oncologic disease in 2021.

DISCUSSION

FTC accounts for approximately 10–15% of DTC and is primarily characterized by hematogenous spread, most commonly to the bone and lungs. Brain metastases, however, remain exceedingly rare, with an estimated incidence of only 0.9–1.5% among patients with DTC, and an even lower frequency reported in the follicular subtype.[5,9,10] Large series, such as the one published by McWilliams et al., have documented only a limited number of cases over several decades, with 16 instances reported, thereby emphasizing the clinical rarity of this phenomenon and the importance of individual case reports in expanding the current body of evidence.[10]

The occurrence of brain metastases from FTC during pregnancy is particularly rare. To date, only a handful of cases have been documented in the literature, including a report by Lopes Rufino et al., which described a 32-year-old pregnant patient with a supratentorial lesion secondary to FTC.[16] These rare cases suggest that metastatic involvement of the CNS may occur during advanced stages of pregnancy, posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges to medical teams. This underscores the clinical relevance and originality of our study.

Pregnancy introduces complex physiological changes, such as increased vascularity, hormonal fluctuations, and immunological modulation, which may accelerate tumor progression.[13,14] These changes are particularly significant in vascularized tumors, such as thyroid carcinoma, and justify early surgical intervention when symptomatic lesions are identified. Although primary brain tumors are uncommon during pregnancy, metastatic lesions often pose a greater challenge due to their rapid progression and the associated systemic disease burden.[7,15]

The decision to proceed with neurosurgical intervention in a pregnant patient requires careful deliberation by a multidisciplinary team. A clinical meeting was held involving the departments of neurosurgery, anesthesiology, maternal-fetal medicine, and the Institutional Ethics Committee to evaluate the risks and benefits for both the mother and fetus. The team considered various clinical scenarios, including the potential for neurological deterioration without treatment, anesthetic risks associated with surgery during the second trimester, and ethical implications of prioritizing maternal health. Current recommendations suggest that surgical procedures may be safely performed during the second trimester when the risks of spontaneous abortion and preterm labor are minimized.[15,18] Our patient, at 19 weeks gestation, met these criteria. Neurosurgery provided a risk-benefit assessment for timely tumor resection, anesthesiology ensured intraoperative maternal-fetal safety under general anesthesia, maternal-fetal medicine coordinated fetal monitoring and gestational risk evaluation, and the ethics committee contributed to balancing maternal autonomy and fetal considerations. A consensus was reached that surgical intervention offered the best opportunity to preserve maternal neurological function without compromising fetal viability, particularly when it was performed under continuous fetal monitoring. This collaborative process underscores the importance of institutional frameworks that enable ethically grounded multidisciplinary decision-making in complex clinical settings.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses are essential for confirming the thyroid origin of brain metastases. In our case, immunostaining for thyroglobulin and thyroid transcription factor-1 confirmed the diagnosis, which is consistent with findings of previous studies[1,5] Although RAS mutations and PAX8/PPARg rearrangements are frequently implicated in FTC and may influence its metastatic behavior, molecular profiling was not performed in this case, representing a key limitation.[11] Including such analyses could provide further insights into tumor aggressiveness and potential therapeutic targets.

Despite a successful surgical outcome and full-term pregnancy, the patient’s long-term prognosis remained poor due to systemic disease progression, an outcome that reflects broader survival trends. Previous studies have reported a median overall survival of <2 years following the diagnosis of brain metastasis from thyroid carcinoma, even with multimodal treatment.[2,5]

At present, there is a lack of standardized guidelines for the management of intracranial metastases during pregnancy. Although several case series and reviews provide anecdotal support for surgical intervention during gestation,[13,15]

Robust clinical protocols are lacking. Although the patient ultimately succumbed to systemic disease, the successful completion of pregnancy and delivery of a viable newborn highlights the potential for favorable fetal outcomes when timely neurosurgical intervention is undertaken during the second trimester. This case reinforces the importance of individualized treatment plans that prioritize maternal neurological function and fetal well-being. For similar high-risk cases, close interdisciplinary follow-up is essential, including ongoing neuro-oncologic evaluation, obstetric surveillance, and consideration of early neonatal support. Establishing standardized postoperative monitoring protocols for pregnant patients undergoing neurosurgical procedures may help improve maternal-fetal outcomes and guide clinical decision-making in future cases.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights a rare but life-threatening scenario of brain metastasis from FTC during pregnancy. This underscores the feasibility and safety of neurosurgical intervention during the second trimester when guided by a multidisciplinary approach. Timely surgical management, coupled with continuous fetal monitoring and ethical deliberation, can preserve both maternal neurological function and fetal viability.

The absence of standardized guidelines for managing intracranial neoplasms during pregnancy reinforces the urgent need for evidence-based protocols to optimize outcomes in these complex and ethically sensitive clinical scenarios.
