Exposure to bisphenols and phthalates and arterial stiffness in women of reproductive age: a New York City cohort analysis
Authors: Rui Ling, Eunsil Seok, Mengling Liu, Shilpi Mehta-Lee, Yu Chen, Anais Hausvater, Elaine Urbina, Leonardo Trasande, Linda G. Kahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:This study aimed to examine associations of bisphenol and phthalate exposure with arterial stiffness among women of reproductive age.Methods:Participants include 637 adult women enrolled in the New York University Children’s Health and Environment Study (NYU CHES), a prospective pregnancy cohort. The concentrations of eight bisphenols and 22 phthalate metabolites were quantified in up to three spot urine samples. Primary outcomes included brachial artery distensibility (BrachD) and carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) as measures of peripheral and central arterial stiffness, respectively, along with blood pressure assessed at repeated study visits up to seven years from exposure measures. Associations between averaged, log2-transformed, creatinine-standardized chemical concentrations and subclinical vascular outcomes of interest were examined using linear mixed-effects models.Results:In covariate-adjusted models, a doubling of bisphenol A was positively associated with BrachD (0.09%/ mmHg, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.02, 0.16), a doubling of bisphenol S was negatively associated with mean arterial pressure (MAP) (−0.52 mmHg, 95% CI = −0.98, −0.05), a doubling of di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) metabolites was negatively associated with diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (−0.81 mmHg, 95% CI = −1.38, −0.23) and MAP (−0.83 mmHg, 95% CI = −1.45, −0.21), and a doubling of antiandrogenic phthalate metabolites was associated with 0.62 mmHg lower DBP (95% CI = −1.22, −0.03) and 0.66 mmHg lower MAP (95% CI = −1.30, −0.02).Conclusion:These findings suggest that routine exposure to bisphenols and DEHP may be associated with vasorelaxation, potentially related to these chemicals’ estrogenic and/or antiandrogenic effects.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death among women both worldwide and in the United States (U.S.) (National Center for Health Statistics., n.d.; Tsao et al., 2023). In 2023, 44% of women in the U.S. were diagnosed with some form of heart disease and CVD contributed to 304,970 women’s deaths (National Center for Health Statistics., n.d.; Tsao et al., 2023). High blood pressure has been recognized as a key risk factor for CVD (Fuchs and Whelton, 2020). Arterial stiffness, another important but relatively underexplored marker of vascular function, closely correlates with blood pressure and atherosclerosis (Kim, 2023). The combination of increased arterial stiffness and elevated blood pressure significantly raises the risk of CVD (Kim, 2023).

Phthalates and bisphenols are ubiquitous chemicals that are detectable in most of the U.S. population due to consistent exposure despite their short half-lives (Kahn et al., 2020). Synthetic chemicals, phthalates can be classified into high molecular weight (HMW) and low molecular weight (LMW) groupings (Wang and Qian, 2021). LMW phthalates are usually used as scent stabilizers in perfumes, pharmaceuticals, and personal and household care products (Wang and Qian, 2021). HMW phthalates, including di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), diisononyl phthalate (DiNP), and di-n-octyl phthalate (DnOP), are plasticizers commonly used in medical tubing, PVC flooring, food packaging, and building materials (National Research Council, 2008). Because of the adverse effects of DEHP on human health, it has been restricted around the globe in food contact packaging and children’s products, and replaced by other phthalates such as DiNP and DnOP (Wang and Qian, 2021). Bisphenol A (BPA) is a high-production-volume chemical used to manufacture polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins, and it is commonly found in food containers as well as a variety of other consumer products (Camara et al., 2019; Cowell et al., 2023; Lehmler et al., 2018). With increasing concerns about its adverse health effects, BPA has been progressively replaced by bisphenol S (BPS) and bisphenol F (BPF), which share similar structures and properties (Lehmler et al., 2018).

Phthalates and bisphenols are endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDC) that mimic or block natural hormonal activities and interfere with homeostasis, including of the cardiovascular system, leading to adverse health outcomes (Ganakumar et al., 2024). The effects of BPA on the cardiovascular system are complex and the mechanisms have not been fully elucidated (Wehbe et al., 2020). In in vitro experiments, BPA approximates the effect of 17-β-estradiol (E2) and evokes vasodilation through binding to estradiol receptors or other pathways (Dias et al., 2022; Feiteiro et al., 2018; Tvrdý et al., 2023). In contrast, animal and human observational studies have found higher BPA to be associated with hypertension (Dias et al., 2022; Zamora et al., 2021), with similar results for BPS (Moreno-Gómez-Toledano, 2022). A U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) study reported that BPA is positively linked with cardiovascular mortality, with a stronger association among women (Chen et al., 2023). Prior research has yielded conflicting results regarding relations between phthalates and CVD, especially di(2-ethylhexyl)phthalate (DEHP) and blood pressure. A study among Chinese adults reported a positive association between DEHP and systolic blood pressure (SBP, Zhang et al., 2018), while a meta-analysis of 10 studies found negative associations of mono-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (MEHP) with SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DPB) among pregnant individuals (Zhang et al., 2023). A study of 73 nonpregnant Mexican women with mean age of 46.6 (standard deviation [SD] = 6.3) years reported that two HMW phthalate metabolites, mono-(carboxyisooctyl) phthalate (mCOP) and mono-(2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl) phthalate (mEHHP), were associated with hypertension and higher DBP, respectively (Zamora et al., 2021).

Despite growing interest in potential effects of phthalates and bisphenols on adult blood pressure, research in this area remains limited and inconsistent (Mariana et al., 2023). Additionally, there are two notable gaps in the literature. First, while researchers have highlighted the significance of these chemicals in women’s vascular function, most of the studies conducted among women of reproductive age have focused on cardiovascular outcomes during pregnancy, especially hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (Belcher et al., 2012; James-Todd et al., 2016; Melzer et al., 2012; Wehbe et al., 2020; Zamora et al., 2021), and have not considered other time points during the female reproductive life course. Second, most research focused on non-gestational outcomes in younger women have concentrated mostly on hypertension as a binary outcome, neglecting subclinical risk factors such as arterial stiffness. Indeed, only a few studies have explored the relation between exposure to these chemicals and arterial stiffness at all. Among them, Wang and colleagues identified negative associations between urinary BPA concentrations and both hypertension and arterial stiffness in Chinese adults aged 40 and older, with stronger associations among women than men (Wang et al., 2015). Another study of 127 Chinese adults with a mean age of 47.3 (SD = 15.8) years found that monobutyl phthalate (MBP) was positively associated with arterial stiffness, with stronger associations observed in males (Bai et al., 2022).

The New York University Children’s Health and Environment Study (NYU CHES) offers a unique opportunity to address these research gaps. NYU CHES has collected multiple spot urine samples from pregnant participants, alongside a comprehensive array of demographic, behavioral, and health information that includes longitudinal measurements of blood pressure and arterial stiffness during follow-up visits over a period of up to seven years postpartum. Leveraging this rich dataset, we aim to investigate associations of bisphenols and phthalates with blood pressure and central and peripheral arterial stiffness, as measured by carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) and brachial artery distensibility (BrachD), respectively, among women of reproductive age.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

NYU CHES is an ongoing prospective birth cohort in New York City that has been enrolling participants since March 2016 (Trasande et al., 2020). Eligible individuals must be 18 years old or older, be less than 18 weeks pregnant, and plan to deliver at an NYU-affiliated hospital (Trasande et al., 2020). Participants and their offspring are followed up at regular intervals postpartum. Participant characteristics and pregnancy experiences are collected through questionnaires administered throughout pregnancy, and health data are extracted from electronic medical records (EMR) (Trasande et al., 2020). All NYU CHES participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the New York University Grossman School of Medicine.

Bisphenol and phthalate measurements

Single random spot urine samples were collected at routine prenatal care visits at up to three timepoints during pregnancy ( < 18, 18–25, and ≥25 gestational weeks) and stored in phthalate- and bisphenol-free tubes at −80 °C. Of the 637 participants included in this analysis, 340 (53.38%) had urine samples at all three timepoints, 201 (31.55%) at two, and 96 (15.07%) at one.

Twenty-two phthalate metabolites were quantified using enzymatic deconjugation of glucuronidated phthalate monoesters, followed by solid-phase extraction and high-performance liquid chromatography-–electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–ESI–MS/MS), as described previously (Gaylord et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022). Phthalate metabolites with concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD) in more than 50% of samples were excluded from further analysis (Table S1). Eleven phthalate metabolites remained and were categorized into four groupings based on their characteristics or parent compounds: LMW phthalates, HMW phthalates, DEHP metabolites, and phthalates with anti-androgenic properties (AntiAnd). Molar sums of individual metabolite concentrations were calculated for each grouping. Only one DiNP metabolite (mono-(carboxyisooctyl) phthalate [mCiOP]) and one DnOP metabolite (mono-(3-carboxypropyl) phthalate [mCPP]) had less than 50% missingness, so the concentrations of these individual metabolites were analyzed as proxies for their parent compounds. Phthalic acid (PA) was used as an indicator of overall phthalate exposure, as it is a common downstream metabolite of other phthalate metabolites.

Eight bisphenols were measured using liquid-liquid extraction followed by HPLC-MS/MS, as previously described (Gaylord et al., 2022). BPA and BPS remained in the analysis after excluding those with more than 50% below the LOD. Urinary creatinine was also analyzed in all samples using HPLC-MS/MS. For statistical analysis, values below the LOD were replaced with LOD/√2. To account for urinary dilution, raw urinary concentrations were creatinine-adjusted via the Boeniger et al. method according to the formula: Adjusted chemical concentration=Observed chemical concentration*(median batch-specific creatinine concentration/observed creatinine concentration)(Boeniger et al.,1993;O’Brien et al.,2016).

Due to the skewness of the distributions of chemical concentrations, all individual and summed phthalate and bisphenol concentrations were log2-transformed. Across all phthalate metabolites and groupings, the median intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) calculated over the three sampling time points was 0.41 (range = 0.23, 0.57). Median ICCs for both BPA and BPS were 0.3. Due to the variability and short half-lives of the exposures, all chemical concentrations were averaged across the three collection timepoints to provide a better estimate of overall baseline exposure.

Blood pressure and arterial stiffness measurements

Follow-up study visits occurred 11–23, 24–35, 36–59, and 60–83 months after delivery. Arterial stiffness was measured by cfPWV and BrachD. cfPWV assesses the speed at which blood pressure pulses travel through the arteries and is calculated by measuring the transit distance and time between the carotid and femoral arteries, with faster wave speeds indicating stiffer arteries (Coutinho, 2014). cfPWV was collected in triplicate during each visit using a SphygmoCor SCOR-PVx System (Atcor Medical, Sydney, Australia).

BrachD reflects the elasticity of the brachial artery, with higher BrachD indicating less stiffness. BrachD is a less common measure than cfPWV, but a useful indicator of peripheral vessel stiffness, which is also a predictor of CVD in adults (Budoff et al., 2003; Urbina et al., 2002). BrachD was measured in triplicate during each visit with a noninvasive DynaPulse Pathway monitor (Pulse Metric, Inc., San Diego, California, USA). Along with BrachD, SBP, DBP, mean arterial blood pressure (MAP), and heart rate (HR) were extracted from DynaPulse.

All outcome assessments were conducted in a clinical setting according to the instrument manufacturers’ protocols. Staff members were trained by an expert in preventive cardiology (EU). Data exported from the instruments were reviewed and adjudicated by EU’s team. Outliers, defined as BrachD = 0 %/mmHg (number of measurement, m = 84), SBP > 270 or < 70 mmHg (m = 2), DBP > 150 or <40 mmHg (m = 1), and cfPWV <3.5 or > 12 m/s (m = 16) based on prior literature and experts’ opinions (Carrillo-Larco et al., 2023), were assigned as missing, as was MAP for readings missing either SBP or DBP (m = 2). The final measures were then calculated by averaging the valid measurements at each visit, rendering a total of N = 637 participants with at least one average BrachD or PWV measures (total visits, n = 1039). If participants reported a subsequent pregnancy in the interval between study visits, the visits (n = 62) taken after the estimated start date of the subsequent pregnancy were excluded, as blood pressures have altered trajectories during pregnancy (N = 637).

Covariates

Covariates were determined using a directed acyclic graph (DAG, Fig. S1) and univariable analyses. Age at enrollment, race/ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian, and other), marital status (married/living with a partner, divorced/separated or single/widowed), education (high school or less, some college but no degree, associate degree, bachelor degree, postgraduate degree), insurance (public, private), employment status (yes, no), and baseline body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) were identified as potential confounders. Parity and BMI at follow-up visit were identified as key precision variables. Because BMI at follow-up visit was highly correlated with baseline BMI, we used the difference between baseline BMI and BMI at follow-up visit (BMI difference) in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted for chemical exposures, participants’ baseline characteristics, and outcomes. Median concentrations and interquartile ranges (IQR) were presented for each bisphenol and phthalate metabolite (ng/mL, urine) and each chemical grouping (nmol/mL, urine). Means and standard deviations (SDs) were presented for age and BMI. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

In univariable analyses, we regressed the five primary outcomes on all identified potential confounding factors and only those that were significantly associated with at least one of the outcomes at the level of α = 0.05 were recognized as relevant confounders in our dataset. Marital status was the only variable removed from the potential adjustment set after the assessment.

For primary analyses, we investigated the averaged log2-transformed concentrations of BPA, BPS, the four chemical groupings (∑LMW, ∑HMW, ∑DEHP, and ∑AntiAnd), individual phthalate metabolites mCPP mCiOP (proxies for DnOP and DiNP, respectively), and PA as exposures. Linear mixed-effect models (LMM) were used to assess associations of each exposure with arterial stiffness and blood pressure measurements across all four visit timepoints. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate model fit, and LMMs with random intercepts and linear terms for exposure were selected over models with quadratic or spline terms.

For each outcome, we fitted both unadjusted and covariate-adjusted models with each individual chemical or grouping of metabolites. The final adjustment set included: age, pre-pregnancy BMI, race/ethnicity, education level, insurance, employment status, BMI change, and parity. Models with cfPWV as the outcome were further adjusted for HR and MAP to account for their effect on wave reflections and distending pressure, in accordance with guidelines from the American Heart Association (Townsend et al., 2015). All baseline covariates included in the adjusted models had <2% missing data, and analyses were performed using complete cases.

As a secondary analysis, we fitted covariate-adjusted linear regression models to examine associations between exposures and outcome measurements during the first two follow-up periods, with additional adjustment for the number of postpartum months at each follow-up visit. Outcome measures collected at the remaining timepoints were not assessed due to the small sample size.

To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the effective number of tests approach (Li and Ji, 2005), which considers correlations among exposures to control for the family-wise error rate (FWER). Only averaged log2-transformed BPA, BPS, ∑LMW, ∑HMW, mCPP, mCiOP, and PA were included when calculating the effective number of tests using the Li & Ji’s method due to the high correlation among ∑HMW, ∑AntiAnd, and ∑DEHP (2005). The adjusted significance threshold was calculated as: 1−((1−0.05)(1/m)), where m represents the effective number of tests. The FWER-adjusted threshold of significance was defined as p-value <0.01.

Additionally, we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, measurements following the first postpartum hypertension diagnosis were excluded, assuming that antihypertensive treatment would begin at that time and affect blood pressure measures. Second, we further excluded participants diagnosed with hypertension disorders of pregnancy (including chronic hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia, and gestational hypertension), as these individuals are often prescribed antihypertensives postpartum. Third, in a subset of 408 participants with available baseline blood pressure data from the EMR, we further controlled for baseline MAP z-score to account for participants’ underlying vascular function and pre-existing differences in blood pressure. Baseline blood pressure was defined as any measurement within the year prior to conception or during the first ten weeks of gestation as a normal pregnancy is characterized by an early reduction in blood pressure, reaching the lowest around 20 weeks (Mustafa et al., 2012). . If multiple blood pressure measurements were available during the baseline period, the one closest to the date of conception was used. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 using RStudio.

Results

Analytic sample and characteristics

Among the 1620 participants enrolled in NYU CHES between 2016 and 2023 who provided at least one prenatal urine sample that was analyzed for chemical exposure, 1521 delivered live births and 1234 consented to participate in follow-up visits after childbirth. Among those, 697 had at least one relevant outcome measure. After excluding measurements taken following the estimated start date of a subsequent pregnancy, the final analysis included 637 participants (Fig. 1), 273 of whom (40.56%) had more than one follow-up visit. Specifically, 580 participants contributed a total of 2425 BrachD measurements, which were taken at 866 visits, and 504 participants contributed 1630 cfPWV measurements, which were taken at 720 visits.

Participants’ characteristics are presented in Table 1. Participants had a mean age of 32 years old at recruitment with mean baseline BMI of 26.4 kg/m2. Fifty-eight percent of participants self-identified as Hispanic, 28% as non-Hispanic White, 8.3% as non-Hispanic Asian, and 3.3% as non-Hispanic Black. Most were married or living with a partner (89.5%). More than a third (38.4%) received a high school degree or less and more than half were employed (60.1%). Fifty-nine percent had public insurance, and around 44% were first-time mothers (nulliparous). Regarding pregnancy complications, 17% had gestational diabetes and 11.1% were diagnosed with at least one type of HDP. Approximately 63% of participants breastfed their children for six months or more. Descriptive statistics of the included phthalate metabolites and bisphenols are provided in Table 2.

Descriptive statistics of the outcome measures according to follow-up visit are provided in Table 3. A comparative analysis was conducted between the analytic sample and NYU CHES participants who had urine samples collected and experienced live births but did not have outcome measures available (Table S2). We observed that participants in the analytic sample had higher median pre-pregnancy BMI, were more likely to have gestational diabetes, and were more likely to self-report drinking alcohol during pregnancy. A higher percentage of participants in the analytic sample were Hispanic, reported lower education level, were unemployed, had public insurance, were parous, and reported longer breastfeeding duration.

Arterial stiffness

After adjusting for covariates, a doubling of urinary BPA was associated with 0.09%/mmHg (95% CI: 0.02, 0.16; FWER-adjusted CI: −0.01, 0.18) higher BrachD (Fig. 2a, Table S3). A doubling of urinary BPS was also positively associated with BrachD (0.06 %/mmHg; 95% CI: −0.01, 0.12; FWER-adjusted CI: −0.04, 0.15). Although no statistically significant associations were observed between bisphenols or phthalates and cfPWV, the measures of effect were consistently positive, except for PA (Fig. 2a, Table S3).

In secondary analysis, results were consistent with those of the longitudinal models when looking at data from the first two follow-up visits separately (Fig. 3a, Table S4). We observed that the significant association of BPA with BrachD remained in both Visit 1 (0.08%/mmHg; 95% CI: 0.002, 0.16; FWER-adjusted CI: −0.02, 0.18) and Visit 2 models (0.14%/mmHg; 95% CI: 0.01, 0.27; FWER-adjusted CI: −0.03, 0.31). We additionally detected a positive association between a doubling of BPS and Visit 2 BrachD (0.15%/mmHg; 95% CI: 0.03, 0.28; FWER-adjusted CI: −0.01, 0.31) despite the wider confidence intervals due to small sample size. For BrachD and cfPWV, effect estimates were greater in magnitude for Visit 2 than Visit 1, although they had wider confidence intervals.

Blood pressure

As shown in Fig. 2b (Table S3), a doubling of urinary BPS concentration was associated with lower MAP in the covariate-adjusted model (−0.52 mmHg; 95% CI: −0.98, −0.05; FWER-adjusted CI: −1.13, 0.10). We observed similar trends for associations of BPA and BPS with SBP and DBP. For phthalates, a doubling of ∑DEHP was negatively associated with DBP (−0.81 mmHg; 95% CI: −1.38, −0.23; FWER-adjusted CI: −1.56, −0.05) and MAP (−0.83 mmHg; 95% CI = −1.45, −0.21; FWER-adjusted CI: −1.65, −0.02). A doubling of ∑AntiAnd was associated with 0.62 mmHg lower DBP (95% CI: −1.22, −0.03; FWER-adjusted CI: −1.41, 0.16) and 0.66 mmHg lower MAP (95% CI: −1.30, −0.02; FWER-adjusted CI: −1.50, 0.18).

In secondary analysis (Fig. 3b, Table S4), we observed negative associations between BPS and MAP (0.55 mmHg; 95% CI: −1.09, −0.02; FWER-adjusted CI: −1.25, 0.15), and between ∑DEPH and MAP (0.77 mmHg; 95% CI: −1.49, −0.06; FWER-adjusted CI: −1.71, 0.16) in follow-up Visit 1.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses largely supported our primary findings (Table S5 – S7, Fig. S2). Result when we excluded outcome data following hypertension diagnosis were not meaningfully different from the primary analysis. When we further excluded participants with HDP diagnoses, the association between BPA and BrachD remained. A doubling of mCPP concentration was associated with higher cfPWV (0.06 m/s, 95% CI = 0.01, 0.11). For blood pressure outcomes, the associations of ∑DEHP with DBP and MAP, and of ∑AntiAnd with DBP and MAP were maintained. In the third sensitivity analysis with the subset of 408 participants who had baseline blood pressure data, associations between BPA and BrachD, ∑DEHP and DBP, and ∑DEHP and MAP persisted. We additionally observed a positive association between BPS and BrachD (0.09%/mmHg, 95% CI: 0.004, 0.18) and a negative association between BPA and SBP (−0.76 mmHg, 95% CI: 1.50, −0.02).

Discussion

In this study, we used linear mixed effect models to examine associations of exposure to bisphenols and phthalates with arterial stiffness and blood pressure over the subsequent seven years in reproductive-age women. After adjusting for confounders, we observed that BPA was positively associated with BrachD, BPS was negatively associated with MAP, and ∑DEHP and ∑AntiAnd were negatively associated with both DBP and MAP. Associations were generally more pronounced as follow-up time increased. These results were robust to several sensitivity analyses.

Bisphenols

In contrast with prior studies that have shown BPA and its analogs such as BPS to be positively associated with blood pressure and hypertension (Bae et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2021; Melzer et al., 2012; Zamora et al., 2021), our results reveal negative associations of BPA with central arterial stiffness and of BPS with MAP, suggesting vasorelaxant response. Similar vasorelaxant effects of bisphenols have been found in ex vivo studies (Dias et al., 2022; Fonseca et al., 2023; Tvrdý et al., 2023). Tvrdý and colleagues (Tvrdý et al., 2023) tested 14 bisphenols, including BPA and BPS, in in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo studies and demonstrated that bisphenols can potentially relax vascular smooth muscles, although the concentrations they used were higher than routine human exposure. Fonseca and colleagues (Fonseca et al., 2023) examined how both acute and chronic BPA exposure affects human umbilical arteries in pregnant women. They found that chronic BPA exposure may alter the vasorelaxation response of human arteries, by inducing changes in the NO/sGC/cGMP/PKG pathway and the L-type calcium channel activity, a mechanism associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Specifically, a nonmonotonic “U-shaped” response was detected in the study. In the epidemiologic literature, one cross-sectional study in China with 3246 participants also found an inverse association between BPA and hypertension (Wang et al., 2015).

Several possible mechanisms could explain our results. Bisphenols are structurally similar to E2, the main type of endogenous estrogen for premenopausal women. E2 induces vasodilation and protects against CVD by interacting with endothelial nuclear estrogen receptors ERα and ERβ. This initiates a signaling pathway that upregulates the gene expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which stimulates NO production, resulting in vasodilation (Dias et al., 2022; Wehbe et al., 2020). E2 also interacts with a transmembrane G protein-coupled estrogen receptor, GPR30, to evoke vasodilatory activity acutely through a non-genomic regulation pathway (Dias et al., 2022; Wehbe et al., 2020).

Calcium signaling is a fundamental regulator of vascular function. Changes in calcium homeostasis can alter smooth muscle contraction, contributing to changes in vascular function (Ramadan et al., 2020). BPA has been shown to dysregulate ion channel signaling and change calcium channel current by inhibiting L-type calcium channel activity (Asano et al., 2010; Dias et al., 2022; Feiteiro et al., 2018; Fonseca et al., 2023; Ramadan et al., 2020; Rottgen et al., 2014). This mechanism has been exploited by hypertension medications such as Amlodipine, Nifedipine, and Felodipine.

Our finding of a positive association between bisphenols and BrachD in contrast to other studies’ findings of a negative effect of BPA on vascular outcomes may reflect a nonmonotonic or dose-dependent relationship between BPA and vascular outcomes, as demonstrated in the CLARITY-BPA Study (Vandenberg et al., 2019) and the Fonseca study (Fonseca et al., 2023). Our finding of an inverse relation between BPA and peripheral arterial stiffness may be due to the lower exposure level detected in our sample. Indeed, median BPA concentrations across each of the three collection timepoints in our analytic sample (0.62–0.64 ng/mL) were lower than the median in an NHANES study (1.24 ng/mL for control and 1.35 ng/mL for cases) that found a positive association between BPA and incident coronary artery disease (Melzer et al., 2012), indicating that our study may be identifying a pattern not visible in prior studies that were conducted among more highly exposed populations. The Chinese study with findings consistent with ours also had a lower median BPA concentration (0.81 ng/mL) in their sample (Wang et al., 2015).

Phthalates

For prenatal exposure to phthalates, we detected negative associations of ∑DEHP and ∑AntiAnd with both DBP and MAP. Mariana and colleagues have highlighted inconsistencies in the literature regarding associations between phthalate exposure and changes in maternal blood pressure (Mariana et al., 2023). Some studies have reported associations between DEHP exposure and higher blood pressure, hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerosis (Mariana et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2022; Xu and Sandler, 2025; Zhang et al., 2018). In the analysis most similar to ours, Wu et al. analyzed prenatal phthalate exposure in relation to maternal postpartum blood pressure trajectories in the Mexico City-based PROGRESS birth cohort and found increasing prenatal exposure to phthalate mixtures was associated with higher SBP and DBP in both short-term (6–18 months) and long-term (24–72 months) postpartum follow-up periods. In contrast, when looking at phthalate biomarkers, they found that ∑DEHP was associated with lower SBP in both follow-up periods, which is similar to our results (Wu et al., 2021).

The negative association we detected between phthalates and blood pressure could be due to phtalates’ estrogenic and anti-androgenic activity, which has been demonstrated in vivo and in vitro (Chen et al., 2014). Mankidy and colleagues investigated the mechanism of endocrine disruption by DEHP in developing fish embryos and found that instead of mimicking estrogen to activate the estrogen receptors like biosphenols, DEHP dysregulated steroid biosynthesis pathways, which then resulted in higher production of E2 and lower testosterone levels (Mankidy et al., 2013).

In addition to the steroid biosynthesis pathway, DEHP may induce vasodilation by inhibiting calcium channels, similarly to bisphenols, as described above. Evidence to support this mechanism is provided by Mariana et al. in a study examining the cardiovascular response of rat aorta to DEHP exposure (Mariana et al., 2018).

Results for ∑AntiAnd were similar to those for ∑DEHP, which is not surprising as the ∑AntiAnd grouping includes all of the metabolites of DEHP available in our dataset.

Comparison across outcomes

We observed that the directions of associations and trends for SBP, DBP, and MAP were consistent, and opposite to those for BrachD, which is expected, as higher BrachD indicates more elastic arteries and thus lower blood pressure. We expected cfPWV associations to track with SBP, DBP, and MAP; however, they were not consistent with the blood pressure outcomes. Indeed, no associations or trends were detected for cfPWV. There are several possible reasons for these observations. First, the sample size for cfPWV is smaller than all other outcomes, reducing our power to detect statistically significant associations. Second, a SphygmoCor system was used to measure cfPWV while a DynaPulse system was used to measure all other outcomes. Finally, the difference may have a biological basis in vascular physiology. Although both BrachD and cfPWV are measures of arterial stiffness, BrachD measures the response of the peripheral arteries while cfPWV measures the response of the central arteries (McEniery et al., 2008; Townsend et al., 2015). It is possible that the peripheral artery system is more susceptible to effects of endocrine disruption, hence our finding of associations for BrachD but not for cfPWV.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of our study is the use of a prospective cohort design, with which we can better establish temporality. Most prior research on this topic has been cross-sectional. Instead of using a single random urine sample, we were able to average chemical concentrations across repeated urine samples, which reduces the error associated with measuring nonpersistent chemicals (Agier et al., 2020). In addition to blood pressure measures that are more commonly used as endpoints in studies of environmental exposure and vasular function, we included cfPWV and BrachD, which are more sensitive measures of incipient arterial stiffness and currently understudied (Urbina et al., 2002). Several articles have emphasized the sex-specific aspect of EDCs’ effects on the cardiovascular system (Mariana et al., 2023; Sol et al., 2020; Valvi et al., 2015; Wehbe et al., 2020; Zamora et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020); ours is one of only a few large epidemiologic studies to investigate the associations of bisphenols and phthalates with cardiovascular outcomes among adult women of reproductive age (James-Todd et al., 2016; Melzer et al., 2012; Zamora et al., 2021).

As for limitations, we acknowledge that there is always the potential for measurement error in both exposure and outcome assessment. Specifically, while cfPWV has been considered the “gold standard” for assessing arterial stiffness (Spronck et al., 2024; Townsend et al., 2015; Wilkinson et al., 2010), the procedure is complex and long relative to a standard blood pressure measurement, potentially leading to more room for human error. However, we do not expect these errors to be differential with respect to exposure; if present, they would most likely bias our results toward the null. Causation cannot be established due to the observational nature of cohort studies and the lack of an unexposed control group, resulting from the ubiquity of bisphenol and phthalate epxosure. While we lacked data on CVD-related medication use, we performed sensitivity analyses to exclude those who may have been treated due to diagnosis of hypertension or HDP.

Implications

Comparing our results with prior literature suggests that cardiovascular effects of EDCs may be non-monotonic, emphasizing the importance of taking chemical exposure distributions into account when comparing results across studies. Also, endocrine disruption may not play out consistently across various types of vascular tissue (e.g., central vs. peripheral), highlighting the needs for multimodal assessments.

Although we observed slight vasodilation for BPA and DEHP in our study, these findings do not suggest that these chemicals are beneficial for the vascular system. Studies and reviews demonstrated that chronic exposure to BPA leads toinflammation andvascular remodeling (Fonseca et al., 2023; Ola-Davies and Olukole, 2018; Ramadan et al., 2020). It is essential to consider the multiple aspects of the cardio-renal system to achieve a comprehensive assessment of overall health.

Conclusions

In a cohort of reproductive-age women, BPA was positively associated with BrachD, BPS was negatively associated with MAP, and DEHP and anti-androgenic phthalates were negatively associated with DBP and MAP across up to seven years of follow-up. While these findings suggest possible vasorelaxation associated with bisphenol and DEHP exposure, potentially due to estrogenic effects at low concentrations, it is important to note that they do not indicate a protective effect of these chemicals. Rather, they emphasize dysregulation of the vascular system. Future research should explore a broader range of EDC concentrations and study the mechanisms underlying the variability across different parts of the vascular system.
