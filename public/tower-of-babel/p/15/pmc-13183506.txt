Identification of a genetic locus responsible for the dominant white-spotting coat phenotype in mice spontaneously isolated from a Japanese wild-derived inbred strain
Authors: Hiromitsu OCHIAI, Gou TAKAHASHI, Yuki ISHIDA, Yoshibumi MATSUSHIMA, Kenta WADA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Agouti-dominant spotting (Ads) is a novel mutant mouse that was spontaneously isolated from KOR1/Stm (KOR1), an inbred strain derived from a Japanese wild mouse. Heterozygous Ads mice exhibit dominant white-spotting on the dorsal coat, whereas homozygous mice display embryonic lethality. We generated [Ads×C57BL/6J (B6)]F1 (F1), F2 and [B6×(Ads×B6)]N2 (N2) mice to investigate the inheritance patterns of the Ads phenotype. The segregation ratio did not conform to the theoretical values expected for a simple autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with recessive embryonic lethality, as there was a reduced number of individuals exhibiting white-spotting in F2 (χ2=29.07, P<0.0001) and N2 (χ2=5.79, P=0.016). Genome-wide linkage analysis revealed that the Ads locus was located within the interval between D4Mit288 (56.8-Mb) and D4Mit69 (136.1-Mb) on chromosome 4. Further fine mapping using additional N2 and N3 progeny and markers narrowed the Ads locus to the genomic interval between Dhcr24 (106.4-Mb) and D4Mit199 (111.7-Mb). Among the genes encoded within this genomic interval, we predicted that the mago homolog, exon junction complex core component (Magoh), was the most promising candidate gene for Ads, as its null mutations have been reported to cause a white-spotting phenotype in Mos2 mice. However, no mutations were identified in the coding region of Magoh in Ads mice, suggesting that the causative mutation for the white-spotting phenotype may reside in a cis-regulatory region of Magoh or in another novel gene within the interval.

INTRODUCTION

Coat color is one of the most variable morphological traits observed in mammals and serves as a valuable target for investigating the genetic basis of phenotypic variation. To date, numerous coat color mutants have been identified in mice, with nearly 1,000 coat color-associated alleles reported across approximately 130 genes [15, 20]. Coat color variation is associated with the production, distribution, and deposition of melanin by melanocytes, which are derived from melanoblasts originating in the neural crest during embryonic development [1]. White-spotting is one of the most common and well-studied phenotypes in mice. In recent years, some gene mutations responsible for white-spotting in mice have been identified, thus contributing to our understanding of human piebaldism and Waardenburg syndrome (WS), which are characterized by pigmentation abnormalities and hearing loss [1, 13, 21].

In this study, we describe a new mutant mouse exhibiting white-spotting on the dorsal coat, designated as agouti-dominant spotting (Ads). The Ads mutant was spontaneously isolated from the Japanese wild-derived inbred strain Koriyama-1 (KOR1/Stm; KOR1) and exhibited homozygous lethality [11]. This study aimed to identify the genetic basis of the Ads mutation and assess its inheritance pattern and genetic background effects. Our results suggest that the white-spotting phenotype does not follow a simple autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, and that the responsible genetic locus is located within an approximately 5.3-Mb genomic interval on chromosome 4.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

All procedures were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments as defined by the Science Council of Japan. All experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tokyo University of Agriculture (approval numbers: 2023157, 2022131, 2021071, 2020095, and 2019108). Experiments involving genetically modified organisms were carried out according to the guidelines of the Committee on Recombinant DNA Security of Tokyo University of Agriculture and were approved by the same committee (approval number: 230005).

Mice

KOR1/Stm-Ads/Stm (Ads) and KOR1 mice were bred, and maintained at the Saitama Cancer Center and Tokyo University of Agriculture and used for mating experiments. C57BL/6J (B6) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory Japan (Tokyo, Japan) and used as mating partners in this study. Genetic analyses were performed using (B6×Ads)F1, F2, and [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N2 progeny. The [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N2 and N3 progeny were further used to map the genetic locus responsible for the Ads phenotype.

Phenotypic analysis and inheritance pattern

The presence and distribution patterns of white spots on the dorsal coat were assessed by gross observation. (B6×Ads)F1, F2, and [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N2 were generated by mating one B6 and Ads, four affected (B6×Ads)F1 individuals, and two B6 and two affected (B6×Ads)F1 individuals, respectively. The inheritance pattern of white-spotting was identified using 22 (B6×Ads)F1, 43 F2, and 56 [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N2 progeny, respectively. The segregation ratios of white-spotting in the intercross and backcross progeny were analyzed using a chi-square test.

Linkage analysis

Mice were euthanized by inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane in accordance with institutional animal care and use guidelines. Genomic DNA was extracted from the liver tissue of the [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N2 and N3 progeny using ISOGENOME (NIPPON Genetics, Tokyo, Japan), following the manufacturer’s protocol. First, genome-wide linkage analysis was performed on the six affected and three unaffected [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N2 progeny generated by mating B6 and two affected (B6×Ads)F1 individuals using polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-simple sequence length polymorphism analysis with 85 polymorphic microsatellite markers between KOR and B6 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Fine mapping was conducted on 29 [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N2 and 17 [B6×(B6×Ads)F1]N3 progeny using four additional microsatellite markers on chromosome 4, including Dhcr24_ms_int2-3 (F: 5′- TAT TGC CTG GCA CAA TGA AC-3′, R: 5′-GGG GTT TGG GGA GAG AAT TA-3′) and D4Nok1 (F: 5′-GTT CAG TCC TAT GCA CTA CTC TCT C-3′, R: 5′-TGG TTA CCT TTC CGC AAC ATA AAT-3′). PCR was performed using KAPA2G Fast ReadyMix with dye (NIPPON Genetics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 sec, annealing at 58°C for 10 sec, and extension at 72°C for 10 sec, and a final extension at 72°C for 2 min. PCR products were separated on a 4% agarose gel stained with GelRed (FUJIFILM Wako, Tokyo, Japan), and visualized using a transilluminator.

Mutation analysis

Mutation analysis of a candidate gene for Ads phenotype was performed by sequencing the coding regions of the gene. Genomic DNA was extracted from the liver tissues of KOR1 and Ads mice using ISOGENOME (NIPPON Genetics). PCR was conducted using KOD FX DNA polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), under the following conditions: one cycle of 93°C for 2 min; 40 cycles of 98°C for 10 sec, and one cycle of 68°C for 4 min. Primer sets are listed in Supplementary Table 1. PCR products were purified using the FastGene Gel/PCR Extraction Kit (NIPPON Genetics), adenine-tailed using the 10× A-attachment Mix (Toyobo), subcloned into the pT7Blue-T vector (Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and transformed into ECOS Competent E. coli DH5α cells (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). The inserted DNA fragments were amplified from more than nine colonies by direct colony PCR using M13 forward and reverse primers [10, 22], purified with ExoSAP-IT (Thermo Fisher Scientific K.K., Tokyo, Japan), and subsequently sequenced on an ABI 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used for this analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Based on a Mouse Genome Informatics analysis, we found three candidate genes for the Ads phenotype within this genomic interval. Among these, the family with sequence similarity 151, member A (Fam151a), is a mammalian homolog of the C. elegans gene menorin (mmr-1) [5, 16]. Although the deletion of Fam151b in mice leads to retinal degeneration characterized by patchy pigmentation of the retina, Fam151a knock-out mice do not exhibit any abnormal phenotypes [5]. T cell acute lymphocytic leukemia 1 (Tal1) plays important roles in hematopoiesis during embryonic development [14, 24]. Upregulation of Tal1 transcript caused by an intracisternal A particle (IAP) insertion in intron 4 leads to patchy alopecia of the trunk and head in Hpt/+ mice [9]. However, there are no reports describing the relationship between Tal1 and coat pigmentation. Therefore, Fam151a and Tal1 were excluded as candidate genes associated with the Ads phenotype. The mago homolog, exon junction complex core component (Magoh), encodes a component of the core exon junction complex (EJC) [19], and its haploinsufficiency leads to hypopigmentation in Mos2 (modifier of Sox10 spotting 2) mice [18]. Therefore, we speculated that Magoh is the most promising candidate gene responsible for the Ads phenotype. The genomic region encoding Magoh was amplified as two DNA fragments, subcloned, and sequenced in both KOR1 and Ads mice. However, no mutations were detected in the coding regions of Magoh in Ads mice compared to KOR1.

DISCUSSION

Several mutant mouse strains exhibiting white-spotting combined with homozygous embryonic lethality have been reported [1]. Dominant megacolon (Dom) mice exhibit white-spotting on the ventral side in heterozygotes and embryonic lethality in homozygotes, which is caused by a mutation in the Sry-related HMG-box gene 10 (Sox10) [1, 2, 21]. Splotch mice, which harbor a heterozygous mutation in paired box gene 3 (Pax3) display, white-spotting on the ventral side and exhibit embryonic lethality in the homozygous state [1, 4, 13]. Dominant white-spotting (W) and steel (St) mutants exhibit white spots on the head and ventral body in heterozygosity and are often embryonic lethal in the homozygous state [17]. These phenotypes are caused by mutations in the KIT proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase (Kit) [1, 6, 7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that these genes, which are associated with hypopigmentation and homozygous lethality, play essential roles in the proper development and survival of neural crest-derived melanocytes [1, 21].

In this study, we described a novel mouse mutant exhibiting hypopigmentation on the dorsal side, suggesting the presence of genetic background effects on its phenotype, and identified a genetic locus on chromosome 4 responsible for white-spotting coat.

The segregation pattern of the Ads mutation deviated from the expected Mendelian model of dominant inheritance for white-spotting combined with embryonic lethality in homozygotes (Table 1). A previous study reported that white-spotting of Mos2 mice harboring a mutation in Magoh exhibits heterozygous lethality with incomplete penetrance, accompanied by white-spotting [19]. Therefore, the deviation from the theoretical segregation ratio of the Ads phenotype may be attributed to partial lethality in heterozygous individuals due to haploinsufficiency. Additionally, variations in white-spotted patterns were observed in the inter- and backcross progeny between KOR1 and B6 (Fig. 1b). Early geneticists reported several instances of “inconstant inheritance” in trait segregation patterns during Mendelian tests [8]. An example of “inconstant inheritance” in pigmentation is the hooded pattern observed in laboratory rats, whose pigmentation area is determined by hooded (h) mutation and its modifier genes [3, 8, 23]. Melanocytes are derived from melanoblasts, which migrate from the neural crest and differentiate during embryonic development [12]. Individual variation in the area and pattern of white-spotting might result from stochastic differences in melanocyte differentiation during development, or may be influenced by the genetic background derived from the B6 strain.

The Ads locus was mapped to an approximately 5.3-Mb genomic interval on chromosome 4 (Fig. 3). Mos2 mice harboring a Magoh mutation exhibit dominant white-spotting and recessive embryonic lethality [18, 19]. Depletion of Magoh in a melanocyte-derived cell line induces G2/M phase arrest in the cell cycle [18]. Therefore, Magoh is the most promising gene responsible for Ads phenotype. However, no mutations were identified in the coding sequences of Magoh in Ads mice. These results suggest that either a novel gene within the Ads genomic interval, or a mutation in the non-coding regions of Magoh, such as a cis-regulatory element, may be responsible for the white-spotting phenotype. To identify gene mutations responsible for Ads phenotype, target resequencing of the narrowed genomic interval would be an effective approach. Although the causative gene mutation for the Ads phenotype has not been identified, our findings provide new insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying pigmentation.
