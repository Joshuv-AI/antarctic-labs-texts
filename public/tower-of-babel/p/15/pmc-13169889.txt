Alagille syndrome caused by p.L2014Vfs*10 in NOTCH2: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Weifeng Xuan, Jintang Zhang, Hao Chen, Bo Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlagille syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by pathogenic variants in JAG1 or NOTCH2, which encode fundamental components of the Notch signaling pathway. Genotype–phenotype correlation studies have not yet shown associations between mutation type and clinical manifestations or severity. Medical management is supportive, focusing on clinical manifestations of the disease.Case presentationIn the present study, a 3.5-month-old Chinese female child of Han nationality with retarded growth, cholestasis, and hepatic function damage was diagnosed with Alagille syndrome. To determine the cause, whole-exome sequencing was carried out. p.L2014Vfs*10, a novel frameshift variation of NOTCH2 caused by two-base-pair deletion at 6040 and 6041 was found and confirmed as inherited from the mother. The clinical diagnosis and treatment process are described in detail.ConclusionEarly diagnosis of Alagille syndrome is difficult, and it is prone to misdiagnosis as biliary atresia. We describe herein a case characterized by cholestatic liver disease in the Chinese context. The whole process of the occurrence, development, clinical diagnosis, and treatment is described in detail, providing guidelines for the management of similar diseases, along with a novel variation to improve the variation profile of NOTCH2, for better understanding of the pathogenesis of Alagille syndrome.

Introduction

Alagille syndrome (ALGS), first described by Daniel Alagille in 1969, is an inherited complex multisystem autosomal dominant disorder with variable expressivity, or sporadic, typically manifesting as cholestasis, potentially leading to end-stage liver disease, or even death [1]. Clinical features span multiple organ systems including hepatic, cardiac, vascular, skeletal, renal, craniofacial, and ocular, occurring with variable phenotypic penetrance, making it very difficult to diagnose and treat. Neonatal cholestasis is a main feature, owing to the paucity of intrahepatic bile ducts; this can rarely develop into cirrhosis, but is also responsible for disabling pruritus and xanthomas [2]. The prognosis depends on the severity of the liver and heart diseases. Liver disease is a major cause of morbidity in this population, whereas cardiac and vascular involvement accounts for most of the mortality [3].

The Notch signaling pathway (NSP), highly conserved in mammals, is an evolutionarily conserved intracellular signalling mechanism that regulates cell fate determination and plays a critical role in embryologic development and adult oncogenesis. NSP consists of four human Notch receptors (Notch 1, 2, 3, and 4) that can be activated by binding of any of the five Notch signalling ligands, including JAG1 and 2 and Delta-like 1, 3, and 4 [4]. NOTCH2 is an important member of the NOTCH receptor family, and germline mutation of the NOTCH2 gene can lead to two rare multisystem diseases: ALGS and Hajdu–Cheney syndrome (HCS) [5]. Current therapies are supportive, focusing on clinical manifestations, but the future is promising for the development of targeted interventions to augment NSP in involved tissues. New therapeutic approaches are under investigation, including ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT) inhibitors and other approaches that may involve targeted interventions to augment the Notch signaling pathway in involved tissues [3, 6].

ALGS is rare and highly variable, with 40% being inherited from the father or mother and the remaining 60% being due to spontaneous mutation. A majority (more than 90%) of patients have a detectable mutation in JAG1, but there is also a smaller percentage with mutations in NOTCH2 [5, 7]. In this study, we report the case of a Chinese fetal proband who presented with ALGS bearing double-base deletion in the end of NOTCH2 (c.6040_6041 del), leading to a frameshift variation p.L2014Vfs*10, a novel variation of NOTCH2, which was inherited from the mother and has not been reported in literature. Our aim in reporting this case is to highlight the importance of liver biopsy pathologic examination, genetic testing, and careful symptomatic treatment.

Case report

Clinical presentation

In November 2021, a pregnant 32‑year‑old female patient of Chinese Han nationality was admitted to Shaoxing Women and Children’s Hospital (Shaoxing, China) at 30 weeks of gestation. Owing to fetal distress in the uterus, a female premature infant of Chinese Han nationality weighing 1100 g was delivered by caesarean section after premature birth at 30 weeks gestation on 29 November. The newborn was hospitalized to the neonatal care unit because of 8 min anhelation after birth and hypoglycemia and also suffered from neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, respiratory failure, hypoglycemia, flared nares, three concave sign, hypotonia of limbs, and preterm appearance. Noninvasive ventilator and invasive ventilator were both used to improve breathing, together with caffeine. Anti-infection with cephalosporin and vancomycin, phototherapy, and supplementation with magnesium and vitamin AD and D were also administrated. The patient presented with hematochezia 3 days after birth, dilatation of intestine and bowel wall thickening were found on radiograph of abdomen (Fig. 2A), and exploratory laparotomy showed intestinal stenosis and ileus. On the basis of this situation, enterectomy and fistulization were performed. Because of severe malnutrition, anemia, and cholestasis, the child patient was readmitted only 3 months after being discharged. The patient was diagnosed with severe jaundice and sustained hypohepatia by high alpha fetoprotein (AFP) and gallbladder with smaller size and poor tension (Fig. 2B), later confirmed by liver biopsy examination (Fig. 3A–D). After careful treatment, the patient’s situation improved significantly, except for being undersize in weight and height.

Copy-number variation and whole-exome sequencing (WES)

Genomic DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood of the proband and parents using a DNEasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s procedures, respectively. For WES, the genomic DNA of the proband was enriched for coding exons using Agilent SureSelect Low Input Reagent Kit and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. The sequencing data size of the proband, father, and mother was 13,744,395,600, 11,088,991,200, and 16,354,266,000 base pairs, covering 99.80%, 99.96%, and 99.82% of coding regions. The average sequence depth was 170.59, 136.67, and 202.47 (99.33%, 99.41%, and 99.46% of targeted regions) with average depth > 20×. For copy number variation sequencing, DNA Fragmentation Reagent (Hieff NGS® Fast-Pace™) was used to cut genomic DNA into fragments, which was prepared for PCR-free library with Hieff NGS® Complete Adapter Kit (Illumina®). WES for affected individuals revealed a maternal heterozygous frame-shift variant, c.6040_6041del (p.L2014Vfs*10) in the ANK domain of NOTCH2 with 2472-aa size (Fig. 4A). Interventions, outcomes, and lessons

Owing to neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, both noninvasive and invasive ventilators were used for respiration support. Because of bloody stool 4 days after birth, pneumatosis intestinalis, abdominal laparotomy, enterectomy, fistulization of terminal ileum, and intestinal biopsies were operated in the following. Omeprazole and metoclopramide were administered by intravenous drip to promote intestinal peristalsis. Ursodesoxycholic acid and compound glycyrrhizin tablets for oral administration were administered to improve liver function and relieve cholestasis. To prevent anemia and improve growth and development, ferrous succinate tablets and vitamin D drops vitamin AD drops were taken daily according to doctor’s advice. Currently, the baby patient is more than 2 years old and looks normal except for slightly small height, weight, and head circumference (Fig. 1D–F).

ALGS is one of the important causes of chronic cholestatic liver disease in infancy. Early diagnosis is difficult, and it can easily be misdiagnosed as biliary atresia. Liver biopsy pathologic examination and genetic testing are helpful for early identification and correct diagnosis.

Discussion

ALGS is a rare, inherited childhood disorder typically manifesting as cholestasis and potentially leading to end-stage liver disease and death [5]. The incidence rate of ALGS was 1 in 70,000–100,000 live births, but the true occurrence rate is probably closer to 1 in 30,000 with the advent and development of molecular diagnosis [8, 9]. Nearly 95% of individuals with ALGS had a pathogenic variant in JAG1, while only a tiny minority of patients with ALGS have been found to have a variant in NOTCH2 [9]. Genetic confirmation is necessary because the clinical presentation and disease severity are highly variable, with some individuals expressing none of the characteristic features of AGLS [5]. Despite a lot of meaningful research and strong understanding of disease-causing variants for ALGS, it is not well understood why different family members with the same pathogenic variants present different phenotypic characteristics and severity. It is of extreme clinical importance to investigate patients with ALGS, provide novel disease-causing variants, and summarize variants in NOTCH2, etc.

JAG1 and NOTCH2 are both single-pass transmembrane proteins, consisting of 26 and 34 exons, respectively [4, 10]. NOTCH2, with a 2471 amino acid residue size, is composed of an intracellular (NICD) domain and extracellular domain (NECD) [9]. As shown in Fig. 4B, the NECD contains 36 epidermal growth factor (EGF) repeats involved in ligand binding, Lin/Notch repeats (LNR), while the NICD comprises seven ankyrin repeats (ANK), RBP-Jκ-associated module (RAM), nuclear localization signal (NLS), and proline/glutamic acid/serine/threonine-rich domain (PEST). NSP is activated when the receptor-bound ligand is modified by the ubiquitin ligase. Genotype–phenotype correlation studies have not yet shown associations between mutation type and clinical manifestations or severity, and it has been hypothesized that modifier genes may modulate the effects of JAG1 and NOTCH2 pathogenic variants [9]. Direct communication between them leads to cleavage and nuclear translocation of NICD, which will interact with transcription factors to influence gene expression thereafter [4]. In patients with ALGS, disrupted NSP is associated with abnormal development of the intrahepatic bile ducts. The probability of the former mutation is much higher, while the latter is a rare event [10].

Besides ALGS, mutations occurring in NOTCH2 also lead to HCS. Similar to ALGS, HCS is a multisystemic disease and follows autosomal dominant inheritance. Figure 4B shows the domain structure scheme of NOTCH2, where previously reported ALGS/HCS-related mutations are located. NSP plays important roles in cell fate decisions throughout the developing and adult organism, and any significant variation ultimately results in changes in gene expression and vital processes [11]. More importantly, this crucial transcriptional regulation could not be separated from the binding between the nuclear effector of the pathway, CSL (C-promoter binding factor 1 (CBF-1), suppressor of hairless (Su(H)), lin-12 and glp-1 (Lag-1)) and ANK domain. In the present study, on the basis of WES results, a two-base gene deletion mutation at 6040 and 6041 was identified at ANK5 of NOTCH2, resulting from a frameshift mutation (p.L2014Vfs*10). Owing to the 10-amino-acid incorrect segment from 2014 and early termination, the mutant NOTCH2 protein with 447 amino acid truncation was 2024 amino acids in length. Thus, such heterozygous variation in an autosomal-dominant manner must affect all NOTCH2-related biological processes. Combined with clinical manifestations, the case was highly suspicious for ALGS, and pedigree sequencing was carried out in a timely manner to reach a definite diagnosis and determine the cause and mechanism.

Conclusion

On the basis of available outpatient medical records since hospital discharge, the patient had close follow-up with her primary care physician, and her situation improved significantly, except for being undersize in weight and height. The focus of our case study was twofold. First, there are very few such studies in the Chinese context, so we wanted to describe the whole process of the occurrence, development, clinical diagnosis, and treatment in detail, to share our experience and instruct other doctors how to handle similar situations. Second, we wanted to describe a novel variation to improve and complete the variation profile of NOTCH2, for a better understanding of the relationship between genetic mutations of NSP and the occurrence of ALGS or even HCS.
