An accelerated Parkinson’s disease monkey model using AAV-α-synuclein plus poly(ADP-ribose)
Authors: Shuyi Liu, Naixue Yang, Yaping Yan, Shaobo Wang, Jialing Chen, Yichao Wang, Xue Gan, Jiawen Zhou, Guoqing Xie, Hong Wang, Tianzhuang Huang, Weizhi Ji, Zhengbo Wang, Wei Si
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe etiology of Parkinson’s disease (PD) remains elusive, and the limited availability of suitable animal models hampers research on pathogenesis and drug development. We report the development of a cynomolgus monkey model of PD that combines adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated overexpression of α-synuclein into the substantia nigra with an injection of poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) into the striatum. Our results show that pathological processes were accelerated, including dopaminergic neuron degeneration, Lewy body aggregation, and hallmarks of inflammation in microglia and astrocytes. Behavioral phenotypes, dopamine transporter imaging, and transcriptomic profiling further demonstrate consistencies between the model and patients with PD. This model can help to determine the mechanisms underlying PD impacted by α-synuclein and PAR and aid in the accelerated development of therapeutic strategies for PD.Keywords: microglia, poly(ADP-ribose), α-synuclein, Parkinson’s disease, non-human primate

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder that lacks robust therapies. The primary pathological characteristics of both sporadic and familial forms of PD include the progressive deterioration of dopaminergic neurons (DAns) in the substantia nigra (SN) and the appearance of Lewy bodies (LBs) and neurites throughout the nervous system.1 LBs are composed of α-synuclein (α-syn; encoded by the SNCA gene) inclusions and aggregates.2 The classical motor symptoms of PD include bradykinesia, muscular rigidity, rest tremor, and postural and gait impairment; moreover, non-motor features are frequently present in patients with PD before the onset of classical motor symptoms.3 While the etiology of PD remains elusive for most patients, numerous genetic causes associated with PD have been identified. Genetic variation is estimated to contribute approximately 25% to the overall risk of developing PD.4 Notably, mutations in the SNCA gene can lead to familial PD, including the mutation of the 53rd amino acid of the SNCA gene from alanine to threonine (A53T mutation), and such mutations confer to α-syn a greater tendency to misfold and aggregate than the wild type (WT).5 Misfolded α-syn can be released outside the cytoplasm by neurons and subsequently acts as a template and promotes aggregation, converting normal α-syn to pathogenic forms, such as Ser129-phosphorylated α-syn (P-α-syn) and oligomers.6 This mechanism of transmission probably underlies the progression of progressive death of DAns and the subsequent emergence of behavioral phenotypes.7 As previously reported, pathological α-syn leads to various cellular impairments, including damage to mitochondria, autophagolysosomes, and nucleus. P-α-syn and LB-like structures have been confirmed in the autopsy tissues of patients with PD.1

Unfortunately, the progression of clinical therapeutic strategies for PD, including drug development and disease management, is hindered by the scarcity of appropriate animal disease models.8 Currently, the most widely used animal models are those induced by specific drugs, such as 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) and 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) in either rodents or non-human primates (NHPs).9 These drug-induced PD animal models offer the advantage of swiftly replicating pathological and motor symptoms associated with DAn depletion, but these drugs act directly on DAns, causing cell death bypassing prodromal symptoms. This drawback of drug-induced PD models may lead to ineffective translation from bench to clinical trials.10 Alternatively, the generation of PD animal models predicated on the theory that α-syn is a major potential PD pathogenic factor has gained favor in recent years. Pre-formed fibrils (PFFs), as a prion-like toxicological protein, could recruit the endogenous α-syn and transform its normal structure into a pathological structure.11 Similarly, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated overexpression of α-syn (AAV-α-syn) also can induce progressive degeneration in midbrain DAns.12 Currently, the PD animal models generated by AAV-α-syn or PFFs are predominated by rodents. Although AAV-α-syn or PFFs induce progressive degenerative changes in DAns and promote P-α-syn accumulation, the loss of DAns is relatively modest in these PD rodent models (typically between 25% and 35%).13,14 Thus, the primary limitation of the AAV-α-syn- or PFF-induced PD animal model lies in the variability in the extent of the neurodegenerative response, which has made it difficult to obtain consistent behavioral impairments. Furthermore, the substantial disparities between rodents and humans in brain structure, brain microenvironment, and behavioral phenotypes restrict the applicability of these PD rodent models in disease diagnosis, drug, and therapeutic strategy development.

In contrast, NHPs exhibit greater similarity to humans in physical characteristics, including lifespan, brain complexity, and cellular metabolism, as well as in social behavior, encompassing cognitive and behavioral traits compared to rodents. Therefore, PD NHP models exhibit significant advantages in the study of pathogenic mechanisms and the development of therapeutic strategies for PD.15 NHP models utilizing AAV-α-syn or PFFs have been developed and shown to exhibited P-α-syn pathology and DAn death.16,17,18,19 However, these models are still limited in recapitulating behavioral phenotypes analogous to those seen in patients with PD.20 Moreover, the manifestation of α-syn pathogenesis in these monkey models is time consuming, typically requiring up to 18 months to observe associated DAn pathology.16,17,18,19 Therefore, there is an urgent necessity for a PD NHP model that not only presents with α-syn pathology and progressive DAn death but also accurately replicates behavior symptoms.

Recently, Kam et al. disclosed that fibrillar aggregates of α-syn can activate the DNA damage repair protein poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP-1), which in turn triggers the generation of poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR), and PAR directly binds to and accelerates the fibrillization of recombinant α-syn into highly pathogenic fibrils.21,22 We propose a hypothesis that the injection of AAV-α-syn into the SN combined with the administration of PAR into the striatum (STR) may significantly accelerate the progression of P-α-syn pathology and further enhance the potential for behavioral symptoms of PD to appear in NHPs. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to utilize these molecular findings to develop a PD model in cynomolgus monkeys, which presents with α-syn pathology and progressive DAn death, and replicate behavioral symptoms of patients with PD.

Results

Unilateral injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR induced progressive impairments in motor behaviors and reduced dopamine transporters

In this study, four cynomolgus monkeys (M1–M4) were unilaterally injected with AAV-α-syn into the SN, followed by an ipsilateral (right hemisphere) injection of PAR into the STR 3 months later (Figure 1A; Table S1). The following behaviors were monitored for up to 18 months in the monkeys. The PD-like behavioral symptoms presented by clinical rating scale (CRS)23 and PD Kurlan24 scores showed gradual upward trends in the monkeys (Figures 1B and 1C). The monkeys exhibited a gradual decline in the moving distance post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (Figure 1D), reflecting a decline in the overall motor ability and motivation. One monkey (M3) presented the typical PD clinical symptom of a resting tremor (Video S1), and another monkey (M2) showed spontaneous unilateral rotation (Video S2). Correspondingly, treatment with the clinical PD drug bromocriptine significantly alleviated the tremor duration in monkey M3 (Figure 1E). The monkeys also exhibited impaired fine motor skills. At 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, the time it took to grab food increased when evaluated using the hand dexterity task (Figure 1F). These results pertaining to motor ability demonstrated that these PD monkey models presented typical motor dysfunction following AAV-α-syn plus PAR injection.

In addition, we performed single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) scans25 to detect the alterations in cocaine derivative N-(3-iodopro-2E-enyl)-2β-carbomethoxy-3β-(4′-methylphenyl) nortropane (PE2I) binding of dopamine transporters (DATs) before and every 6 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (Figure S1A). The average specific uptake ratio (SUR) changes observed in the ipsilateral exhibited a significant decrease at 6 months, followed by a compensatory elevation at 12 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR. Furthermore, a healthy control monkey (C1) and two PD monkey models (M1 and M2) at 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR were examined by positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) (Figure 1G). PET-CT results showed that the ipsilateral of the monkeys at 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR showed a significant decrease in DATs compared with the control monkey. The PET-CT and SPECT results both suggest that the AAV-α-syn plus PAR injection led to a reduction of DAT expression, indicating dopamine dysfunction (Figures S1A and 1G). To verify this observation, we further stained DATs in the STR of a monkey (M3) at 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR and a healthy control monkey (C2) (these two monkeys were also used for the subsequent immunostaining assay). The DAT immunoreactivity showed a bilaterally (left and right hemisphere) remarkable decrease, encompassing the caudate nucleus and putamen (Figure 1H), which corresponded to the results of PET-CT imaging, and quantitative optical density measurements also verified this (Figure 1I).

Reduction of TH immunoreactivities in the nigrostriatal system

Two monkeys (M4 and M3) were sacrificed at 4 and 18 months post-unilateral injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, respectively. First, we detected GFP expression in the SN of M3 and M4 (Figures S1A, S1B1, S1C, and S1C1), which indicated the successful virus transfection and subsequent overexpression of α-syn in the SN. The immunohistochemical staining of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) in the bilateral SN showed a decrease in the number of ipsilateral TH-positive (TH+) DAns at 4 and 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (Figures 2B–2B2 and 2C–2C2) compared to the control monkey (C2) (Figures 2A–2A2), with TH+ area and TH+ number reductions of 20.96% and 52.72%, respectively (Figures 2D and 2E). The immunofluorescence staining results of Nurr1, a transcription factor for DAns, further substantiated the degeneration of DAns (Figures S1D–S1D2 and S1E). Then, we performed Nissl staining to measure changes in the number of neurons, and the results indicated a reduction in the number of neurons in the ipsilateral SN and STR (Figures S1F–S1F3 and S1G–S1G3). Additionally, double immunofluorescence staining for TH and P-α-syn was performed in the ipsilateral SN, and the results revealed that the neurons with P-α-syn inclusions exhibited significantly reduced TH immunoreactivity in M3 and M4 compared to C2 (Figures 2F–2H). In contrast, intense TH immunoreactivity was discernible in the neurons without P-α-syn inclusions in the ipsilateral SN of the monkey injected with AAV-α-syn plus PAR. These results indicated that the damage to neurons through steady deterioration for an extended period lasting at least 18 months (Figures 2G and 2H).

Induction of LB-like P-α-syn pathology and the activation of glia

A recognized characteristic of LBs is resistance to protease K (pK) digestion. We further performed immunohistochemical staining on the SN treated with pK to detect the insoluble P-α-syn, which indicted the presence of LB-like structures. The results showed that no LB-like structures were observed in either the SN or STR of the control monkey (C2) (Figures 3A–3A2 and 3B–3B2). In contrast, at 4 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, pK-resistant P-α-syn was detected in the brain regions of the SN and STR in monkey M4 (Figures 3A, 3A3, 3A4, 3B, 3B3, 3B4, S2A, S2A1, and S2A2). Subsequently, there was a noticeable increase in the quantity of LBs and Lewy protrusions in the SN of monkey M3 (Figures 3A, 3A5, 3A6, 3B, 3B5, 3B6, and S2A–S2A2). Notably, the fibrous form of Lewy protrusions became denser in the STR, accompanied by a significant rise in the number of LB-like structures in the SN, which was particularly noticeable in the monkeys at 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (Figures 3A, 3A5, 3A6, 3B, 3B5, and 3B6). The results suggest that the overexpression of α-syn leads to the presence of developed pK-resistant P-α-syn-aggregated amyloid structures in the nigrostriatal system of the PD monkey model over time.

The accumulation of insoluble P-α-syn also causes the activation of microglia (IBA1+) and astrocytes (GFAP+) in the SN. GFAP is an inflammatory marker for astrocytes,26 and IBA1 is a marker that can label microglia to identify the area and cellular shape.27 At 4 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, the numbers of microglia and astrocytes in the SN increased by 4- and 2.39-fold, respectively, compared with the SN of control monkey (Figures 3C–3C2, Figures 3D–3D2, 3E, and 3F). At 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, the cell morphologies of microglia and astrocytes showed obvious microglial branching and enlarged cell bodies and processes. Additionally, the numbers of astrocytes and microglia significantly increased by 5.2- and 1.84-fold, respectively, in comparison to the control at 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, while no statistically significant difference was observed between 4 and 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (Figures 3C, 3C1, 3C3, 3D, 3D1, 3D3, 3E, and 3F). We also performed immunofluorescence staining of CD68, a marker of activated microglia,28 and S100 calcium-binding protein B (S100B), a marker of matured astrocytes and inflammation.29 The results showed a significant increase in activated microglia and astrocytes in PD monkey models at 4 and 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (p < 0.05) (Figures S2D–S2D2, S2E–S2E2, S2F, and S2G). Furthermore, at 4 and 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, deposits of granular P-α-syn were observed within the somas and protrusions of IBA1+ microglia (Figures S2B–S2B2). Compared to the control monkey, the number of microglia exhibiting phagocytosed P-α-syn was significantly increased in PD monkey models at 4 and 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (Figure S2C). The increased glial amount indicates that the overexpression of α-syn caused long-term inflammatory responses, which may be one of the factors contributing to the persistent pathological alterations in the PD monkey model.

Tissues of the SN and the caudate and putamen of the STR were sampled from monkey M1 and the control monkey (C1) for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Cells from the SN and STR were subsequently clustered into 6 major cell types by unsupervised graph-based clustering based on well-known marker genes in the literature30 (Figures S3A and S3B). Analysis of cell proportions within each sample revealed that microglia were enriched in the SN and STR of the PD monkey model (Figures S3C–S3E) and exhibited transcriptional differences from the control microglial population (Figure S3F). These results were consistent with our immunohistochemical staining of microglia in Figures 3C–3F and scRNA-seq results in patients with PD.27 Furthermore, the analysis of microglia in the SN and STR suggest that the enrichment clusters in the PD monkey model and patients with PD are similar (Figures S5G, S5H, and S4A–S4E), exhibiting the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype (Figure S4F). The transcriptional profiles and gene module analyses showed that the sub-clusters enriched in the PD monkey model predominantly expressed genes related to pro-inflammatory factors (Figure S4G). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis suggests that the microglial sub-clusters enriched in the PD monkey model exhibited an up-regulation of inflammatory pathways (Figure S4H). Moreover, PARP-1 expression was activated in 6 microglial sub-clusters in the PD monkey model (Figure S4J), and the PARP1-related functions were down-regulated (Figure S4I). We suppose that PAR may cause the dysfunction and death of DAns through microglia-mediated neuroinflammation and the activation of the PARP-1 pathway.

The dysfunction of mitochondria and increased autophagy lead to the degeneration of neurons in SN and STR

Previous studies have shown that autophagy is activated, and the mitochondria exist dysfunctionally in PD, which leads to neuron death. Therefore, we analyzed the autophagy and mitochondria in the PD monkey model. There are 9 neuronal cell types in the SN (Figures 4A and 4B), and the proportion of the DA-ALDH1A1 cluster (a marker gene relating to A9 DAns31) declines in the SN of the PD monkey model (Figure 4C), with the function of DA-ALDH1A1 related to immune cell proliferation, inflammation, and apoptosis (Figure 4D). Notably, we identified a neuronal cluster, GABA-NCDN, almost exclusively derived from the PD monkey model (Figures 4B and 4C), which was primarily associated with dopamine secretion and metabolic process (Figure 4D). We speculate that the cells in GABA-NCDN may be involved in dopamine-related signaling and neurotransmitter secretion disorders. Furthermore, we assessed autophagy pathway activity by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and found a significant enhancement in the PD monkey model (Figure 4E). We also downloaded the previously reported data from the midbrain of patients with PD.32 DAns could cluster into DA-ALDH1A1 and DA-LMX1A (Figures S3H–S3G), and the autophagy level increased in DA-ALDH1A1, which was consistent with the results in the PD monkey model (Figure S3I). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining further validated the above finding regarding autophagy. The results demonstrated the co-localization of GFP with ubiquitin (Ubi; a key protein of the ubiquitin-proteasome system) and P62 (a key protein of the autophagy-lysosomal system) in the SN of the PD monkey model (Figures 4F and 4G).

Furthermore, mitochondria played a central role in age-related neurodegenerative diseases, including PD.33 Subsequently, we observed the expression of TOM20 in GFP-positive (GFP+) cells. Normally, mitochondria exhibit a short rod-shaped or spherical configuration.34 However, the mitochondria of GFP+ neurons in the SN presented as fragmented residual mitochondria, alongside diffuse and unlocalized distributions at 4 months post-injection, and the pathological alterations of the mitochondria became further intensified at 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR (Figure 4H). The statistical results of the area of mitochondria also verified the above results (Figure 4I). Furthermore, we conducted an in vitro experiment to validate above findings. We established a WT neural stem cell (WT-NSC) line and established NSC lines overexpressing A53T-α-syn (tdTomato) (A53T-NSCs) by lentiviral transfection. The staining of MitoTracker35 revealed that the addition of PAR to A53T-NSCs (A53T+PAR) significantly changed the mitochondria, which presented with a shortened and round morphology, while the normal mitochondria in WT-NSCs appear slender and long (Figure 5A), and the average mitochondrial area analysis also confirmed this (Figure 5B). In addition, mitochondrial membrane potential is also considered to be one of the important indicators to evaluate mitochondrial function, so we used JC-1 to detect mitochondrial membrane potential. JC-1 accumulates show red fluorescence in normal mitochondria, and JC-1 shows green fluorescence when the mitochondrial membrane potential collapses. WT-NSCs were treated with FCCP as positive controls. The results demonstrated the dysfunctional membrane potentials in the A53T-NSC and A53T+PAR groups (Figure 5C). Immunofluorescence staining showed increased expression of P62 and P-α-syn due to the addition of PAR (Figures 5D and 5E), which was supported by western blot results (Figure 5F). These findings support the contribution of mitochondrial dysfunction and autophagy to the pathogenesis of PD and the development of PD-related pathology and behaviors in PD monkey models.

Loss of DAns was believed to induce the hyperexcitability of spiny projection neurons in the caudate and putamen, suggestive of a pathologic state affecting the brain beyond the SN.36 In the caudate and putamen, neurons were sub-clustered into 6 sub-clusters based on the expression of specific marker genes that are mainly medium spiny neurons (D1/D2-MSNs)37 (Figures S5A and S5B). Although the number of neuronal sub-clusters in the caudate and putamen did not show significant changes (Figures S5C and S5E), both up-regulated and down-regulated genes in the PD monkey model were associated with functions such as the assembly of projections from neuronal axons or dendrites (Figures S5D and S5F). We speculate that the function of D1/D2-MSNs in the STR is disturbed due to degenerative lesions of DAns.

Discussion

In this study, a PD cynomolgus monkey model was generated to efficiently simulate the pathological phenotypes and behavioral phenotypes of PD. It is important for animal models to reproduce these pathological phenotypes, especially the LB-like pathology.38,39 A study in 2003 attempted to establish a PD monkey model in adult marmosets (New World monkey) by injecting AAV-α-syn into the SN,40 which was replicated in another, similar study conducted in 2015 involving both young and old marmosets.19 Both studies reported the loss of TH+ neurons at 3–4 months post-AAV-α-syn injection,19,40 and no LB-like structures were observed. A study conducted in 2019 injected PFFs into the putamen of cynomolgus monkeys20 and revealed a loss about 29% of TH+ neurons in the SN at 15 months post-injection of PFFs. Furthermore, this study detected the presence of P-α-syn pathology, contrary to the findings observed in marmosets described above. However, there is currently no report on generating PD monkey models in Old World monkeys through AAV-α-syn administration. In our study, the cynomolgus monkeys were administered AAV-α-syn to induce PD models, and the application of PAR was combined to expedite the progression of PD pathology. The death of TH+ neurons in the SN and the presence of pK-insoluble P-α-syn in the SN and STR were detected as early as 4 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR. The loss of TH+ neurons further deteriorated in the course of time, with the number of degenerated TH+ neurons increasing from 26% to 50% by 18 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR. Compared to the previous report in cynomolgus monkeys, which injected PFFs into the STR, the number of dead TH+ neurons was 29% in the SN at 15 months post-injection. Previous studies in rats have also shown a loss of TH+ neurons in the SN around 10% at 3 weeks post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PFFs, and there was a slowly developing, progressive loss of TH+ neurons, reaching about 50% until 24 weeks.41,42 In our study, the loss of TH+ neurons was about 26% at 4 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, indicating the high efficiency of our PD monkey model. Meanwhile, in our study, the presence of LB aggregates was observed at 4 months post-injection, and pK-insoluble P-α-syn aggregates showed a progressive increase in both the SN and STR at 18 months. LBs represent a crucial pathological hallmark in PD, and it is widely acknowledged that the quantification of pK-resistant P-α-syn can serve as one of the key criteria for assessing LB aggregation.43 Therefore, we employed pK treatment on α-syn in our study to confirm that the presented LBs are more like those of patients with PD, which has not been reported in previous PD monkey models. The LB-like pathology may cause mitochondrial dysfunction and autophagy enhancement, which accelerate the degeneration of TH+ neurons.

Patients with PD exhibited a concurrent elevation in neuroinflammation, and the excessive activation of microglia and subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines play important roles in the pathogenesis and progression of PD.44 However, previous studies have not reported the crucial pathological phenotype of glial inflammation in PD monkey models induced by either PFFs or AAV-α-syn. Therefore, our study employed immunostaining to reveal the pronounced activation of astrocytes and microglia. We observed the significant activation of both astrocytes and microglia as early as 4 months post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, along with a sustained increase in microglia over the course of 18 months. We also detected phagocytosis of P-α-syn by microglia at 18 months, indicating their potential role not only in pro-inflammatory processes but also in the clearance of P-α-syn aggregates. These findings suggest that glial inflammation may contribute to the pathological progression of PD.

The classical motor features of PD have been postulated to be associated with the loss of DAns and the LBs in the SN, which include bradykinesia, muscular rigidity, rest tremor, and postural and gait impairment. However, previous reported PD monkey models established by either PFFs and AAV-α-syn did not report any PD-associated behavioral phenotypes.19,20,40,45,46,47,48 The PD marmoset models established by AAV-α-syn in 2003 were subjected to amphetamine-induced rotation experiments, but no consistent changes were shown at any time point.40 In our study, the PD monkey models showed a gradual increase in PD Kurlan and CRS scores over time, which were highly correlated with the above progressive DAn death and gradual accumulation of LB-like pathology. In particular, the resting tremor observed in our PD monkey models is very similar to that in patients with PD, which was not observed in PD rodent models nor in other reported α-syn-based PD monkey models. In addition, the PD monkey models in this study showed a decline in fine motor skills, indicating that the finger flexibility and responsiveness of these monkeys were impaired. Previous study has reported a significant reduction in striatal DAT content in patients with PD.49 In our study, SPECT imaging, PET-CT imaging, and immunohistochemical staining results showed a decrease in DAT intake, which is one of the reasons for the obvious behavioral phenotypes of PD monkey models. In contrast, the previously reported PFF-induced PD cynomolgus monkey showed a compensatory increase of DAT uptake detected by SPECT and immunohistochemical staining evaluation up to 15 months after the injection of PFFs and did not show PD-related behavioral symptoms.20 Therefore, the speedup of pathological processes observed in this study, including TH+ neuron degeneration, LB aggregation, and astrocyte and microglia inflammation, may underlie the emergence of behavioral phenotypes in the monkeys. Finally, we also observe depression-like phenotypes in one monkey (M2) from the AAV-α-syn plus PAR group (Video S3).

Limitations of the study

There are certain limitations to this study. The number of monkeys used is small, and only two monkeys were used in the scRNA-seq analysis due to the limitation of animal resources and ethical concerns. This limited the exploration of the PD mechanism at the transcriptional level. The establishment and application of the PD monkey model based on α-syn overexpression combined with PAR should ensure the accuracy of brain localization based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and the accuracy of the operation process, and this technique could also be applied to stereotaxic injections for other brain diseases.

Experimental model and study participant details

Establishment of PD monkey model

Six male cynomolgus monkeys weighing between 5 and 8 kg and aged 12–15 years old, were used in this study. M1-M4 were injected with AAV-α-syn plus PAR. Two healthy male monkeys at similar age were used as control. The monkeys were housed individually in a controlled environment animal room with a temperature range of 18°C–26°C and humidity range of 40–70%. Animals were fed fruits and vegetables daily. They were subjected to a 12-h light-dark cycle (8:00 a.m.-8:00 p.m.). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunming University of Science and Technology (approval number: LPBR20170201). SN and STR were localized under MRI guidance. Briefly, the monkey was intramuscularly administrated ketamine (10 mg/kg, i.m.), xylazine hydrochloride (0.15 mL/kg), and atropine (0.5 mg/kg) to induce prolonged surgical anesthesia, and the head was fixed onto a Kopf primate stereotaxic frame. To accurately locate the SN and STR, five markers were initially labeled on the surface of the monkey’s skull. The first marker served as the ear bar zero and was used as the AP0 reference point. The second and third markers were positioned 10 and 15 mm anterior to the AP0, respectively. The fourth and fifth markers were located 5 mm and 8 mm lateral to the left midsagittal plane from the second and third markers, respectively. Then horizontal, coronal, and sagittal planes with high-resolution 3D T2-weighted MRI were obtained with T2 SPACE sequence (TR = 2,300ms, TE = 3.13ms, TI = 900ms, FA = 7°, matrix = 128 × 128, FOV, field of view = 102mm, slicethickness = 0.8mm) using a 3.0 Tesla MRI scanner (SIMENS). One week after MRI-guided localization of the SN, four monkeys underwent surgery for AAV-α-syn (BrainVTA, rAAV-hSyn-SNCA-P2A-EGFP-3flag-WPRE-hGH Pa, 9-3562-K210308, 1.00E+14 vg/mL) injection. The injections were performed on the right side (ipsilateral) of the brain for each monkey and the left side was used as the contralateral side. According to the guidance of marker signals on MRI image, the marker 1 as AP0. The injection sites of the SN were determined. The injection site of the SN is 10.81 ± 0.8 mm forward the AP0, 5.38 ± 0.36 mm to the right from the midsagittal plane and depth with 31.46 ± 0.50 mm from the site of the skull surface. Then a total of 30 μl AAV-α-syn were slowly injected in SN with 5 μL per site via a 50 μL Hamilton syringe (Hamilton), and followed with PAR (1.25 μL, 5 nM) injection at the ipsilateral side 3 months later.

Method details

Locomotor test and analysis

For locomotor analysis, each monkey was placed in transparent cages (1.2 m × 1 m x 1m) situated in a quiet testing room, and the movements were recorded for 90 min by using a digital video camera (FDR-AK45, SONY). The movement behaviors of each monkey were recorded at one month prior to the AAV-α-syn injection plus PAR and 6-, 12- and 18-month post-injection. The videos were then analyzed by using a commercial primate behavior analysis system (PrimateScan1.0, CleverSys Inc, USA).

PD score and clinical rating scale analysis

The Kurlan scale and CRS were used to grade the PD scores of PD monkey models. Behaviors of each monkey were recorded for 60 min from 10:00 to 11:00 a.m. using digital video cameras (FDR-AK45, SONY) at one-month prior to AAV-α-syn plus PAR injection and 6-, 12-, and 18-month post-injection. Three experienced blinded observers individually evaluated the monkey behaviors and graded the PD scores according to the PD Kurlan scale and CRS. The PD Kurlan scores and CRS scores of each monkey were graded at one month prior to AAV-α-syn plus PAR injection and every six months thereafter.

Hand dexterity task (HDT) test and analysis

The PD monkey models were tested for fine motor function of the hand by using a modified Brimkman board measuring 230 mm × 150 mm with slots 2mm long, 1.2mm wide, and 10mm deep. The HDT was performed at one month before AAV-α-syn plus PAR injection and 6-, 12- and 18-month post injection. The monkeys were placed in individual cage with the right or left window open to access the corresponding hand. The monkey used its hand to retrieve food pellets from 35 slots (17 vertical and 18 horizontal), and the test was recorded with three digital video cameras for offline processing. The test was repeated daily with alternating hands and rewarded with dried raisins or almonds. For the test, if the monkey started with the right hand on day 1, it started with the left hand on day 2 and so on. Each monkey recorded the ipsilateral and contralateral hands for three days. Adobe Premiere Pro was used to analyze the feeding time. The frame count starts when the hand reaches out of the cage and ends when the monkey takes out the peanut. Frames were counted from the time the hand reached out of the cage to the time the monkey removed the peanut. The recorded seconds were statistically analyzed using Graphpad.

PET-CT study and analysis

[18F]-FDG PET imaging was used to characterize brain metabolism in two PD monkey models (M1 and M2) post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR and one control monkey, scans were performed by the first People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province, China (GE Discovery LS16). The monkeys were first anesthetized with ketamine (5 mg/kg, i.m.), and then injected intravenously with tracer (0.3 mCi/kg, i.v.) into their lower limbs. They were then anesthetized with 0.2 mL Zoletil 50 (50 mg/kg) 60 min post-injection of tracer. A transmission scan was performed before PET acquisition to allow attenuation correction, followed by 10 min dynamic emission acquisition. PET images were corrected and reconstructed as previously described.51

SPECT study and analysis

SPECT imaging was performed in vivo to quantify DAT, a protein located only in DAn nerve terminals, by using 99mTc-TRODAT-1. SPECT imaging was performed prior to AAV-α-syn plus PAR injection (M3) and at 6- and 12-month post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR. SPECT scans were absent in the 18-month due to the temporal restriction of the COVID-19 pandemic. Briefly, the monkeys were anesthetized with hydrochloric acidulated ketamine (5 mg/kg, i.m.) and 0.2 mL midazolam (20 mg/ml, i.m.), and then, gavaged with Potassium perchlorate (Alfa Aesar, N19F013, 30mL, 20 mg/mL) for thyroid blockade. The 99mTc-TRODAT-1 was dissolved in 1 mL saline and incubated in a water bath at 95°C for 30 min, and then the monkeys were injected intravenously with the 99mTc-TRODAT-1 solution (2.25 mCi/kg) cooled down to room temperature. Two hours later, the monkey was placed in the supine position with the head being immobilized by a head holder in an SPECT scanner (Philips precedence 6P). SPECT images were acquired with a dual-head rotating gamma camera (Infinia Hawkey, GE Healthcare Ltd) for 40 min using the following parameters. Data acquisition was performed in matrix size of 128 × 128, zoom of 2.19 and 360°arc (180° for each head) with 64 projections (32 projections for each head) and 60s per projection. Three-dimensional tomographic images of transverse, coronal, and sagittal views were reconstructed using the Astonis method.

Brain tissue acquisition and section

Two monkeys (M4 and M3) were sacrificed for histochemical analysis at 4- and 18-month post-injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR, respectively. The monkeys were anesthetized with ketamine (20 mg/kg, intramuscularly) and euthanized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, intravenously). The brain was perfused through the ascending aorta with physiological saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (4% PFA). The brains were collected and post-fixed in 4% PFA for one week at 4°C. The brains were gradually dehydrationed with 15%, 20% and 30% sucrose solutions at 4°C. After dehydration and partition, mid-brain tissues were sectioned by a Leica freezing microtome (CM 1860 UV, Leica, Germany) with a thickness of 20μm and stored at −20°C.

Nissl staining analysis

The Nissl staining, a classic nucleic acid staining method traditionally used to detect of Nissl bodies in the cytoplasm of neurons, was used in our study to detect neurons. First, the sections were rinsed with distilled water for 5 min. Then, Nissl staining solution (Sangon biotech, E607316) was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The sections were washed twice with distilled water for 2 min and were dehydrated in alcohol and cleared in xylene. Finally, the images were captured with a microscope (OLYMPUS-BX-51).

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining and analysis

The P-α-syn, TH, GFAP, IBA1 were visualized by immunostaining method. The specific method is described as follows: the tissue sections taken from −20°C were washed in PBS for 5 min at room temperature and treated with 3% methanol hydrogen peroxide. The tissues were permeabilizated with 0.25% Triton X-100 and blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. The primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C, and the information of the antibodies are listed as follows: TH (Millipore, AB152, 1:400), GFAP (Millipore, SMI21, 1:400), P-α-syn antibody (abcam, ab184674, 1:500) and IBA1(Woka, 019–19741, 1:500). After being washed with PBS for 3 times, the sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermofisher, G-21234, 1:500) for TH/GFAP/IBA1and goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermofisher, TI272496, 1:500) for P-α-syn at room temperature for 2 h. In order to detect of P-α-syn, the sections were incubated with pK (Sigma, 078M4009V, 50 μg/mL) for 30 min at 37°C. The immunohistochemical reaction was completed with Metal Enhanced DAB Substrate Kit (Thermofisher, TG267781), and then the image acquisition was procced with a microscope (OLYMPUS-BX-51).

For immunofluorescence staining, the following primary antibodies were used: TH (Millipore, AB152, 1:500), GFAP (Millipore, SMI21, 1:400), P-α-syn antibody (abcam, ab184674, 1:250) and IBA1 (Woka,019–19741, 1:200), Ubi (abcam, ab140601, 1:500). The primary antibodies were incubated with the tissues overnight at 4°C, and then the sections were further incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG coupled with Alexa Fluor 594 (Abcam, ab150116, 1:1000) and goat anti-rabbit antibody coupled with Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermofisher, TF266577, 1:500) for 2 h at room temperature. The fluorescent-stained sections were visualized with a confocal microscope (Lecia TCSSP8) using excitation wavelength at 405, 488, 594 and 647 nm.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing data analysis

Two monkeys (M1: 18 months after injection of AAV-α-syn plus PAR; Control: healthy monkey) were anesthetized with ketamine (20 mg/kg, intramuscularly) and euthanized with sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, intravenously). The SN, caudate and putamen of each monkey were collected. The tissues were quickly frozen with liquid nitrogen. Nuclei were isolated by adapting the published 10X Genomics protocol for ‘Isolation of Nuclei for Single Cell RNA Sequencing’. The raw data (fastq file) of scRNA-seq was pre-processed using the Cell Ranger Single Cell Software Suite (version 7.0.1) provided by 10× Genomics. The reads were mapped to the Macaca_fascicularis_5.0 reference genome. After obtaining the filtered gene expression profile for each sample, we used R package Seurat (version 4.0.3) to perform the subsequent analysis. Due to the heterogeneity between different tissues, we conduct separate quality control for each sample. Genes expressed in less than three cells were filtered out. In the putamen tissue, cells in the PD monkey model with either fewer than 500 or more than 7000 UMIs, or with over 2950 or fewer than 400 nFeature were filtered out. For cells in the control monkey, those with either fewer than 500 or more than 8500 UMIs, or with over 3500 or fewer than 425 nFeature were excluded. In the caudate nucleus, cells in the PD monkey model with either fewer than 500 or more than 10000 UMIs, or with over 3500 or fewer than 450 nFeature were filtered out. For cells in the control monkey, those with either fewer than 500 or more than 7000 UMIs, or with over 3100 or fewer than 400 nFeature were excluded. To integrate cells from different samples, we used the “FindIntegrationAnchors” function to identify “anchors” between samples and then integrated the datasets with the “IntegrateData” function with 30 dimensions. We scaled the integrated data with the “ScaleData” function. Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA) dimensionality reduction was employed via the “RunPCA” method. In parallel, the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) technique was executed, yielding a compacted representation of the data within a reduced-dimensional space. The clustering procedure was carried out through the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions, which employed a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity optimization-based clustering algorithm to delineate distinct cell clusters.

Gene sets score analysis

The M1 and M2-associated gene sets were obtained from a study. The signature score of gene sets in microglia was calculated using the “AddModuleScore” function in the “Seurat” R package with default parameters. For the score of a single cell shown in the violin plots.

Quantification of tissue enrichment analysis

To evaluate the tissue preference of each sub-cluster, we calculated the ratio of observed to expected cell numbers in each cell cluster across different tissues to get the ratio of observation to expectation (Ro/e). For a given cell cluster, Ro/e > 1 suggests that this cluster are enriched in a specific tissue, and Ro/e < 1 suggests that this cluster are depleted in a specific tissue.

Analysis of gene differential expression and pathway enrichment

The differentially expressed genes between case and control cell clusters were determined by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test using the “FindMarkers” function in Seurat. Genes with “p-values” < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed genes. To assess the biological functions of differentially expressed genes in each comparison, we performed enrichment analysis of gene ontology (GO) biological process (BP) using “clusterProfiler” R package (version 4.0.2). Next, the pathway enrichment results were visualized by “Cytoscape” plugin “ClueGO” (version 2.5.10). Only pathways with adjusted p-values <0.05 were selected to build the annotation network.

Acquisition and culture of A53T overexpressing cells

Human Embryonic Stem Cells (hESCs) (NIH-approved H9 (WA09) hESC) were transfected with Lentivirus-A53T-overexpressing-tdTomato to generate A53T-overexpressing hESCs lines. The transfection was performed using Penicillin-streptomycin-free medium, and fluorescence was observed 48 h later. The hESCs were then dissociated into single cells using ReleSeR digestion enzyme. Single cells exhibiting red fluorescence were selected under a fluorescence microscope using an oral pipette, cultured, and expanded in a 96-well plate coated with Matrigel. After obtaining stable transfections, the hESCs were induced to differentiate through suspension culture in embryoid bodies (EBs) using a mixture of DMEM/F12, neurobasal medium, N2 supplement, B27 supplement, and L-glutamine (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 7 days of culture, the cell pellets were attached to ECM-coated dishes and further cultured in NSCs medium containing Neurobasal, N2 supplement, B27, L-glutamine, SHH, DII4, ITS, Jagged1, and L-AA (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific) to promote rosette formation. The NSCs were then purified and subjected to immunofluorescence analysis for identification. Subsequently, the NSCs were treated with 5nM PAR, and the WT/A53T/A53T + PAR cells from the same culture batch were subjected to immunofluorescence analysis after 48 h.

Immunofluorescence staining analysis, dye staining analysis, mitochondrial membrane potential analysis and western blot analysis of NSCs in vitro

Rosettes were stained with P62 and P-α-syn antibodies (all from Abcam, 1:500) to detect the expression of P62 and P-α-syn in the WT-NSCs, A53T-NSCs, A53T+PAR-NSCs. For immunofluorescence staining analysis of NSCs, cells were cultured on chamber slides, fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. They were then blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer (1% BSA) containing primary antibody (1:500). The samples were washed three times with PBS overnight for 5 min, and the cells were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (1:500) for 2 h at 37°C. The samples were washed with the same wash protocol after secondary antibody incubation and stained with DAPI. Stained samples were examined with an FV1000 fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS).

The dye staining was used the mito-dye (PKMITO Deep Red, Standard Imaging) to marker the mitochondria. WT-NSCs, A53T-NSCs and A53T+PAR-NSCs were washed three times with PBS, followed by fresh medium (2 mL in 6-well plates), followed by mitochondrial dye in a dilution ratio of 1:1000, incubated at 37°C for 30 min, and photographed under a super-resolution microscope (Zeiss Elyra 7). Mitochondria Analyzer plugin of ImageJ was used to analyze mitochondria, and three sets of images were analyzed for WT-NSCs, A53T-NSCs and A53T+PAR-NSCs to obtain the average area parameters of mitochondria, and then Graphpad was used to perform statistical analysis on the average area.

The working fluid of the mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit (TMRE) (Beyotime, C2001S) was prepared, and the amount of working fluid required for each hole of the 6-well plate was 1 mL. The positive control holes were treated with FCCP (10mM) at a ratio of 1:1000 for 20 min before adding the working fluid. After adding the working fluid to WT-NSCs, A53T-NSCs and A53T+PAR-NSCs, the cells were incubated at 37°C for 30 min, and then fluorescence was observed under confocal microscopy (Leica SP8). After TMRE staining, normal cells should show bright red fluorescence, and positive control cells and cells with mitochondrial membrane potential injury will show green fluorescence expression.

The WT-NSCs, WT+PAR-NSCs, A53T-NSCs, A53T+PAR-NSCs were collected, centrifuged at 12000rpm/5min, the supernatant was removed, 200 μL of Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay Lysis buffer (RIPA) lysate were added, protease inhibitor was added at 1:100. The lysate was placed at 4°C for 40 min, during which the lysed sample was oscillated at 4°C for 1 min every 10 min, then centrifuge at 12000 rpm/10 min, gently absorb the supernatant, transfer it to a newly pre-cooled 200 μL centrifuge tube, and store it at minus 80°. The Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (BCA) kit was used to determine the concentration of protein samples, and the absorbance value at 595 nm was determined by the Microplate reader, and the concentration of the protein to be measured was calculated. Then western blot was performed according to the concentration of target protein. First, protein electrophoresis was performed, and the electrophoresis condition was 100V constant pressure for 90 min. After electrophoresis, the protein was transferred to PVDF under 100V constant pressure for 30 min. PVDF was placed in 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) solution after transfer and closed in shakers for 1 h. P62, Ubi, β-actin antibodies (all from abcam, 1:1000) were proportionally diluted with 1%BSA. PVDF was incubated overnight in a 4°C shaker and incubated the second antibody in a 2-h room temperature shaker the next day. After incubation, TBST was used to clean the secondary antibody, and then PVDF was covered with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) for 3 min, and chemiluminescence photos were taken in the gel imager to save the obtained pictures.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data and statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed by Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and figures were plotted and constructed by Prism 6. The percentage of movement distance and time of fetching food were analyzed using Unpaired t test. The numbers of TH positive cells and glial cells are all counted under a 20× microscope (NDP.view2). The variation of SUR%, the TH+ neuron in SN (%) were analyzed by two-way ANOVA. The number of activated microglia and astrocytes were analyzed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. P levels are indicated as ns: p > 0.05, ∗: p < 0.05, ∗∗: p < 0.01, ∗∗∗: p < 0.001, or ∗∗∗∗: p < 0.0001.
