Prenatal exercise interventions result in subtle changes in the human breast milk metabolome and lipidome 7–14 days postpartum: exploratory analyses of the FitMum randomized controlled trial
Authors: Caroline B Roland, Diego Hernandez-Saavedra, Adnan Khan, Kajetan Trost, Thomas Moritz, Nina Brændstrup, Signe dP Knudsen, Anne D Jessen, Stig Molsted, Tine D Clausen, Saud A Alomairah, Ellen Løkkegaard, Bente Stallknecht, Kristin I Stanford, Jane M Bendix
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrenatal exercise has been linked to positive effects on offspring health in animal and human studies, with changes in breast milk composition suggested as a possible explanatory mechanism. This exploratory study investigated whether prenatal exercise interventions affected the metabolome and lipidome of transitional human breast milk in healthy pregnant women compared to standard care.MethodsThe present study is based on the FitMum randomized controlled trial (Copenhagen University Hospital – North Zealand, 2018–2021), which included in total 220 healthy inactive pregnant women (≤15+0 weeks’ gestation). Participants were randomly assigned to structured supervised exercise training (EXE) three times per week throughout pregnancy, motivational counselling on physical activity (MOT) offered as seven individual and group sessions during pregnancy, or standard care (CON). Physical activity was monitored continuously from inclusion throughout one year postpartum by a wrist-worn activity tracker. Samples of transitional breast milk were collected from 99 participants 7–14 days postpartum and exploratory and hypothesis-generating analyses were conducted using untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics.ResultsAfter adjustment for multiple comparisons, no significant overall differences in metabolites or lipids were observed between EXE or MOT and CON. However, subsequent nominal and secondary analyses of specific subsets revealed subtle distinctions. Cluster analysis identified higher levels of 1,7-Dimethyluric acid in both EXE and MOT versus CON (p < 0.05) and elevated oxoglutarate in EXE relative to MOT (p = 0.04). Further nominal species-level analyses of triglycerides and lipid reactions, along with pathway enrichment, showed an overall reduction in triglyceride species in EXE and a predicted conversion pathway from diacylglycerol to phospholipid species in both intervention groups. Importantly, the two interventions impacted different lipid processes and pathways.ConclusionsWhile prenatal exercise interventions did not induce major metabolomic or lipidomic changes in breast milk early postpartum, subsequent nominal analyses uncovered specific metabolite and lipid differences that may influence offspring metabolism.Trial registrationClinicalTrial.gov; NCT03679130; Sep 20, 2018. Background

Prenatal exercise is considered an effective tool to improve the metabolic health of offspring, as extensively studied in animal models, though little is known about its translatability to humans [1–3].

In humans, exercise interventions during pregnancy have been associated with reduced risks of short-term outcomes related to pregnancy and delivery, such as preterm delivery and abnormal birth weight [4, 5], while evidence from human studies on long-term effects on childhood obesity is conflicting [6, 7]. A recent systematic review found no overall association between prenatal lifestyle interventions and weight or BMI in children up to seven years of age [6], while an observational study suggested a lower risk of overweight or obesity in eight-year-old children of mothers who exercised during pregnancy [7]. In rodents, numerous studies have identified beneficial effects of maternal exercise on metabolic health of adult offspring and while there are likely many factors involved in the mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects, recent animal and human research have proposed that exercise-induced adaptations to breast milk composition may be involved in mediating improvements in offspring health [8–10].

For example, recent animal and human studies have identified exercise-induced increases in specific breast milk components [8–10], such as the oligosaccharide 3′-sialyllactose (3′-SL) [8] and the lipokine 12,13-dihydroxy-9Z-octadecenoic acid (12,13-diHOME) - an oxylipin that influences systemic metabolism [11, 12] - which are associated with improved metabolic outcomes in offspring [10]. However, the extent to which structured exercise interventions during pregnancy may alter the human breast milk metabolome and lipidome remains to be fully elucidated.

In the FitMum randomized controlled trial the primary objective was to investigate the effect of offering exercise interventions on physical activity level during pregnancy. More specifically, we investigated the effect of structured supervised exercise training (EXE) or motivational counselling on physical activity (MOT) during pregnancy on moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity during pregnancy compared to standard care (CON). We found that offering EXE enabled a higher physical activity level among pregnant women compared to CON, whereas offering MOT did not [13].

The application of metabolomic and lipidomic profiling can expand our understanding of metabolic responses to exercise [14], and in the present study we utilized untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics to conduct exploratory analyses of breast milk samples to investigate the effects of prenatal exercise interventions (EXE or MOT) on the human breast milk metabolome and lipidome 7–14 days postpartum compared to CON.

Methods

Participants and study procedures

This exploratory study is based on the FitMum randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov #NCT03679130) conducted at Copenhagen University Hospital - North Zealand, Denmark, from 2018 to 2021. Participants for the FitMum study were recruited in early pregnancy in relation to admission to the hospital’s standard prenatal care program. The FitMum study included in total 220 healthy, inactive (structured exercise at moderate-to-vigorous-intensity < 1 h/week during early pregnancy) pregnant women with gestational age (GA) ≤15 weeks and 0 days. Inclusion criteria also included body mass index (BMI) of 18.5–45 kg/m2 and body weight < 150 kg (pre-pregnancy weight or first measured weight in pregnancy), as well as no pre-existing or ongoing obstetric or medical complications, or alcohol/drug abuse [15]. At inclusion, demographic information was obtained and pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated based on self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and height. Obstetric and neonatal outcomes were collected from medical records after delivery. Gestational weight gain (GWG) was calculated after delivery, and maternal weight 7–14 days postpartum was measured at study visit 5, which took place at the same day as breast milk was collected at home prior to the visit. Obstetric and neonatal outcomes as well as GWG for the entire FitMum study population (n = 219) have been published previously [16], and are in the current paper reported as descriptive characteristics for the participants providing breast milk samples (n = 99). Physical activity, including moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity (min per week) and active min (per day), was monitored continuously from inclusion throughout one year postpartum by a wrist-worn activity tracker [17] with a built-in heart rate monitor and accelerometer. Randomization (n = 219) in a 1:2:2 ratio to either CON, EXE, or MOT, respectively, occurred after a one-week baseline period (GA ≤ 16 + 0 weeks). Interventions ran from randomization until delivery. Participants in the EXE intervention were offered one-hour supervised exercise training at moderate intensity three times per week, including two exercise sessions in a gym and one in a swimming pool. The gym sessions consisted of a combination of aerobic and resistance training with 30 min stationary bike training and 30 min of other exercise, for example, using elastic bands. In the swimming pool, participants did 15 min of swimming and 45 min of water exercises with plates, balls etc. The MOT intervention consisted of four individual and three group physical activity motivational counselling sessions of 1–2 h duration during pregnancy and a personalized text message once weekly to motivate to increased physical activity. EXE and MOT sessions were conducted by instructors with a bachelor’s or master’s degree in physiotherapy, exercise physiology or similar. During the COVID-19 pandemic, starting from 11 March, 2020, and throughout the intervention period, most exercise training sessions, motivational counselling sessions and periodically test visits (except delivery) were conducted online from home using Zoom Cloud Meetings or telephone. Breast milk samples of transitional milk were collected from in total 99 participants 7–14 days postpartum (no breast milk samples were collected during the pandemic), and hence n = 99 samples were included in the current analysis [15].

Collection of breast milk

Participants collected a transitional milk sample from a single feed at the first feeding after 6:00 AM at home on the day of their study visit 5 to the clinic, which took place 7–14 days postpartum. The breast was cleaned with a wet washcloth before pumping. One breast was expressed fully into a clean bottle using a manual breast pump (Medela Harmony breast pump) or by hand. The bottle was shaken for 30 sec to account for any variation between fore- and hindmilk, and 2.5 mL was collected in a plastic vial and immediately stored in the participant’s own freezer at around −18 °C for approximately eight hours. Milk samples were kept frozen and transported in insulated bags until storage at −80 °C. Samples were shipped to the Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Basic Metabolic Research, University of Copenhagen, Denmark, for metabolomics and lipidomics analysis. Eight breast milk samples were either stored at −18 °C for longer time (three weeks maximum), obtained at day five or day 15 after birth, or from participants who delivered prematurely, or experienced challenges obtaining the sample. However, these samples did not appear as outliers in statistical random forest analyses for outlier detection run for both metabolomics and lipidomics data (data not shown) and hence, data from all participants were included in the analyses.

Metabolomics and lipidomics analysis

Chemicals and reagents

For a detailed description of the chemicals and reagents purchased for the study analyses please see Gnanasekaran et al. [18].

Sample preparation for metabolite and lipid extraction

For metabolomics analysis, sample preparation has been described elsewhere [19]. Briefly, 200 µL of each breast milk sample was aliquoted in 1 mL Eppendorf tubes and stored in a − 80 °C freezer until use. The samples were treated with 595 μL of MeOH. For quality control and normalization, a 5 μL of internal labeled standards mix (10 µg/mL for all) containing octanoyl-L-carnitine-d3, butyryl L-carnitine-d3, palmitoyl L-carnitine-d3, glycoursodeoxycholic acid-d4, glycochenodeoxycholic acid-d4, taurolithocholic acid-d4, decanoic acid-d19, prostaglandin E2-d4, dihomo-γ-linolenic acid-d6, 12S-HETE-d8, and oleic Acid-d17 (1 mg/L) in MeOH was added to the extraction solvent. Samples were vortexed and precipitated on ice for 20 min. After precipitation, extracts were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min for protein precipitation and metabolite extraction. The supernatant (700 μL) containing the polar metabolites was collected in new 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. For lipid removal, a volume of 700 μL of dichloromethane and 50 μL of water was then added to the supernatant. For a detailed description of metabolites extraction please see Mung et al. [19].

For lipidomics analysis, 10 µL of each breast milk sample was aliquoted in 1 mL Eppendorf tubes and stored in a − 80 °C freezer until use. Samples were treated with 10 µL 0.9% w/v NaCl, and 120 µL chloroform/methanol (2:1) containing 14 internal standards (SPLASH Lipidomix stable isotope labeled internal standards; 2.5 µg/mL for all). The mixture was vortexed and precipitated on ice for 30 min followed by centrifugation at 1000 g for 3 min at 4 °C. Sixty µL supernatant of the lipid-containing chloroform phase (lower level) was extracted to a glass HPLC vial containing 60 µL chloroform: methanol (1:1) [20].

Quality control

For a detailed description of the quality control performed during analyses please see Gnanasekaran et al. [18].

Analysis by ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS)

Untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics profiling were performed using an Ultrahigh Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system (Agilent 1290 Infinity II) connected to a Bruker timsTOF Pro™ instrument equipped with trapped ion mobility spectrometer (TIMS) coupled to a hybrid quadrupole, time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TOF-MS; Bruker, Bremen, Germany). Ions were generated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization mode. The ESI source used 10 L/min of drying gas at a temp of 220 °C. The ESI was set at 4500 V and 3600 V capillary voltage for pos and neg mode, respectively, and a 2.2 bar nebulizer pressure. Detection of the mass/charge ratio (m/z) of ions was set from 50 to 1000 over 17 min. To facilitate the compound identifications, QC samples were analyzed by auto MS/MS. The absolute threshold was set to 1000 counts. MS and MS/MS spectra acquisition rates were set to 2 and 4 Hz, respectively, with a total cycle time of 1 sec for precursor ions collection. The collision energy in MS/MS varied between 10 eV to 60 eV.

For a detailed description of the metabolomics analysis, please see Gnanasekaran et al. [18].

For lipidomics analysis, the samples were randomized and analyzed using reversed-phase ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 Column, 130Å, 1.7 µm, 2.1 mm X 100 mm (Waters, Milford, MA). The column and auto-sampler temperatures were maintained at 50 and 6 °C, respectively. Solvent A consisting of water + 1% Ammonium acetate (1 M) + 0.1% v/v formic acid and solvent B consisting of Acetonitrile: Isopropanol (1:1, v/v) + 1% Ammonium acetate (1 M) + 0.1% Formic acid (3:1, v/v) were used as mobile phases. The injection volume and flow rate were 1 μL and 0.4 mL/min, respectively. The mobile phase gradient was programmed as follows: The gradient was: 0–2 min 35–80% B; 2–7 min 80–100% B; and 7–14 min 100% B, followed by a 4 min re-equilibration period under the initial conditions (35% B) [20].

MS-data processing for metabolomics and lipidomics analysis

Data acquisition was controlled by the otofControl software version 6.0 and Bruker Compass HyStar version 5.0 (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). For a detailed description of the MS-data processing, see Gnanasekaran et al. [18].

Detected molecular features were annotated in two steps. First masses and retention times were compared with an in-house library based on authentic standards. Secondly, the recorded MS/MS spectra in MetaboScape were annotated using SmartFormula and by comparing with the in-house MS/MS spectral library, LipidBlast, Bruker HMDB Metabolite Library, Bruker MetaboBASE Personal Library 3.0, MoNA, and MSDIAL-TandemMassSpectralAtlas using a confidence limit of 5 mDa for parent mass tolerance. In total, the UHPLC–MS untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics analyses detected 219 annotated metabolites and 172 annotated lipids, and 21,434 and 14,278 non-annotated metabolite and lipid mass features, respectively. Statistical analyses were performed on the annotated metabolites and lipids. Lipid species with more than 85% of values below the detection limit were excluded from further analyses. For remaining lipid species, values below the detection limit were treated as left-censored and imputed using median substitution to balance retention of biologically relevant species with minimization of noise from sparsely detected features and to enable multivariate and pathway analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD or ± SEM or 95% confidence intervals [95% CI], median and interquartile range (IQR), or frequencies and proportions. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.6.1) [21], GraphPad Prism (version 9, GraphPad Software), Metaboanalyst (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca, version 4.0), JMP (version 12, SAS Institute Inc.), and Microsoft Excel (version MSO (16.0.14326.21170) 32-bit). Statistical significance defined as nominal p-value < 0.05 (or -Log10 p-value > 1.3) are reported for nominal secondary analyses, while false discovery rate (FDR) q-values (Benjamini-Hochberg, q < 0.1) were used to assess significance after correction for multiple testing. Global metabolite and lipid analyses included two-way hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distances and Ward’s clustering), principal component analyses (PCA) and volcano plots showing log 2 fold differences to CON (p-value < 0.05). Initially, global analyses were conducted using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction method to address multiple testing (q < 0.1); however, no metabolites or lipids were significant at q < 0.1 after FDR correction; Log 2 fold change (Log2FC) was calculated as the log2-transformed ratio vs. CON (Supplementary Table 1 shows this in more detail [see Additional file 1]). Subsequently, we performed nominal cluster analysis, which enabled us to narrow our focus on smaller subsets of metabolites and lipids. Normalized peak areas were log transformed and Z-scores were calculated for each variable, then used for two-way hierarchical clustering, PCA, as well as cluster analysis based on heatmap, area under the receiver operator characteristic (AUROC) curves, and enrichment analyses. Between group differences in normalized peak area of specific metabolites were determined using Kruskal-Wallis tests with Wilcoxon rank sum test with Holm-corrected p-values for pairwise comparisons.

For the lipid analysis, variables with >85% missing values were filtered out from the analysis (below the detection limit) and the remaining species were then used as input for subsequent multivariate analyses. Lipid z-scores were used to perform a soft clustering analysis [22] to identify enrichment of lipid features per group. Normalized Z-scores were analyzed with 1-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. Lipid enrichment analysis was performed in LION (http://www.lipidontology.com) using 1-way ANOVA F-test p-values (all conditions vs. CON) [23, 24]. Distributions of all LION-terms over the ranked list and compared to uniform distributions by using 1-tailed Kolmogorov-Smirnov as previously described [23, 24]. Enriched LION terms are significant when associated lipids are higher ranked than expected by chance, followed by adjustment for multiple testing using FDR, reporting both nominal p-values and FDR-adjusted q-values. Lipid reaction network analysis was performed using BIOPAN (https://lipidmaps.org/biopan/) as previously described [25, 26] by calculating statistical Z-scores for all possible lipid pathways to predict lipid conversions in intervention groups compared to controls (vs. CON). Reactions with higher Z-scores were classified as active or predicted. Triglyceride (TG) species analysis was done using the total acyl chain length (x-axis) and double bonds (y-axis), and log 2 fold changes and -Log10 p-values (vs. CON).

Spearman correlation was used for analyses of correlations between normalized values of those individual metabolites and lipids, and those physical activity measures during pregnancy (moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity and active min) and body weight measures (maternal weight 7–14 days postpartum and pre-pregnancy BMI) that were identified to possibly correlate based on visual inspection of correlograms performed in R. Missing physical activity data due to non-wear time were imputed by multiple imputations in 25 data sets using a prespecified seed, preselected baseline variables (body weight, age, physical activity, education level, and parity), and the random forest imputation model from the mice R package [13, 27], and physical activity data used for linear regression analyses were the average imputed values from randomization to delivery.

Breast milk metabolite analysis

We utilized untargeted metabolomic profiling to identify metabolite adaptations to maternal exercise interventions (Fig. 2). Two-way hierarchical clustering of normalized metabolites showed dispersed differences in metabolite normalized peak areas in both EXE and MOT compared to CON, but no overall differences were identified in response to EXE or MOT (Fig. 2A). PCA showed no clear separation among groups, suggestive of no distinct influence of the two interventions on metabolic perturbations of metabolites (Fig. 2B). We then probed possible exercise-induced changes in individual milk metabolites and visualized them as a volcano plot, compared to CON (Log 2 fold scale with p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C, D). Compared to CON, in EXE, 1,7-Dimethyluric acid was higher (p = 0.03), and p-cresol sulfate and xanthurenate were lower (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). In MOT, nine metabolites were higher, including 1,7-Dimethyluric acid (p = 0.02), compared to CON (Fig. 2D). Neither 3′-SL nor 12,13-diHOME differed in EXE or MOT compared to CON (Fig. 2C, D). Our analysis identified that the caffeine byproduct 1,7-Dimethyluric acid was higher in both EXE and MOT. While no major differences were observed across groups, we utilized nominal p-values to further stratify the metabolite dataset into 6 subclusters (Fig. 2A), which identify metabolite pathways that are representative of each treatment group.

For all six metabolite clusters identified, Z-scores were calculated and visualized to assess differences between groups. Our analysis identified important differences in EXE and MOT compared to CON in clusters 1–5 (Fig. 2E–L and Supplementary Figure 1 [Additional file 2]), thus we focused on these clusters to recognize physiologically relevant metabolite subsets. Compared with CON, in both EXE and MOT, higher levels of metabolites in cluster 1 were observed (Fig. 2E). Next, we tested the ability of cluster 1 metabolites to accurately discriminate between CON, EXE, and MOT using AUROC curves. Metabolites belonging to cluster 1 were able to distinguish between CON, EXE, and MOT (AUROC values CON = 0.87, EXE = 0.75, and MOT = 0.76) (Fig. 2F). Additionally, enrichment analyses of cluster 1 metabolites showed that the caffeine metabolism pathway, which might be involved in lipolysis during exercise, was enriched in EXE and MOT (combined -Log10 p = 2.01), compared to CON (Fig. 2G). This is in line with our global metabolite analysis (Fig. 2C, D) showing higher 1,7-Dimethyluric acid in EXE and MOT compared to CON (p < 0.05) and did not differ between EXE and MOT (Fig. 2H). In cluster 2 (Supplementary Figure 1A-C [Additional file 2]) and 3 (Supplementary Figure 1D-F [Additional file 2]), MOT differed from EXE and CON in opposing directions and only cluster 2 was enriched in tryptophan metabolites (Supplementary Figure 1B [Additional file 2]). Moreover, in cluster 4 (Fig. 2I–L) and 5 (Supplementary Figure 1 G-I [Additional file 2]), EXE differed from MOT and CON in opposing directions and good group prediction for cluster 4 (AUROC values CON = 0.81, EXE = 0.78, and MOT = 0.81) (Fig. 2J). Enrichment analysis of cluster 4 (Fig. 2K) showed numerous metabolic pathways that were higher in EXE, including citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), compared to CON and MOT (combined -Log10 p > 1.3). The metabolite oxoglutarate (also known as α-ketoglutarate, 2-ketoglutaric acid, 2-oxoglutarate, or oxoglutaric acid) accounted for the major differences in pathway enrichment with a higher normalized peak area in EXE compared to MOT (p = 0.04) (Fig. 2L). We tested whether the variables ‘day of collection’, ‘pre-pregnancy BMI’, ‘maternal weight 7–14 days postpartum’, and ‘parity’ influenced the normalized peak area of oxoglutarate and 1,7-Dimethyluric acid in the groups as a random nested factor. None of the tested variables significantly influenced the metabolite levels.

Correlation analysis showed no significant correlation between oxoglutarate and moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity with an R value of 0.03 and p-value of 0.81 (Fig. 4A). Similarly, no significant correlation was observed between oxoglutarate and maternal weight at day 7–14 postpartum (R = 0.16, p = 0.13; Fig. 4B).

Overall, while no major differences in metabolites were found after maternal exercise interventions compared to standard care, we observed higher normalized peak areas of 1,7-Dimethyluric acid and oxoglutarate after maternal exercise interventions in subsequent cluster analysis. Our analysis demonstrated that a small subset of metabolites, namely metabolites in cluster 1 and 4, separately enabled us to distinguish and stratify small metabolite differences between the three groups.

Breast milk lipid analysis

Similar to the metabolite analysis, no major global shifts in lipids were detected by PCA (Fig. 3A), but two-way hierarchical clustering of normalized relative abundance revealed differences in lipids between EXE and MOT compared with CON (Fig. 3B). Soft clustering analysis identified 3 major lipid clusters (Cluster 1, 2, 3) (Fig. 3C–E). Cluster 1 is defined by lipids that were significantly enriched in EXE and different from CON and MOT (Fig. 3C). This cluster of 39 lipids comprised monounsaturated triglyceride species (TG; red dots) and numerous phosphatidylethanolamines and plasmenylethanolamines species (PE, O-PE; cyan dots). Conversely, two clusters (Clusters 2 and 3) are defined by lipids that were significantly enriched in CON and different from EXE and MOT (Fig. 3D, E). These clusters of 46–49 lipids comprised polyunsaturated TGs, phosphatidylcholines (PC; blue dots), diacylglycerides (DG; orange dots) and sphingolipids (SM, SPB; green dots) among others (Fig. 3D, E). Thus, our findings demonstrated that while no major shifts in lipid classes were observed, the EXE intervention was associated with a distinct lipid signature including monounsaturated TG and phospholipids (PE).

To determine whether exercise interventions altered lipid pathways (in a steady state), we conducted lipid reaction analysis (BIOPAN) to measure lipid conversions and employed enrichment analysis (LION) to assess exercise-induced changes in lipid pathways. Reaction analysis showed a similar predicted conversion in EXE (Fig. 3F) and MOT (Fig. 3G) compared to CON, specifically conversion from diacylglycerol (DG) species to PC and PE and to TG (DG > PC, DG > PE, DG > TG) (Fig. 3F, G), as inferred by BioPAN Z-score pathway estimation. Expanded lipid reactions are shown in Supplementary Figure 2 [Additional file 3]. Moreover, EXE markedly (-Log10 q > 1) impacted lipid processes related to PE, plasmalogens (O-PE) and mitochondrial lipid pathways (Fig. 3H), whereas MOT resulted in non-significant (-Log10 q < 1) alterations to TG and lipid storage pathways (Fig. 3I). These findings highlight significant differences in lipid profiles between EXE and MOT particularly in relation to phospholipid differences.

Correlation analysis of lipids revealed statistically significant, positive relationships between PC(36:1) and active min per day (R = 0.23, p = 0.02) (Fig. 4C) indicating that this lipid is directly correlated to physical activity level. In addition, maternal weight at day 7–14 postpartum was negatively correlated with LPC(18:2) (R = −0.24, p = 0.02) (Fig. 4D) and pre-pregnancy BMI was negatively correlated with PG(33:0) (R = −0.24, p = 0.02) (Fig. 4E), indicating that higher levels of these lipids correlated with lower maternal weight measures. No correlations were found between PC(36:2) and active min per day (R = 0.13, p = 0.19) (Fig. 4F) or maternal weight 7–14 days postpartum (R = −0.12, p = 0.25) (Fig. 4G).

In summary, our findings did not uncover major lipid differences resulting from maternal exercise interventions compared to standard care. Nevertheless, in nominal analyses we did observe lower normalized peak areas for specific individual lipids, mainly within phospholipid and plasmalogen species (O-PE), in response to maternal exercise interventions. Subsequent analyses of smaller lipid subsets showed an overall reduction of TG species in EXE, a similar BioPAN-inferred enrichment of diacylglycerol-to-phospholipid conversion (PC and PE) pathway after both interventions, and that the two interventions impacted different lipid processes and pathways. Lastly, PC(36:1) correlated positively with physical activity level.

Discussion

In the present randomized controlled trial, we used UHPLC–MS untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics to investigate the effects of two different prenatal exercise interventions (EXE and MOT) on the human breast milk metabolome and lipidome 7–14 days postpartum compared to standard care (CON). Overall, we found that neither EXE nor MOT elicits major differences in metabolites or lipids compared with CON after adjusting for multiple comparisons. However, subsequent nominal analyses focusing on smaller subsets of metabolites and lipids enabled us to distinguish small metabolite and lipid differences between groups: Cluster analysis of metabolites showed that normalized peak area of 1,7-Dimethyluric acid was higher in EXE and MOT compared to CON, and oxoglutarate was higher in EXE compared to MOT. Species-level analysis of TG species and reaction and enrichment analyses of lipid pathways showed an overall reduction of individual relative abundance of TGs in EXE, a similar predicted conversion from DG species to phospholipid species after both EXE and MOT compared to CON, and that the two interventions impacted different lipid processes and pathways.

Our global analysis findings of no major metabolite or lipid differences after prenatal exercise interventions might be explained by the rather low intervention adherence rate, with EXE participants attending only around half of the planned sessions on average. Moreover, we found a relatively low moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity level, of up to 1 hour per week only, among participants in EXE and MOT. Additionally, we recognize that the current population is healthy, has a low BMI, and no chronic disease indications.

Our data showed that three metabolites and seven lipids were changed in EXE compared to CON, whereas in MOT, nine metabolites were higher and 14 lipids were lower, compared to CON. While our study uncovered some changes in metabolites and lipids in response to exercise training during pregnancy, the numbers of regulated metabolites and lipids in EXE and MOT compared to CON were markedly lower than findings of metabolic changes in previous studies after exercise 28–30. However, these studies investigated the effects of an acute bout of exercise (within 24 hours after endurance or resistance exercise) on metabolic or lipidomic profile in non-pregnant human blood, urine or sweat [28–30].

Based on the metabolite cluster and enrichment analysis we reported that EXE changed metabolites related to several metabolic pathways, among them TCA cycle, gluconeogenesis, amino acid metabolism pathways, and urea cycle. Oxoglutarate or α-ketoglutarate accounted for the major differences in pathway enrichment of multiple metabolic pathways in EXE, and this metabolite was higher in EXE compared to MOT. Higher oxoglutarate abundance in breast milk in EXE might convey adaptations related to the TCA cycle intermediates. Other studies have also found increased oxoglutarate in plasma and urine after both acute endurance and resistance exercise [28, 30], indicating, for example, increased TCA cycle activity and ATP turnover with exercise. A recent study showed that supplementation of α-ketoglutarate to drinking water increased circulating levels of α-ketoglutarate and improved hepatic glucose metabolism, increased lean mass, and decreased blood glucose concentration, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fat mass and body weight in high-fat diet fed mice [31]. Thus, it could be hypothesized that drinking breast milk with increased oxoglutarate/α-ketoglutarate concentration as a result of increased physical activity performed by the mother during pregnancy or postpartum could increase circulating α-ketoglutarate levels in the offspring and induce positive effects on offspring metabolism similar to those shown by Yuan et al. [31]. In contrast, we found no correlation between oxoglutarate and moderate-to-vigorous-intensity physical activity level per se in our study.

Further, we found caffeine metabolism to be enriched in EXE and MOT combined compared to CON. The caffeine biproduct 1,7-Dimethyluric acid was one of the metabolites driving the enrichment of caffeine metabolism with maternal exercise. We showed that the normalized peak area of 1,7-Dimethyluric acid was higher in EXE and MOT compared to CON, supporting the increased caffeine metabolism in human breast milk after maternal exercise during pregnancy. Caffeine might mobilize fatty acids to provide energy by stimulating lipolysis during exercise, as indicated by studies of caffeine supplementation prior to exercise [32]. Caffeine metabolism has been shown to be increased in serum in young males after marathon running [33], but neither the marathon running study, nor our breast milk study controlled for intake of dietary caffeine. Thus, it may be plausible that the increased caffeine metabolism could result from exogenous caffeine intake.

Regarding changes in lipids, our data indicated enrichment of monounsaturated TGs and phospholipid species in the EXE group compared to CON. Increased levels of phospholipids, including O-PE, PE, LPC and PC, after acute exercise have been reported in previous lipidomic studies of plasma, muscle and liver tissue in humans [29] and animals [34, 35]. The findings from these studies are in line with our data showing a positive correlation between PC(36:1) and physical activity level. The most abundant lipid classes in human breast milk are TG and phospholipids [36], but our study did not quantify absolute lipid abundance. Our species-level analysis of milk TG showed an overall reduction in relative abundance of medium- and long-chain polyunsaturated TG in EXE and MOT, compared to CON. This is in line with previous studies showing lower circulating levels of TG in pregnant women after prenatal physical activity [37, 38]. The impact on milk TG species in our study could be related to our findings of predicted conversion from DG species to phospholipids in EXE compared to CON. In contrast, milk TG concentration in rats was not affected after low intensity exercise throughout pregnancy and lactation in a previous study [39]. It is possible that the difference in the number of lipid species with exercise is due to a difference in breast milk volume, but to our knowledge no other studies have indicated that prenatal exercise affects milk volume [40, 41] and we did not measure amount of milk produced. Another study that looked at breast milk composition after acute exercise in humans did not appear to measure quantity of milk produced [10].

The observed differential patterns of lipid regulation in EXE and MOT might suggest distinct maternal adaptations to the mode of physical activity and merit further discussion. To our knowledge, no other studies have focused on the impact of different exercise modalities and intensities on breast milk and offspring outcomes. Another study showed differential metabolomic response to aerobic and resistance exercise in plasma from young healthy males [30], and further studies are needed to explore if the same apply to both milk and offspring outcomes [42].

In our study, we found no changes in the metabolite 3’-SL or the lipid 12,13-diHOME with EXE or MOT compared to CON. This is in contrast to previous studies on human breast milk that have showed that average maternal physical activity measured with accelerometry three times during pregnancy correlated with 3’-SL concentration [8], and that 12,13-diHOME concentration was increased after postpartum acute maternal exercise [10]. Our findings might be explained by the relatively low intervention adherence rate and physical activity level in the FitMum study. Moreover, in the present study breast milk was transitional milk obtained 7–14 days postpartum, whereas previous studies have sampled mature milk later postpartum (after 15 days) [8, 10]. When comparing findings from studies of human breast milk it might be important to consider sampling time point, which possibly influence study findings, as evidenced in studies showing that colostrum, transitional and mature human breast milk differed in phospholipid and TG composition [43, 44]. Future studies should investigate how prenatal as well as postpartum exercise might influence several stages of human breast milk and follow the changes in milk composition over a period of months.

Strengths and limitations

In this study, we standardized breast milk sampling by providing the participants with thorough verbal and written instructions on how and when to sample at home 7–14 days postpartum. However, collecting breast milk in the relatively wide range of day 7–14 postpartum may be a weakness of the study since changes in breast milk composition occur during this period. Further, we did not include any restrictions on exercise or fasting prior to sampling, as seen in other studies applying metabolomics and lipidomics analyses to investigate metabolic response to acute exercise [10, 29, 30, 45]. Human metabolome and lipidome are dynamically changing and metabolomics/lipidomics analyses do only provide a molecular snapshot of phenotypic traits [14]. Therefore, it might be important to control for confounding factors such as medication use in relation to delivery [46], maternal nutrition [47–49], and maternal obesity, which has been associated with changes in the human milk metabolome [50, 51]. Moreover, delivery mode as well as exercise and diet prior to sampling may also be confounding factors that might blur potential effects of maternal exercise training during pregnancy on the metabolome and lipidome in breast milk. However, a strength of our study is that it offered the possibility to investigate the effects of prenatal exercise on the human breast milk metabolome and lipidome in a real-life setting. It is however uncertain if the demonstrated changes in metabolites/lipids are in fact due to the exercise interventions in our randomized controlled trial.

A key limitation of the present study is the exploratory nature of the untargeted omic analyses. High-dimensional metabolomic and lipidomic data inherently increase the risk of false-positive findings, particularly when effect sizes are small and statistical power is limited. Although we applied FDR correction for global analyses, no metabolites or lipids remained significant after adjustment, and subsequent subgroup, cluster, and pathway-based analyses should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Thus, some observed differences may reflect chance findings rather than a true biological effect, which in turn requires validation in independent cohorts or targeted analyses.

The cohort in the present study was relatively fit as indicated by being primarily in the normal BMI range, mean age of 31 years, non-smoking during pregnancy and likely more motivated to increase physical activity during pregnancy given their interest in participation in the study, compared to other pregnant women. Thus, our findings were observed in the absence of obesity and it is not known if these changes will apply to a cohort where the population has advanced maternal age, or who has obesity or metabolic disease. Our cohort being relatively fit may also have resulted in lower potential for exercise to induce changes in the breast milk metabolome and lipidome in our cohort compared to pregnant women in countries with a more pronounced overweight and obesity burden in pregnant women, for example the United States [52]. A limitation is that pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated based on self-reported pre-pregnancy weight obtained at inclusion (GA ≤ 15 weeks and 0 days), which may potentially have increased the risk of recall bias [53]. The proportion of nulliparous participants seemed to be higher in the EXE group, which could be a reason for the slightly higher physical activity level in EXE [54, 55]. A sensitivity analysis performed in our entire FitMum study population investigating if parity influenced intervention effects on physical activity level showed no different results from the primary analysis of effect of interventions on physical activity level. Hence, we did not consider the distribution of nulliparous participants in our study as a limitation and given the randomized controlled design of the FitMum study we also assumed random distribution between groups with regards to baseline characteristics like parity.

Conclusions

This study provides exploratory comprehensive untargeted metabolomic and lipidomic profiling comparing the effects of two different prenatal exercise interventions on metabolite and lipid changes in human breast milk 7–14 days postpartum. Overall, prenatal exercise interventions did not elicit metabolite or lipid differences compared to standard care (CON) after adjusting for multiple comparisons. However, in subsequent nominal analyses focusing on smaller subsets of metabolites and lipids we found subtle changes in certain metabolites and lipids in human breast milk after both structured supervised exercise training (EXE) and motivational counselling on physical activity (MOT) during pregnancy compared to CON. Future research is needed to establish whether exercise-induced adaptations to breast milk constitute an underlying mechanism for improved offspring health.
