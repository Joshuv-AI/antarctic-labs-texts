Low diagnostic yield of presurgical genetic testing in adult patients with epilepsy
Authors: Clara Jünemann, Amanda Stuart, Navprabhjot Kaur, Samuel Wiebe, Nathalie Jette, Shaily Singh, Felippe Borlot, Susanne Knake, The Calgary Comprehensive Epilepsy Program Collaborators, Ping Yee Billie Au, Karl Martin Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the diagnostic yield of genetic testing in patients undergoing presurgical evaluation for epilepsy.MethodsWe conducted a cohort study including 115 adult patients who underwent presurgical evaluation in the Calgary Epilepsy Program between 2019 and 2023 and who had undergone research exome sequencing. A curated epilepsy gene panel comprising 765 Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM)‐listed epilepsy‐associated genes was applied. Variants were classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines and assessed for clinical relevance and association with postsurgical outcomes.ResultsPathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in DEPDC5, NPRL2, KCNT2, and PRRT2 were identified, respectively, in 4 individuals (3.5%, 4/115). All variants met stringent quality criteria with high pathogenicity scores (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) 34–37) and absent or extremely low population frequencies in gnomAD v4.1. None of these patients had intellectual disability, and only one patient (PRRT2) had a positive family history. The patient with the KCNT2 variant underwent epilepsy surgery with good outcome (Engel class ID).SignificanceThis presurgical cohort demonstrates a low diagnostic yield of genetic testing in adult epilepsy surgery candidates. However, three of four patients with (likely) pathogenic variants did not have features that would have prompted clinical genetic testing, indicating that their genetic diagnosis would have been missed based on typical clinical genetic testing criteria in many jurisdictions.Keywords: epilepsy, exome sequencing, genetic testing, presurgical, surgery

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most common chronic neurological disorders, and despite advances in anti‐seizure therapy and adequate treatment, about one‐third of patients with epilepsy remain drug resistant. 1 For these individuals, epilepsy surgery represents a key therapeutic option. Recent studies have suggested incorporating genetic testing into the presurgical workup. However, most available data derive from pediatric cohorts 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 or convenience samples of patients already selected for clinical genetic testing. 6 , 7 , 8 Consequently, unbiased evaluation of adult presurgical cohorts is needed to clarify whether indication for genetic testing should be expanded to all surgical candidates.

Few studies have systematically examined postsurgical outcomes in patients with genetic epilepsies, and reported results vary widely, likely depending on the underlying gene. 9 Interpretation is further limited by selection bias, cancellation of surgery after a genetic diagnosis, and the predominance of pediatric series. Small series of patients with pathogenic variants in the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway suggest favorable surgical outcomes, 8 , 9 , 10 yet sample sizes remain too small for firm conclusions.

Overall, it remains unknown how often routine presurgical genetic testing in adults identifies clinically relevant variants or influences surgical decision‐making and outcome prediction. Hence, genetic testing is not routinely incorporated into the presurgical workup in many adult epilepsy centers. With this study, we aimed to determine the diagnostic yield of genetic testing in patients undergoing presurgical evaluation for epilepsy.

METHODS

All consenting outpatients and inpatients with epilepsy at Foothills Medical Centre, Calgary, were prospectively recruited from 2019 to 2023. From this cohort we selected patients who had been admitted to one of our seizure monitoring units for presurgical evaluation, either before or after recruitment (n = 151). The aim of the admission was to devise a resective or ablative surgical approach. If this was not possible, neuromodulation was considered. Patients admitted purely for non‐surgical purposes were excluded. Eighty‐eight patients with focal epilepsy, genetic generalized epilepsy, and developmental and epileptic encephalopathy received exome sequencing as part of the Epi25 collaborative. 11 An additional 27 patients underwent exome sequencing within the Rational antiepileptic drug selection by combining gene network and ICT analysis (RAISE‐GENIC) consortium based on being responders or non‐responders to anti‐seizure medication (ASM) independent of epilepsy type. 12 Hence, among all pre‐surgical patients, 115 of 151 (76%) underwent research exome sequencing. Of the remaining 36 patients who did not undergo research exome sequencing, 3 had clinical genetic testing (2 targeted gene panels, and 1 chromosomal microarray). The final diagnosis was determined based on the results of the presurgical evaluation.

Exome sequencing reads were aligned to the human genome build GRCh38/UCSC hg38, and variant calling was performed using the integrated Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics (DRAGEN) platform (version 4.2). Variant annotation and filtering were conducted with the Geneyx Analysis platform (version 6.1). Data were stored in Variant Call Format (VCF) at the University of Calgary and processed using a validated pipeline applied in clinical diagnostics. A curated epilepsy gene panel was applied to the exome backbone, comprising 765 Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM)‐listed epilepsy‐associated genes. Variants with <10 supporting reads, a minor allele frequency >.01% in gnomAD v4.1, or classified as benign/likely benign in ClinVar (2024–12) were excluded. Additional filters included predicted inheritance patterns, in silico predictor scores, and overlap with relevant Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms. Variants were classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, 13 and previously reported likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants were flagged. Diagnostic yield was determined for all likely pathogenic and pathogenic variants and was defined as the percentage of patients harboring ≥1 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant(s) (per ACMG criteria) among all patients who received genetic testing. Associations between identified monogenic etiologies and postsurgical outcomes were assessed based on clinical outcome and seizure frequency.

RESULTS

Our presurgical epilepsy cohort consisted of 151 patients of whom 115 (76%) received research exome testing (Table 1). The majority of these had focal epilepsy (106/115, 92%). A small percentage had been admitted for presurgical evaluation but was eventually confirmed to have generalized epilepsy (8/115, 7%) or combined generalized and focal epilepsy (1/115, .9%). Of those tested, 55% (63/115) had nonlesional epilepsy. Structural epilepsy was present in 52 of 115 (45%), with hippocampal sclerosis being the most frequent structural cause (21/115, 18%) followed by focal cortical dysplasia (7/115, 6%) and other malformations of cortical development (6/115, 5%). An acquired etiology due to traumatic or non‐traumatic intracranial hemorrhage or lesion, perinatal postischemic or hypoxic lesion, vascular malformation, or immune‐mediated lesion was present in 10 of 115 patients (9%). Other lesions accounted for the remaining 10 lesional cases (9%).

Exome sequencing with gene panel analysis identified four pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants and three variants of uncertain significance (VUSs). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected in established epilepsy genes, including nonsense variants in DEPDC5 (c.2512C>T; p.R838*) and NPRL2 (c.121C>T; p.Q41*), a frameshift variant in PRRT2 (c.649dup; p.R217Pfs*8), as well as a splice donor loss in KCNT2 (c.1783+2T>A). Pathogenicity scores were high (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) 34–37; Muttaster 1; SpliceAI 0.99), and population frequencies were absent or extremely low in gnomAD v4.1. In addition to classic loss‐of‐function variants, missense variants of uncertain significance were observed, including RELN (c.2690A>G; p.D897G), RORB (c.1196A>C; p.N399T), and a further NPRL2 variant (c.473A>C; p.K158T), with CADD scores from 21.2 to 26.6 and REVEL scores up to .91 (Table 2). All identified variants met stringent read depth and population exclusion criteria.

Overall, the diagnostic yield for (likely) pathogenic variants using exome sequencing was 3.5% (4/115). We collected phenotypic characteristics of the four patients with (likely) pathogenic and variants of uncertain significance (Table 2).

The patient carrying the DEPDC5 c.2512C>T; p.R838* variant was a 39‐year‐old woman who started having focal preserved and focal impaired consciousness seizures at the age of 15 years and focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures at age 24 years. EEG recordings captured interictal epileptiform discharges over the left anterior temporal and right posterior head regions. However, as no seizures were captured, seizure lateralization remained uncertain. The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not show an epileptogenic lesion. She had a nonspecific lesion at the right cervicomedullary junction. There was no evidence of intellectual disability or a familial history of epilepsy. The patient eventually became seizure‐free at age 32 years on lamotrigine and lacosamide. She did not undergo epilepsy surgery.

The patient harboring the NPRL2 c.121C>T; p.Q41* variant, was a 48‐year‐old woman who had focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures starting at age 10 years. From the age of 20 years, she additionally developed focal preserved consciousness seizures. Her video‐EEG monitoring showed intermittent left temporal rhythmic delta slowing, but no other interictal epileptiform discharges or seizures were captured. Her MRI was nonlesional. No intellectual disability or positive family history was reported. At the time of last follow‐up (48 years), she had been seizure‐free for 1 year on the ketogenic diet. No epilepsy surgery was performed.

The patient with the KCNT2 c.1783+2T>A variant was a 45‐year‐old woman who presented with focal impaired consciousness and focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures, with seizure onset at age 36 years. Scalp video‐EEG monitoring indicated a right temporal‐onset seizure. Her MRI showed a small left temporal encephalocele but no other epileptogenic lesion. Subsequently, she underwent stereo‐EEG monitoring, which indicated seizure onset in the right mesiotemporal region and right posterior insula. There was no family history of epilepsy, and the patient showed no evidence of intellectual disability. The patient underwent right temporal lobectomy and right posterior insula excision at age 44 years. She remained seizure‐free after surgery except for a cluster of seizures after missing antiseizure medications (Engel class ID, 19 months follow‐up).

The 67‐year‐old patient with the PRRT2 c.649dup variant had onset of focal preserved consciousness seizures at age 55 years, followed by focal impaired consciousness seizures at age 57 years. He did not have a clear history of infantile seizures. MRI demonstrated left‐sided mesial temporal sclerosis with ipsilateral amygdala enlargement. Scalp video‐EEG monitoring showed left temporal interictal epileptiform discharges, at times with a broad field and left frontopolar maximum. Seizure onset was left anterior temporal. Given his normal memory function, he underwent stereo‐EEG monitoring to clarify if a small memory‐sparing resection would be possible. However, the stereo‐EEG recording showed onset across the entire left mesiotemporal structures including the amygdala as well as anterior and posterior hippocampus with early left anterior insula involvement. Due to concerns for memory decline he did not undergo resection. Genetic testing had already been initiated for clinical reasons, prompted by a positive family history involving five first‐degree relatives affected by epilepsy. The PRRT2 variant was confirmed again using research exome. At the time of last follow‐up (67 years), he had been seizure‐free for 4 years on lamotrigine and levetiracetam.

Of the 36 presurgical patients who did not undergo research exome sequencing, three underwent clinical genetic testing with pathogenic results in two. A 32‐year‐old patient with mild intellectual disability and probable combined focal and generalized epilepsy with seizure onset at age 9 years had trisomy X. Stereo‐EEG monitoring was discussed but not initiated due to the low likelihood of identifying a resectable focus. Another 30‐year‐old patient with severe drug‐resistant focal epilepsy with presumed insular/opercular onset and a family history of focal epilepsy carried a likely pathogenic variant in KCNT1 (c.2882G>A, p.Arg961His). This family was published previously. 14 Based on the results of the video‐EEG monitoring, stereo‐EEG monitoring was considered but not performed.

DISCUSSION

We used exome sequencing to investigate a cohort of 115 patients, who had been admitted to the seizure monitoring unit for presurgical workup, and identified (likely) pathogenic variants in 4 of 115 (3.5%). Our results demonstrate a low yield of genetic testing in adult patients undergoing presurgical workup if no other criteria for genetic testing such as positive family history or presence of intellectual disability are applied.

Prior studies in adult presurgical cohorts reported a higher yield of genetic testing. A recent U.S. study reported pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 23 (10%) of 233 adult patients tested. 6 However, this study only reported on the subset of cases who had received genetic testing based on clinical indications. The size of the total cohort was not specified. A Dutch study from 2019 reported on a mixed cohort including 57% adults and 43% children who had undergone genetic testing, with a (likely) pathogenic variant identified in 40 (12%) of 325 tested patients. 8 Only 14% of the total presurgical cohort had received genetic testing. It is highly likely that the patients undergoing genetic testing were enriched for features that typically trigger clinical genetic testing, such as intellectual disability or family history, 15 , 16 which substantially increased the yield in these studies. Furthermore, the selection of patients for inclusion may have affected the results. We included patients admitted for presurgical workup independent of the final epilepsy diagnosis, resulting in the inclusion of some patients with generalized epilepsies. The Dutch study only included patients discussed in their presurgical case conference, 8 where patients not considered for surgery based on the video‐EEG monitoring results were presumably not presented. When including only patients with confirmed focal epilepsy and excluding acquired etiologies (such as trauma, stroke, infection, or immune cause), the yield would be 4.2% (4/96) in our cohort.

Previous studies in pediatric patients also reported substantially higher yield between 17.5%–42%, 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 which is likely related to the high percentage of patients with additional developmental delay, such as developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. These often have a genetic etiology, thus increasing the yield of genetic testing. 15 , 16 In our adult cohort, only 28/115 patients (24%) would have met criteria for clinical genetic testing (nonacquired cause and presence of intellectual disability or family history for epilepsy) with a yield of 3.6% (1/28). Our cohort was not selected for features that would trigger clinical genetic testing and, therefore, better represents the real‐world situation for patients admitted to a seizure monitoring unit for presurgical workup.

There is some potential for bias in our cohort, as not all patients admitted for presurgical video‐EEG monitoring fulfilled the inclusion criteria of the Epi25 collaborative and Rational antiepileptic drug selection by combining gene network and ICT analysis (RAISE‐GENIC) consortium. Nevertheless, 76% of our cohort underwent research exome sequencing. For focal epilepsies, the Epi25 cohort is enriched for nonlesional cases and hippocampal sclerosis. Some genetic variants are associated with other structural lesions, such as GATOR1 variants in focal cortical dysplasia. 17 A lower prevalence of patients with structural lesions other than hippocampal sclerosis (Table 1) may have reduced the yield in our study. When patients with and without genetic testing were compared in our cohort, there was a trend for a more frequent structural cause in the untested patients (64% vs 45%, p = .051; Table 1). The only characteristic that reached statistical significance was the presence of a vascular malformation, which was more prevalent in the untested subgroup (p = .029, Table 1). Nevertheless, our cohort included 12 patients (11%) with malformations of cortical development including focal cortical dysplasia, which is comparable to typical adult cohorts undergoing presurgical evaluation. 18 Therefore, we conclude that our results are representative for typical adult cohorts admitted for presurgical evaluation but may be an estimate at the lower end of the spectrum.

Clinical impact of identified gene mutations

Pathogenic variants in the cohort were identified in the DEPDC5, NPRL2, KCNT2, and PRRT2 genes. DEPDC5 and NPRL2 pathogenic variants lead to enhanced mTORC1 signaling and underlie familial focal epilepsy with variable foci including a spectrum of focal epilepsies, often associated with focal cortical dysplasia or hemimegalencephaly. 17 , 19 Both patients included in our cohort had nonlesional focal epilepsy. This is consistent with the phenotypic spectrum of DEPDC5 and NPRL2 and confirms that such variants can also occur in epilepsies without MRI‐detected lesions. However, it remains unclear if small dysplastic lesions may be present that could not be identified on MRI. KCNT2 variants represent a rare cause of developmental and epileptic encephalopathy characterized by variable developmental delay, epilepsy, and neurological deficits. 20 Loss‐of‐function variants in KCNT2, as observed in our patient, have been described to be associated with a milder phenotype with less severe developmental delay and focal epilepsy as opposed to developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. 20 Hence, the phenotype observed in our patient was on the mild end of the spectrum with absence of developmental delay. Variants in PRRT2 are associated with self‐limited familial infantile epilepsy. 21 The phenotype of our patient with the PRRT2 variant (mesial temporal sclerosis with amygdala enlargement) is atypical for this gene but this patient had a strong family history consistent with self‐limited familial infantile epilepsy. Later epilepsy onset has rarely been described in patients with PRRT2 pathogenic variants. 22 , 23 It remains unclear if the adult‐onset seizures in our patient are part of the phenotypic spectrum of PRRT2 or occurred independently of the genetic variant.

Of interest, only one of the four patients with pathogenic variants in our cohort showed features that would have triggered clinical genetic testing (positive family history of epilepsy in first‐degree relatives). The other three had no intellectual disability, developmental delay, or family history of epilepsy. Conversely, of the 111 patients without a pathogenic variant, 35 had features that would have triggered clinical genetic testing (positive family history of epilepsy, intellectual disability). This suggests that relying on typical clinical indications for genetic testing may miss patients with a genetic cause for their epilepsy and does not increase yield (1/36, 2.8%).

VUSs were found in NPRL2, RELN, and RORB. The missense variant in NPRL2 remained a VUS as it was unknown if it resulted in a loss of function and segregation data was not available. The RELN variant was not considered as pathogenic as it was heterozygous and the typical mode of inheritance for RELN is recessive. There is weak evidence that heterozygous variants may be linked to autosomal dominant lateral temporal lobe epilepsy and generalized epilepsy. 24 , 25 Pathogenic RORB variants have been described in photosensitive generalized and occipital lobe epilepsies, 26 which was not consistent with the patient's phenotype.

Only the patient with the KCNT2 variant underwent epilepsy surgery and had a good outcome. It is unlikely that the decision to proceed with surgery would have been changed if the variant had been known presurgically. The patient with the PRRT2 variant was not considered for surgery due to concerns of memory decline with a left mesiotemporal resection. The genetic cause was known before proceeding with the stereo‐EEG monitoring and did not influence the surgical decision. The other two patients were seizure‐free at the last follow‐up and, therefore, further presurgical workup was not pursued. However, based on prior data of successful epilepsy surgery in patients with GATOR1 variants, 6 , 9 , 10 , 27 further workup toward possible epilepsy surgery should be considered if seizures recur. Nevertheless, this finding highlights that patients with a pathogenic genetic variant may respond well to ASMs and do not necessarily develop drug‐resistant epilepsy.

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates low diagnostic yield for genetic testing in adult presurgical epilepsy patients. Future studies will need to clarify if the yield in an unbiased presurgical cohort is higher than in unselected patients with nonacquired focal epilepsy. However, as three of the four patients with identified (likely) pathogenic variants did not have any features that would have prompted clinical genetic testing, their genetic diagnosis would have been missed if no systematic testing of this cohort had been performed. Although genetic testing could be recommended for this cohort based on these findings, the identified diagnoses were unlikely to have influenced patient outcomes. Any recommendation for systematic genetic testing in the adult presurgical population should therefore be weighed against available health care resources, and cost‐effectiveness of routine genetic testing in adult presurgical patients needs to be formally evaluated.
