Reframing Left Atrial Dissection
Authors: Edoardo Zancanaro, Roberto Lorusso, Tommaso Hinna Danesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: atrioventricular groove rupture, heart, left atrial dissection, mitral valve surgery, multimodality imaging, structural heart intervention

Atrioventricular (AV) groove rupture remains among the most feared complications of mitral valve surgery. Historically described as type I left ventricular (LV) rupture, a tear at the posterior AV junction immediately behind the mitral annulus, this lesion has been associated with intraoperative mortality exceeding 75% in surgical series, with bailout limited to immediate cardiopulmonary bypass and direct repair under hemodynamic catastrophe.1,2 When the rupture is contained by the atrial wall, blood may dissect within the left atrial myocardium, producing a false lumen, left atrial dissection (LatD), that can remain hemodynamically silent until acute decompensation. LatD and overt type I LV rupture should therefore be considered a single anatomopathological continuum rather than separate entities, with the contained variant offering a critical, but narrow, therapeutic window.3,4

In this case of JACC: Case Reports, Cristiano et al2 report the first fully percutaneous transaortic closure of a covered AV groove rupture complicated by LatD after bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement.5 Their case is more than a procedural success: it crystallizes a paradigm shift in which a historically near-fatal complication may be addressed through a structured, imaging-driven, heart team–led bailout strategy.

Three features deserve closer scrutiny:

A 2-driver model of false-lumen perfusion: Even after the high-pressure LV-to-FL communication was occluded, the false lumen continued to be perfused retrogradely from the right atrium through a residual interatrial septal fenestration. This dual-source pattern, a primary high-pressure ventricular jet plus a secondary low-pressure interatrial shunt, indicates that the dissecting cavity, once established, may be sustained by hemodynamically heterogeneous inflows. Operators should anticipate this when planning sequential closure and accept that ostensibly minor associated defects may dictate therapeutic completeness.

Off-label device repurposing guided by computed tomography: The choice of a muscular ventricular septal defect occluder rather than a paravalvular leak plug or a duct occluder reflects mechanically informed decision-making. The double-disc configuration provided bidirectional sealing along a serpiginous tunnel, while the 7-mm waist length was selected to match the longitudinal extent of the defect on cardiac computed tomography (CCT). In a region where the coronary sinus and right coronary artery course within millimeters of the deployment zone, submillimeter sizing is not optional. CCT-derived landing-zone planning, transposed from transcatheter aortic valve replacement and paravalvular leak closure workflows, here proved indispensable for a lesion with no dedicated device.

The retrograde transaortic route: Compared with transseptal or transapical alternatives, the retrograde approach offered 3 advantages: direct access to the high-pressure ventricular side of the defect (favoring stable disc apposition), avoidance of the already-fragile interatrial septum, and complete reliance on standard percutaneous coronary intervention infrastructure. This last point is nontrivial in extremis, when assembling complex hybrid setups is impractical.

These technical achievements were enabled by 2 nonprocedural prerequisites that deserve as much editorial emphasis as the device choice itself. First, multimodality imaging functioned not as an adjunct but as an architecture: intraoperative 2-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) made the diagnosis, serial 3-dimensional TEE quantified anatomical evolution and identified early LV inflow compromise, CCT defined the tunnel and its perilous neighbors, and intraprocedural TEE-fluoroscopy fusion guided deployment.6,7 Second, a structured heart team converted imaging information into action under conditions of profound therapeutic uncertainty, balancing the prohibitive risk of redo surgery against the experimental nature of percutaneous bailout, a balance unattainable without a culture of multidisciplinary commitment to the individual patient.8

Important questions remain (Table 1). The long-term durability of repurposed septal occluders implanted into postsurgical, friable, evolving tissue is unknown; late hemolysis, device dislodgement, and residual or recurrent shunting deserve systematic surveillance. The relationship between AV groove rupture and identifiable preoperative or intraoperative risk markers, including mitral annular calcification burden, transseptal vs transatrial surgical access, and prosthesis sizing relative to the atriovalvular junction, requires prospective characterization. A dedicated multicenter registry of LatD and contained AV groove rupture, capturing conservative, surgical, and percutaneous trajectories, would generate the selection criteria a single case cannot provide. Finally, the upstream question, prevention through annular reinforcement or modified surgical technique in vulnerable anatomy, has been overshadowed by the rescue narrative and warrants renewed attention.

For now, Cristiano et al have demonstrated that what was once an almost uniformly catastrophic surgical event may, in selected hemodynamically tolerant patients with progressive false-lumen expansion and prohibitive surgical risk, be neutralized in the catheterization laboratory. The lesson is not that percutaneous bailout should replace surgery, but that the spectrum of viable strategies has widened, and that imaging integration, device repurposing, and heart team discipline are the 3 pillars that have made this widening possible. Future iterations of valvular heart disease guidelines should begin to acknowledge this expanded armamentarium, even as we await the multicenter evidence to formalize it.
