DNA methylation signatures distinguish extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma and EBV-positive nodal T/NK-cell lymphoma and identify prognostic subgroups
Authors: The Phyu, Sai Mun Leong, Shangying Chen, Wenjun Gu, Anja Fischer, Qiao-Yang Sun, Henry Yang, Nicholas L. Syn, Lichang Deng, Louis Ching Yi Low, Shih-Sung Chuang, Seiichi Kato, Joseph D. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) and primary Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-positive nodal T/NK-cell lymphoma (nodal-TNKL) are aggressive lymphomas with overlapping clinicopathologic features but distinct underlying biology. While their genomic landscapes have been increasingly defined, comparative epigenetic characterization remains limited. We performed methylated DNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeDIP-seq) on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples from ENKTL, nodal-TNKL, ENKTL cell lines, and control tissues. ENKTL displayed extensive promoter hypermethylation associated with repression of tumor suppressor genes, lineage regulators, and lymphoid signaling genes, including LEF1 and BANK1, together with focal hypomethylation of immune- and interferon-responsive genes such as IFITM1. In contrast, nodal-TNKL showed global hypomethylation, particularly affecting cytotoxicity, immune-response, and antigen-presentation pathways; TET2-mutated nodal-TNKL cases exhibited locus-specific methylation changes. Across all samples, global DNA methylation levels correlated with genomic instability. Unsupervised clustering identified two epigenetically distinct ENKTL subgroups, one characterized by higher global methylation, TP53 loss, increased copy number alterations and loss of heterozygosity and significantly poorer overall survival. Together, this study defines fundamental epigenetic differences between ENKTL and nodal-TNKL and links DNA methylation dynamics to genomic instability and clinical outcome, highlighting the value of methylation profiling for refined classification, risk stratification and therapeutic guidance.

Introduction

Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) and primary Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-positive nodal T- and NK-cell lymphoma (nodal-TNKL) are aggressive, EBV-associated lymphomas that share overlapping clinicopathologic features but differ in their underlying molecular biology. Recent studies have highlighted the unique molecular landscape of nodal-TNKL, including immune pathway activation and frequent mutations in genes involved in epigenetic regulation [1, 2]. Nodal-TNKL also exhibits lower genomic instability (GI) compared to ENKTL [1], and it is currently uncertain whether this reflects disruption of the DNA damage response during cancer progression [2, 3]. Despite these advances, the epigenetic mechanisms underlying ENKTL and nodal-TNKL remain poorly defined, and comparative DNA methylation profiling is lacking.

DNA methylation is a central regulator of gene expression, and in cancer its dysregulation extends beyond tumor suppressor silencing to reprogram lineage identity and developmental programs linked to plasticity, immune evasion, and therapy resistance [4, 5]. Accumulating evidence supports an important role for DNA methylation in ENKTL pathogenesis [6, 7]. Mutations in epigenetic modifier genes, such as TET2 (10.3%) and KMT2D (12.6%) have been reported in this disease [8]. Furthermore, genome-wide profiling has revealed a CpG-island methylator–like phenotype, with widespread promoter hypermethylation and silencing of tumor suppressor genes (TSGs), such as PRDM1, BCL2L11 (BIM), and PTPN6 (SHP1). Demethylating treatment in ENKTL models can restore their expression and induce growth inhibition, underscoring the central role of aberrant DNA methylation in this lymphoma [9–11].

Unlike ENKTL, the epigenetic landscape of nodal-TNKL remains largely unexplored. To date, no studies have systematically profiled DNA methylation in nodal-TNKL, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of its epigenetic regulation. This gap is particularly important given that nodal-TNKL is frequently associated with mutations in epigenetic regulators such as TET2 and/or DNMT3A in >60% of cases, and associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) [1, 12]. Comparative analysis of methylation patterns across ENKTL and nodal-TNKL is essential to define shared and disease-specific epigenetic programs in these EBV-associated lymphomas and to clarify how CHIP-associated mutations shape their epigenetic programs.

In this study, we performed methylated DNA immunoprecipitation and sequencing (MeDIP-Seq) on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples from patients with ENKTL, nodal-TNKL, and normal controls to characterize global methylation patterns, identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs), and assess biological differences between these two lymphomas. Our results reveal divergent methylation signatures between ENKTL and nodal-TNKL and uncover two epigenetically distinct subgroups within ENKTL with differential survival outcomes. These findings advance our understanding of the distinctive epigenetic landscape in ENKTL and nodal-TNKL, supporting refined molecular subtyping of ENKTL.

Materials and methods

Samples

This study included FFPE samples from patients diagnosed with ENKTL (n = 43, samples from 39 patients), nodal-TNKL (n = 12), and ENKTL-derived cell lines (n = 5). Twenty-eight control samples included FFPE tissues from reactive tonsils (n = 10), lymph nodes (n = 2), nasal mucosa (n = 6), and purified primary T (n = 5) and NK cells (n = 5) from peripheral blood. This study was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board B (2009/00212).

MeDIP-seq library preparation and sequencing

MeDIP-seq was performed following the published protocol [13] with modifications. Purified total genomic DNA (input amounts ≥ 100 ng) was fragmented using NEBNext Ultra II FS Enzyme provided in NEBNext® Ultra™ II FS DNA Library Prep Kit (Cat. No. E7805, E6177, New England Biolabs). The fragmented DNAs were ligated with NEBNext multiplex adaptors, followed by U excision of the adapter using the USER enzyme, and the ligated products were purified using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No./ID: 28006). Methylated DNA immunoprecipitation (MeDIP) was performed using the MagMeDIP kit (Diagenode). DNA was captured using an anti-5-methylcytosine monoclonal antibody (clone 33D3) and magnetic beads, then purified and PCR-amplified. The amplified libraries were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platform. Benchmarking of MeDIP-seq QSEA-derived beta estimates against Illumina EPIC v2 array methylation data was performed (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Data and statistical analysis

Genome-wide methylated regions and between-group differences were identified in R (v4.4.1–v4.4.2) using QSEA v1.30.0. DMRs were retained if adjusted p ≤ 0.01 and |log2FC | ≥1.5. DMRs with higher and lower methylation in the tumor than in the controls were classified as hyper-DMRs and hypo-DMRs, respectively. DMRs were annotated with ChIPseeker v1.38.0, and associated genes were defined as hyper-DMR–related genes (hyper-DMGs) or hypo-DMR–related genes (hypo-DMGs) and annotated using the hg38 human reference genome. Motif enrichment analysis was performed using Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif Enrichment analysis (HOMER). Promoter methylation values were correlated with gene expression from Clariom D microarrays. Genes with p ≤ 0.05 and r ≤ −0.4 were overlapped with ENKTL versus control differentially expressed genes.

Cell type deconvolution of MeDIP-seq and gene expression microarray data was performed using decemedip and CIBERSORT, respectively, to compare the relative immune subsets between the two ENKTL subgroups. DMRs were intersected with Roadmap consolidated chromatin states using NK cells (E046) and CD8 + T cells (E047). The chromatin states were categorized into promoter, enhancer, transcription, repressed polycomb and heterochromatin categories. Copy number (CN) profiles were generated using OncoScan® FFPE assay and CN alterations were analysed using GISTIC. Scores for loss of heterozygosity, Genomic instability (GI) and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) were calculated from the CN data as previously published [1].

Comparison of clinical and sample variables between ENKTL-group 1 and 2 was done using Mann–Whitney U, Fisher’s exact, or chi-square tests, and overall survival was analyzed using the log-rank test and Cox proportional hazards analysis.

For a detailed description of methods, please refer to the supplementary methods. The code used for the analyses is available upon request.

A total of 9232 DMRs, corresponding to 5068 differentially methylated genes (DMGs) (adjusted p ≤ 0.01, |log2FC | ≥1.5), were identified across ENKTL, nodal-TNKL, and controls and used for principal-component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering, adapted from previously described MeDIP-seq analyses [14]. Both approaches show distinct separation between ENKTL and nodal-TNKL (Fig. 1B, C), likely reflecting underlying biological differences. Notably, duplicate control samples processed in different MeDIP-seq batches (ENKTL3 and ENKTL17, ENKTL45 and ENKTL46 in Fig. 1C) clustered tightly together, indicating high cross-batch reproducibility. There was also no observable confounding effect of sample age on sample distribution (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Relative to controls, ENKTL samples showed widespread hypermethylation, consistent with prior reports [9]. In contrast to ENKTL, nodal-TNKL samples exhibited extensive hypomethylation (Fig. 1C), supporting their epigenetic distinction from ENKTL.

ENKTL is characterized by extensive hypermethylation affecting key genes and regulatory pathways

To elucidate the DNA methylation landscape of ENKTL, we compared ENKTL samples with all controls and identified 7301 DMRs (mean Δβ 0.26) using adjusted p ≤ 0.01 and |log2FC | ≥1.5, corresponding to 4195 DMGs (Supplementary Table S2). Applying the same thresholds when the analyses were restricted to lineage- and site-specific control tissues only, a similar list of DMGs and signalling pathways enriched in tumor compared to controls was obtained, supporting the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Fig. S5 in supplementary methods). To gain biological insight, we examined the gene composition of hypermethylated regions. Several promoter hypermethylated genes identified in our dataset, including ASNS, CHST15, NCAM2, PTPN6 (SHP1), SOCS6 and TET2, overlapped with prior reports [6, 11, 15, 16] (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, PRDM1 (BLIMP1), a previously reported hypermethylated TSG in ENKTL, fell just below our strict DMR cutoff; nonetheless, targeted comparison confirmed significantly increased methylation in ENKTL (p = 4.1 × 10⁻⁶, Δβ value 0.26; Supplementary Fig. S6). Together, these findings validate assay robustness in FFPE-derived DNA.

Beyond known targets, we identified previously unreported hypermethylation of epigenetic regulators (DNMT3A, DNMT3B, KDM5B), T/NK-lineage transcription factors (e.g., LEF1, TCF7, BCL11B), JAK/STAT and WNT/NOTCH signaling components, and receptor tyrosine kinases (EGFR, KIT, MET, NTRK1) (Supplementary Table S2). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed overrepresentation of oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K-AKT, MAPK, RAP1, Hippo, and WNT (Fig. 2B, C).

To identify transcription factors (TFs) associated with methylation changes, HOMER motif enrichment analysis was performed to identify transcription factor (TF) motifs associated with methylation changes. De novo motif analysis showed that hypermethylated DMRs were enriched for motifs linked to developmental and lineage-associated TFs, particularly homeobox-class motifs such as Pitx1 and Hmx3, as well as motifs related to immune or stress-associated regulation (HIF1A) and zinc-finger or repressor-associated factors and PRDM1 (Fig. 2D). No significant de novo motif enrichment was detected in hypomethylated DMRs. Known motif analysis similarly showed enrichment of homeobox and zinc finger motifs in hypermethylated DMRs (Supplementary Fig. S7; Supplementary Table S4). Overall, these findings suggest that ENKTL hypermethylation preferentially affects regulatory regions with developmental and stress-related sequence features, although motif enrichment alone does not indicate direct TF activity.

ENKTL stratifies into two epigenetically distinct subgroups with divergent clinical outcomes

Having identified two methylation-based ENKTL subgroups (ENKTL-group 1 and ENKTL-group 2) (Fig. 2A), we next examined whether this subgrouping was associated with clinical or technical variables. We systematically evaluated tumor content (based on histology assessment and copy number data derived from Oncoscan [1]), biopsy site, sample age/storage time, International Prognostic Index (IPI) score, disease stage, lineage, patient age, gender, ethnicity, sample source, experimental batch (Fig. 4A) and overall survival (OS). Only OS differed significantly between the two groups (p = 0.0055), with ENKTL-group 2 exhibiting poorer OS (Fig. 4B). We also repeated survival analysis after excluding deaths occurring within 90 days of diagnosis [20]. The survival difference between ENKTL group 1 and group 2 remained significant (p = 0.0067) (Supplementary Fig. S9), indicating that the observed subgroup effect was not driven solely by early deaths. Notably, ENKTL-group 2 samples exhibited global hypermethylation compared to ENKTL-group 1 samples (Fig. 4C), and higher methylation levels across promoters, 5′UTR, exon and intronic regions (Fig. 4C). Differential methylation analysis identified 27 significant DMRs (mean Δβ 0.29) mapping to 27 genes (adjusted p ≤ 0.05, |log2FC | ≥1), including 26 hypermethylated and one hypomethylated gene in ENKTL-group 2, enriched for developmental and differentiation pathways (Fig. 4D) (Supplementary Table S6).

Given prior evidence that hypermethylation of DNA repair genes is associated with GI [21] and increased methylation is associated with increased copy-number aberrations (CNA) in ENKTL [11], we compared the CNA obtained from our previous study between the two groups [1]. Total CNA burden and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) were significantly higher in the hypermethylated ENKTL-group 2 than in group 1 (p = 0.03) (p = 0.0011) (Fig. 4E, F). The most prominent focal alteration was 17p13.1 loss encompassing TP53, observed more frequently but not statistically significant in ENKTL-group 2 cases (45%, 5/11) than ENKTL-group 1 (12%, 2/17) (Fig.4G; Supplementary Table S7). Other GI metrics did not differ significantly (Supplementary Fig. S11).

Together, these data identify ENKTL-group 2 as a hypermethylated epigenetic subtype associated with increased CNA, frequent TP53 loss, higher LOH burden, and poorer OS.

Gene Ontology analysis of hypomethylated genes revealed strong enrichment of immune activation pathways, including leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, antigen processing and presentation, and T-cell–mediated cytotoxicity and its regulation (Fig. 5C). The hypomethylated gene set was enriched for antigen presentation and immune signaling genes, including multiple MHC class I (HLA-A, -E, -F, -G) and class II (HLA-DRA, HLA-DMB, HLA-DOA) genes, together with the antigen-processing transporters TAP1 and TAP2, core components of the peptide-loading complex, as well as genes implicated in NK/T-cell signaling (PTPRC, NCR1, LAIR1) and interferon/chemokine pathways (IRF7, IFNGR2, CCL5) (Supplementary Table S8).

Recurrent mutations of epigenetic modifier genes, including TET2 and/or DNMT3A, occur in the majority of nodal-TNKL samples [1, 22]. Given the role of TET2 in catalyzing the conversion of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), a critical step in active DNA demethylation [23], we hypothesized that TET2 mutations would be associated with DNA methylation changes. While global methylation levels did not differ between TET2 mut⁺ and TET2 mut⁻ cases (Supplementary Fig. S13), differential analysis identified 2109 DMRs (mean Δβ 0.29; 882 hypermethylated and 1227 hypomethylated) associated with 1 909 DMGs (p < 0.05, |log₂FC | > 1) (Supplementary Table S9) [24].

To assess the functional consequences of these methylation differences, we performed gene set enrichment analysis comparing TET2 mut⁺ (n = 5) and TET2 mut⁻ (n = 3) tumors, revealing enrichment of PI3K–Akt signaling, IgSF cell adhesion, and PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint pathways in TET2 mut⁺ cases (Fig. 5D, E). Despite a limited sample size, consistent clustering and methylation differences support a distinct epigenetic signature in TET2-mutated nodal-TNKL.

Given the striking contrast between ENKTL and nodal-TNKL methylation patterns, we further examined the genomic distribution of DMRs for region-specific enrichment. In ENKTL, most hypermethylated DMRs are localized to promoter regions (Fig. 6A, Left), whereas nodal-TNKL showed widespread hypomethylation across non-repetitive genomic regions, including promoters, introns, exons, and intergenic regions (Fig. 6A; Right, Supplementary Fig S14). Such widespread hypomethylation extends beyond promoters, potentially disrupting splicing and transcript stability [25]. Consistent with this, MeDIP-seq-derived normalized enrichment scores showed higher methylation levels in ENKTL, particularly at promoters and 5′-UTRs, compared with nodal-TNKL (Fig. 6B). We next annotated the DMRs according to chromatin state. Compared to controls, DMRs in ENKTL are predominantly hypermethylated and enriched in promoter-associated chromatin (~70%), with additional representation in repressed polycomb (~20%) and enhancer (~10%) states, suggesting epigenetic silencing of tumor suppressor and lineage-regulatory genes (Fig. 6C, Left). In contrast, nodal-TNKL DMRs were largely hypomethylated and enriched in repressed polycomb chromatin (~65%), with additional localization to transcription-associated (~25%) and enhancer (~10%) states (Fig. 6C, Right).

We previously showed that nodal-TNKL exhibits lower GI scores and fewer CNAs than ENKTL [1]. In this study, we observed hypermethylation of several genes involved in DNA damage response (DDR) genes in ENKTL, such as GADD45 and SLFN11 (Supplementary Table S2). As promoter hypermethylation of DDR genes has been linked to CNA accumulation and GI [26], we examined this relationship and observed a significant positive correlation between global methylation and GI scores (R = 0.52, p = 0.0005; Fig. 6D). Notably, ENKTL-group 2 clustered at the high-methylation, high-GI end, whereas nodal-TNKL samples showed lower methylation and GI, supporting a link between DNA hypermethylation and GI in these lymphomas.

Discussion

FFPE tissues are an invaluable resource for translational cancer research, but FFPE processing induces DNA fragmentation and cross-linking, limiting the performance of many conventional genome-wide assays [27]. In this setting, MeDIP-seq is well-suited for DNA methylation profiling from degraded FFPE DNA, using an antibody against 5mC to enrich methylated fragments without restriction-enzyme bias, enabling unbiased genome-wide coverage from fragmented DNA [13]. Unlike array-based platforms such as the Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip, which targets over 935,000 predefined CpG sites and requires bisulfite conversion and adequate DNA integrity [28], MeDIP-seq is not probe-limited and can survey intergenic, repetitive, and globally deregulated regions under-represented on arrays [29]. A recent FFPE-adapted MeDIP-seq workflow has demonstrated technical feasibility in targeted analytical contexts [30]; here, we extend this approach to systematic genome-wide interrogation of DNA methylation architecture across EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphomas using FFPE specimens. Although MeDIP-seq provides lower CpG resolution than Infinium MethylationEPIC, it avoids bisulfite-induced degradation while capturing genome-wide methylation patterns, making it particularly suitable for defining global methylation architectures and identifying novel features in FFPE samples [31]. Benchmarking and correlation between MeDIP-seq and the Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip beta values demonstrated significant correlation between the beta values derived from both assays, indicating there is good concordance between these two methods.

Using this approach, our MeDIP-seq analysis extends prior observations [9, 32, 33] by revealing that hypermethylated promoters in ENKTL are not randomly distributed but are enriched in gene sets representing specific biological pathways, supporting broader remodeling of T/NK-cell biology. Integration of methylation and gene-expression data underscores the functional consequences of these epigenetic alterations in ENKTL and identifies 81 genes with concordant methylation-expression changes, spanning pathways central to lymphoma biology, including RAS–MAPK/PI3K–AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, JAK–STAT, NF-κB, immune-response programs, and DNA repair (Fig. 3A, B).

Within this set of hypermethylated genes, BANK1 emerged as a notable target showing an inverse relationship between promoter methylation and gene expression, with promoter hypermethylation accompanied by reduced transcript levels in ENKTL. BANK1 encodes a scaffold protein involved in antigen receptor–proximal signaling and has been implicated as a tumor suppressor in lymphoid malignancies, with prior studies demonstrating BANK1 inactivation through IGH-associated translocation and promoter hypermethylation in B-cell lymphomas [34, 35]. Functional studies further support a growth-suppressive role for BANK1 in lymphoid contexts [35]. In parallel, the LEF1 locus, encompassing both LEF1 and its antisense transcript, LEF1-AS1, also showed inverse promoter methylation-gene expression association, with hypermethylation accompanied by reduced expression. LEF1 is essential for T-cell development and lineage fidelity and functions downstream of Wnt/β-catenin survival signaling [36], and LEF1 protein expression is downregulated in ENKTL [37]. In some cancers, LEF1-AS1 can epigenetically support LEF1 transcription via recruitment of chromatin-modifying complexes such as MLL1 and promotion of H3K4me3 at the LEF1 promoter [38]. Together, these observations are consistent with methylation-associated repression of the LEF1/LEF1-AS1 locus and its reduced expression in ENKTL, which may be linked to disruption of developmental programs required for maintaining T/NK-cell identity in ENKTL.

By contrast, IFITM1 showed an inverse promoter methylation-gene expression association with promoter hypomethylation accompanied by increased expression in ENKTL. IFITM1 is an interferon-induced transmembrane protein implicated in immune signaling, membrane organization, and host–virus interactions, and has been recurrently associated with lymphoid malignancies and interferon-responsive states [39–41]. In lymphoma models, IFITM family members support oncogenic signaling by organizing membrane-associated receptor and kinase complexes, while IFITM1 is frequently induced in EBV-associated and interferon-driven B-cell lymphomas [42, 43]. These findings support a concordant association between promoter hypomethylation and increased IFITM1 expression in ENKTL, linked to tumor survival and immune modulation.

Motif-level analyses provide additional regulatory context for ENKTL transcriptional changes. Using HOMER on hypermethylated DMRs, we observed enrichment of developmental/lineage, immune/stress, and zinc-finger/repressor-associated motifs (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S7), suggesting preferential targeting of these regulatory networks. However, motif enrichment reflects sequence composition rather than direct transcription factor binding and thus indicates context rather than definitive activity.

Our MeDIP-seq data further uncover two epigenetically distinct subgroups of ENKTL. One group (ENKTL-group 2) is characterized by a higher methylation level and significantly worse overall survival. This group also exhibits a higher CNAs and LOH, suggesting that extensive promoter-centric hypermethylation coexists with increased GI and a more aggressive disease biology, in keeping with previous reports [11, 44]. Loss-of-function alterations in TP53 are well documented in ENKTL and associated with adverse outcomes [1, 45–47]. The enrichment of 17p13.1 deletion and TP53 loss in the hypermethylated ENKTL group 2, which also exhibits the highest CNA burden and poorest survival, suggests that TP53 inactivation, extensive promoter hypermethylation, and higher GI may converge to drive a more aggressive disease biology. This observation aligns with a prior report linking high GI, manifested as increased mutation burden, CNAs, and structural variants, to poor prognosis in ENKTL [44]. This interplay also raises the possibility that TP53 dysfunction is associated with, and may contribute to, aberrant DNA methylation programs [48], potentially via impaired DDR, dysregulated DNMT activity, or compromised genome stability, although whether TP53 loss actively promotes hypermethylation or simply co-evolves with it remains to be determined.

A recent study reported that a subset of ENKTL exhibits an epigenetically immature phenotype characterized by widespread DNA hypermethylation, preferentially affecting Polycomb-repressed regions [11]. The degree of hypermethylation correlated with the burden of genomic copy-number aberrations, suggesting that these global epigenetic alterations may contribute to tumor genome instability. Consistent with these findings, our ENKTL subgroup 2 shares key features with the stage 4–like tumors, including DNA hypermethylation, a higher LOH/CNA, increased GI, and inferior survival. Larger-cohort studies will be required to clarify whether the differences between our ENKTL subgroups reflect variation in NK-cell developmental state.

In striking contrast to ENKTL, nodal-TNKL exhibits a fundamentally different epigenomic configuration. Nodal-TNKL is characterized by extensive global hypomethylation and regions enriched for T-cell cytotoxicity, immune-related genes and pathways (Fig. 5C), suggesting that uniform, genome-wide hypomethylation may act as a key enabling mechanism for the constitutive immune-signaling activation signature that we previously reported in nodal-TNKL [1]. Additionally, the hypomethylated loci involved multiple HLA class I and II loci and antigen-processing transporters which possibly contribute to dysregulated antigen-presenting capacity and the inability of the host immune response to effectively prevent chronic EBV infection, similar to ENKTL [49]. Chromatin-state analysis reveals that hypomethylated DMRs are enriched in Polycomb-repressed, transcription-associated, and enhancer states (Fig. 6C), suggesting a complex epigenomic rewiring in which loss of DNA methylation is associated with increased accessibility at immune and signaling loci, while Polycomb-mediated repression preserves silencing of developmental programs. This pattern mirrors epigenetic models described in other malignancies, where global hypomethylation is accompanied by redistribution of H3K27me3 and other repressive histone marks [50, 51].

Nodal-TNKL is associated with recurrent TET2 and DNMT3A mutations and clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) [1, 12]. Consistent with TET2’s role in active DNA demethylation, TET2-mut⁺ tumors exhibited a distinct epigenetic signature; however, this was not reflected as a global increase in methylation across genomic regions (Supplementary Fig. S13), but rather as locus-specific differential methylation and reshapes the methylome in a selective, context-dependent manner rather than imposing uniform hypermethylation (Fig. 5D). Pathway analysis of TET2-stratified DMRs revealed enrichment of signaling programs related to PI3K–Akt signaling, IgSF cell adhesion molecule signaling, and immune checkpoint PD-1/PD-L1 pathway (Fig. 5E), suggesting that TET2-associated epigenetic remodeling preferentially affects pathways governing survival signaling and tumor–immune interactions, consistent with epigenetic programs recurrently altered in certain hematologic malignancies [52–54].

We evaluated whether global DNA methylation states relate more broadly to GI across the EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphomas studied herein. Integrating DNA methylation data with copy-number profiles reveals a close association between epigenetic state and GI. ENKTL exhibits a higher global methylation burden and increased GI compared with nodal-TNKL, and within ENKTL, the hypermethylated subgroup shows the greatest CNA and LOH burden together with the poorest clinical outcomes. Notably, several DDR genes, including GADD45 and SLFN11, are hypermethylated in ENKTL, paralleling observations in other hematologic malignancies in which epigenetic silencing of DNA repair pathways contributes to GI and aggressive disease behavior [55, 56]. In contrast, nodal-TNKL is characterized by genomic stability, with low CNA burden and uniformly low GI scores (Fig. 6D) [1], a feature that may be associated with its globally hypomethylated epigenome. It remains uncertain if the absence of promoter-centric hypermethylation affecting DDR genes in nodal-TNKL may be linked to the observed genomic stability and represent a biologically distinct signature of this tumor. Although these associations do not establish causality, they suggest that DNA methylation patterns may reflect, and potentially relate to, GI in EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphomas.

Our study has its limitations. The rarity of ENKTL and nodal-TNKL results in modest cohort sizes, particularly in TET2-stratified analyses, limiting statistical power. In addition, the multivariable adjusted survival analysis between ENKTL group 1 and 2 analyses may remain underpowered and subject to residual confounding given the substantial treatment heterogeneity. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts, functional model systems, and integration with genetic, transcriptomic, and single-cell or spatial profiling will be required to further elucidate how epigenetic alterations shape tumor biology, immune evasion, and therapeutic response.

In summary, our study defines fundamentally distinct DNA methylation landscapes in ENKTL and nodal-TNKL, providing new insights into their divergent biology and clinical behavior. ENKTL is characterized by extensive promoter-centric hypermethylation with repression of TSGs, developmental programs, and lineage-associated transcriptional regulators such as LEF1/LEF1-AS1, together with signaling modulators like BANK1. In contrast, nodal-TNKL displays widespread genome-wide hypomethylation associated with immune activation pathways, particularly antigen processing and presentation. Importantly, we identify two previously unrecognized epigenetic subgroups within ENKTL, of which the hypermethylated subgroup is associated with increased GI and inferior survival in this cohort. Taken together, these entity-specific epigenomic architectures suggest fundamentally different mechanisms of disease progression and distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities: ENKTL, particularly the hypermethylated, genomically unstable subgroup, may benefit from strategies targeting DNA-damage response pathways and epigenetic regulators [6, 7, 57, 58], whereas nodal-TNKL, with its immune-activated, hypomethylated profile, may be more amenable to immune-modulatory approaches [1, 59]. Collectively, our integrative epigenomic analysis distinguishes ENKTL from nodal-TNKL, refines molecular subtyping within ENKTL, and provides a conceptual framework for leveraging methylation signatures to potentially support the distinction between the two lymphomas, risk stratification of ENKTL, and rational, biology-guided therapeutic prioritization in EBV-associated T/NK-cell lymphomas.
