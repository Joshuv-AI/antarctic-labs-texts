A pneumatized inferior turbinate (inferior concha bullosa): a case report
Authors: Mohammad G. Raslan, Areej S. Alqarzaie, Khaled Nasr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Concha bullosa refers to the aeration of a nasal turbinate, a finding that is very common in the middle turbinate. Conversely, inferior turbinate aeration is rarely detected on computed tomography (CT) imaging. Clinically significant inferior concha bullosa may contribute to nasal obstruction or headaches.Case presentation:Here, we present a 21-year-old male who complained of headache and bilateral nasal blockage. CT paranasal sinuses demonstrated a deviated nasal septum with middle and inferior turbinate pneumatization on the left side. Septoplasty with submucosal diathermy of the right inferior turbinate and excision of the free margin of the left inferior turbinate were performed. The patient’s headache and nasal blockage significantly improved after surgery.Conclusion:Inferior turbinate pneumatization is an uncommon anatomical variant that should be considered in patients with unilateral or refractory turbinate enlargement. CT imaging confirms the diagnosis and informs surgical planning. Symptomatic cases are effectively managed with tailored, mucosa-preserving turbinate reduction, often in conjunction with the correction of coexisting septal deviation. Larger series with longer follow-up and objective outcome measures are needed to better define optimal management.

Background

The lateral wall of the nose encompasses three bony projections, namely the superior, middle, and inferior turbinates. An additional small structure, known as the supreme turbinate, may occasionally be found at the top of the superior turbinates. While the superior and middle turbinates are ethmoidal in origin, the inferior turbinate is a separate bone that communicates with the ethmoid, palatine, and lacrimal bones and forms the medial boundary of the nasolacrimal duct. Functionally, the inferior turbinate is the largest and plays a pivotal role in the humidification and warming of inspired air; its anterior end also helps to form the lateral wall of the nasal valve, which is the narrowest section of the nasal passage[1,2].

Pneumatization inside a turbinate is referred to as a concha bullosa (CB). CB is common in the middle turbinate – especially among patients with chronic rhinosinusitis – where reported prevalence approaches 50% in some series[3]. Pneumatization of the superior turbinate is less frequent but well described[4]. In contrast, most cases of inferior turbinate enlargement are attributable to mucosal hypertrophy driven by chronic rhinitis (allergic or vasomotor), recurrent infections, or environmental irritants. It is very uncommon for inferior turbinate hypertrophy to result from pneumatization of the turbinate[2]. Inferior concha bullosa (ICB) is most often unilateral and has been reported predominantly in isolated case reports and small radiologic series, with estimates generally <1%[5]. ICB can be asymptomatic or present with nasal obstruction and/or headache; it may coexist with or contribute to nasal septal deviation (DNS)[5,6].

Here, we report a young adult male with ICB together with DNS, presenting with nasal obstruction and headache. This case adds to the limited literature by illustrating unilateral ICB in association with DNS and concurrent pneumatization of the ipsilateral middle turbinate. It also highlights the diagnostic reasoning required when symptoms are bilateral, but the principal pneumatized turbinate is unilateral, and it demonstrates a conservative, mucosa-preserving surgical strategy tailored to this anatomical constellation. This case report has been prepared in line with the SCARE checklist[7].

Case report

A 21-year-old healthy male, who had been experiencing periodic headaches and bilateral nasal blockage for almost 2 years, visited the outpatient clinic. He had been prescribed intranasal fluticasone elsewhere for 3 months with minimal improvement. He reported no history of previous nasal surgery, facial trauma, or chronic systemic disease.

Rigid nasal endoscopy (4-mm 0° scope) revealed a rightward septal deviation with a bony spur contacting the right inferior turbinate and nearly occluding the right nasal cavity. The left inferior turbinate was enlarged and obstructive.

Computerized tomography of the nose and paranasal sinuses showed mild mucosal thickening of both maxillary sinuses and the right ethmoidal sinus, with a small retention cyst in the right maxillary antrum. There was a right-sided DNS with a bony spur, in addition to aerated left middle and inferior turbinates. The left inferior turbinate contained a well-defined air cavity consistent with ICB (Fig. 1A,1B). Taken together, these findings suggest that the patient’s bilateral nasal obstruction is multifactorial. The right-sided nasal airway compromise is attributable mainly to the deviated septum and bony spur, with additional mucosal bulk from the right inferior turbinate. On the left side, the enlarged inferior turbinate and its pneumatized bony component likely contribute to airway narrowing. The patient’s headache is considered possibly related to mucosal contact points and structural nasal obstruction.

Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent septoplasty, right inferior turbinate submucosal diathermy, and left inferior turbinate reduction. On the left side, a limited free-edge mucosal incision was made over the pneumatized segment. The lateral bony component of the CB was then carefully resected with turbinectomy scissors, preserving as much mucosa as possible and avoiding the creation of a communication with the maxillary sinus. On the right side, submucosal diathermy of the inferior turbinate was performed to reduce mucosal bulk adjacent to the septal spur and optimize the postoperative airway after septal correction. Twice-daily isotonic saline irrigations were prescribed for 1 month postoperatively. At the 1-month follow-up, the patient described much better nasal breathing and headache resolution, with an unremarkable endoscopic examination. Six months after surgery, the patient reported total resolution of his preoperative symptoms with no complications.

Discussion

In 1988, Zinreich et al published the first documentation of ICB[8]. Since then, only a limited number of publications have addressed this condition, most of which are isolated case reports[9–14]. Reported prevalence varies by methodology and population. A large retrospective series of 2500 computed tomography (CT) scans identified only 10 cases[15], whereas another long-term radiologic survey reported incidences around 0.03%[16]. Another imaging cohort has cited higher rates, up to 4.87%, likely reflecting selection differences and imaging criteria[17].

Based on embryological development, the prochordal plate is the precursor of the inferior turbinate, which has two chondral lamellae and dual ossification centers that typically fuse by the ninth month of gestation. Epithelial invagination between the lamellae may entrap air, resulting in pneumatization. An alternative hypothesis proposes that articulatory deficiencies between the maxillary process of the palatine bone and the nasal process of the maxilla allow air from the maxillary sinus to extend into the adjacent inferior turbinate[2]. Supporting this view, Yang et al identified this extension in 44% of ICB cases[16].

A 2021 systematic review reported that nasal blockage and headache were the most frequent complaints among patients with ICB. The same study also reported a significant correlation with DNS, with 61% of patients with ICB exhibiting DNS on clinical assessment[14]. Clinically, ICB may be indistinguishable from mucosal hypertrophy on anterior rhinoscopy and endoscopy, where a bulging, poorly decongesting inferior turbinate is often seen. High-resolution CT with coronal and axial reformats is the diagnostic modality of choice, aiding distinction from a laterally bent inferior turbinate abutting the lateral nasal wall. ICB can be unilateral or bilateral and may coexist with the middle or, rarely, the superior turbinate CB[18].

In the present case, the diagnostic reasoning required consideration of multiple structural contributors to bilateral symptoms. On the right side, the deviated septum and bony spur nearly occluded the nasal cavity through contact with the right inferior turbinate. On the left side, both enlargement of the inferior turbinate and its pneumatized bony component contributed to narrowing. Thus, although the ICB was unilateral, it was not the sole explanatory finding. Headache may reflect mucosal contact points, altered airflow, or associated inflammatory changes. The substantial improvement in the patient’s symptoms after correction of the structural abnormalities supports the likelihood that the nasal anatomical findings contributed to his complaints.

Management should be restricted to symptomatic patients only. Topical intranasal corticosteroids may be attempted but often provide limited benefits in anatomically driven obstruction[14]. For the management of a pneumatized inferior turbinate, a number of surgical methods have been described. Technique selection should be individualized according to the degree of pneumatization, the relationship of the cavity to the maxillary sinus, anticipated bleeding risk, and the importance of maintaining mucosal integrity.

Minimally invasive maneuvers, such as out-fracture of the inferior turbinate using a Freer dissector or crushing of the inferior concha with forceps, may only be successful in patients with a small concha. Submucosal reduction techniques (e.g., diathermy and microdebrider-assisted turbinoplasty) are aimed at achieving more volume reduction while preserving mucosa[10,19].

Limited free-edge resection and conventional lateral laminectomy may be suitable for larger or more rigid pneumatizations, with attention to maintaining a continuous mucosal surface. However, resection should be avoided when there is visible continuity with the maxillary sinus because it may result in an inferior meatal antrostomy and mucociliary disruption, and thus should be avoided in that scenario.[20,21]

Additional mucosa-preserving options have also been described for CB surgery, including holmium:YAG laser-assisted resection, which offers precise tissue handling and hemostatic control in selected cases[22].

In the present case, limited mucosa-preserving free-edge resection of the left ICB was selected because the degree and position of pneumatization permitted conservative reduction of the bony component while maintaining mucosal continuity and minimizing the risk of creating an inferior meatal antrostomy. This was combined with septoplasty and contralateral submucosal diathermy to address the multifactorial nature of the patient’s bilateral nasal obstruction. The clinical outcome was favorable, with subjective improvement in breathing and resolution of headaches.

This report has important limitations. First, it describes a single patient and, therefore, cannot support broad generalizations regarding treatment efficacy. Second, follow-up was limited to 6 months, which is insufficient to assess long-term symptom control, recurrence, or preservation of mucosal function. Third, postoperative assessment relied on clinical examination and the patient’s subjective symptom improvement, without objective airflow measures or validated patient-reported outcome instruments. Accordingly, the present case should be interpreted as an illustrative example of tailored surgical management rather than definitive evidence for any specific operative technique.

Conclusion

Inferior turbinate pneumatization is an uncommon anatomical variant that should be considered in patients with unilateral or refractory turbinate enlargement. CT imaging confirms the diagnosis and informs surgical planning. Symptomatic cases are effectively managed with tailored, mucosa-preserving turbinate reduction, often in conjunction with the correction of coexisting septal deviation. Larger series with longer follow-up and objective outcome measures are needed to better define optimal management.
