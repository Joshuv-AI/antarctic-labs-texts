Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of Olutasidenib and Ivosidenib in Isocitrate Dehydrogenase 1-Mutated Relapsed/Refractory Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Justin M Watts, Eunice S Wang, Brian A Jonas, Florence R Wilson, Julie E Park, Shannon Cope, Aaron Sheppard, Amber Thomassen, Stéphane de Botton, Jorge E Cortes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOlutasidenib and ivosidenib are isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) inhibitors approved for relapsed/refractory (R/R) IDH1 mutant (IDH1m) acute myeloid leukemia (AML).MethodsA matching-adjusted indirect comparison estimated relative treatment effects using registrational Phase I/II data for olutasidenib (Study 2102-HEM-101; individual patient data) and ivosidenib (Study AG120-C-001; study-level data) since a head-to-head trial is unlikely. Weights were estimated using a logistic propensity score model adjusted for pre-defined covariates identified from a literature review, validated by clinical experts. Eight covariates were determined to be the most important prognostic factors/effect modifiers for the target population as reported in the Food and Drug Administration labels: number of prior systemic therapies, age, prior hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, AML type, relapse type, cytogenetic risk, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, and IDH1 mutation.ResultsOlutasidenib versus ivosidenib adjusted rates of complete remission (CR; odds ratio [OR] 1.12, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.61–2.08), CR plus CR with partial hematologic recovery (CR + CRh; OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.46–1.50), and median CR duration (difference in medians 11.18 months, 95% CI − 4.30 to 22.72) were not significantly different. Median CR + CRh duration was significantly longer for olutasidenib (difference in medians 9.84 months, 95% CI 3.24–22.28), accompanied by a numerical non-significant trend in overall survival that should be considered exploratory (hazard ratio 0.75, 95% CI 0.53–1.07).ConclusionWhile not confirmatory, these findings may be clinically relevant in the context of this difficult-to-treat R/R IDH1m AML population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03522-6.Keywords: Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1-mutated, Ivosidenib, Matching-adjusted indirect comparison, Olutasidenib, Relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Olutasidenib and ivosidenib are two allosteric type II IDH1 inhibitors available for the treatment of patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) IDH1 mutant (IDH1m) acute myeloid leukemia (AML); these agents were approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) based on results from two pivotal, single-arm Phase I/II trials, 2102-HEM-101 and AG120-C-001, respectively.Given that a head-to-head randomized controlled trial directly comparing olutasidenib and ivosidenib is unlikely in this rare population, the current study evaluated the comparative effectiveness of these interventions for patients with R/R IDH1m AML using an unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC), adjusting for between-study differences in baseline patient characteristics.What was learned from the study?Findings of the MAIC suggested that olutasidenib provides a comparable response rate to ivosidenib, while patients who achieve complete remission (CR) or CR with partial hematologic recovery (CRh) have a longer median duration of response (i.e., duration of CR + CRh) when treated with olutasidenib versus ivosidenib; olutasidenib was associated with a numerical non-significant improvement in overall survival compared to ivosidenib.While not confirmatory, these MAIC findings suggest that olutasidenib may offer clinically meaningful improvements in duration of response over ivosidenib for this difficult-to-treat R/R IDH1m AML population.

Introduction

Mutations in the isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH1) gene are identified in approximately 7–14% of individuals diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [1–3]. Patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) IDH1 mutant (IDH1m) AML have a poor prognosis, with a median overall survival (mOS) of only 5.9 and 4 months following first and subsequent lines of salvage therapy, respectively [2].

For patients with R/R IDH1m AML, two allosteric type II IDH1 inhibitors are available for therapy, olutasidenib and ivosidenib [4]. These agents differ in their molecular structures, as well as in their IDH1 binding characteristics and kinetics. While both effectively inhibit mutant IDH1, olutasidenib exhibits greater selectivity towards targeting the mutant form without inhibiting wild-type IDH1 [5–8]. The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [9, 10] approved these treatments based on two pivotal, single-arm Phase I/II trials, 2102-HEM-101 [11] and AG120-C-001 [12], respectively.

Possible benefits of olutasidenib over ivosidenib were identified in a qualitative comparison of observed outcomes between these pivotal trials in terms of complete remission (CR), CR plus CR with partial hematologic recovery (CR + CRh), median duration of complete remission (DoCR), median duration of CR + CRh (DoCR + CRh), 18-month OS among CR + CRh responders, and mOS in the overall population [13]. This analysis, however, did not estimate relative treatment effects, nor did it adjust for between-study differences in patient characteristics. Given that a head-to-head randomized controlled trial (RCT) directly comparing olutasidenib and ivosidenib is unlikely in this rare population, the aim of the current study was to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of these interventions for patients with R/R IDH1m AML using an unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) that adjusts for between-study differences in baseline patient characteristics.

Methods

Evidence Base

Olutasidenib

Study 2102-HEM-101 (NCT02719574; index trial for MAIC; June 18, 2021 data cut-off [DCO]) was a single-arm, open-label, multicenter trial encompassing multiple Phase I and II cohorts that evaluated olutasidenib either as monotherapy or in combination with azacitidine among patients with AML and myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) [11]. The MAIC used individual patient data (IPD) from the pivotal Phase II Cohort 1, including 147 patients with R/R IDH1m AML treated with olutasidenib monotherapy at 150 mg twice daily in continuous 28-day cycles. The primary efficacy endpoint was the rate of CR + CRh by investigator assessment.

Ivosidenib

Study AG120-C-001 (NCT02074839; external trial for MAIC) was a Phase I, single-arm, open-label, multicenter trial of ivosidenib monotherapy with dose escalation (n = 78) and dose expansion (n = 180) cohorts including patients with AML, MDS, or other hematologic malignancies. Patients in the dose expansion cohort received ivosidenib monotherapy at 500 mg per day in continuous 28-day cycles. The primary endpoints included response by investigator assessment and adverse events. Several analysis populations were reported across various trial sources identified in a literature review: DiNardo and colleagues [12], the ivosidenib FDA label [10], and the therapy’s New Drug Application multidisciplinary review document [14]. Patient characteristics and outcomes were extracted, and the OS IPD were reconstructed based on the digitized Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve [15]. The revised FDA R/R AML population (n = 174; November 10, 2017 DCO) was selected for the MAIC to align with the ivosidenib FDA label and availability of the covariates for matching.

Covariates for Propensity Model

The prognostic factors and/or treatment effect modifiers included in the MAIC were identified based on a literature review that was validated by clinical experts (Table S1). Eight covariates were determined to be the most important prognostic factors/effect modifiers for the target patient population as reported in the FDA labels: (1) number of prior systemic therapies; (2) age; (3) prior hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT); (4) AML type; (5) relapse type (i.e., relapsed vs. refractory); (6) cytogenetic risk; (7) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status; and (8) IDH1m type. This ranking was additionally informed by a published propensity score matching analysis of the IDH2 inhibitor enasidenib [16]. Additional factors outside of these eight covariates in the model were deprioritized based on clinical expert consensus and lack of reporting in the FDA labels.

Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison

An unanchored MAIC was performed to estimate the comparative effectiveness of olutasidenib (Study 2102-HEM-101) versus ivosidenib (Study AG120-C-001) in terms of CR, CR + CRh, DoCR, DoCR + CRh, and OS. Consistent with established methods [17, 18], weights for Study 2102-HEM-101 were estimated using a logistic regression model adjusted for eight pre-specified covariates to match the weighted mean baseline characteristics from Study AG120-C-001. The effective sample size (ESS) was estimated to provide a diagnostic regarding the degree of overlap between studies.

Treatment comparisons were performed based on the weighted (olutasidenib) and observed (ivosidenib) outcomes using a logistic regression model for binary outcomes and Cox model for time-to-event outcomes. When median survival was reported without a KM curve for Study AG120-C-001 (i.e., no reconstructed IPD available), simulated median survival for Study AG120-C-001 assuming a log-normal distribution and bootstrap sample-based median survival for Study 2102-HEM-101 were used to estimate the difference in median survival. Naïve and MAIC-adjusted outcomes were summarized in terms of absolute effects (response rates, medians, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs)] and relative treatment effects [binary outcomes: odds ratios (ORs); time-to-event outcomes: hazard ratios (HRs) or difference in medians, and corresponding 95% CIs]. Given a violation in the proportional hazard for OS, restricted mean survival time (RMST) up to 37.8 months was estimated (shortest maximum OS time of the two KM curves), which reflects the area under the survival curve up to that time point [19]. Relative treatment effects were summarized in terms of the difference in RMST at 37.8 months.

An unanchored simulated treatment comparison (STC) was performed as a sensitivity analysis for CR and CR + CRh [17]. This involved an outcomes regression model using IPD from Study 2102-HEM-101 to predict the response rate for olutasidenib in the Study AG120-C-001 population characterized in terms of the eight covariates used in the MAIC. Marginalization (i.e., G-computation method) was used to obtain the average predicted response rate for olutasidenib [20]. The log odds of this estimate were compared with the log odds of the reported ivosidenib response rate to derive the OR. The 95% CI was calculated using the bootstrap variance of the log odds for olutasidenib.

The base case analyses considered the efficacy evaluable population from Study 2102-HEM-101 (n = 147) and the revised FDA R/R AML cohort of Study AG120-C-001 (n = 174) and included all eight covariates of interest. Six covariates were reported in this cohort of Study AG120-C-001, while information for two covariates (number of prior systemic therapies and AML type) were obtained from alternative analysis populations with similar sample sizes (i.e., the original FDA R/R AML population [10] and the R/R AML dataset [12], respectively). For OS, no KM was reported for the revised FDA R/R AML population. Therefore, the original FDA R/R AML cohort (n = 173) was used in the base case (KM with no sample size at risk reported), including all eight covariates. A sensitivity analysis was performed based on the AG120-C-001 primary efficacy population (n = 125), which reported the OS KM curve with the sample at risk over time, including only five covariates (number of prior systemic therapies, age, and relapse type were not reported for this population). Another sensitivity analysis for OS was performed using the AG120-C-001 primary efficacy population (n = 125) including all eight covariates, which used the proportion of patients with three missing covariates from the original FDA R/R AML cohort (n = 173) [10] or the revised FDA R/R AML cohort (n = 174) [12]. Finally, a sensitivity analysis was performed including three co-mutations (FLT3, NPM1, and TP53) as additional covariates in the MAIC given observed between-study imbalances and clinical relevance [4, 21, 22]; these covariates were not included in the base case analyses due to overlap with the cytogenetic risk variable and lack of reporting in the FDA labels.

Ethical Approval

Results presented in this article are based on a retrospective analysis of data from published studies, and therefore no institutional board review was required. Review boards at participating institutions approved all procedures for the 2102-HEM-101 [11] and AG120-C-001 [12] trials.

Results

Between-Study Differences

The trial design characteristics, eligibility criteria, and baseline patient characteristics are summarized in the supplementary material (Tables S2, S3, and S4). The enrollment criteria were largely comparable and included adult patients with R/R IDH1-R132 gene-mutated AML with ECOG performance status of 0–2. Between-study differences in baseline characteristics in terms of age, AML subtype, ECOG performance status, relapse type, cytogenetic risk, and prior HSCT were balanced following matching (Table 1). The most inclusive model (with all eight covariates) resulted in an ESS of 73.03 for the Study 2102-HEM-101 efficacy evaluable population, representing a 50.3% reduction of the original sample size of 147.

In the naïve comparison, the ORs for CR and CR + CRh were comparable between treatments; point estimates favored olutasidenib for CR (OR 1.43; 95% CI 0.88–2.34) and to a lesser extent for CR + CRh (OR 1.09; 95% CI 0.68–1.74), but were not statistically significant. MAIC results were similar, also suggesting comparable rates of CR (OR 1.12; 95% CI 0.61–2.08) and CR + CRh (OR 0.83; 95% CI 0.46–1.50) between treatments; the adjusted point estimate indicated a numerical trend in favor of olutasidenib for CR and ivosidenib for CR + CRh but was not statistically significant. Results of the STC sensitivity analysis for CR (OR 1.10; 95% CI 0.63 to 1.95) and CR + CRh (OR 0.82; 95% CI 0.48 to 1.41) were consistent with the MAIC, although the STC provided slightly more precise 95% CIs (Figure S1).

The median DoCR in the naïve comparison was 28.1 months [95% CI 13.8 to not evaluable (NE)] for olutasidenib versus 10.1 months (95% CI 6.5–22.2) for ivosidenib. The median DoCR for olutasidenib after matching was 21.3 months (95% CI 12.0 to NE) and while the point estimate was still greater than the median DoCR for ivosidenib (difference in medians: 11.18 months; 95% CI − 4.30 to 22.72), the benefit for olutasidenib versus ivosidenib in terms of the difference in medians did not reach statistical significance. A similar pattern was observed for median DoCR + CRh following adjustment, although in this case olutasidenib significantly extended the DoCR + CRh versus ivosidenib (difference in medians: 9.84 months; 95% CI 3.24–22.28). Results from the sensitivity analysis including FLT3, NPM1, and TP53 as additional covariates in the MAIC were consistent with the base case, with a significantly longer median DoCR + CRh for olutasidenib versus ivosidenib (difference in medians: 9.57 months; 95% CI 1.79–22.23).

For OS, patients treated with olutasidenib had a slightly longer median than patients treated with ivosidenib [11.5 months (95% CI 8.3–14.9) vs. 9.1 months (95% CI 8.1–10.1)], which resulted in a significant improvement in the naïve comparison (HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.55–0.92). Following adjustment, median OS for patients treated with olutasidenib was shorter (9.7 months; 95% CI 5.6–16.4), and, while the MAIC result was no longer statistically significant, the point estimate still indicated a numerical trend in favor of olutasidenib (HR 0.75; 95% CI 0.53–1.07). The pattern of results for RMST up to 37.8 months was consistent with the OS HRs. In the naïve comparison, the RMST was greater for patients treated with olutasidenib (16.2 months; 95% CI 13.8–18.6) than ivosidenib (12.2 months; 95% CI 10.6–13.9) with a significant difference of 3.93 months (95% CI 1.02–6.84). After adjustment, the RMST for patients treated with olutasidenib remained fairly consistent (15.6 months; 95% CI 12.1–19.2); however, the benefit for olutasidenib versus ivosidenib in terms of the difference in RMST was no longer statistically significant (3.39 months; 95% CI − 0.51 to 7.29). The OS findings were not sensitive to the Study AG120-C-001 analysis population or number of covariates included in the model (Tables S5 and S6).

Discussion

The introduction of olutasidenib and ivosidenib reflects an important evolution in the treatment landscape for patients with R/R IDH1m AML. Given the overlap in the target populations in Study 2102-HEM-101 and Study AG120-C-001, and given that both treatments are recommended for patients with R/R IDH1m AML in current guidelines [4], it is important for clinical decision-makers to understand the comparative effectiveness of these treatment options, especially since an RCT is unlikely to be performed in this rare population. The current analysis used MAIC methods to adjust for between-study differences in the single-arm pivotal trials in order to compare the efficacy of olutasidenib versus ivosidenib in a population similar to Study AG120-C-001. Based on the naïve comparison, olutasidenib had comparable response, improved duration of response, and improved survival versus ivosidenib. Adjusted olutasidenib patients had slightly lower responses, duration of response, and survival than the observed values. The adjusted median DoCR + CRh was significantly longer for olutasidenib than ivosidenib by 9.84 months. There were no statistically significant differences in relative treatment effects for CR, CR + CRh, DoCR, and OS, although benefits may be considered clinically relevant in some cases. Findings were robust to the MAIC and STC sensitivity analyses explored.

The relationship between response type and duration is clinically relevant. Although the MAIC did not detect a significant difference in response rates between olutasidenib and ivosidenib, olutasidenib was associated with a longer duration of response, reaching statistical significance for DoCR + CRh. This may relate to olutasidenib’s rapid on–off binding kinetics and unique selective binding to mutant IDH1 which minimizes effects on gluconeogenesis and normal hematopoiesis [7]. The MAIC-adjusted difference in OS RMST was 3.39 months (95% CI − 0.51 to 7.29) for olutasidenib versus ivosidenib. If we consider an absolute RMST gain of at least 3 months as a threshold for minimally important benefit [23], the point estimate met this threshold, whereas the confidence intervals span below this threshold; therefore, OS findings may be considered exploratory and relevant for decision making rather than confirmatory. In addition, OS could not be estimated by response category in the MAIC given a lack of patient characteristics for patients treated with ivosidenib achieving CR or CRh, and the impact of subsequent therapies on OS could not be evaluated given that the proportion of patients treated with ivosidenib receiving subsequent therapies was not reported in the available aggregate data. Further research in real-world settings is needed to confirm whether the durability of response may translate to improved survival among responders.

A strength of the analysis was that eight potential prognostic factors and effect modifiers were identified a priori via a literature review with clinical validation and ranked as important for inclusion in the logistic regression model for both the MAIC and the STC. After matching all included covariates in the efficacy populations, these patient characteristics were well balanced between Study 2102-HEM-101 and Study AG120-C-001. For the OS analysis, information regarding the number of prior systemic therapies and AML types were leveraged from alternative Study AG120-C-001 populations to maximize the inclusion of relevant covariates for matching, assuming overall consistency in the proportion of patient characteristics across analysis populations. The reduction in ESS of 50.3% from the original sample size of 147 (efficacy) patients was within an acceptable range [24], suggesting that patients in the trials were sufficiently similar to inform the comparison. However, this reduction in ESS increases the uncertainty in the weighted estimates, particularly for the duration of response endpoints that are based on a smaller subset of responding patients.

However, as with any analysis of single-arm or non-comparative studies, there is uncertainty about whether all relevant prognostic factors and effect modifiers that could influence the outcomes of interest captured in the model, which assumes no unmeasured confounders. For example, differences in co-occurring mutations (e.g., FLT3, NPM1, and TP53) were identified as potential covariates in the literature review but were not included in the final list of ranked variables for the base case model. Although cytogenetic risk was included in the MAIC, which considers such mutations as part of the NCCN Guideline’s® [4] risk stratification, a naïve comparison of values reported in the primary publications suggested that TP53 mutations were more prominent in patients treated with ivosidenib, while FLT3 mutations were more prominent in patients treated with olutasidenib [11, 12]. Imbalances in the distribution of these deleterious mutations were anticipated to act in opposing directions and to have minimal impact on the MAIC results, and a sensitivity analysis confirmed this: including FLT3, NPM1, and TP53 as additional covariates in the MAIC produced results consistent with the base case for all outcomes, with the difference in median DoCR + CRh remaining statistically significant in favor of olutasidenib. Beyond the cytogenetic and co-mutation variables included in these analyses, additional molecular risk characteristics were not captured but may act as potential confounders. Adjusting for between-study differences in observed covariates is inherently challenging, especially in small studies and in the presence of heterogeneity in how characteristics are defined and reported across sources. Despite the comprehensive approach to covariate identification and selection for the MAIC, there remains a risk that differences in unmeasured or unavailable confounders may have biased the results.

Beyond potential residual confounding, it is important to acknowledge differences in study design that were not accounted for in the analysis. For example, given that the studies were conducted several years apart, some patients treated with olutasidenib in Study 2102-HEM-101 received front-line treatment options that were not available during the AG120-C-001 study period. In Study 2102-HEM-101, 12 of 147 patients had received prior venetoclax, which was approved in 2018 by the FDA in combination with azacitidine, decitabine, or low-dose cytarabine for newly diagnosed AML [25]. In contrast, patients in Study AG120-C-001 did not receive venetoclax as front-line therapy because it was not yet available. Front-line venetoclax has since become the standard of care for older patients and those considered unsuitable for intensive induction chemotherapy [4]. Patients who receive venetoclax-based therapy often achieve higher initial response rates and longer OS than those treated with intensive chemotherapy, but patients who relapse following front-line venetoclax tend to have high-risk disease and poorer outcomes than those who relapse after intensive chemotherapy [26]. The population enrolled in Study 2102-HEM-101 may therefore have included a subset of patients with higher-risk disease, which may have contributed to more conservative MAIC results for olutasidenib, potentially underestimating its relative benefit. Additional evidence regarding this subgroup will be important moving forward as patients with R/R IDH1m AML will be increasingly likely to have received front-line venetoclax.

In addition, given that ivosidenib is approved for the treatment of patients with newly diagnosed IDH1m AML, additional data on the efficacy of olutasidenib monotherapy following ivosidenib exposure is important to inform treatment sequencing decisions.

The current study focused on efficacy outcomes. Although outside the scope of the current analysis, the relative impact of these IDH1m inhibitors on safety outcomes, health-related quality of life, and economic endpoints are also of interest from a decision-making perspective. Should sufficient data become available, investigation of these outcomes would be valuable to further support decision making processes related to treatment selection between olutasidenib and ivosidenib in R/R IDH1m AML.

Conclusion

The findings of the MAIC suggest that olutasidenib provides a comparable response rate to ivosidenib. Overall, patients who achieve CR or CRh have a longer median duration of response (i.e., DoCR + CRh) when treated with olutasidenib versus ivosidenib. OS results should be interpreted as exploratory given the trend favoring olutasidenib versus ivosidenib was not significant. Taken together, findings may be clinically relevant in the context of this difficult-to-treat R/R IDH1m AML population.
