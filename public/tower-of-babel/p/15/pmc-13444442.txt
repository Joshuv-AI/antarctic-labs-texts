Gastroduodenal Perforation Secondary to Heterotopic Pancreas in a Patient With Systemic Amyloidosis and End-Stage Renal Disease on Hemodialysis: A Case Report
Authors: Edgar Eduardo Sordia Marquez, Victor Hugo Garzón Ortega, Javier González Reyes, Karen Michelle Gonzalez Chiu, Francisco Javier Reyna Sepúlveda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterotopic pancreas (HP) is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by pancreatic tissue lacking anatomical and vascular continuity with the orthotopic pancreas. Although usually asymptomatic and incidentally diagnosed, HP may rarely become clinically significant due to inflammation, ulceration, bleeding, obstruction, or gastrointestinal perforation. Systemic conditions such as amyloidosis, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and chronic hemodialysis may further increase gastrointestinal vulnerability through vascular fragility, impaired mucosal healing, and recurrent splanchnic hypoperfusion.We present the case of a patient with systemic amyloidosis and end-stage CKD on maintenance hemodialysis through a recently created brachial-basilic arteriovenous fistula who developed progressive gastrointestinal symptoms followed by acute abdomen. Initial manifestations included nausea and projectile vomiting one day after fistula creation, followed by progressive right upper quadrant abdominal pain radiating to the lumbar region, semiliquid bowel movements, dehydration, and hemodynamic instability. Imaging studies suggested a perforated viscus with intra-abdominal inflammatory changes. Emergency exploratory laparotomy revealed perforation at the antro-duodenal junction with diffuse fibrinous peritonitis and multiple cystic peritoneal lesions suspicious for malignant disease. Surgical management consisted of segmental gastroduodenal resection, extensive peritoneal lavage, targeted peritoneal biopsies, cholecystectomy, and appendectomy. Histopathological examination demonstrated heterotopic pancreatic tissue associated with ulceration and perforation. Immunohistochemistry of the peritoneal lesions showed positivity for WT1 and calretinin, consistent with benign peritoneal inclusion cysts.This case highlights an exceptionally rare cause of gastrointestinal perforation and underscores the importance of integrating clinical evolution, systemic comorbidities, intraoperative findings, pathology, and immunohistochemistry into surgical decision-making. The coexistence of amyloidosis, CKD, and hemodialysis-related hypoperfusion likely contributed synergistically to mucosal vulnerability and perforation.

Introduction

Heterotopic pancreas (HP), also referred to as ectopic pancreas, is defined as pancreatic tissue located outside the normal pancreas and lacking anatomical or vascular continuity with it. Reported incidence ranges from 0.5% to 13% in autopsy series and approximately 0.2% to 0.9% in gastric surgical specimens [1,2]. The stomach and duodenum represent the most frequent sites of involvement [3].

Although the majority of patients remain asymptomatic, heterotopic pancreatic tissue may become clinically significant when enzymatically active, producing local inflammation, ulceration, obstruction, bleeding, or, in exceptionally rare cases, gastrointestinal perforation [2-4]. Functional ectopic pancreatic tissue retains exocrine secretory capacity capable of generating local enzymatic injury and chronic inflammatory changes [4].

Systemic comorbidities may significantly influence the severity of gastrointestinal complications. Amyloidosis has been associated with vascular fragility, impaired tissue repair, and submucosal ischemic compromise [5]. Likewise, chronic kidney disease and maintenance hemodialysis are associated with chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, recurrent hypotension, and splanchnic hypoperfusion, all of which may increase gastrointestinal vulnerability [6,7].

To our knowledge, gastroduodenal perforation caused by HP in the setting of systemic amyloidosis and end-stage renal disease requiring hemodialysis has rarely been reported. This unusual combination represents a significant diagnostic challenge and provides valuable educational insights regarding the evaluation and management of complex acute abdominal presentations.

We report a complex surgical case of gastroduodenal perforation secondary to heterotopic pancreatic tissue in a patient with systemic amyloidosis and end-stage chronic kidney disease on hemodialysis, emphasizing the chronological clinical evolution, diagnostic challenges, intraoperative findings, immunohistochemical analysis, and multidisciplinary perioperative management.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old male with a known history of systemic amyloidosis and end-stage chronic kidney disease requiring maintenance hemodialysis through a brachial-basilic arteriovenous fistula presented with progressive gastrointestinal symptoms and subsequent acute abdomen.

The patient initially (Day 0) developed persistent nausea associated with multiple episodes of projectile vomiting containing gastric contents. One day prior to symptom onset, a brachial-basilic arteriovenous fistula had been surgically created. The patient initially sought outpatient medical attention and received symptomatic management with ondansetron and omeprazole.

Over the following days, symptoms progressively worsened. On Day 5, the patient developed moderate colicky abdominal pain localized to the right upper quadrant with radiation toward the lumbar region. This was associated with semiliquid bowel movements occurring approximately five times daily without mucus, tenesmus, or overt obstructive symptoms.

The patient subsequently presented to our institution on Day 7, with progressive clinical deterioration. He was admitted under the Internal Medicine service, which requested surgical consultation for evaluation of the recently created arteriovenous fistula and associated abdominal symptoms.

On admission, vital signs demonstrated relative hemodynamic instability with a heart rate of 89 beats/minute, respiratory rate of 20 breaths/minute, blood pressure of 88/57 mmHg, temperature of 36.4°C, and oxygen saturation of 96%. Weight was 80 kg, and height was 1.80 m, resulting in a body mass index of 24.7 kg/m².

Physical examination revealed dry mucous membranes and delayed capillary refill greater than 3 seconds, suggesting significant dehydration and hypoperfusion. The patient was conscious and oriented. Abdominal examination demonstrated generalized tenderness to palpation without overt peritoneal irritation during the initial assessment. The abdomen was soft and depressible with preserved bowel sounds. Murphy, Rovsing, and heel percussion signs were negative. Examination of the right upper extremity demonstrated a functioning arteriovenous fistula with an audible bruit on auscultation.

Initial management included intravenous fluid resuscitation with isotonic saline, broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy consisting of metronidazole and ciprofloxacin, and stool studies including coproparasitoscopy, stool culture, and Clostridioides difficile toxin testing.

Despite initial conservative management, the patient demonstrated progressive abdominal symptoms and systemic deterioration. Cross-sectional computed tomography demonstrated a distended stomach with hyperdense intraluminal content and marked antropyloric wall thickening. An irregular fluid collection with peripheral enhancement and internal gas locules was identified in the right paracolic gutter, findings highly suggestive of a perforated viscus and intra-abdominal sepsis (Figures 1, 2).

Given the progressive clinical course and suspicion for perforated viscus with intra-abdominal sepsis, emergency exploratory laparotomy was indicated.

Exploratory laparotomy revealed perforation at the antro-duodenal junction associated with diffuse fibrinous peritonitis and significant intra-abdominal contamination. Multiple cystic lesions involving the visceral and parietal peritoneum were identified intraoperatively. These lesions contained clear citrine fluid and mucinous material, raising concern for malignant peritoneal disease, including carcinomatosis or mesothelioma. Additional findings included severe inflammatory changes of the gallbladder consistent with gangrenous acute cholecystitis. Given the patient’s physiological fragility and septic status, the operative strategy prioritized rapid and adequate source control while avoiding unnecessary radical procedures. Surgical management consisted of segmental gastroduodenal resection, extensive peritoneal lavage, targeted resection and biopsy of peritoneal cystic lesions, cholecystectomy, and appendectomy. Broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy was continued postoperatively with renal dose adjustment. Hemodynamic support and close postoperative monitoring were provided.

Histopathological examination of the gastroduodenal specimen demonstrated acute ulceration and perforation associated with heterotopic pancreatic tissue composed of well-formed pancreatic acini and ducts. Peritoneal lesions corresponded to benign peritoneal inclusion cysts lined by mesothelial cells without cytologic atypia. Gross pathological examination of the resected specimen is shown in Figure 4. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positivity for WT1 and calretinin, supporting a benign mesothelial origin and excluding malignant mesothelioma. Absence of features such as BAP1 loss or significant EMA overexpression further supported the benign nature of the lesions. Gallbladder pathology demonstrated gangrenous acute cholecystitis.

Following surgical intervention and intensive supportive care, the patient demonstrated progressive clinical improvement. Resolution of the systemic inflammatory response and stabilization of hemodynamic status were achieved. No evidence of malignant disease was identified on final pathology. Postoperative management included antimicrobial therapy adjusted according to renal function, fluid optimization, nutritional support, and continued multidisciplinary management involving surgery, nephrology, and internal medicine.

Discussion

Gastrointestinal perforation secondary to HP is exceptionally rare and represents one of the least frequently reported complications of this congenital anomaly [2-4]. Functional heterotopic pancreatic tissue may retain exocrine secretory capacity capable of producing local enzymatic autodigestion, chronic inflammation, ulceration, and eventual perforation [4].

The present case is particularly noteworthy because several systemic factors may have acted synergistically to increase gastrointestinal vulnerability. Amyloidosis is known to cause submucosal vascular deposition, tissue fragility, and impaired healing capacity, thereby compromising mucosal integrity [5]. Similarly, chronic kidney disease contributes to chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, impaired immune response, and increased susceptibility to severe infections [6].

An important aspect of this case is the temporal relationship between arteriovenous fistula creation and the onset of gastrointestinal symptoms. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be established, recent fistula creation in a patient with advanced renal disease may have contributed to hemodynamic redistribution and relative splanchnic hypoperfusion. Recurrent vomiting, dehydration, and hypotension at presentation may have further exacerbated gastrointestinal ischemic vulnerability. In addition, hemodialysis-related hemodynamic shifts and episodes of intradialytic hypotension have been recognized as contributing factors to mesenteric hypoperfusion and ischemic gastrointestinal injury [7].

Another remarkable finding was the presence of multiple benign peritoneal inclusion cysts, which complicated the intraoperative assessment by mimicking malignant peritoneal disease [8]. In emergency surgical settings, distinguishing between benign and malignant peritoneal lesions is critical because unnecessary radical oncologic procedures may significantly increase morbidity, particularly in physiologically compromised patients.

Immunohistochemical evaluation played a key role in establishing the final diagnosis. Positivity for WT1 and calretinin supported benign mesothelial differentiation and helped exclude malignant mesothelioma. The absence of additional malignant features further confirmed the benign nature of the lesions and guided appropriate postoperative management [9,10].

This case underscores the importance of integrating clinical evolution, hemodynamic physiology, surgical findings, pathology, and immunohistochemical analysis into the decision-making process. Recognition of the potential interaction between heterotopic pancreatic tissue and systemic conditions such as amyloidosis and end-stage renal disease may facilitate earlier diagnosis and more effective management of similar complex presentations.

This report has several limitations. As a single-case report, it cannot establish a causal relationship between systemic amyloidosis, end-stage renal disease, and gastroduodenal perforation secondary to HP. The contribution of these comorbidities to tissue vulnerability remains inferential. In addition, long-term postoperative follow-up was unavailable. Nevertheless, the pathological confirmation and the rarity of this presentation provide clinically relevant information for surgeons managing complex acute abdominal conditions.

Conclusions

This case describes a rare presentation of gastroduodenal perforation secondary to heterotopic pancreatic tissue in a patient with systemic amyloidosis and end-stage chronic kidney disease on hemodialysis. The coexistence of multiple factors affecting tissue integrity and perfusion likely contributed to the development of ulceration and subsequent perforation.

Recognition of HP as a potential cause of acute abdomen is important, particularly in medically complex patients. Careful integration of clinical findings, surgical exploration, histopathological examination, and immunohistochemical analysis is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management while avoiding unnecessary radical interventions.

Surgeons should consider HP in the differential diagnosis of unexplained gastroduodenal perforation, particularly in medically complex patients with conditions that may impair gastrointestinal perfusion or tissue integrity.
