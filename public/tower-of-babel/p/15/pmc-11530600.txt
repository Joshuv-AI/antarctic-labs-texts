Introduction

Erythrocyte blood transfusions are strongholds in the therapy of anaemic patients, leading to a superior list of routine medical approaches in hospitalized patients [1]. Blood transfusion is performed in 0.5–3 % of women with obstetric hemorrhage and accounts for 1 % of all transfused blood products in high-income countries [2].

Anesthesiologists face a significant issue in identifying the necessity for a transfusion in a patient with obstetric hemorrhage, which is due in part to the fact that more than half of blood transfusions for obstetric hemorrhage are given to women who have no risk factors [3].

When treating a hemorrhaging patient, obtaining coagulation and hemoglobin data is crucial for making transfusion decisions. Standard laboratory-based information requires a blood sample to be collected and submitted to a hematology laboratory, with a turnaround time of up to 60 min [4].

A hemolytic transfusion reaction (HTR) is the destruction of red cells caused by immunological incompatibility between the donor and the recipient, not only incompatibility but also, rarely, compatible blood transfusion, which may cause a hemolytic transfusion reaction [5].

A hemolytic transfusion reaction occurs when the transfusion causes symptoms as well as clinical or laboratory indicators of increased red cell death [6].

In an analysis of transfusion-related mortality from 2013 to 2017, hemolysis accounted for 32 of 185 (17 %) cases. Among these 32 deaths, approximately one-third (6 % of the total) were due to ABO incompatibility, and the other two-thirds (11 % of the total) were caused by non-ABO responses [7]. The report is in line with the SCARE criteria [8].

Presentation of the case

We present a 27-year-old Gravida II Para I mother who was blood group AB positive with anaemia, hypotension secondary to antepartum hemorrhage, and a mentally conscious mother who was transferred to our obstetric emergency operation theatre. As she experienced bleeding on arrival, we assessed the patient's history and performed anaesthesia-related physical examinations, such as cardiovascular examination, respiratory examination, central nerve system examination, and airway examination. The results revealed that she has a distant S1 and S2, had clear respiratory air entry, had a thyromental distance of 7 cm, and had a sternomental distance of 13 cm, and she was classified as Mallampati class II.

After this, we decided to administer single shoot rapid sequence spinal anaesthesia with 0.5 % hyperbaric bupivacaine via an aseptic technique. While she was transferring from maternal reception to the operation theatre, we prepared essential equipment for spinal anaesthesia and cesarean section.

The physical examination at the time of maternal reception revealed the following: her blood pressure was 110/62, her respiratory rate was 24 breaths/min, her heart rate was 98 beats/min, her temperature was 37.2 °C, her oxygen saturation was 95 %, and she had moderate anaemia and pale conjunctiva. Laboratory tests on admission (Fig. 1) revealed findings that corresponded with anaemia secondary to APH. The hematogical investigations such as PTT, Apt, Fibrinogen, clotting factors, and arterial blood gas analysis to rule out DIC due to Abruptio placentae was not done because of patient economic and resource limitation.

Preoperative laboratory result of the patient.

Spinal anaesthesia was administered safely with an anaesthesia specialist (Msc anaesthesia professional), and the score ranged from T8-T10 to the sensory level and the class III Bromage scale for motor assessment. After cross-matching, two recently collected nonold, noncold units of AB-positive blood were prepared, an emergency cesarean section was then performed, and the infant survived. Moreover, after 10 min, we started the transfusion, and she started complaining of headache, mild confusion, a rash around the chest, urticaria, and oozing through the IV line immediately after the transfusion of 20–30 ml from a blood unit.

Her vital signs included a blood pressure of 88/52 mmHg, an oxygen saturation of 95 %, and a heart rate of 125 bpm. Thereafter, we performed a differential diagnosis for these findings, such as high spinal, total spinal, and local anaesthetic agent toxicity. We declared that there was no problem related to spinal anaesthesia.

We discontinued the transfusion and requested that the sample blood be sent to the laboratory for an urgent recheck; unfortunately, this request was correct. For emergency reasons, we ordered O-negative blood and a transfusion with O-negative blood, and we looked to the end of the transfusion, which was good. No reactions, such as headache, confusion, or blood pressure, were corrected with volume expansion and 32 μgm of noradrenaline.

As a consequence of hemolytic reactions, we are concerned with acute kidney injury, acute lung injury, and profound hypotension. We communicated with the patient and attendant fluently without language barriers, and we informed the patient of the possible outcomes up to dialysis for the family. However, for this scenario, we used a vasopressor, fluid compressor, and blood warmer.

We followed the patient postoperatively until discharge from the hospital, and 12.9 g/dl hemoglobin, 36.3 % haematocrit, 402 × 103 platelet count, and 0.9 mg/dl creatinine were detected (Fig. 2). After satisfactory postoperative vital signs and laboratory results were obtained, the patient was discharged from the hospital after 3 days.

Clinical discussion

Red blood cell (RBC) transfusion can save lives, but it can potentially be fatal if an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction develops [7].

The acute hemolytic transfusion reaction is a medical emergency with an estimated frequency of one per 70,000 blood product transfusions and an estimated fatality rate of five per 10 million RBC unit transfusions [9,10].

Importantly, the traditional triad of fever, flank pain, and red or dark urine is uncommon. However, these symptoms may not be immediately visible if the patient is under anaesthesia; in such circumstances, seeping from venipuncture and dark urine caused by DIC and hemoglobinuria, respectively, may be the only observations [7].

In this case, the patient presented the characteristic triad a few minutes after receiving an RBC transfusion, and the newborn was rescued by an urgent cesarean delivery. This was the only obstetric patient brought to our hospital in the last few months who had AHTR caused by an ABO-compatible blood transfusion.

Our patient developed insignificant problems from ARF, necessitating dialysis, a lengthy hospital stay, and a lengthy rehabilitation period. In the context of hemolysis-associated AKI, although the use of dialysis to remove hemoglobin or uric acid to prevent or alleviate ARF has not been demonstrated, it may be necessary in some cases to address metabolic abnormalities [7]. This is because of the early detection of reactions to transfusion.

Even in affluent countries, noninfectious transfusion complications are the leading cause of morbidity and mortality. AHTRs and accompanying mortality are estimated to occur at 1 in 76,000 and 1 in 1.8 million units transfused, respectively [11,12].

Preventive initiatives, such as the correct identification of patients, samples, and units; antibody identification; and red cell antigen testing, are critical for lowering HTR morbidity. Improvements in antigen matching and the development of “universal” blood components could prevent HTR in the future [5].

Conclusion

Blood transfusion is performed in 0.5–3 % of women with obstetric hemorrhage and accounts for 1 % of all transfused blood products in high-income countries. Anesthesiologists face a significant issue in identifying the necessity for transfusion in patients with obstetric hemorrhage. Hemolytic reactions after blood transfusion are common during emergency patient management. For this reason, the World Health Organization has developed guidelines for early detection and management.
