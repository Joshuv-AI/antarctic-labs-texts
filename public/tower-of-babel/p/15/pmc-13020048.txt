Organizational cultural values and social capital in enhancing COVID-19 response capacity of community health centres in Zhejiang, China: a structural equation modelling study
Authors: Xin Zhou, Jiamin Tang, Jie Yang, Zhou Zhou, Hailong Zhu, Tao Sun, Xianhong Huang, Yongjian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAgainst the backdrop of normalized COVID-19 prevention and control, this study focuses on community health service centers. Here, “Cultural values” refer to the shared beliefs, norms, and attitudes within a community or organization that shape behavior and decision-making, such as a sense of collective responsibility, mutual support, and adherence to public health guidelines. This study explores the impact of Cultural values and social capital on their COVID-19 response capabilities and proposes targeted improvement strategies.MethodsA self-designed questionnaire was used to survey 983 medical staff from 15 community health service centers in each of six cities in Zhejiang Province. Differences in COVID-19 response capabilities across centers with varying demographic characteristics were compared using t-tests and variance analysis. Hierarchical linear regression was employed to analyze influencing factors, while structural equation modeling path analysis explored the impact pathways and mechanisms of Cultural values and social capital on COVID-19 response capabilities.Key findingsCultural values have a significant impact on the COVID-19 response capability. The overall effect value, direct effect value, and indirect effect value of cultural appeal on the COVID-19 response capability of community health service centers are 0.784, 0.070, and 0.070 respectively. Social capital exhibits a partial mediating effect. Among them, the overall effect value of structural social capital is 0.094, that of relational social capital is 0.260, and that of cognitive social capital is 0.241. The influence of cultural appeal on pandemic response capability is mainly transmitted indirectly through relational and cognitive social capital, while the mediating effect of structural social capital is not significant.ImplicationsCultural values and social capital positively influence COVID-19 response capabilities in community health service centers, with Cultural values indirectly affecting these capabilities through relational and cognitive social capital. Thus, centers can enhance their COVID-19 response capabilities by strengthening Cultural values, fostering social capital, integrating health resources, and establishing collaborative mechanisms, ensuring community residents’ health and safety during crises like sudden outbreaks and natural disasters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14298-4.Keywords: Cultural values, Social capital, COVID-19 response capacity, Community health service centers, Structural equation modeling

Background

Since the 21st century, major global public health emergencies, including SARS (2003), H1N1 influenza (2009), Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (2012), Ebola hemorrhagic fever (2014), and COVID-19 (2019), have severely impacted health, economies, and global governance [1]. While China has made grassroots health emergency management a priority, aiming to enhance community health service centers’ emergency response capabilities through policy guidance and collaboration [2], significant shortcomings persist. These include inadequate material reserves [3], insufficient training [4], and limited drills [5]. Existing research predominantly focuses on constructing indicator systems [6] and identifying influencing factors [7]. However, these studies often overlook the theoretical integration of cultural values and social capital into emergency response models. While some literature hints at their potential importance, there is a lack of critical, in-depth analysis of their action mechanisms. For instance, current models fail to clarify how cultural values—such as collective responsibility and mutual support—interact with different types of social capital (structural, relational, and cognitive) to influence emergency response capabilities. This gap limits our understanding of the underlying dynamics that drive effective crisis management at the grassroots level. This study employing structural equation modeling to explore the influencing paths of cultural values and social capital on COVID-19 response capability in community health service centers. Specifically, it clarifies the mediating role of social capital between cultural values and emergency response capability, providing a more nuanced understanding of how these factors interact. By doing so, the study offers theoretical support for improving grassroots health emergency response, contributes to community resilience construction, and enriches the existing knowledge base with a critical, evidence-based approach.

Theories and hypotheses

Cultural values in the context of community health service centers’ COVID-19 response

The term “culture” has its roots in Latin and has gradually evolved into its current form. When examining domestic academic research, we find diverse perspectives on cultural values. Jia Chunfeng, from the standpoint of cultural functions, contends that cultural values serve as a potent internal driving force [8]. It has the capacity to unleash the inherent potential and enthusiasm of individuals or groups, pushing them towards specific objectives.However, there exists a degree of conceptual ambiguity surrounding “cultural values”. It often oscillates between being an ideology, an aspect of organisational culture, a set of moral norms, and a symbol driven by policies, such as the concept of “cultural walls”. This lack of clarity makes it challenging to precisely define and measure cultural values.Huang Yanrong, through systematic research, puts forward a four - element model of cultural values, which includes cohesion, guiding force, organizational force, and leadership [9]. These elements are interconnected and mutually interactive, collectively shaping the internal structure of cultural values. From the perspective of cultural systems, Li Lamei regards cultural values as a comprehensive system centered on values, covering multiple levels such as spiritual culture, institutional culture, behavioral culture, and material culture [10]. A number of foreign scholars also stress the dominant position of one’s own culture and advocate cultural confidence, viewing it as a crucial foundation for the exertion of cultural values [11]. Yet, when synthesizing the research findings of scholars both at home and abroad, we notice that despite differences in expressions and focuses regarding cultural values, they all attempt to reveal its essential characteristics and significant roles from various angles.In this study, we define cultural values in the context of community health service centers’ COVID − 19 response as a set of shared beliefs, norms, and attitudes that influence the behavior and decision - making of individuals and groups within these centers. It is closely related to emergency culture, emphasizing how cultural elements can enhance the ability to respond effectively to public health emergencies. This definition helps to distinguish it from general organisational culture or collectivist values, focusing specifically on the emergency - related cultural aspects.Given the unique cultural and social environment in China, our study focuses on the Chinese context. However, we also recognize the potential global relevance of our findings. The concept of cultural values influencing emergency response can be applied in other countries with similar cultural characteristics or in the process of cross - cultural learning and adaptation. Current research also lacks in - depth exploration of the application of cultural values in the field of public health emergencies. Community health service centers, as a crucial part of the primary - level medical and health service system, play a pivotal role in responding to the COVID − 19 pandemic. Therefore, this study focuses on the impact of cultural values on the COVID − 19 response capabilities of community health service centers, aiming to provide theoretical support and practical guidance for enhancing their pandemic response abilities.

Social capital

Since the concept of social capital was first proposed, numerous sociologists have conducted in - depth research on it and offered different definitions. James Coleman believed that social capital is the capital property of social - structural resources owned by individuals, encompassing aspects such as trust, norms, and mutual benefit [12]. Robert Putnam pointed out that social capital is the organizational network environment in which people are situated. A well - established relationship network construction helps participants to act together more effectively in pursuit of common goals, thereby enhancing social efficiency [13]. Putnam’s definition has currently received fundamental recognition and widespread acceptance from various quarters. Alejandro Portes, on the other hand, argued that social capital is the ability of individuals within a network or a broader social structure to mobilize scarce resources [14]. In terms of domestic research, the definition of social capital in China exhibits diverse characteristics. Zhang Qizi defined social capital as social networks, viewing them both as a crucial type of interpersonal relationship and an important way of resource allocation [15]. Bian Yanjie defined social capital as “the connections between actors and society and the ability to acquire scarce resources through these connections” [16].From the perspective of research levels, the academic community generally divides social capital into three levels: micro, meso, and macro. Micro - level social capital is mainly applicable to the study of personal social networks, focusing on the individual level. Macro - level social capital adheres to the view of embedded structures and mainly focuses on government - level research. Meso - level social capital, which is often associated with structured social networks and organizational - level research, needs to be clearly distinguished from general organizational networks. While organizational networks emphasize the formal and informal connections within and between organizations, meso - level social capital goes beyond this by highlighting the social relationships and resources embedded within these networks. It focuses on the organizational level but also takes into account the social dynamics and interactions that contribute to the mobilization of resources [17]. At present, research on macro - level and micro - level social capital has been relatively abundant. Given that the research object of this study is community health service centers, which belong to the organizational level, this study proceeds from the perspective of meso - level social capital. Nahapiet and Ghoshal classified social capital into structural, relational, and cognitive types, and this study draws on this classification method [18]. Structural social capital focuses on the structural characteristics of social relationship networks, such as network size, density, and connection patterns. Relational social capital emphasizes interpersonal relationships formed based on emotional factors such as trust, reciprocity, and identification. Cognitive social capital focuses on cognitive - level factors such as common language, symbols, and values. Regarding the relationship between cultural values and social capital, it is not merely an assumed connection but one that can be thoroughly theorized. Cultural values play a crucial role in the production of social capital. Firstly, cultural values shape norms within a community or organization. Norms are the unwritten rules that guide behavior and interactions. When cultural values emphasize cooperation, mutual support, and integrity, they create a normative environment that encourages positive social interactions. For example, in a community health service center, if the cultural values prioritize patient - centered care and teamwork, it will lead to the development of norms where staff members are more likely to collaborate, share information, and assist each other, thereby fostering the growth of social capital. Secondly, cultural values contribute to the development of trust. Trust is a fundamental element of social capital. Cultural values that promote honesty, reliability, and fairness can build trust among individuals and between individuals and organizations. In the context of community health service centers responding to the COVID − 19 pandemic, when the cultural values emphasize transparency in communication and a commitment to public health, it can enhance trust between the center and the community it serves. This trust, in turn, facilitates the flow of information, resources, and support, strengthening the social capital of the center. Finally, cultural values create a shared understanding. Shared understanding refers to the common knowledge, beliefs, and interpretations that individuals within a group or organization hold. When cultural values are well - established and widely accepted, they provide a common framework for understanding situations and making decisions. In a community health service center, shared cultural values about the importance of pandemic prevention and control can lead to a unified approach among staff members. This shared understanding enables them to work more effectively together, mobilize resources more efficiently, and enhance the overall social capital of the center. This study tends to believe that social capital is a social relationship network that depends on interactions among people, between people and organizations, between organizations, and among members within an organization. It is built on the foundation of trust and reciprocity, enabling individuals and organizations to obtain various resources and assistance. It is also the ability of individuals and organizations to mobilize resources from social networks. This understanding of social capital, combined with the theorized relationship between cultural values and social capital, is conducive to in - depth exploration of its role in the process of community health service centers responding to the COVID − 19 pandemic, especially as a mediating variable, how it influences the relationship between cultural values and pandemic response capacity.

Emergency response capacity of community health service centers

Emergency management capacity generally refers to the ability to manage emergency affairs. The domestic academic community mainly explores the connotation of emergency capacity from the following different perspectives: Holistic Perspective: Liu Wanzhen and Chen Xingli believe that emergency management capacity is the ability of organizations within a certain region, as well as government agencies, enterprises, institutions, social organizations, families, and individuals in the region to prevent and respond to emergencies, encompassing mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery capabilities [19]. Yu Qing pointed out that the disaster-reduction-oriented community health emergency response capacity includes four aspects: prevention and preparedness, monitoring and early warning, emergency rescue and disposal, and crisis recovery and reconstruction [20]. Subject Perspective: Yang Yu and Wang Zilong proposed that the health emergency response capacity of the public is an individual psychological characteristic formed through planned and purposeful learning and training to effectively respond to emergencies, including early warning, self-rescue, and mutual-aid capacities [21]. Type Perspective: Taking Qingfeng Community in Jiangnan Middle Street, Haizhu District, Guangzhou City as an example, Zhang Xiaoli and Hou Xiaoxin believed that earthquake disaster prevention and mitigation capacity building mainly includes the construction of various emergency mechanisms, publicity arrays, building surveys, surveys of secondary disaster sources, earthquake-resistant fortification construction, self-rescue and mutual-rescue system construction, publicity and education activities, and emergency drills [22]. Link Perspective: Zhao Runzi, from the perspective of emergency preparedness, argued that emergency preparedness capacity is a comprehensive manifestation of the work results in institution building, risk assessment, monitoring and early warning, emergency plans, emergency drills, resource security, and publicity and education during the emergency preparedness process, taking into account the actual situation of the region and the level of economic and social development, and following the principles of “appropriate investment” and “adaptation to local conditions” [23]. Hu Fei, from the perspective of emergency response, proposed that emergency response capacity includes both the personal strength of the “first responders” and the overall strength of comprehensive emergency teams (Fei, H., 2018).In summary, most current research on health emergency response capacity is relatively broad and lacks specificity. Given that the COVID-19 pandemic has entered a stage of normalized prevention and control, research on the capacity to respond to the COVID-19 pandemic is of great practical significance. Internationally, the United States took the lead in conducting comprehensive evaluation research on the emergency management capacity for emergencies. James L et al. [24] constructed an evaluation procedure system, incorporating 13 government management functions such as emergency procedures, warning systems, financial management, and emergency drills into it, and evaluated key public departments in each state. As an area prone to earthquake disasters, Japan formulated the “Public Sector Disaster Prevention Capacity Evaluation System,” which covers nine aspects including emergency preparedness, disaster mitigation strategies, emergency reserve supplies, communication systems, risk assessment, rapid disaster reporting, emergency plans, organizational hierarchy, and publicity and drills [25]. George D. Haddow and Jane A. Bullock proposed that emergency management (or disaster management) capacity is a science about handling and avoiding risks, involving preparations before disasters, response to disasters, and support and social reconstruction after natural or human-made disasters [26]. Most studies by foreign experts and scholars on strategies for enhancing emergency management capacity in response to public health emergencies focus on the practice of public health drills and the integration of emerging digital technologies [27, 28]. Facing the challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic, [29] emphasized that it is crucial to fully leverage cutting-edge technological means such as artificial intelligence, telemedicine, mobile health, big data analytics, 5G communication, and the Internet of Things to provide robust support for pandemic prevention and control efforts. Shapiro J S believed that by establishing efficient Health Information Organizations (HIOs) and Health Information Exchange (HIE) platforms, the timeliness and accuracy of public health reporting can be significantly improved, thereby indirectly enhancing the overall emergency response capacity [30]. Therefore, this study defines the emergency response capacity of community health service centers as a combination of basic health emergency response capacity and COVID-19 pandemic response capacity, focusing on exploring the prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery management capacities demonstrated by community health service centers in preventing and handling the COVID-19 pandemic.

Impact of cultural values on community health service centers’ COVID-19 pandemic response capability

Fengping Y examined the impact of grassroots emergency culture on the evaluation of emergency management capabilities in Beijing [31]. This study argues that grassroots emergency culture can effectively prevent and respond to sudden public health incidents, ultimately reducing their associated losses. A well-developed emergency culture facilitates the mobilization of intangible resources during emergency activities, establishes common emergency goals, and motivates individuals to work toward them. This, in turn, decreases the cost of disaster prevention and relief and enhances emergency efficiency. Mannheim, in Germany, posited that cultural force represents people’s experiences and reflections on the significance of their own creative, practical activities. Integrating culture with emergency activities yields significant benefits [32]. Domestic scholars have also explored the relationship between cultural forces and emergency crisis management. Tiezhen [33] discussed the incorporation of cultural vitality into management and institutional innovation, leveraging the power of culture to enhance crisis emergency management. Furthermore, Jinwang L summarized the influence of cultural forces on emergency response capacity, highlighting that the external cultural environment of an organization, the internal cultural structure of an organization, and the cultural composition of managers and employees directly impact the execution of emergency activities [32].

Although there have been discussions on the connection between Cultural values and emergency response capacity at the domestic and international levels, empirical research on their interconnection is lacking. This study aimed to apply Cultural values to the context of health emergencies, specifically exploring the pathways and mechanisms of Cultural values on the COVID-19 pandemic response capability of community health service centers. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis: Hypothesis 1 (H1): Cultural values positively affects the COVID-19 pandemic response capability of community health service centers.

Impact of social capital on community health service centers’ COVID-19 pandemic response capability

Partelow conducted an empirical study on post-disaster recovery following the August 2018 earthquake in Lombok, Indonesia [34]. This study tested the hypothesis that social capital plays a key role in post-disaster community recovery. Partelow argued that social capital enables collective action by providing social, psychological, financial, and material resources for post-disaster self-organization. Individuals and communities are often connected through shared experiences, actions, and activities, which create emotional and meaningful ties. Lijun Z suggested that trust is essential in public crisis management [35]. Interpersonal trust is the primary mechanism for early warning in public crisis management. Trust from the public is crucial for the sustainability of non-governmental organizations (NGOs), whereas recognition and trust from the government are necessary for effective NGO operations. Governments rely on public trust as the foundation for management efforts and influence the efficacy of crisis management. The lack of trust at different levels in China has led to challenges in public crisis management, emphasizing the importance of building trust relationships among governments, NGOs, and individuals. Establishing trust, creating a conducive environment, fostering trust-based social capital, and promoting cooperation are essential for building a cohesive collective for effective public crisis management. Extensive research exists on social capital in the context of public crisis management. Studies have examined various aspects of social capital distribution in urban poverty, its influence on social governance, and its establishment. Yue highlighted the significant impact of the 2003 SARS epidemic in China, emphasizing the necessity of collective involvement in addressing major public crises (Yue, 2011). This involvement includes the leadership of NGOs in public welfare, the cultivation of strong social capital, and the effective utilization of social resources. The SARS epidemic demonstrates that whereas government capacity may be limited during crises, societal support is invaluable. Therefore, effective public crisis management requires collaboration between the government and NGOs, with a focus on consensus building and cooperative strategies. Yupei Z’s research [36] on community-based social capital suggests that communities with robust social resources are better equipped to recover from disasters [36]. Similarly, Furong G et al., emphasized the importance of building trust, reinforcing norms and laws, implementing control measures, and establishing cooperative networks online and offline to enhance governance capabilities [37]. Overall, existing studies have underscored the essential role of social capital in public crisis management by facilitating cooperation, coordination, and resource integration through trust, norms, and networks. Therefore, research on public crisis governance must consider the influence of social capital.Although existing research on social capital in public crisis emergency response centers primarily on ecological and environmental disasters, there is a noticeable gap in understanding sudden public health events, such as infectious diseases. This study aimed to bridge this gap by applying social capital theory to health emergency response, seeking to uncover novel insights and providing valuable research to enhance the emergency response capacities of community health service centers. As a result, we propose the following hypothesis: Hypothesis 2 (H2): Social capital positively influences the COVID-19 pandemic response capability of community health service centers.

Social capital plays a mediating role between cultural values and community health service centers’ COVID-19 pandemic response capability

In recent years, scholars have increasingly focused on the role of social capital as a mediating factor in crisis emergency response. Xuedong et al. [17] highlighted that social capital is dynamic and is influenced by factors such as capabilities, economic status, and culture. Haojie [38] showed that social capital mediates between government support and emergency organization response capability, facilitating collaboration in disaster relief efforts. Biao et al. [39] found that trust-based social capital partially mediated the relationship between institutional perception and compliance behavior in COVID-19 prevention and control. Additionally, An discovered that organizational social capital partially mediated the relationship between organizational learning and the resilience of public emergency organizations. Current research has extensively examined the mediating role of social capital on factors such as support intensity, learning ability, and emergency capabilities, providing strong theoretical support for its significance as a mediating variable in public crisis emergency research. However, there is a gap in the literature regarding the mediating role of different types of social capital between cultural values and health emergency capabilities. Therefore, this study investigates the mediating role of different types of social capital in the relationship between cultural values and health emergency capabilities. We propose the following sub-hypotheses:

H3a

Structural social capital mediates the relationship between cultural values and community health service centers’ COVID-19 response capability. This refers to the network structures, such as formal and informal connections, that facilitate the flow of information and resources, thereby enhancing the centers’ ability to respond to the pandemic.

H3b

Relational social capital mediates the relationship between cultural values and community health service centers’ COVID-19 response capability. This involves the trust, mutual understanding, and shared norms among individuals and groups, which can foster cooperation and coordination in emergency situations.

H3c

Cognitive social capital mediates the relationship between cultural values and community health service centers’ COVID-19 response capability. This encompasses the shared interpretations, meanings, and mental models that enable effective communication and problem-solving during the pandemic response.

Methods

Survey participants

This study specifically targeted individuals employed at community health service centers in Zhejiang Province. The inclusion criteria required participants to have been employed at the center for a minimum of six months, possess normal language comprehension abilities, have no history of mental disorders, and participate voluntarily. The survey used stratified random sampling to select cities in Zhejiang Province based on economic conditions. Hangzhou and Ningbo were chosen from areas with good economic conditions; Wenzhou, Jiaxing, and Huzhou from those with medium economic conditions; and Lishui from areas with relatively low economic conditions. Community health service centers were randomly selected from these cities for the survey. In each city, 15 community health service centers were selected using a convenience sampling method, resulting in 983 questionnaires being distributed from March 1th to May 20th in 2023. During the questionnaire distribution process, a combination of online and paper-based questionnaires was employed to ensure the broad scope and convenience of data collection. Meanwhile, a standardized data collection procedure was strictly adhered to, guaranteeing the anonymity of the data collection process and fully protecting the privacy of respondents. Subsequently, the data from valid questionnaires was organized and entered.

Survey tools

The survey used a self-developed questionnaire to evaluate the COVID-19 response capabilities of community health service centers (The English version of the survey can be found in the supplementary file). The questionnaire comprised four sections: basic demographic characteristics, including age, sex, educational level, marital status, field of study, job position, years of work experience, and type of community. A Cultural values survey, based on the cultural assessment indicators by Fengping [31], consisted of three items that were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate stronger Cultural values. The social capital survey was adapted from the social capital measurement scale developed by Jinyan et al. [40] and customized for the study objectives. This study includes three dimensions, with 18 items covering structural, relational, and cognitive social capital. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better social capital. A questionnaire was developed to assess the COVID-19 response capabilities of community health service centers. Drawing from Zi et al.,’s primary prevention measures questionnaire and informed by relevant literature, the questionnaire was refined to address the current challenges faced by these centers [41]. This includes areas such as personnel control, participation in coordination mechanisms, intelligent prevention and control using big data, and medical order. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with one indicating “strongly disagree” and five indicating ”strongly agree.” A higher score indicates a stronger pandemic response capability of the community health service center (Supplementary Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Since this study collected data through self-administered questionnaires, with both the independent variable (perception of assessment) and the dependent variable (behavioral intention) filled out by the same respondents, there may be a risk of Common Method Bias. Therefore, the following two methods were employed to test for Common Method Bias:① Harman’s Single-Factor Test: All measurement items were included in an exploratory factor analysis. If the variance explained by the first factor is less than 50%, it indicates that Common Method Bias is not severe.② CFA Marker Variable Technique: A theoretically unrelated marker variable (e.g., H5) was selected, and a CFA model incorporating this marker variable was constructed. The correlations between the marker variable and other latent variables were examined. If these correlations are not significant, it suggests that Common Method Bias is not pronounced.In this study, SPSS version 25.0 and AMOS 28.0 were used to analyze the survey data. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to assess the reliability of the scales, whereas factor analysis was used to examine construct validity. Frequency and percentage were used to describe categorical variables, and the mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with quartiles represented continuous variables based on distribution. Variations in emergency response capability scores among community health service centers with different demographic characteristics were analyzed using the t-test, ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, U test, and H test, depending on data distribution. Five regression models were developed to explore the impact of systems, Cultural values, and social capital on COVID-19 response capability. A structural equation model in AMOS 28.0 investigates the mechanism of impact on pandemic response capability, with Cultural values and social capital as exogenous latent variables and pandemic response capability as the endogenous variable. The maximum-likelihood method was used for model fitting, with the Bollen-Stine bootstrap for correction if the fit was inadequate. The parameter estimation results and pathways of the effects were interpreted in the final model.

Results

Basic situation description

Reliability and validity of questionnaire

The Cronbach’s alpha values for the Cultural values scale (0.969), all three dimensions of the social capital scale (above 0.9), and the community health service center’s COVID-19 pandemic response capability scale (0.974) indicated good reliability (S1 Table). Additionally, the KMO values, Bartlett’s test of sphericity, and factor loadings for each scale demonstrated good construct validity. For instance, factor loadings for Cultural values ranged from 0.961 to 0.976, whereas factor loadings for network interaction, network size, and community participation on the structural social capital scale ranged from 0.857 to 0.899, 0.650 to 0.709, and 0.739 to 0.818, respectively. Similarly, factor loadings for community trust and mutual benefits on the relational social capital scale ranged from 0.742 to 0.824 and 0.687 to 0.737, respectively. The factor loadings for cognitive, social capital ranged from 0.938 to 0.966. The COVID-19 pandemic response capability scale also exhibited good construct validity, with factor loadings ranging from 0.813 to 0.936 (S2 Table).

Basic information about the survey participants

The survey included 983 community health service center staff members, with a predominantly female representation of 78%. The most represented age group was 31–40 years, accounting for 37.5% of the participants. Most participants (58.7%) were not affiliated with any political party. Regarding educational background, 68% held a bachelor’s degree. Approximately 79.8% of the participants were married. Professionals comprised the largest group, with 41.1% of the participants, whereas junior professional titles were the most common (49.2%). In terms of tenure, 72.5% had worked for more than six years. Concerning workplace systems, 80.2% worked in communities with a hazard identification and risk assessment management system, and 88.0% worked in communities with a clear accountability system for public health emergencies. Regarding technology adoption, 77.6% affirmed the substantial utility of smart tools in combating COVID-19. In addition, as shown in the S3 Table, 72.6% of the staff worked in general communities.

Current status of cultural values, social capital, and the COVID-19 pandemic response capability of community health service centers

The results revealed that the average score for Cultural values was 4.24, with an SD of 0.56. The average score for structural social capital was slightly lower, at 4.1,3, with an SD of 0.56. Within the three dimensions of structural social capital, community participation ranked highest, with an average score of 4.25, with an SD of 0.62, whereas network size had the lowest average score of 4.00, with an SD of 0.69. In relational social capital, the average score was 4.22 (SD = 0.52). Among the two dimensions of relational social capital, the community trust dimension scored the highest average of 4.23, with an SD of 0.55, whereas the mutual benefit dimension scored the lowest average of 4.21, with an SD of 0.57. The average cognitive social capital score was 4.19 (SD = 0.60). Finally, the average COVID-19 response capability score was 4.31, with an SD of 0.54 (Table 1).

Cultural values, social capital, and COVID-19 pandemic response capability scores of community health service centers

Univariate analysis of the COVID-19 pandemic response capability

This study analyzed how demographic characteristics and institutional settings impact the COVID-19 response capability of community health service centers. Results indicated statistically significant differences in response capability scores according to job position and community type (< 0.05). In addition, there were significant differences in scores concerning the presence of risk identification systems, clear accountability systems for public health emergencies, and the evaluation of smart tools in the pandemic response (all P < 0.001). Communities with risk identification systems had higher scores (4.37 ± 0.51) than those without (4.16 ± 0.61) or unaware of such systems (4.02 ± 0.55), with statistically significant differences (all P < 0.001). Similarly, communities with clear accountability systems had higher scores (4.36 ± 0.52) than those without (4.18 ± 0.63) or unaware of any (3.87 ± 0.45), with significant differences (all P < 0.001). Evaluation of smart tools also showed significant differences in scores, with healthcare workers valuing smart tools for pandemic control having higher scores (4.41 ± 0.52) compared to those who found them helpful (3.99 ± 0.46) or moderately useful (3.79 ± 0.65), all statistically significant (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 5).

Correlation analysis between cultural values, social capital, and the COVID-19 pandemic response capability

Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to investigate the associations between cultural cohesiveness, structural social capital, relational social capital, cognitive, social capital, and the response capability of community health service centers to the COVID-19 pandemic. The results indicate a significant positive correlation between cultural cohesiveness and pandemic response capability (r = 0.765, P < 0.01). Structural social capital dimensions such as network interactions, network size, and community participation showed positive correlations with pandemic response capability, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.496 to 0.614 (all P < 0.01). The highest correlation was observed between community participation and pandemic response capability (r = 0.614), whereas the lowest was observed between network interactions and pandemic response capability (r = 0.496). Similarly, all dimensions of relational social capital, including community trust and mutual aid, showed positive correlations with pandemic response capability, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.665 to 0.693 (all P < 0.01). The highest correlation was observed between mutual aid and pandemic response capability (r = 0.693), and the lowest was observed between community trust and pandemic response capability (r = 0.665). Cognitive social capital also displayed a positive correlation with pandemic response capability (r = 0.740, P < 0.01) (Supplementary Table 4).

Hierarchical regression analysis of the COVID-19 pandemic response capability at community health service centers

Hierarchical linear regression analysis was conducted to assess the COVID-19 pandemic response capability of community health service centers. The variance inflation factors (VIF) for each independent variable were all below 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The model included demographic characteristics and relevant policies, structural social capital, relational social capital, cognitive, social capital, and cultural cohesiveness, all of which were statistically significant. The changes in the R-squared (ΔR²) values were 0.164 (ΔF = 17.26, P < 0.001) for demographic characteristics and relevant policies, 0.281 (ΔF = 163.39, P < 0.001) for structural social capital, 0.122 (ΔF = 135.44, P < 0.001) for relational social capital, 0.07 (ΔF = 187.288, P < 0.001) for cognitive social capital, and 0.024 (ΔF = 67.805, P < 0.001) for cultural cohesiveness.

In Layer 5, the study found that higher evaluations of the role of smart tools during the pandemic were associated with lower scores for COVID-19 response capability (β = − 0.088, P < 0.001). It may be because the use of functional tools has increased the workload of medical staff, which in turn undermines their COVID-19 response capability.Furthermore, within the dimensions of structural social capital, community health service centers demonstrated increased pandemic response capability with higher scores for community participation (β = 0.114, P < 0.001), community trust (β = 0.128, P < 0.001), and mutual benefit (β = 0.073, P < 0.05) within relational social capital, cognitive social capital (β = 0.233, P < 0.001) within cognitive social capital, and cultural cohesiveness (β = 0.305, P < 0.001) within cultural cohesiveness (S4 Table).

Structural equation model for COVID-19 pandemic response capability

Model construction and fit

To investigate the impact of structural, relational, cognitive, and cultural cohesiveness on the COVID-19 pandemic response capability of community health service centers, we developed a model using AMOS software version 28.0. Initially, the model’s fit indices did not meet the desired criteria, which led to expansion based on the covariance modification indices. Additional paths were added between the specific residuals, resulting in the final revised model depicted in Fig. 1. The model fit results, as shown in Table 2, indicate that, despite the large sample size, the chi-squared to degrees of freedom ratio (X2/df) was relatively high. The root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was 0.079, which fell within an acceptable range of < 0.08. The incremental fit index (IFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and comparative fit index (CFI) demonstrated excellent values, exceeding 0.9.It indicates that the overall fit of the revised model is good.

Discussion

Analysis of the current status of cultural values in community health service centers

This study reveals that the average score of cultural values in community health service centers in Zhejiang Province is 4.24 ± 0.56, which is higher than the result reported in Jianbo Z’s research [42], indicating a relatively high level. This phenomenon can be analyzed from multiple perspectives. Compared with large general hospitals, community health service centers generally have lower daily business volumes and work pressures, allowing staff to allocate more time and energy to cultural education. In daily management, these centers place greater emphasis on cultural promotion and construction, thereby enhancing cohesion and a sense of mission among internal members. From a theoretical standpoint, different cultural backgrounds shape cultural values in distinct ways. In China, with its collectivist culture, collective interests are prioritized and group collaboration is emphasized. This cultural trait makes it easier for community health service centers to foster a sense of collective honor and responsibility among staff, encouraging them to work together towards common goals. In contrast, societies with individualistic cultures place greater emphasis on personal independence and autonomy, which may lead to different formation and functioning mechanisms of cultural values compared to collectivist societies.Among the three items measuring cultural values, the statement “Your community has corresponding cultural walls to promote its vision and cultural characteristics” scored the highest at 4.25 ± 0.563, which is higher than the result reported in Feng’an W’s research [43]. This may be because Zhejiang Province has prioritized the strengthening of core socialist values and issued policy documents to provide clear guidance and support for grassroots organizations, thereby promoting active practice in community health service centers. This phenomenon reflects the positive role of policy guidance and cultural activities in promoting cultural construction in grassroots organizations within China’s unique cultural context. However, regional differences may exist, and the situation in Zhejiang Province cannot be simply generalized to the entire country.From a global perspective, the cultural construction of community health services varies across different cultural backgrounds. Some Western countries, in their cultural construction of community health services, place greater emphasis on protecting personal privacy and conveying the concept of personalized services, with their cultural values more reflected in meeting individual unique needs [44]. In contrast, under China’s collectivist culture, the cultural values of community health service centers focus more on collective interests and group collaboration. The high level of cultural values in community health service centers in Zhejiang Province not only reflects the achievements of internal management and cultural construction but also demonstrates the positive effects of shaping employees’ sense of mission and values within the unique Chinese cultural context, providing strong support for improving service quality and employee cohesion.

Analysis of the current status of social capital in community health service centers

This study indicates that the average score of social capital in community health service centers in Zhejiang Province is 4.17 ± 0.50, which is significantly higher than the result reported in Ling H’s research [45]. This reflects significant advantages in social capital, a high level of development, a rich total amount of social capital, and strong organizational social relationship networks and resource mobilization capabilities.From a global comparative perspective, factors such as regional economic development levels and socio-cultural backgrounds influence the formation and development of social capital. Zhejiang Province’s rapid economic development provides a material foundation for social capital accumulation. Among the three types of social capital, relational social capital has the highest overall average score (4.22 ± 0.52), consistent with the research result of Yu et al. [46]. This indicates that the region values the establishment and development of interpersonal relationships, possibly due to the high quality and educational level of grassroots medical staff, who possess stronger communication and collaboration awareness and abilities. High-level relational social capital facilitates information flow, resource sharing, and collaborative cooperation, playing a crucial role in improving the quality and efficiency of community health services. In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, after experiencing the fight against the epidemic, grassroots medical staff have become more aware of the importance of cohesion and collaborative governance, establishing closer relationship networks with relevant organizations such as neighborhood committees and sub-district offices, as well as residents. This aligns with international research emphasizing the importance of good interpersonal relationship networks in resource integration and crisis response in crisis situations. Additionally, among structural social capital, the community participation dimension has the highest mean score (4.25 ± 0.62), while the network size dimension has the lowest (4.00 ± 0.69), similar to the research result of Jiayuan L [47]. This indicates that active participation of community residents in community affairs helps enhance governance efficacy and service targeting at the community level. However, this also suggests that there is room for improvement in the cooperation and resource integration between community health service centers and external organizations, which can be enhanced by expanding partnerships and strengthening cross-sectoral collaboration. Internationally, some successful community health service models emphasize multi-sectoral cooperation and extensive social participation, which community health service centers in Zhejiang Province can learn from to optimize their structural social capital. Among relational social capital, the community trust dimension has the highest mean score (4.23 ± 0.55), consistent with the research of Bochao [48]. This may be because Zhejiang Province’s “Urban Health Check” plan has achieved remarkable results, with community health service centers achieving full coverage and being closely linked to residents’ lives. During the pandemic, they played a crucial role as the frontline of grassroots prevention and control, greatly earning residents’ trust. The establishment and maintenance of such trust relationships are crucial in the development of community health services in many countries, as good trust relationships can improve residents’ acceptance and adherence to health services. From an international comparison perspective, different countries have varying experiences in cultivating and utilizing social capital. Some Nordic countries have cultivated highly trusting and actively participating social capital through a comprehensive social welfare system and community self-governance model, enabling efficient operation of community health services supported by good social capital [49]. Community health service centers in Zhejiang Province have achieved remarkable achievements in the development and utilization of social capital, but they need to continue to optimize and strengthen various dimensions to further improve the quality, accessibility, and sustainability of community health services, meet healthcare needs, and safeguard residents’ health and well-being.

Analysis of the current status of COVID-19 response capability in community health service centers

The survey shows that the overall average score of COVID-19 response capability in community health service centers in Zhejiang Province is 4.31 ± 0.54, which is significantly higher than the research results of scholars such as Kun and Yewei [50], Yewei et al. [51]. This may be because Zhejiang Province has taken a series of early, proactive, and comprehensive measures in pandemic prevention and control. It was the first to initiate a first-level response to public health emergencies, establish a leading group for prevention and control work, and launch a province-wide joint prevention and control mechanism, forming excellent Zhejiang experience. Meanwhile, it adopted a precise smart control mechanism of “one map, one code, one index,” which focuses on constructing a pandemic map, health codes, and a precise smart control index, enabling community health service centers to pay more attention to classified and precise prevention and control work, facilitating dynamic management of the population within their jurisdictions, greatly improving their ability to respond to the pandemic, and demonstrating good emergency performance.From an international comparative perspective, different countries and regions have varying pandemic prevention and control strategies and mechanisms. Some countries emphasize the widespread application of technology, such as using big data and artificial intelligence for pandemic monitoring and prediction [52]; some countries focus on social mobilization and public participation, improving public awareness of epidemic prevention and self-management abilities through extensive publicity and education [53]. Zhejiang Province’s precise smart control mechanism of “one map, one code, one index,” combining technological means and refined management concepts, plays a unique role in pandemic prevention and control, providing beneficial references for other regions in China and even the international community. However, we should also recognize that different countries and regions have different national conditions, and the Zhejiang experience cannot be completely replicated; it is necessary to explore suitable pandemic prevention and control models based on local actual conditions.

The impact of cultural values on COVID-19 response capability

The research results indicate that cultural values have a significant positive impact on COVID-19 response capability, consistent with the research conclusion of Meng et al., [54]. This impact stems from the sudden outbreak of the pandemic and its severe threat, which brought enormous psychological pressure and panic to the public. Community health service centers fully utilized cultural forces to transform cultural power into grassroots emergency motivation.From the perspective of cultural theory, different cultural types have varying impacts on individual and group behaviors. Under China’s collectivist cultural background, traditional cultural values inspire people’s collective consciousness and sense of belonging, prompting them to unite and face challenges together in difficult times. This unity and collaboration help integrate social resources and improve the efficiency and effectiveness of pandemic response. Especially in the context of the global pandemic, the call of Chinese traditional culture has inspired the great spirit of solidarity and mutual assistance in the fight against the pandemic, prompting medical staff to move towards common emergency goals, strengthening their beliefs and determination to face the pandemic, and enhancing their response capabilities. The values and moral norms in cultural traditions influence people’s behavioral patterns and choices, serving as guiding principles for behavior in the face of the pandemic, prompting people to consciously comply with prevention and control measures, reducing the risk of pandemic transmission, facilitating the orderly conduct of emergency work, and further improving pandemic response capabilities. Compared with some societies emphasizing individualistic cultures internationally, China’s collective actions and unity and collaboration in pandemic prevention and control are more prominent. In regions dominated by individualistic cultures, pandemic prevention and control may place greater emphasis on personal protection and individual responsibility [55], while under the influence of collectivist culture, community health service centers in China can better organize and mobilize the masses, forming a powerful collective force for fighting the pandemic. Therefore, the role of cultural values in pandemic response cannot be ignored, and the government and society should value and utilize the power of traditional culture, strengthen cultural education and dissemination, and enhance the overall anti-pandemic capability of society. However, we should also recognize that the role of cultural values may vary due to regional cultural differences, and it is necessary to guide and leverage it in combination with local cultural characteristics in practical applications.

The impact of social capital on COVID-19 response capability

The research results show that social capital has a significant positive impact on COVID-19 response capability, consistent with the research result of Yu et al. [46]. Among the impacts of social capital, community trust in relational social capital and community participation in structural social capital have been identified as key factors in pandemic response capability, consistent with the research conclusion of Furong G [37]. Community trust is the core of relational social capital. Through the establishment of trust, community health service centers can obtain more collaboration and support, improving the effectiveness of health emergency activities. In the field of international health emergencies, trust relationships are also regarded as key factors. For example, in some international rescue operations, rescue organizations can carry out rescue work more smoothly and improve rescue efficiency by establishing trust relationships with local communities [56]. Structural social capital’s community participation prompts multiple forces to participate in pandemic prevention and control, forming a collaborative governance situation and improving the comprehensive ability to combat the pandemic. The reason for this positive impact is that when community health service centers carry out health emergency activities in the context of a pandemic outbreak, if they can gain the trust of other organizations and residents, they will receive more cooperation and support. The establishment and maintenance of social capital through joint participation and mutual trust help deepen the professional ethics of medical staff in community health service centers, form a good working atmosphere, and are more conducive to gathering multiple forces to form a diversified collaborative governance in the face of the pandemic. Relevant research points out that trust-based social capital helps the efficient transmission of pandemic information and improves response capabilities. In international pandemic prevention and control, information sharing and rapid transmission are crucial for timely control of pandemic spread, and good trust relationships can promote smooth information flow among different subjects.In addition, the research result of Qionglian [57] confirms the positive impact of cognitive social capital on pandemic response capability. Cognitive social capital helps community health service centers operate in an orderly manner according to regulations, ensure the effective conduct of emergency work, prevent chaotic situations, and reduce the probability of accidents. Overall, social capital plays a positive role in the COVID-19 response, emphasizing the importance of community participation, trust establishment, and norm enforcement. The government and society should strive to strengthen the cultivation of social capital and improve the overall level of social health crisis response. Different countries and regions have varying experiences in cultivating and utilizing social capital [58], and community health service centers in Zhejiang Province can learn from international advanced practices to further optimize their social capital structure and improve pandemic response capabilities.

Analysis of the mediating effect of social capital between cultural values and COVID-19 response capability

The structural equation model analysis results of this study show that cultural values can positively influence COVID-19 response capability through three types of social capital, similar to the research result of Xu’e [59]. Among them, relational social capital has the strongest indirect effect, consistent with the research result of Biao et al. [60]. This may be because when community health service centers with positive values gain the trust of other organizations and residents, they form trust-based social capital, prompting residents to be willing to cooperate and consciously accept queries, inspections, and vaccinations, further improving the detection rate and reducing the infection rate, thereby enhancing the health emergency capability of community health service centers. In international health service research, the importance of trust relationships in the process of medical service provision and acceptance is widely recognized. Patients’ trust in medical institutions can improve their adherence to treatment plans, thereby improving treatment outcomes. In pandemic prevention and control, residents’ trust in community health service centers can also promote the effective implementation of various prevention and control measures. Additionally, structural social capital also has a strong effect, similar to the research conclusion of Arianpoor Arash and Naeimi Tajdar Seyyed Sajjad [61]. This may be because in community health service centers with a good cultural atmosphere, or between them and other organizations and residents, a good participation network is established. When facing the COVID-19 pandemic, the community can spontaneously organize relevant volunteers to support the medical work and prevention and control tasks of community health service centers, reducing work pressure and improving response capabilities. In international community development practices, the construction of community participation networks is regarded as an important means to enhance community resilience and crisis response capabilities [62]. The practical experience of community health service centers in Zhejiang Province provides beneficial references for the development of international community health services. By strengthening cultural construction and cultivating social capital, the response capability of communities in public health events such as pandemic prevention and control can be effectively improved.

Research limitations and future prospects

(1) Research Methodology: This study employs a cross - sectional research design. While it can preliminarily infer relationships between variables, it is difficult to directly establish causal connections. Subsequent research could incorporate a longitudinal design, such as conducting long - term follow - up observations, to precisely dissect the relationships and mechanisms among influencing factors. Additionally, combining in - depth interviews and other methods can help collect more specific and in - depth data, thereby enriching the research content.In terms of the structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis, it should be noted that certain model modifications, such as the inclusion of correlated error terms, were made. The rationale behind these modifications was to account for potential shared variances among measurement items that were not captured by the original hypothesized model. For example, some items measuring similar constructs might have been influenced by common external factors or shared measurement biases, leading to non - zero correlations between their error terms. By incorporating these correlated error terms, we aimed to improve the model’s fit and more accurately represent the underlying relationships among the variables. However, this approach also has its limitations and requires further exploration in future research. (2) Sample Selection: The sample in this study is focused on Zhejiang Province. Although stratified sampling was conducted across six cities, the final sample selection was influenced by convenience factors. This may, to some extent, limit the generalizability of the research findings. Future research will expand the sample coverage to include regions with different geographical locations and economic development levels, thereby enhancing sample representativeness. (3) External Validity: This study has not been cross - validated with authoritative tools such as those from the WHO or OECD, which may have some impact on the applicability of the research findings in broader contexts. Subsequent research will introduce authoritative tools for comparative analysis to further validate and improve the external validity of the study. (4) Model Fit: Due to the large sample size in this study, the X² statistic is highly sensitive to model misspecification, resulting in a X²/df ratio that exceeds conventional thresholds. Moreover, robust correction methods such as the Bollen–Stine bootstrap were not employed. As mentioned earlier, although we made some model modifications like adding correlated error terms to improve the fit, the lack of robust correction methods remains a limitation. Future research will explore and utilize more appropriate correction methods to enhance the accuracy and reliability of model fit, enabling a more precise revelation of the relationships among variables.

Conclusions

(1) Within the scope of this study, community health service centers in Zhejiang Province have generally demonstrated a relatively satisfactory performance in responding to the COVID − 19 pandemic. However, there is still significant room for improvement in their emergency prevention and post - disaster recovery capabilities. It should be noted that these findings are specific to the study population in Zhejiang and may not be directly generalizable to other regions. (2) Among the community health service centers in the study, the establishment of hazard identification and risk assessment management systems, as well as accountability systems for public health emergencies in the communities where they are located, along with the degree of emphasis placed on intelligent tools, all have an impact on their COVID − 19 response capabilities. Specifically, centers with well - established hazard identification and risk assessment management systems and accountability systems for public health emergencies, and those that attach great importance to intelligent tools, tend to perform relatively better in responding to the pandemic. This finding directly supports the need to focus on these aspects for improving response capabilities. (3) Both cultural values and social capital have an influence on COVID − 19 response capabilities within the study population. Cultural values, as well as structural, relational, and cognitive social capital, are all positively correlated with COVID − 19 response capabilities. Cultural values not only directly affect COVID − 19 response capabilities but also indirectly influence them through the three types of social capital. This empirical evidence forms the basis for the following recommendations. (4) Based on the above empirical findings, to enhance the COVID − 19 response capabilities of community health service centers within the study context, the following recommendations are made: Strengthen cultural atmosphere construction: Given that cultural values have a direct and indirect positive impact on COVID − 19 response capabilities, it is recommended to strengthen the construction of a cultural atmosphere and fully leverage the role of cultural values. This can be achieved by promoting relevant cultural values through community activities, training programs, and public awareness campaigns. Reinforce social capital: Since structural, relational, and cognitive social capital are positively correlated with COVID − 19 response capabilities, efforts should be made to reinforce social capital. This can involve organizing community - building activities to foster better relationships among community members, encouraging information sharing and cooperation, and building a favorable social network to provide strong soft - environment support. Improve normative designs: The establishment of hazard identification and risk assessment management systems and accountability systems for public health emergencies has been shown to affect response capabilities. Therefore, it is recommended to improve normative designs to offer solid institutional guarantees. This can include developing clear guidelines and procedures for hazard identification, risk assessment, and emergency accountability. Utilize big data technology: As the degree of emphasis on intelligent tools is related to better response capabilities, it is advisable to make full use of big data technology and implement intelligent emergency response to improve efficiency. This can involve using big data for real - time monitoring, early warning, and resource allocation during the pandemic.
