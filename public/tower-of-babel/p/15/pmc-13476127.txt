Double Thoracic Duct Cyst: Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Luis Pacheco-Ojeda, Lennyn Albán-León, Patricia Pontón-Villalba, Alison Ruiz-Chico, Carlos Ríos-Deidán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The thoracic duct, the largest lymphatic vessel in the human body, originates from the cisterna chyli in the abdomen, ascends through the thorax, and drains into the left venous angle, at the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins in the lower cervical region. A lymph collection (lymphocele) resulting from persistent leakage of a lymphatic duct in this region is occasionally observed following surgical procedures. In contrast, true cysts arising from the thoracic duct are exceedingly rare.We report a unique case of a double cervical thoracic duct cyst in an elderly patient, successfully managed with surgical treatment. The diagnostic workup, therapeutic options, and treatment outcomes, including complications, are discussed.

Introduction

The thoracic duct is the largest lymphatic vessel in the human body. It measures approximately 38 to 45 cm in length, originates from the cisterna chyli in the abdomen, ascends through the thorax, and drains into the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins in the lower left cervical region. Its diameter ranges from 2 to 5 mm, and it is responsible for lymphatic drainage of the entire body except for the right side of the head and neck, the right hemithorax, and the right upper limb [1].

The wall of the thoracic duct consists of three layers - intima, media, and adventitia - and includes a basement membrane. At the junction between the lymphatic and venous systems, a bicuspid valve prevents venous reflux into the lymphatic circulation [1].

A lymphocele is defined as a collection of lymph resulting from persistent leakage of a lymphatic duct and lacks an epithelial lining, most commonly occurring after surgical procedures. In contrast, a lymphatic cyst is lined by epithelium and usually represents a developmental malformation of the lymphatic system. Congenital weakness of the thoracic duct wall and inflammation-mediated degeneration have been proposed as predisposing factors for cyst formation [2]. Thoracic duct cysts are exceedingly rare, and most reported cases originate from the thoracic or abdominal segments [3]. In a recent systematic review focusing on cervical thoracic duct cysts, only 16 cases were identified [4]. Previously, Abelardo et al. reported a case and reviewed 47 cases, of which all but one were left-sided lesions [5].

Given the rarity of this condition, no standardized treatment has been established. Both surgical and conservative management strategies have been described. We report a unique case of a double cervical thoracic duct cyst in an elderly patient.

Case presentation

An 85-year-old otherwise healthy male patient presented with an asymptomatic left lower cervical mass, first noticed one year earlier. The lesion had remained stable in size and asymptomatic. His personal and family medical history were unremarkable.

Physical examination revealed a firm, renitent, fixed mass measuring approximately 5 × 4 × 4 cm at level IV of the left neck (Figure 1).

No cervical lymphadenopathy was detected. Ultrasound demonstrated a 5.2 × 1.9 x 3.0 cm bilobulated cystic lesion in the left supraclavicular region (Figure 2).

Bilobed cystic lesion located at the medial aspect of the left supraclavicular region

An MRI was performed. Coronal Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR) sequences revealed two well-defined cystic lesions at the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins. Axial T2-weighted images confirmed these cystic structures, located posterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle and in close contact with the left thyroid lobe and the ipsilateral common carotid artery. One cyst was situated laterally, while the second extended medially. Preserved cleavage planes between the two lesions were clearly identified (Figures 3, 4).

Contrast-enhanced axial T1-weighted image at the level of the thyroid gland. The cystic lesions at the left jugular-subclavian junction show no enhancement after intravenous contrast administration (red arrows).

The patient underwent surgical exploration through a horizontal supraclavicular approach. Two cystic lesions were identified and carefully dissected. The lateral cyst, measuring 5 × 4 × 4 cm, had a pedicle arising from the terminal portion of the thoracic duct, which was ligated before cyst excision (Figure 5).

Left-sided thoracic duct cyst

A second, independent cyst was found medially, extending to the right side behind the common carotid artery and internal jugular vein and posterior to the hypopharynx. This cyst had a long pedicle originating from the right side of the neck, possibly from the right lymphatic duct; it was ligated and completely excised (Figures 6, 7).

Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient remained in excellent condition at seven-month follow-up.

Two specimens were submitted for pathological examination and were classified as thoracic duct cysts. Each tubular cyst measured approximately 4 × 1.8 cm and had a smooth, shiny, grayish external surface with attached adipose tissue. The wall thickness was approximately 2 mm. The internal surface was also smooth and grayish. Microscopic examination revealed a cyst wall composed of an intima lined by flat endothelial cells, a media layer consisting of circularly arranged smooth muscle fibers and fibrous connective tissue, and an adventitia composed of fibrousconnective tissue. The lumen was dilated and filled with proteinaceous material containing numerous lymphocytes. No evidence of malignancy was identified (Figures 8, 9).

Discussion

According to Kakamad et al.'s review, the mean age of patients with cervical thoracic duct cyst was 47.8 ± 17.8 years, making our 85-year-old patient considerably older than previously reported cases [4]. The condition appears to be more common in females [4]. Approximately half of the patients are asymptomatic, as in our case. When present, symptoms may include cough, dyspnea, dysphagia, neck pain or swelling, and hoarseness, most of which are attributable to compression of adjacent structures [1].

The clinical differential diagnosis includes branchial cleft cyst, lymphangioma, lymphatic malformations, cystic or lymphomatous lymphadenopathy, nerve sheath tumors, carotid or subclavian artery pseudoaneurysms, and cysts arising from the thymus or parathyroid glands [5].

Fine-needle aspiration biopsy and lymphangiography have been rarely used. In contrast, ultrasound, CT, and MRI are highly accurate and commonly employed imaging modalities [6]. Lymphoscintigraphy has been considered the diagnostic gold standard; however, with the advent of high-resolution CT and MRI, its use has become uncommon [7].

Definitive diagnosis is established by histopathological examination, with immunohistochemical staining of the endothelial lining confirming the lymphatic origin of the cyst [3].

Management options include observation for small, asymptomatic cysts and surgical excision for large or symptomatic lesions. Less common alternatives include aspiration with or without sclerotherapy, dietary fat restriction, and cyst-venous anastomosis [5,7]. In Abelardo et al.’s review, surgery was performed in 60% of patients, while conservative management was used in 18% [5]. In Kakamad et al.’s systematic review of 16 patients, management included observation in six, surgical excision in five, low-fat diet in two, aspiration in one, combined surgery and diet in one, and unspecified treatment in one case [4].

During surgery, identification of the cyst is usually straightforward. However, meticulous dissection and secure ligation of the pedicle are essential to prevent chyle leak, which can be a serious complication. Other potential complications include injury to adjacent nerves (accessory, phrenic, or laryngeal), major vascular structures,or pneumothorax. Internal jugular vein anastomosis of the cyst has been described in rare cases [5,7].

Bilateral lymphatic cysts have been reported in three cases by Hamilton et al., all occurring as postoperative complications of neck dissection or mediastinal surgery and thus more accurately classified as lymphoceles [6]. To our knowledge, spontaneous bilateral cervical thoracic duct cysts, as in our patient, have not been previously reported.

Early cadaveric studies have shown that thoracic duct drainage most commonly occurs into the left internal jugular vein, the left subclavian vein, or their junction (left venous angle), as well as the innominate vein. Right-sided drainage has been reported in approximately 2-3% of cases, while bilateral drainage occurs in about 1-1.5% of cases [5].

Regarding right lymphatic duct cysts, only eight cases have been described in the literature. Five were reported by different authors [8-12], and three solitary spontaneous cysts were reported by Hamilton et al. [6]. Among the first five patients, three were treated with aspiration, one surgically, and one declined treatment. The latter three cases were imaging reports without specified management.

Long-term follow up was not mentioned in most patients of Kakamad et al.’s and Abelardo et al.’s reviews. The patient treated by cyst-venous anastomosis, reported by Dorch et al., was in good conditions after a two-year follow up [7]. Outcomes in surgically treated thoracic duct cyst patients seems, however, to be excellent [4,5].

To our knowledge, spontaneous double cervical thoracic duct cysts, probably of bilateral origin, as in our patient, have not been previously reported. Complete surgical excision with careful ligation of the pedicle represents an effective and definitive treatment, as demonstrated in our patient.

Conclusions

Cervical thoracic duct cysts are exceedingly rare, and the presence of a double cyst, as in our case, has not been previously reported. A left-sided supraclavicular location and characteristic high-resolution MRI findings should raise suspicion for this diagnosis. Complete surgical excision, identying the pedicle is very important. This excision, with careful ligation of the pedicle represents an effective and definitive treatment, was demonstrated in our patient.
