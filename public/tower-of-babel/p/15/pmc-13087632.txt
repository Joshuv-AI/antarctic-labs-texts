Pediatric Diagnostic Reference Levels for Chest X‐Rays in the Northern Cape Province of South Africa: A Descriptive Study
Authors: O L Lackay, J Horn‐Lodewyk, H Muller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe International Commission on Radiological Protection emphasizes dose optimization for pediatric patients due to their higher radiosensitivity. Establishing diagnostic reference levels (DRLs) supports this goal during radiological examinations. This study aimed to establish pediatric diagnostic reference levels (PiDRLs) for anteroposterior (AP) chest X‐rays across four hospitals in the Northern Cape Province, South Africa.MethodsPiDRLs were calculated for 375 pediatric patients (birth to 12 years) categorized by weight bands and age groups. Image quality was assessed using an image quality assessment checklist. DRLs were determined as the 75th percentile of the dose distribution for fixed X‐ray units. An image quality assessment was performed to establish if the dose levels consistently produced images of acceptable diagnostic quality.ResultsMean PiDRLs for weight groups < 5 kg, 5– < 15 kg, 15– < 30 kg, 30– < 50 kg were 0.2 mGy, 0.2 mGy, 0.2 mGy, and 0.3 mGy, respectively. Corresponding values for age groups < 1 year, 1– < 5 years, 5– < 10 years, and 10– < 12 years were 0.2 mGy, 0.2 mGy, 0.3 mGy, and 0.3 mGy, respectively. Image quality was consistently high, with the 75th percentile slightly exceeding international benchmarks. The image quality assessment confirmed that the dose levels produced images of acceptable image quality.ConclusionPiDRLs match select international benchmarks, offering a baseline for South African optimization. This study contributes valuable regional data to support radiation safety and dose optimization in pediatric medical imaging in South Africa.Keywords: age groups, chest X‐rays, dose optimization, image quality, pediatric; diagnostic reference levels, radiation protection, weight bands

Introduction

Pediatric radiological imaging is an indispensable diagnostic tool; however, it presents unique challenges compared to adult imaging, particularly in achieving a diagnostic‐quality image on the first attempt to avoid repeat exposures [1]. Adherence to the fundamental principle of keeping radiation doses “as low as reasonably achievable” (ALARA) is especially critical for children, as pediatric patients are significantly more radiosensitive than adults due to their rapidly proliferating tissues [2]. This heightened sensitivity, coupled with a longer post‐exposure life expectancy, which increases the opportunity for stochastic effects such as cancer to manifest, underscores the vital need for robust radiation protection frameworks. Therefore, Diagnostic Reference Levels (DRLs) for pediatric chest X‐rays provide a crucial benchmark for optimizing radiation dose and safeguarding this vulnerable patient population.

DRL is an essential quality assurance tool designed to optimize radiation safety and protection strategies in medical imaging [3, 4]. First endorsed as a formal concept by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) in 1991, the framework for DRLs was subsequently incorporated into the European Commission's Directorate‐General for Energy Basic Safety Standards Directive [5]. This international guidance is reflected locally, as the South African Department of Health's Directorate: Radiation Control mandates the implementation of DRLs in all radiology facilities [6].

The establishment of separate DRLs for adults and children is a key recommendation of the EuroSafe Imaging Call for Action 2018, with Action 4 specifically promoting dose management systems to establish DRLs on local, national, and European levels [7]. This aligns with the principle that DRLs can and should be established at various tiers, including the facility, regional, or national level [3]. The process of establishing local DRLs involves identifying X‐ray equipment that requires optimization within a specific area's healthcare facilities. As defined by the ICRP, a local DRL is determined for a defined clinical imaging task “based on the 75th percentile value of the distribution of the appropriate DRL quantity” from a sample of X‐ray rooms (typically 10–20) [8]. Consequently, a facility's calculated dose for a specific imaging task must be compared against the corresponding DRL for similar equipment to guide optimization. This practice is gaining traction in South Africa, as evidenced by work to establish local DRLs for fluoroscopy nationally by Malan et al. 2023 [9] and for interventional radiography in Gauteng province by Slave and Mahomed 2023 [10].

International Guidance

International guidance, notably from the ICRP (2017) [4], recommends that DRLs be modality‐specific and based on appropriate dosimetric quantities. Notably, for radiography, these are commonly the entrance surface dose (ESD) and dose‐area product (DAP), which characterize equipment output for standard‐sized patients rather than individual doses. The ICRP further advises using the median value of collected data to mitigate the effect of outliers. Although DRLs are a crucial tool for auditing and optimizing radiation protection, DRLs are not sufficient on their own to ensure dose optimization [8], especially as inadequate image quality often correlates with unnecessary exposure [11]. Furthermore, this is particularly critical for pediatric patients (PiDRLs), whose increased radiosensitivity and greater long‐term risks from ionizing radiation make such benchmarks essential [12, 13].

Local Implementation

Establishing PiDRLs necessitates adapting international guidance to local settings and clinical practice, where they serve as essential benchmarks for dose evaluation and quality assurance. These local PiDRLs must be regularly compared against national or regional levels to identify optimization opportunities. Pediatric body composition lacks a standard; therefore, patients must be grouped by weight or age for valid dose comparisons (ICRP, 2017) [4]. Furthermore, pediatric DRLs differ from adult DRLs, which often use a broad weight range (e.g., 50–90 kg) to estimate a standard size. Given the significant variation in pediatric size and development, local PiDRLs must be meticulously stratified to reflect these differences to provide accurate and actionable dose monitoring.

Technical Considerations

Several established dosimetric quantities can be used for radiation dose measurements in medical X‐ray imaging. The chosen metric must be both modality‐appropriate and readily accessible across all equipment. General radiography, ESD, and DAP are widely accepted and practical for establishing DRLs due to their standardization [14]. Implementation requires collecting a representative patient sample per procedure and equipment type, with the median dose value providing a robust basis for the reference level. Primarily, DRLs must be applied in conjunction with assessments of image quality to simultaneously ensure diagnostic adequacy and effective radiation protection.

Introduction to the Study

Chest imaging is a vital diagnostic tool for the rapid management of pediatric pulmonary disease [15], with the anteroposterior (AP) supine projection serving as the recommended technique. This approach minimizes patient stress, improves inspiration, facilitates respiratory monitoring, and reduces staff radiation exposure by requiring fewer personnel for immobilization [16]. Pediatric patients are significantly more radiosensitive than adults, and improving radiation safety in medical imaging has become a paramount global public health concern [17, 18]. The development of robust radiation protection policies that maximize the diagnostic benefits of imaging while rigorously minimizing associated health risks is crucial. A fundamental component of such optimization is the establishment of DRLs. Therefore, this study aims to establish local DRLs for pediatric chest X‐rays (CXR) within the Northern Cape Province of South Africa, thereby providing a crucial benchmark for dose optimization and enhanced protection for this vulnerable population.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the University of the Free State, Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (HSREC) (Ref: UFS‐HSD2020/0456/2909). Subsequently, permission was obtained from the management of all participating radiology practices. The study was also registered with the National Health Research Database, and approval was granted from the relevant provincial Department of Health (DoH) for data collection within public hospitals. Following ethics clearance, a pilot study was undertaken. However, the implementation of COVID‐19 restrictions (specifically, South Africa's Lockdown Level 2) presented a significant challenge, as it prevented parents/guardians from accompanying pediatric patients to the X‐ray department as originally planned. A formal amendment to the study protocol was submitted, which was reviewed and approved by the HSREC (Ref: UFS‐HSD2020/0456/290901). The key amendments included:

Study Design

In this study, a descriptive, cross‐sectional design was employed, which was conducted in two distinct phases to establish PiDRLs for CXR examinations within the Northern Cape Province, South Africa. The first phase involved the establishment and validation of a PiDRL calculation checklist. This checklist was designed to capture a comprehensive set of variables suitable for use in the calculation of PiDRLs. In the second phase, the checklist was used to collect data from a sample of pediatric patients (aged 0 to 15 years) undergoing routine chest X‐ray examinations. These data were collected from the patients visiting the radiology departments of four hospitals within the Northern Cape Province and used to calculate PiDRLs.

Participants

For non‐complex examinations, such as CXR imaging, the European Commission recommends performing specific testing on radiographic equipment for a minimum of 10–20 adults per patient group [3]. This study adapted this guidance to a pediatric population, which was stratified into five distinct weight‐based groups and accompanied age intervals (Table 1). These weight categories corresponded approximately to age groups from newborn to under 12 years. Weight was chosen as the primary factor over age since it more accurately relates to radiation dose reference levels [19]. The study population was stratified according to the radiology departments (five X‐ray rooms) where pediatric CXR examinations were most frequently performed [20, 21]. Consequently, a sample size of 30 pediatric patients was proposed for each weight‐based group per X‐ray room, resulting in a total sample size of 750 patients.

PiDRL Calculation Checklist Design

The PiDRL calculation checklist was developed in alignment with the fundamental principles for establishing diagnostic reference levels, as outlined in the recommendations of the International Commission on Radiological Protection, publication 103 (ICRP, 2017) [4]. The checklist was structured to capture all exposure parameters essential to the accurate calculation of Entrance Skin Kerma (ESK) or Entrance Skin Air Kerma (ESAK) with backscatter, which are the primary dosimetric quantities used to establish PiDRLs. As these calculations are specific to the imaging context, the checklist was designed to ensure that data could be linked to a specific radiographic examination, department, and X‐ray machine. The checklist was organized into three distinct sections (Table 3).

Section A: Equipment Information

This section captured metadata about the imaging equipment, which included the X‐ray machine manufacturer and model, the inherent filtration of the X‐ray tube, and the type of digital imaging system. Technical parameters, such as whether the cassette or image receptor was used with or without a grid, were also recorded. The checklist recorded the dates of the last annual and monthly quality control (QC) tests to verify equipment performance and calibration status.

Section B: Patient Information and Measurements

Section B focused on the pediatric patient‐specific variables essential for accurate dose assessment and age‐group categorization. In this section, the patient's weight and age were captured to assign the patient to a standard pediatric sub‐group (recorded in both months and years for precision). The patient's thickness was also measured at the seventh thoracic vertebra (T7), the standard centering point for AP chest imaging.

Section C: Technical Exposure Parameters

In the final section of the checklist, the technical factors used for each individual's exposure were recorded. This included the tube potential (kVp), the tube current‐exposure time product (mAs), and the source‐to‐image distance (SID) recorded in centimetres.

Checklist Pilot

This checklist was piloted for clarity and usability before being deployed for data collection. It was implemented at one of the hospitals, with a sample of seven pediatric participants, covering the five weight groups. A medical physicist then performed a validation exercise, using the pilot data to determine if the PiDRL calculation was feasible. The PiDRL calculation formulas were embedded in an Excel Spreadsheet containing the PIDRL calculation checklist. The checklist was deemed comprehensive, as it included all relevant parameters required for the calculations.

Data Collection

A pilot study was first conducted using prospective data to validate the PiDRLs calculation checklist. Following this, the main study commenced with prospective data collection. However, the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic drastically reduced patient numbers, making it challenging to achieve the target sample size of 30 participants per group. To mitigate this, the methodology was adapted to include retrospective data collection. Parental consent for these cases was obtained telephonically. Consequently, while radiographers at the participating hospitals were initially trained to collect prospective data, recording patient measurements such as weight, height, and chest thickness with callipers on the checklist, the researcher subsequently gathered all retrospective data and obtained the specific measurements from parents during the telephonic consent process.

Statistical Analysis

Data were recorded in Microsoft Excel (version 3.2.7; Microsoft Corporation) and analysed using SAS (v. 9.4; SAS Institute Inc.). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were calculated for the technical parameters and patient thickness. The comparability of the retrospective and prospective datasets was assessed using the Wilcoxon two‐sample test on weight groups. The lack of a statistically significant difference (p > 0.05) indicated that the weight distributions were similar, validating the combination of both datasets into a single cohort for analysis (Table 4).

Image Quality Assessment

The implementation of an image quality assessment is a necessary component of the methodology following the calculation of DRLs. This requirement stems from the ALARA principle, which must be balanced with the image quality needed for an accurate diagnosis. Therefore, dose reduction efforts must always include a check to ensure the image is still adequate for diagnosis. An image quality investigation was performed using an assessment scorecard based on the European Commission guidelines (1996) [25]. These image criteria for newborn babies provided a valid guideline for an erect AP CXR projection that is free of pathology and contributed to dose optimization in the radiographic imaging of newborn babies.

The assessment was only performed on the neonate category using the PBU‐80 Newborn Whole‐Body Phantom (weighing 3.5 kg) because other representative weight equivalent phantoms were not available. The image quality assessment was used to determine if the 75th percentile mean exposure settings for this weight group produced optimal image quality when using the same exposure factors employed in data collection. Results

Sample Population Frequencies Per Weight Group

Data were collected from pediatric patients across the research sites during the study period. A total of 375 patients who met the inclusion criteria and required a CXR were included. The final sample fell short of the proposed target, a limitation largely attributable to data collection occurring during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Figure 1 shows that the distribution of patients across the pre‐defined weight groups was uneven, reflecting the higher clinical frequency of CXR requests for certain pediatric cohorts. The 5 kg to < 15 kg group was the most represented. A relatively high number of patients were also observed in the < 5 kg, 15 kg to < 30 kg, and 30 kg to < 50 kg groups. In contrast, the 50 kg to < 80 kg group contained only two patients.

Parameters Per Age Group

Considerable variation in kVp, mAs, and patient thickness, per age group, was observed between and within the research sites. In Hospital 4, the lowest kVp values (55–65 kVp) were applied, while Hospitals 1 and 2 applied a wider, higher range (Table 6). The mAs values generally increased with age but varied substantially by site; for example, Hospital 2, Room 1, consistently applied the highest mAs values. Patient thickness and weight, as expected, increased with age. The notable size range in the oldest cohort (10 to < 12 years; thickness: 14.0–22.9 cm, weight: 24.9–45.0 kg) highlights the technical challenge of imaging this group.

The 75th percentile PiDRL values for the different age groups showed a consistent pattern. The reported values were low across all cohorts, with the youngest patients, under 1 year and from 1 to 5 years, exhibiting the lowest 75th percentile PiDRL (Table 8). A slight increase was observed for older children. This indicates that while radiation exposure was low, it is moderately higher for the older pediatric groups compared to the youngest.

Discussion

At the time of this study, no published data on the 75th percentile for conventional AP/PA pediatric chest examinations in South Africa were available. Trauernicht and Pitcher (2021) confirmed that limited research had been conducted in South Africa on DRLs across different imaging modalities. Furthermore, most existing South African DRL studies have focused on fluoroscopy and interventional radiographic procedures for both adult and pediatric patients [26].

The ESD value generally increases with patient weight or age; however, the mean ESD in this study remained consistent across groups. This consistency, particularly among adjacent weight groups, warrants further investigation to determine whether the exposure is optimized for the lower‐weight group. While most international studies calculate ESD by age group, both the ICRP [4] and the European Commission [5] recommend weight‐based grouping. This study adopted that approach, calculating PiDRL by weight group with corresponding age intervals, thereby enhancing the reliability of the results [19]. Although weight was used for grouping in this research, the 75th percentile PiDRL remained consistent for higher weight groups and their corresponding age categories. This consistency may be attributed to variations in exposure techniques across radiology departments and differences in X‐ray equipment [24].

The DRL values from this study differed from those reported in previous research. A study in Japan reported an ESD of 0.14 mGy for the 0–3 years age group, which is notably lower than the value of 0.2 mGy identified in this study [27]. Research from Austria documented ESD values for pediatric patients younger than 1 year 0.055 mGy, 1 to < 5 years 0.069 mGy, 5 to < 10 years 0.082 mGy, and 10 to < 15 years 0.108 mGy, all substantially lower than the results found in this study [28]. In certain age groups, the DRL values from this study aligned with European benchmarks. European DRLs for the groups 0 to < 1 year, 1 to < 2 years, 2 to < 3 years, 3 to < 8 years, 8 to < 12 years, and ≥ 12 years were 0.131 mGy, 0.240 mGy, 0.146 mGy, 0.228 mGy, 0.434 mGy, and 0.455 mGy, respectively [5]. Specifically, while the European value for the 0 to < 1 year group (0.131 mGy) is lower than that found in this study, the result for the 1 to < 5 years group (0.240 mGy) shows close agreement. Conversely, a study from Ethiopia reported significantly higher ESD values across all weight groups compared to this research [29], and while the findings from this study demonstrate lower ESD values for all weight and age groups than a study conducted in Asaba, Nigeria [30].

Limitations

This study had several limitations. Data collection was significantly hindered by the COVID‐19 pandemic, which impacted sample size attainment, particularly for the 50–80 kg weight group and its corresponding age cohort. Radiographer shortages reduced available trained staff, increasing workloads and further constraining sampling, especially after restrictions eased and patient influx surged. Image quality assessment was limited to the 3.5 kg newborn phantom.

Conclusion

In this study, local PiDRLs for conventional AP CXR examinations across four hospitals in the NCP of South Africa were successfully established. Critically, an accompanying image quality assessment confirmed that these newly established dose levels consistently produced images of acceptable diagnostic quality, adhering to relevant guidelines. These PiDRLs mark a crucial first step toward standardizing radiation protection practices for the pediatric population in this region. Looking forward, these findings provide a foundational benchmark for future dose optimization initiatives. A key recommendation for further research would be to investigate the potential for lowering these PiDRL values through the refinement of exposure parameters and imaging protocols. Ultimately, the continuous pursuit of dose optimization, guided by local DRLs, is essential for upholding the fundamental principle of ALARA and ensuring the highest standard of care for pediatric patients.
