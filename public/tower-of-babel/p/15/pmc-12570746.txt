ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) has greatly enhanced the diagnosis of aortic dissection (AD). However, there is currently no comprehensive reporting requirement for the diagnosis of AD. Structured reporting templates are becoming increasingly common in radiology and other medical fields. Drawing on the standardized writing methods of structured reports for different diseases, the authors propose a structured reporting template for the CTA diagnosis of AD and suggest details for the template while also exploring several hot topics related to AD.Keywords: Aortic dissection, AD, Computed tomography angiography, CTA, Structured reporting template

Overview of aortic dissection

Acute Aortic Dissection (AAD) is a rare but highly fatal condition, defined as a separation of the layers of the aortic wall due to an intimomedial tear [1]. It is estimated that the incidence rate of aortic dissection ranges from 2.9 to 3.5 per 100,000 person-years [2, 3].

In classic AAD, a disruption in the vessel wall’s intimal layer accompanies blood inflow into a new channel in the media layer, creating a new channel called the false lumen (FL). The new intermedial channel may propagate antegrade (most commonly) or retrograde at variable distances longitudinally within the aorta wall [4]. AAD is typically characterized by the presence of a dissection flap, two aortic channels, and a primary entry tear [5]. The treatment of AAD largely depends on the extent of the dissection, the location of the primary entry tear, and any evidence of organ malperfusion [6].

CT Angiography (CTA) is a crucial imaging modality for diagnosing AAD [7]. Once CTA confirms the presence of AAD, the key imaging objectives are to identify the primary entry tear and its proximity to the vital vascular branches, classify the origin and extent of the dissection, distinguish between true and false lumens, and assess for branch vessel involvement and complications [8, 9]. It is necessary to emphasize the importance of delayed phases scanning in the diagnosis of aortic dissection. Only an arterial scan may not observe the ischemic perfusion of organs, and whether there is thrombosis in the false lumens.

Aortic morphology

Aortic valve

Bicuspid Aortic Valve (BAV) is characterized by the presence of a two-leaflet aortic valve instead of a three-leaflet valve in Fig. 1. BAV is the most common congenital heart malformation, with an estimated prevalence of 0.77–1.4% [10, 11]. It frequently leads to severe aortic complications, such as aortic aneurysm and aortic dissection [12]. BAV patients have a notably higher incidence of AAD, with a relative risk of 8.4 compared to the general population [13]. According to IRAD data, 3.3% of aortic dissection patients have BAV, and they exhibit a unique dissection pattern, with the entry tear frequently located in the aortic root [14].

Zones of the aorta

The “Reporting standards for thoracic endovascular aortic repair” provides a more technical classification of aortic anatomy, recommended by the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS), the European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS), the Society for Vascular Surgery/Society of Thoracic Surgeons (SVS/STS), the American College of Cardiology (ACC), and the American Heart Association (AHA). This classification is used to plan, guide, and report aortic interventions, especially for endovascular stent-grafting [18–22]. In this system, the thoracic and abdominal aorta are divided into 11 zones, facilitating the description of vascular segments and potential branches that may be covered or replaced during repair in Fig. 3.

Disease staging

Traditionally, Aortic Dissection (AD) has been classified as “acute” within the first two weeks of symptom onset and “chronic” beyond the second week. This method of classification has been used for decades [23].

However, the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection (IRAD) proposed dividing AD into four temporal phases based on survival rates: hyperacute (< 24 h), acute (2–7 days), subacute (8–30 days), and chronic (>30 days). This approach considers different treatment methods and prognostic recommendations, offering a more comprehensive framework for characterizing patients with AAD and planning appropriate interventions [24]. Despite its merits, this classification has not gained widespread acceptance.

Currently, what is more, recognized is the most contemporary temporal classification system, proposed by the SVS and STS, which similarly divides aortic dissection into 4 temporal types: hyperacute, < 24 h; acute, 1 to 14 days; subacute, 15 to 90 days; and chronic, >90 days to improve prognostication and guide decision making about the timing and types of potential intervention [21, 22, 25].

Chronic AD should include the following people: (1) After surgery or intravascular interventional therapy for acute AD, patients have persistent AD. (2) Only drug treatment can stabilize the disease and transform it into chronic aortic dissection, mainly stable acute type B aortic dissection. (3) This is a small number of patients who have not been diagnosed and treated in the acute stage, and had never received medical intervention, but are lucky to survive. For these chronic aortic dissections, this structured report is only applicable to the second and third cases, not the first case, because the focus of postoperative attention is not included [26].

For patients with chronic aortic dissection, the functional morphology of the aortic valve requires additional evaluation, as long-term aortic valve insufficiency predisposes them to asymmetric stretching of the valve leaflets, annular dilation, and deterioration of valve geometry [27].​.

Stanford classification, TEM classification, and SVS/STS classification

Stanford classification

The Stanford classification is commonly used for AD and is based on the extent of the dissection, regardless of the location of the primary tear. It classifies dissections into two types, type A and type B, based on whether the ascending or descending aorta is involved [28]. The advantage of the Stanford system is its ability to guide treatment by categorizing dissections that require surgical intervention (Type A) and those that can be managed conservatively (Type B, except for complex type B) [29]. However, its limitation lies in its lack of information about the distal extent of the dissection and the omission of primary tear location [28].

Type A, dissection involving the ascending aorta, defined as a dissection proximal to the brachiocephalic artery, with or without extension into the aortic arch and descending aorta (i.e. DeBakey Types I and II), regardless of the site of the primary intimal tear.

Type A, dissection involving the ascending aorta, defined as a dissection proximal to the brachiocephalic artery, with or without extension into the aortic arch and descending aorta (i.e. DeBakey Types I and II), regardless of the site of the primary intimal tear.

Type B, originating distal to the left subclavian artery and involving the descending aorta but not the aortic arch or ascending aorta (i.e. DeBakey Type III).

Type B, originating distal to the left subclavian artery and involving the descending aorta but not the aortic arch or ascending aorta (i.e. DeBakey Type III).

TEM classification

The Type-Entry-Malperfusion (TEM) classification system is recently proposed framwork for AD [6]. It integrates the anatomical characteristics of the dissection and key prognostic features, using the dissection type (based on the Stanford classification), entry tear location, and malperfusion status as its core parameters in Fig. 4.

Schematic diagram of TEM

The advantage of TEM is that it improves the Stanford classification. In the TEM classification dissection, type (T) is defined by dissection extension as proposed by the Stanford classification and not by entry location. The TEM classification system extends the Stanford classification, which identifies Type non-A non-B aortic dissection, referring to those dissections that arise in the aortic arch and the descending aorta but do not involve the ascending aorta, accounting for approximately 10%−16.5% of all aortic dissections [30, 31].

The key advantage of the TEM classification is that it extends the Stanford classification by providing information about the location (E) of the primary entry tear and malperfusion status (M). If the primary entry tear is not visible, the entry position is ‘0’; ‘1’if it is located in the ascending aorta; ‘2’ if in the arch; and ‘3’ if in the descending aorta (E0, E1, E2 and E3). If there is no malperfusion, malperfusion status is noted by a ‘0’; ‘1’ for coronary arteries are affected; ‘2’ for supra-aortic vessels; ‘3’ for the visceral/renal and/or a lower extremity (M0, M1, M2, and M3). A plus (+) is added if malperfusion is clinically present and a minus (-) is added if it is a radiological finding [32].

SVS/STS classification

The Society of Vascular Surgery (SVS) and Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) guidelines proposed a revised system based on aortic pathology, location of primary intimal tear, and the proximal and distal extent of pathology. In this new SVS/STS classification, aortic dissection is not defined by dissection extension, but by the entry location. If a patient has an entry in the aortic arch or the descending aorta (in zone 1 or more distally) and the dissection extends down to the aortic valve, it is a type B aortic dissection. Type A aortic dissection are only those dissections with entry in the ascending aorta and proximal arch [21].

The SVS/STS classification highlights the position of the primary intimal tear and the range of the false lumen involved, which is relatively easy to be popularized in clinical practice. Within the SVS/STS classification for aortic dissection, the distinction between type A and type B is predicated on entry tear location alone in Fig. 5. In a type A dissection, the entry tear originates only in zone 0. The distal extent of a type A aortic dissection is then simply designated by zone. Type B dissections include any aortic dissection with an entry tear originating in zone 1 or beyond and are further characterized by two subscripts (BP, D), “P” (proximal extent) and “D” (distal extent) [21].

The SVS/STS classification definition type B AAS as any aortic dissection with an entry tear originating distal to the ostium of the innominate artery. In this way, non-A and non-B dissections are classified as type B dissection, which is not classified according to the particularity of aortic arch dissection, which is not widely recognized [33].

The location and measurement of primary entry tear and Re-entry tear

The key to diagnosing aortic dissection is to identify of the false and true lumens in the aorta (if present) and identify antegrade and/or retrograde aortic dissection [34]. The presence of an intimomedial flap, which separates the true and false lumens, is a major diagnostic feature [35]. Discovering the primary entry tear not only helps to understand the mechanism of aortic dissection but also facilitates the adoption of different treatment methods. High-risk imaging findings in uncomplicated acute type B aortic dissection include entry tears larger than 10 mm and tears located on the lesser curvature of the aorta [36, 37].

The location of the primary entry tear

The risk of dissection is highest in the proximal aorta because it is subject to the largest fluctuations in pulse pressure. The proximal entry tear may occur at any point of the aorta, but the tear most often occurs in the right lateral wall of the ascending aorta (an area under great hydraulic stress) or in the proximal segment of the descending thoracic aorta, close to the origin of the left subclavian artery [38]. The intimal tear is the point at which the innermost layer of the aorta, the intima, has been disrupted, allowing blood to enter the media layer and creating a false lumen.

The location of the main entrance tear is marked according to the 12 zones of the aorta, which is beneficial for the SVS/STS classification and also relatively easy to transform into the staging requirements of the TEM classification (E1 entry tear means in Zone 0; E2 entry tear means in Zone 1 or 2; E3 entry tear means in Zone 3 to Zone 11) [21, 32].

In cases of type B AD with the main entrance tear located in zone 3 or zone 4, measuring the distance from the main entrance tear to the distal edge of the left subclavian artery is critical during the planning of endovascular intervention. This measurement ensures an adequate proximal landing zone. Generally, a 20 mm segment of disease-free aorta is sufficient to achieve stable fixation and effective sealing [39].

Primary entry tear measurement

The intimal tear is the point at which the innermost layer of the aorta, the intima, has been disrupted, allowing blood to enter the media layer and creating a false lumen. Measuring the entrance or intimal tear in an aortic dissection is crucial for understanding the origin and extent of the dissection. This measurement provides information about the size and location of the tear, which is essential for determining the severity of the dissection and planning treatment strategies. The dimensions of the tear can influence the progression of the dissection and the potential for complications such as rupture. Therefore, accurate measurement and description of the intimal tear are important components of the diagnostic process (Fig. 6).

The location and measurement of Re-entry tear (Communications between lumina)

In AD, the occurrence of one or more distal (re-entry) tears is common. These tears allow communication between the true and false lumens, extending along the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Re-entry tears are often found in the descending thoracic aorta, abdominal aorta, or iliac arteries, though they can be challenging to detect due to their small size. These distal tears, also known as fenestrations within the intimomedial flap, often signal the cessation of distal intimomedial tearing. The presence of a re-entry tear at the level of branch vessels can be advantageous, as it allows for the maintenance of organ perfusion by enabling blood flow to re-enter the true lumen while preserving the patency of the false lumen [40–42]. The location of these tears should be carefully documented (see Fig. 7).

Aortic measurements

Aortic diameters should be measured from from the outer edge to the outer edge, if the vessel wall is visible. Aortic diameters should be measured perpendicular to the angle of blood flow using the centerline technique. Whenever possible, orthogonal measurements are used as the preferred method for accurate evaluation and follow-up comparisons. Systemic assessment of aortic dimensions included: (a) maximum total aortic diameter, (b) maximum true lumen diameter, (c) minimum true lumen diameter, (d) minimum total diameter of aorta, and (e) the aortic true lumen compression ratio.

Aortic diameters should be recorded at levels of interest, particularly in the zones adjacent to the primary tear and those containing the largest aortic diameters in both the thoracic and abdominal aorta.

To study complete aortic morphology, a more comprehensive approach that measures the maximal diameter within each individual zone may be necessary. A straight line can be used to measure the total aortic diameter, including both the true lumen and false lumen. This line bisects the center of the intimal flap and is perpendicular to the blood flow plane. The sum of the true lumen and false lumen diameters equals the total aortic diameter. When reporting the diameter of aortic dissection, the maximum and minimum diameters of the true lumen and the maximum total diameter of the aorta should be included (Fig. 8). High-risk imaging findings in uncomplicated acute Type B aortic dissection include: maximal aortic diameter >40 mm; false-lumen diameter >20–22 mm; and an increase in total aortic diameter of >5 mm between serial imaging studies [36, 37, 43].

Mechanism of malperfusion

Malperfusion refers to a decrease in blood flow in the arterial bed of important organs. Malperfusion syndrome occurs when this diminished flow results in ischemia and subsequent functional impairment. End-organ malperfusion is one of the most dreaded complications of AAD, caused by either static or dynamic obstruction of branch vessels. The mortality rate of patients with acute TAAD accompanied by malperfusion syndrome is increased by 43% [49].

The treatment strategies for patients with static or dynamic obstruction are different. The dynamic occlusion of branch arteries can often be resolved by repairing the aorta, thereby stabilizing the true lumen and addressing visceral reperfusion issues [50]. For patients experiencing malperfusion primarily due to static occlusion of branch vessels, endovascular fenestration or additional branch stenting is typically required [51, 52].

Complications and high-risk features

Several serious complications may arise from AD, leading to catastrophic consequences, such as acute aortic regurgitation (AR), myocardial ischemia, cardiac tamponade, acute stroke, aortic rupture, or malperfusion syndromes. Those with refractory pain or hypertension were considered high-risk patients with complicated dissection.

There are several known risk factors of the radiographic feature that may disrupt the stability of the course of dissection, leading to subacute and late complications, including: the anatomical location of the primary entry tear at the inner curvature or proximity to the LSA, arch entry non-A non-B aortic dissection, maximal thoracic aortic diameter >40 mm, ongoing aortic expansion of >1 mm/day, a proximal entry tear with a defect dimension of 10 or more mm, a false lumen diameter >22 mm, primary entry located < 20 mm to the left subclavian artery, and so on [56–58].

For chronic aortic dissection, besides delayed aortic rupture, the main serious complication is new important organ ischemia, such as new limb or mesenteric ischemia, and another important complication is the formation of aneurysm [26].

Outlook

In the past two decades, more comprehensive and accurate imaging identification of patients with aortic dissection has made significant progress in its treatment. Radiologists need to provide more comprehensive and developmental imaging features. This structured report aims to optimize the existing CTA diagnosis of acute aortic dissection. It is expected that while applying structured reports, the heterogeneity that may exist in the reports will continue to decrease, and more standardized professional terms will be used as much as possible to reflect the disease characteristics of acute aortic dissection comprehensively and whether there are high-risk factors that need urgent treatment.

However, we will certainly have some shortcomings. We look forward to more peer practice and provide the best modification suggestions to help us further improve the existing structured report.
