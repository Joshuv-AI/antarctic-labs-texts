Plant and soil transcriptomics reveal the consequences of bioaugmentation of co-contaminated soil with Pseudomonas qingdaonensis ZCR6 during bacteria-assisted phytoremediation
Authors: Magdalena Pacwa-Płociniczak, Agata Daszkowska-Golec, Sara Gobetti, Aki Sinkkonen, Tomasz Płociniczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur study evaluated the bioaugmentation potential of the Pseudomonas qingdaonensis ZCR6 strain to support the phytoremediation of multicontaminated soil. This metal-resistant strain expresses several plant growth–promoting features and petroleum hydrocarbon degradative potential in biochemical tests. A microcosm experiment with maize (Zea mays) treated with a suspension of live or thermally inactivated ZCR6 cells was performed (representing a scenario of unsuccessful bioaugmentation). Although the ZCR6 colonized the rhizosphere and plant tissues, its application did not impact the removal of hydrocarbons compared to the control (17.4% vs. 19.6%, respectively). In contrast, treatment of soil with thermally inactivated bacterial biomass significantly increased hydrocarbon removal efficiency by approximately 8%, compared to the nontreated control. The Zn accumulation in the roots of maize treated with either live or thermally inactivated cells of ZCR6 was about 30% higher compared to the control. The soil transcriptomic analysis revealed downregulation of bacterial genes involved in hydrocarbon degradation after soil inoculation with live ZCR6, whereas plants showed upregulation of stress-related genes. No increase in the expression of bacterial genes encoding plant growth–promoting mechanisms was observed in any of the treatments. Our research has shown that the utility of each strain considered for bioaugmentation must be properly verified prior to in situ application. This should also include testing scenarios assuming a lack of survival and/or activity of the introduced strain and assessing its actual impact on the metabolic activity of plants and soil microorganisms by applying advanced transcriptomic approaches.Key points• Bioaugmentation significantly downregulated bacterial genes of degrading pathways.• Soil inoculation increased transcription of genes involved in maize stress response.• Treatments didn’t increase the expression of genes crucial for phytoremediation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13763-8.

Introduction

Environmental multicontamination, typical of industrial areas, remains one of the most difficult ecological problems to solve. Co-contamination with petroleum hydrocarbons (PHs) and heavy metals (HMs) represents such a threat, as it poses a much greater risk to the environment than the risks posed by the presence of either alone. This is largely due to their different chemical properties, which make their simultaneous removal difficult (Dai et al. 2020). Moreover, pollutants from one group may affect the removal efficiency of the other group. For example, low concentrations of heavy metals in soil may initially reduce hydrocarbon degradation, as heavy metal cations bind to negatively charged sites on the cell surface of hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms, blocking potential adsorption sites. As metal cation concentrations increase, microbial surface charge becomes neutralized, reducing hydrophilicity and enhancing hydrocarbon adsorption (Liu et al. 2017). At the same time, high concentrations of heavy metals may compete with essential microelements (e.g., Mg2+, Ca2+) that serve as enzyme cofactors or bind to protein sulfhydryl groups, inhibiting enzymatic activity required for hydrocarbon degradation (Karaca et al. 2010; Guo et al. 2010). Awareness of the adverse and often unpredictable effects of these two contaminant groups has driven the development of new or modified remediation strategies (Zhang et al. 2022). However, due to the complexity of multicontamination, the effectiveness of biological removal approaches remains limited. One promising option is phytoremediation, which uses plants and their associated microorganisms to remove organic and inorganic compounds from soil. In this technique, plants accumulate heavy metals in their tissues (phytoextraction) and support the degradation of organic pollutants by microorganisms in the rhizosphere (rhizodegradation) and the endosphere (phytodegradation) (Bleicher 2016). Nevertheless, due to the limited efficiency of this technique under certain environmental conditions, phytoremediation can be supported by introducing beneficial bacteria (bioaugmentation) or nutrients (biostimulation). These treatments are intended to improve plant growth and remove pollutants (Cao et al. 2022).

Plants used in phytoremediation should be able to take up and accumulate metal pollutants and exhibit tolerance to high concentrations of HMs and PHs in soil. Nevertheless, plant exposure to these xenobiotics induces oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can inhibit plant growth or cause tissue necrosis when ROS production exceeds the plant’s antioxidant capacity (Molina and Segura 2021). To cope with oxidative stress, plants activate both enzymatic and nonenzymatic defense mechanisms, including the production of enzymes (such as catalase, peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione reductase) and phytohormones (e.g., ethylene, abscisic acid, salicylic acid, and auxins), respectively (Tognetti et al. 2017; Zandalinas et al. 2020; Nguyen et al. 2020; Inal et al. 2025). However, these well-characterized mechanisms represent only part of the complex metabolic response of plants to stress. Therefore, a thorough understanding of these responses requires advanced methods such as transcriptomics, which enables global profiling of gene expression in plants exposed to pollutants. This enables the identification of physiological and molecular pathways involved in tolerance or susceptibility to PHs and HMs (Singh et al. 2016). In this context, maize (Zea mays) represents a particularly suitable model species for phytoremediation studies of soils co-contaminated with hydrocarbons and heavy metals. Z. mays has served as a key model organism in basic research for nearly a century, and recent advances in maize genomics, including the complete genome assembly and annotation of nearly 40,000 genes, have enabled state-of-the-art transcriptomic analyses using RNA-seq. Moreover, maize is a fast-growing, high-biomass plant with an extensive, deeply penetrating root system, resulting in a large rhizosphere volume where degradation of organic contaminants occurs most intensively. The ease of harvesting both aboveground biomass and roots—tissues that may accumulate heavy metals—further supports its applicability in phytoremediation research.

Recent evidence indicates that plant growth benefits from interactions with microorganisms, especially under environmental stresses such as soil pollution, salinity, or drought. The positive impact of plant-associated microorganisms that inhabit the rhizosphere or endosphere is also crucial during phytoremediation, as they may support plants in removing pollutants from soil by degrading hydrocarbons, producing biosurfactants/bioemulsifiers, and promoting plant growth. These microorganisms also contribute to the phytoextraction of metals, facilitating their mobilization and uptake by plants. Many of these functions are attributed to plant growth–promoting bacteria (PGPB), which play a key role in improving phytoremediation efficiency (Chojnacka et al. 2023; Montreemuk et al. 2024). In the present study, the bacterial strain Pseudomonas qingdaonensis ZCR6 was selected as a bioaugmentation agent due to its origin from the rhizosphere of maize cultivated in soil co-contaminated with hydrocarbons and heavy metals. As many plant-associated bacteria exhibit host specificity, using a strain naturally associated with maize was intended to minimize the risk of incompatibility between the microbial inoculant and the plant host.

Although many specific mechanisms may be involved in bacterial-assisted phytoremediation of soils co-contaminated with hydrocarbons and heavy metals, the exact nature of plant–microbe interactions is still unknown. This is because the biochemical methods used so far, which focus on a few selected traits, have not addressed the fundamental role of bacteria in supporting these processes. Therefore, the latest recommendations on phytoremediation emphasize the use of advanced omics technologies, such as metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, and metaproteomics, as powerful tools for studying the structure, function, and activity of microbial communities (Kuppan et al. 2024; Gupta et al. 2024; Bora et al. 2025). In line with these recommendations and to address existing knowledge gaps, we applied these approaches in the present study.

Our study aimed to evaluate the bioaugmentation potential of P. qingdaonensis ZCR6 in supporting the phytoremediation of soil co-contaminated with PHs and HMs, using an integrative approach that included the analysis of the global gene expression of bacteria and plants during the remediation process. In the experiment, Z. mays plants were supported either by ZCR6 strain (bioaugmentation), by nutrients derived from its thermally inactivated biomass (representing a scenario of unsuccessful bioaugmentation) or by water (control). Based on these treatments, we tested the following hypotheses: (1) bioaugmentation enhances the removal of PHs and HMs compared to the other treatments, (2) the introduction of the live ZCR6 strain results in greater improvements in maize growth and fitness compared to its thermally inactivated biomass, (3) live bacteria introduced into soil express genes essential for phytoremediation and significantly influence global gene expression in the soil, and (4) inoculation of soil with live cells of the ZCR6 mitigates the plant stress response by downregulating the expression of genes associated with stress defense.

Materials and methods

Soil

Loamy sand soil co-contaminated with petroleum hydrocarbons and heavy metals, used in the phytoremediation experiment, was collected on 26 October 2020 from a depth of 0.2 m in an industrial area located in the vicinity of the “Jadwiga” Coke Plant in Zabrze (Upper Silesia, southern Poland, 50.336010, 18.826797). The soil was stored at 4 °C until the start of the experiment. According to the FAO system, the soil was classified as an Albic Luvisol (FAO 2015) and had a pH of 6.97 ± 0.15, an organic matter content of 4.03 ± 0.15%, an organic carbon content of 2.62 ± 0.19%, and a total nitrogen content of 0.63 ± 0.10%. The initial concentration of TPH in the soil was 2330 ± 331 mg kg−1 DW, while the initial concentrations of Zn, Cd, and Cu were 2360 ± 118, 11.70 ± 1.04, and 41.5 ± 1.2 mg kg−1, respectively. A more detailed list of the soil’s chemical and physical parameters was provided in Pacwa-Płociniczak et al. (2024).

Bacterial strain

The P. qingdaonensis ZCR6 strain (genome sequence deposited in GenBank under accession JADWSX000000000) was previously isolated from the rhizosphere of Z. mays grown in co-contaminated soil, which was identical to that used in the present experiment. Detailed biochemical and genetic characterization of this strain has been reported by Chlebek et al. (2022). The strain exhibited several traits recognized as important for plant growth promotion. Under laboratory conditions, it showed the ability to produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) (3.59 ± 0.11 mg mL⁻1), solubilize Ca₃(PO₄)₂ (6.5 ± 0.53 mm clear halo), and synthesize siderophores and ammonia. It also exhibited cellulase activity and ACC deaminase (ACCD) activity (296.73 ± 1.27 µmol α-ketobutyrate mg⁻1 h⁻1). Based on comparisons with values reported in the literature, strains with both higher and lower activities have been described; therefore, the ZCR6 strain was classified as exhibiting moderate activity of the tested mechanisms. However, these results indicate only the potential of the ZCR6 strain to promote plant growth under laboratory conditions, since its real activity depends on environmental factors such as pH, nutrient availability, and pollutant presence and concentration. Moreover, ZCR6 demonstrated the ability to degrade petroleum hydrocarbons (PHs) under laboratory conditions in M9 medium supplemented with crude oil as the sole carbon source. It also produced surface-active compounds and showed resistance to heavy metals, including Cd, Zn, and Cu (Chlebek et al. 2022). The rifampicin-resistant spontaneous mutant was selected according to the method of Płociniczak et al. (2013) and exhibited the same characteristics as the parental strain. Next, the ZCR6rf strain was labeled with enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) as described by Chlebek et al. (2020). This double labeling was used to track the fate of the inoculant during the bioaugmentation experiment.

Inoculum preparation

For soil inoculation, the ZCR6rf+egfp strain was cultured in 10% Luria–Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with rifampicin (150 µg mL−1), tetracycline (50 µg mL−1, added to maintain expression of the GFP-encoding pMP4655 plasmid), crude oil (1% v/v), and ZnSO4 (3 mM) (pH = 7) on an orbital shaker at 120 rpm and 28 °C for 48 h. The bacterial count in the inoculum was estimated from optical density measurements and subsequently validated by the plate count method. An appropriate volume of the bacterial suspension was centrifuged (4,650 RCF, 4 °C, 20 min, Sigma 4–16 K), and the pellet was washed twice with sterile 0.9% NaCl and resuspended in 50 mL sterile water. An equal number of thermally inactivated bacterial cells was prepared by autoclaving (121 °C, 1 atm, 30 min). Prior to thermal inactivation, the bacterial suspension was centrifuged, washed, and resuspended following the same procedure described above. The efficiency of the sterilization process was determined using the plate count method. No bacterial growth was observed for this suspension.

Experimental design

The phytoremediation experiment was performed under laboratory conditions using the soil described in Sect. "Soil". Two-week-old Z. mays plants (P9363, Corteva Agriscience) grown in soil co-contaminated with petroleum hydrocarbons and heavy metals were treated with a suspension of live (bioaugmentation, BL) or thermally inactivated (biostimulation, BTI) cells of the P. qingdaonensis ZCR6rf+egfp strain or with sterile water (control, W). To simplify the text, the strain used is referred to as ZCR6. Fifty-four pots were prepared (three treatments × three replicates × six time points). Before the experiment, the soil was air-dried and passed through a 2-mm sieve. Soil moisture was adjusted to 50% of the maximum water-holding capacity and maintained at this level during the experimental period. The experiment was carried out in pots (2-L volume; 20-cm height, 12-cm width, and 12-cm length) filled with 1 kg of polluted soil (dry weight—DW) in a growth room under controlled conditions (14-h photoperiod at 15,000 lx; temperature 23/18 °C; light/dark). Seeds of Z. mays (2 per pot) were placed into the soil, and 2 weeks after germination, bacterial inoculation was performed as follows: (BL) 50 mL of the bacterial suspension of the ZCR6 strain (107 CFU [colony forming units] g−1 DW of soil); (BTI) 50 mL of thermally inactivated bacterial suspension of the ZCR6 strain (107 CFU g−1 DW of soil); and (W) 50 mL of sterile water. To determine the impact of bioaugmentation and biostimulation treatments on plant metabolism and bacterial activity in soil, RNA was isolated from maize tissues and rhizosphere soil sampled in the middle of the experiment, on day 20 after bacterial treatment. Maize tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and the soil samples were stored at − 80 °C until RNA isolation. This time point was chosen based on the results of our previous studies on enhanced phytoremediation (Płociniczak et al. 2017, 2019), which indicated that the highest metabolic activity of inoculants with high survival rates was observed approximately 3 weeks after soil bioaugmentation. In the first few days after inoculation, the introduced bacteria adapted to the new environment, and their activity was limited. Similarly, in the final days of the experiment, owing to the reduction in their number, no significant impact on the process was observed.

Effect of the ZCR6 strain on plant biomass

On days 3, 10, 20, 30, and 40, individual pots were utilized to obtain the samples; the plants were gently removed from the pots without damaging the roots. The root system was washed with tap water, then rinsed three times with deionized water to remove soil particles, and finally dried with a paper towel. The shoots (leaves and stems) and roots were then separated, and the fresh weights of these parts were determined.

Chlorophyll a fluorescence and pigment content

Chlorophyll a fluorescence was measured using a Plant Efficiency Analyzer (PocketPEA fluorimeter, Hansatech Instruments Ltd., England) according to Daszkowska-Golec et al. (2019). Briefly, measurements were performed in three biological replicates, each on a second fully developed leaf (two plants per replicate). Measurements were taken in plants from all treatments on days 10, 20, 30, and 40 after bacterial treatment. Day 3 was omitted because the leaf area was insufficient for reliable measurements at this time point.

The leaf chlorophyll, flavonol, and anthocyanin contents were measured using a Dualex Scientific + polyphenol and chlorophyll meter (Force-A, France). Measurements were performed on days 10, 20, 30, and 40 of the experiment on three biological replicates (two plants per replicate). Additionally, the nitrogen balance index (NBI), which combines chlorophyll and flavonol content (related to nitrogen/carbon allocation), was determined.

The fate of the ZCR6 strain in the soil and its ability to colonize plant tissues

The number of the ZCR6 strain in the soil was quantified using the dilution-plate method on LB agar plates supplemented with rifampicin (100 µg mL−1) on days 1, 3, 10, 20, 30, and 40 following soil inoculation, as described by Pacwa-Płociniczak et al. (2019). No bacterial growth was observed on the rifampicin-containing medium for soil suspensions prepared for the BTI and W experimental groups.

Additionally, the number of the ZCR6 cells in soil was assessed using flow cytometry according to a modified method of Khalili et al. (2019). For this purpose, 1 g of soil was suspended in 9 mL of a solution composed of 250 mM tetrasodium pyrophosphate (TSP; pH 8.0) and Tween 80 (final concentration of 0.5%). The mixture was then shaken horizontally for 20 min at room temperature on a Vortex-Genie 2 digital mixer. In the next step, after 2 min of sedimentation, 2 mL of soil slurry was layered onto 1 mL of Nycodenz solution (80% [wt/vol] prepared in 50 mM TSP buffer) contained in a new 15-mL Falcon tube. Bacterial cells and soil particles were separated by centrifugation (4650 RCF for 30 min). The supernatant with bacterial cells was collected in a fresh Falcon tube and examined by flow cytometry (CyFlow SPACE) with a blue laser (488 nm) and FL1 536/40 filter. Plant colonization by the ZCR6 strain was assessed on days 1, 3, 10, 20, 30, and 40 after soil inoculation, as described by Płociniczak et al. (2016). Briefly, maize roots and shoots were surface sterilized sequentially with 70% ethanol, 10% hydrogen peroxide, and 5% bleach (25 s for shoots and 35 s for roots). Between the use of subsequent solutions, the tissues were rinsed in sterile deionized water. Then the samples were rinsed three times with sterile distilled water (3 × 5 min). The sterilization process was verified by plating the final wash onto LB agar. The roots and shoots were macerated separately in 9 mL of 0.9% NaCl using a mortar and pestle, and 100 µL of the suspension was plated onto an LB agar plate supplemented with rifampicin (100 µg mL−1) at 28 °C for 48 h. Despite labeling the ZCR6 strain with the GFP protein, this feature could not be used to assess the number of cells of this strain colonizing plant tissues due to high background fluorescence.

Effect of the ZCR6 strain on TPH removal from the soil and heavy metal accumulation in plant tissues

The total petroleum hydrocarbon (TPH) concentration in the soil was measured separately for each pot on days 0, 20, and 40 of the phytoremediation experiment, following the ISO 16703:2011 procedure described by Pacwa-Płociniczak et al. (2016), with five technical replicates per pot.

The concentrations of zinc (Zn), cadmium (Cd), and copper (Cu) were assessed separately in the soil (on days 0 and 40 of the experiment) and in the shoots and roots of maize (on day 40 of the experiment). Briefly, 1 g of fresh soil, as well as shoot and root tissues, was oven-dried at 105 °C until a constant weight was reached, followed by digestion using a mixture of HNO3 (65%; 8 mL) and H2O2 (30%; 2 mL) in a Milestone Ethos One microwave oven (Milestone, USA). Mineralization was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The total concentrations of Zn, Cd, and Cu were determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (ICP-OES, OPTIMA 8300DV, Perkin Elmer, UK). Based on the results of metal accumulation in the shoots and roots, the translocation factor (TF), representing the ratio of metal concentration in shoots to that in the roots, was calculated.

Total activity of soil microorganisms

The assessment of fluorescein diacetate (FDA) hydrolysis activity in soil was conducted on days 20 and 40 of the experiment, following the methodology outlined by Sułowicz et al. (2016). Briefly, 2 g of DW soil was mixed with 15 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (60 mM; pH 7.6) and 0.2 mL of FDA (1 mg mL−1), and the samples were incubated at 30 °C on a rotary shaker. A blank sample was prepared by substituting 0.2 mL of distilled water for the FDA. Hydrolysis was stopped by adding 15 mL of a chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v) mixture, followed by centrifugation at 2000 RCF for 3 min. The filtered solution was analyzed for free fluorescein concentration at 490 nm using a Helios Epsilon spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the concentration was determined based on a calibration curve.

Transcriptomics analysis of plant tissues

Total RNA was extracted from roots and shoots (n = 3 per plant part and treatment) using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. RNA concentration and purity were measured with an Implen NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Implen GmbH, Munich, Germany). RNA quality and integrity were examined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). cDNA library preparation was performed with the TruSeq mRNA (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) protocol; obtained libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp, 40 million reads per sample) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Clean reads were mapped to the maize custom transcriptome created using the gffread tool in Linux (Pertea and Pertea 2020) based on the genomic sequence B73 NAM 5.0 and the gff annotation file (downloaded from the Ensembl Plants release 52). Reads were mapped with Kallisto v. 0.46.1 (Patro et al. 2017), followed by differential gene expression analysis with the 3DRNAseq pipeline in the R environment (https://3drnaseq.hutton.ac.uk/app_direct/3DRNAseq/). CPM (counts per million) and TPM (transcripts per million) values were generated from the Kallisto output using the tximport R package (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/tximport.html). Low-abundance transcripts and genes were filtered based on the mean–variance relationship, and only genes meeting defined CPM thresholds were considered expressed. The TMM (trimmed mean of M values) normalization method was applied to read counts, and principal component analysis showed no noticeable batch effects. Gene expression levels were compared using the limma R package (Ritchie et al. 2015), and DEGs (differentially expressed genes) with significant expression changes were determined for each contrast group. Differential expression significance was set at |log2FC|≥ 2, and a BH (Benjamini-Hochberg) adjusted P value < 0.05. GO (Gene Ontology) enrichment was analyzed using the TopGO package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/topGO/), and the results were visualized using the ggplot R package.

Metatranscriptomics analysis of soil

RNA was extracted from soils (n = 3 per treatment) using an RNeasy PowerSoil Total RNA Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. RNA concentration and purity were measured with an Implen NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Implen GmbH, Munich, Germany). RNA quality and integrity were examined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). rRNA depletion was carried out with the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (Bacteria) (New England BioLabs, Germany). Subsequently, library preparation was performed with the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) protocol, and the obtained libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp, 100 million reads per sample) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Data analysis was performed using ATLAS v2.14.2 with the GeneCatalog pipeline and default parameters unless otherwise specified (Kieser et al. 2020). Raw paired-end reads were subjected to quality control using tools from the BBTools suite v39.01 (including adapter trimming, quality filtering, optical duplicate removal, and removal of contaminant sequences (PhiX)). Reads were additionally error-corrected, and overlapping read pairs were merged prior to assembly. Quality-filtered reads from all samples were co-assembled using rnaSPAdes v.3.15.5 as implemented in ATLAS. Only contigs with a minimum length of 1000 bp and sufficient read support were retained for downstream analyses (Nurk et al. 2017). Filtered reads from individual samples were subsequently mapped back to the assembled contigs for transcript quantification. Gene prediction was performed on the assembled contigs, and predicted genes were clustered into nonredundant gene catalog using the linclust algorithm as implemented in MMseqs2 v13.45111 with a minimum nucleotide identity of 90% and a minimum gene length of 270 bp. Functional annotation of the gene catalog was carried out using eggNOG v5.0 (Huerta-Cepas et al. 2019). Differential abundance was assessed using the MaAsLin2 (Microbiome Multivariable Associations with Linear Models, Bioconductor). This package utilizes a multimodel approach for conducting multivariable association testing in microbiome datasets (Mallick et al. 2021) implemented in the R software. Normalization of read counts was performed using the TMM (trimmed mean of M values) method, followed by a negative binomial (negbin) model. Genes exhibiting an adjusted P value (p-adj) < 0.05, and an absolute coefficient value ≥ 2, were considered differentially expressed. For further insight, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the topGO (Alexa and Rahnenführer 2023) and clusterProfiler (Wu et al. 2021) packages to identify biological functions and pathways affected by the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), respectively.

Taxonomic classification of the sequences was performed using Kraken2 (Wood et al. 2019), which employs a k-mer–based algorithm, with the k2_pluspfp_20240904 database. The obtained taxonomic profiles were analyzed in R using the phyloseq package. Only bacterial taxa were retained. Alpha diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou) were calculated from relative abundance data and compared among treatments using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (P < 0.05). Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and visualized by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Treatment effects on bacterial community composition were tested using PERMANOVA (999 permutations), and homogeneity of dispersion was verified using betadisper. Differential taxonomic abundance at different levels was assessed using DESeq2 with a negative binomial mode. Taxa with adjusted P value (p-adj) < 0.05 and |log2FC|≥ 1 were considered significant.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed with STATISTICA 13.3 PL software (StatSoft, Tulsa, USA). An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was initially conducted, and Tukey’s HSD test was subsequently applied to determine the significance of individual treatments on plant biomass, TPH removal, HM accumulation, the translocation factor, and total microbial activity. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on three biological replicates.

Results

The impact of the ZCR6 strain on plant biomass, TPH removal, and heavy metal accumulation

The inoculation of soil with live cells of the P. qingdaonensis ZCR6 strain did not significantly increase the fresh biomass of the shoots and roots of Z. mays during the experimental period compared to the experimental groups treated with thermally inactivated ZCR6 and water (Fig. S1).

Due to the complex composition of diesel oil–derived hydrocarbons, changes in hydrocarbon content were quantified and presented as total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH), which provides an integrative and robust measure of remediation efficiency under these conditions. The assessment of hydrocarbon removal from the soil under various treatments was performed on days 20 and 40 after bacterial introduction (Table 1). On day 20, a significantly (P < 0.05) lower efficiency of petroleum hydrocarbon removal was observed in soil inoculated with live cells of the ZCR6 strain (BL, 7.95 ± 1.22%) compared to soils treated with thermally inactivated cells of this strain (BTI, 24.56 ± 5.48%) and water (W, 18.14 ± 1.50%). For the BTI and W treatments, the TPH removal between days 20 and 40 was limited. On day 40, compared to day 20, a significant increase in the efficiency of PH removal was observed only in BL soil (17.39 ± 1.62%); however, it remained significantly lower than that for BTI (25.15 ± 2.53%), but was comparable to that of W (19.61 ± 1.55%). The fate of the ZCR6 strain in the soil and colonization of Z. mays tissues

The number of ZCR6 cells in soil was monitored, and their ability to colonize maize roots and shoots was determined using two independent techniques: the plating method on agar medium supplemented with rifampicin (soil and plant tissues) and flow cytometry (soil). ZCR6 cells were detected in the soil throughout the experiment using both techniques. Using the dilution-plate method, we observed a gradual decrease in the number of ZCR6 cells from an initial value of 7.8 to 3.87 log10 CFU g−1 DW of soil estimated on day 40 of the experiment (Fig. 2a). In turn, the results from the flow cytometry analysis revealed a decrease in the number of ZCR6 cells only within the first 3 days of the experiment. In this time the number of ZCR6 cells decreased from the initial 7.8 to 6.5 log10 CFU g−1 DW of soil; however, until the end of the experiment, their number remained at a stable level around 6.7 log10 CFU g−1 DW of soil (Fig. 2b). Chlorophyll a fluorescence and pigment content

To analyze the photosynthetic performance of maize under the tested treatments throughout the experimental period, chlorophyll a fluorescence was measured. First, the OJIP transient fluorescence was studied; however, no differences were observed between treatments (Fig. S2).

Similarly, biophysical photosynthesis parameters were calculated (φP0, PIABS, ABS/CS, TR/CS, ET/CS, DI/CS, and RC/CS, explanations of these parameters are given in Fig. S3); however, no significant (P > 0.05) differences in their values were reported between plants grown in soils treated with the ZCR6 strain (BL and BTI) and those grown in W. The only significant difference (P < 0.05) was related to the age of the plants (Fig. S3).

The chlorophyll, flavonol, and anthocyanin contents in the leaves of maize during the phytoremediation experiment were also measured (Fig. S4a-c). On each sampling day (10, 20, 30, and 40), no significant changes (P > 0.05) in the content of these pigments were observed between plants from different treatments (BL, BTI, and W). For all plants, the nitrogen balance index (NBI) was also calculated, but we did not observe significant changes (P < 0.05) in the values of this parameter between the experimental groups (Fig. S4d).

Discussion

Scientific literature dominates the reports of successful phytoremediation attempts, and failures are most often attributed to the inappropriate selection of the plants used or to the too high initial concentration of pollutants and/or their resistance to biodegradation. In such experiments, known as microbial-assisted phytoremediation, plants are supported by introduced microorganisms that should exhibit desirable traits, such as promoting plant growth and/or degrading pollutants, under laboratory conditions. However, detailed knowledge of the actual activity of inoculants under environmental conditions remains very limited. Although introducing beneficial microorganisms into single- and/or multicontaminated soils can enhance phytoremediation, the observed effects may not necessarily result from the metabolic activity of the inoculants.

Most studies assume that bioaugmented strains will exhibit the same beneficial traits observed during biochemical characterization under laboratory conditions (Agnello et al. 2016; Wojtowicz et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2026). In such studies, however, the fate of microorganisms introduced into contaminated soils to enhance contaminant removal is typically not monitored. Likewise, detailed assessments of the activity of introduced strains are rarely conducted and are often limited to measurements of selected enzyme activities. Such approaches do not provide unambiguous evidence of inoculant activity and may instead reflect changes in the activity of indigenous microorganisms in response to inoculation. In these studies, increased pollutant removal efficiency is commonly attributed to plant growth–promoting traits and contaminant degradation mechanisms demonstrated by the inoculated strains under laboratory conditions. However, such a statement is not obvious and remains an assumption that requires confirmation through appropriate analyses. For instance, our previous research does not support this assumption. In previously published research, we confirmed that thermally inactivated bacterial biomass (necromass) (Ptaszek et al. 2020) and biofertilizers, such as meat and bone meal (Pacwa-Płociniczak et al. 2024), may have similar effects. This means that without additional analysis, it cannot be confirmed that inoculated strains survive the inoculation step, compete effectively with native microbiota, and remain metabolically active.

Since isolating suitable strains, characterizing them, and preparing large volumes of inoculum are time-consuming and costly, it is essential to ensure that strains introduced into the soil actively support phytoremediation through their metabolic activity. Otherwise, such an approach should be reconsidered in favor of simpler, cost-effective alternatives, such as biostimulation. However, such conclusions can only be drawn after detailed molecular analyses of plants and rhizosphere soil, including transcriptomic and metatranscriptomic profiling. This study employed a global transcript analysis to evaluate changes in plant and rhizospheric bacteria gene expression in response to bioaugmentation with live bacteria or biostimulation with thermally inactivated biomass, compared to a water-treated control.

Monitoring the survival potential of introduced bacteria in the soil and their ability to colonize plant tissues should be a mandatory part of bioaugmentation experiments, as this type of phytoremediation support requires the activity of inoculants in the target environment. In our study, we used a rifampicin-resistant ZCR6 mutant carrying the pMP4655 plasmid with constitutive egfp expression to track the fate of the strain in soil via two independent methods, which showed divergent trends. We observed a decrease in the number of rifampicin-resistant cells of the ZCR6 strain, whereas a less intensive decrease was observed in the number of marked cells when the GFP detection was applied. These results confirm our previous findings (Ptaszek et al. 2020; Pacwa-Płociniczak et al. 2024) that most of the introduced bacteria, after being introduced into the soil, enter at least temporarily the VBNC (viable but nonculturable) state and cannot grow under laboratory conditions. This response may be associated with the introduction of microorganisms into a harsh environment, which imposes bacterial stress due to pollutant exposure and the need to compete with well-established indigenous microbial communities. Despite the confirmed survival in soil and plant (root) colonization potential, the ZCR6 did not enhance plant growth and phytoremediation efficiency. On day 20, PH removal in the BL soil was significantly lower than in the BTI and W groups. By day 40, PH loss in BL reached the control (W) level but was still significantly lower than in the BTI group. Our observations are consistent with the field-scale studies by Jørgensen et al. (2000), which demonstrated that bioaugmentation does not necessarily enhance the removal of petroleum hydrocarbons. In their composting-based remediation experiment on mineral oil-contaminated soil, the application of commercially available microbial inocula did not result in increased TPH removal, compared to noninoculated treatments. In contrast, stimulating indigenous microorganisms through aeration, nutrient amendment, and the supply of organic bulking agents was sufficient to achieve more effective contaminant degradation, particularly under conditions that favored the activity of native microbial communities. These findings also highlight the need for complementary functional analyses to support conclusions regarding the real contribution of microbial inoculants to bioremediation processes. This result also indicates the enormous difficulty of creating a universal inoculum that will be effective regardless of soil type and/or the type of contamination.

Metatranscriptome analysis of soil bacteria from BTI and BL treatments revealed that introducing thermally inactivated bacterial biomass (also in BL due to limited inoculant survival) enhanced bacterial nutrient uptake, as evidenced by the upregulation of genes related to ribosomes, oxidative phosphorylation, and protein export. Unexpectedly, significantly downregulated pathways in BL soil were associated with the degradation of organic compounds (e.g., xylene, naphthalene, ethylbenzene), explaining the two- to threefold lower PH loss in BL compared to W and BTI soils during the first part of the experiment, up to the 20th day. Importantly, no increased expression of bacterial genes associated with plant growth promotion was observed in BL, compared to W, explaining the similar plant biomass in those experimental groups. This indicates limited or absent activation of phytoremediation-supporting mechanisms by the ZCR6 strain under soil conditions, despite their previously demonstrated activity under laboratory conditions. The reduced microbial activity is attributed to the temporal disturbances in the ecological balance of soil microbial communities caused by inoculation. This interpretation is supported by taxonomic analysis of the metatranscriptome, which revealed a shift in the structure of the active bacterial communities in BL soil, characterized by reduced transcriptional activity of taxa commonly associated with hydrocarbon degradation (e.g., Rhodanobacteraceae, Nocardiaceae, Alcaligenaceae, Moraxellaceae) (Kanaly et al. 2002; De Carvalho et al. 2009; Lee et al. 2010; Guo et al. 2012; Song et al. 2016; Martinez Alvarez et al. 2022; Juárez et al. 2023; Lamprea Pineda et al. 2025). At the same time, in the BL experimental group, an increased contribution of Candidatus Azoamicaceae and Caulobacteraceae, together with the upregulation of the Caulobacter cell cycle pathway (ko04112), suggests a shift in microbial activity from degradation-oriented processes toward regulatory and stress-adaptive strategies. The mentioned taxa are commonly detected in oxygen-limited or chemically stressed soils and are associated with nitrogen transformation processes (Wilhelm 2018; Graf et al. 2021; Speth et al. 2024), suggesting that their activity reflects adaptation to altered redox and nutrient conditions in BL soil.

Plant transcriptome analysis revealed organ- and treatment-specific patterns of gene expression in maize. Bioaugmentation with live ZCR6 induced more changes in gene expression in roots, while biostimulation had a more substantial effect in shoots. These results can be explained by findings from previous studies, which demonstrated the effective transport of nutrients, e.g., DNA released from dead bacterial biomass to aboveground plant tissues, primarily leaves, thereby providing essential elements such as phosphorus and nitrogen that influence plant metabolism (Gahan et al. 2003; Paungfoo-Lonhienne et al. 2010; Płociniczak et al. 2020). GO enrichment analysis showed significant upregulation of two biological processes, regulation of biosynthetic process and macromolecule biosynthetic process, in roots of both BL and BTI plants. Shared upregulated genes included those encoding WRKY, ethylene-responsive (ERF), and PISTILLATA-like transcription factors, suggesting their expression as a response to environmental disturbances, such as shifts in rhizosphere microbial communities after ZCR6 introduction, regardless of viability of bacterial inoculum. WRKY and ERF proteins are known regulators of stress responses, defense, development, and senescence (Zhao et al. 2011; Müller and Munné-Bosch 2015; Wang et al. 2018). The similar transcriptional response in BL and BTI plants may result from rapid nutrient release from dead bacteria in both treatments. This phenomenon also occurred in the BL variant, where some bacteria also did not survive inoculation and caused an effect similar to that caused by dead bacterial biomass in the BTI group. Surviving cells may, however, still exhibited limited metabolic activity (e.g., biosurfactant production and/or their release, influencing soil conditions), which could explain the transient differences in PH removal among BL and BTI groups observed between day 20 and 40. Additional, WRKY and ERF genes were also upregulated in roots, further supporting the hypothesis that both treatments elicited stress-associated transcriptional responses, including accelerated maturation (Wada and Takeno 2010).

The response of the maize shoot transcriptome to BL treatment was limited relative to that observed in BTI plants. Nevertheless, BL.S samples, similar to BL.R and BTI.S plants, showed significant upregulation of biological processes associated with the activity of DREB (dehydration-responsive element binding) transcription factors, which bind specifically to DRE cis-elements and regulate the expression of target genes. DREBs play a key role in plant tolerance to drought, cold, heat, salt, and potentially also heavy metals (Qin et al. 2007; Zhou et al. 2016). For instance, DREB genes were among the top 50 most upregulated transcripts in rice under Cd stress (Ogawa et al. 2009). Moreover, their role in Cu tolerance has also been demonstrated in both rice (Sudo et al. 2008) and black mustard (Merakli et al. 2021). In BTI shoots, DREB TFs, MYB78, AP2/ERF, WRKY, ERF, zinc-finger proteins, as well as other broad stress-response regulators, were also upregulated. These results suggest that genes associated with heavy metal tolerance were activated regardless of the treatment. Soil analyses revealed that heavy metal concentrations exceeded permissible limits. Both BL and BTI treatments resulted in approximately 30% higher Zn accumulation in maize roots compared to the control, with similar, but not statistically significant, trend observed for other metals. Importantly, the enhanced Zn accumulation was accompanied by a significant decrease in soil pH in both treatments. Soil acidification by microorganisms and chemical compounds is a well-established factor increasing the solubility and bioavailability of zinc and other metal ions. This likely explains the increased Zn uptake observed in plants grown under BL and BTI treatments, without the measurable plant growth–promoting effects. These findings indicate that the application of the ZCR6 strain, particularly in the form of dead biomass, influenced indirectly metal bioavailability primarily through effects on soil chemistry, such as pH modification, rather than through classical plant growth–promoting mechanisms. Such soil acidification may be caused, e.g., by microorganisms that have multiplied after nutrients were introduced into the soil in the form of dead biomass.

Similar response to bacterial inoculation was reported for maize by Araújo et al. (2023), who demonstrated that the plant growth–promoting Arthrobacter sp. strain and its consortium with Streptomyces alboflavus did not consistently enhance maize growth, and even exerted negative effects on its growth under fluctuating soil moisture conditions. Importantly, the authors demonstrated that inoculation outcomes were strongly dependent on environmental factors and interactions between inoculants and the indigenous microbiota, rather than solely on the plant growth–promoting potential of the strains. Together with our transcriptomic data, these observations suggest that bacterial inoculation may trigger stress- and environmental-related transcriptional responses in plants, without necessarily leading to measurable growth promotion.

In addition to the desired activity that strains used to support bioremediation processes should exhibit, the safety of their use is also crucial. The P. qingdaonensis used in this study is currently not included in international biosafety risk group classifications (WHO, NIH, EU Directive 2000/54/EC). Based on its environmental origin and the lack of documented pathogenicity, the ZCR6 strain was handled under Biosafety Level 1 conditions in this study. Genome analysis revealed concededly the presence of genes whose products are sometimes associated with virulence in pathogenic bacteria, including flagella, type IV pili, alginate biosynthesis, pyoverdine production, and hydrogen cyanide synthesis; however, these traits are commonly reported in beneficial plant-associated Pseudomonas spp. In most cases, they contribute to motility, root colonization, biofilm formation, iron acquisition, stress tolerance, and adaptation to the rhizosphere environment rather than pathogenicity. Importantly, no genes encoding the LPS O-antigen characteristic of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, phenazine biosynthesis pathways, harpins, or other known bacterial toxins were detected. With the exception of hydrogen cyanide, no genetic determinants associated with the synthesis of toxic compounds were identified. Nevertheless, given the limited number of studies available for this species, any future practical application of P. qingdaonensis ZCR6 would require a dedicated, case-specific biosafety assessment.

Conclusions

Although the results of our studies are largely specific to the experimental system used, the lack of positive effects of the applied live bacterial strain on maize growth or hydrocarbon removal provides important context for researchers working on this issue. The findings indicate that microbial traits demonstrated under laboratory conditions do not necessarily translate into functional effectiveness in complex soil environments, highlighting the need for in situ validation of bioaugmentation strategies. Nevertheless, we do not exclude the possibility that, under different experimental conditions, particularly in soils with distinct contamination characteristics, strain ZCR6 could effectively support phyto- and/or bioremediation processes. In the context of the present study, however, biostimulation emerges as a relevant reference approach, as it proved comparable to, or even more effective than,

bioaugmentation in contaminant removal under the conditions tested.
