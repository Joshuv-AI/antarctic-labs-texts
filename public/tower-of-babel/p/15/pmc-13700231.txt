A case series evaluation of a schema therapy-coaching programme for carers of patients with moderate to severe anorexia nervosa: the CAREFREE programme
Authors: Emma Mawdsley, Stacey McNicol, Alasdair C. Rutherford, Lesley Symon, Debbie Smith, Hazel Elliott, Susan Simpson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnorexia Nervosa (AN) can be a complex condition to treat, often complicated by high chronicity, complexity and comorbidity. Treatments for AN with comorbidity and complexity are limited, with carers having to take a large role in supporting these individuals. Carer distress can be high, impacting carer wellbeing and contributing to familial dynamics which can also perpetuate AN behaviours as a way of coping. This study evaluated a novel carers group, the CAREFREE programme, for the specific needs of carers of Moderate to Severe AN (MS-AN: adults with AN who have not responded to previous treatments, have a complex comorbidity, and/or complex aetiology), using schema therapy-focused coaching to improve carer distress and family functioning.MethodsA non-concurrent group case series design was employed. Outcome measures included carer distress, expressed emotion, family functioning and communication, mood, and attachment-related anxiety and avoidance. Outcomes were analysed at 4-timepoints, (i.e. pre-intervention, mid-point [6 weeks], post-intervention [12-weeks] and 3-month follow-up), using multi-level modelling.Results53 carers commenced the programme in the study across 5 groups. Carer distress, expressed emotion, family functioning, mood and attachment-related avoidance significantly improved after the end of the group and remained significantly improved at follow-up. Attachment-anxiety did not lead to statistically significant changes, although a trend of improvement was seen.ConclusionsPreliminary evidence suggests improvements in carer wellbeing and family functioning following participation in the CAREFREE programme for carers of MS-AN. Attachment outcome measures that did not lead to statistically significant change may be related to the time-limited nature of the intervention and the group intervention itself, with greater change in attachment outcomes often requiring individualised and more intensive interventions. Future research is required to explore what impact this has on patient outcomes.Trial registration This study was registered with clinicaltrials.gov (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06624397).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40337-026-01704-9.

Background

The treatment of Anorexia Nervosa (AN) is complex, with current evidence indicating poor long term efficacy even with the evidence-based, recommended interventions [1, 2]. Outcomes are poorer for adults with the condition [3]. Long-term follow-up studies show that approximately two thirds have not reached recovery at 9 years after onset, and one-third remain unrecovered at 22 years [4]. The term ‘severe and enduring anorexia nervosa’ (SE-AN) has been used to refer to those with at least 5–10 years of illness [5]. The majority of adult patients are categorised as such [6]. Complexity and comorbidity is high in adult AN. Around half meet diagnostic criteria for a personality disorder [7], and there is high prevalence of other physical and psychological comorbidities [8, 9].

Comorbidity in AN negatively affects treatment outcomes [10] and increases risk of mortality [11]. This treatment journey for severe and longstanding AN (SL-AN) or comorbid AN often requires significant input from family members or partners as carers. There is a dire need for long term treatment and support options for SL-AN and their carers. However, even with this need, a recent UK health report indicates over a three-fold funding deficit to adult eating disorder (ED) care compared to that of child and adolescent services [12].

Caring for a family member affected by AN has been linked to significant psychosocial and financial strain, impacting family communication, interpersonal relationships and carers’ own health and wellbeing [13]. Carer distress includes substantial impairment to quality of life and high levels of loneliness. More than half of carers experience clinically significant anxiety and 13% experience depression [14–16]. Day to day life for carers can be affected by attempting to fulfil the complex role of ‘treatment provider’. Carers often receive little support or expert-provided knowledge regarding the range of complex factors underlying the illness [17, 18]. Carers can struggle with the financial and logistical implications of their caring role, including reduced working hours and income, as well as greater expenditure for necessary travel to appointments or sourcing private treatment [13]. Carer distress can substantially impact how carers communicate with patients, potentially leading to patterns of interaction that maintain the eating disorder. For example, high levels of anxiety and concern in carers can manifest as overprotective or critical communication styles, patterns that fall under the construct of expressed emotion (EE) [19].

High levels of caregiver EE have been shown to have significant negative impacts on caregiver mental health, including higher rates of depression [19]. Unintentionally, unhelpful communication dynamics within families can easily become entrenched, potentially exacerbating patients’ maladaptive coping behaviours (e.g. eating disorder behaviours such as restriction, excessive exercise) and impacting motivation for change. Families can find themselves accommodating the symptoms of AN, which can subsequently reinforce and maintain these patterns. Although unintentional, communication can become negatively focused and lead to a vicious cycle. In this cycle, carers’ attempts to provide emotional support are perceived by the individual with AN as overprotective or controlling. This often results in a push-back response whereby the individual tries to assert their autonomy via their eating behaviours, thereby further reinforcing the problem and triggering expressed emotion from the caregiver [20, 21]. Factors associated with AN have also been shown to exacerbate emotional expression in the carer, such as longer duration of illness, more severe ED symptoms, and older age of patient [22–24]. Specific skills are required to manage EE in families with adults with AN in a manner which leads to a healthy and recovery-friendly environment.

Family functioning has been shown to be negatively affected both before the onset on AN and during the course of the condition [25, 26]. Problems in families, such as conflict between parents, have been shown to contribute to more severe AN symptoms and worse familial functioning [26]. Furthermore, people with AN can view their family functioning as poorer than their mother’s perceptions [27], indicating that people with EDs can be more sensitive to difficult dynamics in families. In adults with AN, family functioning dynamics differ importantly from adolescent populations, as intimate partnerships often become the primary relationship context [28, 29]. All close relationships—including parent-child, sibling, and intimate adult relationships—are typically affected and reorganised in the presence of an eating disorder, with the illness often taking a central position within the family system [28]. Partners of adults with AN face unique challenges in navigating the balance between an adult-to-adult relationship and a carer-patient dynamic, often experiencing difficulties with challenging family dynamics, divided loyalties, and familial stress [30]. Research indicates that romantic relationships commonly experience significant strain related to AN, with partners reporting the particular difficulty of maintaining intimacy while simultaneously attempting to support recovery [29, 30]. The quality of these partner relationships and the broader family functioning both influence treatment outcomes and the well-being of all family members involved.

Research has increasingly indicated the role of parental bonding and attachment styles in predicting outcomes in EDs [24, 31]. In particular, attachment insecurity has been associated with worse longitudinal trends of ED symptoms [32]. The two main types of attachment insecurity are referred to as attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance [33, 34], with the latter more commonly linked to AN [35]. Ruptures in parental bonding have been associated with the development of attachment difficulties in eating disorders, with parenting styles characterised by high control and low affection commonly reported [36–38]. Involving parents in the treatment of their family member with AN and addressing how their own attachment and parental bonding influences current relationships, may be helpful in enhancing the whole family dynamic and thereby benefit the patient’s outcome [39].

In adults with AN, romantic partners often become the primary attachment figure, presenting unique challenges as they navigate both intimate and caregiving roles [40]. Partners report relationship insecurity, fears of rejection, and caregiving distress similar to parents [29, 41], suggesting that interventions addressing attachment dynamics should consider both parental and partner relationships to comprehensively support families affected by AN [40].

A new paradigm is required which reflects the needs of adults with AN with moderate to high levels of severity and complexity, whilst recognising the potentially crucial role of significant others in supporting change. Previous research indicates that carer interventions can improve outcomes for people with AN, especially when completed within a separate space away from the person with AN [42]. Carer groups have become increasingly available for carers of people with EDs in recent years, although the majority are focused on parent carers of children and adolescents with EDs [42–44]. For adults, these have largely been limited to the provision of ‘support’, often with pre-recorded materials and limited direct input from expert clinicians [e.g. 24, 25]. An exception to this is the ‘New Maudsley Method’ [45, 46], a leading framework for the delivery of carer support and skills groups offering a series of clinician and/or volunteer with lived experience led workshops, indicating positive outcomes for carer and patient [47–49]. To the authors’ knowledge, no current carer groups focus specifically on the needs of carers of adults with AN with moderate to high severity and/or comorbidity. Innovative treatment approaches are required focusing on improving quality of life and relationships within the family, reducing harm associated with AN symptoms (such as medical complications and psychological distress) and ultimately reducing further experiences of treatment non-responsiveness. This approach should engage carers as key figures in the recovery process in order to improve the quality of their own lives as well as that of the patient.

The current study

The CAREFREE programme is grounded in Schema Therapy (ST) with additional techniques from Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT). Both models extend beyond skills-based approaches by emphasising experiential growth and change. The programme also draws on Treasure and Schmidt’s [50] model of carer involvement in AN, which highlights how temperament and attachment styles shape both carer and patient behaviours, acting as predisposing and maintaining factors. ST is well suited to address these self-reinforcing interpersonal cycles by fostering emotional fluency, self–other understanding, and compassion. Through working with different ‘modes’ (e.g. Inner Critic, Child, Coping, and Healthy modes), carers are supported to build insight, motivation, and compassion for themselves and their loved one. Experiential methods are used to explore interpersonal patterns that may hinder or support recovery. Emerging evidence supports the use of ST in eating disorders [51–53] with promising outcomes for SL- AN [53, 54]. As an integrative model, ST emphasises the interaction of temperament and unmet emotional needs in childhood as key vulnerabilities, and uses experiential, somatic, and cognitive-behavioural methods within a safe therapeutic relationship to promote change [55, 56].

Due to the geographical constraints in Scotland, access to carer interventions is highly dependent on the local health board jurisdiction within which the adult with AN is living. Many health boards having minimal provision for EDs, and geographical remoteness impedes attendance for many living in rural areas [57]. This resulting ‘postcode lottery’ can mean many families are in dire need of intervention and support, but often unable to access it. Online, national groups can overcome some of these barriers and improve access to psychological interventions for carers in Scotland.

Methods

Design

This study used a non-concurrent group case series design. Five non-concurrent groups were run over two years, with within-group repeated measures.

A case series was chosen as the most appropriate design for the initial evaluation of this intervention. This approach enables feasibility, safety, and acceptability to be established, while providing preliminary signals of clinical benefit and allowing refinement of the programme prior to a larger trial [59]. Importantly, the case series design also allowed the intervention to be delivered and evaluated within a naturalistic NHS setting in Scotland, reflecting real-world conditions where carers actually receive support. A Randomised Controlled Trial (RCT) at this early stage would have been premature, resource-intensive, and impractical in this context. By using repeated measures, each group served as its own control, strengthening internal validity while maintaining ecological validity. This design therefore offered a pragmatic and clinically meaningful first step in developing the evidence base for the programme.

Participants

Carers

Participants were carers, adult family members and spouses with an adult family member (> 17 years) with MS-AN. MS-AN was defined as those with a diagnosis of AN provided by an NHS service, and at least one of the following: (1) Complex aetiology (e.g. trauma, abuse, complex family dynamics); (2) complex pathway (i.e. shifting between transdiagnostic ED diagnostic categories); (3) absence of recovery in response to first-line treatments; (4) Has relapsed following initial improvements; (5) chronicity (AN has been present > 4 years; 6) Has complex comorbidity (e.g. post-traumatic stress disorder, obsessive compulsive disorder, personality disorder).

Inclusion criteria for carers were: (1) Have a family member, partner or spouse with MS-AN; (2) age > 17 years; (3) can speak and read English; (4) no acute psychotic mental health state.

Procedure

Participants for the project were recruited from participating Scottish NHS adult services, as well as being advertised through BEAT Scotland, an ED charity and third-sector service. Participants were either recruited indirectly via the patient with AN, directly through carers involved in services, or voluntarily through flyers available in participating services or on the BEAT website. Patients with MS-AN were invited by their therapist to offer their carers participation. Potential participants were offered a virtual screening meeting to discuss study procedures and enrolment into the study.

Five non-concurrent 12-week therapeutic groups were run online. Carer quantitative measures were completed at pre-, mid- (6-weeks), post-intervention (12-weeks), and 3-months follow-up. Baseline assessments required completion before commencing the group. All participants completed the questionnaires via online software.

All groups were facilitated by two members of the research team (SS and EM), both doctoral-level clinical psychologists trained in ST and CFT, specialising in EDs. Treatment fidelity was assessed by means of a checklist developed for the treatment protocol, adapted from the Group Schema Therapy Rating Scale - Revised [60]. The checklist contains key components of group ST and the main tasks or goals to be accomplished each session. During each group, a minimum of 1 session per group was observed by a researcher to rate the fidelity to the intervention.

Intervention

The CAREFREE programme covers a range of topics based on the ST framework, including psychoeducation, practical information regarding AN, and use of experiential imagery-based and chairwork techniques from which behavioural goals are set. The group programme consists of 12 weekly sessions, each lasting 75 min. The ST-based group places particular emphasis on helping carers recognise and respond to core emotional needs, framed within the programme as emotional ‘vitamins’, as a route to enhanced wellbeing, emotional homeostasis, and more connected caregiving relationships. The programme explores ways in which carers and their loved ones with AN may have developed coping strategies which provide short term relief but ultimately led to emotional and interpersonal disconnection. The carers reflect on the care that they received in their early lives and consider how this affected them and their caring style now. The role of food and eating are explored as attempted solutions to overcoming feelings of disconnection which can keep carers and their loved ones locked in interpersonal dynamics that increasingly lead to burnout and greater suffering. Carers are encouraged to connect with their own emotional needs as well as those of their loved ones, and to develop relational and communication skills which facilitate direct expression of vulnerability and emotional connection. The group format allows natural group processes, including peer modelling, feedback, support, reinforcement, and positive social comparison. The majority of group sessions included spaces for breakout rooms for participants to discuss issues from the group content and either a schema-focused cognitive-behavioural or experiential intervention, with further intervention details reported in the original feasibility trial [58]. Participants were provided with session slides and a video summary of each session for future reference.

Sample characteristic measures

The Young Parenting Inventory-revised, YPI-R2 [68] was completed at mid-point (rather than pre-intervention) to ensure that carers were familiar with basic parenting concepts introduced in the programme, thereby enhancing their capacity to recognise the meaning of questionnaire items and engage in self-reflection. As the YPI-R2 measures the person’s recalled childhood experiences of parenting (rather than current state), responses are unlikely to be influenced by the intervention itself. The YPI-R2 is a 36-item questionnaire measuring the person’s perceived childhood experience of the parenting they received, with 6 subscales: Competitiveness and Status seeking; Degradation and Rejection; Emotional Inhibition and Deprivation; Overprotection and Overindulgence; Punitiveness; Controlling. Subscale scores are calculated as mean scores with higher scores indicating stronger perceived unhelpful parenting. The YPI-R2 has been shown to be a reliable and valid measure of perceived parenting. YPI-R2 subscale scores were used to characterise the sample (Supplementary Material 2) and were not included as outcomes in the longitudinal analyses.

Weekly paper measures

Session Rating Scale (SRS) was administered after each session, with 1-item from a visual analogue scale measuring session satisfaction overall [69]. For the weekly SRS, mode and mean scores over the 12 sessions were calculated and explored visually.

Analysis

The original proposal anticipated a sample size of eight to fifteen participants in each of five groups, for a minimum of 40 participants. A priori power analyses estimated that a sample of 34 participants would provide sufficient statistical power to detect a medium difference of 0.5 standard deviations in the primary outcome measures, based on a paired T-test (α = 0.05, β = 0.2) comparing baseline to post-intervention.

A multi-level (mixed-effects) modelling approach was used, as the data comprised repeated observations nested within individuals [70]. Multi-level models accommodate unbalanced data, in which the number and timing of observations vary across participants, by using all available observations under a missing-at-random assumption rather than discarding incomplete cases through listwise deletion. This retains statistical power and reduces the bias that can arise from case deletion, and is one reason multi-level models are generally preferred to repeated-measures ANOVA for longitudinal data of this kind [71].

Mixed-effects linear regression models were used, with each scale and subscale score treated as a continuous outcome variable. Seventeen models were estimated, one per outcome. Each model included two fixed effects: time point (the primary predictor, capturing change across the four assessments); and group number (capturing differences between the five groups). They also include a random intercept for participant ID to account for the clustering of repeated measures within individuals. Models were estimated in Stata 17 using the default maximum likelihood (ML) method, and all converged within a single iteration. For each model, the overall Wald chi-square test of joint fixed-effect significance, factor-level joint (omnibus) tests for time and group, and the intraclass correlation (ICC) were obtained, the last describing the proportion of outcome variance attributable to between-person clustering. Unadjusted estimates are reported in the tables. Estimated marginal means are visualised for all combined scales and selected sub-scales, with remaining subscale figures in Supplementary Material 1. Pairwise comparisons between time points were estimated from the model margins and Bonferroni-adjusted for the six within-model comparisons (Supplementary Material 3).

Results

Descriptive statistics

Acceptance and retention

Of the 75 carers assessed for eligibility, 63 (84.0%) showed interest in the group. 53 commenced the intervention, of which 36 (67.9%) attended at least 75% of sessions showing acceptable retention. A flow chart of recruitment, retention and reasons for non-completion can be seen in Fig. 1.

Sessions were generally rated highly throughout via the SRS, with average scores ranging from 5.7 at time 1 to 6.5 at time 8, and finally 6.3 at time 12. The mode for each session was 7, which is the highest satisfaction rating, indicating overall that carers were satisfied with the weekly sessions. Item non-response ranged from 0.6% (EDSIS sub-scales) to 6.3% (Family Questionnaire), with full details available in the supplementary material 2. In the statistical modelling, complete case analysis was used for each outcome measure, with between 1 and 10 cases excluded due to missing data on one or more items (supplementary material 2).

53 carers completed the baseline survey, with 39 completing at mid-point, 34 at the end-point, and 33 at follow-up (providing the anticipated statistical power). Of the 42 carers who completed a demographic survey (Table 1), the mean age was 54.3 and the majority were female (74.4%). 83% of carers were a parent (of which 82% were mothers), with remaining carers as spouses/partners (of which 19% were female).

Sample descriptive characteristics

The mean age of the person cared for was 25.5, with a mean age of onset of AN at 19. All 53 carers stated the person they cared for were assigned female at birth, with one questioning gender identity. The sample were characterised by the parenting they received in childhood, with highest mean scores on the YPI for emotional inhibition and deprivation. All of the subscales, except one, were equal between parents or higher for the father (see Supplementary Material 2 for mean scores). The overprotection and overindulgence scale was higher for mothers.

Fidelity ratings

A total of 8 fidelity ratings were completed at random time points across the 5 groups. Mean scores for fidelity ratings were 5.4 (between very good and excellent), with a range of 4 on individual items (between adequate and excellent), indicating good treatment fidelity.

Outcome measures

Across all 17 models the overall effect of group was non-significant (all χ²(4) ≤ 5.16, p ≥ 0.27; Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5), indicating no evidence of differences between the five groups; the following sections therefore focus on the overall effect of time and on time-point contrasts relative to baseline.

Carer distress

The overall effect of time was statistically significant for the EDSIS total and all four subscales (time χ²[3] from 15.68 to 47.49, all p < 0.002; Table 2). For the total score, relative to baseline the estimated EDSIS score was significantly lower at end-point (β=−7.703, SE = 1.724, Z = − 4.47, p < 0.001) and at follow-up (β=−9.859, SE = 1.714, Z = − 5.75, p < 0.001), but not at mid-point, suggesting an improvement in caregiver strain over the study period. The random intercept accounted for 72.6% of residual variance (ICC = 0.726). The same pattern of scores significantly lower at end-point and follow-up relative to baseline, but not at mid-point was seen across the social isolation, guilt, nutrition and dysregulated behaviour subscales (Table 2), with the random intercept accounting for 81.8%, 64.2%, 60.9% and 81.1% of residual variance respectively (Fig. 2).

The overall effect of time was statistically significant for the SCORE-15 total and for the Overwhelmed by Difficulties and Disrupted Communication subscales (time χ²[3] = 19.67, 22.22 and 10.81; p < 0.001, p < 0.001 and p = 0.013 respectively), but did not reach significance for the Strengths and Adaptability subscale (χ²(3) = 7.78, p = 0.051; Table 3). A similar pattern of lower scores at end-point and follow-up relative to baseline was observed for the Overwhelmed by Difficulties and Disrupted Communication subscales (Table 3).

Attachment-related anxiety and avoidance

For attachment-related anxiety, the overall effect of time was not statistically significant (χ²(3) = 4.33, p = 0.228; Table 5), with no time point differing significantly from baseline (e.g. end-point β=−0.284, SE = 0.163, Z = − 1.74, p = 0.082). For attachment-related avoidance, the overall effect of time was significant (χ²(3) = 14.08, p = 0.003): relative to baseline, avoidance was significantly lower at mid-point (β=−0.366, SE = 0.126, Z = − 2.91, p = 0.004), end-point (β=−0.461, SE = 0.135, Z = − 3.40, p = 0.001) and follow-up (β=−0.288, SE = 0.139, Z = − 2.08, p = 0.038), suggesting a reduction in attachment-related avoidance over the study period (Fig. 6). The random intercept accounted for 63.5% of residual variance (ICC = 0.635).

Robustness checks

As a robustness check, alternative model specifications including group × time interactions were estimated (Supplementary Material 4), with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons (Supplementary Material 5). Joint (omnibus) tests of the interaction terms were non-significant for 15 of the 17 models (χ²[12] ≤ 19.91, p ≥ 0.069), indicating that change over time was broadly consistent across the five groups. Two exceptions were observed: a significant interaction for ECR-R attachment anxiety (χ²(12) = 27.58, p = 0.006) and a weaker one for the EDSIS nutrition subscale (χ²[12] = 24.20, p = 0.019). In both cases the effect appeared to be driven by a single group (group 1 for ECR-R anxiety; group 2 for EDSIS nutrition), and given the small per-group sample sizes and the number of exploratory tests conducted, these isolated interactions should be interpreted with caution rather than as evidence of systematic between-group differences in response.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to explore the potential of the CAREFREE programme for supporting wellbeing and family functioning for carers of someone with MS-AN in a real-world setting. Overall, the results indicate statistically significant improvements after participation in the programme, continuing at the 3-month follow-up period, across carer distress, EE, family functioning and communication, attachment-related avoidance and mood. Across all of these models, a trend of improvement can be seen during participation in the programme. This suggests progressive therapeutic change across the 12-week programme, and stability of these changes after the end of the intervention. There was a trend for positive change within the secondary outcomes of attachment-related anxiety and avoidance, with only the latter reaching statistical significance. This result is somewhat surprising given that attachment characteristics are known to be relatively resistant to change [72, 73], and a 12-week programme is brief in the context of addressing these.

The intervention showed acceptable retention and completion of data, comparable to other skills-based interventions for carers of adults with AN [74]. There are several key differences that likely impact retention of the CAREFREE programme compared to other carers groups. Examples likely include the specific recruitment for carers of MS-AN, meaning families with complex dynamics were specifically recruited for, and the length and intensity of the intervention being greater than many other available carers groups [42]. The high levels of baseline family difficulties and carer distress may have contributed to challenges engaging in group carer interventions. This is perhaps a reason why several carers who consented to participate did not end up commencing the group programme or disengaged early on. Alike to patients with AN, carers may also require being at a preparation or action stage of change [75] to be able to engage fully in an intervention.

The majority of participants in this study were parents, and less than a fifth were spouses. These preliminary findings suggest the CAREFREE programme may be helpful at supporting the different types of caregiving relationships with AN. This expands on the literature of ST-coaching carer groups in adolescence which specifically focus on parent carers [76]. Approximately three quarters of the carers who participated were female. This mirrors research showing mothers often take on a large proportion of caregiving duties for someone with AN, which can disproportionately affect female carer distress [77]. Whilst this may affect generalisability to male carers, this is likely representative of the caring population and indeed similar to other research on carer interventions [49, 78].

The primary outcome of carer’s distress saw statistically significant improvement in every area measured, including managing nutritional situations, dealing with dysregulated behaviours, caregiving guilt, and social isolation. This aligns with research on other carers groups, demonstrating the group method as an effective intervention for carer distress [79, 80]. The group process itself may normalise carers’ distress and reduce feelings of isolation in their caregiving role. A further qualitative analysis of carers’ experiences of the group was also conducted and will be published separately. In the CAREFREE programme, carers learn about what “lies beneath” AN. Carers’ learn about their own coping styles, and the “modes” that serve functional, protective motives through different AN behaviours for the patient, and rescuing, “fixing,” or avoidant ways of coping for the carer. Managing carer distress is not only important for the carer’s own wellbeing, but central to lead to behavioural change in how they care for someone with AN. When an individual becomes more capable of regulating their own emotions, they also become more open to reacting to others’ emotions with empathic understanding [6], without feeling threatened or vulnerable [81]. In turn, this resulting openness supports change in family functioning and EE [82].

The improved family functioning and communication dynamics in this study can be interpreted in line with research showing the mediating relationship of carers’ own emotional care, with higher EE and worsening ED behaviours in the patient [83]. These interpersonal dynamics, which tend to occur at times of emotional distress, can become reinforced and unintentionally keep the ED entrenched, ultimately hindering recovery. Indeed, parent-child emotional misattunement has been reported in around 70% of mothers of adolescents with AN, and around 50% of fathers [84], with the higher levels in mother-child relationships hypothesised related to over-involvement. Interestingly, a similar pattern was mirrored in our data of the perceived parenting that carers received in their own childhood. Mothers and fathers recalled high levels of emotional inhibition and deprivation, and high levels of maternal overprotection and overindulgence. Often unintentionally, a caregiver’s caring style is influenced by the care that they received in their own childhood, with known learning, hormonal and epigenetic factors influencing intergenerational transmission of attachment styles and trauma [85–87]. The CAREFREE programme’s focus on disentangling interpersonal communication dynamics through experiential techniques may enhance compassionate attunement to carers’ own and the person with MS-AN’s emotions, leading to the positive outcomes in EE and family functioning.

It could be hypothesised that carer interventions which place a strong focus on practical solutions to manage eating behaviours may unintentionally perpetuate unmet needs. Focusing on eating behaviours may contribute to emotional misattunement in the primary attachment relationships of individuals with AN, especially when caregivers struggle with their own insecure attachment style. Indeed, outcomes for Family-Based Treatment (FBT, a child and adolescent evidence-based treatment for AN which gives carers the role of managing the young person’s eating behaviours in the early stages), has poorer outcomes in families with high EE, with a need identified for interventions which specifically address and modify EE for improved outcomes [88, 89]. In clinical settings, families who have completed FBT and experienced poorer outcomes, such as those with high EE, often transition into adult services without having received guidance on more suitable approaches matched to the person with AN’s new developmental stage. Carers may continue the strong focus on practically managing the person’s nutritional intake throughout early adulthood. The CAREFREE programme may better meet the needs of families with high EE and emotional misattunement through the direct focus on familial communication styles, use of interventions to improve emotional attunement and learning positive forms of coping between carer and the person with AN. This idea fits with recent research demonstrating carers of adults with AN find benefit from transitioning from trying to “fix” the ED, to letting go and focusing instead on consistently seeking connection on an emotional level [90]. This necessary work to support carers of adults with MS-AN is complex. Part of the reason for the positive outcomes in the CAREFREE programme may also be due to experienced Clinical Psychologists running the programme and able to manage the complex emotions and process issues that can arise for carers in sessions.

Limitations

This study was originally intended to be a non-concurrent multiple baseline across group case series design, a variation on the non-concurrent multiple-baseline across individuals [91]. This would have been a more robust design to show clear therapeutic change from the intervention. Unfortunately, due to poor participation in weekly measures during the baseline periods in the early part of the study, data was insufficient for this design, and instead a non-concurrent group case series was adopted. It may be that carers did not fully understand the importance of repetitive completion of ratings prior to the intervention. Future studies may benefit from more effort placed on explaining the rationale for this and/or developing better methods for collecting this data so as to enhance engagement and reduce participant fatigue. The case design of this study enabled participants to serve as their own control which helps control for confounding factors that remain stable within a person over time (e.g. genetics, personality). While this study employed a case series rather than an RCT, this design was an appropriate and necessary first step, enabling evaluation of the intervention in a real-world NHS setting and providing preliminary signs of benefit. Future larger scale RCTs are needed to verify these findings.

Due to the challenges in recruiting adults with MS-AN to a study, such as medical or psychological stability to be able to ethically participate in research, it was not a requirement of this study for patients to be involved in the research. Measures were used to explore the impact of the carers’ intervention on the person with MS-AN via the selection of systemic outcome measures, but these nonetheless rely on self-report data from the carer. Quantitative and qualitative data were also collected from a small number of adults with MS-AN, whose carers took part in this study, which will be published separately.

Additionally, the different needs for partners and parents were not assessed directly in this study. Given the groups were mixed with different types of caring relationships, it may mean that the programme has differing trends for improvements for partners and parents. More robust findings would result from the separating spouses and parents into separate groups. However, given this group was designed to be relevant to real-world settings and delivery in NHS services, it may not be practical for clinicians to offer separate groups for partners and parents, and may delay intervention for the relatively few spouses who wanted to participate in the carers group at any one time.

Future directions

Whilst the aim of this study was specifically exploring feasibility of the CAREFREE programme in a real-world NHS setting, future research would benefit comparing the efficacy of the CAREFREE programme to other commonly used models of carers interventions in a RCT. This would enable understanding of the value of the CAREFREE programme versus other programmes for the unique needs of families and carers with MS-AN, and support clinician’s decision making for which carers’ intervention would be best suited for the needs of different carers. A more diverse carer sample would also benefit being recruited given the higher proportion of parents and female carers in this trial. The communication dynamics between parents and partners with their loved one with AN are likely to be different, and a larger spousal sample would better support this comparison as well as assessing efficacy in separate groups run for parents and partners.

The CAREFREE programme was designed specifically to meet the needs of carers with MS-AN, however it is not currently known whether the intervention, or a close adaptation of, would also meet the needs of carers of adults with earlier onset anorexia or other EDs. In line with recent government recommendations in Scotland [92], there is a necessary move for ED services to expand service criteria to all EDs and all levels of severity. Clinically, this may mean that transdiagnostic programmes may be utilised. Future research to explore the efficacy of the programme for carers of transdiagnostic EDs would be beneficial. Additionally, given the potential mediating relationship of self-compassion [83], assessing changes in self-compassion would be a valuable outcome measure. Whilst it was not a requirement in this study for patients to be included in the programme, future research would benefit from a more robust measurement of the impact the CAREFREE programme on patient outcomes with MS-AN. This is a highly under-researched population group due to the stability and level of functioning, however close liaison with the clinical team may support participation without contraindications of research participation.

Conclusions

Partners and family members as carers play a significant role in the long-term support and treatment of those with MS-AN. Findings showed statistically significant improvements in carers’ capacity to manage nutritional situations, deal with dysregulated behaviours, and reduce suffering from caregiving guilt, social isolation, and symptoms of depression and anxiety. Preliminary findings from this study suggest that carer wellbeing and family functioning improved following participation in the CAREFREE programme for carers of MS-AN. Further research is required to explore how the CAREFREE programme compares to other models of intervention, not specifically designed for MS-AN, and whether the programme is suitable for carers of transdiagnostic EDs.
