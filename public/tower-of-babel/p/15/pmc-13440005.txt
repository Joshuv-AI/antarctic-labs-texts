Introduction

Graves’ disease is a disease that causes hyperthyroidism and is often associated with diffuse goiter, Graves’ ophthalmopathy, antithyroid peroxidase antibodies, TSH receptor antibodies, and high serum thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) levels [1]. Hyperthyroidism causes changes in the cardiovascular system that can be explained by the direct effects of thyroid hormones on vascular smooth muscle cells and cardiomyocytes. On the one hand, heart rate, myocardial contractility, preload, blood volume, and tissue thermogenesis increase, while on the other hand, afterload and systemic vascular resistance decrease [2]. In hyperthyroidism, increased sympathetic nerve activity, decreased vagal tone, activation of the angiotensin-aldosterone axis, and decreased nitric oxide (NO) release from endothelial cells cause arterial wall stiffness [3–5].

Nitric oxide (NO), a powerful vasodilator, plays a key role in maintaining vascular homeostasis. Asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), a non-proteinogenic amino acid, is formed through the post-translational methylation of arginine. Its primary function is to act as an endogenous inhibitor of nitric oxide synthase (NOS), the enzyme responsible for generating NO from L-arginine. Elevated ADMA levels have been observed in conditions such as hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and other diseases, and are closely linked to cardiovascular disorders and endothelial dysfunction [6, 7].

Hyperthyroidism affects adipocyte function and lipid metabolism, causing lipolysis to exceed lipogenesis, leading to decreased cholesterol levels and increased plasma free fatty acids [8, 9]. Fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) is a cytoplasmic lipid transporter that regulates lipid transport and metabolism in adipocytes [10, 11]. FABP4 is found in mature adipocytes and macrophages and has a regulatory role in insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism [10, 11]. Increased levels of FABP4 in the circulation have been shown to be associated with obesity, metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, atherosclerosis, left ventricular dysfunction, and heart failure [12–16]. FABP4 levels have been independently associated with PCSK9, which plays a role in cardiovascular diseases and hyperlipidemia by binding to the low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor and affecting the receptor, and it has been shown that basal FABP4 levels independently predict atherosclerosis progression with annual carotid intima-media thickness change [12, 13, 17].

The Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) is a parameter calculated based on plasma triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels and is used to assess the risk of atherosclerosis. It is the molar logarithm value of the ratio of circulating triglyceride concentration to HDL-cholesterol concentration. High AIP has been associated with higher cardiovascular risk. AIP is inversely proportional to LDL particle size. In this context, it has been shown that small dense LDL-C is very vulnerable to oxidative damage and consequently induces atherosclerotic lesions. AIP score between 0.30 − 0.11 is considered low risk, 0.11–0.24 is considered medium risk, and > 0.24 is considered high risk [18–20].

The aim of this exploratory pilot study was to evaluate cardiovascular disease risk in patients diagnosed with Graves’ disease by investigating the relationship between thyroid hormone levels and plasma ADMA, FABP4 levels, AIP, and CRI.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study between January 2023 and January 2024 at the Endocrinology and Metabolism Department of Adana City Training and Research Hospital, following approval from the ethics committee. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study included 30 newly diagnosed patients with Graves’ disease, aged 18–65 years, who were referred to the clinic. The diagnosis was confirmed clinically, biochemically, and through sonographic evaluation. All patients with Graves’ disease were newly diagnosed and evaluated prior to the initiation of any antithyroid treatment. Patients with known cardiac disease, hypertension, diabetes, impaired glucose tolerance, renal or hepatic disease were excluded. As a control group, 30 euthyroid individuals aged 18–65 years, without thyroid disease, cardiac disease, hypertension, diabetes, or impaired glucose tolerance were selected. The control group was selected to be comparable in terms of age and sex distribution.

Demographic data, including age and gender, were recorded, and Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated. Blood samples were collected to measure routine parameters such as Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH), Free Triiodothyronine (fT3), Free Thyroxine (fT4), Thyroid Receptor Antibodies (TRAB), Glucose, Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), Creatinine, Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST), Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT), Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR), C-Reactive Protein (CRP), High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL), Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL), Total Cholesterol, and Triglycerides. In addition, the levels of Asymmetric Dimethylarginine (ADMA) and Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4) were analyzed.

Serum ADMA and FABP4 levels were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits in the ELISA laboratory of the Department of Medical Biochemistry, Mustafa Kemal University, in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions. ADMA levels were quantified using an ELISA kit (Elabscience, Cat. No: E-EL-0023), and FABP4 levels were measured using a dedicated ELISA kit (Elabscience, Cat. No: E-EL-H0012). All samples were analyzed in duplicate to ensure analytical precision. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Epoch, USA). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV) were < 6.5% and < 8.2% for ADMA, and < 5.8% and < 9.1% for FABP4, respectively. ADMA and FABP4 concentrations were expressed in ng/mL. Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast and processed within 30 min of collection. Following centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, serum samples were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. To minimize pre-analytical variability, repeated freeze–thaw cycles were avoided. The Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) was calculated using the molar logarithmic ratio of triglyceride concentration to HDL-cholesterol concentration. The Castelli Risk Index (CRI) was calculated as the ratio of total cholesterol to HDL cholesterol (CRI-I = TC/HDL-C).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA) and MedCalc software for Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve assessments. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), while categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages. The normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The choice between parametric and non-parametric tests was based on data distribution. Variables showing normal distribution were analyzed using parametric tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using non-parametric methods. To compare continuous variables between groups, Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman correlation analysis was used for correlation analyses involving non-normally distributed variables. To reduce the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to correlation analyses. ROC curve analysis was performed to explore the discriminatory performance of the parameters, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for each parameter. The significance of the AUC was tested using the z-test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Internal validation of ROC analyses was performed using bootstrap resampling. A total of 1000 bootstrap samples were generated with replacement to estimate optimism-corrected AUC values and their 95% confidence intervals. The stability of the AUC estimates was assessed based on the distribution of bootstrap-derived statistics. To assess independent associations, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed with AIP as the dependent variable. Independent variables included FABP4 and ADMA, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and glucose levels. Standardized beta coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were reported. No missing data were observed in the study variables; therefore, no imputation methods were applied.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the association of plasma ADMA, FABP4 levels, and AIP with CVD risk in patients with Graves’ disease. Our findings suggest significant associations between these biomarkers and established cardiovascular risk factors. ADMA, a marker of endothelial dysfunction, exhibited strong positive correlations with BMI, inflammatory markers such as CRP, lipid abnormalities (including LDL and total cholesterol), and thyroid hormones like fT4, indicating its potential contribution to increased cardiovascular risk in this patient cohort. Similarly, FABP4, a protein linked to metabolic syndrome and lipid metabolism, was closely related to obesity markers (BMI), impaired glucose regulation, and dyslipidemia, highlighting its significance as a cardiovascular risk biomarker. Additionally, AIP, an indicator of atherogenic dyslipidemia, showed significant associations with triglycerides, glucose levels, and BMI, further supporting its value in cardiovascular risk assessment, particularly in patients with Graves’ disease. These findings indicate that these biomarkers are associated with established cardiovascular risk factors and may reflect underlying metabolic and inflammatory processes in patients with Graves’ disease. However, given the cross-sectional design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than indicative of causality or predictive value.

Graves’ disease is linked to an elevated risk of CVD due to the hypermetabolic state it induces. This condition can cause metabolic disturbances, including dyslipidemia, elevated heart rate, hypertension, and atrial fibrillation, all of which contribute to a higher likelihood of cardiovascular complications. Additionally, excessive thyroid hormones, particularly in hyperthyroid states, can further intensify these issues, making it crucial to closely monitor cardiovascular health in patients with Graves’ disease [21–23]. Wang et al. found that patients with lower but clinically normal fT3 levels had a higher prevalence of atherosclerosis compared to those with mid- and high-normal fT3 levels. High fT4 levels were linked to a lower risk of atherosclerosis. The study highlights the importance of thyroid hormone levels in assessing cardiovascular risk among euthyroid diabetic patients [24]. Suleymanoglu et al. investigated the association between atherogenic indices, including the AIP and CRI, with coronary slow flow (CSF) in a cohort of 130 patients, comparing them to 130 controls with normal coronary flow. The study found that both AIP and CRI were independent predictors of CSF, indicating a potential role of atherogenic dyslipidemia in the development of CSF. Additionally, smoking and male gender were identified as significant risk factors. These results highlight the relevance of lipid-related indices in assessing the risk of coronary microvascular dysfunction [25]. Klomp et al. discussed a 49-year-old female with Graves’ disease who experienced myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries due to coronary artery vasospasm. The vasospasm, affecting both the right coronary artery and left main coronary artery, was confirmed through coronary angiography and was successfully treated with intracoronary nitroglycerine. The absence of atherosclerosis was confirmed through optical coherence tomography. The report emphasizes the need for thyroid function testing in similar cases, especially in young females [26]. Our findings are consistent with previous reports showing associations between lipid-derived indices and cardiovascular risk-related parameters in patients with Graves’ disease. The observed perfect correlation between CRI and AIP is attributable to their mathematical dependence, as both indices are derived from lipid parameters involving HDL and triglyceride or total cholesterol levels. Therefore, this relationship should not be interpreted as an independent biological association, but rather as a reflection of shared calculation components. In addition, the association of thyroid hormone levels, particularly fT3 and fT4, with lipid-related indices further supports the role of metabolic alterations in cardiometabolic risk profiles.

Although many studies have evaluated these biomarkers in metabolic disorders such as diabetes and obesity, evidence specifically focusing on hyperthyroidism and Graves’ disease remains limited. However, hyperthyroidism is characterized by increased metabolic rate, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which may influence cardiometabolic biomarkers in a disease-specific manner. Therefore, extrapolation of findings from other metabolic conditions should be made with caution, as the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms in Graves’ disease may differ.

Our analysis found that ADMA, a marker of endothelial dysfunction, was significantly correlated with several cardiovascular risk factors, including BMI, CRP, LDL, and Total Cholesterol, which are established predictors of cardiovascular disease. These correlations are consistent with the work of Poredos et al., who also reported significant associations between elevated ADMA levels and atherosclerosis progression, highlighting ADMA’s role as a mediator of cardiovascular risk [27]. However, despite these associations, ADMA showed limited discriminatory ability in ROC analysis, and the lack of statistical significance suggests that it should not be considered a reliable diagnostic marker in this context. This contrasts with findings from Ye et al. reported that the prognostic value of ADMA levels in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD). Data from 11 studies with 9,496 CAD patients show that higher ADMA levels are associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). The disparity in our findings may be due to the differences in study populations, as ADMA may play a more prominent role in chronic cardiovascular conditions than in thyroid-specific diseases [28]. However, in multivariable analysis, ADMA was not independently associated with AIP after adjustment for confounders, suggesting that its observed associations may be influenced by underlying metabolic factors.

FABP4 demonstrated strong correlations with BMI, glucose, and triglycerides, similar to its well-documented role in metabolic syndrome and lipid metabolism disorders [17]. In our study, FABP4 was also inversely correlated with HDL, which is consistent with its known role in promoting lipid accumulation and increasing atherogenic risk. Importantly, multivariable linear regression analysis demonstrated that FABP4 remained independently associated with AIP after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and glucose levels, supporting its potential role in cardiometabolic risk pathways. Gormez et al. have similarly reported that elevated FABP4 levels are associated with increased cardiovascular risk in patients with metabolic syndrome and obesity. They stated the relationship between FABP4 expression in different types of adipose tissues (epicardial, pericardial, and subcutaneous) and the extent of coronary atherosclerosis in patients with metabolic syndrome (MS). Results showed that FABP4 expression in epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) was significantly associated with the severity of coronary atherosclerosis and EAT volume. Additionally, certain genetic polymorphisms of the FABP4 gene were linked to increased expression in MS patients, suggesting FABP4’s role in coronary artery disease progression [14]. This finding is in agreement with some studies in the literature which was reported the utility of FABP4 as a predictor of cardiovascular risk in various metabolic disorders [13, 29].

AIP and CRI both emerged as significant predictors of cardiovascular risk, with CRI showing a strong association with BMI, glucose, and triglycerides, which aligns with its role in assessing overall cardiac risk [30, 31]. Our ROC analysis showed that CRI and AIP had a high AUC (0.726 and 0.695, respectively) and were significantly associated with cardiovascular risk. Jouad et al. explored atherogenic indices in patients with thyroid dysfunction, including both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, and their associated CVD risks. The study revealed a marked increase in indices such as the Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP), Castelli Risk Index I (CRI-I), Castelli Risk Index II (CRI-II), and the cholesterol index in both patient groups compared to healthy controls. These results highlight the critical need for monitoring lipid profiles in individuals with thyroid disorders to enable early detection of CVD risk and to promote timely lifestyle interventions [32]. Prasad et al. examined the predictive value of atherogenic indices, including the AIP and CRI, for CVD risk in patients with subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) and overt hypothyroidism (OH). The study revealed significant dyslipidemia and higher atherogenic indices in both the SCH and OH groups compared to euthyroid controls. Of all the indices analyzed, AIP demonstrated the strongest predictive power for assessing CVD risk in these patients [31]. Our findings further corroborate the importance of AIP in assessing cardiovascular risk in patients with Graves’ disease, highlighting the role of dyslipidemia in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis in this population. Overall, these results support the contribution of metabolic and lipid-related parameters to cardiometabolic risk; however, they should be interpreted within an associative rather than causal framework.

Unlike previous research that identified strong associations between ADMA and cardiovascular outcomes, our results indicate that although ADMA correlates with various risk factors, its diagnostic value appears to be less significant when compared to other markers like FABP4, AIP, and CRI. This variation could be attributed to the unique characteristics of the Graves’ disease population studied. Additional research with larger cohorts is needed to better understand ADMA’s role in this context. Overall, while multivariable analysis strengthens the observed associations, the relatively small sample size and potential risk of model overfitting should be considered when interpreting the regression findings. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study limits causal inference. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating, and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, biomarkers such as FABP4, AIP, and CRI were found to be associated with cardiovascular risk-related parameters in patients with Graves’ disease. Although ADMA was correlated with several variables, its diagnostic performance was limited and not statistically significant. Therefore, its clinical utility should be interpreted with caution. Larger studies are required to further investigate the role of ADMA and other emerging biomarkers in predicting cardiovascular outcomes in thyroid disorders. These findings align with existing evidence on lipid metabolism and cardiovascular risk but should be interpreted with caution given the study limitations. This study should be considered an exploratory pilot investigation, and larger multicenter longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as a single-center study, the results may reflect institutional biases and may not be fully generalizable to other populations or clinical settings. Secondly, the sample size (30 patients and 30 controls) was relatively small, which could limit the statistical power and introduce type II errors. Thirdly, the lack of longitudinal follow-up restricts the ability to assess long-term cardiovascular outcomes and causality. The cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish causal or temporal relationships between the investigated biomarkers and cardiovascular risk. A formal sample size calculation and power analysis were not performed prior to the study, which may limit the statistical power of the analyses. Furthermore, although multivariable regression analysis was performed, the relatively small sample size in relation to the number of included covariates may increase the risk of model overfitting and unstable regression estimates. Therefore, the regression findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. Information on smoking status, alcohol consumption, dietary habits, blood pressure levels, duration and severity of hyperthyroidism, and detailed medication use (e.g., beta-blockers) and menopausal status was not systematically recorded. Additionally, while significant correlations were observed, the cross-sectional design precludes drawing definitive conclusions about the predictive value of ADMA, FABP4, and AIP in cardiovascular risk. Larger, multicenter studies with extended follow-up periods are required to confirm and expand upon these findings.
