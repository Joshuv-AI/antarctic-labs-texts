Ecological Resonance Is Reflected in Human Brain Activity
Authors: Vicente Raja, Klaus Gramann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe designed an object interception task using virtual reality and mobile brain/body imaging to test two core hypotheses of ecological psychology and radical embodied cognitive (neuro)science: the ecological resonance hypothesis and the information‐based control laws hypothesis. These two hypotheses define an alternative explanatory strategy that aims to understand the organization and control of behavior without appealing to internal models or representations of any kind. The ecological resonance hypothesis was evaluated in relation to the ecological variable known as tau (τ) or time‐to‐contact (TTC). Concurrently, the hypothesis of information‐based control laws was investigated by examining the τ‐coupling control law. The results of the study show ecological resonance to τ along with τ‐coupling between stimulation, behavior, and neural activity, therefore offering support to both hypotheses.

Introduction

Understanding the organization and control of behavior is one of the core explanatory aims of the cognitive sciences. What is the source of the organized set of movements that constitute behavior? How are organisms with different anatomical and physiological endowments able to generate and control such organization? And what is the role of perception in that process? Mainstream contemporary cognitive science, including cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience, favors a strategy that postulates some form of internal model as the source of behavioral organization and control (e.g., Welniarz et al. 2021; Wolpert et al. 2003). This strategy has been productive, but it faces several limitations. An important limitation has to do with the lack of contact with both the real body of the organism and the real environment. Internal models represent the environment, but they are not the environment (Raja 2022). Similarly, internal models represent and command the body of the organism, but they are not bodies of organisms (e.g., Jordan and Wolpert 1999). These two aspects of the internal model strategy lead to the issues of perceptual grounding and motor organization (for contemporary discussions, see Latash 2025; Reschechtko and Pruszynski 2025). Conceptually speaking, it is difficult to provide an account of perceptual representations—that is, internal perceptual models—and their accurate depiction of the real environment without relying on some form of pre‐established organization (Bickhard and Terveen 1995; Raja 2022 ; Warren 2021). The same need for some form of pre‐established organization goes for internal models of behavioral control (Kelso 1995; Kugler and Turvey 1987; Turvey 2019; Warren 2006). Both issues thus seem to reframe the problem of explaining the organization and control of behavior as one of characterizing prior organization and knowledge rather than explaining the phenomenon of interest. Some researchers see this as an explanatory regress (e.g., Warren 2006) and try to find alternative explanatory strategies.

Ecological psychology, the dynamical hypothesis in cognitive science, enactivism, and radical embodied cognitive (neuro)science are the main sources of those alternative explanatory strategies (Anderson 2014; Chemero 2009; Gibson 1966, 1979; Pessoa 2022; Segundo‐Ortin and Raja 2024; Varela and Thompson 2001). Concretely, ecological psychology and, by extension, radical embodied cognitive (neuro)science rest upon two fundamental motifs or ideas (Raja 2024) regarding perception and the control of action. The first motif pertains to ecological information, proposing that each perceivable property of the environment, no matter how subtle, is specified by a corresponding higher order variable of information, regardless of its complexity (Warren 2021). The term “ecological information” refers to those high‐order patterns of stimulation flows that constitute perceptual information (Fajen 2021; Segundo‐Ortin and Raja 2024; Warren 2006, 2021). These patterns are multiple, from the horizon ratio of a given figure (Sedgwick 2021) and the time to contact (TTC) known as tau (τ; Lee 2009) to the centrifugal expansion of the optical flow in forward locomotion (Warren 1998) and the infinitesimal accretion/deletion of textures and non‐diffeomorphic patterns in object segmentation (Tsao and Tsao 2022). Ecological psychologists use ecological information in their work because it is readily available in stimulation and informative of behaviorally relevant environmental events. For instance, τ or TTC is a property of stimulation flows that informs about the time it will take for a gap in the environment to be completely closed. Thus, it can be used to time behavior with respect to that class of events. As τ or TTC is a property of stimulation flows, it is directly available for the perceiving organism, which is relieved from the necessity of representing it. In this sense, τ or TTC, as well as other variables of ecological information, provide an alternative to the internal model strategy to account for the role of perception in behavioral organization and control. In other words, τ allows for motor control without internal models.

Along with the motif of ecological information, ecological psychologists and radical embodied cognitive (neuro)scientists propose the self‐organization of behavioral control in the spirit of James Gibson's famous motto “behavior is regular without being regulated” (1979, 215). This second motif states that motor control consists in the establishing of brain–body–environment coordinative structures (or synergies) constrained by ecological information (Kelso 1995; Turvey 2019). As in the previous case, this approach spares the organism, and more concretely the brain, from constructing an internal model of the body–environment system because the explanatory strategy already includes both the real body and the real environment in it. The two proposed motifs can be summarized as follows: Behavior is controlled with respect to perceptual information available in the flow of stimulation, where perceptual (ecological) information is usually described in terms of high‐order patterns of that flow. This is the information‐based control laws hypothesis (Kelso 1994, 1995; Warren 2006; Wilson and Bingham 2008).

The information‐based control laws hypothesis entails the search for control laws of behavior—that is, regularities that prescribe the temporal evolution of behavioral self‐organization—that include ecological information. Scientists have explored this hypothesis for decades now, and a good number of these control laws have been proposed in the literature (for a review, see Segundo‐Ortin and Raja 2024; Blau and Wagman 2023). An example of an information‐based control law is to be found in the context of τ‐coupling (Lee 1998, 2009). τ‐coupling is a description of behavioral dynamics (i.e., the way a behavior evolves in time; see Warren 2006) that entails a control law for timing behavior with respect to a gap‐closing event (e.g., with respect to an object approaching a particular area of the environment). Such control law prescribes the way organisms generate forces in order to match the τ of their own movement to the τ of that event to produce the τ‐coupling behavior. τ‐coupling has been tested in a variety of experimental setups, including interception tasks—that is, tasks in which participants are asked to catch an (often moving) object in a particular area of their immediate environment (for a review, see Lee 2009; for particular examples, see Craig and Lee 1999; Craig et al. 2000; Craig et al. 2005; Schögler et al. 2008)—and several control laws associated with it have been proposed in the literature (e.g., Lee 2009; for a different kind of control law related to τ and its first derivate, see Foo et al. 2000). In general, the presence of τ‐coupling suggests the presence of an information‐based control law related to τ.

The just mentioned studies are, however, strictly behavioral and an outstanding question remains open: How are organisms sensitive to a variable of ecological information such as τ? Using the ecological psychology jargon with regard to the nervous system, the question can be reframed as: How is the nervous system able to resonate/to be tuned to ecological information? In the ecological context, “resonance” or “tuning” must be understood in the typical neurophysiology way; that is, in the same way as when it is said that a single neuron or cell is tuned to a particular aspect of stimulation. In neurophysiology, when the claim is that a single neuron is tuned to this or that aspect of stimulation, we expect to find such tuning process reflected in the activity of the neuron (e.g., in the spiking activity). Similarly, the ecological resonance hypothesis (Raja 2018, 2021, 2024) states that, if a variable of ecological information is constraining the behavior of an organism and, therefore, the organism is sensitive to it, we must find signatures of such sensitivity in the systemic neural activity.

Despite its theoretical developments (e.g., Raja 2018, 2021), the ecological resonance hypothesis has been scarcely addressed in the neuroscience literature. In the particular case of τ, there are only a handful of studies that aim to find its signatures in neural activity (e.g., Field and Wann 2005; Lee et al. 2001; Merchant et al. 2004; Port et al. 2001; Tan et al. 2009; Wang and Frost 1992; van der Weel and van der Meer 2009), and only one of them (van der Weel et al. 2022) explicitly tackles τ‐coupling as the behavioral dynamics that entail an information‐based control law related to τ. From the point of view of the ecological resonance hypothesis, these studies suffer from a couple of limitations. First, many of them rely on single‐neuron or single‐region recordings, which are not compatible with the more systemic understanding of tuning ensued by ecological resonance. Second, those studies based on EEG recordings are focused on classic event‐related potentials (ERPs), such as N200 and P300, that occur relatively early in the time period of interest and are bound to a clearly defined event onset. While it is in principle possible to be tuned to τ in a discrete fashion (see Lee et al. 1983), this strategy misses the possibility of addressing the ongoing online control of movement in terms of continuous τ‐coupling dynamics (see Zhao and Warren 2015). Additionally, as these studies focus on the EEG time domain, they do not consider the information available in the EEG time–frequency domain.

The current study comes to fill these gaps by combining the information‐based control laws hypothesis and the ecological resonance hypothesis in one single experimental paradigm that aims to serve as a standard to explore alternatives to internal model‐based explanations of motor control. Building upon behavioral evidence for τ‐coupling in interception tasks (e.g., Bootsma 1988; Jacobs and Michaels 2006; Port et al. 1997; Smith et al. 2014), we adapted the van der Weel et al. (2022) experimental setup to a virtual reality environment and used mobile brain/body imaging (MoBI; Makeig et al. 2009) to explore the coupled behavioral and neural dynamics of the participants in the study (Gramann et al. 2014; Jungnickel et al. 2019). Our main aim was to find neurophysiological signatures of the perception of ecological information and its role in motor execution dynamics. To do so, we first investigated the perceiving of τ at the neural scale. We focused on the activity of an electrode located in the occipital lobe with special emphasis in the comparison between its τ‐value and the τ‐value of the to‐be‐intercepted object (i.e., the stimulus). Afterwards, we analyzed the neural signatures of τ‐coupling in the execution of the interception task. To do so, we focused on the activity of an electrode located in the central‐parietal lobe with special emphasis in the comparison between its τ‐value and the τ‐value of the interceptive movement (i.e., the action). By following this strategy, we tested both the perception of τ in terms of ecological resonance as well as the role of τ‐coupling as entailing an information‐based control law, respectively. For the first time in the literature, we targeted these two hypotheses in both the time and the time–frequency domains in one single study, therefore extending on classic ERP analyses by analyzing the EEG data in a more continuous fashion over the entire trial length.

Methods

Participants

Seventeen right‐handed volunteers, nine men and eight women, ranging in age from 22 to 65 years (mean 32.2, SD 11.9), participated in the experiment. The participants had no known prior history of pathology in the upper limbs nor neurological disorders. All participants gave their informed consent to participate in the experiment and were compensated for their time. This study was conducted at the Technical University of Berlin (TUB, Berlin, Germany) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Western University (London, Ontario, Canada).

Procedure

In the experimental task, participants used their right hand to intercept a moving object within a defined goal area (see Figure 1a). A virtual environment with a table, the goal area on it, and a moving object were used to run the experiment. The virtual environment was developed in unity. Participants were comfortable sitting in front of a real table while wearing a VR headset, a mobile EEG cap, and a motion capture tracker on the right hand.

(a) A consented picture of one of the participants of the experiment. (b) The structure of each trial. This same structure was shared across blocks and conditions. (c) An example of what participants saw in the virtual environment at three different moments in time within a trial: the beginning of the trial (left panel), the middle of the trial (central panel), and the interception moment (right panel).

The object to be intercepted was a white cube that moved on a straight line from the left or the right side of the participant and toward a goal area (a purple dot) at three different constant velocities: fast velocity (0.6 m/s), medium velocity (0.3 m/s), and slow velocity (0.15 m/s). The distance between the white cube and goal area was of 50 cm at the onset of each trial. Participants were asked to intercept the white cube when it was fully within the purple dot or, at least, when it was touching the purple dot (Figure 1b). They could see a virtual version of their hand in the virtual environment (Figure 1c). The distance between the virtual hand and the goal area was of 25 cm at the onset of each trial.

The experiment consisted of one training block and three experimental blocks. During the training block, participants got used to the task by catching 15 objects at different velocities. After the training block, participants engaged in three experimental blocks of 100 trials each. Each block belonged to one condition: fast velocity, medium velocity, and slow velocity. The order of the conditions was randomly selected. Overall, each participant completed 15 training trials and 300 experimental trials.

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

We recorded participants' brain activity using 64 gel‐based electrodes (AntNeuro Waveguard EEG Cap) located according to the 10% system (Chatrian et al. 1985). The size of the EEG cap was selected to comfortably fit the head of each participant, and gel was used in order to keep the impedance of all electrodes under 10 Ω. The EEG data were recorded using the eego sports system (ANT Neuro) at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. A DC recording mode was employed, with the reference electrode positioned at the CPz location. The head and hand movements of each participant were recorded at a frequency of 90 Hz during the complete duration of the experimental session by using the native tracking system of the virtual reality device (HTC VIVE Pro 2) that registered the headset position and a hand position tracker. The headset of the virtual reality device was used for eye tracking as well. The elements of the virtual environment, like the positions of the objects to be intercepted and the position of the goal, were constantly tracked as well. We collected multimodal datasets from 17 participants including the time series of 64 EEG channels, head movement tracking, hand movement tracking, eye tracking, and object and goal tracking. All time series were synchronized using Lab Streaming Layer (LSL; Github, version https://github.com/sccn/labstreaminglayer; accessed on 5 April 2022).

For the current study, only the EEG and the hand movement time series were analyzed. One participant was excluded from the analysis because part of the data were lost, and other part of data were corrupted. The datasets of the other 16 participants were automatically preprocessed using the BeMoBIL preprocessing pipeline for mobile EEG data (Klug et al. 2022). EEG data were low‐pass‐filtered to 125 Hz and resampled to 250 Hz using a filter‐based method, and motion data were interpolated to the same timestamps. Frequency‐specific noise was removed using the EEGLab plugin Zapline‐plus (Klug and Kloosterman 2022). Subsequently, noisy channels were detected if their correlation with neighboring channels was lower than 0.85 for more than 50% of the recording duration and interpolated using a random sample consensus (RANSAC) sampling of surrounding channels. On average, 13% of the channels were removed and subsequently interpolated (mean 8.6, SD 2.7) using spherical spline interpolation. Finally, an adaptive mixture independent component analysis (AMICA; Palmer et al. 2011) with a 1.25 Hz cutoff filter was performed on the rank‐reduced data. As recommended by Klug et al. (2022), samples with a log‐likelihood estimation that is 3 SDs below the average were removed in 10 iterations ensuing the algorithmic rejection of those samples with suboptimal model fit. The resulting independent components (ICs) were fitted using equivalent dipole models and subsequently automatically labeled using ICLabel (Pion‐Tonachini et al. 2019) to reject ICs reflecting eye movements.

After data preprocessing, the time series of all modalities were epoched and aligned with respect to the stimulus movement onset event (0 s). The epochs lasted 1.25 s for the fast velocity condition, 2 s for the medium velocity condition, and 5 s for the slow velocity condition. The three conditions included 0.5 s of baseline activity before the stimulus movement onset. These epochs were visually inspected, and some of them with possible remaining artifacts were further removed based on the visual features of the most common artifacts. On average, 14.2 epochs (SD 12.1) were removed out of the 300 epochs each participant completed for the study. 95.3% of the recorded epochs was used in the data analysis.

A dynamic time warping (DTW) analysis was conducted between the grand average of hand movement and the grand average for each electrode and condition. DTW calculates the similarity between two time series that are not perfectly aligned in time. Thus, DTW allows for finding similar patterns within time series along with the temporal relationship between them (e.g., in our study, it can find whether patterns of movement temporally precede EEG activity such that they could be causing an artifact). For each time series of the EEG activity, we selected the ~800 ms before interception and ran a global, symmetric DTW algorithm comparing them with the ~800 ms before interception of the different dimensions of hand movement (displacement, velocity, and acceleration). This temporal selection is aligned with that of the Data Analysis (see next section). In order to make all the time series as similar as possible for the DTW analysis, we normalized them and set all their minima to 0, therefore making data points in all of them always positive and avoiding dissimilarities in terms of units, ranges, and trends present in the raw data.

Data Analysis

A major part of the analysis conducted in this study is based on tau (τ) or TTC (Lee 2009). Classically understood, τ is an optical variable defined as the inverse of the relative rate of change of the subtended solid angle of an enclosed contour or an aperture in the visual environment of a perceiving organism. Under this definition, τ is related to the closing of a gap in the environment and, more concretely, to the time it will take for that gap to be completely closed.

In a typical example, when an object approaches a point of observation from a distance Z at a velocity Z‐dot, TTC—that is, time to contact or the time it will take for the object to reach the position of the point of observation—is defined as Z/Z‐dot. Given this, it can be mathematically proved that the ratio between the solid angle φ at the point of observation and its rate of change φ‐dot is equivalent to Z/Z‐dot. Tau (τ) is then defined as (1) τ=φ/φ˙ where τ is tau, φ is a solid angle from a given point of observation, and φ‐dot is its rate of change. τ can be used to control behavior (as an aspect of a control law; see Warren 2006) in order to match a behavioral strategy named tau coupling or τ‐coupling (Lee 1998). τ‐coupling entails the timing of movements with respect to gap‐closing environmental events by maintaining a tight proportionality between the tau value of the movement (τM) and the tau value of the environmental event (τE). Formally, τ‐coupling is defined as (2) τM=kτE where τ is tau and the parameter k describes the quality of the contact that differs depending on the intended action. For instance, a bird landing on a tree branch wants a soft contact, while a baseballer hitting a ball wants a hard contact. 1

Imagine, for instance, an object (a ball) is approaching a goal area (a dartboard). From the point of observation of a perceiver, the ball‐dartboard (BD) gap, which is orthogonal to the perceiver's point of observation, is closing at a rate τBD. Still, there is a solid angle subtended at the perceiver's point of observation for the BD gap, and therefore, τBD is available in the optical flow. Suppose now that the perceiver wants to use a limb (a hand H) to catch the ball when it reaches the dartboard. The strategy based on τ‐coupling consists in moving the limb to close the hand‐dartboard (HD) gap at a rate τHD, such that τBD = kτHD. Notice that τHD is not an optical variable, but HD is a gap that can be closed, and therefore, a τ for it can be calculated. In this sense, τ is a measurement that can be generalized to any closing gap (see Lee 2009).

In the current study, the abstract situation just illustrated is reproduced and a white cube takes the place of the ball, a purple dot takes the place of the dartboard, and a virtual hand is the hand of the perceiver. We used Equations (1) and (2) to study the τ‐values and the τ‐coupling of all the relevant modalities of our study: the movement of the object (white cube) in the virtual environment, the movement of the hand of the participants, and the brain activity at selected electrodes both in the time domain and the time–frequency domain. Except when noted otherwise, we used custom scripts and relevant packages in the R Studio environment (version 2024.04.2.764; Posit Team 2024) for data analysis.

For the time domain analysis, we first calculated the average interception time for each experimental condition—that is, the average time in which participants intercepted the moving white cube within the goal region. These average times were 881.6 ms in the fast velocity condition, 1518.8 ms in the medium velocity condition, and 4113.1 ms in the slow velocity condition. For each condition, we considered these average values as the moments in which the interception activity ended. Moreover, we took the average interception time in the fast velocity condition, 881.6 ms, to be the relevant window of time for the interception task, and we selected the last ~800 ms before interception in each condition for the τ analysis. The ~800 ms windows included most of (if not all) the relevant stimulus, movement, and EEG activity for the three conditions. With these constraints in place, we calculated the τ‐value time series of the closing gap between the moving white cube and the goal area. For each experimental condition, we gathered the time series of the white cube's position using the internal tracking system of the virtual environment and numerically differentiated these time series to calculate the white cube's velocity. After doing this, we applied Equation (1) to the resulting pair of time series for each condition to calculate the τ‐value time series. We called the three resulting τ‐value time series Stimulus Tau. Second, and again for each experimental condition, we calculated the τ‐value time series of the closing gap between the participant's moving hand and the goal area. Both the hand's position and velocity were computed from the motion capture data. We applied Equation (1) to these data in order to calculate the τ‐value time series. We called the three resulting τ‐value time series Movement Tau. In the case of EEG activity, we selected the electrodes Oz and Cz. Following previous literature, we made this selection as we could reasonably hypothesize that the two electrodes, respectively, reflect an important part of perceptual and motor activity (see, e.g., Amengual et al. 2014; Ofner et al. 2019; Schwarz et al. 2020; van der Weel et al. 2022). We calculated the grand average for each electrode in the three experimental conditions. This process resulted in six time series, one per electrode and experimental condition. We low‐passed filtered the EEG time series at the low‐delta/sub‐delta range (< 1 Hz) in order to get smooth slopes. This facilitates numerical differentiation while avoiding non‐physiological signal jumps due to noise or zero‐crossing in the signal (see Cohen 2014). We then numerically differentiated each of the EEG time series and used Equation (1) to calculate their respective τ‐value time series. For the non‐physiological jumps that remained after the calculation of the τ‐value time series, we linearly interpolated the time series, as it was the less aggressive strategy to deal with those jumps. After we finished this process, we called the three resulting τ‐value time series per electrode: the τ‐value time series of the visually evoked potential (VEP) at the Oz electrode (Oz VEP Tau) and the τ‐value time series of the motor‐related cortical potential (MRCP) at the Cz electrode (Cz MRCP Tau), respectively.

Having calculated Stimulus Tau, Movement Tau, Oz VEP Tau, and Cz MRCP Tau for each of the three experimental conditions, the next step was to explore the τ‐couplings between them. To do so, we used the τ‐coupling algorithm from the ecological psychology literature (see Lee 1998, 2009; van der Weel et al. 2022). The τ‐coupling algorithm calculates the correlation (r) of two τ‐value time series iteratively, removing one data point from each time series until the correlation value reaches a threshold (r > 0.95). Then, it calculates the percentage of data points that remain in the τ‐value time series that meet the threshold requirement. That percentage of the time series is the one said to be τ‐coupled. We applied this algorithm to the selected ~800 ms time windows from all possible τ‐coupling pairs in our dataset: Movement Tau—Stimulus Tau; Oz VEP Tau—Stimulus Tau; Cz MRCP Tau—Stimulus Tau; Oz VEP Tau—Movement Tau; Cz MRCP Tau—Movement Tau; Cz MRCP Tau—Oz VEP Tau.

For the time–frequency domain analysis, we used EEGLab (Delorme and Makeig 2004) to calculate the power values in both electrodes for the three conditions including the theta (4–7 Hz), alpha/mu (8–13 Hz), beta (15–30 Hz), and low‐gamma (30–40 Hz) bands. Our aim was to quantify the power dynamics of the different EEG bands and, more importantly, to test whether those dynamics were τ‐coupled. In order to do so, we used the event‐related de‐synchronization/synchronization algorithm (ERD/S; see Bodda and Diwakar 2022; Pfurtscheller 2001; Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva 1999). The logic of ERD/S is that, in terms of neural activity, the power of a particular band is related to the number of neurons that are sycnchronically active: the more synchronous activity, the more power. When neurons of a particular region are not perturbed by any external input (stimulus) or activity, they tend to synchronize (i.e., their endogenous tendency is toward synchronization). Thus, synchronization, and therefore high power, is a mark of little exogenous activity, little perturbation, waiting, and/or idling (see Pfurtscheller 2001; Scanlon et al. 2022). On the contrary, when neurons of a particular region are perturbed by an external input (stimulus) or activity, they tend to de‐synchronize. Thus, desynchronization, and therefore low power, is a mark of high exogenous activity and perturbation (see Pfurtscheller 2001; Scanlon et al. 2022). ERD/S is an index of synchronization/de‐synchronization dynamics and provides the amount of change in synchronization (%) with respect to a baseline.

To calculate ERD/S, we first filtered three frequency bands (alpha, beta, and mu—mu is the same as alpha but in the central electrodes) in the two electrodes and for each of the three conditions. ERD/S is formally defined as (3) ERD/S%=A−R/R×100 where A is instantaneous power and R is baseline power. Following Pfurtscheller (2001), we defined R as the average power of the 300 ms before the stimulus movement onset. After calculating R, we run a sliding window of ~200 ms (50% overlap) through the power time series of each band and their average power was calculated. This provided us with 10–50 power data points depending on the condition. These data points were used to calculate the ERD/S time series by applying Equation (3).

ERD/S is a relative measurement (a percentage) and τ‐value time series are as well a relative measurement in themselves. For this reason, instead of calculating the τ‐value time series of the ERD/S time series to then apply the same τ‐coupling iterative algorithm used in the time domain, we decided to just evaluate to what extent the ERD/S time series were correlated (r)/coupled to Stimulus Tau. This does not speak to canonical τ‐coupling, but it speaks to the influence of τ in ERD/S dynamics. To perform this calculation, we needed to interpolate the ERD/S time series in order to get the same number of data points as the Stimulus Tau time series.

Finally, we wanted to test the functional connectivity between the regions involved in the task—that is, between the visual and the motor cortex. We used the instantaneous frequency (IF) correlation of the two electrodes selected for the study, Oz and Cz, to do so (see Cohen 2014). In calculating IF, we followed the algorithm proposed by Cohen (2014) that includes filtering the data by bands, computing the analytic signal of the filtered data via the Hilbert transform, extracting the phase angle time series (ϕ), differentiating it (ϕ−dot), and converting the units into Hz by the formula ϕ−dot/2π times the sample rate of the dataset. After doing this, Cohen (2014) recommends applying a median filter with a ~400 ms sliding window (w/overlap) to attenuate the non‐physiological jumps in the values of ϕ‐dot due to the process of numerical differentiation. For the IF correlation, we also followed Cohen (2014) and calculated the (Spearman) correlation.

We also run a Granger causality analysis to explore lead–lag dynamics in the IF time series. Granger Causality is informative of whether one time series helps predict another one and, in this sense, it is a good proxy for lead–lag dynamics when applied to time series in a windowed fashion. We applied the Granger Causality test to the alpha/mu IF time series in each of the three conditions. We used a window of size length equal to half of each time series and slid it with 50% of overlap. This entails each time series had three windows of analysis (earlier, middle, and later in the epoch). The same procedure was repeated for the time series of the ~800 ms before interception time windows we used in the time domain analysis.

Statistical Analysis

To test the significance of our results, we bootstrapped a permutation of shuffled data with repetition (1000 times) and then applied the relevant algorithm—τ‐coupling, ERD/S‐τ correlation, or IF correlation—to the resulting 1000 time series for each measurement in order to create a distribution of by‐chance coupling/correlation values. We then checked where the values of the original data fell with respect to those distributions. The position of the original data in the by‐chance distributions of coupling/correlation values indicated whether our results are significant: They are significant if they fall on the extremes of the distribution. We chose this approach to significance (inspired by studies of representational similarity; see Wilford et al. 2022) because, after applying the algorithms for data analysis, we ended up with just a few coupling/correlation values. Having so little data, the bootstrapping strategy is more robust than the classic tests to check significance.

Later in the epoch, the main ERP features are the visual evoked potential (VEP) of the Oz electrode, a slow‐slope positive increase in the Oz activity that peaks right after the average interception time (solid black lines) in all experimental conditions, and the motor‐related cortical potential (MRCP) of the Cz electrode, a slow‐slope negative increase in the Cz activity that peaks a few hundred milliseconds before the average interception time in all experimental conditions. Both the Oz VEP and the Cz MRCP begin > 500 ms before movement onset (dotted black lines). The Cz MRCP peaks around ~300 ms after movement onset, and therefore, between 100 and 300 ms before interception depending on the experimental condition—a schema of this activity can be seen in Figure 2e.

A DTW analysis accounts for similarity relations between the Cz MRCP and hand movement (Figure 2f). The average DTW distance between the normalized, re‐scaled EEG activity (Cz MRCP) and the normalized, re‐scaled hand movement displacement is large (mean 89.5, SD 57.8). The vertical blue line in Figure 2f indicates that the activity of the Cz MRCP initiates before the activity of hand movement.

Time Domain Analysis

We calculated the τ‐value (TTC) of all relevant modalities (Stimulus Tau, Movement Tau, Oz VEP Tau, and Cz MRCP Tau) for a window of ~800 ms before the interception time in each experimental condition. Figure 3a shows a general schema of the way the τ‐value is calculated for the defined gap in each modality. Figure 3b shows the τ‐value for all those modalities. The τ‐value of all modalities converges toward Stimulus Tau in all conditions.

(a) Schema of the τ‐value calculation. For any defined gap (blue line; e.g., the gap between the origin and the goal position of the hand or the gap between the baseline and the peak of a given electrode's activity), τ‐value is calculated by dividing the instantaneous position of the gap by the instantaneous velocity (orange line) at which that gap is closing. At any given moment of time, the τ‐value of the gap (purple line) informs about the time (in seconds) it will take for that gap to be closed. Therefore, the final τ‐value of the defined gap will be τ = 0 when it is closed. (b) The τ‐value of all modalities plotted against time (~800 ms before the interception time) for the fast velocity condition (left panel), the medium velocity condition (the middle panel), and the slow velocity condition (right panel). More details in the main text. (c) Percentage of τ‐coupling between all modalities across all experimental conditions. More details in the main text. (d) Significance of the τ‐coupling percentages. More details in the main text.

In the three panels of Figure 3b, Stimulus Tau is represented by a black straight dashed line. The straight line indicates the velocity of the stimulus is constant. The convergence of the other modalities toward Stimulus Tau is an indicative τ‐coupling. The nature of this coupling depends on (i) the slope of the τ‐value change and (ii) the location of τ‐values in the graph. Similar slope in different modalities entails the same rate of τ‐value changes. A similar location of τ‐values indicates that different modalities have similar τ‐values at that moment in time. When the τ‐value of one modality falls right over the black straight dashed line, that modality's τ‐value is perfectly τ‐coupled to Stimulus Tau. If τ‐values of different modalities share slope with Stimulus Tau but remain on the white area above the black straight dashed line, those τ‐values are τ‐coupled to Stimulus Tau, but the former values are slower. On the contrary, if τ‐values share slope with Stimulus Tau but remain on the gray area below the black straight dashed line, those τ‐values are τ‐coupled to Stimulus Tau, but the former values are faster. Movement Tau (blue line) perfectly converges to Stimulus Tau from several hundreds of milliseconds before interception in all three experimental conditions. Oz VEP Tau (red line) and Cz MRCP Tau (orange line) enter the gray area in both the fast and the medium velocity conditions. In the case of the slow velocity condition, Cz MRCP Tau behaves as in the other two conditions, but Oz VEP Tau was interpolated due to a non‐physiological jump in τ‐value, the product of the method of numerical differentiation (see Cohen 2014) and shows a different shape.

The percentage of τ‐coupling between all modalities and across all experimental conditions was also calculated and is shown in Figure 3c. On average, all pairs are over 25% τ‐coupled, and most of them are over 50% τ‐coupled. The best τ‐coupling on average (100%) is the one between Cz MRCP Tau and Movement Tau. The second best τ‐coupling on average (~75%) is the one between Oz VEP Tau and Movement Tau. Oz VEP Tau and Stimulus Tau are the fourth best τ‐coupling (> 60%), while the weakest τ‐coupling is the one between Cz MRCP Tau and Stimulus Tau. Figure 3d shows the significance of the average τ‐coupling percentages. The six percentages from the original data are the six most extreme values on the upper tail of the shuffled data distribution (purple density). This entails that they are larger than 99.99% of the values in that distribution (p < 0.001).

Another observed pattern when visually inspecting the IF time series is a lead–lag relationship between the Oz alpha band and the Cz mu band. Concretely, the Oz alpha band leads the beginning of the epoch and the Cz mu band leads at the end of the epoch. A Granger causality analysis was used to test this observation both in the case of the full time series and in the case of the ~800 ms before interception time series for the three experimental conditions. The analysis was applied to a window of size equal to half of each time series. This window was slid through the whole time series with a 50% of overlap, resulting in three time windows per case and condition: early window, middle window, and last window. The left panel of Figure 5d shows a more common leading role of the Oz alpha band (alpha‐mu direction) at the beginning of the time series and a more common leading role of the Cz mu band (mu‐alpha direction) at the end of the time series in the case of the full time series (green color marks significance and black dots show the significance level). The right panel of Figure 5d shows the leading role of the Cz mu band (mu‐alpha direction) increases for all windows and all experimental conditions in the case of the ~800 ms before interception. Similarly, the leading role of the Oz alpha band (alpha‐mu direction) slightly decreases all conditions.

Discussion

These results are compatible with both the hypothesis of information‐based control laws and the hypothesis of ecological resonance. As two main aspects of the perception–action loop, the combination of these two hypotheses constitutes the core both of the ecological approach to motor control and of ecological/radical embodied neuroscience (Chemero 2009; Turvey 2019; Raja and Anderson 2019; Warren 2006). Thus, these results support the possibility of an alternative approach to perceptually guided action that avoids postulating internal models as central controllers of behavior (Latash 2025; Raja 2021, 2024).

The data show the expected patterns of stimulus' displacement and velocity for the three conditions as well as the typical behavioral patterns of hand displacement and velocity in interception tasks. The behavioral results are further corroborated by the neural dynamics that demonstrate time as well as time–frequency domain features to reflect τ‐coupling with the τ‐value of the stimulus as well as the hand movement. Regarding the time domain, our focuses were the visual (VEP) and motor‐related (MRCP) components of the ERP analyzed at Oz and Cz, respectively.

The MRCP is a complex EEG activity in which several components can be identified. Before the movement onset—defined as the peaking slope of the first derivative of acceleration (for an alternative but similar strategy to calculate movement onset from movement data, see Amengual et al. 2014)—the classic readiness potential (aka Bereitschaftspotential or BP; see Deecke et al. 1969; Kobler et al. 2020; Ofner et al., 2019; Olsen et al. 2021; Shibasaki and Hallett 2006; Schwarz et al. 2020), a delta/sub‐delta negative potential associated with upper limb movement planning and execution usually found in central electrodes is observed. The BP is often associated with a contingent negative variation (CNV; see, e.g., Rektor et al. 2001; Shakeel et al. 2015) as both share similar activation patterns. For the three conditions, the BP/CNV activity begins well before movement onset. This pattern is shared by the Oz VEP. After movement onset, we observe a movement monitoring potential (MMP; Shakeel et al. 2015; Sosnowska et al. 2021; for other ways to refer to MMP, see Shibasaki and Hallett 2006) associated with movement execution and online movement feedback in the Cz MRCP. It lasts between 400 and 600 ms after movement onset depending on the condition (peak ~200 ms before interception). Such a pattern of Cz MRCP activity—with activation both before and after movement onset—is the typical one in the literature for hand and arm movements (e.g., Miyamoto et al. 2020; do Nascimento et al. 2006; Pereira et al. 2018; Sosnowska et al. 2021). The DTW analysis shows that activity changes in the EEG signal occur earlier than in hand movement for all experimental conditions, confirming the described structure of the MRCP consisting of a BP/CNV component and an MMP component. In the case of the Oz VEP, the slow‐slope positive activity continues until right after the interception of the moving object for the three conditions.

The results of the time‐domain analysis show that hand movement, stimulus movement, and brain activity are all τ‐coupled to different degrees. A relevant feature of this coupling is that Oz VEP Tau and Cz MRCP Tau of the fast and medium velocity conditions converge with the Stimulus Tau slope while maintaining a slightly lower τ‐value. The same is true for the Cz MRCP Tau of the slow velocity condition, but not for its Oz VEP Tau as it was interpolated due to a non‐physiological jump after numerical differentiation (Cohen 2014). Generally speaking, the lower τ‐values in the activity of both electrodes are compatible with them contributing to planning, enabling, and generating hand movement, therefore slightly anticipating the actual timing of the action.

Our results support the idea that control laws of behavior are organized with regard to ecological information. In this particular case, τ‐coupling is the description of the behavioral dynamics that entails a control law shaped by the ecological variable τ. τ‐coupling between hand movement and stimulus movement arguably entails an information‐based control law that prescribes at least one relevant aspect of the behavior (its temporal evolution) in terms of just one variable of perceptual (ecological) information. Simply put, it can be said that the exhibited behavioral dynamics are produced by a control law that states something similar to “produce forces as to keep/improve τ‐coupling.” In this context, τ‐coupling of both the Cz MRCP and the Oz VRP with both hand movement and stimulus movement also supports ecological resonance: the variable of ecological information (τ) that constrains behavioral dynamics and their ensued information‐based control law also constrains neural dynamics (Raja 2018, 2021, 2024). It also points to the idea that the perceptual control of action is a unitary process in which ecological information can be seen as the vehicle of the perception–action unity. As already pointed out, and according to the ecological approach to perception and action, ecological information is directly available in the stimulus inflow and, therefore, perceivers can control their action with respect to it by a simple act of detection. In other words, thanks to ecological information, perceivers are liberated form creating the information they need to control their action and from building up an internal model of the environment to do so. Therefore, one strength of the ecological approach is to account for behavior in less‐demanding terms than the mainstream theories (e.g., by requiring less computational resources). The logic behind the ecoloigcal strategy has to do with the availability of perceptual information: τ‐value is not only available in the environment but it also exhaustively informs τ‐coupling and the control law entailed by it. For this reason, there is no need for representing and further manipulating an internal model of the gap‐closing situation in order to successfully control behavior as these models are originally postulated in contexts of informational scarcity. This is not the case in gap‐closing situations where τ‐value and τ‐coupling (along with its entailed control law) are enough to exhibit successful behavior. To be clear, and due to the very nature of explanations in cognitive (neuro)science (see Raja 2024), it is likely still possible to provide an internal model‐based account of the results in this study. However, it is difficult to imagine a good reason why such an account would be necessary.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first one in the literature that extends the analyses beyond classic ERPs with the onset of a defined event to explore τ and τ‐coupling. Previous studies have explored the influence of τ in those ERPs (see van der Weel and van der Meer 2009, van der Weel et al. 2022). However, a more general exploration of τ‐coupling for longer time periods of the perception–action coupling was lacking. Previous studies have explored the information‐based control law in terms of τ‐coupling in a discrete fashion (i.e., at one temporal point at the beginning of the EEG epoch) while the current study explores it in a more continuous fashion. This novel approach allows for a better characterization of the online control of the interceptive movement in terms of τ‐coupling. Additionally, the MRCP explored in this study is one of the main EEG components used in brain–computer interfaces (BCIs; see, e.g., Huang et al. 2012; Zhao and Zhang 2007). The fact that this motor‐related activity is τ‐coupled opens a new route for the design and control of BCI devices. In the spirit of the computational parsimony of the ecological approach, instead of computing velocity, force, etc., BCIs could in principle be controlled by a similar τ‐coupling strategy in which τ functions as the main variable and k as the main (and only) control parameter, likely reducing computational costs—for example., a similar strategy has already been proposed in other fields, like the language sciences (Elie et al. 2023).

The results in the time–frequency domain, which are as well a complete novelty in the literature, further support the previous considerations. First of all, they show the expected patterns in terms of power: a power decrease in the alpha band over the visual cortex due to stimulus processing and a power decrease in the beta and mu bands due to movement preparation and execution (Chiu et al. 2012; Darch et al. 2020; Nelli et al. 2017; Wagner et al. 2016; Zani et al. 2020). The ERD/S analysis shows a peak in synchronization in the vicinity of the stimulus onset in all conditions (see Figure 5a). This is compatible with the idea of “idling” until the movement of the stimulus causes a perturbation but also with the idea of a phase reset with stimulus onset that leads to a brief synchronization. This hypothesis is reinforced by the fact that the alpha band at Oz shows that pattern in a higher degree than the other two bands. After such an increase in synchronization, a tendency to general de‐synchronization in all three conditions can be observed.

The just described general ERD/S in all relevant frequency bands including alpha over visual cortex and mu as well as beta over the motor areas are correlated to the Stimulus Tau. An important aspect of this correlation is that it suggests τ influences neural dynamics at different temporal scales that depend on the activities of different brain regions and some basic physiological principles—from 7 to 30 Hz. This is compatible with one of the main ideas underlying the ecological resonance hypothesis: the idea of wider or more systemic tuning. James Gibson (1966, 1979) took the notion of resonance directly from Lashley and, therefore, directly from neurophysiology. Ecological resonance, however, is different from the ideas of resonance and tuning in mainstream neurophysiology. Concretely, instead of focusing on single‐cell/single‐network tuning to simple properties of stimulation, the ecological notion of resonance purports a more distributed or systemic tuning to high‐order properties of stimulation. The tuning to τ (high‐order variable of stimulation) at the two different locations (Oz and Cz) and at the different frequency bands is precisely the result expected from the ecological resonance hypothesis.

The ecological resonance hypothesis is further reinforced by the IF analysis and the relationship between the Oz alpha band and the Cz mu band. IF is a measure of functional connectivity (Cohen 2014) and, in that regard, it speaks to the relationship between the different locations tested in this study. This relationship not only points toward a form of systemic neural activity but also the Granger causation analysis uncovers a pattern typical of non‐linear resonators in which driven systems tend to anticipate driver systems when coupled to them (Large 2008). This property of non‐linear resonators is compatible with the systemic tuning ensued by the ecological resonance hypothesis.

Our study aimed to advance in the testing of two core hypotheses of ecological psychology and radical embodied neuroscience: the information‐based control laws hypothesis and the ecological resonance hypothesis. To do so, we explored an interceptive task in terms of τ and τ‐coupling. The results of both the time and the time–frequency domains offer support for both hypotheses that should be further tested in other tasks and with respect to other informational variables. However, the study has limitations that should be addressed in future work. First, additional steps are needed to explore the generality of both ecological hypotheses. Concretely, a complete description of the τ‐based information‐based control law entailed by τ‐coupling in this particular case must be provided. This first obvious candidate is TauG (Lee 2009), a control law that describes the production of forces in the organism in order to match different τ‐values. More work in this direction will be pursued. Second, a more widespread exploration of neural activity needs to be performed to keep testing the systematic nature of ecological resonance. This study focused on two particular EEG locations, but its methodology is in principle scalable to many more. Future work should develop such methodological possibility. Overall, and despite these limitations, the current study opens a new path for the development of radical embodied neuroscience.
