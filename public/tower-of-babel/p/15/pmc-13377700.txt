Rational structural design for the dual enhancement of catalytic activity and thermal stability of hyaluronidase from Citrobacter portucalensis
Authors: Yuanying Zhu, Shijunyin Chen, Feiyang Li, Yaya Yao, Yuejia Ji, Wei Feng, Zhongkai Wang, Xuepeng Li, Haitao Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyaluronidases (HAase) offer a practical route to produce valued low molecular weight hyaluronic acid (LMW-HA) and HA oligosaccharides (o-HA), which are valued across medical, cosmetic, and biomaterials applications due to their enhanced bioactivity and tissue penetration. However, reliable production and scale-up of LMW‑HA and o‑HA remain complicated due to inefficient production method, potential pathogenic hosts, enzymes with limited catalytic proficiency and prone to thermal denaturation. Here, we employed a structure-guided multi-strategy engineering approach to express and optimize a PL8 family hyaluronidase in E. coli, aiming to overcome these industrial bottlenecks. Using the HA tetrasaccharide binding site mapping, residue conservation analysis, and binding energy calculations, we obtained two mutants (T204R and Y97R) with significantly improved catalytic activities of 1.56 × 10⁶ U/mg and 1.43 × 10⁶ U/mg, respectively. Building on the best-performing variant (T204R), stability-focused design yielded T204R/N206H, which extended the inactivation half-life at 45 °C by up to 1.4-fold without loss of activity. This engineered enzyme efficiently generates LMW-HA and o-HA with tunable size distributions under mild conditions. These findings expand the hyaluronidase toolbox with a recombinant PL8 enzyme, enabling controlled enzymatic production of LMW-HA and o-HA for biomedical and personal care applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-026-02772-4.

Introduction

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a linear glycosaminoglycan composed of disaccharide repeating unit [4)-β-d-GlcpA-(1 → 3) -β-d-GlcpNAc(1 →] whose biological effects depend strongly on molecular weight: high-molecular weight HA (HMW-HA, > 1,000 kDa) derived from animal sources or microbial fermentation broth has been used extensively in cosmetics, orthopedics, and ophthalmology due to its unique viscoelasticity, lubrication, anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties [1–4]; LMW-HA (10 ~ 1,000 kDa) and o-HA (< 10 kDa) exhibit distinct activities relevant to skin permeability, wound repair, immunomodulation and angiogenesis, showing potential for drug delivery and ophthalmic formulations [5, 6]. These divergent bioactivities stem from differential receptor engagement and downstream signal: HMW-HA could crosslink with CD44 to affect T lymphocyte polarization [7, 8], while LMW-HA could disrupt CD44 clustering and binding to TLR2/4, thereby amplifying immune responses and triggering rapid neutrophil activation [9]. Furthermore, the bioactivity of o-HA is highly contingent upon cell type and metabolic context: tetrasaccharides are the minimal motif required to stimulate macrophage- mediated immune responses, whereas disaccharides could work as competitive antagonists for TLR4 and TLR2, inhibiting secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [10]. Driven by the expanding requirements in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering, the surging demand for LMW-HA and o-HA underscores the critical necessity to augment industrial production capacities [9, 11, 12].

LMW-HA and o-HA are produced by depolymerizing HMW-HA through physical, chemical, or enzymatic methods [13–16]. As physical and chemical methods involve harsh conditions and limited control over chain-length distribution, potentially damaging HA’s structure [5, 17, 18], enzymatic degradation using HAase is a widely preferred approach owing to its selectivity, mild conditions, and better bioactivity preservation [18, 19]. Although HAases span diverse origins, the animal-derived glycoside hydrolases (GHs) like bovine testicular hyaluronidase face contamination risks [2, 26, 27], and most well-characterized microbial polysaccharide lyases (PLs) originate from pathogens like Streptococcus species, raising safety, regulatory, and manufacturing concerns for large-scale use [20], requiring novel HAases from non-pathogenic industrial microorganisms [21, 22]. Moreover, existing HAases typically exhibit optimal activity around or below 37℃ and require prolonged incubation, and the low stability, low efficiency, and complex production processes prevent HAase from meeting the demands of large-scale industrial production [23].

The integration of heterologous expression with protein engineering offers a versatile solution for the industrial bottlenecks. For carbohydrate-active enzymes, structure-guided strategies such as rigidifying flexible regions or tuning surface electrostatics have been widely used to improve catalytic performance and thermostability [24, 25]: Human GH-type PH20 and leech GH-type HAase were expressed in P. pastoris via promoter and protein engineering, yielding activities up to 2.12 × 106 U/mL [1, 19, 28], Streptomyces PL16 HAase expressed in B. amyloliquefaciens produces HA fragments spanning 3.58 kDa to 1,590 kDa [29], and E. faecalis PL8 HAase reached 85.49 U/mg in E. coli [16], while C. freundii and T. composti PL8 HAase achieved 2.65 × 105 U/mL and 10.91 U/mg (70 °C), respectively [30]. These studies underscore the value of non-pathogenic host expression and rational design [31]. But achieving a robust balance between catalytic activity and thermostability remains a central challenge for harsh industrial conditions, reducing waste and enabling more efficient biocatalysis.

In this study, we developed a recombinant expression system for HAase in E. coli BL21(DE3), encoding a PL8-family hyaluronidase from Citrobacter portucalensis, a strain that was previously isolated and characterized by our laboratory [31]. Through structural modeling, active-site analysis, residue conservation studies, and binding energy calculations, we designed and characterized site-directed mutants to improve catalytic efficiency. Using the engineered T204R variant as a scaffold, we applied stability-focused design strategies to enhance thermal performance, achieving a 1.4-fold extension in the inactivation half-life at 45 °C while preserving catalytic activity. Collectively, this work enriches the HAase toolbox with a recombinantly accessible PL8 enzyme, establishes quantitative links between structure-guided mutations and functional gains in activity/stability, and enables enzymatically controlled production of low-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid (LMW-HA) for downstream applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Strains: Citrobacter portucalensis, preserved in China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Accession No: M20232108); Escherichia coli Top10 (Qingke Bio); E. coli competent cells from laboratory stock.

Reagents: Sodium hyaluronate (SH, Xinjiang Fufeng Biotechnology, China); plasmid mini-prep kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China); His-tag protein purification kit, Bradford protein concentration assay kit (Beyotime Biotech, Beijing, China); 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) reagent (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology, China); Gel permeation chromatography (GPC, Shanghai Microspectrum Detection Technology, China).

Sequence analysis of hyaluronidase

The genomic sequence of Citrobacter portucalensis was analyzed using the RAST (https://rast.nmpdr.org/rast.cgi) and CAZy (http://www.cazy.org/) databases [32, 33]. AlphaFold3 (https://deepmind.google/technologies/alphafold/alphafold-server/) was employed to predict the protein's 3D structure. Sequence similarity analysis, 3D structural comparison, and substrate-binding range determination within the HAase active pocket were performed using NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and PDB (https://www.rcsb.org/) databases [34, 35]. Molecular docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.5.6 Sep_17_14 [36]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA 12 software [37]. Bootstrap values for 1,000 replicates were shown at the node of the tree.

Expression and purification of recombinant enzymes

pET-28a(+) was used to construct recombinant vectors. The signal peptide of the target protein was predicted using SignalP 5.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/) and removed, and the coding sequence was optimized. His6 tag were added to both the N- and C-termini of the enzyme [38]. The optimized sequence was commercially synthesized by Qingke Biotech (China) and subsequently cloned into the pET-28a(+) plasmid. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells via heat shock and screened on LB plates containing kanamycin. The positive clones were confirmed by PCR, and the primer sequences used were listed in Table S1.

To express the recombinant HAase, the transformants were inoculated into LB liquid medium containing 0.1 mM kanamycin and cultured at 37 °C until the OD600 value of 0.6–0.8 was reached. Induction was then performed by adding 0.2 mM IPTG, and the culture was continued at 16 °C for 20 h. After induction, the cell suspension was subjected to ultrasonication disruption, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant, yielding the crude enzyme solution.

For enzyme purification, the crude enzyme solution was incubated with affinity resin (nickel column) for 2 h, then washed and eluted with imidazole solutions of varying concentrations to prepare the purified hyaluronidase enzyme solution. Protein expression was analyzed by 10% (w/v) SDS-PAGE, followed by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method [39].

Enzyme activity measurement

5 mL of 0.5 mg/mL enzyme solution was mixed with 0.5 L of 5% (w/v) high molecular weight sodium hyaluronate (SH, MW = 1,000 kDa) under optimal enzymatic conditions and incubated in a horizontal shaker at 220 rpm for 1 h. Then, 1 mL of the reaction mixture was mixed with 2 mL of DNS solution and incubated at 100 °C for 5 min. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 540 nm.

Enzyme activity unit (U) is defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 μg of reducing sugar equivalent from hyaluronic acid (HA) within 1 h at optimal pH and temperature.

Enzyme kinetic properties

Determination of optimal pH and pH stability

The substrate was prepared by dissolving SH to a final concentration of 0.5% (w/v) in 0.1 M citric acid–phosphate buffers (pH 3.0–8.0) or 0.1 M glycine–sodium hydroxide buffers (pH 9.0–12.0). The enzyme solution was mixed with buffer solutions of different pH and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Then the enzyme solutions were mixed with the substrate and incubated for 30 min under optimal pH and temperature conditions before enzyme activity measurement using the DNS method. An unprocessed enzyme solution was used as a control.

Determination of optimal temperature and thermal stability

Under the optimal pH conditions, enzyme-catalyzed reactions were carried out at temperatures ranging from 4 °C to 70 °C for 30 min to determine the optimal temperature.

For the thermal stability experiment, the enzyme solution was incubated in water baths at 40 °C, 45 °C, and 50 °C for 0 to 60 min, followed by enzyme activity measurement by the DNS method. Unheated enzyme solution served as the control.

Enzyme storage stability

The enzyme solution was stored under different conditions: at − 80 °C, − 20 °C, room temperature, and 37 °C, with regular monitoring of enzyme activity retention.

In all experiments, the highest enzyme activity was defined as 100%. Enzyme activity was measured using the DNS method, as described above.

Product molecular weight determination and distribution

The molecular weight and polydispersity of the products were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC). The chromatographic system was equipped with two columns in tandem, Waters Ultrahydrogel 120 and Waters Ultrahydrogel 1000, and signals were monitored using a refractive index detector (RID). Isocratic elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min at 40 °C, using 0.1 M NaNO3 as the mobile phase. A calibration curve was constructed using polyethylene glycol/polyethylene oxide (PEG/PEO) standards of known molecular weights. Based on this calibration, the peak molecular weight (Mp), weight-average molecular weight (Mw), number-average molecular weight (Mn), and polydispersity index (PDI) of the fragments were calculated.

Enzyme kinetic parameters and molecular dynamics simulation

The kinetic parameters of the wild-type enzyme and its mutants were determined using SH as the substrate. SH was prepared in citrate–phosphate buffer with concentrations ranging from 0.25% to 3%. Enzyme catalytic activity was measured at various substrate concentrations during the initial reaction time. Data was analyzed by GraphPad Prism v9.3.1.471 using the Michaelis–Menten model to obtain the enzyme’s kinetic parameters [40].

Molecular dynamics simulations of the enzyme were performed using YASARA 16.3.5 software (default force field: YAMBER, NaCl concentration: 0.9%, water solvent density: 0.997 g/mL) [41]. The simulation temperature was set to 298 K, and the pH was set to 7. The simulation was carried out without any restraints for 30 ns.

Enzyme catalytic activity and thermal stability modification, and detection methods

The mutation sites were selected based on occurrence frequency and evolutionary conservation. Occurrence frequency refers to the probability of a specific amino acid appearing at a particular position within a multiple sequence alignment (MSA). Evolutionary conservation refers to the degree to which amino acid residues remain unchanged across species during evolution. Residues with higher conservation are more critical for protein structure and function. The evolutionary conservation of enzyme amino acid residues was analyzed using the ConSurf online database [42]. Residues with conservation grades of 8 or 9 were considered highly conserved and excluded from mutation targets. Within a 6 Å range of substrate HA-4, residues with a conservation score < 8 and amino acid usage frequency < 50% were selected as mutation targets. For residues 6–8 Å from HA-4, non-conserved residues with a conservation score < 5 were targeted.

FoldX was used to calculate the binding free energy between the substrate and enzyme with designed mutations. Mutations with ΔΔG < 0 and the smallest free energy change were chosen [43].

For combination mutants, the mutant with the highest catalytic activity was selected as the starting enzyme. The enzyme sequence was optimized using the PROSS online database [44]. The design was based on the evolutionary conservation of amino acids and mutation site locations.

Equal amounts of each mutant enzyme were incubated at 45 °C for 2 h. Samples were taken every 10 min, and enzyme activity was measured using the DNS method. Untreated enzyme solution served as the control (100% activity). The inactivation rate constant (kd) was calculated using the equation for first-order reactions: ln A = -kd·t, where A is enzyme activity retention (%) and t is inactivation time. The half-life (t1/2) was determined as the time for enzyme activity to decrease by half at a specific temperature. For first-order reactions, t1/2 = 0.693/kd.

Results

Genome annotation and structural prediction of a PL8-family HAase

Based on the genomic annotation of Citrobacter portucalensis using the RAST and CAZY databases, a novel HAase gene has been identified and characterized as belonging to the PL8 family. The gene fragment is 2400 bp long and encodes a protein composed of 799 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of 88.3 kDa (Fig. S1). This protein exhibits substantial sequence divergence from all known enzymes whose three-dimensional structures have been experimentally determined (Fig. 1). Structural superposition analysis revealed this enzyme with an architecture comprising α-helices, β-sheets, and coil regions that form an extended substrate-binding cleft.Fig. 1The phylogenetic analysis of the Citrobacter portucalensis HAase

The phylogenetic analysis of the Citrobacter portucalensis HAase

To investigate the catalytic mechanism and action mode of hyaluronate lyase, sequence retrieval was performed against the PDB database. The results revealed that hyaluronate lyase from Streptococcus pneumoniae exhibited 31.23% sequence similarity and 70% sequence coverage compared to the target enzyme. Given this high sequence coverage, sequence alignment and structural homology modeling were conducted using the experimental structure of the S. pneumoniae enzyme as a template [45]. The N-terminal region of the enzyme is assembled by 15 α-helices into a characteristic twisted tubular structure, with its catalytic active site located in the hydrophobic cleft between the α-domain and β-domain.

The active site volume is estimated to be 1438 Å3, with positive charges at both ends of the cleft enhancing the interaction with the negatively charged HA molecule. The reducing end of the HA substrate is positioned at the narrow end of the active site, likely facilitating precise substrate binding and catalysis. Evolutionary conservation analysis using Consurf identified 38 key amino acid residues involved in the interaction with the HA-4 substrate, and electrostatic mapping showed that the substrate-binding pocket has an asymmetric electrostatic distribution: the non-reducing end is enriched in positively charged and polar residues, interacting with the substrate via salt bridges and hydrogen bonds to ensure substrate adsorption and stability, while the reducing end guides the substrate into the reaction pocket via positive electrostatic patches and facilitates product release through internal negative charge regions. Non-polar residues around the catalytic center accurately position the substrate’s pyranose ring via hydrophobic interactions, ensuring effective enzyme–substrate binding.

Further evolutionary conservation and 3D structural analyses confirmed that the enzyme follows the typical β-elimination catalytic mechanism of the PL8 family. Key catalytic residues Asn-214, His-264, and Tyr-273 work in concert to facilitate proton transfer and glycosidic bond cleavage, exhibiting endo-type enzyme characteristics and generating various products, including disaccharides, tetrasaccharides, and hexasaccharides (Fig. S2).

Catalytic activity modification

Structure-guided construction of recombinant hyaluronidase and mutants

Based on pocket proximity, conservation scores, and binding-energy estimates, 11 single-point mutations were constructed: T94D, Y97R, N148G, T204D, T204R, N333R, D338R, S342Q, E384R, N444G, and S627K, as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Visualization of the enzyme active site pockets for the mutants T94D, Y97R, N148G, T204D, T204R, N333R, D338R, S342Q, E384R, N444G, and S627K

Visualization of the enzyme active site pockets for the mutants T94D, Y97R, N148G, T204D, T204R, N333R, D338R, S342Q, E384R, N444G, and S627K

Homology analysis with S. pneumoniae HAase (PDB: 1LXK) revealed that residues N148 and N444 define the narrowest bottlenecks of the active site cleft (Fig. 2A). Although these residues are evolutionarily conserved in the PL8 family, substituting them with side-chain-free glycine—N148G (Fig. 2B) and N444G (Fig. 2C)—was expected to widen the pocket. This strategy aims to reduce steric hindrance, thereby increasing the probability of effective substrate-enzyme collisions and enhancing catalytic turnover.[46].

Residue T204 is located on a flexible loop at the edge of the narrow end (reducing end) of the cleft (Fig. 2D). Two contrasting mutations were designed to investigate its role: T204R (Fig. 2E) introduces a guanidinium group that forms a hydrogen bond (1.6 Å) with the C1-hydroxyl of the HA sugar ring, while its positive electrostatic patch synergistically facilitates substrate recruitment. Conversely, T204D (Fig. 2F) exhibited an increased binding free energy, suggesting that the introduction of a negative charge may accelerate product release through electrostatic repulsion.

The third category of designs aims to enhance the substrate capture capacity and the stability of the enzyme–substrate complex at the wide-end entrance. By introducing basic residues (Arg/Lys) at sites Y97, N333, D338, E384, and S627 (Fig. 2H), a strong positive electrostatic potential region was constructed on the surface of the wide-end cleft. This strategy utilizes salt bridges and electrostatic interactions to strengthen the initial capture of the substrate. For T94D and S342Q (Fig. 2I), the extension of polar side chains was employed to optimize the hydrogen-bonding network. FoldX calculations revealed that the binding free energies decreased by 0.21 and 0.40 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating that the introduction of polar residues further stabilizes the interaction between the enzyme and the substrate.

FoldX calculations and Consurf evolutionary-conservation data for above designs are summarized in Tables S2 and S3.

All variants and the wild-type were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) and purified. The purified enzymes exhibited a protein size around 88 kDa as shown in Fig. S3, indicating that modifications to the enzyme active site had little effect on the overall protein size.

Characterization and molecular weight distribution of products from WT and mutant enzymes

The enzyme activity of mutants was initially screened by monitoring depolymerization of HMW-HA by agarose gel electrophoresis. The enzymatic hydrolysis rates of different enzymes were determined based on the distance from the bands to the loading wells and calibrated against hyaluronic acid (HA) standards of varying molecular weights. As shown in Fig. 3, WT, T204R, T94D, S342Q, E384R, and Y97R produced similar degradation patterns, while N148G, N333R, S627K, N444G, T204D and D338R exhibited slower degradation than WT. To more precisely assess mutant enzyme activity, the molecular weights of degradation products after 1 h were measured and are listed in Table 1. Compared to WT treated group, Y97R and T204R displayed faster degradation than WT (Table 1). The two mutants have been further evaluated in pure water free from buffer impurities. As shown in Fig. S4, T204R and Y97R not only retain high catalytic activity but also demonstrate significantly faster enzymatic hydrolysis speeds. The GPC molecular weight distributions of Y97R and T204R are shown in Fig. S5, demonstrating narrower polydispersity and more concentrated molecular weight distributions. Other mutants show similar or worse degradation ability, and T204D exhibits the poorest degradation ability. Enzymatic properties of wild-type and mutant enzymes

(1) Optimal pH and pH stability of the enzyme

The experimental results demonstrate that site-directed mutagenesis markedly altered the enzyme’s pH-dependent properties. As shown in Fig. 4A, the WT enzyme exhibited an alkaline optimum pH of 9.0, whereas the mutants displayed distinct shifts in their pH profiles: N333R shifted further toward alkalinity to pH 9.3, D338R and N444G shifted toward neutrality, and N148G and T204R underwent pronounced shifts toward the acidic range (pH 5.0). Regarding pH stability (Fig. 4B), all mutants retained substantial tolerance except N148G, which showed a significantly narrowed stability window.Fig. 4A, B Detection results of the optimal reaction pH and pH stability for WT and various single-point mutant enzymes, respectively; C Specific activities of WT and all mutants; D Detection results of the optimal reaction temperature for WT and various single-point mutant enzymes

A, B Detection results of the optimal reaction pH and pH stability for WT and various single-point mutant enzymes, respectively; C Specific activities of WT and all mutants; D Detection results of the optimal reaction temperature for WT and various single-point mutant enzymes

Homology modeling and molecular docking based on PDB 1LXK indicated that this enzyme belongs to the PL8 family, with its core catalytic region formed by a His/Tyr/Asn catalytic triad, consistent with the canonical β-elimination mechanism. Structural analysis revealed a distinctive electrostatic gradient within the active-site cleft, suggesting that remodeling of this microenvironment is a major determinant of the altered pH profiles. N333R is located in a positively charged region near the non-reducing end of the binding pocket, and the introduction of the positively charged Arg residue further strengthens electrostatic attraction to the negatively charged substrate (HA). This enhanced interaction compensates for the efficiency loss caused by deprotonation of catalytic residues under high pH conditions, thereby sustaining alkaline activity. This strategy of modulating catalytic residue pKa through peripheral electrostatic potential aligns with previous findings in β-glucosidase engineering reported by Zuo et al.[47].

It should be noted that T204R, despite introducing a basic residue, caused a substantial shift toward acidic pH. Similar phenomena have been observed in β-glucosidase by Nielsen et al.[48], indicating that pH characteristics are governed by precise local electrostatic distribution rather than simple charge addition. Simulation results suggest that T204 is situated at the edge of the reducing-end pocket, and Arg insertion likely disrupts the pre-existing hydrogen-bond network and alters the local dielectric environment, lowering the pKa of catalytic residues (e.g., His) and enabling optimal protonation at lower pH.

The significantly reduced pH stability range of N148G is closely associated with the nature of this residue. N148 resides in a key loop region critical for maintaining the active-pocket geometry. Its replacement with the small-side-chain Gly increases local conformational flexibility. As noted by Shi et al.[49], reducing side-chain volume often sacrifices structural stability in exchange for enhanced flexibility. The increased flexibility renders the active pocket more susceptible to inactivation under non-optimal pH conditions, resulting in a noticeably contracted stability profile relative to WT.

(2) Optimal temperature and thermal stability of the enzyme

As illustrated in Fig. 4D, the WT enzyme exhibited an optimal reaction temperature (Topt) of 30 °C. In contrast, mutants T204R, N333R, and Y97R displayed a thermophilic shift, with Topt increasing to 37 °C. This elevation suggests that substitutions at these sites optimized the conformational flexibility of the active pocket and its periphery, achieving a superior balance between catalytic efficiency and structural rigidity at physiological temperatures. Temperature stability at 40,45,50 °C (Fig S6) and half-life (t1/2) data (Table 3) revealed a distinct gradient in structural robusticity among the variants. At 45 °C, T204R (55.4 min) and Y97R (51.0 min) maintained stability comparable to WT (52.5 min). Notably, under extreme thermal stress at 50 °C, E384R (5.48 min) and T204R (5.03 min) exhibited significantly prolonged half-lives relative to WT (3.12 min). In the molecular engineering of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), reinforcing hyper-flexible regions and optimizing surface electrostatics are established strategies for enhancing thermostability [49, 50]. Structural analysis based on the 1LXK model indicates that T204 is located within a loop region flanking the active site. The substitution of Threonine with a long-side-chain, positively charged Arginine (T204R) potentially induces loop rigidification through steric hindrance or the formation of novel local salt bridges and hydrogen bond networks with adjacent acidic residues. Such modifications fortify regions inherently susceptible to thermal fluctuations.

These observations align with the paradigms proposed by Feng et al.[51], wherein thermostability is enhanced by incorporating stabilizing motifs from homologous thermophilic enzymes. Conversely, the t1/2 of N148G plummeted to 6.8 min at 45 °C (only 13% of WT). This drastic reduction confirms that replacing the polar Asparagine with a side-chain-deficient Glycine increased local conformational entropy, rendering the enzyme architecture prone to collapse upon surpassing the thermal activation energy barrier.

The thermal profiles of the lead mutants, particularly T204R, demonstrate substantial industrial potential. Given that the large-scale production of LMW-HA typically operates within 20–50 °C, these variants facilitate higher processing temperatures. This enhancement effectively reduces substrate viscosity and accelerates mass transfer rates while extending the operational lifespan of the biocatalyst, thereby optimizing overall process economy.

(3) Enzyme stability and preservation

Enzyme stability is critical for reliable industrial production. To assess storage stability, wild-type (WT) enzyme and the high-performance mutants T204R and Y97R were stored at − 80 °C, − 20 °C, 37 °C, and room temperature (25–28 °C) for 30 days, with enzyme activity measured daily. As shown in Fig. 5, all three enzymes retained over 75% activity at − 80 °C and − 20 °C after 30 days, enabling long-term low-temperature storage. At room temperature, WT retained more than 70% activity at day 15, while Y97R retained only 29% at day 13; T204R still maintained 96% activity at day 15. At 37 °C, WT and Y97R retained 50% activity for 2.9 days, whereas T204R reached 4.2 days (1.4-fold longer). The enhanced stability of T204R was attributed to the threonine-to-arginine substitution at position 204 in the flexible loop of the α-domain, which increased loop rigidity. Structural impact of mutation sites on enzyme thermal stability

The T204R/A408L (Fig. S8) mutation enhances van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions between nonpolar amino acid residues, increasing the rigidity of the loop region and strengthening the hydrogen bond network, which in turn improves the enzyme’s stability [52]. Additionally, the T204R/T248Y (Fig. S9) mutation introduces an aromatic ring, boosting interactions between nonpolar amino acids and stabilizing polar amino acid interactions, further enhancing the stability of the α-helices. Notably, the T204R/N206H (Fig. S10) mutation demonstrates the highest thermal stability in this study. By introducing the basic amino acid (His), it not only eliminates potential deamidation effects but also strengthens electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions between amino acids, significantly reducing solvent exposure and further improving both the enzyme's stability and catalytic activity. Additionally, the T204R/Q250E (Fig. S11) mutation introduces an acidic amino acid, increasing electrostatic interactions and further enhancing thermal stability. Collectively, these mutations optimize the charge distribution and interactions of surface amino acids, thereby significantly enhancing the enzyme’s stability and potentially improving its catalytic performance for industrial applications.

Conclusion

In this study, the hyaluronan lyase (HAase) from Citrobacter portucalensis was heterologously expressed and purified for the first time. By combining enzymatic mechanisms, structural analysis, and the molecular weight distribution of the product, we designed and validated 11 single-point mutants and 3 combinatorial mutants. Two mutants, T204R and Y97R, showed significantly enhanced catalytic activity. The specific activities of T204R and Y97R were (1.56 ± 0.010) × 106 U/mg and (1.43 ± 0.009) × 106 U/mg, respectively, corresponding to 1.21 and 1.11 times higher than the wild-type (WT) activity of (1.29 ± 0.006) × 106 U/mg. According to recent review of heterologously expressed hyaluronidases, the highest reported activity is 2.12 × 106 U/mL, followed by 1.68 × 106 U/mL [53]. In comparison, the enzyme activity in this study is among the highest and could serve as an important enzyme source for industrial hyaluronic acid production. The mutants also maintained high catalytic activity in the sodium hyaluronate solutions. Moreover, T204R exhibited stable enzyme activity under various storage conditions, demonstrating its potential for long-term use.

The T204R mutation, which replaces Thr204 with Arg, markedly enhanced the catalytic activity of the enzyme. Structurally, the guanidine group of Arg204 increases the positive electrostatic potential and forms a hydrogen bond with the anomeric hydroxyl group of HA pyranose ring, strengthening enzyme–substrate interaction at the reducing end [54]. Together with pre-existing positive patches bind to the non-reducing end of HA, this change promotes the entry of the negatively charged substrate into the active site, thereby improving catalytic efficiency. These findings are consistent with earlier reports that arginine residues frequently participate in the active sites of enzymes acting on anionic substrates [55]. The present study further corroborates the critical role of arginine in the ligand-binding sites and underscores its contribution to enhancing enzymatic catalysis.

The T204R/N206H mutant, built upon the T204R, exhibited a broader optimal temperature range. The N206H substitution replaced Asn206 with His, introducing a basic amino acid that enhanced electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions, further improving enzyme stability. Consistent with existing literature, mutations in surface loop regions and the introduction of the nonpolar amino acids play crucial roles in enzyme stability. Our experiments validated this strategy and demonstrated that heterologous expression in E. coli significantly enhanced the performance of C. portugalis-derived hyaluronidase.

Both T204R and T204R/N206H mutants showed significant improvements in catalytic performance and thermal stability. Compared to the native host C. portugalis, these mutants exhibited substantially higher activity in the E. coli expression system, highlighting their great application potential. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the industrial production of low-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid (HA) and offer new insights and methodologies for enzyme engineering and applications.
