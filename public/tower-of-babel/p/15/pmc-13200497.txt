Repositioning of Ertiprotafib via supramolecular assembly enables biomarker-guided therapy for acute kidney injury
Authors: Hochung Jang, Dongwon Shin, Byeongmin Park, Jae-Hyeon Lee, Youngri Ryu, Junha Lee, Eunbyeol Ko, Yoon Sook Ko, Hee Young Lee, Young Eun Choi, Se Won Oh, Sang-Kyung Jo, Sangmine Lee, Jooho Park, Kwangmeyung Kim, Sun Hwa Kim, Myung-Gyu Kim, Yoosoo Yang, Man Kyu Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a rapid loss of renal function that has become a global health concern, with incidence rates reaching up to 50% in critically ill patients. Despite its growing prevalence, no effective pharmacological therapy for AKI currently exists, underscoring an urgent need for new treatments. Here, we present a drug repositioning approach in which the diabetes drug ertiprotafib is formulated within a supramolecular nanomedicine platform to enable biomarker-guided AKI therapy. Covalent modification of ertiprotafib with a multifunctional peptide drives the drug to spontaneously self-assemble into supramolecular nano-assemblies (SENs) via intermolecular interactions. In an ischemia–reperfusion AKI mouse model, these SEN nanoparticles preferentially accumulate in inflamed kidneys by penetrating the interstitium through disrupted tight junctions, a disease-induced structural biomarker. Subsequently, biomarker-guided enzymatic activation of the assemblies by the upregulated cathepsin B in injured tubular cells triggers the release of ertiprotafib, resulting in protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) inhibition and modulation of the Src/ERK signaling pathway to suppress inflammatory responses and ameliorate disease progression. In contrast, healthy tubular cells and other organs (with low cathepsin B expression) experience minimal prodrug activation, which mitigates ertiprotafib’s off-target toxicity. Collectively, this coordinated supramolecular nanotherapy demonstrates an effective and safer strategy for AKI treatment by repurposing an existing drug.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04357-7.Keywords: Acute kidney injury (AKI); Drug repositioning; Ertiprotafib; Prodrug; Supramolecular assembly, Nanomedicine

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a serious clinical syndrome characterized by a rapid decline in renal function within hours to days, with a prevalence exceeding 20% among hospitalized patients and up to 50% in intensive care units [1, 2]. The prognosis remains poor, as diverse insults such as ischemia–reperfusion, sepsis, and nephrotoxic drugs converge to induce microvascular dysfunction, tubular epithelial injury with inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic reprogramming [3]. Despite its high incidence and mortality, current management of AKI relies almost exclusively on supportive care, including fluid management and dialysis in severe cases, with no approved targeted therapies [4, 5]. These limitations highlight an urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches that can address the multifactorial and heterogeneous mechanisms underlying AKI.

Protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B), a negative regulator of insulin and leptin signaling, has long been investigated as a therapeutic target for obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [6, 7]. Over the past two decades, numerous compound-based PTP1B inhibitors were developed, yet none achieved clinical success [8]. The most advanced candidate, ertiprotafib, entered Phase II trials for diabetes but was discontinued due to insufficient efficacy and dose-limiting adverse effects [9]. Mechanistic analyses revealed that ertiprotafib binds PTP1B in a non-specific manner, inducing enzyme oligomerization and aggregation, thereby reducing selectivity and promoting off-target toxicity [10]. Despite these setbacks in metabolic disease, accumulating evidence suggests that PTP1B is also a key regulator of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and Src signaling. PTP1B modulates ER stress pathways such as PERK and directly interacts with Src by dephosphorylating its inhibitory Tyr527/529 residue, thus influencing downstream inflammatory and fibrotic responses [11]. Given that both ER stress and Src activation are central drivers of tubular injury, immune cell infiltration, and fibrosis in AKI, targeting the PTP1B–Src/ERK axis offers a compelling therapeutic strategy [12, 13]. From this perspective, we hypothesize that overcoming the limitations of ertiprotafib through a disease-tailored delivery platform could enable its effective repositioning for AKI. This rationale provides the basis for exploring a disease-responsive prodrug strategy, in which ertiprotafib can be selectively activated within the unique pathological environment of AKI.

Unlike chronic metabolic disorders, AKI presents a unique disease microenvironment that can be exploited for drug targeting. Notably, proteolytic enzymes such as cathepsin B are markedly overexpressed and activated in injured proximal tubular cells during AKI, while expression remains low in healthy renal tissue [14]. To leverage this feature, we devised a prodrug strategy to repurpose ertiprotafib by chemically conjugating it with a cathepsin B-cleavable FRRG peptide (Scheme 1A). Importantly, these conjugates self-organize into supramolecular ertiprotafib nanoassemblies (SENs) via intermolecular interactions between aromatic segments [15–17]. This nanoscopic prodrug format is a key feature of our platform, enabling both enhanced delivery and prolonged retention of the drug in diseased kidney tissue. Compared with traditional nanomedicines, such as polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, micelles and inorganic nanoparticles [18–21], this supramolecular formulation offers several technical and practical advantages: (i) exceptionally high drug-loading capacity, (ii) minimized premature payload leakage during systemic circulation, (iii) reduced potential adverse events caused by excessive carrier material and (iv) ease of process optimization for quality control (QC) and mass production [22–25].

Biomarker-guided acute kidney injury (AKI) treatment using supramolecular ertiprotafib assemblies (SENs). (A) Chemical structure of SENs. Conjugation of ertiprotafib with FRRG enables spontaneous self-assembly through intermolecular interactions, selectively releasing the active ingredient via cathepsin B–mediated enzymatic cleavage. (B) Mechanism of biomarker-guided AKI therapy. In an AKI mouse model, intravenously administered SENs accumulate in the inflamed kidney by penetrating the interstitium facilitated by tight junction disruption during disease onset followed by enzymatic activation by the AKI biomarker cathepsin B to precisely deliver ertiprotafib into injured tubular cells. Ultimately, ertiprotafib inhibits PTP1B-dependent Src/ERK signaling pathways, thereby significantly improving inflammatory responses, renal barrier integrity and histopathological outcomes

This biomarker-guided supramolecular medicine offers therapeutic and safety advantages that overcome ertiprotafib’s prior limitations. SENs remain in an inactive state during circulation and selectively accumulate in inflamed kidneys through tight junction disruption and vascular leakage, where elevated cathepsin B ensures site-specific activation of the prodrug (Scheme 1B). Such confined drug release achieves high local concentrations in injured tubular cells, resulting in potent inhibition of the PTP1B–Src/ERK signaling pathway to suppress inflammation, preserve renal function, and restore barrier integrity—outcomes unattainable with free ertiprotafib. Equally important, minimal activation occurs in healthy tissues, thereby mitigating systemic toxicity. By expanding the safety margin and restricting drug activity to diseased sites, this supramolecular prodrug platform successfully repurposes ertiprotafib as a targeted and clinically viable therapy for AKI.

Results and discussion

Design, preparation and characterization

SENs were derived from the supramolecular self-assembly of drug conjugates, which were prepared via a one-step amide condensation between the FRRG peptide and ertiprotafib (Figure S1). Following synthesis, their purity and molecular weight were confirmed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC/MS; Figure S2). Repeated syntheses demonstrated that the production process reliably provides samples with > 60% yield and > 95% purity, highlighting low batch-to-batch variability (Figure S3). The SENs exhibited a uniform size distribution under aqueous conditions, with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 139.23 ± 0.39 nm and a spherical morphology (Fig. 1A). Their zeta potential was determined to be 2.51 ± 0.41 mV; such near-neutrally charged nanoparticles are well suited for prolonged systemic circulation and are therefore advantageous for targeting pathologies involving increased vascular permeability, such as AKI [26, 27]. Exceptional stability of SENs was demonstrated under physiological conditions, as evidenced by the maintenance of hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index (PDI) in both PBS and mouse serum over 24 h without significant changes (Fig. 1B and S4). When SENs were incubated with various proteins, such as immunoglobulin G (IgG), complement component 3b (C3b), albumin and apolipoprotein A-1 (ApoA1), their absorption behavior was comparable to that of hyaluronic acid (HA) or poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles (Figure S5) [28–30]. These results suggest that SENs are likely to exhibit similar levels of protein corona formation, opsonization behavior and macrophage uptake in vivo to those observed with conventional nanoparticles.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations identified π-π stacking interactions between the aromatic rings of phenylalanine and ertiprotafib as the driving force behind the self-assembly (Fig. 1C and S6) [31–33]. This process was accompanied by a gradual decrease in Lennard-Jones potential energy, indicating that spontaneous assembly led to progressive structural stabilization (Fig. 1D). These highly stable particles, with an average size of approximately 100 nm, show enhanced accumulation in inflamed renal tissues by being captured through disrupted tight junctions during the onset of AKI [14].

The PTP1B-inhibitory effect was assessed in the human proximal tubular cell line HK-2. For cellular uptake analysis, fluorescently labeled SENs were prepared by co-assembling 1 wt% Cy5.5-conjugated drug conjugates with unmodified conjugates. The intracellular uptake of SENs gradually increased in a time-dependent manner, reaching a maximum after 24 h of incubation (Fig. 1E and S7). Treatment with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) resulted in significant upregulation of PTP1B in tubular cells due to the inflammatory response, but subsequent incubation with the nanoassemblies restored PTP1B expression to levels comparable to those in naive cells (Fig. 1F and S8). Importantly, their effects in downregulating the target protein were minimized by treatment with the cathepsin B inhibitor CA-074; indeed, there were no significant differences in PTP1B levels between H2O2-treated HK-2 cells and those additionally incubated with SENs together with CA-074. In addition, ertiprotafib conjugated with the FGRG peptide, a scrambled sequence of the cathepsin B-cleavable RR motif, failed to regulate intracellular PTP1B levels (Figure S9). These findings indicate that SENs undergo selective enzymatic activation mediated by cathepsin B to release ertiprotafib, ultimately counteracting inflammation-induced PTP1B overexpression. In previous studies, FRRG motif-derived supramolecular assemblies were efficiently internalized across various cell types (e.g., cancer cells, normal cells, and immune cells), yet remained inactive in cellular environments with relatively low cathepsin B expression [34–36]. Overall, the activation of SENs is determined by the abundance of the target enzymatic biomarker rather than by selective cell entry.

In vitro anti-inflammatory effects

The anti-inflammatory effects resulting from PTP1B inhibition were evaluated in tubular cells. Exposure of HK-2 cells to H2O2 induced a significant increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), which leads to cellular oxidative stress and plays a crucial role in the regulation of kinases involved in kidney diseases [37]. Subsequent treatment with SENs effectively reduced intracellular ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A and S10) and significantly decreased ROS-induced cell death (Fig. 2B). In contrast, free ertiprotafib further aggravated cell damage in H2O2-treated tubular cells due to its well-known dose-limiting toxicities [38, 39]. These results suggest that SENs maintain an appropriate intracellular concentration of ertiprotafib through cathepsin B-mediated enzymatic cleavage, thereby promoting anti-inflammatory effects. Annexin V/PI staining provided additional insight into the distinct patterns of cell death. SENs decreased the proportions of early apoptosis (Annexin V+PI−), late apoptosis (Annexin V+PI+) and necrosis (Annexin V−PI+) in H2O2-treated HK-2 cells, whereas ertiprotafib increased all modes of cell death (Fig. 2C).

We further examined tight junction integrity by evaluating ZO-1 expression in tubular cells. Exposure of H2O2 to HK-2 cells led to significant reduction in ZO-1, which ertiprotafib treatment failed to restore, resulting in discontinuous junctional staining (Fig. 2D and E and S11). In contrast, SENs reinstated continuous ZO-1 expression to levels comparable to healthy controls, highlighting their ability to preserve epithelial barrier integrity (Fig. 2F). Taken together, these findings imply that under AKI conditions, SENs effectively suppress inflammatory responses, limit tubular cell death, and restore disrupted tight junctions—mechanistic outcomes that align with their expected therapeutic benefits in vivo.

Enhanced renal accumulation in AKI model

Next, we aimed to identify biomarkers that facilitate kidney-targeted drug delivery of SENs in vivo. Successful modeling was confirmed by significant upregulation of several ischemia–reperfusion–induced acute kidney injury (IR-AKI) markers (Figure S12). Renal tissues from mice with IR-AKI exhibited marked overexpression of cathepsin B compared with healty controls (Fig. 3A and S13). In particular, renal cathepsin B expression in injured tissues was significantly correlated with kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), indicating upregulation of the target biomarker in tubular cells damaged by AKI. Additionally, a time-dependent and progressive disruption of tight junctions following IR injury was observed in the kidneys of mouse models, as evidenced by the loss of two major markers, occludin-1 and ZO-1, relative to healthy tissues (Fig. 3B and S14). Nanoparticles larger than 150 nm are unable to penetrate the interstitium through the disrupted tight junctions under AKI conditions, whereas smaller particles efficiently accumulate in inflamed renal tissues [14]. Therefore, we hypothesized that SENs would enable biomarker-guided AKI treatment, mediated by both structural changes and altered enzyme expression.

As shown in Fig. 3C, SENs demonstrated prolonged in vivo circulation, with improved pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters and detectable levels in the bloodstream for up to 48 h. The biodistribution and renal accumulation kinetics of SENs were monitored via real-time near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging. As expected, SENs exhibited substantial renal accumulation (highlighted by the red line) in AKI mice compared to the sham group (Fig. 3D). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs further confirmed significantly greater SEN accumulation in inflamed kidneys relative to healthy tissues during the initial 1 h, followed by gradual elimination over time (Fig. 3E). Notably, renal accumulation of SENs remained consistently higher in AKI mice than in normal mice, with the most pronounced difference (1.49-fold) observed at 6 h post-administration (Fig. 3F). Histological analysis revealed preferential localization of SENs in the renal cortex, particularly within the outer stripe of the outer medulla (OSOM), the region most susceptible to ischemic damage (Fig. 3G and S15) [40]. Together, these results indicate that SEN accumulation is driven by compromised tubular barrier integrity and elevated cathepsin B activity, enabling selective retention and distribution in diseased renal tissue.

Therapeutic efficacy of biomarker-guided therapy in an AKI model

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of SENs was assessed in an IR-induced AKI (IR-AKI) (Fig. 4A). Intravenous administration of SENs at 1 or 3 mg/kg (ertiprotafib equivalent) did not significantly improve renal function, whereas a 5 mg/kg dose restored blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine levels to nearly those of the sham group (Fig. 4B). Increasing the administered dose up to 10 mg/kg produced no further benefit compared with 5 mg/kg. Notably, treatment with 5 mg/kg ertiprotafib failed to improve renal function, highlighting the superior efficacy of SENs due to kidney-targeted drug delivery.

Subsequently, histopathological analyses were performed following treatment with either ertiprotafib or SENs at an equivalent active ingredient dose of 5 mg/kg. Kidney tissues stained with H&E showed a greater degree of tubular damage in the PBS and ertiprotafib groups compared with the sham group, whereas SEN treatment considerably reduced renal lesions (Figure S16). Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining revealed reduced renal congestion and tubular damage in the SEN group than the PBS or ertiprotafib groups (Fig. 4C and S17A). Additionally, treatment with SENs downregulated the infiltration of macrophages (F4/80) and neutrophils (Ly6G) in the renal tubulointerstitium (Fig. 4D and S17B). TUNEL staining showed a decrease in apoptotic cell death in kidneys from AKI mice treated with SENs in comparison to those treated with PBS or ertiprotafib (Fig. 4E and S17C). Importantly, whereas free ertiprotafib aggravated renal injury—causing extensive structural damage, immune cell infiltration, and apoptosis due to its toxicity—SENs effectively mitigated these adverse effects [38, 39].

Given that IR injury triggers inflammatory responses in the kidneys, cytokine profiles and related protein expressions were evaluated [41]. The SEN group showed a notable decrease in the levels of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-6) and IL-1β compared to the PBS and ertiprotafib groups (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was restored to that of the sham group following SEN treatment (Fig. 5B). Mechanistically, these effects were associated with inhibition of the PTP1B-dependent Src/ERK signaling pathway [42]. Although SEN treatment did not directly reduce renal total PTP1B levels due to its allosteric mode of inhibition, downstream inflammatory signaling was significantly suppressed relative to the PBS and ertiprotafib groups (Fig. 5C and S18).

We next assessed whether the anti-inflammatory effects of SENs translated into restoration of barrier integrity. In both the PBS- and ertiprotafib-treated groups, severe disruption of renal tight junctions was observed, as indicated by markedly reduced expression of occludin-1 and ZO-1 (Fig. 5D). In contrast, SENs restored their expression to near-normal levels, thereby reconstructing renal barrier integrity. Taken together, these results demonstrate that SENs provide effective biomarker-guided therapeutic benefit in AKI by improving renal function, mitigating histopathological injury, alleviating inflammatory responses, and repairing structural barriers.

In vivo safety of SEN treatment

The safety of SEN treatment was assessed in healthy mice following single or multiple intravenous administrations. Neither body weight nor serum biochemical parameters related to hepatic (Aspartate transaminase, AST; and alanine transaminase, ALT), cardiac (Lactate dehydrogenase, LDH; and creatine kinase, CK) and renal (blood urea nitrogen, BUN; and creatinine, CRE) function were significantly altered after a single 5 mg/kg dose of ertiprotafib or SENs compared to the non-treated control group (Fig. 6A and B and S19). However, multiple doses (six times) of ertiprotafib resulted in body weight loss and hematological toxicity, whereas SEN administration at the same regimen did not elicit considerable side effects. Histological analyses further revealed widespread structural abnormalities across major organs in the multiple-dose ertiprotafib group, while no such toxicity was observed with SENs (Fig. 6C). Considering therapeutic efficacy, dose optimization of ertiprotafib is unfeasible due to severe toxicity at ineffective concentrations, whereas SENs exhibited a favorable safety profile at repeated doses while demonstrating significant therapeutic potential. These findings indicate that the SENs minimize the toxic side effects associated with ertiprotafib through enzymatic biomarker-mediated selective drug release in injured tubular cells, supporting their great potential as a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for AKI.

Conclusion

In the present study, we repurposed the diabetes drug ertiprotafib for the treatment of AKI through supramolecular assembly. A simple one-step modification using the functional FRRG peptide enabled the formation of nanoassemblies via intermolecular interactions, without the need for additional carrier materials. The resulting SENs efficiently targeted inflamed renal tissues by penetrating the interstitium formed due to tight junction disruption during the onset of AKI. Subsequently, the nanoassemblies selectively released ertiprotafib in injured tubular cells through cathepsin B-mediated enzymatic activation. Through this biomarker-guided therapy, SENs significantly improved renal function, histopathological outcomes and renal barrier integrity in the AKI model while minimizing potential side effects associated with the dose-limiting toxicity of ertiprotafib. Ultimately, this study presents a rational nanomedicine design established within the framework of supramolecular chemistry for repositioning ertiprotafib in the treatment of AKI, a condition that remains clinically refractory due to the lack of effective therapeutic options.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of supramolecular ertiprotafib nanoassembly (SEN)

To prepare drug conjugate, C-terminal-amidated FRRG peptide (Phe-Arg-Arg-Gly; DG peptides, China) and ertiprotafib (CAS No. 251303-04-5, TargetMol Chemicals Inc.) were subjected to amide coupling reaction in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) as coupling reagents. After dissolving all components in DMF, N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added in catalytic amounts as a non-nucleophilic base. The reaction mixture was stirred for 3 h at room temperature, and the product was precipitated using anhydrous ethyl alcohol (CAS No. 64-17-5, anhydrous 99.9%, GR, DAEJUNG Chemicals & Metals Co.). The resulting crude product was dissolved in a binary solvent system comprising acetonitrile (AH015-4, Honeywell International Inc.) and distilled water in 8:2 (v/v) ratio. The dissolved product was analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (HPLC/MS; Agilent 1200 Series HPLC system) equipped with an XBridge® C18 analytical column (No. 186003117, 5 μm particle size, 4.6 mm × 150 mm, Waters Co.) for structural characterization and purity assessment. As a control, FGRG-conjugated ertiprotafib was synthesized using C-terminal-amidated FGRG peptide (Phe-Gly-Arg-Gly; DG peptides, China) following the same procedure described above. The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). The morphology was visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, CM-200, Philips).

Dot blot analysis of protein corona formation

1 mg/mL of SENs, HA nanoparticles or PLGA nanoparticles were incubated with human serum at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio at 37 °C for 1 h and 24 h to form protein corona. Following incubation, the samples were layered onto an equal volume of 0.7 M sucrose solution and centrifuged at 15,900 g for 90 min at 4 °C to separate nanoparticle-protein complexes from free proteins. The resulting pellets were washed three times with PBS to remove loosely bound proteins (soft corona), then resuspended in PBS to the original volume [43]. For dot blot analysis, samples (2 µL) were spotted onto nitrocellulose membranes and air-dried. Membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in TBS-T (TBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with IgG (31410, Invitrogen), Complement C3b (Invitrogen, MA1-70053), Albumin (PA5-89332, Invitrogen), ApoA-1 (PA5-29557, Invitrogen) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive signals were detected using ECL substrate and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Cellular uptake and PTP1B inhibition

The cellular uptake of SENs was assessed in the human renal proximal tubule cell line HK-2 (CRL-2190™, American Type Culture Collection). For NIRF imaging, fluorescent dye Cy5.5-conjugated drug conjugates were prepared and co-assembled with unmodified conjugates at a 1:99 wt% ratio. Briefly, Cy5.5 carboxylic acid (0.13 mmol; Lumiprobe, Wan Chai, Hong Kong), COOH-FRRG(Fmoc) peptide (0.26 mmol; Peptron Co.), EDC (0.65 mmol), NHS (0.65 mmol) and N, N-diisopropylethylamine (0.156 mmol; DIPEA, Sigma Aldrich) were dissolved in anhydrous DMF. After 3 h of reaction, Cy5.5-FRRG(Fmoc) was purified using RP-HPLC, followed by Fmoc deprotection by incubating DMF containing 20% pipeidine. Conjugation of Cy5.5-FRRG-NH2 and ertiprotafib was conducted as described above. The resulting Cy5.5-FRRG-ertiprotafib and FRRG-ertiprotafib were then dispersed in saline at a 1:99 wt% ratio to yield fluorescently labeled nanoassemblies.

HK-2 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 10⁵ and cultured for 24 h, after which they were treated with the Cy5.5-labeled SENs. The cells were fixed with fixative solution for 10 min, followed by nuclear staining with Hoechst 33,342 solution (H3570, Invitrogen) for 10 min in a dark conditions. Near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging was performed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica® TCS SP8 Laser Confocal Microscope). The fluorescence intensity in the NIRF images was quantified using ImagePro Plus software (Media Cybernetics). For cytotoxicity assays, HK-2 cells were treated with ertiprotafib or SENs at concentrations ranging from 0 to 10 µM for 24 h, followed by additional incubation with 10% CCK-8 solution.

To demonstrate the specific role of cathepsin B in the cleavage and activation of SENs, HK-2 cells were pre-treated with CA-074 (20 µM, Cat No. HY-103350, MedChemExpress®), a selective cathepsin B inhibitor, overnight at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Following cathepsin B inhibition, the tubular cells were simultaneously treated with hydrogen (H2O2) at 500 µM concentration along with either ertiprotafib or SENs for 1 h. FGRG-ertiprotafib was treated at the same concentration of 5 µM. After treatment, cells were fixed using fixative solution for 10 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min to allow antibody penetration into the cellular interior. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin solution, cells were incubated with anti-PTP1B antibody (EPR22468-6, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber, and additionally incubated with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibody for 1 h. Finally, cells were examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy to visualize PTP1B protein expression and cellular localization.

DCF-DA Assay

HK-2 cells were seeded at a density of 3.0 × 10⁵ cells and incubated for 24 h to allow proper cell attachment and growth. The cells were then divided into five experimental groups as follows: (i) Control (non-treated, NT), (ii) H2O2 only (PBS), (iii) H2O2 with 1 µM SENs, (iv) H2O2 with 2 µM SENs and (v) H2O2 with 5 µM SENs. For all groups receiving H2O2, cells were pretreated with 500 µM H2O2 for 1 h to induce oxidative stress. Subsequently, the respective concentrations of SENs were incubated for an additional 4 h. Subsequently, 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCF-DA) was diluted to a final concentration of 20 µM in phenol red-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) medium (-/-) and incubated with tubular cells for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired using an EVOS® M5000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) equipped with a 450 nm excitation filter to detect DCF-derived fluorescence.

Flow cytometry

HK-2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per 60 pi culture dish (SPL Life Science) and incubated with medium containing 500 µM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). After 1 h of treatment, both tubular cells were incubated with Annexin V (1 µL) and propidium iodide (PI, 2 µL) for 10 min in the dark at room temperature. For flow cytometric analysis, an unstained control was included to set the baseline for gating and compensation. Flow cytometric assessment of cell death was performed using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and flow cytometry data were acquired and analyzed to assess apoptotic and necrotic cell populations by FlowJo vX.0.7 Software.

Recovery of tight junction

HK-2 cells were seeded into confocal dishes at a density of at least 3.0 × 10⁵ cells per well in DMEM/F12 (+/+) medium for 2 weeks. Following H2O2 exposure, cells were washed twice with PBS and subjected to treatment with 5 µM ertiprotafib or SENs for 4 h. Permeabilization was performed by incubating with Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature, followed by blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin. ZO-1 (D6L1E, Alexa Fluor™ 647 conjugate, Cell Signaling Technology®) antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C. Finally, cells were examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy to visualize ZO-1 expression.

Biodistribution

All experiments involving animals were conducted in accordance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, approved number: KIST-IACUC-2023-013-1) at the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST). Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from OrientBio (Republic of Korea) and maintained under pathogen-free conditions. For pharmacokinetic (PK) analysis, blood samples were collected, centrifuged at 2,200 rpm for 20 min and analyzed for fluorescence intensity using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS; Lumina III, PerkinElmer). PK parameters were calculated by Prism 10 (GraphPad). The animals were kept on a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water, except for a fasting period on the day of bilateral kidney surgery. Prior to the procedure, anesthesia was induced using a combination of Zoletil and Rompun. AKI was induced by clamping the renal pedicles for 30 min to cause ischemia, followed by reperfusion (IRI). The in vivo behavior of SENs was assessed in healthy mice and IR-AKI models. Using AMI HT in vivo imaging instrument (Spectral Instruments Imaging), NIRF imaging was conducted following intravenous injection with Cy5.5-labeled SENs (5 mg/kg based on ertiprotafib content). Fluorescence intensity in the images was quantified using Aura In Vivo Imaging software. Time-dependent ex vivo NIRF imaging was conducted with major organs collected from mice. Cathepsin B and KIM-1 in renal tissues from healthy and AKI mice were visualized by immunostaining using antibodies against cathepsin B (sc-365558, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and KIM-1 (PA1-86790, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.).

Therapeutic efficacy evaluation

C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into following groups: (i) healthy mice (Sham), (ii) IR only (PBS), (iii) IR-AKI treated with ertiprotafib 5 mg/kg and iv-vii) IR-AKI treated with SENs (based on 1, 3, 5 or 10 mg/kg ertiprotafib concentration). Blood samples collected from mice were spun down to separate serum, which was analyzed for renal injury indicators, including BUN and creatinine. Supernatants derived from homogenized kidney tissue were examined for inflammatory cytokines—TNF-α (43907, BioLegend), IL-1β (MLB00C, R&D Systems), IL-6 (M6000B, R&D Systems) and IL-10 (DY217B, R&D Systems)—through ELISA assays. Furthermore, kidney tissue sections were subjected to histological staining using H&E (H9627, HT1102128, Sigma-Aldrich), PAS (355-N05865, VENTANA), F4/80 (MCA497G, Serotec) antibody, Ly6G (11-9668, eBioscience) antibody, kidney injury molecule-1 antibody (KIM-1; PA1-86790, Invitrogen), hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha antibody (HIF-1α; ab308433, Abcam), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin antibody (NGAL; ab216462, Abcam) or TUNEL to evaluate inflammation and tissue damage. Tubular injury features, such as necrosis, cast formation and loss of brush borders, were graded on a 0–4 scale, and the mean value was determined. All slide assessments were carried out under blinded conditions. For quantification, 5–6 high-power fields were randomly chosen per slide, and the proportion of positive staining was measured and compared across groups using Fiji ImageJ software (NIH).

In vivo safety analysis

For in vivo safety evaluation, healthy male C57BL/6 mice received intravenous injections of either ertiprotafib or SENs. Treatments were given as a single-dose (5 mg/kg based on ertiprotafib content) or as repeated doses (six times with 5 mg/kg based on ertiprotafib content). Body weight was recorded each day. On the seventh day, blood samples and major organs were collected for further analysis. Serum was tested for AST, ALT, LDH and CK levels, while structural alterations in major organs were examined by H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance between the two groups was analyzed using Student’s t test. In the case of more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, and multiple comparisons were performed using the Tukey–Kramer post-hoc test. Survival results were plotted using Kaplan–Meier curves and analyzed using the log-rank test. All results are presented as mean ± SD, and P values of < 0.05*, < 0.01**, < 0.001*** and < 0.0001**** were considered statistically significant, and ‘ns’ denotes not significant (P > 0.05).
