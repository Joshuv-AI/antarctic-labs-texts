Fluorinated Hexa‐Peri‐Hexabenzocoronene Derivatives‐Modified CNT Scaffolds Enabling Ultrahigh Capacitance in Hierarchical NiCu‐LDH Hybrid Supercapacitors
Authors: Hsieh‐Chih Chen, Pei‐Jung Liang, Mei‐Chia Chou, Chi‐Fang Sung, Hui Qi Wong, Chen‐Sheng Lo, Hsiao‐Ching Yang, Hung‐Ju Yen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving high capacitance while maintaining rapid charge transport and structural stability remains a major challenge in the design of battery‐type supercapacitor electrodes. Herein, a molecularly engineered strategy is presented for constructing hierarchical hybrid electrodes by integrating petal‐like NiCu‐LDH nanosheets onto 3D HBC‐x (x = H, F, OMe)‐functionalized CNT paper via a one‐step hydrothermal process. The incorporation of HBC effectively mitigates CNT agglomeration and constructs an interconnected conductive framework that enhances charge transport, shortens ion diffusion paths, and reduces internal resistance. Among the variants, fluorinated HBC‐F offers the most pronounced performance improvement, owing to the formation of polar semi‐ionic C–F bonds that elevate local charge density and boost redox kinetics. The resulting NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode achieves an ultrahigh specific capacitance of 5270 F g−1 at 1 A g−1, maintaining 80% retention even at 20 A g−1. Furthermore, an all‐solid‐state asymmetric device assembled with NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT as the positive electrode and CNT paper as the negative electrode delivers an energy density of 21.5 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 800 W kg−1 over a wide operating voltage window of 1.6 V. This study presents a novel molecular engineering approach to constructing hierarchical, high‐performance electrode architectures, offering a promising pathway for next‐generation supercapacitor technologies.

Introduction

In response to the increasing demand for flexible and portable electronics, such as smart watches, skin‐mounted biosensors, and foldable displays, there is a growing need for next‐generation energy storage devices that can deliver both high power density and long‐term durability. To effectively support such applications, these systems must not only exhibit exceptional electrochemical performance but also maintain operational stability under a wide range of mechanical and environmental stresses. Among the various contenders, supercapacitors (SCs) have emerged as highly promising candidates due to their unique advantages, including ultrafast charge–discharge rates, high specific power, and excellent cycling stability.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] However, the relatively low energy density of conventional SCs remains a major bottleneck, limiting their application in high energy output.[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] To address this limitation, hybrid supercapacitors (HSCs) have attracted considerable attention. Typically, HSCs integrate a battery‐type faradaic electrode (typically as the positive electrode) and a capacitive‐type electrode (commonly used as the negative electrode) to form an asymmetric configuration.[ 8 , 9 ] This design allows HSCs to simultaneously achieve high energy density (from the battery‐like electrode) and high power density (from the capacitor‐like electrode), while maintaining fast charge–discharge kinetics, long cycling stability, and high operational safety.[ 10 , 11 , 12 ]

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have been widely investigated as electrode materials for SCs owing to their high specific surface area, intrinsic porosity, and excellent electrical conductivity.[ 13 , 14 ] These features provide accessible ion transport channels and promote rapid charge transfer, both of which are essential for achieving high‐rate performance. Nevertheless, CNTs are prone to strong van der Waals interactions, which induce aggregation and bundling, thereby reducing the accessible surface area and impeding ion diffusion and electron transport.[ 15 , 16 ] This aggregation significantly compromises electrochemical performance and limits the practical utility of CNT‐based electrodes. To overcome this challenge, the integration of nanographene‐based modifiers, particularly hexa‐peri‐hexabenzocoronene (HBC), has emerged as a compelling strategy. HBC is a well‐defined polycyclic aromatic nanographene synthesized via bottom‐up chemical methods, allowing precise control over molecular structure and functionalization.[ 17 ] Compared with conventional graphene, HBC exhibits a higher density of edge sites, providing abundant electroactive sites for charge storage.[ 18 ] Its intrinsic π‐conjugated framework ensures high electrical conductivity, while its 3D rigidity and nanoscale dimensions effectively suppress restacking and aggregation, maintaining high surface area and accessible ion transport pathways. Importantly, HBC can be readily functionalized with various electron‐donating or ‐withdrawing groups to modulate its electronic properties and interfacial interactions with other components in the electrode architecture.[ 19 ] For instance, Park and colleagues employed twisted fluorinated HBC as anode materials in lithium‐ and sodium‐ion batteries, achieving a specific capacity of 160 mAh g−1 at a current density of 100 mA g−1.[ 20 ] Yen et al. demonstrated that 3D HBC‐OMe derivatives delivered an exceptionally high specific capacity of 950 mAh g−1 in lithium‐ion batteries.[ 19 ] Moreover, HBC‐OMe was also reported as a pseudocapacitive anode material for lithium‐ion storage with a specific capacity of 100 mAh g−1 at 8 A g−1 and outstanding cycling stability exceeding 13 000 cycles with negligible capacity degradation.[ 21 ]

Among various battery‐type materials explored for next‐generation SCs, layered double hydroxides (LDHs) have garnered significant attention owing to their intrinsically porous lamellar structure, large interlayer spacing, and rich redox activity.[ 22 , 23 , 24 ] Their unique 2D architecture provides an extended surface area that enhances electrolyte accessibility, while the interlayer galleries facilitate efficient ion intercalation and diffusion.[ 25 , 26 ] Additionally, the incorporation of transition metal cations (e.g., Ni2+, Co2+, Mn2+, Fe3+) within the brucite‐like layers introduces abundant redox‐active sites, which contribute significantly to pseudocapacitive charge storage via reversible faradaic reactions.[ 27 ] Despite these intrinsic advantages, pristine LDHs face key limitations that hinder their electrochemical performance, particularly at high current densities. Their poor electrical conductivity restricts electron mobility, while severe nanoparticle aggregation and structural degradation during cycling further compromise their rate capability and long‐term stability.[ 28 , 29 ] Consequently, extensive efforts have been devoted to designing LDH‐based hybrid architectures with enhanced conductivity, surface area, and structural integrity. Among these, integrating LDHs with conductive carbonaceous frameworks, such as graphene, activated carbon, and CNTs, has proven to be an effective strategy to mitigate agglomeration, improve electron transport, and maintain high electrolyte accessibility.[ 30 , 31 ] Moreover, engineering advanced morphologies, such as ultrathin nanosheets, hollow structures, and hierarchical architectures, has enabled further improvements in electrochemical kinetics and durability.[ 32 ]

Building on these design principles, we present a novel strategy for fabricating hierarchical NiCu‐LDH nanosheets uniformly anchored onto HBC‐x (x = H, F, OMe)‐functionalized CNT paper via a one‐step hydrothermal method. This approach achieves direct growth of petal‐like NiCu‐LDH architectures on a conductive and robust scaffold, forming a highly integrated electrode structure for high‐performance SC applications. Notably, this work reports, for the first time, the use of functionalized nanographene derivatives, specifically HBC‐x, as molecularly engineered dispersants within the CNT matrix for SC systems. The incorporation of HBC into the CNT network plays a critical role in addressing CNT agglomeration caused by van der Waals attractions. Owing to its extended π‐conjugation and inherent 3D rigidity, HBC assembles into nanostructures that effectively separate CNTs, forming an interconnected conductive scaffold. This framework enhances electrolyte diffusion, increases the electrochemically active surface area, and maintains structural integrity during cycling. Additionally, the incorporation of Cu2+ into the Ni‐based LDH lattice—enabled by the similar ionic radii of Ni2+ and Cu2+—modifies the electronic structure, enhancing electrical conductivity and promoting synergistic redox behavior, despite Cu2+’s limited standalone electrochemical activity in alkaline media.[ 32 , 33 , 34 ] In this study, the optimal Cu/Ni feeding molar ratio of 1:2 was found to produce a highly active NiCu‐LDH phase. Among the synthesized materials, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT hybrid delivers the highest performance. The introduction of fluorine into the HBC backbone significantly boosts electron transport and redox kinetics via electron‐withdrawing effects and semi‐ionic C–F interactions, leading to a remarkable specific capacitance of 5270 F g−1 at 1 A g−1. An asymmetric all‐solid‐state HSC was assembled using NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT as the positive electrode and CNT paper as the negative electrode. The device operates within a wide voltage window of 1.6 V and delivers a high energy density of 21.5 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 800 W kg−1. These results underscore the potential of molecularly engineered HBC‐modified CNT frameworks for the development of high‐performance hybrid SCs.

Results and Discussion

Design Rationale and Application Prospects

To tailor the electronic environment and optimize the structural characteristics of each molecular building block, a variety of electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing substituents were covalently introduced onto the HBC core, yielding a series of derivatives denoted as HBC‐x (where x = H, F, or OMe) (Figure 1a). The incorporation of these peripheral substituents on the HBC core enables systematic modulation of the π‐electron density, thereby allowing fine‐tuning of intermolecular interactions, electronic conductivity, and compatibility with surrounding electroactive materials. Furthermore, the intrinsic 3D twisted geometry of HBC‐x derivatives, characterized by an average torsion angle of approximately 138°, plays a pivotal role in suppressing the agglomeration and restacking of CNTs. The HBC‐x molecules intercalate efficiently within the CNT network, acting as molecular spacers that prevent close packing and maintain an open, interconnected architecture. This design significantly enhances ion‐accessible surface area and facilitates rapid ion diffusion throughout the electrode matrix. As a result, the HBC‐x/CNT hybrid composites exhibit markedly improved electrochemical performance, including higher specific capacitance, superior rate capability, and excellent long‐term cycling stability.

a) Chemical structures and DFT‐optimized geometries of HBC‐H, HBC‐OMe, and HBC‐F molecules. b) Schematic illustration of the synthetic strategy for constructing NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐x/CNT hierarchical composites.

The fabrication protocol for the hierarchical NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐x/CNT composites is schematically depicted in Figure 1b. Initially, freestanding HBC‐x/CNT papers were fabricated via a vacuum‐assisted filtration method. In this step, HBC‐x and CNTs were co‐dispersed in ethanol using ultrasonication, which facilitated the breakdown of agglomerates and ensured homogeneous mixing. The resulting stable suspension was filtered through membrane paper to obtain a flexible, interconnected conductive film, which was then dried in an oven at 60 °C overnight. The incorporation of merely 2 wt% HBC‐x was found to significantly enhance the dispersion stability of CNTs and improve film formation, thereby yielding a structurally robust and electrically conductive HBC‐x/CNT paper. Subsequently, the as‐prepared HBC‐x/CNT papers were immersed in a precursor solution containing nickel nitrate, copper nitrate, urea, and ammonium fluoride. This solution provided the necessary metal cations (Ni2+ and Cu2+) and structure‐directing agents to facilitate LDH formation. The composite was then subjected to a hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 4 h, during which time vertically aligned NiCu‐LDH nanosheets nucleated and grew in situ on the surface of the HBC‐x/CNT scaffold. After thorough rinsing and vacuum drying, a petal‐like nanostructure was obtained, wherein the ultrathin LDH nanosheets were uniformly anchored onto the conductive framework. This hierarchical architecture offers several key advantages: 1) the vertically oriented nanosheets provide a high electrochemically active surface area; 2) the intimate interface between the LDH and conductive scaffold facilitates efficient electron transport; and 3) the open mesostructure shortens ion diffusion lengths and ensures rapid electrolyte accessibility.

Surface Morphology and Microstructural Characterization

The surface morphology and microstructural features of the synthesized NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT, NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐H/CNT, and NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐OMe/CNT composites were systematically investigated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Figure 2a–c. All three composites exhibit a uniform distribution of well‐defined petal‐like nanosheets anchored onto the HBC‐x/CNT substrate. These nanosheets are intricately interconnected to form an open 3D porous network that provides abundant electrochemically active sites and promotes efficient ion transport pathways. Among the series, NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT exhibits markedly thinner, more densely aligned nanosheets with higher packing density compared to its HBC‐H and HBC‐OMe analogues. Such structural refinement is anticipated to enhance electrolyte penetration, facilitate rapid ion diffusion, and improve the electrochemical kinetics of the electrode (vide infra). In sharp contrast, NiCu‐LDH@CNT (Figure S1, Supporting Information) adopts a curled, intertwined flower‐like architecture with poorly defined nanosheet boundaries. This morphology offers a significantly lower density of accessible active sites and reduced electrolyte–electrode interfacial contact, which is expected to limit its electrochemical performance relative to the HBC‐functionalized counterparts. High‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT composite (Figure 2d) reveals distinct lattice fringes corresponding to interplanar spacings of 0.206 and 0.385 nm, which can be indexed to the (006) crystal plane of NiCu‐LDH and the (002) plane of CNT network, respectively, confirming the successful integration of NiCu‐LDH onto the conductive CNT substrate. The strong interfacial contact between the LDH nanosheets and CNT substrate is anticipated to enhance electron transport and mechanical stability during repeated charge–discharge cycles. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (inset of Figure 2d) exhibits concentric diffraction rings, indicative of the polycrystalline nature of the composite.[ 35 ]

The crystalline features of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐x/CNT composites were further investigated by X‐ray diffraction (XRD), as shown in Figure 2e. All samples display a broad diffraction peak centered around 2θ ≈ 25.5°, corresponding to the (002) reflection of the CNT structure.[ 13 ] In addition, the characteristic diffraction peaks at 13.9°, 22.0°, 35.8°, and 46.8° can be indexed to the (003), (006), (012), and (015) planes of the NiCu‐LDH phase, respectively, in accordance with the standard JCPDS PDF#09‐0418.[ 34 , 36 ] Interestingly, despite the incorporation of 2 wt% HBC‐x derivatives, no additional diffraction signals attributable to HBC‐x are observed. This absence is likely due to two factors: 1) the low concentration of HBC‐x falls below the detection threshold of the instrument, and 2) the HBC‐x molecules are presumed to adopt a disordered or amorphous configuration when embedded within the CNT network, precluding the formation of well‐defined Bragg reflections. These factors are also consistent with the absence of identifiable HBC‐x lattice fringes in the HRTEM images.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were conducted to assess the textural properties of the synthesized composites (Figure 2f and Figure S2, Supporting Information). All samples exhibit type IV isotherms with a hysteresis loop, characteristic of mesoporous materials. Among them, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT composite exhibits the most favorable porosity, with an average pore diameter of 12.6 nm and the highest specific surface area of 199.67 m2 g−1. The combination of a large surface area and mesoporous structure promotes rapid electrolyte access, shortens ion transport distances, and minimizes charge‐transfer resistance, collectively contributing to enhanced electrochemical performance.[ 37 ] Additionally, Raman spectroscopy (Figure S3, Supporting Information) was employed to probe the structural characteristics and defect density of the composite electrodes. Notably, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode displays the highest I D/I G ratio among all samples, indicating an increased degree of structural disorder. Such disorder is often linked to the presence of abundant defect sites and edge‐plane terminations, which can serve as highly accessible electrochemically active centers for charge storage. Moreover, the introduction of fluorinated HBC moieties may facilitate charge delocalization and alter the local electronic environment, further enhancing redox kinetics at the electrode/electrolyte interface. This synergistic combination of defect‐rich architecture and tailored surface chemistry is expected to contribute significantly to the superior capacitive performance of NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode. Furthermore, energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Figure 2g) confirms the homogeneous distribution of Ni and Cu elements throughout the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT composite, verifying the uniform incorporation of the bimetallic LDH phase into the hierarchical framework.

The chemical composition and oxidation states of elements within the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT composite were further investigated by X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The full survey spectrum (Figure 3a) confirms the presence of Ni, Cu, O, C, and F elements, consistent with the designed composition of the hierarchical hybrid architecture. The high‐resolution Ni 2p spectrum (Figure 3b) exhibits characteristic spin‐orbit doublets at 854.9 eV (Ni 2p3/2) and 873.2 eV (Ni 2p1/2), each accompanied by well‐defined satellite peaks at 861.1 and 879.3 eV, respectively. A smaller peak at 857.7 eV is assigned to Ni3+ species, indicating the coexistence of mixed‐valence Ni2+/Ni3+ states.[ 38 ] This mixed‐valence nature is advantageous for enhancing redox activity and promoting charge transfer, thereby contributing to the pseudocapacitive behavior of the electrode. In parallel, the high‐resolution Cu 2p spectrum (Figure 3c) reveals four distinct peaks: Cu 2p3/2 at 932.9 eV, Cu 2p1/2 at 952.7 eV, along with shake‐up satellite features at 941.8 and 961.6 eV, characteristic of Cu2+ oxidation states.[ 39 ] Moreover, the Ni 2p and Cu 2p spectra of NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT show more prominent shifts toward lower binding energies compared to those in HBC‐H and HBC‐OMe analogues (Figure S4, Supporting Information), suggesting stronger electron interactions due to the electron‐withdrawing effect of fluorine. This indicates enhanced charge transfer between LDH nanosheets and the fluorinated π‐system.

a) XPS survey spectrum and corresponding deconvoluted b) Ni 2p, c) Cu 2p, d) O 1s, e) C 1s, and f) F 1s spectra of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT composite.

The O 1s core‐level spectrum (Figure 3d) is deconvoluted into two primary components. The peak centered at 530.9 eV corresponds to hydroxide groups (OH−), integral to the LDH structure, while the shoulder at 533.0 eV is assigned to adsorbed water molecules. These features collectively reflect the hydrated nature of the NiCu‐LDH phase and its potential for engaging in proton‐coupled redox reactions. The C 1s spectrum (Figure 3e) reveals four distinct peaks located at 283.9, 285.4, 286.7, and 289.9 eV, corresponding to C–C, C–O, O═C–O, and C–F bonding environments, respectively. The presence of oxygen‐ and fluorine‐containing functional groups suggests enhanced chemical compatibility and electronic coupling between the HBC‐F moieties and the CNT network. These interactions are expected to reinforce interfacial adhesion and electron transport across the heterostructure. Further insight into the fluorine environment is provided by the F 1s spectrum (Figure 3f), which displays a dominant peak at 685.7 eV, attributable to semi‐ionic C–F bonds.[ 40 ] In sp2‐hybridized carbon systems, C–F bonding can adopt either covalent or semi‐ionic character. The semi‐ionic configuration represents a partially delocalized electronic state with enhanced bond polarity, imparting a partial positive charge on the carbon site. This increased polarity is anticipated to improve electron delocalization and interfacial conductivity, thereby contributing to the superior electrochemical behavior observed for the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT composite.

Electrochemical Performance Evaluation

The electrochemical properties of the synthesized composite electrodes were systematically investigated by cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) in a standard three‐electrode configuration. All measurements were conducted in 6 m KOH aqueous electrolyte, employing a Pt plate as the counter electrode and an Hg/HgO electrode as the reference. Figure 4a compares the CV curves of NiCu‐LDH@CNT and NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrodes at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 within a potential window of 0–0.6 V. Both electrodes exhibit well‐defined redox peaks, indicative of reversible faradaic processes characteristic of battery‐type electrodes.[ 41 ] These redox reactions are primarily associated with the redox transitions of Ni and Cu species within the LDH framework. Notably, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode displays a substantially larger CV area compared to its CNT‐only counterpart, suggesting a significant enhancement in specific capacitance upon incorporation of the HBC‐F functional component. This enhancement can be attributed to the synergistic architecture of the composite: the conductive HBC‐F/CNT framework forms an interconnected network that facilitates rapid electron transport and ion diffusion, while concurrently reducing internal resistance. Meanwhile, the NiCu‐LDH nanosheets provide a high surface area and abundant electroactive sites, which support efficient faradaic reactions and contribute to superior charge storage. The GCD profiles further corroborate the capacitive properties of the materials (Figure 4b). Both electrodes exhibit distinct voltage plateaus during charge–discharge cycles, which correspond well with the redox couples observed in CV, again confirming their battery‐type characteristics. Notably, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode delivers a significantly longer discharge time relative to the NiCu‐LDH@CNT, indicating enhanced charge storage capacity.

Comparison of c) CV curves and d) GCD profiles of NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐H/CNT, NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT, and NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐OMe/CNT electrodes. e) GCD curves of NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode at varied current densities. f) Specific capacitance and coulombic efficiency as a function of current density. g) Nyquist plots with the corresponding Randles equivalent circuit shown in the inset. h) Bode plot of impedance phase angle versus frequency.

To evaluate the influence of different HBC functional groups, CV measurements were also conducted for NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐H/CNT, NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐OMe/CNT, and NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT (Figure 4c). Among these three, NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT shows the largest CV area, suggesting superior electrochemical performance. The results can be ascribed to the strong adsorption affinity of the fluorinated HBC derivative toward the CNT surface, which promotes a uniform and stable dispersion of the composite across the electrode. Such homogeneous distribution ensures intimate contact between the LDH nanosheets and the conductive scaffold, thereby enhancing electron mobility and minimizing interfacial resistance. Additionally, the incorporation of fluorine atoms enhances the electronic coupling within the composite matrix, improving electrical conductivity and structural stability during extended cycling. The NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode exhibits pronounced redox activity, as evidenced by the sharp and symmetric redox peaks. These features result in an outstanding specific capacitance of 4501 F g−1, demonstrating its exceptional energy storage capability. The dominant charge storage process is primarily governed by reversible pseudocapacitive behavior, originating from fast and reversible redox transitions of Ni2+/Ni3+ and Cu2+/Cu3+ redox couples, as represented by the following reactions:[ 42 ] (1) NiOH2+OH−↔NiOOH+H2O+e− (2) CuOH2+OH−↔CuOOH+H2O+e−

Upon charging, Ni(OH)2 is electrochemically oxidized to NiOOH via a one‐step electron transfer, while Cu2+ in Cu(OH)2 is partially oxidized to Cu3+, forming CuOOH. These processes occur at the electrode–electrolyte interface and are highly reversible under alkaline conditions. The use of a 6 m KOH aqueous electrolyte facilitates rapid OH− ion mobility and ensures fast charge compensation during the redox transitions, thereby contributing to the high‐rate capability of the electrode. Consistent with the CV results, the GCD profiles (Figure 4d) confirm the superior performance of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode, which exhibits the longest discharge duration and highest specific capacitance among all tested materials.

The GCD curves of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT hybrid electrode at various current densities are shown in Figure 4e. The evident nonlinearity of the charge–discharge curves across all tested current densities is indicative of battery‐type behavior, primarily governed by faradaic redox processes. Remarkably, the electrode retains approximately 80% of its initial specific capacitance (from 5270 to 4236 F g−1) as the current density increases from 1 to 20 A g−1, highlighting excellent rate capability. This retention demonstrates the electrode's ability to maintain efficient charge storage and rapid ion/electron transport even under high‐current operating conditions. Moreover, the nearly symmetric charge–discharge profiles across all current densities point to high coulombic efficiency and minimal energy dissipation, reflecting the highly reversible nature of the redox reactions involved. This suggests that the composite architecture facilitates fast interfacial charge transfer and stable electrochemical cycling. A direct comparison with the unmodified NiCu‐LDH@CNT electrode underscores the beneficial role of HBC‐F functionalization. As shown in Figure 4f, the specific capacitance of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode is approximately twice that of the pristine counterpart, demonstrating the significant impact of HBC‐F molecular integration on charge storage performance. The superior performance of the HBC‐F‐modified electrode can be attributable to several factors: 1) the incorporation of HBC‐F enhances π–π stacking and interfacial contact between the NiCu‐LDH nanosheets and CNTs, facilitating charge transfer and improving electronic conductivity; 2) the electron‐withdrawing fluorine substituents modulate the electronic structure of the HBC core, enhancing redox activity and improving charge‐transfer stability; and 3) the presence of HBC‐F prevents CNT restacking, thereby preserving accessible surface area and maintaining the availability of electroactive sites. In contrast, the NiCu‐LDH@CNT electrode retains only ≈75% of its initial capacitance at 20 A g−1, emphasizing the importance of HBC‐F in achieving enhanced rate capability. Furthermore, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode exhibits nearly ideal coulombic efficiency (≈100%) across high current densities, confirming minimal energy loss and excellent reversibility during charge–discharge cycling.

EIS was further employed to elucidate the superior electrochemical behavior of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode. The Nyquist plots of the three targeted electrodes, along with their corresponding equivalent circuit model, are presented in Figure 4g and Table S2 (Supporting Information). All three electrodes exhibit a nearly identical intercept at the real axis, indicating a similarly low equivalent series resistance (R s), which reflects minimal intrinsic ohmic losses from the electrolyte, active materials, and current collector. Notably, none of the electrodes display a pronounced semicircle in the high‐frequency region, suggesting minimal charge transfer resistance (R ct) and efficient electron transport across the electrode/electrolyte interface. This low resistance can be attributed to two key structural features: 1) the excellent intrinsic electrical conductivity of the CNT framework, and 2) the intimate integration of mesoporous NiCu‐LDH nanosheets directly grown on the CNT paper, which significantly shortens ion diffusion pathways and ensures fast electrochemical kinetics. Among them, NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode shows the smallest R ct value of 0.05 Ω, substantially lower than those of H‐ (R ct = 0.19 Ω) and OMe‐modified (R ct = 0.38 Ω) counterparts, confirming that the F‐functionalized scaffold most effectively promotes interfacial charge transfer. This remarkable reduction in R ct is ascribed to the incorporation of fluorinated HBC moieties, which facilitate enhanced electron delocalization and stronger π–π interactions with CNTs, resulting in improved interfacial conductivity. The semi‐ionic C–F bonds present in the HBC‐F structure further promote charge accumulation and efficient charge transport by introducing localized dipoles and enhancing electrostatic interactions with electrolyte cations.[ 43 ]

The Bode phase plot (Figure 4h and Figure S5, Supporting Information) reveals that the phase angle of NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT at low frequencies approaches −71.2°, confirming its predominantly battery‐type nature. For comparison, the maximum phase angles of NiCu‐LDH@CNT, NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐H/CNT, and NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐OMe/CNT are −75.9°, −74.1°, and −77.8°, respectively. Although all values fall below the ideal −90° expected for a pure capacitor, the deviation is consistent with pseudocapacitive behavior involving surface redox reactions in addition to electrical double‐layer capacitance.[ 13 , 44 , 45 ] To further assess rate capability, the characteristic frequency (f 0) corresponding to a phase angle of −45° was used to determine the dielectric relaxation time constant (τ 0), where τ 0 = 1/f 0. A smaller τ 0 signifies faster ion diffusion and charge transfer kinetics. The NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode exhibits an f 0 of 26.1 Hz, yielding a τ 0 of 0.038 s. This is significantly lower than the τ 0 values of the other three electrodes (see Figure S5 in the Supporting Information), demonstrating rapid charge–discharge response and exceptional rate capability. Moreover, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode exhibits outstanding cycling durability, retaining 98% of its initial capacitance even after 10 000 continuous charge–discharge cycles (Figure S6, Supporting Information), thereby confirming its excellent long‐term electrochemical stability.

Theoretical Simulations and Computational Insights

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations reveal that the adsorption affinity of HBC‐x molecules on CNT surfaces follows the trend HBC‐F > HBC‐OMe > HBC‐H (Figure 5a–c). Among them, HBC‐F demonstrates the most extensive and uniform adsorption on the CNT network, which can be attributed to its semi‐ionic C–F bonds and strong dipole interactions with the CNT surface. These interactions promote more intimate π–π stacking and increase surface coverage, which is crucial for maximizing ion‐accessible surface area and enhancing electron transport pathways. To further investigate interfacial ion coordination, simplified MD models incorporating Ni2+ and Cu2+ ions in aqueous electrolytes were constructed (Figure 5d–f). Each model consisted of a single HBC molecule (HBC‐F, HBC‐OMe, or HBC‐H), multiple randomly oriented small‐diameter CNTs, and electrolyte ions, designed to emulate the dynamic interface encountered during electrochemical cycling. These simulations aimed to probe the coordination affinity of each HBC functional group for transition metal ions and to monitor any ion clustering or aggregation phenomena that may occur at the interface. In the HBC‐F/CNT system (Figure 5d), fluorinated HBC molecules exhibit strong anchoring on CNT surfaces while forming close‐range, stable interactions with Ni2+ (brown spheres) and Cu2+ (green spheres) ions. This observation suggests a well‐defined coordination environment, which may facilitate more effective ion trapping and redox activity. Conversely, HBC‐H (Figure 5e) and HBC‐OMe (Figure 5f) systems show more dispersed ion distributions, indicating weaker electrostatic interactions and less favorable ion coordination geometries.

Final configuration snapshots obtained from the last 30 ns of MD simulations for a) HBC‐F/CNT, b) HBC‐H/CNT, and c) HBC‐OMe/CNT systems. d–f) Representative MD snapshots of simplified composite systems comprising a single HBC molecule, multiple short CNT segments, and solvated Ni2+/Cu2+ ions. Ni2+ ions are depicted as brown spheres, while Cu2+ ions are shown as green spheres. g) Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis between HBC molecules and CNTs. h) Statistical quantification of the number of HBC molecules adsorbed within 5 Å of CNT surfaces. i) Total energy evaluations of the three systems.

These trends are quantitatively confirmed by radial distribution function (RDF) analysis (Figure 5g) and coordination number calculations (Figure 5h). HBC‐F shows the highest peak intensity in the RDF curve and the largest number of molecules within 5 Å of the CNT surface, validating its superior adsorption strength and metal ion binding propensity. However, total energy calculations (Figure 5i) reveal a counterintuitive trend: the HBC‐OMe/CNT system exhibits the highest structural energy and lowest thermodynamic stability, despite showing intermediate adsorption strength in MD simulations. This apparent discrepancy is likely attributed to the intrinsic behavior of HBC‐OMe molecules, which exhibit a higher tendency toward self‐aggregation rather than adsorption onto the CNT surface. The electron‐donating ‐OMe substituents enhance intermolecular interactions among HBC‐OMe units, diminishing their interfacial affinity with CNTs. As a result, localized molecular clustering occurs, leading to suboptimal dispersion and unfavorable conformational relaxation. Importantly, electrochemical measurements align more closely with the trend of structural stability rather than adsorption strength alone. The specific capacitance of the composite electrodes follows the order: NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT > NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐H/CNT > NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐OMe/CNT, matching the sequence of decreasing structural energy stability from Figure 5i. While HBC‐F offers both high adsorption and structural stability, the HBC‐OMe variant suffers from reduced long‐range ordering and increased energetic penalties, which hinder charge transport and ion diffusion, thereby lowering capacitance.

Charge Storage Mechanism and Asymmetric SC Devices

To elucidate the underlying charge storage mechanism, the correlation between redox peak currents and scan rates was examined. As shown in the Randles–Sevcik plot (Figure 6a), a linear relationship between the redox peak currents and the square root of the scan rates confirms that the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode exhibits reversible and diffusion‐controlled electrochemical behavior even at high scan rates. To further explore the charge storage kinetics, the power‐law relationship was applied:[ 46 , 47 ] (3) i=avb where i(v) is the peak current, v is the scan rate, and b is a fitting parameter indicative of the charge storage mechanism. The derived b‐value of 0.516 (Figure 6b) suggests a predominately diffusion‐controlled, battery‐type behavior. To deconvolute the contributions from capacitive and diffusion‐controlled processes, a kinetic analysis based on the following equation was conducted:[ 48 ] (4) iv=icap+idiff=k1v+k2v1/2 where k 1 and k 2 represent capacitive‐controlled (k 1 v) and diffusion‐controlled (k 2 v 1/2) contributions, respectively. As shown in Figure 6c, the diffusion‐controlled process dominates, contributing 77.2% of the total charge storage, with capacitive processes accounting for the remaining 22.8%, confirming that the overall capacitance of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode is primarily governed by redox reactions. Additionally, the relative contributions of surface‐controlled and diffusion‐controlled processes at various scan rates are illustrated in Figure 6d. As the scan rate increases, the relative contribution of the diffusion‐controlled component gradually declines (70.6% at 10 mV s−1, 63.5% at 20 mV s−1, and 59.1% at 50 mV s−1). This trend can be attributed to kinetic limitations at higher scan rates, where the reduced interaction time between electrolyte ions and redox‐active sites restricts faradaic reactions. Conversely, at lower scan rates, ions have sufficient time to diffuse into the electrode matrix, enabling deep redox activity and maximizing diffusion‐limited charge storage. Despite this shift in mechanism at higher scan rates, the overall charge storage behavior remains predominantly diffusion‐governed, indicating the battery‐type nature of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode and its ability to sustain redox reactions under rapid cycling conditions.

To evaluate the practical viability of the tailored electrode, an all‐solid‐state asymmetric supercapacitor (ASC) device was assembled using NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT as the positive electrode and CNT paper as the negative electrode (Figure 7a). The working potential window was optimized based on the electrochemical behavior of both electrodes. CV curves of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT//CNT ASC device (Figure S7, Supporting Information) demonstrate stable performance up to 1.6 V. The well‐preserved CV shapes across different potential ranges confirm excellent electrochemical reversibility. Figure 7b shows the CV curves of the ASC device recorded at scan rates from 5 to 50 mV s−1. The highly retained curve shapes at elevated scan rates reflect outstanding rate capability and rapid charge transfer kinetics. GCD measurements at various current densities (Figure 7c) further confirm a high specific capacitance of 60.5 F g−1 at 1 A g−1. The non‐linear GCD profiles reaffirm the battery‐type redox mechanism of the positive electrode. The energy density and power density of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT//CNT ASC device, along with its potential window and cycling stability, are summarized in Figure 7d. Notably, it delivers an energy density of 21.5 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 800 W kg−1, outperforming several previously reported systems, including NiCo‐LDH/CNT (16.3 Wh kg−1 at 650 W kg−1),[ 49 ] NiCu‐LDH/MXene (12.4 Wh kg−1 at 500 W kg−1),[ 50 ] 4M‐P@NiCo‐LDH//AC (18.1 Wh kg−1 at 750 W kg−1),[ 51 ] and GRH‐NiCo2S4 (19 Wh kg−1 at 703 W kg−1).[ 52 ] These results confirm that the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT//CNT ASC device is capable of delivering both exceptional energy storage capability and power performance, making it a strong candidate for advanced energy storage applications. In addition, the CV curves of the ASC device recorded at different bending angles (Figure S8, Supporting Information) display almost identical shapes and integrated areas, indicating negligible performance loss during mechanical deformation. These results confirm the outstanding mechanical robustness and flexibility of the composite electrode, suggesting its strong potential for application in flexible and foldable electronic devices. Long‐term cycling stability, a decisive parameter for evaluating the practical applicability of electrochemical energy storage devices, was systematically examined. As shown in Figure 7e, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT//CNT ASC device demonstrates remarkable durability, retaining 96% of its initial capacitance after 10 000 consecutive charge–discharge cycles at a current density of 10 A g−1. Comparatively, the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐H/CNT//CNT and NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐OMe/CNT//CNT counterparts maintain slightly lower capacitance retentions of 93% and 91%, respectively (Figure S9, Supporting Information). The superior cycling performance of the HBC‐F‐functionalized electrode can be ascribed to its stronger interfacial interactions with both the CNT scaffold and the NiCu‐LDH nanosheets, which help suppress structural degradation and preserve electrode integrity during repeated ion insertion/extraction processes. Furthermore, the practical utility of the NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT//CNT device was demonstrated by its ability to continuously power a red LED for more than 3 min.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a series of 3D HBC‐x derivatives bearing different functional groups via a bottom‐up synthetic strategy, and integrated them into CNT frameworks to construct advanced hybrid electrodes for high‐performance SCs. The incorporation of HBC moieties within the CNT network not only enhanced electronic conductivity through strong π–π interactions but also facilitated the formation of a stable self‐assembled hierarchical architecture. This architecture effectively mitigated CNT restacking and preserved structural stability under long‐term electrochemical cycling. Among the synthesized variants, the HBC‐F/CNT hybrid electrode exhibited the most outstanding performance. Structural characterizations revealed that the incorporation of electron‐withdrawing fluorine groups into the peripheral aromatic rings of HBC molecules induced the formation of polar semi‐ionic C–F bonds, which promoted localized positive charge accumulation and significantly enhanced cation adsorption capacity. Molecular dynamics simulations further corroborated that HBC‐F exhibits the strongest interfacial binding to CNTs and favorable coordination with Ni2+/Cu2+ ions, thus achieving an optimal balance between interfacial stability and ion‐accessible active sites. The NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT electrode delivered an ultrahigh specific capacitance of 5270 F g−1 at 1 A g−1, and retained 4236 F g−1 even at 20 A g−1, demonstrating excellent rate capability. Furthermore, an all‐solid‐state ASC device assembled using NiCu‐LDH@HBC‐F/CNT as the positive electrode and CNT paper as the negative electrode delivered a wide operating voltage window of 1.6 V. The device achieved a high energy density of 21.5 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 800 W kg−1. This study not only elucidates the critical role of molecular functionalization in modulating the interfacial chemistry and electrochemical behavior of carbon‐based electrodes but also provides a rational and scalable design strategy for high‐performance SCs via precise molecular engineering of π‐conjugated building blocks.
