Risk factors and outcomes of secondary co-infections in hospitalised COVID-19 patients in Malaysia: a case control study
Authors: Nurfarah Lydia Hambali, Nik Mohd Noor Nik Zuraina, Yean Yean Chan, Zakuan Zainy Deris, Wei Chuan Chua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospitalised COVID-19 patients are susceptible to co-infections. This study aims to evaluate the risk factors and outcomes of secondary co-infections in hospitalised COVID-19 patients in Malaysia. A total of 929 COVID-19 patients were admitted to our centre from January 2021 to December 2022. Thirty-four cases of secondary co-infections among hospitalised COVID-19 patients were identified using standard microbiology culture. A control group of 136 hospitalised COVID-19 patients without secondary infections, admitted during the same period, was randomly selected. Additionally, 48 archived COVID-19 samples were analysed for primary co-infections involving 33 respiratory pathogens using multiplex real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR).ResultsThe prevalence of secondary co-infections in hospitalised COVID-19 patients was 3.7%. Thirty-nine pathogens were isolated where Acinetobacter baumannii and Candida spp. were among the commonest isolated pathogens (4 isolates each, 10.3%). Significant factors associated with secondary co-infections included longer hospital stay (p ≤ 0.001) and hypertension (p = 0.032). Laboratory findings associated with secondary co-infections were thrombocytopenia (p = 0.021) and elevated alkaline phosphatase (p = 0.024). Among patients with secondary co-infections, 52.9% died, compared to only 14.7% in the control group (p = 0.008), highlighting the poor clinical outcomes of patients with co-infections. Secondary co-infections were also significantly associated with ICU admission (p = 0.045). Out of 48 archived samples, 32 samples (66.7%) were found to have positive respiratory pathogens of primary co-infection.ConclusionAlthough secondary co-infections occurred in a relatively small subset of hospitalised COVID-19 patients, their profound association with fatal outcomes underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance when these specific risk factors are present.Keywords: COVID-19, co-infections, prevalence, risk factors, outcomes

Background

The recent COVID-19 pandemic has taught many important lessons in medical vigilance and infectious disease management. Among patients who died from COVID-19, up to 50% had secondary bacterial or fungal infections, or co-infections with other respiratory viruses [1]. Although majority of individuals infected with COVID-19 developed mild diseases and not requiring hospitalisation, certain populations including those with comorbidities, specific risk factors, and the elderly are at higher risk for more severe disease and hospital admission. Co-infections among hospitalised COVID-19 patients are more common than previously thought [2, 3].

A study focusing on the risk factors, clinical and laboratory characteristics, and outcomes of coinfections in hospitalised COVID-19 patients would provide valuable insights into demographic patterns and prognostic indicators specifically for Southeast Asian countries. The recognition of risk factors, both host-specific and pathogen specific, coupled with early identification of patients with severe disease and rapid laboratory workflow (rapid pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing) could allow for early diagnosis and management of co-infections in COVID-19 patients to improve the clinical outcomes. Furthermore, analysis of causative agents, antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, and antimicrobial prescription practices may also provide useful information to guide more appropriate and effective anti-infective therapy in patients with COVID-19 co-infections.

We aim to conduct a study to investigate the prevalence, risk factors, and outcomes of hospitalised COVID-19 patients with secondary co-infections in our centre. The findings may provide invaluable insight into the epidemiology of co-infections of in COVID-19 patients in Malaysia and contribute to the preparedness for future pandemic planning.

Methods

Study design

Approval from the local institution ethical committee was obtained for this study (Ref. no : USM/JEPeM/COVID-19-44). This is a retrospective observational study that analysed clinical data of hospitalised COVID-19 patients from January 2021 to December 2022 at a tertiary healthcare centre in Northeastern Malaysia with 800 beds. A list of COVID-19 patients admitted during this period was screened based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria were all patients hospitalised for COVID-19 between January 2021 and December 2022. For the case group, all patients admitted for COVID-19 with co-pathogen(s) detected during the course of care for SARS-CoV-2 infection were included. The controls were included at 1:4 of case: control, consisted of randomly selected from patients admitted for COVID-19 who did not have any co-pathogens detected during the study period. The diagnosis of COVID-19 was confirmed by RT-PCR (LyteStar, Malaysia), GeneXpert (Cepheid, USA) or rapid antigen test (Goldsite Diagnostics, China). After selecting the subjects, patient medical records were reviewed to collect the data on demographics, clinical characteristics, comorbidities, vaccination status, baseline laboratory profiles and clinical outcomes.

Isolation of secondary co-pathogen

In our centre, samples were processed according to standard microbiology techniques. Blood cultures were inoculated into BacT/Alert Blood Culture System (BioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA) and subcultured onto solid medium (blood, MacConkey and chocolate blood agars) whenever the system signalled growth. Respiratory and urine specimens were submitted in sterile universal containers and subcultured onto blood and MacConkey agars. Additionally, respiratory and urine specimens were also inoculated onto chocolate blood agar and cystine-lactose-electrolyte-deficient agar, respectively. The agars incubated aerobically at 35 ± 2 °C for 24–48 h. Isolates were identified by biochemical test and subsequently confirmed by Vitek2 Automated Identification System (BioMerieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France).

Operational definitions

A person was considered to have a COVID-19 infection upon receiving a positive test result and supported by clinical judgement. According to Malaysian Ministry of Health guidelines, there are five clinical stages of COVID-19: Category 1 is asymptomatic individuals, Category 2 is symptomatic individuals without signs of pneumonia, Category 3 is symptomatic individuals with pneumonia, Category 4 is symptomatic individuals with pneumonia requiring supplemental oxygen, while Category 5 is critically ill patients who need ventilation [4]. Hospitalisation is generally required for patients classified as Category 3 to 5. COVID-19 co-infections were defined as an infection with a pathogen other than SARS-CoV-2 in a patient diagnosed with COVID-19 [5]. Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores were calculated based on clinical and laboratory parameters available on admission [6].

Primary co-infections refer to an infection due to other pathogen that was present at the time of initial COVID-19 presentation, while secondary co-infections were defined as the infection caused by other pathogen during the course of care for SARS-CoV-2 infection i.e. an infection that develops during the illness or hospitalisation due to COVID-19 [7–9]. For this study, the primary co-infections were taken into consideration whenever the multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of archived samples (initial nasopharyngeal swab taken for diagnosis of COVID-19) detected respiratory pathogens. In contrast, the secondary co-infections were based on positive cultures of clinical specimens collected during hospitalisation for COVID-19. Secondary co-infections can be further divided into community acquired and hospital acquired infections (isolated within and after 48 h of admission, respectively) [10].

Fully vaccinated individuals were defined as those aged 18–60 who have received two complete doses of either BNT162b2 (Pfizer BioNTech, New York), ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 (Oxford AstraZaneca, Cambridge) or CoronaVac (Sinovac Biotech, Beijing), with at least 14 days having elapsed since the second dose [5]. Antimicrobial therapy was considered appropriate if its initiation was clinically justified, targeted the identified organisms, was supported by antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and was administered within 72 h of pathogen isolation [11]. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2, based on WHO Asia-Pacific region guidelines. This definition accounts for the fact that individuals of Asian descent have been shown to experience higher morbidity and mortality at lower BMI compared to other populations [12]. The reference ranges for laboratory results were based chemical pathology and haematology reference standards of our centre [13–15]. In this study, clinical outcomes were categorized as either survival (discharged alive) or mortality (death during hospitalisation) [16].

Determination of primary co-infections from representative archived samples

The searching of residual samples of COVID-19 test from the subjects selected in this study were done. These archived samples in viral transport media (VTM) were thawed from − 80 °C storage. After nucleic acid extraction, the determination of respiratory pathogens was performed using multiplex RT-PCR assay (Fast Track Diagnostics, Luxembourg). This assay is able to detect 33 respiratory pathogens include: influenza A virus, influenza A (H1N1) virus (swine lineage), influenza B virus, influenza C virus, human coronaviruses, human parainfluenza viruses, human metapneumoviruses, human rhinovirus, human respiratory syncytial viruses, human adenovirus, enterovirus, human parechovirus, human bocavirus, P. jirovecii; Mycoplasma pneumoniae; Chlamydophila pneumoniae; Streptococcus pneumoniae; Haemophilus influenzae type b; Staphylococcus aureus; Moraxella catarrhalis; Bordetella spp.; Klebsiella pneumoniae; Legionella pneumophila-Legionella longbeachae; Salmonella spp.; Haemophilus influenzae (non-type b); while equine arteritis virus (EAV) serves as internal control.

Statistical methods

All the data were analysed using SPSS version 28.0.1.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Chicago). The prevalence of secondary co-infections was presented as cases per 100 hospitalised COVID-19 patients. Risk factors, laboratory profiles and clinical outcomes associated with secondary co-infection were analysed using simple logistic regression. The risk factors that have p < 0.25 by simple logistic regression were then analysed using multiple logistic regression. The results were then tabulated as shown in the Results section. For primary co-infections, descriptive statistics will be applied to summarize the pathogen detected.

Results

Prevalence of secondary co-infections

From January 2021 to December 2022, a total of 929 COVID-19 patients were admitted to the hospital. The prevalence of secondary co-infections was 3.66 cases per 100 hospitalised COVID-19 (95% CI 2.55–5.08%), where 34 patients with positive cultures. Thirty-nine pathogens were isolated from these 34 patients, including Enterobacteriaceae (23.1%), non-Enterobacteriaceae (30.8%), Gram positive organisms (30.8%) and fungal (15.4%). Acinetobacter baumannii (10.3%), Candida spp (10.3%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (7.7%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (7.7%) and Escherichia coli (7.7%) were among the commonest isolated pathogens. Candida albicans(7.7%) and Candida tropicalis(2.6%) were the most commonly isolated fungal organisms.

Majority of the secondary infections were bloodstream infections (53.8% ,n = 21), followed by respiratory infection (33.3%, n = 13), urinary tract infection (10.2%, n = 4) and peritonitis (2.6%, n = 1). Of note, we isolated 3 cases of community acquired bacteremia (14.2% of bacteremic COVID-19 secondary co-infections), caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, Salmonella spp and Streptococcus pneumoniae, respectively.

Clinical outcomes associated with secondary co-infections

27.5% (n = 47) of hospitalised COVID-19 patients were admitted for intensive care and 70.2% (n = 33) of them required mechanical ventilation. Clinical outcomes associated with secondary co-infections were analysed using univariate model(Table 5). Mortality (OR 6.52, CI 2.88–15.1, p ≤ 0.001), ICU admission (OR 4.15, CI 0.91–5.14, p ≤ 0.001) and SOFA score ≥ 2 (OR 3.30, CI 1.44–7.57, p = 0.004) were found to be significantly associated with secondary co-infections. Multivariate analysis confirmed that mortality (OR 4.65, CI 1.85–11.68, p ≤ 0.001) and ICU admission(OR 2.42, CI 1.02–5.75, p = 0.045) is independently associated with secondary co-infections in COVID-19 patients (Table 6).

Primary co-infections

We were able to retrieve 48 archived samples from 170 study subjects from the same study population. Thirty-two samples (66.7%) were found to have positive respiratory pathogens with total of 43 pathogens detected. Out of 43 pathogens isolated, K. pneumoniae were the most common detected in 19 samples, followed by Staphylococcus aureus (n = 11), Streptococcus pneumoniae (n = 7), Moraxella catarrhalis (n = 2), Pneumocystic jirovecii (n = 2), Hemophilus influenzae (n = 1) and human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) (n = 1).

Discussion

Secondary co-infections are a subsequent isolation of other pathogens during SARS-CoV-2 infection care [7, 8]. An earlier study by Cox et al. reported as high as half of COVID-19 patients who succumbed to the disease were having secondary infections, including bacterial, fungal, and other respiratory viruses [1]. The prevalence of secondary co-infections in this study was 3.7%. This is comparable with other studies that use culture methods for pathogen isolation. These include studies in Brunei (4.4%) [17], Barcelona (3.1%) [18], nationwide Spain study (2.3%) [19], United Kingdom (2.7%) [20], and Netherlands (1.2%) [21]. Although the bacterial and fungal co-infections are uncommon, the empiric antimicrobial agents use during COVID-19 pandemic is abundant [21]. Thus, restriction of empirical antimicrobial use during COVID-19 infection was advocated by some of the clinical recommendations, to prevent unnecessary use of the agents [22].

However, using multiplex PCR method, the proportion of primary co-infection in archived positive COVID-19 samples were 66.7%. The high proportion is probably due to more sensitive methods. This is comparable with a recent report from Guangzhou, China that use molecular method, had a prevalence of 82.3% [23]. However, the clinical significance of molecular detection of these respiratory pathogen is unclear. Correlation with clinical presentation, radiological finding, as well as the microbial load (semi-quantitative using cycle threshold of the PCR) are required.

Gram negative organisms were the main causative agents of secondary co-infections. In our series, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter spp and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were the predominant causative agents. Majority of K.pneumoniae isolated (75%) were carbapenem-resistant, all Acinetobacter spp and 33.3% of P.aeruginosa were multidrug-resistant. These organisms mainly caused bloodstream and respiratory tract infections. 75% of Candida spp were isolated from blood culture, causing bloodstream and/or catheter-related infections. Central venous catheter, mechanical ventilation, prolonged use of broad spectrum antibiotics and corticosteroid are among the risk factors for invasive candidiasis in COVID-19 patients [24]. Isolation of these organisms may contribute to the poor clinical outcomes in hospitalised COVID-19 patients as discussed below, possibly due to limited treatment options. These findings underscore the importance of rapid susceptibility testing, antimicrobial stewardship and infection control measures to mitigate the impact of these infections [25]. Additionally, isolation of community acquired organisms such as Burkholderia pseudomallei and non-typhoidal Salmonella may be an indictor of endemicity of these infections in the local community [26].

The factors found to be significant with secondary co-infected patients in our study were length of stay and hypertension. Longer length of stay was statistically significant to be associated with secondary co-infections, with mean of 11.4 days compared to only 5.9 days in control group (adjusted OR 1.3 days). During early COVID-19 pandemic, about 15% of COVID-19 patients were hospitalised for moderate to severe COVID-19 management [27]. This inpatient management exposed the patients to secondary bacterial/fungal co-infections and subsequently resulted in high mortality among COVD-19 patients.

Hypertension is a common co-morbidity among adults in our population. This factor has significant association with secondary co-infections, with 76.5% of co-infected patients were having hypertension. It is established that the agent of COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2 enters cells through the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, an important receptor for blood pressure modulation [28]. In earlier meta-analysis, Lippi et al. had shown hypertension associated with an approximately 2.5-fold increment for severity and mortality in COVID-19 patients [29]. Thus, our study supports one of possible mechanisms of poor outcomes in hypertensive COVID-19 patients was, in part, due to their susceptibility to secondary co-infections. Uncontrolled hypertension also contribute to a more severe COVID-19 disease course, due to the associated organ damage [30], although whether it is an independent predictor of mortality or hospitalisation is uncertain [31]. The interaction of hypertension with other comorbidities and risk factors in patients with co-infection warrant further investigation.

Secondary co-infections were associated with thrombocytopenia and elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels. Thrombocytopenia is a common finding in COVID-19 infection and has prognostic value in indicating high mortality risk [32]. Some proposed mechanisms by which SARS-CoV-2 causes thrombocytopenia include impaired haematopoiesis, resulted from systemic inflammation or direct viral infection of the megakaryocytes via ACE2, CD13 or CD66a.

In our series, elevated liver enzymes were observed in 29.4–47.1% and 20.6–31.6% in cases and controls, respectively. This is comparable with earlier international reports [32, 33]. These abnormal liver enzymes probably due to pre-existing liver disease in a significant proportion of patients, hepatotoxic effect of the medication used in managing COVID-19, and although with less clinical evidence, direct cytopathic effect of SARS-CoV-2 to the liver cells [33]. Elevated liver enzymes themselves were shown to correlate with severity and prognosis of COVID-19 [16].

Risk factors associated with increased severity in COVID-19 patients are well described [34]. Our findings also suggest that the risk factors for COVID-19 co-infections and severity are interrelated, as both are influenced by underlying health conditions, immune responses, and factors associated with hospitalisation. In our study, increased age, and comorbidities such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease were found to be significant risk factors for COVID-19 co-infections in initial analysis but was found to be not significant in multivariate analysis. This could be explained in part due to 83.6% of our study population was fully vaccinated, thereby attenuating effect of risk factors on acquisition of secondary co-infections [35]. The availability of mathematical and biological modelling may provide insight into the complex relationship between the role of specific co-infecting pathogen, risk factors and disease severity [36, 37].

Our study showed that secondary co-infections in COVID-19 patients were significantly associated with poorer clinical outcomes, namely mortality and ICU admission. In this study, 52.9% of secondary co-infected patients died, slightly higher than 24–26% in the literature [38, 39]. The mortality rate in COVID-19 patients was significantly higher in the group with co-infection, with a crude mortality among secondary co-infection group of 6.52 compared to the controls. The increased ICU admission rate among COVID-19 patients may lead to increased acquisition of secondary co-infections. This could subsequently resulted in a high mortality in the co-infected group. Notably, the average SOFA score on admission is higher in the co-infected group (2.45) than the control group (1.82), but this is not statistically significant. However, a SOFA score of ≥ 2 is significantly associated with co-infections in COVID-19 patient in univariate analysis. WHO defines sepsis in COVID-19 as an increase in sepsis-related SOFA score ≥ 2 points [40]. Our findings suggest that co-infections increase the severity of COVID-19 patients in sepsis [41]. Thus, aggressive management and prevention of secondary co-infection especially among hospital pathogens are important to reduce the severity and mortality among COVID-19 patients [22].

Although co-infections are relatively uncommon, the high mortality associated with them represents a significant burden to the healthcare system in outbreak settings, more so in a country with limited resource. Risk factors and control of non-communicable diseases, namely hypertension and chronic liver disease, if addressed accordingly, may significantly reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with COVID-19 co-infections [42, 43]. The availability of local epidemiological data, implementation of rapid diagnostics, and modification of antimicrobial regime in the light of susceptibility data are crucial to improve the outcome of co-infections in COVID-19 patients [44]. The importance of proper infection control practice and appropriate use of antimicrobial therapy in managing COVID-19 cases, especially those with co-infections cannot be overstated. Proper planning for optimisation of limited manpower, diagnostic and therapeutic capacity, to mitigate future outbreaks of epidemic or pandemic scale should also be implemented [45].

This study had some limitations. First, this is a retrospective study, therefore, certain clinical data may not be available, and certain diagnostic tests may not have been performed, potentially leading to underdiagnosis or missed diagnosis. Second, some of the medical records were unavailable, resulting in the exclusion of several early patients. Third, distinguishing between true infections, colonisations or contaminations remains challenging due to overlapping clinical features of COVID-19 and many of the primary and secondary co-infections.

Conclusion

Our study identified Gram-negative bacteria as the predominant pathogens of co-infections in COVID-19 patients, with the prevalence rate aligning with global epidemiological data. Notably, hypertension, prolonged hospital stay, thrombocytopenia and high alkaline phosphatase were identified as significant risk factors associated with secondary co-infections. Co-infections also were also independently associated with ICU admission and mortality. Our results highlight that patients presenting with these specific comorbidities and laboratory abnormalities may warrant heightened clinical vigilance, given the severe clinical trajectories associated with co-infections.
