Evaluation of the Johne’s disease surveillance program in dairy herds in Hokkaido, Japan, using a stochastic individual-based simulation model
Authors: Shinichi SAKAKIBARA, Kazuhiro MIYANE, Shintaro HONMA, Hideki HANYU, Tomomi MASUKO, Takehiro HARADA, Hiroaki HANEDA, Goshi NAKATA, Jun HAYAKAWA, Takehisa YAMAMOTO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractJohne’s disease (JD), caused by Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis (MAP), imposes major economic burdens on dairy production. Japan’s legally mandated surveillance includes regular surveillance (RS) every 5 years in MAP-negative herds and follow-up surveillance (FS) in MAP-positive herds, consisting of 5 tests over 3 years. However, the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of this program have not been quantitatively assessed. We developed a stochastic, individual-based simulation model to evaluate MAP transmission, farm-level economic impacts, and government costs in dairy herds in Hokkaido, Japan’s largest dairy-producing region. Eight scenarios were compared: no surveillance, the current program, and variations in RS interval, FS duration, and FS testing frequency. Results indicated that, compared with no surveillance, the current program slowed the increase in within-herd prevalence and increased the probability of MAP fade-out, thereby reducing economic losses. Shortening the RS interval to 3 years enhanced early detection and more efficiently achieved MAP fade-out, which in turn reduced government costs. Compared with the current program, shortening FS duration or reducing testing frequency lowered detection efficiency of the surveillance program and increased MAP persistence, while extending duration or increasing frequency provided little additional benefit. Overall, the current JD surveillance program in Hokkaido effectively promotes MAP fade-out within herds through early detection by RS combined with repeated high-frequency FS testing, although a shorter RS interval may further strengthen early detection and JD control.Keywords: economic impact, Johne’s disease, stochastic simulation, surveillance program, within-herd transmission

INTRODUCTION

Johne’s disease (JD), or paratuberculosis, is an infectious chronic inflammatory disorder of the intestines that affects domestic and wild ruminants, including dairy cattle [39]. The disease is caused by infection with Mycobacterium avium subsp. paratuberculosis (MAP), a bacterium that is relatively resistant to environmental conditions [42]. Transmission occurs either in utero or via ingestion of milk or feces contaminated with MAP. The disease progresses slowly over several years, with clinical signs such as diarrhea, reduced milk production, and emaciation [39]. The economic impact of JD is primarily driven by reduced milk production and losses due to premature culling, with the average cost estimated at $33 USD per cow [34]. JD is distributed globally. In a survey of 48 countries, more than 20% of herds and flocks were infected with MAP in about half of the countries. Formal JD control programs were present in 22 countries, with prevalence reduction being the primary objective in most cases [41].

In Japan, the first case of JD was reported in an imported dairy cow that died in 1927. As JD became endemic across the country, it was added to the list of reportable animal diseases under the Domestic Animal Infectious Diseases Control Law in 1971. Since then, MAP-infected cattle have been subjected to compulsory culling with compensation, and culling is conducted within two weeks after confirmation of MAP infection. Milk from MAP-infected cattle is also discarded. In addition, national serological surveillance for JD in cattle was launched in 1998 as a legally mandated program. The national active surveillance program consists of two components: regular surveillance (RS), conducted every 5 years in MAP-negative status herds, and follow-up surveillance (FS), implemented in MAP-positive status herds when infected animals are detected. In both RS and FS, all eligible cattle above the target age are tested simultaneously. In FS, blood and fecal samples are collected from cattle for antibody and fecal tests performed 5 times over a 3-year period. In addition, movement of cattle from MAP-positive status herds to other herds is restricted to prevent between-farm transmission of MAP. Affected herds regain MAP-negative status if no additional cases are found in these tests, whereas the FS cycle is restarted if additional cases are detected [13, 23, 44]. Because JD has a long incubation period [44] and the sensitivity of diagnostic tests for JD is low (15–71% depending on disease stage) [24], frequent and repeated testing is required to maintain surveillance accuracy.

In Hokkaido, the largest dairy-producing region in Japan, approximately 800,000 dairy cattle are raised, accounting for about 60% of the total dairy cattle population in the country. Each year, around 400,000 cattle undergo legally mandated testing, and around 1,000 MAP-infected cattle are identified and compulsorily culled. Notably, about 90% of MAP-infected cattle detected nationwide are found in Hokkaido [21], making Hokkaido a focal region for JD surveillance in Japan. Although significant government resources have been invested in this surveillance program [29], its effectiveness has not been thoroughly evaluated.

Simulation models are frequently employed to evaluate the effectiveness of infectious disease control strategies [8, 14], and a number of such models have been developed for JD [1, 3, 4, 12, 19, 34]. Because JD has a prolonged incubation period [44] and low diagnostic sensitivity [20], field-based evaluation of surveillance programs requires long-term observation. One of the advantages of simulation modeling is the ability to rapidly predict and compare multiple scenarios under controlled assumptions [8].

In the present study, we developed a stochastic, individual-based model to simulate MAP transmission dynamics within dairy herds in Hokkaido. Using this model, we compared scenarios with and without the surveillance program to assess its impact on within-herd MAP prevalence, farm-level economic losses, and associated government costs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

To simulate MAP transmission within a dairy herd in Hokkaido and evaluate the JD surveillance program, we adapted previously reported stochastic, individual-based models [3, 4, 19]. The model was implemented in R version 4.4.1 [32] and operated with a monthly time step.

Herd dynamics

We modeled the dynamics of a typical dairy herd in Hokkaido, Japan, assuming that animals were kept indoors year-round. For simplicity, grouping within the herd was not considered. Cows calved for the first time at 24 months of age and subsequently calved every 13 months [18]. The removal age of each cow was assumed to follow a parity-based probability distribution: parity 1: 0.165, parity 2: 0.218, parity 3: 0.225, parity 4: 0.179, parity 5: 0.112, parity 6: 0.056, parity 7: 0.026, and parity 8: 0.018 [18]. Cows aged 24 months or older were defined as adult cows, and the herd size was maintained at 100 adult cows [18]. Each month, the number of replacement cows reaching adulthood was assumed to match the number of adult cows removed that month. Specifically, if X cows were removed at time t, then X out of Y calves born at time t–24 months were selected and raised as replacement heifers. If fewer than X calves (Y<X) were available, the deficit was supplemented with female calves born in subsequent months. Female calves not selected as replacements and all male calves were assumed to be sold in their birth month. The mortality rate during the month of birth was set at 0.066 [11], and no further removals occurred between birth and 24 months of age. The herd remained closed throughout the simulation period, except for the initial introduction of MAP-infected cattle. Calves were separated from their dams immediately after receiving colostrum.

Infection dynamics

Six disease stages were modeled: susceptible (S), latently infected (IL), transiently infectious (IT), moderately infectious and seronegative (IMsn), moderately infectious and seropositive (IMsp), and highly infectious (IH). When susceptible cattle were infected with MAP, they typically progressed through IL, IMsn, IMsp, and IH. A subset of animals could transiently enter the IT stage during the IL period. Cattle in the IL stage are infected but do not shed detectable MAP and are not identifiable by current diagnostic tests. In the IT stage, animals may shed MAP transiently shortly after infection. The IMsn stage includes infected animals that shed MAP at moderate levels and remain seronegative, whereas IMsp animals have detectable antibody responses. The IH stage corresponds to advanced infection, in which animals shed large quantities of MAP and are more likely to be detected by both serological and fecal tests. This stage structure captures key features of JD pathogenesis, including a prolonged latent period, variable and intermittent shedding, the delayed development of detectable serum antibody responses relative to MAP shedding, and eventual progression to high-level shedding, as reported in previous studies [22, 39, 44]. The mean and standard deviation of the duration of each disease stage are listed in Table 1. Stage durations were stochastically assigned at the time of infection for each animal. Cattle died either upon reaching the IH terminal stage or were culled earlier according to the parity-based removal rule described in the Herd dynamics section, if their predefined productive lifespan ended before MAP-induced death or removal. Cattle in stages IT, IMsn, IMsp, and IH were infectious and shed MAP in feces. Additionally, cattle in the IMsn, IMsp, and IH stages may shed MAP into colostrum and may cause in utero infections. MAP transmission occurred through environmental exposure to contaminated feces, ingestion of contaminated colostrum (directly or indirectly), or via in utero transmission.

Surveillance programs

RS was conducted on MAP-negative status herds. The first test was administered in a randomly selected month within the first 5 years (60 months) of the simulation, and subsequent tests were conducted every 60 months thereafter. Cattle aged 24 months or older were tested using a two-step testing strategy: serum ELISA screening followed by fecal qPCR (SE-FqPCR) for ELISA-positive cattle. Herds with at least one SE-FqPCR-positive animal were classified as MAP-positive status herds.

FS was conducted on MAP-positive status herds 5 times over a 3 years (36 month) period, with testing at 3, 6, and 12 months in the first year, and at 24 and 33 months thereafter. SE-FqPCR was applied to cattle aged 6 months or older, and fecal culture was additionally conducted for animals between 6 and 13 months of age. Shipment of milk from MAP-infected cows is prohibited for human consumption under Japanese law. Fecal-culture testing of cattle aged 14 months or older that may begin lactation within 3 months was not conducted because culture testing requires up to 3 months to obtain results and these cattle will start lactation in this period. If all cattle tested negative in all 5 rounds of FS and no new cases were detected within 36 months, the herd was classified as having MAP-negative status.

The results of SE-FqPCR were assumed to be available in the same month, whereas fecal culture results were available 3 months later. Both SE-FqPCR and fecal culture were conducted on an individual animal basis. Cattle that tested positive were culled in the month their results became available, irrespective of whether the results were true or false positives and regardless of pregnancy status. Test specificity and stage-dependent sensitivity were based on previous studies and are summarized in Table 2. Among the six disease stages in this model, IMsp and IH cattle were detectable by ELISA, whereas earlier stages (IL, IT, IMsn) generally remained undetectable by this method. In contrast, qPCR and fecal culture can detect animals in any shedding stage (IT, IMsn, IMsp, and IH), reflecting the presence of MAP in feces. Furthermore, as only 0.41% of MAP-infected cattle detected in Hokkaido in 2023 were identified through clinical examination, detection based on clinical signs was assumed not to occur.

Model calibration

To identify parameters that strongly influenced the number of test-positive cattle, we performed a global sensitivity analysis using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) and the random forest algorithm. The output variable was defined as the cumulative number of test-positive cattle detected under the surveillance program.

Twenty-eight parameters with high uncertainty were selected for this analysis. Their names and ranges are listed in Tables 1 and 2. Parameter sets were generated using LHS with the lhs package in R [5], yielding 10,000 stratified samples across the parameter space. For each set, the stochastic model was executed and the number of test-positive cattle was recorded. The randomForest package in R [16] was then used to analyze the relationship between parameter values and outputs. Variable importance was quantified by the percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE). Accordingly, βe, representing the fecal–oral transmission rate, was identified as the most influential parameter among the 28 examined (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Subsequently, model calibration was performed for βe by comparing model outputs with field surveillance data and adjusting parameter values accordingly. We defined N36 as the cumulative number of test-positive cattle in RS and FS within the subsequent 36 months from the first detection. The parameter βe (default value: 9.5 × 10−7 × 30.4 [19]) was varied on a base-10 logarithmic scale in increments of 100.1, ranging from 9.5 × 10−9 × 30.4 to 9.5 × 10−5 × 30.4. For each βe value, the model was run and the corresponding estimate of N36 was denoted as N36p.

To compare model results with field data, we examined 70 herds in Hokkaido with 50–150 adult cows that were first detected as MAP-positive through RS between 2013 and 2021. Although the initial infection condition in the model—namely, the introduction of a single MAP-infected cow—may not fully correspond to actual field conditions, the introduction of multiple infected cattle is considered unlikely in Hokkaido due to its relatively low prevalence. Therefore, we regarded this assumption as reasonably consistent with the field conditions. The number of N36 per 100 adult cows was calculated and defined as N36o. The distributions of N36p and N36o were compared using the wasserstein1d function from the transport package in R [36], which calculates the Wasserstein distance as a measure of similarity between probability distributions. The βe value that yielded an N36p distribution most similar to N36o was selected. Consequently, βe = 9.5 × 10−8.1 × 30.4 was identified as the best-fit value and was used in subsequent simulations (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Economic calculations

Farm profit was calculated as the difference between total revenues and total costs. Revenues were classified into milk production, calf sales, and slaughtered cows. Costs were assumed to include feed, labor, reproduction management, veterinary care, and other farm operating expenses, and were classified into rearing costs for calves and heifers and production costs for adult cows.

Monthly revenue from milk production was calculated by multiplying the total herd milk yield, derived from the monthly milk yield per cow (Supplementary Table 1 [17]), by the farm-gate milk price [26]. Revenues from calf sales and slaughtered adult cows were calculated based on the number of removed calves and adult cows, and their respective market prices [9]. Among removed adult cows, 6.4% were assumed to die [6] and were therefore assigned zero market value. In contrast, test-positive cattle were classified as culled cattle, and compensation was set at 80% of the age-specific market price [10], reflecting the legally mandated 20% reduction. According to a previous study in Hokkaido, cows in the IH disease stage produced 86.7% of the normal milk yield, while JD was assumed not to affect cattle market prices [35]. Although some studies have reported that JD may reduce slaughter value due to decreased body weight [7], a study conducted in Hokkaido found no significant difference in body weight between MAP-infected and non-infected cattle [35]. Therefore, in this study, slaughter value was assumed not to be affected by JD. Monthly rearing and production costs were calculated based on national statistical data [27], and the presence of JD was assumed not to influence these costs. All prices and cost parameters used in the analysis are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. In this study, all monetary values were expressed in 2023 terms, and temporal changes in value were not considered.

Government costs

Government costs included material costs, labor costs, and compensation for the culling of test-positive cattle, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Costs for sampling, testing, and culling materials were derived from procurement records maintained by the Hokkaido Prefectural Government, including key supplies such as syringes, sample tubes, and test kits. Labor costs were calculated by multiplying the average hourly wage of civil servants in Hokkaido by the required person-hours [28], with further details provided below. Compensation costs were identical to those described in the Economic calculations section and represent the government expenditure counterpart to farmer revenues.

To estimate labor costs, we recorded the actual time required and number of personnel involved for each task based on field data collected in Hokkaido (Supplementary Table 4). Tasks were categorized into four components: travel, sampling, testing, and culling. Time units were standardized as follows: (i) per testing event for travel, and (ii) per animal for sampling, testing, and culling tasks. Travel time varied substantially depending on the distance from the office to farms and their geographical distribution; therefore, representative values were used. RS typically involved visits to multiple farms per trip, resulting in a shorter average travel time per farm due to the efficiency of inter-farm travel. By contrast, FS was conducted as single-farm visits targeting herds with test-positive animals, leading to longer travel time per farm. Similarly, follow-up fecal sampling after ELISA-positive cattle were detected, as well as the culling of infected cattle, were conducted as single-farm visits. Sampling time was recorded separately for blood collection and fecal collection. Testing time included ELISA, fecal culture, and qPCR, with time calculated per individual sample. Culling time encompassed both the physical removal of cattle and the administrative time required for compensation calculation.

Scenarios

To evaluate the effectiveness of the current JD surveillance program in Hokkaido, we defined eight simulation scenarios. Scenario 1 assumes no surveillance and serves as the reference scenario. Scenario 2 represents the current JD surveillance program, in which FS is implemented following RS detection of test-positive cattle. It involves a 5-year RS interval, an FS duration of 3 years, and FS testing frequencies of three times in the first year and once annually thereafter.

Scenarios 3–8 are variations of Scenario 2, designed to evaluate the contribution of each component to the overall effectiveness of the program. Scenarios 3 and 4 represent a shorter and longer RS interval, respectively. Scenarios 5 and 6 represent a shorter and longer FS duration, respectively. Scenarios 7 and 8 represent a reduced and increased FS testing frequency, respectively. The details of each scenario are summarized in Table 3.

Simulations

The stochastic model was run for 40 years (480 months) to evaluate the long-term impact of the surveillance programs. At the beginning of the simulation (month 1), MAP was introduced into a MAP-negative herd through the purchase of a 24-month-old cow congenitally infected with MAP. For each scenario, 10,000 stochastic repetitions were conducted to ensure stable model outputs by reducing stochastic variability.

Outputs

Temporal changes in disease stage-specific prevalence were analyzed only in Scenario 1, and simulation runs were stratified according to whether secondary transmission occurred or not. The analysis tracked the proportion of cattle in any of the five infected stages: IL, IT, IMsn, IMsp, and IH.

The proportion of simulation runs by prevalence category was evaluated for Scenarios 1–8 across the entire simulation period. Simulation runs were classified into six categories based on within-herd prevalence: 0%; >0–10%; >10–20%; >20–40%; >40–60%; and >60%.

The relationship between the number of months after MAP introduction to the first RS-positive result and the total number of detected cattle over the full simulation period was evaluated.

Economic outcomes by prevalence category were evaluated in only Scenario 1, to assess the direct impact of JD without surveillance interventions. For each farm, monthly values for prevalence, revenues, costs, and profit were calculated throughout the simulation. These values were averaged on an annual basis to smooth short-term fluctuations. Farms were then assigned to a prevalence category each year based on annual average prevalence, and economic outcomes were aggregated by category.

Five-year trends in farm profit were evaluated for Scenarios 1–8. Profit was calculated by averaging monthly profit over each 60-month interval, and then annualized by multiplying the mean monthly profit by 12. This profit analysis also included a variant of Scenario 2 in which compensation for culled cattle was excluded, to isolate the impact of the compensation scheme on farm profitability.

Cumulative government costs were calculated from the start of the simulation. The cumulative period was evaluated at 5-year intervals, and cumulative costs at 5, 10, …, and 40 years after MAP introduction were computed for each scenario. Because government costs differed substantially between simulation runs with and without secondary transmission, cumulative costs were calculated separately for these two groups within each scenario.

RESULTS

Temporal changes in disease stage-specific prevalence in herds with and without secondary transmission under no surveillance

In simulation runs in Scenario 1 (no surveillance) in which secondary transmission occurred, IH-stage cattle first emerged, after which the within-herd prevalence showed an overall increase during the first 20 years after MAP introduction. This increase was accompanied by periodic fluctuations with a periodicity of approximately 5 years across the disease stages (Fig. 1). Notably, cattle in the IMsp and IH stages, which are detectable by ELISA, were intermittently absent during certain periods within this time frame. These intervals, during which ELISA-detectable animals were nearly absent, are hereafter referred to as “low-detection periods.” After approximately 20 years, this periodic pattern became less apparent, and the proportion of cattle in all disease stages increased monotonically, consistent with the overall increase in within-herd prevalence. In contrast, in simulation runs without secondary transmission, MAP fade-out (i.e., disappearance of MAP from herds) typically occurred before the emergence of IH-stage cattle, as the initially infected animal was usually removed from the herd through routine herd dynamics before reaching the IH stage.

In Scenario 2 (the current JD surveillance program), MAP fade-out was also concentrated in the first 5 years. However, MAP fade-out continued to occur gradually thereafter. The proportion of simulation runs with persistent MAP infection decreased to 44.8%, 18.5%, and 2.8% at years 5, 20, and 40, respectively. Among runs in which MAP persisted, simulation runs with >10% prevalence appeared by around year 5, peaked at year 15, and declined thereafter. At years 20 and 40, 48.1% and 39.4% of runs in which MAP persisted exceeded 10% prevalence, respectively.

Scenario 3 (shorter RS interval) exhibited the most rapid rate of MAP fade-out among all scenarios. The proportion of simulation runs in which MAP persisted declined to 39.7%, 6.5%, and 0.5% at years 5, 20, and 40, respectively. Among these runs, 16.1% and 23.1% at years 20 and 40, respectively, exceeded 10% prevalence, both of which were lower than the corresponding values in Scenario 2.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we modeled the transmission of MAP within dairy herds in Hokkaido to evaluate the effectiveness of the current JD surveillance program in reducing within-herd MAP prevalence, mitigating farm-level economic losses, and reducing government costs.

Compared with Scenario 1 (no surveillance), Scenario 2 (the current JD surveillance program) was predicted to result in a slower rate of increase in within-herd prevalence and a higher probability of within-herd MAP fade-out. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have reported that the detection and culling of infected cattle are effective in reducing within-herd prevalence [1, 3, 10]. While more than half of countries have reported a herd-level prevalence of approximately 20% [41], data held by the Hokkaido Prefectural Government indicate that only 5.0% of dairy herds in Hokkaido are classified as MAP-positive. This substantial difference suggests that herd-level prevalence in Hokkaido is relatively low by international standards. This relatively low prevalence likely reflects a combination of the recent introduction of MAP into the region [23] and the sustained impact of the current surveillance program in promoting MAP fade-out within herds.

In Scenario 1 (no surveillance), approximately half of the simulation runs exhibited natural MAP fade-out within 5 years after MAP introduction, whereas simulation runs without fade-out showed increasing within-herd prevalence and a very low likelihood of subsequent spontaneous elimination. These results indicate that within-herd MAP transmission dynamics were observed to bifurcate around 5 years after introduction. In simulation runs without secondary transmission, IH-stage cattle rarely emerged, suggesting that the presence or absence of IH-stage cattle contributes to the observed bifurcation in transmission dynamics. Cattle in the IH stage are highly infectious due to the large quantities of MAP shed into the environment [39, 40]. Environmental MAP can persist even after the removal of IH-stage cattle, thereby contributing substantially to the increasing pressure on within-herd prevalence [39, 42]. The preferential detection and removal of IH-stage cattle have therefore been proposed as one of the control strategies for JD [2]. Across all surveillance scenarios, simulation runs in which infected herds were detected by RS within 5 years after MAP introduction consistently showed low cumulative numbers of detected cattle. This suggests that the ability to detect and remove infected animals during the early phase after MAP introduction, that is, before the emergence of IH-stage cattle, is a key determinant of whether subsequent within-herd spread can be prevented. Scenario 3 (shorter RS interval) achieved the most rapid rate of MAP fade-out among all scenarios. Although Scenario 3 did not substantially reduce the probability of secondary transmission, the increased testing frequency markedly raised the likelihood of detecting infected herds early after MAP introduction, resulting in the smallest cumulative number of detected cattle. These findings suggest that frequent RS testing enables reliable identification and removal of infected cattle while within-herd prevalence is still low, thereby facilitating efficient MAP fade-out within herds. Accordingly, shortening the RS interval appears to be an effective strategy for promoting MAP fade-out through earlier detection.

Scenario 4 (longer RS interval) was predicted to result in a slightly faster rate of MAP fade-out compared with Scenario 2 (the current JD surveillance program). This finding was contrary to the initial expectation that extending the RS interval would delay MAP fade-out. In Scenario 4, the probability of detection by RS within the first 5 years after MAP introduction was lower than in Scenario 2. However, between years 5 and 20 after MAP introduction, Scenario 4 exhibited a higher detection probability. This study also showed that low-detection periods, defined as intervals during which cattle in ELISA-detectable stages (IMsp and IH) are temporarily absent, emerge at approximately 5-year cycles. When RS is conducted at 5-year intervals, as in Scenario 2, the testing years can coincide repeatedly with these low-detection periods, substantially reducing herd-level diagnostic sensitivity and delaying detection until within-herd prevalence increases sufficiently for ELISA-detectable cattle to reappear. In contrast, the RS timing in Scenarios 3 and 4, which used shorter or longer RS intervals, deviated from this 5-year cycle, resulting in first detections occurring predominantly outside low-detection periods. Even if a given RS fell within low-detection periods, the subsequent RS would be conducted in an offset year, increasing the chance of detection when ELISA-detectable cattle were present. Thus, although Scenario 4 showed a lower probability of early detection within the first 5 years, largely due to its reduced testing frequency, the reduced synchrony between RS timing and low-detection periods ultimately increased opportunities for timely detection, thereby promoting a more efficient rate of MAP fade-out relative to Scenario 2. However, this interpretation is based on model-derived dynamics and should be regarded as a hypothesis. A possible biological basis for such low-detection periods is discussed below.

Low-detection periods are thought to arise from characteristic features of JD: the prolonged antibody-negative phase and the comparatively short antibody-positive phase. This interpretation is consistent with previous observations; seroconversion in MAP-infected cattle may take more than 3 years after infection [44], whereas more than 95% of high-shedding cattle, which are more likely to test positive by ELISA, are removed within 1 year [22]. After infectious cattle in ELISA-detectable stages transmit MAP to susceptible herd mates and are subsequently removed from the herd, several years are required for newly infected animals to progress to ELISA-detectable stages. This temporal lag may result in periods during which almost no cattle in the herd are detectable by ELISA, suggesting that such low-detection periods could plausibly occur under field conditions. Among 129 herds in Hokkaido in which MAP-positive cattle were detected through RS since 2013 and subsequently also detected during FS, at least 33 herds experienced periods of more than 1 year without detection of positive animals, followed by re-detection. However, these field-observed intervals without detected positives have not been rigorously validated. They do not necessarily correspond to the low-detection periods described in this study and may instead arise from other factors, such as reintroduction of MAP. Further validation is therefore required, including analyses incorporating the age distribution of test-positive cattle and herd introduction dynamics, as well as molecular epidemiological approaches. In particular, the length and timing of the apparent periodicity are likely to depend on model parameterization. The rate of disease stage progression is influenced by multiple factors, such as the ingested MAP dose and the age at infection [25], and this aspect was not explicitly considered in the present study. Consequently, the periodicity of stage emergence may vary substantially among herds. Accordingly, under field conditions, even if RS is conducted at 5- or 7-year intervals, the testing year may still coincide with low-detection periods. A longer RS interval does not necessarily improve detection efficiency or promote a higher rate of MAP fade-out and may compromise the stability and robustness of the surveillance system.

Relative to Scenario 2 (the current JD surveillance program), both Scenario 5 (shorter FS duration) and Scenario 7 (reduced FS testing frequency) were predicted to hinder the rate of MAP fade-out within herds. In both scenarios, a substantially larger cumulative number of detected cattle was observed even among herds that were detected early by RS after MAP introduction, indicating that insufficient follow-up surveillance allowed within-herd transmission to continue despite early initial detection. In Scenario 5, shortening the FS duration appeared to undermine effective surveillance. If all follow-up tests fall within low-detection periods, false-negative herd classification may occur, allowing within-herd prevalence to rise again before the next round of RS. This repeated cycle would reduce overall detection efficiency, highlighting the need for a minimum FS duration to maintain effective surveillance. By contrast, extending the FS duration to 5 years did not substantially change prevalence trends compared with the current program. This suggests that the current FS duration of 3 years (Scenario 2) is generally adequate, as it already spans low-detection periods and provides little additional benefit from further extension. Similarly, in Scenario 7, reducing the FS testing frequency to once per year weakened the ability of follow-up surveillance to suppress ongoing transmission. Even when herds were detected early by RS, less frequent FS testing allowed infected cattle to remain undetected for longer periods, facilitating continued within-herd spread. Previous studies have reported that more frequent testing leads to a more rapid decline in within-herd prevalence [1], supporting the importance of sufficiently frequent FS testing to compensate for the limited sensitivity of diagnostic tools such as ELISA. However, increasing the testing frequency beyond the current level did not result in substantial additional reductions in prevalence, indicating that the current FS testing frequency is generally appropriate. Although testing frequency is an important component of FS, our findings suggest that appropriate timing is equally crucial to FS effectiveness. Because the probability of detecting MAP is low during low-detection periods, conducting frequent FS during these phases is likely to result in limited epidemiological benefit while incurring unnecessary government costs. In this context, the current JD surveillance program, which maintains a relatively high FS testing frequency immediately after initial detection and subsequently reduces testing intensity over time, can be regarded as an effective strategy for balancing effectiveness in suppressing within-herd transmission and operational efficiency.

Higher within-herd prevalence was predicted to be associated with lower farm profits. One contributing factor is that infected cattle, particularly those in the IH stage, produce less milk [35]. In the present study, herds with higher within-herd prevalence had more IH-stage cattle, which led to reduced milk production. By contrast, revenues from slaughter tended to increase in herds with higher within-herd prevalence. Previous studies have reported that JD reduces productivity, particularly milk yield, thereby increasing culling rates [38]. These findings suggest that in herds with higher within-herd prevalence, MAP-infected cattle are culled before completing their productive lifespan, which accelerates the replacement cycle and increases the number of cattle sent to slaughter. This accelerated replacement cycle increases the number of calves and heifers raised within herds, thereby contributing to higher rearing costs as within-herd prevalence increases. The increase in replacement costs has been recognized as one of the contributors to the economic burden associated with JD [34]. Consistent with this, the present study also predicted greater replacement-related costs in higher-prevalence herds. Furthermore, herds with higher within-herd prevalence are likely to retain more calves for herd renewal, thereby reducing surplus calves available for sale and decreasing revenues from calf sales. In addition, the increase in the number of susceptible calves raised within herds may facilitate transmission and accelerate the increase in within-herd prevalence. Previous study has also suggested that higher replacement rates are associated with faster increases in prevalence [35]. On the other hand, because the present model was designed to maintain a constant number of adult cows within each herd, no substantial changes were observed in production costs for adult cows at the herd level, even as within-herd prevalence increased. These findings suggest that JD imposes both direct economic losses, such as reduced productivity of MAP-infected cattle, and indirect costs from the accelerated replacement cycle, resulting in complex and multifaceted economic impacts at the farm level. In addition, under current Japanese regulations, milk from JD-diagnosed cows is legally prohibited from sale, and the slaughter of such cattle is subject to legal restrictions. These regulatory constraints reduce revenues from milk sales and slaughter, potentially exacerbating the overall economic losses attributable to JD.

Compared with Scenario 1 (no surveillance), farm-level economic losses were predicted to be reduced under the current JD surveillance program. This outcome likely reflects the program’s effectiveness in suppressing increases in within-herd prevalence and promoting MAP fade-out. Even under the current program, some farms were predicted to experience increased within-herd prevalence and associated economic losses. However, because prevalence remained much lower than in Scenario 1, the overall magnitude of these losses was relatively small. Furthermore, government compensation for the culling of test-positive cattle is expected to partially offset such losses, indicating that the net economic burden on farms would be relatively limited. Nevertheless, the current model did not account for several additional potential sources of economic loss. These include loss of sales opportunities due to movement restrictions imposed on cattle from MAP-positive status herds, increased facility and labor costs resulting from the inability to outsource heifer rearing, and the labor and psychological burdens on farmers due to diagnostic testing and control measures. Although not incorporated into the simulation, these factors may represent non-negligible burdens in actual farm management.

The economic outcomes presented in this study are based on conditions specific to Hokkaido, including its production system, economic values, and regulatory framework, such as compulsory culling with partial compensation. Therefore, the economic results reported here may not be directly applicable to other regions or countries with different production systems, economic structures, and policy environments. Previous studies have indicated that the magnitude of economic losses due to JD varies not only with within-herd prevalence but also with production systems and economic characteristics [34]. Accordingly, caution is warranted when extrapolating the quantitative results, and region-specific parameterization would be required for precise economic evaluation. However, the relative effectiveness of the surveillance strategies evaluated in this study is primarily driven by general epidemiological mechanisms, including testing frequency, diagnostic performance, and the timing of detection. These mechanisms are not specific to Hokkaido and are likely to be applicable across other regions and countries.

Simulation runs without secondary transmission were associated with substantially lower government costs than those in which secondary transmission occurred. This is because early detection and culling of introduced MAP-infected cattle prevented within-herd transmission, thereby reducing the need for subsequent culling and follow-up testing. Thus, surveillance strategies that can prevent within-herd transmission at an early stage are likely to be critically important for improving the cost-effectiveness of JD control.

In Scenario 3, the cumulative government costs over the 40-year simulation period were predicted to be the lowest among all scenarios under conditions of secondary transmission. This was attributable to the high likelihood of early detection after MAP introduction, which enabled removal of infected cattle before substantial within-herd spread occurred. Although cumulative government costs during the first 10 years were higher than in other scenarios—reflecting front-loaded expenditures for early detection and culling—overall costs were reduced over the full 40-year period because subsequent culling and follow-up testing were minimized, thereby improving cost-effectiveness. In contrast, Scenario 3 was predicted to have relatively higher cumulative government costs than Scenario 2 when considering only simulation runs without secondary transmission. However, because government costs in runs without secondary transmission were substantially lower than those in runs with secondary transmission, this higher cumulative cost is likely to be of limited practical importance at the individual-farm level. At the regional level, where the majority of farms are MAP-negative, shortening the RS interval may increase total government expenditures due to more frequent testing. Nevertheless, the overall cost-effectiveness of Scenario 3 remains favorable given its strong impact on early detection and MAP fade-out. In some settings, the use of lower-cost diagnostic approaches, such as environmental testing, may further improve efficiency [37].

Government costs were also predicted to remain higher in the scenarios with shortened FS duration or reduced FS testing frequency (Scenarios 5 and 7). This is likely because the reduction in FS testing failed to sufficiently suppress within-herd MAP transmission, leading to higher within-herd prevalence and, consequently, increased requirements for testing and culling. Although FS entails substantial costs, merely reducing the number of tests may, in the long term, result in an overall increase in government costs.

It is important to note that the government cost estimates in this study did not include certain components, such as the depreciation of general-purpose laboratory equipment (e.g., PCR thermal cyclers), utility expenses, or personnel time associated with administrative procedures. Therefore, actual government costs may be higher than the model-based estimates presented here.

One limitation of the present simulation is that the risk of MAP reintroduction was not incorporated into the model. Under field conditions, herds are continually exposed to the possibility of MAP reintroduction through cattle purchases, as well as the movement of people and vehicles. Consequently, by not accounting for reintroduction, the model may have overestimated the MAP fade-out rate relative to that observed under field conditions. Furthermore, the rate of disease progression in MAP-infected cattle has been reported to be faster when infection occurs earlier in life and when the ingested dose of MAP is greater [25]. Under field conditions, an increase in prevalence is likely to elevate the infection risk among young cattle, potentially resulting in both higher ingested doses and earlier exposure. This, in turn, may accelerate the within-herd spread of infection, further amplifying transmission dynamics. Taken together, these considerations suggest that MAP prevalence, and consequently the associated economic losses and government costs, may have been underestimated relative to field conditions. Therefore, the actual burden may be greater than the model predictions, which should be carefully considered when interpreting the findings. In interpreting the results of this study in light of discrepancies between the model assumptions and field conditions, it should be noted that the results should be evaluated relatively across scenarios rather than in absolute terms. The reason is that, in relative comparisons, systematic errors in absolute predictions tend to cancel out. However, it has also been pointed out that comparisons between scenarios may still be affected by errors in absolute predictions [14]. The omission of MAP reintroduction is likely to have a greater impact in scenarios where within-herd prevalence is effectively reduced and MAP fade-out occurs frequently. In such scenarios, particularly those with shorter RS intervals, the model may overestimate the probability of sustained MAP elimination, as reintroduction events in real-world settings could re-establish infection. Consequently, the relative advantage of more intensive surveillance strategies may be somewhat overestimated in the absence of reintroduction. In contrast, the assumption of a closed herd without cattle introduction may have a greater impact in scenarios with higher within-herd prevalence. In Hokkaido, where the overall prevalence is low, introduced cattle are likely to be uninfected and are typically pregnant heifers with relatively low susceptibility. Therefore, under field conditions, the introduction of cattle into already contaminated herds may partially dilute the pool of susceptible individuals and slow the rate of within-herd transmission. Taken together, these assumptions may lead to scenario-dependent biases in transmission dynamics, which should be considered when interpreting the results.

In addition to these assumptions, herd size was fixed at 100 adult cows in the present model. Herd size has been reported to be associated with MAP prevalence and may influence within-herd transmission dynamics and herd-level detection probability for MAP infection [20, 30]. However, the mechanisms linking herd size to within-herd transmission dynamics have not been clearly established. To assess the impact of this assumption, additional simulations were conducted using herd sizes of 50 (small herd) and 200 (large herd) adult cows, while fixing the initial number of infected animals at one. Under the current JD surveillance program, the relative prevalence was higher in smaller herds during the early phase following MAP introduction, reflecting the higher initial proportion of infected animals (Supplementary Fig 3). However, no substantial difference in prevalence was observed between herd sizes 40 years after MAP introduction, suggesting that herd size may have a limited influence on long-term infection dynamics under the assumptions of the present model. In contrast, field studies have reported higher prevalence in larger herds [33]. This discrepancy may reflect the influence of factors not explicitly represented in the model, including variation in stocking density and management practices across herd sizes. Therefore, the present model may not fully capture the potential impact of herd size on transmission dynamics under field conditions, and the results should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to herds of different sizes.

Based on the present findings, the current JD surveillance program in Hokkaido is likely to have a meaningful effect in promoting MAP fade-out within herds and mitigating associated economic losses. This effect appears to be achieved through the combination of early detection of MAP-positive herds by RS and repeated, sufficiently frequent FS testing. However, the current RS interval carries a risk of delayed detection. In recent years, the number of MAP-infected cattle detected in Hokkaido has shown an increasing trend [21]. This suggests that, if current trends persist, MAP infection may expand further and become increasingly difficult to control. Our simulation results indicate that shortening the RS interval could substantially improve early detection and further enhance MAP fade-out within herds. Therefore, from an epidemiological perspective, adopting a shorter RS interval should be considered to improve the effectiveness of JD control programs.
