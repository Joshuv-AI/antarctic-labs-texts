Improving normothermic machine perfusion and blood transfusion through biocompatible blood silicification
Authors: Chuanyi Lei, Zeyu Li, Shuhao Ma, Qi Zhang, Jimin Guo, Qing Ouyang, Qi Lei, Liang Zhou, Junxian Yang, Jiangguo Lin, Romy Ettlinger, Stefan Wuttke, Xuejin Li, C. Jeffrey Brinker, Wei Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceBlood resources are extremely valuable in modern healthcare systems, and the multiscenario applicability of blood and its erythrocyte fragility and surface antigen recognition system contribute to the clinical status of blood shortages. This study marks a significant advancement by achieving nearly 100% cryoprotection of blood, effectively resolving resource wastage issues. The method presented offers a straightforward, efficient, and cost-effective approach to developing universal blood. Silicified blood offers the advantages of allogeneic mechanical perfusion and xenotransfusion. In conclusion, the engineered blood modification technology pioneered in this study is simple, reliable, and easily scalable, with excellent biocompatibility, and has the potential to trigger a revolution in future clinical applications.

Normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) is a technique in which oxygen carriers, nutrients, and medication are pumped into the recipient organ to mimic the natural blood circulation process (1, 2). This technology has proven to be effective in the functional recovery and objective functional evaluation of transplanted organs, which has significantly increased the utilization rate of marginal donations after circulatory death, brainstem death donors, and discarded transplanted organs (3). In 2018, Nasralla et al. reported a randomized controlled trial of 220 liver transplants, which showed a lower abandonment rate (11.7%) and longer preservation time via NMP (4). Meanwhile, Eshmuminov et al. realized the preservation of injured human livers in a functional state for 1 wk using an integrated perfusion machine (5). These promising studies demonstrate that NMP can greatly optimize organ utilization, leading to an increase in the donor pool, the number of transplant patients, and the success rate of organ transplantation.

During NMP the oxygen-carrying components play an important role in maintaining the physiological metabolic behavior of the recipient organ, which can be divided into two main categories: artificial oxygen carriers and natural oxygen carriers (red blood cells, RBCs). Artificial oxygen transporters (noncellular), including hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (e.g., HBOC-201) (6–8) and perfluorocarbons (9, 10), exhibit the features of long-shelf life time, no cross-matching issues, and abundant sources, however, they show the tendency to cause vasoconstriction, induce nephrotoxicity, and other potentially negative consequences. In contrast, the most common physiological, natural oxygen-carrying RBCs, offer longevity in circulation, excellent mechanical flexibility, and enhanced enzyme systems associated with carbon dioxide transport (5, 11, 12). Nevertheless, several drawbacks limit their application in real-life scenarios, including i) short storage time—RBCs are stored for only 4 wk at 4 °C, which currently leads to enormous wastage (at least 33%) (13). Long-term storage of RBCs can be achieved by cryopreservation, however, the added cryoprotectants face the issue of potential toxicity coupled with inevitable hemolysis. To date, 100% cryopreservation of RBCs has not yet been reported (14–16) ii) blood group cross-matching—the diversity of antigens on the surface of RBCs results in the risks of cross-matching for both allogeneic and xenogeneic transfusions (i.e., using blood from two different individuals of one or two different species for transfusions) (17–20) iii) mechanical damage—RBCs undergo squeezing deformation, distortion, and fragmentation during perfusion, leading to nonnegligible hemolysis (21, 22); and iv) blood source scarcity—mechanical perfusion of blood crossing different species may significantly address the current widespread blood scarcity problem, especially with respect to the huge demand for blood during perfusion (23–25). To address the above challenges and related ethical issues, the development of a universal, biocompatible, easily scalable, and efficient RBC engineering technology is of great importance.

Currently, approaches to engineer RBCs include i) covalent/noncovalent surface grafting of various drug molecules, antibodies, or hitchhiking nanoparticles (NPs) (26–28); ii) encapsulation of small molecules or functional nanoobjects inside erythrocytes under hypotonic conditions (29–31); iii) enzymatic cleavage of RBC surface antigens to overcome blood group mismatch (32, 33); iv) gene editing by regulated expression of programmable nucleases (34, 35); and v) cell-in-shell to form nanometric artificial protective or shielding shells at the periphery of RBCs using layer-by-layer assembly (e.g., polyelectrolytes) (36), in situ polymerization (e.g., polydopamine) (37, 38) and superassembly approach (e.g., coordination assembly of NPs with organic building blocks) (39, 40). Despite successes in RBC structural reconstruction and functionalization, current engineering strategies for machine perfusion applications urgently need to be improved, as they are time-consuming (e.g., hypotonic loading), not easily scalable (e.g., layer-by-layer assembly of polyelectrolytes), and conducted under specific, restrictive conditions that often result in RBC lysis. In addition, engineered RBCs (e.g., created by surface grafting or hypotonic treatment) are more fragile and environmentally sensitive than the parent RBCs, which accelerates mechanical damage during perfusion. Importantly, none of the above engineering procedures allow for the long-term storage of RBCs.

The biomimetic silicification approach, wherein nanoscopic siliceous exoskeletons are formed over all intra- and extracellular interfaces, provides fragile cells with mechanically strong, chemically inert, nondegradable silica armor conferring chemical and thermal stability as well as resistance to harsh environmental stresses (e.g., heat, desiccation, and lytic enzymatic degradation). Its simplicity and demonstrated performance have established it as an attractive strategy for modulating cell properties and cellular functions (41–44). Our previous work has demonstrated that the self-limiting, protein-catalyzed silica condensation process can not only preserve cellular structures from the nanoscale (e.g., DNA, organelles) to the macroscale (e.g., mammalian cell, organs) with high fidelity, but also enables the preservation of biomolecular functions for periods up to years at room temperature—in essence serving to “freeze life-in-silica” as an alternative to cryopreservation (15, 45–48).

To enhance the capability of RBCs for NMP and even further applicable to allogeneic/xenogeneic perfusion, a biocompatible and biomimetic silicification approach called “shielding-augmenting RBC-in-nanoscale amorphous silica (SARNAS)” is described here (Fig. 1). The key is to precisely control the hydrolysis form of silicic acid to achieve conformal stabilization of RBC within silica-based exoskeletons in a controlled manner. First, hydrolyzed large silica nanoclusters are preformed as they tend to rather interact noncovalently with the extracellular surface of RBC and interchange with the hydrogen-bonded water at interfaces (due to the higher energy barriers to cross RBC membrane), and then these slowly condensed to form a nanoscopic continuous silica network at low temperature. This results in the controllable formation of armored RBC/silica biocomposites which not only maintain the structural features and dimensions, the membrane integrity, and normal cellular functions (e.g., metabolism, oxygen-carrying capability) of the parent RBCs, but also exhibit enhanced resistance to external stressors (such as detergent, toxic NPs, and freezing conditions. This work highlights the outstanding performance of such RBC/silica biocomposites obtained through the SARNAS strategy with respect to i) their flow dynamics and ideal mechanical properties and protection of RBCs against damage stemming from ice crystallization during cryopreservation; ii) their good biocompatibility and biodistribution confirmed in in vivo experiments; and iii) their efficient shielding of surface antigens, which enables them to serve as universal blood for allogeneic mechanical perfusion in rat liver transplantation and for xenogeneic, cross-species transfusion in a mouse model with human blood.

Results

RBC Silicification and Structure Characterization.

The interplay between inorganic silica and RBCs can significantly impact the behavior and function of the formed RBC/silica biocomposites. Self-limited cellular silicification (usually referred to as silica bioreplication) occurs at approximately pH 3.0, where soluble silicic acid species are maintained in a monomeric, uncharged state in which they rapidly diffuse and interchange with water at all intra- and extracellular interfaces and are converted to nanoscopic conformal amorphous silica by proximal protein-catalyzed silica condensation (49–51). However, simultaneous extracellular/intracellular silicification mechanically strengthens/stiffens the entire RBC structure thereby reducing RBC deformability during circulation. Also pronounced erythrocyte hemolysis was observed at pH 3.0 during silicification (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Increasing the pH during the prehydrolysis of siloxane can avoid erythrocyte hemolysis and accelerate the hydrolysis/condensation of silica, resulting in the formation of larger silica nanoclusters. In SI Appendix, Fig. S1B, no erythrocyte hemolysis was found at pH 5.0 across various silicification concentrations (ranging from 0 to 100 mM). Moreover, wide-angle X-ray scattering was used to investigate the tetramethylorthosilicate (TMOS) hydrolysis and condensation processes. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2, results indicate that at pH 3.0, peak intensity remained largely unchanged, consistent with previous reports indicating predominant monomeric silicic acid without condensation (49). Conversely, with increasing pH to 5.0, the peak intensity gradually increased, and at pH 7.0, a rapid increase in peak intensity was observed, confirming pH’s role in accelerating silicic acid condensation. Calculations revealed that at pH 5.0, after 30 min of reaction, the radius of silica cluster reached ~1.3 nm, compared to the 0.3 to 0.4 nm radius of monomeric silicic acid. At pH 7.0, the cluster size exceeded the calculable range after 30 min of reaction. Previous investigations by Piane et al. confirmed that while the transmembrane free energy of silica monomers is akin to that of water molecules, larger silica clusters present greater challenges for transmembrane passage due to the high energy barrier, ΔG‡, leading to their accumulation and subsequent deposition outside the cell membrane (Fig. 2A) (52, 53). Nonetheless, localizing the silica precursor primarily on the surface of erythrocytes promotes the development of a core-shell structure, potentially preserving the internal deformability of RBCs while augmenting their ability to withstand harsh environments. Simultaneously, this process achieves antigen shielding, thereby resulting in universal blood compatibility. Based on the above considerations, in our case, we performed in vitro silicification at pH 5.0 with a reduced reaction temperature of 4 °C.

In a standard procedure, RBCs are suspended at a density of 1.5 × 108 cells/mL in an isotonic aqueous solution of 154 mM NaCl and 10 mM TMOS at pH 5.0 for 60 min at 4 °C and then rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline. To ensure the integrity of the RBCs and to avoid cell aggregation caused by excessive silicification, the concentrations of TMOS were optimized (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The overall appearance of the native and low concentration silicifiedRBCs (Si-RBCs) (0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10 mM) was smooth and maintained a biconcave shape. Conversely, Si-RBCs formed at high TMOS concentration (30, 50, 100 mM) exhibited roughness and aggregation, and were relatively similar when analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and bright field (Fig. 2 B and E and SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S5). Compared to native RBCs (Fig. 2 C and D), the ideal concentration for effective silicification of the cell surface was found to be 10 mM TMOS, yielding an apparent silica layer approximately 25 nm thick as observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Fig. 2 F and G) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (Fig. 2H and SI Appendix, Figs. S6–S9). Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy confirmed that higher silica precursor concentrations resulted in higher Si-content of silicified blood, with approximately 0.157 and 0.178 mg Si per mL of blood for the samples treated with 5 mM and 10 mM TMOS, respectively (Fig. 2K).

Following the silicification process, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy showed new peaks at 1,080, 950, 790, and 450 cm−1, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching of Si-O and Si-OH, symmetric stretching of Si-O, and bending vibrations of Si-O-Si, respectively (Fig. 2L). The presence of two unaltered bands at 1,645 and 1,525 cm−1, associated with the stretching modes of amide I and II from cellular proteins, suggests that the silicification process does not disrupt the structure of cellular proteins. Instead, it serves as a catalyst without altering their configuration (41). A significant difference in size was observed especially for the high concentration treatment (100 mM), confirming the increasing silicification on the surface of individual cells (Fig. 2J). Furthermore, the number of RBCs was examined before and after mineralization, and no significant change in the number was found at low concentrations of TMOS (0, 5, 10, 30 mM), but the number of individual RBCs decreased dramatically at high concentration, probably due to TMOS hydrolysis producing abundant Si-OH bonds that cross-link erythrocytes together resulting in significant agglomeration (Fig. 2I) (54). The Si-RBCs samples can be stably stored in Alsever’s solution and exhibit the same hemolysis profile as native RBCs (Fig. 2M).

Native and Abiotic Properties Study.

To determine the physical properties and biofunctional changes in Si-RBCs, the major membrane proteins, cell surface charge, cell membrane damage, metabolites, and oxygen-carrying function of the Si-RBCs and native RBCs were measured and compared. The conventional sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) result in Fig. 3A showed that the key erythrocyte membrane proteins including the cytoskeletal proteins α/β-spectrin, band 3, and protein 4.1/4.2, were well preserved, suggesting that no protein mutation or deletion occurred during silicification. In order to verify that the silicification treatment did not induce disruption of the erythrocyte membrane, the silica shell was labeled with FITC. Subsequently, the silicified erythrocytes were subjected to treatment with a dilute HF-NH4F buffer solution to entirely eliminate the silica shell layer from the erythrocyte surface. Examination using Annexin-V reagent, as depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S10, demonstrated that the treated erythrocytes still maintained their integrity, indicating the absence of membrane ectropion. Also, only a slight decrease in surface charge (Fig. 3B) was observed in Si-RBCs.

To further investigate whether silicified RBCs (Si-RBCs) could preserve the biological functions of erythrocytes in vitro, all the Si-RBCs samples were characterized using 2,3-diphosphoglyceric acid (2,3-DPG), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and oxygenation curve determination and then compared with native RBCs. ATP is a key substance in maintaining the basic biconcave shape and energy potential of erythrocytes. As shown in Fig. 3D, the ATP content decreased slightly after silicification, and no significant difference was observed compared with the control group, P = 0.8791 and P = 0.3161, respectively. Meanwhile, 2,3-DPG is an important intermediate product of erythrocyte metabolism, which is related to the affinity of erythrocytes for oxygen and their oxygen-carrying function. The 2,3-DPG content did not change before and after treatment, and the mean values were 3,517.8 mmol/L, 3001.4 mmol/L, and 2,965.4 mmol/L, respectively (Fig. 3C). Testing the oxygen-carrying capability of RBCs showed that the time required for Si-RBCs to reach 50% oxygenation was almost identical to that of native ones (Fig. 3E), indicating that the silicification process and the resulting silica exoskeleton do not affect the binding rate of hemoglobin to external oxygen (45). The corresponding maximum absorption peaks in the oxygenated and deoxygenated states are also the same, at 415 nm and 430 nm, respectively, with reversible transitions (Fig. 3 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). On the other hand, CD47 serves as a crucial self-recognition signal in erythrocytes, aiding in the evasion of phagocytosis. Through incubation of CD47 antibody with both native and silicified erythrocytes, the outcomes presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S12 demonstrated the effective recognition of the CD47 antigen on silicified erythrocytes, indicating that the silicification process minimally influenced self-marker recognition. Furthermore, fluorescent bleaching experiments showed that the Si-RBC membrane also has excellent elasticity (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). These results indicate that the biological properties of Si-RBCs are almost unchanged, which provides the basic assurance for perfusion applications.

To benchmark the protective effect of the formed silica exoskeleton, both native and Si-RBCs were exposed to various harsh environmental conditions (Fig. 3H). When the cells were tested with different concentrations of hemolytic toxins (HIO) and Stöber NPs, which are known to readily cause erythrocyte hemolysis, the 10 mM Si-RBCs remained unaffected, with a hemolysis rate of less than 5%, even at the highest concentration of 0.92 µg/mL and 2 mg/mL, respectively (Fig. 3 I and J). On the other hand, both native RBCs and low concentration 5 mM Si-RBCs showed an increase in hemolysis rate with increasing concentration up to 99.2% and 73.9% (for toxins), and 90.0% and 49.3% (for particles), respectively. These results suggest that the introduced silica exoskeleton provides effective resistance to toxins and can protect RBCs from hemolysis.

Long-term cryopreservation of RBCs plays an important role in clinical medicine. During freezing, ice crystal recrystallization can mechanically damage RBCs, causing osmotic imbalance and increased hemolysis. Therefore, native RBCs and those protected by a silicified shell were exposed to liquid nitrogen (−196 °C) for 2 h, followed by a slow thaw at 4 °C over several hours. Native RBCs displayed a low recovery rate of approximately 38.1%, whereas 48.8% of 5 mM Si-RBCs could be recovered. For 10 mM Si-RBCs, cell recovery was increased to an astounding 96.9% without the addition of any toxic solvent (Fig. 3K). To compare the effectiveness of the SARNAS strategy, we also examined RBCs protected by polydopamine and Fe3+-tannic acid-based protective layers. These yielded much lower recoveries of 37.9% and 47.8% than Si-RBCs, respectively (Fig. 3K). To evaluate whether the mechanical properties of the treated RBCs contribute to their increased freezing recovery, AFM experiments were conducted and we found that the mechanical stiffness of the RBCs’ surface progressively increases with increasing silicification, to 0.9 Kpa, 3.26 Kpa, and 5.53 Kpa, respectively. In contrast, PDA and Fe3+-TA treated RBCs had a much lower stiffness of 0.99 Kpa and 2.93 Kpa, respectively (Fig. 3L). These results indicate that the improved mechanical properties of Si-RBCs may contribute to their resistance to ice crystals and recrystallization, ultimately leading to incredible cell recovery.

Flow Dynamics and Mechanical Study.

A crucial feature of engineered RBCs is the ability to maintain the deformability necessary to pass through narrow capillaries. To quantify the mechanics, morphology, and flow dynamics of the Si-RBCs, the shear modulus (μ), bending rigidity (κ), and surface area-to-volume ratio (SA/V) were first measured using micropipette aspersion. Fig. 4A shows the deformation of the Si-RBCs treated with different TMOS concentrations. The shear modulus (Fig. 4 D–F), bending rigidity, and surface area-to-volume ratio of the RBCs in the control group were found to be ca. 7.8 ± 3.7 µN/m, 26.9 ± 10.8 kBT, 1.32 ± 0.04 µm−1, respectively, which were in agreement with values determined in previous studies of about 4 to 12 µN/m (55–59), 10 to 50 kBT (60, 61), and 1.21 to 1.50 µm−1 (62), respectively. For the 5 mM Si-RBCs, the mechanical and morphological properties are measured to be µ = 7.9 ± 4.9 µN/m, 35.1 ± 29.6 kBT, and 1.29 ± 0.09 µm−1, which are very close to the cells in the control group. With increasing concentrations of TMOS, the mechanical and morphological properties of Si-RBCs change significantly. The shear modulus increases to 30.1 ± 6.8 µN/m, 236.5 ± 135.0 µN/m, and 482.2 ± 163.6 µN/m, for the 10 mM, 15 mM, and 20 mM Si-RBCs, respectively. The bending rigidity increases to 120.2 ± 137.7 kBT and 493.5 ± 449.0 kBT for 10 mM- and 15 mM Si-RBCs, respectively. Furthermore, the surface area-to-volume ratio changes to 1.28 ± 0.13 µm−1 and 1.17 ± 0.08 µm−1 for 10 mM, 15 mM Si-RBCs, respectively.

Based on the experimental data, mechanical and dynamical simulations of Si-RBCs were performed. To investigate the associated elastic response, we subject the Si-RBCs to stretching deformation by applying an external tensile force in diametrically opposite directions, analogous to optical tweezers experiments. The total stretching force, F, is applied in opposite directions to 3% vertices of the Si-RBCs membrane in the model. The stretching response of the Si-RBCs is characterized by the variation of the axial DA and transverse DT diameters (Fig. 4G). The stretching response of 5 mM Si-RBCs is very similar to that of healthy RBCs, indicating good deformability. However, the 15 mM and the 20 mM Si-RBCs deform little, just like the RBCs parasitized by Plasmodium malignancies at the schizont stage. These results demonstrate that the mechanical properties and morphology of Si-RBCs treated with low concentrations of TMOS remain virtually unchanged compared to native RBCs. In contrast, Si-RBCs become stiffer and more spherical with increasing concentration. Changes in these properties have been shown to be important factors affecting the deformation and flow dynamics of RBCs in capillary and splenic slits (63–65).

To test the ability of Si-RBCs to flow through capillaries, a microfluidic chip was fabricated (Fig. 4B). The microchannel width W = 5 μm, height H = 5 μm, and length L = 30 μm were chosen to mimic the dimensions of natural capillaries. The pressure at the inlet of the channel was set to be P = 25 to 100 mm H2O. The average velocity of healthy RBCs passing through the microchannel is about 148.8 ± 64.5 µm/s, 233.5 ± 69.8 µm/s, 317.7 ± 118.4 µm/s, and 393.0 ± 89.3 µm/s, for P = 25 mm H2O, 50 mm H2O, 75 mm H2O, and 100 mm H2O, respectively. These test results are similar to those observed for capillary and splenic slits (66, 67) In this experiment, two different flow patterns of Si-RBCs were observed, passing or being trapped (Fig. 4C). Fig. 4H shows the velocity of Si-RBCs that are able to pass through the microchannel. For 5 mM and 10 mM Si-RBCs, all cells can pass through the microchannel. For 5 mM Si-RBCs, the measured velocities were about 142.1 ± 24.5 µm/s, 217.1 ± 63.4 µm/s, 274.2 ± 77.9 µm/s, and 356.8 ± 121.0 µm/s, for P = 25 mm H2O, 50 mm H2O, 75 mm H2O, and 100 mm H2O, respectively. And for 10 mM Si-RBCs, the velocity is reduced to be about 115.6 ± 33.0 µm/s, 192.0 ± 43.9 µm/s, 291.7 ± 45.4 µm/s, and 313.4 ± 42.1 µm/s, for P = 25 mm H2O, 50 mm H2O, 75 mm H2O, and 100 mm H2O, respectively. These fluid dynamics can be captured by computational models as well (66, 67), see Fig. 4 C, I, and J showing the different flow patterns of Si-RBCs treated with different concentrations of TMOS. All the healthy RBCs, 5 mM and 10 mM Si-RBCs are able to pass through the microchannel. Part of the 15 mM Si-RBCs can pass under P = 75 mm H2O and 100 mm H2O, while all are trapped under P = 25 mm H2O and 50 mm H2O. For the 20 mM Si-RBCs, no cells are able to pass through the microchannel. For the 5 mM and 10 mM Si-RBCs, both the microfluidic experiments and the simulations show that the effect of limited rigidity increase on the flow of cells in the capillaries is small. Previous experiments and simulation studies have confirmed that even in the narrowest splenic slits in the body, it is the surface area-to-volume ratio that the key factor which influences flow rather than the mechanical properties of the cell membrane. However, compared to the control group, the surface area-to-volume ratio of 5 mM and 10 mM Si-RBCs changes by only about 2% and 3%, so the effect of these changes in mechanical properties on the flow is considered to be acceptable, as confirmed in subsequent animal experiments. Taken together, the SARNAS approach provides a powerful strategy to modulate the mechanical properties of RBCs.

Immune Recognition Study.

Immunocamouflage of RBCs occurs when recognition of the antigen by the relevant antibody is blocked. In our case, agglutination assays of anti-A, anti-B, and anti-D (Rh) sera with native and Si-RBCs were used to assess the effectiveness of continuous silica exoskeleton encapsulation in preventing recognition of antigenic sites. When treated with their anti-type sera [anti-A, anti-B, or anti-D (Rh)], 10 mM Si-RBCs remained unaffected, while native and 0.5 mM, 1 mM, 2 mM, and 5 mM Si-RBCs harshly agglutinated (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Meanwhile, the surface antigens of silicified erythrocytes were further quantified by flow cytometry by incubating blood A antibodies with native and silicified erythrocytes, respectively. The results showed that the antigen positivity of untreated erythrocytes was close to 100%, whereas that of silicified erythrocytes was very low, indicating the highly effective antigen shielding capability of the developed silicification technique (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). This indicates that a certain thickness of the silicified layer is essential for an effective camouflage material to prevent activation of immune system and thus allow the survival of engineered RBCs. Due to the effective surface antigen blockade effect, 10 mM Si-RBCs were selected for the in vivo immune response study. To accomplish this, rabbits were immunized intramuscularly with 1× phosphate buffer saline (PBS), RhD (+) RBCs, or Si-RBCs, respectively. Rabbits were vaccinated twice at 14-d intervals and sera were collected every week after the first immunization (Fig. 5A), while immunoglobulin G enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was used to detect the potential immune response. Treatment with RhD (+) erythrocytes resulted in a rapid and robust activation of the immune response in the experimental animals after one and two boosters (Fig. 5B), whereas only low levels of antibody titers were induced by Si-RBCs and RhD (−) erythrocytes. Specifically, RhD (+) RBCs group antibody titer levels in the rabbit sera were much higher than those of the Si-RBCs group (P = 0.0319) and RhD (−) RBCs (P = 0.0330), respectively, in the 5th week (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that the silicified layer protects the epitopes of RBCs in vivo, suggesting the direct elimination of molecular recognition between antigen and antibody. The biosafety of three groups in rabbits was further evaluated, mainly including hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity. The level of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALP) enzyme levels for assessing liver injury, and the level of creatinine (CREA), urea nitrogen (BUN), and uric acid (UA) enzyme for assessing acute kidney injury were measured, and all the above experimental results are within normal range, indicating the biosafety and biocompatibility of Si-RBCs (Fig. 5D). The in vitro and in vivo data confirm that the Si-RBCs can avoid immune responses and agglutination reactions in vivo, thus ensuring successful and healthy blood transfusion.

In Vivo Metabolic Behavior Study.

Due to the effective antibody blockade to avoid immune response, Si-RBCs could serve as universal blood to solve the problems of unequal distribution of transfusion resources, deficiency, and mismatch during transfusion. To confirm this conjecture, blood transfusion experiments in BABL/c mice were designed, which were preceded by mouse cross-matching and followed by the administration of type-matched native RBCs and Si-RBCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Organ distribution was monitored based on fluorescence labeled with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DIR) dye with IVIS Spectrum imaging and quantified in photons at 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h. Mice treated with the Si-RBCs group showed substantial aggregation of fluorescent signals in two important metabolic filtering organs, including the liver and spleen, consistent with the native RBCs group (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S17). The quantitative measure of organ distribution showed that there was no significant difference in both groups (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Figs. S18–S20), indicating that the native and Si-RBCs have similar metabolic pathways. Furthermore, the transfusion capacity of Si-RBCs and the associated metabolic safety were assessed by multiple long-cycle transfusion experiments. To accomplish this, mice were treated with labeled-DIR type-matched RBCs or Si-RBCs (1.5 × 108/200 µL), respectively. Mice were injected intravenously three times at 0, 3, and 6 d intervals, and sera were collected every 3 d after the first injection (Fig. 5G) to examine their effects on the immune system and activation of inflammatory factors. The whole process took 51 d (37). In Fig. 5K, the transport diagram showed that the fluorescence signal gradually increased after each injection of native RBCs or Si-RBCs, and gradually decreased. Meanwhile, the change trend of the two groups was consistent, indicating that the in vivo circulation cycle of RBCs after silicification is not affected. Routine blood tests are used to determine blood conditions and diseases by examining changes in the amount and morphological distribution of blood cells, which are sensitive to many pathological changes in the organism. The results showed that three transfusions of Si-RBCs did not cause any index abnormalities (SI Appendix, Fig. S21), and their content was indistinguishable from that of native ones, proving their potential for routine transfusion. In addition, there were no significant differences (P = 0.3818, P = 0.3572 respectively) in serum levels of inflammatory cytokines [cytokines interleukin6 (IL-6) and Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α) (Fig. 5 H and I)] and immune activators (P = 0.9838, P = 0.9420 respectively) [complement 3 and complement 4 (Fig. 5J)], suggesting that Si-RBCs have the same primitive properties of inactivating inflammatory and immune responses as native RBCs. The biosafety of Si-RBCs was further evaluated by assessing the extent of damage to the liver and kidneys, the major metabolic organs in mice. The results revealed that the serum levels of enzymes and metabolites (ALT, AST, ALP, TBIL) related to liver function (Fig. 5M) and metabolites (CREA, UREA, UA) related to kidney function (Fig. 5L) were in the normal range, indicating that the injection of Si-RBCs did not cause significant liver and kidney injury. Tissue section structures of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were further examined to determine injury and damage. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed intact microstructure in the tissue sections of each organ, with clear and contrasting staining of the nucleus and cytoplasm, and no obvious abnormalities (SI Appendix, Fig. S22). All the results confirmed that the Si-RBCs had good biocompatibility and excellent in vivo circulation effect, while showing no immunogenicity, inflammatory reaction, or organ damage.

Normothermic Mechanical Perfusion Study.

To analyze the biosafety, high oxygen-carrying capacity, and excellent stability, the Si-RBCs were investigated in a rat liver transplantation model. The liver model was constructed by microsurgery and then connected to the perfusion device for normothermic (37 °C) perfusion after a short cold saline perfusion time of 20 min (Fig. 6A). The perfusion device recapitulates blood supply through the portal vein of the liver. The portal flow rates of 2.0 to 2.47 mL min−1g−1 were kept almost constant at a normal level throughout the whole perfusion (Fig. 6D). This suggests that Si-RBCs do not cause vasospasticity and obstruction (68). The portal vein receives oxygen-rich blood (maintained at 440 to 570 mmHg) to prevent liver congestion (Fig. 6E). The CO2 tension (maintained at 28.7 to 38.6 mmHg) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23A) is adjusted via the oxygenator to maintain blood pH between 7.3 and 7.42 (Fig. 6F). Morphological photographs of perfused livers at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h were taken (Fig. 6 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S24), which showed a red and shiny surface with uniform texture and clear borders in both native and Si-RBCs groups. Liver tissues were collected 0, 2, 4 h after the surgery. The pathological condition of the internal liver tissues was further examined by pathological section analysis. H&E-stained pictures of liver tissues showed punctate necrosis of hepatocytes, vacuolar degeneration of hepatocytes, and hepatocellular edema at the initial stage of perfusion (0 h). With the prolongation of the perfusion time, the liver tissues in native and Si-RBCs groups showed no serious lesions, including hepatocytes and bile duct cells, indicating that Si-RBCs were biologically safe, had a good oxygen-carrying capacity and no side effects on the liver (Fig. 6 B and C). The perfusion solutions were collected 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h after surgery. Insulin was added to the perfusion at 0 h to maintain physiological levels of glucose metabolism. The perfused livers showed an immediate increase in lactate and a similar release of glucose and increasing bile production, which decreased after 1 h (native RBCs group: mean peak lactate = 6.85 mmol L−1 and mean peak glucose = 28.8 mmol L−1; Si-RBCs group: mean peak lactate = 6.47 mmol L−1 and mean peak glucose = 20.5 mmol L−1). Perfusate glucose continued to decrease with oxygen delivery (Fig. 6 G and H), indicating that the liver has been consuming glucose to produce ATP and carrying out gluconeogenic functions. Meanwhile, the lactate content was positively correlated with the glucose content. In addition, liver injury during perfusion was further evaluated. The levels of cell injury markers AST and ALT were determined. The rat liver treated with Si-RBCs shows a mean peak level of AST and ALT of 119 UL−1 and 24.5 UL−1, respectively, which is consistent with the native RBCs (112.5 UL−1, 22.5 UL−1), indicating the efficacy of Si-RBCs against liver injury (Fig. 6 I and J). The ALP content in the Si-RBCs group showed a decreasing trend during perfusion (Fig. 6K), and the native RBCs group showed a similar trend, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.7473). Acute cytokine levels in the perfusate also remained low [peak IL-6 < 2 pgmL−1; peak IL-10 < 10 pgmL−1; SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2]. Analysis of the perfusate collected from the portal vein revealed elevated levels of sodium ion during perfusion, and a blood sodium level in the perfusate >165 mmolL−1 would be considered detrimental to the liver and would induce hypernatremia (4, 68), but the results of both groups in this experiment showed that the sodium level was consistently below this level (<155.57 mmolL−1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23B). Potassium (3.23 to 5.07 mmolL−1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23C) and chloride (112 to 120 mmolL−1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23D) levels were maintained at normal levels during perfusion, which indicates that the liver has an intact cell membrane structure and a functioning ion pump that can perform the physiological functions of ion metabolism. BUN and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) are waste products of liver metabolism, and because this perfusion system does not have a dialysis unit, the level of BUN is continuously elevated during perfusion (Fig. 6L and SI Appendix, Fig. S23E), providing additional evidence that the liver is continuously metabolizing. Bile duct injury often occurs during perfusion, affecting subsequent clinical experiments. The volume of bile collected gradually increased with increasing perfusion time (Fig. 6M). Bile levels of gamma-glutamyltransferase, a biomarker of biliary epithelial injury, were <3 (U/L) during perfusion (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2), indicating that no biliary injury occurred during perfusion. Meanwhile, the content of bicarbonate (HCO3−) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23F), total carbon dioxide content (TCO2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23G), Anion Gap (SI Appendix, Fig. S23H) were all at normal levels. The engineered Si-RBCs provide good biocompatibility, excellent mechanical properties, and long-term storage time, making them a viable solution in the development of an ideal organ perfusate.

Xenotransfusion Study: From Human Blood to Mice Blood Transfusion.

Xenotransfusion transfusion can achieve straightforward utilization of blood resources across species and greatly alleviate the clinical situation of insufficient blood resources. Therefore, using Si-RBCs may be a solution to overcome remaining challenges including i) agglutination reaction, ii) immune response caused by foreign proteins and other substances, and iii) damage to RBCs due to changes in hydrodynamic environment (Fig. 7A). To evaluate whether xenogeneic blood can perform normal transfusion functions in mice without causing damage to important metabolic organs, the liver and kidney in vivo, BABL/c mice were injected with PBS (1×), human RBCs (4 × 108/200 μL), and silicified-human RBCs (4 × 108/200 μL), representing approximately 4% of the total blood volume of the mouse and meeting the clinical criteria for blood transfusion. To assess liver injuries, it was analyzed with respect to hepatocyte, biliary cell injury, and liver tissue damage and to evaluate liver function, Suzuki’s scores were used as indicators. The ALT and AST mean levels in the serum of the PBS group of ALT = 29 UL−1 and AST = 134 UL−1 showed a clear increase in the human RBCs group (mean level ALT = 107.3 UL−1, mean level AST = 158.3 UL−1), indicating hepatocellular disease. Compared with the human RBCs group, the silicified-human RBCs group caused about three times less overexpression and showed only a slight increase in mean serum levels to ALT = 40.67 UL−1 and AST = 131.67 UL−1 3 d after injection (Fig. 7B). When serum ALP and TBIL levels were checked, no significant difference was found between the PBS group and the silicified-human RBCs group (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, histologically tissues were examined microscopically, and each slide was scored according to Suzuki’s criteria (Fig. 7C). The liver sections from the silicified-human RBCs group showed larger areas of typical cellular necrosis, cellular inflammatory aggregates, and cellular ballooning than those from the PBS and human RBCs group, which showed only slight cellular ballooning, and the Suzuki scores were obviously higher in the PBS group and the human RBCs group (Fig. 7D). Consistently, serum levels of UREA, CREA, and UA and kidney sections were used to evaluate kidney damage, which showed that serum levels were not significantly different in the three groups and were within a reasonable range, and kidney section analysis showed three groups, with normal kidney cells and tissues (Fig. 7 E and F). Meanwhile, heart, lung, and spleen sections were found to be normal (SI Appendix, Fig. S25). On the basis of these positive results, a further 20% transfusion experiment was performed and serum was obtained after 4 h to detect relevant biosafety indicators. Both the Si-RBCs group and control group also showed normal physiological behavior and good biosafety when the transfusion volume was 20%, while the native RBCs group showed signs of liver damage and inflammation (significant increase in TBIL values, P = 0.0131 and P = 0.0007) (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). All these results reaffirm that Si-RBCs not only escape immune activation in different species, but also function perfectly for oxygen transport. Our technique of creating an exoskeleton around the RBC membrane presents an innovative experimental basis for interspecies blood transfusion, which will hopefully lead to the liberty of blood transfusion in the future.

Discussion

In this work, we have developed a universal artificial silicified blood that can be used in any transfusion situation and offers significant advantages over current chemical-based approaches. The silicified blood retains all essential functions of RBCs, has superior mechanical properties, is resistant to adverse environmental conditions, can be stored for extended periods, and is highly effective in preventing immune system activation. Additionally, our method requires only basic manufacturing and storage procedures. Studies using homozygous normothermic mechanical perfusion and xenotransfusion demonstrate that our Si-RBCs are nontoxic, nonimmunogenic, and provide excellent long-term oxygen transport. These results highlight the immense potential of silicified erythrocytes as a safe and efficient transfusion alternative, which effectively meets the growing clinical demand for blood without the risk of cross-matching for both allogeneic and xenogeneic transfusions.

Materials and Methods

RBC Silicification.

First, RBCs (1.5 × 108/mL) were added to 50 mL silicification solution. Then, the mixture was placed in 20 rpm rotator and spun for 1 h at 4 °C. The Si-RBCs were rinsed with 1× PBS for 3 times and stored in 1× PBS.

A Multiple Blood Transfusion In Vivo.

BALB/c mice with age of 9 wk were used for this experiment to investigate the transport ability of native RBCs and Si-RBCs in vivo. Blood transfusion experiments were preceded by cross-matching experiments to screen donors and recipients with matching blood groups. This method is also used when blood group matching is required in subsequent experiments. Whole blood was harvested from different donor mice (type-matched) by eyeball extirpation and isolated and purified with Ficoll-Paque PLUS solution to obtain RBCs, then stored in 10 mM PBS buffer at 4 °C. Collecting RBCs were subjected to membrane staining by DIR dye, and a portion of RBCs were silicified as described above. See SI Appendix for more details.

In Vivo Immunogenicity Evaluation in Immunostimulation.

An in vivo immune response study was carried out using New Zealand rabbits. The animals were evenly assigned into three experimental groups, each consisting of three rabbits, to assess the immunogenicity of different treatments. Group 1 was administered an intraperitoneal injection of 500 μL of RhD-positive RBCs initially, followed by a second identical injection 3 wk later. Group 2 served as a control, receiving an intraperitoneal injection of 500 μL of 1× PBS solution initially and a second injection of the same solution 3 wk later. Group 3 was given an intraperitoneal injection of 500 μL of Si-RBCs initially and a second injection of Si-RBCs 3 wk later. Blood samples were collected from the rabbits on a weekly basis, starting from the first week of the experiment. For further details regarding the experimental procedures and outcomes, SI Appendix should be consulted.

Rat Liver Acquisition and Establishment of Rat Liver NMP System.

200 to 250 g SD rats, fasted for 12 h without water, and anesthetized with isoflurane gas. The liver was exposed by a median abdominal incision, and the left subphrenic vein, esophageal vein plexus, right renal vein, and right adrenal vein plexus were sequentially ligated, then the skeletal portal vein was freed and the pyloric portal vein was ligated, and a homemade biliary stent was placed. Then, the liver was rinsed and removed, which was immediately weighed. The rat liver NMP used in this study was a homemade single-circulation system consisting of an organ chamber, a peristaltic pump, a membrane oxygenator, a de-embolizer, a heated water bath, and a pressure monitor. The system temperature was maintained at 36 to 38 °C. See SI Appendix for more details.

Xenotransfusion: From Human Blood to Mice Blood Transfusion.

BALB/c mice with age of 9 wk were used for this experiment to investigate the xenotransport in vivo. Human blood was collected from a volunteer and isolated and purified with Ficoll-Paque PLUS solution to obtain erythrocytes and then stored in 10 mM PBS buffer at 4 °C.
