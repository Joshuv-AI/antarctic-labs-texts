Cross‐Species Reprogramming of Developmental Plasticity and Metabolic Rewiring via Banana‐Derived WUS2 Developmental Regulator
Authors: Roni Chaudhary, Surender Singh, Usman Ali, Siddharth Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant regeneration is governed by intrinsic gene regulation and phytohormonal cues. WUSCHEL (WUS) gene promotes regeneration, but its broader functional role remains unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that the constitutive and inducible expression of banana‐derived WUS2 (GN‐WUS2) enhances regeneration in Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco) and Musa acuminata (banana) cv. Grand Naine, even in hormone‐free MS medium. Constitutive (CaMV35S::GN‐WUS2) expression promoted shoot formation and modulated hormonal and morphogenic gene expression, as evidenced by molecular, biochemical and histological analyses. However, it caused some pleiotropic effects. To overcome this, glucocorticoid receptor‐based inducible GN‐WUS2 expression enabled healthy shoot development. The upregulated expression of HMGR1, IPPI2 and SMT1‐2 in transgenic tobacco lines boosted isoprenoid and phytosterol biosynthesis and correlated with increased cell division, biomass, pod size and seed yield. Proteomic analysis of seeds from transgenic tobacco lines revealed an enrichment of lipid‐associated proteins and the accumulation of the novel lipid adipic acid, supported by expression profiling of NtKA and NtSA genes. Collectively, these results establish GN‐WUS2 as a master regulator that integrates developmental reprogramming with novel phytosterol biosynthesis and yield enhancement, presenting its versatile role in next‐generation regeneration and crop improvement.

Introduction

Plants possess a remarkable capacity to regenerate their entire structure from somatic cells. In most species, this potential is quite evident when explants are cultured in vitro on the nutrient media supplemented with plant growth regulators (PGRs). Although hormonal control of in vitro regeneration has been extensively studied in several plants (Xu et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025) but the molecular basis of developmental regulators (DRs) has only recently come into sharper focus (Lian et al. 2022; Bennett et al. 2025; Zhong et al. 2025). Several DRs involved in somatic embryogenesis (SE) have been identified, mainly including BABY BOOM (BBM), LEAFY COTYLEDON1 (LEC1/2), FUSCA3 (FUS3), ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE3 (ABI3) and AGAMOUS‐LIKE 15 (AGL15) (Lee and Wang 2023; Xu et al. 2024). Others, such as WUSCHEL (WUS), CLAVATA3 (CLV3) and SHOOT MERISTEMLESS (STM), are known to be responsible for differentiation of the shoot apical meristem (SAM) (Youngstrom et al. 2025). WUS, a member of the WUSCHEL‐related homeobox (WOX) gene family, encodes a homeodomain transcription factor, and its expression is regulated by a feedback loop with CLV3. Notably, the high levels of CLV3 expression repress WUS activity and modulate the stem cell population size (Plong et al. 2021).

Ectopic expression of WUS has been shown to promote somatic embryo and shoot formation across diverse plant species. In Arabidopsis thaliana , AtWUS expression induces shoot and SE (Zuo et al. 2002). Similar results have been reported with WUS orthologs from Gossypium hirsutum , Coffea canephora , Medicago truncatula and Nicotiana tabacum (Arroyo‐Herrera et al. 2008; Xiao et al. 2018; Kadri et al. 2021; Sato et al. 2024). WUS functions closely with hormonal cues, particularly cytokinin, through mutual feedback loops (Wang et al. 2017). Various evidences suggest that auxin levels also critically influence WUS‐mediated SE (Jha et al. 2020; Yakovleva et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2025). Recent approaches have coexpressed WUS with other DRs or repressor motifs to promote de‐novo shoot regeneration with improved transformation and genome editing efficiency (Maher et al. 2020; Shin et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2024; Kumar et al. 2024, 2025). Overexpression of TaWOX5 enhanced regeneration efficiency across wheat genotypes (Wang et al. 2023). However, constitutive overexpression of DRs often leads to pleiotropic effects and developmental abnormalities, highlighting the need for their controlled regulation (Chen et al. 2022; Youngstrom et al. 2025).

In our previous work, we profiled DRs expression during various stages of SE in Musa acuminata (banana) cultivar (cv.) Grand Naine and found that WUS2 was significantly upregulated compared to the non‐embryogenic cultures (Shivani et al. 2017). This prompted us to functionally characterise GN‐WUS2 using both constitutive and inducible expression systems, initially in tobacco and subsequently in banana.

Banana is a globally important fruit crop with an annual production exceeding 135 million tonnes, ranking it fourth after rice, wheat and maize (FAOSTAT 2023). The Cavendish subgroup, particularly cv. Grand Naine represents nearly half of global production (FAO 2025; Khan et al. 2025). However, conventional breeding is impeded by its polyploidy, parthenocarpy and sterility (Lakhani et al. 2023, 2025). DR‐based genetic modification can offer a viable strategy to overcome these limitations and facilitate targeted trait improvement for biofortification, disease resistance, herbicide tolerance and shelf‐life extension (Awasthi et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2024; Lokya et al. 2025).

WUS orthologs such as AtWUS and GhWUS have been characterised as regulators of stem cell maintenance and regeneration competence (Zuo et al. 2002; Xiao et al. 2018; Aluko et al. 2025). Emerging evidence indicates that developmental metabolic reprogramming is mediated by other DR (LEC), which regulate embryonic lipid biosynthesis and central carbon metabolism (Tan et al. 2011; Jo et al. 2019; Wei et al. 2025). Based on these observations, we hypothesised a potential WUS–LEC mediated metabolism regulatory axis that may be regulated directly or indirectly by GN‐WUS2. Here, we report the functional analysis of GN‐WUS2 through constitutive and inducible expression in tobacco and banana. We demonstrate GN‐WUS2's ability to promote regeneration in PGR‐free MS medium and uncover its additional roles in seed development, phytosterol biosynthesis, and yield enhancement in transgenic tobacco plants. Consistent with these phenotypes, transcriptomic and metabolic analyses revealed associations between GN‐WUS2 expression and pathways related to glycolysis and lipid biosynthesis. Moreover, these results demonstrate the multifunctional potential of GN‐WUS2 for improving regeneration and transformation efficiency, particularly in recalcitrant crop species.

Results

GN‐WUS2 Expression Enhances Shoot Regeneration in Tobacco

To assess the regeneration‐promoting ability of GN‐WUS2, we evaluated its role as a DR by transforming tobacco leaf explants with the MWS (CaMV35S::GN‐WUS2) construct and cultured them on hygromycin‐containing hormone‐free MS basal medium (Figure 1a–c). In three independent experiments (each with 100 explants), GN‐WUS2 expression significantly enhanced shoot regeneration and yielded transgenic shoots in 82.35%, 76.50% and 83.33% of explants (Table S1). In contrast, control explants, either non‐transformed or lacking GN‐WUS2 expression, exhibited no visible organogenic response on MS basal medium and gradually turned necrotic 4 weeks after incubation (4 WAI). By 2 WAI, GN‐WUS2‐expressing explants began forming green organ‐like structures on hormone‐free MS basal medium, indicating that GN‐WUS2 alone was sufficient to trigger autonomous cell differentiation (Figure 1b). By 4 WAI, these explants exhibited vigorous proliferation and developed numerous hygromycin‐resistant shoots (Figure 1b,c). However, constitutive expression of GN‐WUS2 (MWS) induced pleiotropic effects and exhibited two distinct phenotypes. Type I shoots displayed normal shoot‐root development, whereas type II shoots failed to establish a functional shoot–root system even after 6 weeks of selection on hygromycin‐containing MS basal medium (Figure 1c). qRT‐PCR confirmed high levels of GN‐WUS2 transcripts in regenerated MWS lines at 8 WAI, with one of the lines (MWS L1) showing up to 1712.37‐fold increase over the control (Figure 2a).

Inducible GN‐WUS2 Expression Mitigates Developmental Defects

To circumvent the pleiotropic defects (Type II shoots) observed with constitutive GN‐WUS2 expression, we designed a glucocorticoid receptor (GR)‐inducible MWSI (GR::GN‐WUS2) construct (Figure 1d). Tobacco leaf explants transformed with MWSI were cultured on kanamycin‐containing MS basal medium with different dexamethasone (DEX) concentrations (0.1–1 μM) following 3 days of co‐cultivation. Non‐transformed explants grown on MS basal medium with DEX (dissolved in DMSO) or without DEX served as controls (Figure 1e). DEX‐mediated activation enabled spatiotemporal control of GN‐WUS2 expression and minimised developmental abnormalities. Unlike constitutive MWS lines, MWSI explants generated predominantly healthy type I shoots. Neither of the control treatments exhibited any morphological alterations, indicating the absence of DEX‐ or solvent (DMSO)‐related effects (Figure 1e).

Transcript analysis of 8 WAI shoots showed GN‐WUS2 expression increased proportionally with DEX, peaked at 1050.56‐fold at 0.5 μM and then declined at 1 μM likely due to DEX‐induced cytotoxicity (Figure 2b). Beyond 0.5 μM, shoot abnormalities and tissue browning became pronounced (Figure 1e). Compared to MWS lines (Figure 2a), MWSI lines exhibited generally lower GN‐WUS2 transcript levels (Figure 2b) and fewer morphological defects (Figure 1e). The transformation efficiency with MWSI construct on 0.5 μM DEX is reported ranging from 66.67% to 75.00% (Table S1). Hence, MWSI lines demonstrated the utility of an inducible DR expression system to avoid pleiotropic effects.

Application of Inducible GN‐WUS2 System in Banana Transformants Regeneration

Building on the success in tobacco, the MWSI system was evaluated in the banana cv. Grand Naine embryogenic cell suspension (ECS) cultures. Healthy plantlet regeneration occurred at 0.25 and 0.5 μM DEX containing MS basal medium supplemented with kanamycin, in contrast to non‐transformed ECS cultured with DEX (dissolved in DMSO) or without DEX (Figure 1f). Lower (0.1 μM) or higher (1 μM) concentrations of DEX led to callus formation without shoot regeneration, whereas long‐term exposure to 1 μM proved to be lethal. Regenerated banana shoots (MI lines) on 0.5 μM DEX exhibited > 1000‐fold GN‐WUS2 expression relative to non‐transformed control ECS lines (Figure 2c), supporting its broad utility across species.

Confirmation of Transgenic Lines by PCR

PCR analysis confirmed the presence of transgenes in tobacco (MWS and MWSI) and banana (MI) lines. MWS lines amplified 1029 bp (hptII) and 706 bp (GN‐WUS2) fragments (Figure S1a,b), whereas MWSI lines yielded 795 bp (nptII) and 350 bp (pZmUbq) fragments (Figure S1c,d). MI banana lines showed a 750 bp amplicon using GR‐LHG4‐specific primers (Figure S1e).

GN‐WUS2 Induces Expression of Differentiation‐Promoting Genes

qRT‐PCR profiling in young leaves of MWS and MWSI tobacco and MI banana lines revealed upregulation of key differentiation‐promoting genes (DPGs) (Figure 2d–f). In MWS tobacco lines, NtLEC2.2, NtLEC2.1 and NtFUS3.1 were most strongly induced (1747.32‐fold, 1398.48‐fold and 600.58‐fold, respectively) (Figure 2d). In MWSI lines, LEC2 and FUS3 homologues showed DEX‐dependent expression patterns, with NtLEC2.2, NtLEC2.1 and NtFUS3.1 reaching 1479.07‐fold, 848.34‐fold and 563.60‐fold at 0.5 μM, respectively, and declining thereafter (Figure 2e). In banana MI lines, MaFUS3.2, MaABI3.1 and MaLEC2 exhibited high expression, that is, 462.93‐fold, 94.40‐fold and 61.33‐fold at 0.5 μM DEX, respectively (Figure 2f). These patterns of gene expression correlated with shoot regeneration outcomes in respective lines.

GN‐WUS2 Modulates Hormone Biosynthesis Genes (HBGs) Expression

To elucidate hormonal contributions, we profiled expression of gibberellin (GA3, GA20 and CYCB2), auxin (YUCCA6/8) and cytokinin (IPT) biosynthesis genes in young leaves of MWS, MWSI and MI lines (Figure 2g–i). In MWS tobacco lines, NtGA3 and NtYUCCA8 were highly upregulated (2015.84‐fold and 53.74‐fold, respectively) (Figure 2g). In MWSI tobacco lines, expression increased with DEX concentrations; NtGA3 (1464.40‐fold) and NtYUCCA8 (105.03‐fold) peaked at 0.5 μM and then declined at 1 μM (Figure 2h). In MI banana lines, 0.5 μM DEX induced MaGA20, MaYUCCA6 and MaIPT up to 153.10‐fold, 106.07‐fold and 135.40‐fold, respectively (Figure 2i). These results indicate a GN‐WUS2‐driven activation of hormone biosynthesis genes and subsequent regeneration response in transgenic lines.

GN‐WUS2 Alters Leaf Anatomy and Promotes Growth

To gain more insight into the developmental changes induced by constitutive GN‐WUS2 expression in type 1 shoots (normal), histological analysis revealed that MWS lines exhibited distinctly thickened leaves due to additional palisade cell layers, increased vascular bundle density, and reduced intercellular spaces compared to control plants (Figure 3a–c). Transgenic leaves exhibited increased thickness, with a width of 1847 μm in MWS Line 1 and 966 μm in MWS Line 2, compared to 635 μm in the control.

Intense GFP fluorescence in microtome‐processed sections confirmed high GN‐WUS2 expression in the vascular and mesophyll tissues, as well as in the roots of MWS lines (Figure 3d,e). This result indicated constitutive expression of GN‐WUS2 + GFP fusion in the MWS lines. MWS lines (Type 1) also displayed more lateral root formation (Figure 3f) and a ~50% increase in plant height with early maturity compared to control plants, as represented by four lines (L1–L4) of 80 and 240‐days‐old plants for more precise and statistically valid estimation (Figure 3g–i).

GN‐WUS2 Promotes Trichome Density

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed increased trichome density and altered trichome morphology in MWS lines (Figure 3j). In control plants, trichomes were sparsely (193.2 μM distance) distributed, whereas the MWS Line 1 displayed a denser (176.2 μM distance) trichome arrangement (Figure 3j). To support these observations, expression of key trichome regulators NtGL1 and NtGL2 was significantly upregulated in three MWS lines compared to control plants (Figure 3k). The highest expression of NtGL1 (87.05‐fold) and NtGL2 (6.57‐fold) was noted in MWS Line 1 (Figure 3k). A predicted molecular mechanism underscores the role of GN‐WUS2 in orchestrating trichome development within the MWS transgenic line of tobacco, proposed to be mediated through the action of gibberellic acid (GA3), notably upregulating the glabrous genes NtGL1 and NtGL2 (Figure 3l).

Liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) based quantitative hormone estimation showed elevated levels of gibberellic acid (GA3), indole acetic acid (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA) and zeatin (Z) in leaves of all three MWS lines than in the control (Figure 4). The chromatograms of IAA (Figure 4a), ABA (Figure 4b), GA3 (Figure 4c) and Z (Figure 4d) in the plant samples are shown. The control sample did not display any detectable peak, revealing that hormonal estimation fell below the threshold level at this sample size compared to MWS lines (Figure 4e). Among the MWS lines, Z showed the lowest concentration, with a maximum increase of up to 36 ng/100 mg. Although IAA, GA and ABA levels were significantly elevated up to 164 ng/100 mg, 20 000 ng/100 mg and 83 300 ng/100 mg, respectively (Figure 4f). Furthermore, corresponding chromatograms of standards of IAA, ABA, GA3 and Z are shown in Figure 4g–j.

GN‐WUS2 Accelerates Seed Germination and Enhances Yield

MWS lines exhibited faster seed germination and greater pod yield than controls (Figure 5a; Figures S2a,b and S3). Further, the sample size of MWS lines was methodically expanded in stereomicroscopy to obtain a precise and statistically defensible yield estimate. In stereomicroscopy analysis, it showed a yellowish seed coat in MWS lines, whereas controls exhibited dark colour seed coats (Figure 5b). This colour change in MWS lines is indicative of reduced proanthocyanidin and increased lipid accumulation (Tian et al. 2021) (Figure 5b). The T3 generation analysis showed 30%–50% greater seed yield in MWS lines (L1–L5) across pod weight and seed number metrics, supporting the agronomic potential of GN‐WUS2 (Figure S3).

GN‐WUS2 Modifies Seed Morphology and Elemental Composition

SEM analysis showed that MWS seeds were smaller and rounder than control seeds, with distinct size metrics (Figure 5c–h). The width and length of the control (548.6 and 720.4 μM), MWS line 1 (493.8 and 565.3 μM) and MWS line 2 (526.7 and 668.8 μM) seeds were presented in Figure 5c–h.

The energy‐dispersive X‐ray (EDX) spectroscopy was conducted to analyse carbon, nitrogen and oxygen elements in the seeds of MWS lines and control plants (Figure 5i–l). It showed a significant increase in weight and atomic percentages of nitrogen in seeds of MWS lines (9.9%–11%) compared to controls (2.3%–2.4%), suggesting altered nitrogen metabolism and its possible role in lipid and terpenoid biosynthesis (Mishra et al. 2009; Milton et al. 2025) (Figure 5i–l).

GN‐WUS2 Alters Seed Lipid Profiles

GN‐WUS2 enhanced expression of isoprenoid and phytosterol biosynthetic genes NtHMGR1, NtIPPI2 and NtSMT1 in the T3 seeds of three MWS tobacco lines (Figure 6a). Out of these, NtIPPI2 was most responsive and showed up to 358.30‐fold induction, followed by NtHMGR1 (5.44‐fold) (Figure 6a). These gene expression changes suggest a link between GN‐WUS2 activity and metabolic pathways beyond morphogenesis which had been further confirmed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS).

The GC–MS analysis of MWS seeds revealed increased accumulation of key fatty acids, palmitic (hexadecanoic acid), stearic (octadecanoic acid), margaric (heptadecanoic acid), oleic (cis‐9‐octadecenoic acid), linoleic (cis,cis‐9,12‐octadecadienoic acid) and linolenic (cis,cis,cis‐9,12,15‐octadecatrienoic acid) acids, alongside the predominant appearance of adipic acid in MWS Line 1 and MWS Line 2 (Figure 6b). The MWS Line 1 exhibited a pattern in which elevated succinic acid levels were associated with increased adipic acid production, whereas MWS Line 2 displayed lower succinic acid levels but higher adipic acid accumulation. These contrasting observations are summarised in a proposed mechanism of adipic acid biosynthesis through feedback regulation in transgenic plants (Figure 6c). The presence of adipic acid was further validated by expression profiling of ketoacyl‐CoA synthase (NtKA) and succinyl‐CoA synthase (NtSA) genes. In MWS Line 1, NtKA and NtSA showed marked upregulation, with fold increases of 128.66 and 213.35, respectively. In contrast, MWS Line 2 showed a 380.76‐fold increase in NtKA but only a modest 1.09‐fold increase in NtSA (Figure 6d).

To investigate further differences in lipid accumulation, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed across MWS lines and control plants (Figure 6e). The results revealed that the first principal component (PC1) accounted for 24.20% of the total variation with strong positive correlation, whereas the second principal component (PC2) explained 21.30% of the variance, also with positive correlation.

Furthermore, pathway enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) indicated an enrichment of the unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis pathway by four‐fold in the MWS line 1 compared to the control (Figure 6f,g).

Proteomics Data Analysis

Raw LC–MS/MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (ThermoFisher, version 3.2) with built‐in pipelines for protein identification and quantification. The resulting output files were curated and analysed using Python (https://www.python.org/) in the Visual Studio Code environment. Data cleaning and normalisation steps removed contaminants and low‐confidence identifications. A protein spectral match (PSM) matrix was generated to represent protein abundance across samples, serving as the input for downstream heatmap and clustering analyses with Matplotlib, Seaborn and Scikit‐learn.

The heatmap (Figure 7a) highlighted differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) such as acyl‐coenzyme A thioesterase 13‐like (A0A1S4BGQ5), glycosyltransferase (A0A1S3X6H9), pinin‐like (A0A1S3XUD3) and floral homeotic protein AGAMOUS‐like (A0A1S4BCG1), which were enriched in MWS Line 1 seeds relative to the control and implicated in lipid accumulation and transport. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/). In MWS Line 1 seeds, DEP A0A1S3XUD3 (LOC107768853) was found to interact with other lipid‐related proteins, whereas pyruvate kinase I (A0A1S3ZGK8, LOC107786604), a central metabolic enzyme, showed interactions within primary metabolism networks (Figure 7b). A global PPI analysis of DEPs from both control and MWS Line 1 seeds revealed their integration into central metabolic and transcriptomic pathways (Figure S7). These proteomic insights further supported the biosynthesis of adipic acid in seeds of transgenic lines.

Discussion

The present findings demonstrate that ectopic expression of GN‐WUS2 is sufficient to induce de‐novo organogenesis in differentiated tissues and significantly enhances regeneration efficiency in both tobacco and banana on hormone‐free MS basal medium. Constitutive expression of DRs under the CaMV35S promoter effectively promoted shoot formation but also resulted in pleiotropic phenotypes, consistent with earlier reports in Malus domestica and Capsicum annuum (Heidmann et al. 2011; Chen et al. 2022). To mitigate these effects, we employed a DEX‐inducible system, which provided spatiotemporal control of GN‐WUS2 expression. As expected, DEX‐mediated activation triggered nuclear translocation of the GR‐DEX complex, allowing precise transcriptional regulation (Figure 1d and Figure S4). The optimal DEX concentration (0.5 μM) induced peak GN‐WUS2 expression (1150.56‐fold in tobacco, 1083.39‐fold in banana), beyond which phenotypic abnormalities reappeared. This inducible system enabled efficient regeneration without exogenous PGRs and minimised developmental defects. These observations were corroborated with previous studies in Malus domestica and Oryza sativa (Vlad et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2022).

In both tobacco and banana, GN‐WUS2 expression led to transcriptional reprogramming of key DRs. Members of the LAFL‐B3 family (LEC1, LEC2, FUS3 and ABI3) were differentially expressed in transformed lines. Upregulation of NtLEC2.1, NtLEC2.2, NtFUS3.1 and NtFUS3.2 correlated with increased lateral root formation, consistent with their reported roles in auxin‐mediated root development (Tang et al. 2017; Banda et al. 2019; Song et al. 2021). Conversely, reduced NtLEC1.1/NtLEC1.2 expression coincided with elevated NtGL1/NtGL2 and increased trichome density, supporting prior evidence of LEC1‐mediated repression of trichome formation (Huang et al. 2015; Jo et al. 2019). The regulatory responses observed in tobacco were largely conserved in banana. Transgenic banana lines (MI) expressing GN‐WUS2 exhibited enhanced transcript levels of MaLEC2, MaFUS3, MaABI3, MaYUCCA6, MaGA20 and MaIPT, facilitating the regeneration of ECS on hormone‐free MS medium. These results underscore the value of an inducible expression system in fine‐tuning DR activity and enabling efficient regeneration across taxa.

Phytohormone profiling revealed that GN‐WUS2 expression activates biosynthetic pathways for gibberellin, auxin and cytokinin. In tobacco MWS lines, NtGA3 and NtGA20 expression were upregulated 2015.84‐fold and 20‐fold, respectively, whereas MWSI lines exhibited moderate induction (1464.40‐fold and 21.70‐fold). Elevated NtGA3 and NtGA20 expression correlated with increased GA3 accumulation, plant height and trichome density, and were associated with reduced NtCYCB2, a B‐type cyclin (Jiao 2016). Upregulation of NtGL1 and NtGL2 further supported enhanced glandular trichome development as confirmed by SEM imaging (Figure 4a–e).

Auxin biosynthesis genes NtYUCCA6 and NtYUCCA8, known as key factors of the TAA/YUC pathway (Cao et al. 2019), were also strongly induced. NtYUCCA8 expression increased 53.74‐fold in MWS and 105.03‐fold in MWSI lines, promoted ABA accumulation, lateral root elongation, improved vascular architecture and healthy leaf development (Figure S5). These phenotypes are consistent with prior studies reported in A. thaliana , Solanum lycopersicum and Populus euphratica (Koenig et al. 2009; Israeli et al. 2019; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025). Histological analyses (Figure 3a–c) and confocal microscopy (Figure 3d,e) further confirmed increased vascular bundle density and well‐organised mesophyll layers in MWS lines.

Cytokinin biosynthesis was similarly activated and exhibited increased NtIPT expression in MWS (11.45‐fold) and MWSI (16.82‐fold) tobacco lines. This upregulation of IPT gene aligns with the established role of cytokinin in de‐novo shoot formation and lamina expansion, as also evident previously in AtWOX5 overexpressing A. thaliana and Oryza sativa (Lee et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2023). Multi‐modal phenotyping, such as histology, SEM, confocal imaging and EDX analyses, corroborated the above‐mentioned phenotypic observations and revealed substantial anatomical divergence between transgenic and control plants. Quantitative LC–MS/MS analysis further confirmed elevated phytohormone levels in leaves of the promising MWS line, which contained ~36 ng/100 mg Z, ~164 ng/100 mg IAA, ~20 000 ng/100 mg GA3 and ~83 300 ng/100 mg ABA. These analytical observations are correlated with enhanced plant stature, vegetative growth, seed yield and lipid accumulation, as also reported in previous findings in Paeonia ostia and Camellia oleifer (Li et al. 2025; Liu, Sheng, et al. 2025). GC–MS profiling revealed the accumulation of unsaturated fatty acids, suggesting a broader role of GN‐WUS2 in metabolic regulation.

Beyond morphogenesis, GN‐WUS2 expression modulated genes linked to isoprenoid and phytosterol biosynthesis. NtHMGR1, a key gene in the mevalonate pathway, was upregulated 5.44‐fold, consistent with its housekeeping function in sterol biosynthesis and developmental regulations (Liao et al. 2014). In contrast, NtHMGR2 remained relatively unchanged, aligned with its stress‐responsive nature. The IPPI gene encodes an enzyme that interconverts IPP and DMAPP (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025). In MWS lines, cytosolic NtIPPI2 was upregulated 358.30‐fold, whereas plastid‐localised NtIPPI1 remained low, reinforcing its role in isoprenoid biosynthesis, which also corroborated with previous findings (Hemmerlin et al. 2012; Liao et al. 2014; Nguyen et al. 2021). NtSMT1‐2, involved in cycloartenol methylation, a key step in phytosterol biosynthesis, showed modest (~2‐fold) upregulation, whereas the expression of NtCYP85A1, NtSQS and NtSMT2‐2 remained unchanged, suggesting GN‐WUS2 mediated pathway specificity (Holmberg et al. 2003). Consistent with these transcriptional changes, GC–MS analysis of MWS seeds revealed increased levels of unsaturated fatty acids and novel lipid metabolites such as adipic acid, a commercially valuable dicarboxylic acid, also known as a nylon precursor. These metabolic shifts also correlated with elevated NtLEC2.1 and NtLEC2.2 expression, echoing earlier findings in A. thaliana and N. tabacum, where LEC2 activated gene expression involved in fatty acid biosynthesis and seed maturation (Andrianov et al. 2010; Kim et al. 2014, 2024). Furthermore, proteomic and expression profiling of NtKA and NtSA genes supported the presence of adipic acid in the transgenic lines, consistent with earlier evidence (Cheong et al. 2016).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report characterising GN‐WUS2 as a dual‐function DR that not only promotes hormone‐independent regeneration but also rewires metabolic networks including phytosterol and lipid biosynthesis. The present results reveal a previously underappreciated dimension of WUS‐mediated regulation and provide GN‐WUS2 as a versatile candidate for advancing regeneration, transformation and trait enhancement in various crop plants. Although the functional conservation of GN‐WUS2 activity in tobacco supports its cross‐species utility, the primary significance of this work lies in its translational potential for banana improvement. In banana, embryogenic callus induction and subsequent transformation efficiency remain major bottlenecks due to poor recovery of transformants, making the overall process time‐consuming and inefficient (Shivani et al. 2017; Shivani and Tiwari 2019). Notably, DEX‐inducible activation of GN‐WUS2 in banana ECS significantly enhanced shoot regeneration at 0.5 μM DEX in the absence of exogenous phytohormones. This strategy provides a practical alternative to conventional regeneration of transformants that depend on genotype‐specific optimisation of cytokinin‐auxin ratios, a process that is often ineffective for elite and recalcitrant cultivars. By enabling regeneration under simplified culture conditions, GN‐WUS2 induction has the potential to reduce transformation timelines and costs, and to facilitate more efficient deployment of transgenic and genome‐editing‐based traits.

Although these findings suggest that GN‐WUS2 may coordinate developmental regulation with broader metabolic processes, further studies will be required to determine whether these effects represent conserved functions of WUS proteins or species‐specific attributes of the banana ortholog. Nevertheless, these results highlight the multifunctional potential of GN‐WUS2 and support its utility as a valuable translational tool for improving regeneration efficiency and transformation protocols, particularly in recalcitrant crop species.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Tobacco ( Nicotiana tabacum ) seeds were surface sterilised and germinated on half‐strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 30 g/L sucrose and 0.8% agar. Seeds were stratified at 4°C for 3 days and then cultured in a growth chamber (Conviron, Canada) under controlled conditions (25°C, 70% relative humidity, 100 μmol m−2 s−1 light intensity; 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod). The leaves and various tissues of the grown‐up plants were considered for various experiments.

Embryogenic cell suspension (ECS) cultures of banana cv. Grand Naine were established from immature male flower explants and maintained in liquid medium as described previously (Shivani et al. 2017). ECS cultures were then used for genetic transformation.

Construct Design and Vector Assembly

Constitutive Overexpression Construct

The coding sequence of GN‐WUS2 was amplified from the banana cv. Grand Naine cDNA using primers listed in Table S2. The amplified gene was fused in‐frame with GFP at the N‐terminus using a GSGSGS linker and cloned into the binary vector pCAMBIA1302 under control of the CaMV35S promoter using BglII and SpeI sites. This designed construct CaMV35S::GN‐WUS2 (MWS) carries the hygromycin phosphotransferase (hptII) gene for hygromycin resistance plant selection (Figure 1a). The vector was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 via electroporation (Gene Pulser Xcell, BIO‐RAD, USA) and used for plant transformation (Den Dulk‐Ras and Hooykaas 1995).

Steroid‐Inducible Construct

To enable controlled GN‐WUS2 expression, we employed a steroid‐inducible two‐component system (GR‐LhG4/6XOP) (Samalova et al. 2005; Ram et al. 2020). The GN‐WUS2 coding sequence was cloned into a vector containing a CaMV35S promoter with six times operator sites (6XOP), resulting in the 6XOP::GN‐WUS2::Ter construct. Separately, the chimeric GR‐LhG4 activator was placed under the maize ubiquitin promoter (pZmUbq), resulting in the pZmUbq::GR‐LhG4::Ter construct. Both expression cassettes were assembled into the pRI101AN binary vector via Golden Gate (NEBridge Golden Gate Assembly Kit) cloning using BsaI, HindIII and EcoRI sites and primers information were mentioned in Table S2. The final construct GR::GN‐WUS2 (MWSI) carries the neomycin phosphotransferase (nptII) gene for kanamycin resistance plant selection (Figure 1d and Figure S4). In this construct, chimeric GR‐LhG4 usually forms an inactive complex in the cytoplasm with heat shock protein (HSP) in the absence of dexamethasone (DEX). Though the presence of DEX unbinds the inactive complex and binds with GR, which allows the inducible expression of the GN‐WUS (Figure 1b and Figure S4). Finally, MWSI was electroporated into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 and used for plant transformation (Den Dulk‐Ras and Hooykaas 1995).

Agrobacterium‐Mediated Transformation

Tobacco

Tobacco transformation was carried out using a standard leaf disc method (Heidari Japelaghi et al. 2018). In brief, leaf discs from in vitro‐grown plants were infected with A. tumefaciens carrying the MWS or MWSI construct. Bacterial cultures were grown in LB medium with appropriate antibiotics (kanamycin 50 mg/L and rifampicin 50 mg/L) and kept at 28°C in a shaker at 180 rpm (Kuhner shaker, Switzerland) for 2 days. Agrocultures were then pelleted down at 4000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in liquid MS medium (OD600 = 0.8) with 200 μM acetosyringone, and incubated at 28°C in a shaker (90 rpm) for 1 h. Explants (0.5–1 cm leaf discs) were incubated with the bacterial suspension for 30 min, blotted, and co‐cultivated on semi‐solid MS medium containing sucrose (30 g/L) and agar (0.8%) for 3 days at 25°C in the dark. Transformant selection and regeneration were carried out on PGR‐free MS basal medium supplemented with cefotaxime (250 mg/L), hygromycin (30 mg/L) for MWS or kanamycin (100 mg/L) for MWSI lines. Cultures were incubated under standard conditions (25°C, 100 μmol m−2 s−1 for 16 h light and 8 h dark) inside the tissue culture chambers (Percival, USA) and subcultured every 3 weeks.

Banana

Transgenic banana plants were generated by transforming ECS with the MWSI construct following the published method (Shivani and Tiwari 2019). Briefly, after 3 days co‐cultivation, ECS was washed with liquid MS basal medium containing cefotaxime (200 mg/L) and transferred to PGR‐free MS basal medium containing kanamycin (30–50 mg/L) and cefotaxime (250 mg/L) for regeneration.

Control (non‐transformed) explants of both tobacco and banana were also cultured using identical conditions but without bacterial inoculation or antibiotic selection on PGR‐free MS medium with DEX (dissolved in DMSO) or without DEX.

Optimisation of DEX Concentration for GN‐WUS2 Induction

DEX (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was prepared as a 10 mM stock in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −20°C. To determine optimal induction conditions, tobacco (leaf discs) and banana (ECS) explants transformed with MWSI were cultured on PGR‐free MS basal medium with kanamycin (50 mg/L) and varying DEX concentrations (0.1, 0.25, 0.5 and 1 μM). Regeneration frequencies of tobacco were recorded based on three independent experiments (A4, A5 and A6) of 100 explants for each treatment, which were calculated after 6 weeks (two subcultures of 3 weeks duration each). Plants generated from DEX‐treated tobacco and banana cultures were used for gene expression analysis.

Confirmation of Transgenic Lines by PCR

Genomic DNA was isolated from young leaves of the transgenic tobacco and banana plantlets using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). PCR amplification was performed with gene‐specific primers (Table S2) targeting GN‐WUS2 (706 bp), hptII (1026 bp), nptII (795 bp) and pZmUbq (350 bp) in respective lines. All PCR reactions were performed in 20 μL, containing 100 ng DNA, 10 pmol primers, 10× PCR master mix (Takara, India), and nuclease‐free water. PCR products were visualised on 1% agarose gel using the gel documentation system (Azure Biosystems, USA). Genomic DNA from non‐transformed plants was included in the reaction as a negative control.

Identification of Candidate Genes and qRT‐PCR Analyses

Arabidopsis thaliana homologues of developmental regulators (DRs), hormone‐responsive, isoprenoid and trichome development genes were retrieved from the Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR10_genome_release; https://www.arabidopsis.org) and used for BLAST searches against tobacco (Sol Genomics Network; https://solgenomics.net) and banana (Banana Genome Hub; https://banana‐genome‐hub.southgreen.fr) genomes by considering an e‐value threshold < 10−4. Names of identified genes and their primer sequences considered for expression analysis in tobacco and banana are listed in Tables S2 and S3. Two closely related sequences of leafy cotyledon 1 (NtLEC1.1 and NtLEC1.2), leafy cotyledon 2 (NtLEC2.1 and NtLEC2.2) and FUSCA3 (NtFUS3.1 and NtFUS3.2) were identified in the tobacco genome and aligned using Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo) for sequence similarity analysis (Figure S6) and included in the expression studies.

For qRT‐PCR, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, USA), treated with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen, USA), and used for cDNA synthesis with the RevertAid First Strand kit (Thermo Scientific, USA) as per the manufacturer's protocols. qRT‐PCR was performed using ABI 7500 (Applied Biosystems, USA) and SYBR Green chemistry. Expression was normalised using Actin1 (GenBank Accession No. U60489 for tobacco; AF246288 for banana) as a housekeeping gene, and relative fold change was calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001; Chaudhary et al. 2022). GraphPad Prism 10 software was used for data plotting and presentation (Mavrevski et al. 2018).

Histology and Confocal Imaging

Fully expanded leaves from 3‐month‐old T2 generation transgenic (MWS) and control tobacco plants were fixed in FAA (formalin:acetic acid:ethanol, 1:1:18 v/v/v) fixative, dehydrated through ethanol series (25%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100%), embedded in paraffin wax, and sectioned by following the previously described method (Shivani et al. 2018). Sections were stained with Safranin red (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) and imaged under a stereomicroscope (Leica Biosystems, Germany) for the histology studies. GFP expression was analysed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880, Germany).

Seed Germination and Yield Assessment

Germination indices for T1 and T2 seeds from three independent transgenic (MWS lines) and control tobacco plants were assessed on MS basal medium with/without hygromycin. To assess more accurate and statistically valid yield data, the sample size of MWS lines was systematically increased where T3 seed pods from ten T2 plants of each of five independent lines, along with controls, were harvested to determine total dry weight 10 pods (g), average dry weight per pod (g), total dry seed weight from 10 pods (g) and average seed number per pod per 10 pods were also calculated.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy‐Dispersive X‐Ray Spectroscopy (EDX) Analysis

Leaf trichomes and seed morphology of MWS lines and control plants were analysed via SEM (JEOL 6000 NeoScope, USA). Elemental profiling of seed coat was performed using EDX (JEOL 6000 NeoScope, USA) following critical point drying and gold particles coating (Frassine et al. 2025). SEM‐processed seeds were assessed through EDX for elemental content (carbon, nitrogen and oxygen) to infer phytochemicals (terpenoids and phytosterols) accumulation (Mishra et al. 2009; Milton et al. 2025).

Seed Lipid and Phytosterol Profiling

Seed lipids from T3 MWS lines were analysed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were prepared following previous report (Tian et al. 2021). In brief, 10 mg seeds were treated with methyl esterification solution containing 5% sulphuric acid in methanol (1 mL), 0.2% butylated hydroxytoluene (25 μL) and toluene (300 μL). The mixture was incubated at 90°C for 2 h, then cooled at room temperature, and mixed with 0.9% sodium chloride (1.5 mL) and hexane (1 mL). The FAMEs were extracted in hexane and analysed by using GC–MS with 5973 quadrupole detector (Agilent, USA). Compound identification was performed by comparing mass spectra with the NIST database (https://www.nist.gov/pml/atomic‐spectra‐database) using B.06.00.116 software version (Agilent MassHunter, USA) (Li et al. 2006; Tian et al. 2021).

Phytohormone Estimation by Liquid Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)

Leaves from controls and three independent MWS T2 generation lines were analysed for gibberellic acid (GA3), indole acetic acid (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA) and Zeatin (Z) content by liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) (QTRAP 5500+, Applied Biosystems SCIEX, USA) following a previous report (Cao et al. 2020). In brief, hormones were extracted in cold methanol:water:acetic acid (80:19:1), shaken for 10 min, and incubated on a rotary shaker (4°C in the dark for 16 h). Supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 13 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, filtered using 0.22 μm nylon syringe filter, dried in a vacuum concentrator and then resuspended in methanol.

Chromatographic separation was performed by injecting 10 μL extract into Atlantis Premier BEH C18 AX2 column (Waters, Ireland) with a methanol/water gradient. Hormones were quantified via multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) against the standards calibration curve. MRM transitions for specific hormones are listed in Table S4. Standard calibration curve for each hormone was prepared using serial dilutions (50, 25, 12.5, 6.25 and 3.125 ng).

Protein Isolation

Total proteins from T3 seeds of the MWS lines were extracted as described previously with minor modifications (Wang et al. 2006; Wu et al. 2014). Briefly, ~100 mg of tobacco seeds were ground in liquid nitrogen, followed by precipitation in 10% (v/v) TCA/acetone and centrifugation at 16 000 g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was rinsed once with 80% methanol containing 0.1 M ammonium acetate, followed by a wash with ice‐cold acetone under the same centrifugation conditions. During each wash, the pellet was fully resuspended either by vortexing or by gentle aspiration through a syringe needle.

The final pellet was air‐dried and solubilised in 5 mL of 1:1 (v/v) phenol, pH 8.0 (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA): SDS buffer containing 30% sucrose, 2% SDS, 0.1 M Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5% 2‐mercaptoethanol (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. The upper phenol phase obtained after centrifugation (16 000 g, 30 min, 4°C) was mixed with methanol containing 0.1 M ammonium acetate and stored overnight at −20°C. After a final centrifugation (16 000 g, 30 min, 4°C), the pellet was air‐dried, resuspended in SDS buffer, and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Protein concentration was determined using a NanoPhotometer NP80 (IMPLEN, Germany).

Sample Preparation for Quantitative LC–MS/MS Analysis

Protein samples from MWS line 1 and control seeds were prepared as previously described (Shevchenko et al. 2006). For each sample, ~70 μg protein was resolved by SDS–PAGE, and gel slices were excised for in‐gel digestion. Proteins were reduced with 30 μL of 10 mM DTT at 60°C for 30 min, cooled to room temperature, and alkylated with 60 μL of 55 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 1 h. Gel pieces were washed twice with 100 mM NH4HCO₃/ acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) for 30 min. Trypsin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was added at a ratio of 1:50 (w/w, trypsin:protein), and samples were incubated overnight at 37°C. The resulting peptides were desalted using ZipTips (Merck Millipore, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions and dried under vacuum to remove residual primary amines from the reagents.

TripleTOF Nano‐LC–MS/MS

Peptide samples were analysed on an Eksigent Nano LC 2D system (SCIEX) coupled directly to a SCIEX TripleTOF 5600 ESI mass spectrometer. Samples were first concentrated on a Nano Trap cartridge (ChromeXP C18, 3 μm, 120 Å pore size; SCIEX, part number 5016752), washed with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and 2% (v/v) acetonitrile in water, and then separated on a Nano column (ChromeXP C18, 3 μm, 120 Å; SCIEX, part number 805‐00120) at a flow rate of 0.3 μL min−1. The mobile phases were: solvent A—98% water, 2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid (v/v); solvent B—98% acetonitrile, 2% water, 0.1% formic acid (v/v). A multi‐step gradient was applied: 5% B for 1 min, 25% B for 1–16 min, 25%–80% B from 16 to 18 min, 80% B from 18 to 24 min, 5% B from 24 to 27 min and 5% B from 27 to 45 min.

Mass spectra were acquired using a data‐dependent acquisition mode, beginning with a full scan in the Orbitrap (resolution 120 000), followed by MS/MS scans of the 20 most intense precursor ions with a relative collision energy of 5% in the HCD cell, a repeat count of 1, and a dynamic exclusion duration of 35 s.

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data were preferably derived from three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates. qRT‐PCR results are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using two‐way ANOVA with significance levels noted at *p ≤ 0.01; **p ≤ 0.001; ***p ≤ 0.0001 and ****p ≤ 0.00001 in GraphPad Prism v10 (GraphPad Software Inc. USA). Leaf thickness measurements in histological sections were quantified and adjusted using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment, heat map and PCA plot for GC–MS data interpretation were performed using KEGG pathways database and R packages (R v. 4.3.3, https://www.R‐project.org/), respectively.
