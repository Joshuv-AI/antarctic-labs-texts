Button-related finger injuries in textile workers: the hidden danger of the industry
Authors: Mehmet Şah Şakçı, Mümin Karahan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to evaluate button-related injuries, which are increasingly common in the textile and apparel manufacturing sector, to contribute to diagnosis and treatment and to raise awareness among employers and workers.Methods:This retrospective study included 23 patients with button-related injuries. The study evaluated patient demographics, the department they presented to, the level of hand injury, accompanying pathologies (osseous, neurological, vascular, and tendinous injuries), tetanus immunization status, occupational health and safety training status, awareness of workplace physicians, prior awareness of such injuries, history of occupational accidents, and long-term outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v21. Descriptive data were summarized as frequency (%) or mean ± SD, and the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality.Results:Among the 23 patients (14 males, 9 females), 16 sustained injuries to the distal phalanx and pulp level, while 7 had injuries at the middle phalanx level. At presentation, 4 patients exhibited hypoesthesia on the affected side of the finger, and 9 patients had reduced blood flow. Two patients had accompanying phalanx fractures. No tendinous pathologies were observed in any of the patients. All patients underwent surgical intervention. Seven patients developed nail deformities in the follow-up.Conclusion:Timely intervention and appropriate treatment after an injury are crucial for limb salvage and functionality. In long-term follow-ups, most patients healed without major complications; however, some the patients developed nail deformities.Level of Evidence: Level IV, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

Occupational accidents, which are a major problem in working life, cause significant social and economic issues along with loss of life and physical injuries. For this reason, occupational accidents are a serious global public health concern.1 According to the International Labour Organization (ILO), approximately 2.93 million people lose their lives annually due to occupational accidents and diseases, and about 395 million workers experience non-fatal occupational accidents.2

In Türkiye, industrial injuries have increased with rapid industrialization and migration from rural to urban areas.3 In approximately 75% of workplaces in the textile sector, occupational health and safety (OHS) efforts have been reported to be inadequate or only partially adequate.4 As automatization increases, the nature of accidents and injuries may also vary.5

Textile and apparel manufacturing workers frequently experience injuries such as hand cuts, button-related injuries, needle punctures, crushes, and compressions. Button-related injuries, like other foreign body injuries of the hand, can lead to severe damage involving nerves, tendons, muscles, blood vessels, and bones, potentially resulting in finger amputations.6 When removing foreign bodies with specific characteristics from the hand, it is crucial to exercise caution and to be familiar with the appropriate removal techniques in order to prevent injury to neurovascular structures and other tissues.6,7 A review of the literature indicates insufficient data on button-related injuries, as well as a lack of consensus regarding diagnosis and treatment, coupled with a lack of awareness among employers and workers. This study aimed to manage cases of button-related injuries, which are likely to become more common in the textile and apparel manufacturing sector, to contribute to diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and methods

The study was designed as a retrospective and descriptive study. Written informed was obtained from all patients. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Batman Training and Research Hospital on January 23, 2024, with protocol number 234967216. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study included 23 cases presenting with button-related injuries to a tertiary education and research hospital between January 1, 2022, and January 1, 2024.

Inclusion criteria were defined as cases admitted to the emergency department or orthopedic outpatient clinic of the study center with button-related injuries while working in textile and apparel manufacturing facilities between January 1, 2022, and January 1, 2024. Cases who admitted outside the period between January 1, 2022, and January 1, 2024, sustained button-related injuries occurring outside of textile manufacturing facilities or received initial treatment at another center but presented for follow-up were excluded from the study.

The study examined patients’ gender, age, presenting department, level of hand injury, accompanying pathologies (osseous, neurological, vascular, tendinous injuries), tetanus immunization status, OHS training status, availability of workplace physicians, awareness of such injuries, history of prior occupational accidents, neuropsychiatric conditions, and long-term outcomes. Patients were followed for a minimum of 1 year (12 months).

Treatment plans were developed based on the initial examination findings of the patients, considering accompanying osseous, vascular, tendinous, and neurological injuries as well as nail bed damage and tissue defects. Interventions were performed under sterile conditions in the operating room with digital block anesthesia. In 11 patients, button components were removed using a K-wire cutter. In the remaining 12 patients, the button was dissected and removed from the surrounding tissues as a whole without requiring a K-wire cutter. Irrigation and debridement were performed with saline solution. In patients with nail bed injuries, the nail bed and pulp were repaired together. Patients with osseous pathologies were treated with the application of a finger splint after the procedure. All patients were administered 1 g of first-generation cephalosporin as antibiotic prophylaxis at their initial presentation. Tetanus vaccination was given to 9 patients whose vaccination status could not be determined or whose last vaccination was more than 10 years prior, provided that their wounds were not contaminated. For 9 patients with distal phalanx injuries who presented with decreased capillary refill in the pulp at the initial visit, acetylsalicylic acid (100 mg, once daily) was prescribed for 7 days following the intervention. Patients returned for follow-up in the second week, and subsequent follow-ups were conducted at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months.

Descriptive statistics for categorical variables are reported as frequency (n) and percentage (%), and continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the age variable, which was found to follow a normal distribution. Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 23 patients with button-related injuries were included in the study. All injuries occurred while attaching buttons using machinery in textile and apparel manufacturing facilities. Around 39.1% (n = 9) of the patients were female, and 60.9% (n = 14) were male. The age distribution of the cases was tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the ages were found to follow a normal distribution (P = .908). The mean age of the patients was 28.4 ± 4.8 years, with the youngest being 19 years old and the oldest 38 years old (Table 1).

In 39.1% of the patients (n = 9), the middle finger was injured, while the second finger was injured in 34.7% (n = 8) and the thumb in 26% (n = 6).

Additionally, 8.7% (n = 2) of the patients presented to the orthopedic and traumatology outpatient clinic, while 91.3% (n = 21) presented to the emergency department. Injuries were located at the middle phalanx level in 30.4% (n = 7) of the patients, and at the distal phalanx level in 69.6% (n = 16). At the time of presentation, 8.7% (n = 2) of the patients had accompanying phalanx fractures (Figure 1A), 17.4% (n = 4) had hypoesthesia in the finger, and 39.1% (n = 9) had decreased blood flow at the fingertip (Figure 1B) (Table 2). No tendinous pathologies were observed in any of the patients.

In 4 patients with middle phalanx injuries, the hypoesthesia on the side where the button was located resolved in follow-up visits after the button was removed and treatment was administered. In 9 of the 16 patients with distal phalanx injuries, decreased blood flow at the fingertip observed during examination was no longer present in follow-ups after the button was removed and treatment was given. Regarding tetanus immunization status, 39.1% (n = 9) of the patients were either inadequately vaccinated or had an unknown vaccination status, while 60.9% (n = 14) were fully vaccinated for tetanus (Table 2).

None of the patients reported receiving OHS training, and none were aware of the presence of an occupational physician at their workplaces. When asked about prior awareness of button-related injuries, 13% (n = 3) of the patients reported having heard of such injuries, while 87.0% (n = 20) had not. Regarding the history of prior occupational accidents, 39.1% (n = 9) of the patients had experienced an occupational accident, while 60.9% (n = 14) had not (Table 3).

In terms of long-term outcomes, 30.4% (n = 7) of the patients developed nail deformities, while 69.6% (n = 16) achieved full recovery (Figure 2A and B) (Table 4).

No neuropsychiatric conditions were identified in any of the patients based on their medical history or previous records.

Discussion

The textile sector is one of the most significant industries within the manufacturing sector. Due to the intensive manual labor required in textile factories, injuries to the fingers and other parts of the hand are likely. In accidents occurring in the United States during 2021-2022, the rate of hand injuries in textile factories was approximately 26%, while it was around 28.5% in textile product factories.6 In Türkiye, one of the world’s largest textile exporters, the textile sector ranks second in accident frequency rates in the subsector of textile and leather product manufacturing and third in the subsector of apparel manufacturing when compared to leading European Union countries.8 However, no data on the rate of finger and hand injuries in occupational accidents within the textile sector in Türkiye exist in the literature.

To better understand button-related injuries, it is essential to know the working principles of buttons and button press machines. The textile and apparel manufacturing factory was visited to examine buttons and button press machines. Buttons consist of components that are fitted into each other by a machine. Depending on their intended use, button parts can be made entirely of metal or partially of plastic. While there are various types of button machines, their working mechanism is the same; they interlock 2 button parts and are called button press machines (Figure 3A). The pressing force of a button press machine (Figure 3B) is 9806 newtons. Some buttons, once assembled, have a partial gap between their components, while others have no gap (Figure 3C and D).

Although the textile industry is classified as a hazardous or less hazardous work group in terms of risk assessment, workplace accidents adversely affect the physical and mental health of workers, leaving them in difficult social and economic circumstances. The main causes of button-related injuries include negligence, fatigue, and concentration problems due to long working hours, the necessity for workers to work quickly to meet deadlines, and inadequate knowledge of occupational safety.9 A study investigating the adequacy of OHS efforts across sectors in Türkiye reported that the textile sector had the poorest OHS practices.4 The fact that none of the patients in ther study had received OHS training aligns with the literature. Additionally, the high rate of prior occupational accidents among the patients indicates inadequate risk assessments and inspections.

The fingers and fingertips are crucial parts of the hand, and any injury to them can impair functionality and compromise esthetics. Since most patients with finger injuries present to emergency departments, they should be evaluated by triage and emergency physicians and, if necessary, consulted with a hand surgeon as soon as possible in cases requiring replantation or microsurgery.10,11 Patients with button-related injuries should seek medical attention at the nearest healthcare facility without delay. Delays increase the risk of infection and worsen circulatory disorders in the finger, potentially leading to severe outcomes such as amputation. Patients may need to be referred to experienced orthopedic or plastic surgery departments. For buttons with gaps between their components, the parts can be separated using a K-wire cutter. Buttons without gaps, which crush all surrounding tissues, including bone, must be carefully dissected and released from surrounding tissues as a whole. Pulling on the button after the injury should be avoided, as it may cause severe soft tissue and osseous pathologies.12 When removing buttons with a wire cutter, other fingers can also be damaged. To prevent this, all patients underwent digital block anesthesia before intervention and intervened carefully after the finger was completely numb. In these cases, the other fingers were not injured while the buttons were removed. The primary goal should be to preserve the existing finger as much as possible while achieving a functional and esthetically satisfactory outcome.

Hand injuries are among the most common injuries in the body, accounting for 6.6%-28.6% of all musculoskeletal injuries.13 The hand is a complex structure composed of tightly packed tissues (skin, nerves, tendons, vessels, bones, joints, and intrinsic muscles) within a small area.14 Any or all of these tissues can be injured during hand injuries. In the literature, tissue injury rates in crush hand injuries differ from those in the study. Studies conducted in North India and Ethiopia reported varying rates of tendon, neurovascular, and osseous injuries in hand injuries.15,16 In this study, initial examinations revealed decreased capillary refill in the fingertips of 9 patients, hypoesthesia on the affected side of the finger in 4 patients, and associated distal phalanx fractures in 2 patients. In long-term follow-ups, 7 patients developed nail deformities, while the other 16 patients healed without complications.

In the literature, studies on button-related injuries exist only as case reports.17,18 In 1 study, 2 cases involved injuries at the distal phalanx level of the index finger,17 while another study reported a case of injury at the distal phalanx level of the middle finger.18 None of these cases had associated injuries. In all 3 cases, no functional deficits developed during the follow-up period, aside from cosmetic concerns. This study includes a total of 23 patients. Among these patients, 9 had injuries to the middle finger, 8 to the index finger, and 6 to the thumb. Seven patients sustained injuries at the middle phalanx level, and 16 at the distal phalanx level. At the time of injury, 2 patients had associated distal phalanx fractures, 4 had hypoesthesia, and 9 experienced reduced blood flow at the fingertip. In the long-term follow-up, 7 patients developed nail bed deformities, but all patients showed complete functional recovery. Given the large sample size and the variety of associated injuries, this study provides a valuable contribution to the literature. The descriptive nature of the study and its single-center design are its limitations. Multicenter studies are needed to evaluate the causal factors of these injuries and implement preventive measures.

Button-related injuries are preventable occupational accidents. Such injuries can be prevented by selecting suitable workers for the job, providing adequate OHS training, improving working conditions, using protective equipment, adhering to safety regulations, and implementing effective inspection mechanisms. Timely intervention and appropriate treatment after an injury are crucial for limb salvage and functionality.
