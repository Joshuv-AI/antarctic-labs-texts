Ensuring respiratory protection to improve population health, economic vitality, and national security
Authors: Stephanie M Holm, Kristin J Cummings, Sundaresan Jayaraman, Shawn G Gibbs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryRespiratory protection decreases the risk of inhalation-related illnesses, but only if such protection is available, with a robust framework in place to support it. There are many current and potential inhalational hazards, and without adequate respiratory protection, these threaten not only health, but also economic prosperity and national security. The United States needs to build off existing expertise to implement a framework for respiratory protection of the entire populace. A recent National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine Report laid out detailed recommendations for such a framework, and there are steps that can be taken now. Starting such a framework in the most cost-effective and efficient way possible requires preserving the existing expertise, experience, and infrastructure in NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) and the National Personal Protective Technologies Laboratory.Keywords: Respiratory protection, Respirators, NIOSH, Occupational medicine, Environmental medicine, Pollution

Introduction

Inhalation-related illnesses in the United States (US) are often preventable and the result of inadequate knowledge and access to respiratory protection. This poses national risks related to individual and population health, economic costs from lost workdays and productivity, national security threats from susceptibility to intentional respiratory harm, and many others. In this article, we describe the current state of respiratory protection for workers and the public, provide examples of increasing respiratory threats, present recommendations that have been made for systemic solutions for respiratory protection, and propose ways the nation could efficiently move toward an improved system now, utilizing existing expertise and infrastructure that have been built over decades of investment by our nation to enhance occupational health and safety.

Respiratory protection for workers

Under the current US framework,1 workers who are regularly exposed to well-defined inhalation hazards receive fit testing, training, and well-fitted respiratory protective devices—that have undergone National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) approval—as part of an Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)-mandated respiratory protection program (RPP). Yet many workers “remain exposed to inhalation hazards that increase their risk of illness.” (1) Many are not covered by an RPP, including those with informal work arrangements (such as gig workers, day laborers, etc.), some employees of state governments, workers on small farms, and those who have less predictable and less regular exposure to respiratory hazards (such as outdoor workers being exposed to wildfire smoke). In addition to the concerns regarding gaps in RPP coverage, there are simply few or no approved respiratory protection options for some worker groups such as people with atypical facial structures or disabilities, those with particularly small faces and those with facial hair, etc. Because these features are more likely in certain subpopulations, this lack of existing equipment disproportionately affects certain groups. For example, deaf and hard-of-hearing workers, female Asian workers, child workers, and male Sikh workers are likely to fall into these categories. Even for those with access to NIOSH-approved respirators that might fit them through the marketplace, such devices are approved on the assumption that they will be used in the context of an RPP. In the absence of fit testing, training, and other RPP components, the protection afforded to workers by such devices may be diminished.

Respiratory protection for the public

The general public is an even more diverse population than the workforce, including members who are very young and very old, and with a wider range of health statuses and abilities. The public needs protection from environmental hazards, such as volcanic emissions and wildfire smoke; artisans and hobbyists also need respiratory protection from some of the same exposures encountered by workers in RPPs. Challenges to obtaining the needed protection include ensuring proper fit given the physical and demographic heterogeneity of the population and socio-economic-behavioral considerations that impact access to and effective use of respiratory protective devices. Unfortunately, the public lacks components1 of respiratory protection that are robust for some worker groups, such as the work done by NIOSH to ensure that products meet established standards and the fit testing and education that are performed in RPPs. There is no formal system for coordinating the development and distribution of respiratory protection guidance to state and local health agencies or the public. The lack of a comprehensive regulatory or educational framework makes it difficult to anticipate the level of protection that could be achieved by respiratory protective devices for the public against a known or unknown inhalation hazard.

Benefits of respiratory health to the nation

In addition to our ethical responsibility to protect workers and the public from respiratory hazards, the economic vitality, and security of the nation depend on a healthy workforce and population. Employees’ poor health costs their employers thousands of dollars per employee per year,2 which dwarfs the marginal cost of providing each employee with regular respiratory protection3 in addition to their other health benefits. In fact, a recent cost-benefit analysis looking at the use of N95 respirators in medical settings during a viral pandemic found that provision of N95s saved money.4 Business continuity planning—the concept of risk mitigation through preemptively assessing potential risks and their effects and proactively developing and implementing plans to keep business functioning as usual during unusual circumstances—should include consideration of respiratory protection as a strategy for mitigating the effects of respiratory hazards. It is in the best interest of businesses to have an easy-to-implement structure or framework that supports their adoption of respiratory protection in their business continuity planning.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention have long recommended the use of respiratory protection by those responding to chemical or biological terrorism incidents,5 as was demonstrated in the 2001 Anthrax attacks6 when postal workers were highly affected; workers in other professions may also benefit from respiratory protection during active threats. During the early 20th century world wars, the provision of “gas masks” (respirators) to the public was widespread. Despite a global biodefense market that is currently estimated to be in trillions of dollars, little of that has been spent expanding education on available equipment for and access to respiratory protection. Because the United Nations has concluded that conflict and violence are increasing,7 improved respiratory protections for workers and the public would increase the US's ability to protect itself and its population from potential inhalational threats. In fact, many respiratory hazards are increasing, making critical the need for improved regulation, training, and research around respiratory protection.

A flurry of inhalation hazards pose urgent risks

The respiratory health of workers and the public is under constant threat from a range of inhalation hazards arising out of wildfires, infectious diseases, natural disasters, and air pollution.

Wildfires

Since 1983, U.S. wildfires have become more destructive and have burned more acreage per year.8 The 10 years with the largest area burned have all occurred since 2004, including peaks in 2015 and 2020.8 In addition, since 2000, there has been a 36% increase in the wildland–urban interface (WUI) globally, with 85% of the growth occurring between 2010 and 2020.9 In the continental United States, an estimated 32% of all houses are now located in the WUI.10 As a result of these changes, there are more opportunities for workers and members of the public to be exposed to harmful components of wildfire smoke.11 During the 2018 Camp Fire, the city of Sacramento, California had the planet's worst 24-h average fine particulate matter (PM2.5) levels.12 The January 2025 Los Angeles fires that devastated Pacific Palisades and Altadena were associated with spikes in airborne lead and arsenic concentrations.13 And the impact is not limited to the west: in June 2023, the plume of smoke from Canadian wildfires affected over 100 million people in the northeastern US.14 Wildfire smoke exposure has been robustly associated with respiratory disease and all-cause mortality.15 Children also have worsened respiratory health following smoke exposure; and, exposure during pregnancy increases the risk of premature birth and fetal growth issues.16 There is also increasing concern for long-term impacts of wildfire smoke,17 especially because many people are exposed to multiple wildfire smoke events. In fact, it has been estimated that the direct costs of wildfire smoke exposures in the US are more than $80 million per year,18 with most of those costs arising from long-term consequences of the exposures.

Infectious diseases

There are also emerging airborne infectious disease threats to workers and the public. Influenza A H5N1 has been circulating in birds for two decades, causing sporadic human cases with a case fatality rate of greater than 50%.19 In 2024, an unprecedented outbreak of H5N1 began in dairy cows, affecting over 900 herds in 16 states.20 As of late February 2025, there have been 70 confirmed cases in humans in the United States, including in 41 dairy workers.20 While most cases have been characterized by mild illness,21 one fatal case was recently reported in a community member.22 Sustained transmission of H5N1 in dairy cattle and other mammals raises the specter of human-to-human airborne transmission and an influenza pandemic, with dire consequences for both workers and the general public.23 Another example is not a novel virus, but a well-known one, that was declared eliminated from the United States in 2000: measles. Measles is highly contagious and readily transmitted through the airborne route, with a 90% attack rate and survival time in the air of up to 2 h.24 Prolonged outbreaks of imported measles have threatened the US measles elimination status, and recently, an outbreak of measles was identified in Texas in early 2025 with 309 cases, 40 hospitalizations, and one death reported as of late March, with more cases anticipated.25 Measles is a preventable but untreatable disease, which not only causes a rash and severe flu-like illness but can also lead to death or lifelong complications like intellectual disabilities or deafness.24 The median societal cost of each measles case has been estimated to be more than 30 thousand dollars,26 making this a particularly contagious, serious, and costly respiratory hazard.

Hurricanes

Warmer ocean temperatures are contributing to more intense hurricanes that bring heavier rainfall and greater flood risks.27 Eight of the ten most active years of North Atlantic hurricane activity have occurred since the mid-1990s.28 These storms and floods leave behind water-damaged buildings that are prone to fungal and bacterial growth, negatively impacting indoor air quality and posing a threat to workers and members of the public.29 First responders, homeowners, workers, and volunteers involved in response and recovery after storms and floods can be exposed to elevated airborne concentrations of microbes and their by-products, particularly during remediation and demolition work.30 In New Orleans following Hurricane Katrina in 2005, nearly half of randomly selected homes had visible mold growth and 16% had at least 50% mold coverage.31 Air sampling documented elevated levels of culturable mold, spores, endotoxin, and glucan at baseline31,32 and during restoration activities.33 Following Hurricane Sandy, which caused flooding in the New York area in 2012, inhalational exposures were common among a group of New Yorkers: 37% reported exposure to mold or damp environments and 33% reported reconstruction exposure.34 Among a cohort of 151 adolescents with asthma in North Carolina, 26% reported their home had mold or mildew as a result of flooding or damage from Hurricane Matthew in 2016.35 Mold exposures are associated with increased allergic and respiratory symptoms,36 as well as cognitive effects like brain fog.37 More recently, massive flooding in North Carolina, following landfall of Hurricane Helene in fall 2024, wiped out roads and devastated local infrastructure, leaving many without access to clean drinking water.38

Air pollution

Air pollution continues to pose a threat to workers and the general public. Since the enactment of the Clean Air Act in 1970, the United States has experienced steady improvement in ambient air quality.39 However, since 2016, wildfire smoke has begun to reverse decades-long gains in air quality in much of the continental US, eroding more than 50% of the improvements in PM2.5 in many western states.40 Furthermore, ongoing emissions from vehicles, factories, and other sources impact air quality throughout the country.39 In 2024, more than 90 million Americans lived in counties with particulate levels that exceeded the national air quality standard.41 Among the most polluted cities, concentrations of PM2.5 increased by an average of about 0.25 mcg/m3.41 Air pollution is one of the largest contributors to the global burden of respiratory diseases.42 This is because air pollution exacerbates and contributes to both acute respiratory illnesses like colds and pneumonias, and chronic illnesses like Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) and Asthma. More than 40 million Americans have one of these two chronic diseases with annual costs estimated to be over 100 million dollars for these diseases combined.43, 44, 45, 46

People affected

The impacts of these respiratory hazards are unequally distributed across the population. Children are particularly susceptible to respiratory hazards because of their higher breathing rate relative to their body size and developing airways. Other susceptible groups include those with pre-existing health conditions, which can be exacerbated by exposure to these respiratory hazards, and people of limited economic means who may be less able to take other protective actions. Workers who are not part of an existing respiratory protection program are vulnerable to respiratory hazards since they are unlikely to have had training and access to respiratory protective devices. These imply that businesses without existing RPP, and those that either serve or employ high-risk populations and their caregivers, may be increasingly hit with economic costs of these rising respiratory hazards. Another group at particular risk of respiratory hazards—including ones that may be more difficult for us to anticipate—are those serving our country in other parts of the world. That includes people in the military and foreign service, as well as their families.1

Weathering the perfect storm of these threats to population health, economic vitality, and national security

The hierarchy of controls47—a foundational concept in occupational/environmental health and industrial hygiene—explains that provision of personal protective equipment (PPE) should be used only when other control methods are unavailable or impossible. As such, it is key that the other options for controlling hazards (elimination, substitution, engineering, and administrative controls) are supported and encouraged both within and outside workplaces. This includes actions such as vaccination for measles and forest management practices to decrease wildfires.

It is impossible to eliminate or control all potential inhalation hazards for all populations. Thus, having appropriate and accessible respiratory protective devices to use as PPE is a necessary safety measure to protect the population, economy, and national security.

A suggested framework

A recent National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine (NASEM)1 consensus study developed a blueprint for transformative action to weather this perfect storm and enhance the nation's preparedness by proposing frameworks for protecting workers and the public from inhalation hazards arising from inhalational threats like those mentioned above. As shown in Fig. 1, the proposed framework incorporates seven core functions, each comprising specific sub-functions, which may be ongoing or triggered by a specific hazard. In the context of emergencies, the ongoing sub-functions represent critical aspects of national preparedness. The functions encompass the critical elements necessary to ensure effective respiratory protection for everyone (e.g., hazard identification and assessment, product design and manufacturing, and device selection, training, and use). The framework addresses the need for an accountable entity to coordinate all the functions and incorporate multiple feedback loops across the milieu of stakeholders to ensure ongoing improvement in the diverse functions encompassed by the framework. With the ongoing threats from coast to coast and across the globe, the time to implement these recommendations is now.

The framework, applicable to both workers and the public, is intended to evolve over time, and to be agile in anticipating and responding to emerging threats. By design, the framework for action shown in Fig. 1 is generic and it will be instantiated for the specific threat because the responses or actions taken to provide respiratory protection will depend on the specific threat. For instance, as part of Function F1, “Assess Hazard and Determine Need for Respiratory Protection,” the potential impact of the specific hazard will be assessed based on metrics such as the nature (type) of hazard, the severity of the threat, the duration of the threat, the available countermeasures, size of the population affected, and so on, to determine the need for respiratory protection. Using the results of this analysis in Function F1, the necessary respiratory protective device to protect against the specific threat is determined in Function F2. Likewise, the other functions in the framework are carried out to determine the tailored response at each stage to the specific threat. Therefore, the key performance indicators (KPIs) will be determined and defined based on the specific threat in each function and iteratively improved as part of Function F5. For example, at a high level, these could include the effectiveness of the device against the threat, the type of training needed to use the device, and compliance with the use of the device, among others. The recommendations in the NASEM report were detailed and tailored to address the distinct needs of workers and the public (nine recommendations each).

For the workers, it began with the recommendation that OSHA respiratory protection requirements should apply to all workers and recommended that Congress should revise the OSH Act to address gaps in OSHA coverage and that OSHA should revise its statutory interpretations regarding definitions of employers and employees. It recommended that State legislatures should require employers to protect workers who are not currently under OSHA jurisdiction from inhalation hazards through legislation and regulations. For workplaces where respirators are currently necessary, OSHA's Respiratory Protection Standard requires employers to establish a written RPP overseen by a qualified program administrator.48 The program must be worksite-specific and include procedures for the selection, use, maintenance, and storage of the equipment. Medical evaluation and respirator fit testing must be provided before employees are required to use a respirator in the workplace. The NASEM committee recommended further empowering OSHA as the appropriate coordinating entity for the development, functioning, oversight, evaluation, and continuous improvement of its proposed framework for workers not currently covered by RPPs. To address the greatly expanded coverage of workers and the need to carry out the efforts on a strong scientific foundation, the recommendations included increasing the capacity of the National Personal Protective Technology Laboratory (NPPTL) and the research and surveillance activities of NIOSH.

Unfortunately, this occupational approach is neither suitable nor feasible for the public. As such, coordination at the federal level is necessary to address the core functions shown in Fig. 1 and ensure that members of the public have access to, and understand the appropriate use of, respiratory protective devices. For the public, the principal recommendation was to establish a “Coordinating Entity” to oversee the functions in the framework in Fig. 1 to provide respiratory protection for the public. It recommended that Congress should establish a coordinating entity within the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) with the necessary responsibility, authority, and resources to provide a unified and authoritative source of information and effective oversight of respiratory protective devices for the public. It recommended the establishment of an interim office in HHS to begin the tasks of the coordinating entity until its formal establishment. The other recommendations defining the pathway to action for the public are detailed in the report. In the interest of promoting efficiency within the federal government, we propose utilizing the existing expertise and infrastructure in HHS.

The partnership being proposed in the upcoming sections is a first step to begin the implementation of the framework defined in the NASEM report to ensure respiratory protection for workers and the public. The proposed partnership is even more critical in the currently evolving landscape of NIOSH's organizational structure.

Efficient and cost-effective actions that should happen now

In 2025, HHS has been undergoing a historic reorganization, and the situation remains dynamic. Prior to that reorganization, there were multiple governmental organizations with input into the governmentally regulated respiratory exposure standards, respirator approval processes, and respiratory protection programs. Occupational Safety and Health Administration provides respiratory protection standards that govern respiratory protection programs with National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), which was subject to a massive reduction in its workforce and elimination of its components, providing testing standards and approvals of these devices. NIOSH also provides the exposure limits that dictate exposures of concern in the workplace and the US Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) does so outside of the workplace. There are also many professional organizations that should have a role in determining the appropriate exposure levels as well as the appropriate standards and capabilities of respiratory protective devices (American National Standards Institute, American Society for Testing and Materials, American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists, American Industrial Hygiene Association, American Society of Safety Professionals, etc.). Recommendations for educational materials and training programs aimed at the general public not covered under RPPs should come from all stakeholder organizations; however, they need to be coordinated and centralized under a non-regulatory governmental agency such as NIOSH or National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS). This will allow for the sharing of best practices nationwide, equipping all people to opt-in to the best respiratory protection available.

Moving forward: principal entities for ensuring respiratory protection

NIEHS and NIOSH have a long history and familiarity with this issue, the devices, the critical infrastructure, and the programs involved. Either one is well suited for coordinating respiratory protection efforts, but the involvement of both entities is necessary to protect both workers and the public. NIOSH and their National Personal Protective Technologies Laboratory are the governmental experts on improvement and research into personal protective equipment, including respiratory protective devices.49

NIEHS's Worker Training Program (WTP) has four decades of experience training workers, volunteers, and the public to improve health, safety, and worker well-being within the work environment and public.50 They deploy and provide expertise, training, and implementation during times of national crises, including national disasters and terrorist attacks. One potential model to move this forward could be their joint leadership to create a programmatic paradigm through stakeholder involvement. This joint leadership could allow for alignment and collaboration that would be able to tackle the issues surrounding respiratory protection of both workers and the public. Then once the paradigm is finalized, NIEHS WTP could take the lead in the operationalization and dissemination to the nation through the workplace and beyond, similar to how NIEHS did with their Infectious Disease Biological Hazards Training model that was instrumental in early COVID-19 response. As with all public health programs, this would not be a linear design model but a cyclical model that incorporated program evaluation, new scientific or technological developments, and other factors as part of its iterative process improvement plans. This centralized infrastructure would provide programmatic materials and a variety of training modalities with the goal of wide adoption by both workers and the general public.

Given the ongoing evolution in the structure of NIOSH and other related federal agencies, it is critical to preserve the core functions and activities that are essential to ensure occupational safety and health in our nation and thereby guard population health, promote economic vitality, and preserve national security. Fig. 2 identifies the classes of high-level core functions that need to be preserved, nurtured, and grown to ensure the future protection of the workforce along with the technical expertise, managerial experience, and infrastructure to carry them out in an efficient and effective manner.

The classes of essential core functions for occupational safety and health.

As shown in Fig. 2, Science, Engineering, Technology, and Education provide the basic foundation underlying the classes of high-level core functions associated with ensuring occupational safety and health. These include the Assessment and Evaluation of Health Effects and Basic, Clinical and Field Studies both of which are driven to ensure Protection against all Hazards, e.g., Respiratory. The Research and Development function ensures advancements to occupational health and safety while the Standards and Conformity Assessment function leads to the deployed solutions being safe and effective in practice. Information represents the lifeblood of occupational safety and health and its optimal usage is ensured by the Health Informatics and Artificial Intelligence function. The Surveillance function ensures the continuous assessment of occupational safety and health as new technologies and practices are introduced in the field over time. Serving as the Source of Scientific Evidence for Regulations, e.g., OSHA regulations, is yet another core function. Finally, the function of serving as a Trusted Knowledge Source is critical to facilitate the timely dissemination of information that will ensure the safety and health of workers and the public. The details of how these high-level core functions are executed across the various occupations/industries, e.g., mining, healthcare, firefighting, and manufacturing, is beyond the scope of this paper and are not discussed here. Since 1970, NIOSH has worked to ensure safe and healthful working conditions and worked to protect the health and safety of the American workforce. Thus, for NIOSH to continue to fulfill its mission, it is essential that personnel with the necessary expertise and experience to carry out these core functions effectively are preserved and grown for the future along with the required advanced tools, technologies, and infrastructure to ensure occupational health and safety in our nation.

There remain many potential overlaps between the agencies involved that need to be addressed prior to the implementation of such a framework (e.g. NIOSH and NIEHS in training functions and USEPA's role in non-occupational settings), and additional overlaps that will need to be coordinated even after the framework is implemented. Given the uncertainty within all organizations involved, the most flexible way to move this forward would be through a mechanism such as a Joint Task Force that includes representation from the agencies involved as well as outside, but committed, third-party representation. This Joint Task Force requires a clear charge and authority to establish coordination mechanisms for the initial implementation as well as mechanisms to identify and overcome challenges in the collaboration. These components are necessary for the long-term success of the framework, which likely will require the formation of Joint Powers Agreements and changes in funding allocation. A clear charge, with the authority to establish collaborations and agreements, would help to overcome some of the barriers that can hamper interagency collaborations.

The financial and political resources to achieve such a paradigm shift and adjustments to the mission of NIOSH and/or NIEHS pale in comparison to the cost savings that will be achieved through the reduction in lost workdays and medical care associated with respiratory disorders that can be prevented or reduced through the appropriate usage of respiratory protective devices, and the greater preparedness for national security and crisis events. Comprehensive economic and health economic analyses will help better understand the potential impact of such a program. Once an appropriate paradigm is created and adopted within the United States, it could be exported and adapted globally by any nation, which could exponentially magnify the impact of such programs.

Conclusion

There are grave threats to the respiratory health of individuals – both workers and the public – with potentially devastating effects on population health, economic vitality, and national security. Furthermore, there are widespread misunderstandings related to respiratory protection that affect our nation's preparedness in dealing with inhalation hazards from various threats. An efficient and cost-effective path forward within the US could be a collaboration between NIEHS and NIOSH to provide shared leadership for an improved process of providing respiratory protection for workers and the public.

It all begins with the preservation and growth of NIOSH's technical expertise, managerial experience, and infrastructure that have ensured the occupational health and safety of workers in our nation since its formation in December 1970. Thus, building on the proposed NIOSH-NIEHS partnership to protect both workers and the public will harness the existing resources and thereby require less upfront investment than creating a new process that starts with a public-facing entity that has never worked on respiratory protection. The framework suggested in the National Academies' report could then be used as the roadmap to guide the activities in this NIOSH-NIEHS partnership. But preserving the existing national expertise in respiratory protection for ensuring the health and safety of workers – NIOSH – is the imperative first step.

The ensuing benefits to our nation will be enormous: the improved and efficient process will provide health benefits to the population and generate cost savings through the reduction in lost workdays and medical care associated with respiratory disorders that can be prevented or reduced through the appropriate usage of respiratory protective devices. Finally, a population protected from respiratory hazards will be healthier, more resilient, and less susceptible to national security threats. The path forward must be a deliberate and well-thought-out one, which harnesses the nation's existing expertise and robust infrastructure built over several decades of investment and brings along the entire population. The time to act is now.
