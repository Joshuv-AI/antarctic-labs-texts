Transcriptional regulation of nucleotide metabolism in medulloblastoma subtypes and prognostic implications analyzed by RNA-Seq
Authors: Rong Huang, Xiaoxu Lu, Xueming Sun, Hui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSubtypes of medulloblastoma (MB), WNT, SHH, Group 3 and Group 4, have different prognoses and the impact of abnormal nucleotide metabolism remains unclear. Multi-omics data was integrated to analyze the effect of nucleotide metabolism genes on MB molecular characteristics, prognosis and drug sensitivity. The aim was to identify subtype-specific therapeutic targets to inform treatment.MethodsA total of 132 MB samples datasets were accessed, UMAP and hierarchical clustering analysis were performed using the expression profiles of 1804 nucleotide metabolism genes and association with molecular subtype was evaluated. Genes were screened and prognostic signatures constructed by univariate Cox regression, cross-validation and LASSO-Cox regression and predictive efficacy was verified in training and independent validation sets. Pharmacogenomic data were combined to predict differences in drug sensitivity between high- and low-risk groups.ResultsNucleotide metabolism gene expression profiles were distinct among the four major MB subtypes, indicating coupling of metabolic reprogramming and tumor lineage. 51 prognostic genes were screened and were involved in RNA splicing, anatomical structure maintenance and purine compound metabolism. A signature was constructed from 17 nucleotide metabolism genes which distinguished high from low-risk (p < 0.001) groups and gave an independent prognosis in multivariate analysis. Drug sensitivity analysis showed the high-risk group to be more sensitive to MEK/ERK inhibitors and the low-risk group to PLK1, IGF1R/IR, ROCK and mTORC1/2 inhibitors.ConclusionNucleotide metabolism-transcription coupling endows MB subtypes with heterogeneity and affects prognosis. The signature is a quantitative tool for individualized risk assessment and metabolism targeted therapy.Keywords: Nucleotide metabolism, Medulloblastoma, Molecular subtype, Prognostic model, Drug sensitivity

Introduction

Medulloblastoma (MB) is the most common malignant brain tumor in children, accounting for approximately 20% pediatric central nervous system tumors (Luo et al. 2025). MB usually originates from progenitor cells in the external granular layer of the cerebellum and may be subdivided according to transcriptional and epigenetic characteristics into WNT-activated (WNT-MB), SHH-activated (SHH-MB), group 3 (G3-MB) and group 4 (G4-MB) (Peterson et al. 2024). Current multimodality treatment, encompassing surgical resection, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, achieves an approximately 70% cure rate in children over 3 years old but is challenging and has significant long-term toxicities (Penco-Campillo et al. 2023), such as neurocognitive disorders, endocrine diseases and hearing loss (Diaz et al. 2025). In addition, highly aggressive molecular subgroups, especially G3-MB which shows MYC amplification, have poor prognosis under existing treatment options (Wang et al. 2025; Pham et al. 2022). The molecular heterogeneity of MB emphasizes the importance of identifying subgroup-specific therapeutic targets for patient stratification to improve treatment outcomes.

Nucleotide metabolism has previously been investigated in the context of cancer research since the synthesis of DNA and RNA is required for cell growth, proliferation, differentiation and repair (Ling et al. 2025). Nucleotide metabolism must be regulated to meet normal physiological needs but cancer cells have a greatly increased demand to facilitate rapid rates of proliferation (Hou et al. 2025; Rasche et al. 2025). Abnormal nucleotide metabolism is particularly prominent in MB (Manfreda et al. 2023). Cancer cells may upregulate nucleotide synthesis via a variety of mechanisms and the nucleotide synthases, thymidylate synthase and dihydrofolate reductase, are overexpressed in MB cells, promoting deoxynucleotide synthesis (Hashimoto et al. 2018). Purine and pyrimidine salvage synthesis pathways are also stimulated in tumor cells, using pre-existing nucleosides and bases to resynthesize nucleotides (Funke et al. 2023). In addition, nucleotide metabolism has been linked to MB drug resistance. Many chemotherapeutic drugs inhibit nucleotide metabolism but changes to nucleotide metabolism pathways by increasing target enzyme expression, changing enzyme structure or reducing enzyme-drug affinity may allow cancer cells to resist treatment (He et al. 2025). Moreover, MB molecular subgroups show differences in nucleotide metabolism which affect tumor growth rate, invasiveness and response to treatment (Muto-Fujita et al. 2025; Gajjar et al. 2021). For example, MYC-amplified G3-MB has enhanced de novo purine/pyrimidine synthesis, TCA cycle and antioxidant glutathione synthesis pathways in vivo (Wang et al. 2025).

CRISPR-screening and non-targeted metabolomics have allowed the identification of DHODH, the rate-limiting enzyme in pyrimidine synthesis, as a survival requirement for MYC-driven G3-MB. DHODH inhibition causes uridine deficiency, downregulates MYC protein and inhibits xenograft tumor growth. The small molecule inhibitor, Brequinar, prolonged the survival of G3-MB mice, an effect that was reversed by exogenous uridine, confirming the target as DHODH-mediated de novo pyrimidine synthesis (Tsea et al. 2024). MYC-MB was shown to be particularly sensitive to pyrimidine synthesis blockade by multi-omic analytical targeting of DHODH (Gwynne et al. 2022). Purine production may also be targeted and MB and G3-MB cells were shown to be highly dependent on de novo GTP synthesis mediated by IMPDH. Small molecule IMPDH inhibitors or gene knockdown inhibited tumor growth and had little effect on neural progenitor cells (Kieliszek et al. 2024). Multi-omic integrated analysis showed the expression of nucleotide metabolism enzymes to be controlled by transcription factors, such as MYC/E2F/OTX2, and influenced by subtype-specific epigenetic regulation and RNA processing. Collectively, these mechanisms may be regarded as a coordinated "metabolism-transcriptional programming module" that affects cell lineage determination, chromatin accessibility and DNA damage response (Godbole et al. 2024). Nucleotide metabolism status may thus be a molecular marker for MB subtypes and a factor affecting treatment sensitivity and prognosis.

Metabolic transcriptional coupling is considered a node for determination of malignancy differences in MB subtypes and gives a quantifiable molecular entry point for development of integrated computational models to predict individual risks and drug responses. Pharmacogenomic databases, such as GDSC, CTRP and CCLE, give sensitivities of various pyrimidine/purine metabolism inhibitors in MB cell lines, enabling a data framework to construct drug recommendation systems based on metabolic signatures. However, nucleotide metabolism remains to be systematically integrated with transcriptional regulatory networks and clinical outcomes of the four MB subtypes and cohort validation of prognostic assessment and drug sensitivity is required. The current study has the aim of mining prognostic nucleotide metabolism genes through cross-validation of large sample data set to construct and verify a prediction model and explore potential drugs to inform MB treatment based on nucleotide metabolism.

Materials and methods

Download and preprocessing of bulk transcriptomic data

Training set

We collected clinical data of 101 MB patients who received treatment at Henan Cancer Hospital from 2012 to 2025. This study has been approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee (2024-265). Inclusion criteria: (1) Age < 18 years old; (2) Received surgery and postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy treatment in our hospital; (3) Diagnosed as medulloblastoma with complete pathological records and surgical specimens. Finally, 26 eligible patients were included, and all participants were followed up through outpatient visits, medical record reviews, or telephone interviews after treatment. Obtaining surgical specimens from these 26 patients, RNA extraction, library construction, and RNA sequencing were entrusted to Novogene (Beijing, China) for completion. After passing the library inspection, different libraries are pooled according to the effective concentration and target data volume requirements for sequencing. Filter the obtained raw data first using Transpipe15 (v 0.1.5, available at https://github.com/cdesterke/transpipe15, accessed on May 28, 2025) to remove duplicates based on gene names, retain records with the highest average expression in all samples, perform log2 (TPM + 1) transformation and retain genes with an average expression of > 2 TPM. PreprocessCore (v 1.68.0) was used for quantile normalization. Data was divided into two batches using kmeans of the stats (v 4.4.3) package and batch correction was performed with sva (v 3.54.0). The optimal threshold was determined using the survival time and status of patients in the training set (via the surv_cutpoint function from the survminer package), and this method divided 26 patients into 13 high-risk patients and 13 low-risk patients (Table 1).

Validation set PBTA data

Gene set

GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org) was searched with keyword “nucleotide metabolism” and section selection: Disorders, Text-Mined Disorders, Drugs & Compounds, Function, Pathways, Publications, Expression in Human Tissues, Genomics, Phenotypes, Proteins. A total of 1,804 genes with Relevance score > 20 were identified.

Drug sensitivity data

GDSC2 Half Maximum Inhibitory Concentration (IC50) and CTRPv2 Area Under the Concentration–Time Curve (AUV) matrices were obtained from https://osf.io/c6tfx/ and GDSC2 Expr (RMA Normalized and Log Transformed).rds, GDSC2 Res.rds, CTRP2 Expr (TPM, not log transformed).rds and CTRP2 Res.rds were downloaded (Yang et al. 2024). GDSC2 used RMA-normalized and log-transformed expression data and IC50 response data and CTRP used TPM expression data (transformed by log2(x + 1)) and AUC response data.

Nucleotide metabolism and MB molecular phenotype

Dimensions of corrected data were reduced using Umap (v 0.2.10.0) and visualized by ggplot2 (v 3.5.2). Heat maps were drawn in Transpipe15 (v 0.1.5) with Euclidean distance and Ward.D2 method used as distance calculation and aggregation criteria for clustering.

Cross-validation screening of nucleotide metabolism genes associated with MB prognosis

Univariate cox analysis was performed on training and validation sets with loopcolcox (v 1.0.1, available at https://github.com/cdesterke/loopcolcox, accessed on May 28, 2025). Risk coefficient, β and p value of each candidate gene were obtained with FDR < 0.05 as the threshold and a volcano map was drawn in loopcolcox. Nucleotide metabolism genes related to prognosis were grouped according to the β symbol: β > 0 = risk gene, β < 0 = protective gene and intersection gave nucleotide metabolism genes with relevance for prognosis. Venn diagrams were drawn using VennDiagram (v 1.7.3). A scatter plot to show beta coefficients was drawn using ggplot2 (v 3.5.2). The STRING online proteomics tool (https://string-db.org, accessed May 28, 2025) was used to construct a PPI network with interaction score threshold of 400. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis was performed with clusterProfiler (v 4.14.6).

Construction and validation of nucleotide metabolism prognostic signature

LASSO Cox regression analysis was performed with tenfold cross validation Glmnet (v 4.1–8). The input was the expression matrix of nucleotide metabolism genes related to prognosis. The optimal lambda value was used to extract model coefficients and 17 genes with non-zero coefficients were screened out to generate prognostic signatures. The risk score was calculated as Σ(expr i × β i). Optimal cutoff value was calculated by Survminer (v 0.5.0) and training and validation samples were divided into high- and low-risk groups. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed by Survival (v 3.8–3) and survminer (v 0.5.0). Risk curves and survival scatter plots were drawn with Ggplot2 (v 3.5.2) and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plots of high- and low-risk samples by Stats (v 4.4.3). Heat maps showing signature gene expression were constructed by Pheatmap (v 1.0.12).

Prognostic independence of signature

Univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis of risk score and clinical variables of age, gender, race, ethnicity, molecular subtype and resection were performed using the coxph function of survival (v 3.8–3) and results plotted with forestplot (v 3.1.6). A nomogram of 1–5 year OS was constructed with psm(dist = "lognormal") in rms (v8.0–0) and drawn in regplot (v 1.1). Bootstrap 1000 times was used to generate 1/2/3/4/5 year calibration curves.

Potential therapeutic drugs based on signature

Samples were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on median risk score by Stats (v 4.4.3). Spearman correlation coefficient and Wilcoxon rank sum test were calculated for each drug from ≥ 30 valid samples and Benjamini–Hochberg method was used to correct for multiple comparisons. Scatter plots of correlation coefficient vs-log10 (p value) were generated in Ggplot2 (v 3.5.2) and grouped box plots were produced for the top 4 drugs.

Results

MB molecular subtypes were distinguished by nucleotide metabolism genes

Standardized expression profiles for 1804 candidate nucleotide metabolism genes were extracted from training and validation sets. The UMAP graph constructed from these expression profiles showed WNT and SHH samples to be in two discrete clusters while group 3 and group 4 were closer but still with distinct distributions. A few unclassified samples were scattered between group 3 and group 4 (Figs. 1A, 2A). The same gene matrix was hierarchically clustered by Euclidean distance and a heat map was drawn. Four highly consistent clusters representing the four subtypes could be seen on the global transcriptional map and this distribution was verified in the training and validation sets (Figs. 1B, 2B). The molecular heterogeneity of MB was thus represented by genes of the nucleotide metabolism pathway.

Cross-validation screening of nucleotide metabolism genes associated with MB prognosis

Univariate Cox analysis was performed on 1804 candidate genes in the training and validation sets and 118 (validation set) and 76 (training set) risk and 41 (validation set) and 20 (training set) protective genes were identified (FDR < 0.05). The intersection was obtained by grouping according to β > 0 and β < 0. 40 genes indicating poor prognosis and 11 genes indicating favorable prognosis were identified, giving a total of 51 prognostic genes (Figs. 3A, B). The 51 genes showed a significant positive correlation between the β coefficients of the two cohorts, verifying the consistency of the effect direction (Fig. 3C). The PPI network showed a high-density interaction core among the 51 proteins (Fig. 4A). GO/KEGG enrichment analyses suggested gene involvement in tRNA aminoacylation, GMP biosynthesis, integrated stress response and amino acid metabolism. KEGG enrichment was confined to the metabolic axes of "Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis" and "Biosynthesis of amino acids", emphasizing the coupling of protein and nucleic acid metabolism in MB progression (Fig. 4B).

Risk score calculation correlates with MB prognosis

The 51 genes were input into LASSO-Cox tenfold cross validation and 17 non-zero coefficient genes were identified to give the final risk signature (Table 3). In the training set, Kaplan–Meier curves show high-risk patients to have lower survival and worse prognosis than low-risk patients (HR = 16.78 (95% CI 5.05–55.80, p = 1.8 × 10⁻9), Fig. 5A). Risk curve and survival scatter plots show a density of death events for high-score patients (Fig. 5B). The 17 genes were expressed in gradient differences in high- and low-risk samples and were not affected by subtype confounding, such as WNT/SHH (Fig. 5C). PCA showed the signature to completely distinguish between high- and low-risk groups in the principal component space (Fig. 5D). In the validation set, Kaplan–Meier curves showed lower probability of survival and worse prognosis in high- compared with low-risk patients (HR = 5.38 (95%CI 3.28–8.84, p = 9.92 × 10⁻14)), verifying model robustness (Fig. 6A). Risk curve and survival scatter plot showed high density of death events among high-score patients (Fig. 6B). The 17 genes were expressed in gradient differences in high- and low-risk samples and were not affected by subtype confounding, such as WNT/SHH (Fig. 6C). PCA showed the signature to completely distinguish between high- and low-risk groups in the principal component space (Fig. 6D).

The risk score was an independent marker of poor prognosis (Fig. 7A, B). Drug sensitivity prediction analysis based on risk score

Recent advances have been made in immunotherapy but chemotherapy remains a postoperative treatment choice. To identify potential anti-MB therapeutic agents, we leveraged data from two independent public drug sensitivity resources: the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC; https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) and the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP; https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp/). Both databases primarily contain drug sensitivity profiles derived from cancer cell lines. Through this analysis, MEK inhibitors, Mirdametinib (PD0325901), Selumetinib and Trametinib, and ERK inhibitor, SCH772984_1564, were found to be suitable for patients with high risk scores (Fig. 8). PLK1 inhibitor, BI-2536_1086, IGF1R/IR inhibitor, BMS-754807_2171, ROCK inhibitor, GSK269962A_1192, mTORC1/2 inhibitor, OSI-027_1594, MET/multi-target inhibitor, tivantinib, demethylase, JMJD3/KDM6B, UTX/KDM6A dual inhibitor, GSK-J4, Aurora inhibitor, VX-680, and BRD-K01739880 may be more effective for patients with low risk scores (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Disordered synthesis and degradation of nucleic acids is a common feature of cancer cells and excessive proliferation, resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, immune escape, invasion and metastasis all depend on enhanced nucleotide metabolism (Seashore-Ludlow et al. 2015). Four molecular subtypes could be distinguished in the MB training and validation sets by gene expression profile analysis in the current work. UMAP and hierarchical clustering analysis showed WNT and SHH subtypes to form independent clusters. Groups 3 and 4 partially overlapped but could still be distinguished. Thus, nucleotide metabolism reprogramming was highly consistent with MB molecular typing, verifying subtype heterogeneity.

Abnormal nucleotide metabolism affects tumor-microenvironment interaction, metabolic reprogramming and immune responses (Wu et al. 2025). Nucleotide metabolism pathways were found to impact patient prognosis through tRNA aminoacylation, GMP biosynthesis and integrated stress response. Dysregulation of these pathways is related to tumor aggression, especially in Group 3 and SHH subtypes. For example, impaired tRNA aminoacylation may lead to disordered protein synthesis, exacerbating cellular stress and genomic instability and abnormal GMP biosynthesis may provide raw materials for rapid proliferation of MYC-amplified tumors (Li et al. 2024; Girard et al. 2025). Synergism between nucleotide metabolism and oncogenic signals, such as MYC/E2F, explains the poor prognosis of high-risk patients (Shiode et al. 2025) In addition, PPI network analysis showed a dense interaction network among prognostic tRNA aminoacylation and amino acid biosynthesis genes, highlighting KARS1 and MYC as unfavorable hub genes. Metabolic transcriptional regulation may also show synergism, perhaps exposing intervention targets.

51 prognostic genes were identified. DNA-activated protein kinase catalytic subunit peptide (Protein Kinase, DNA-Activated, Catalytic Polypeptide, PRKDC), recombinant human RNA polymerase I polypeptide C (Recombinant Human Polymerase (RNA) I (DNA directed) Polypeptide C, POLR1C) and adenylosuccinate lyase (ADSL) were found to be dysregulated in MB. This suite of genes affected prognosis by promoting tumor proliferation, survival and treatment resistance. For example, DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK), encoded by PRKDC, forms a molecular channel to maintain genome stability during DNA repair (Soto-Palma et al. 2022). Abnormal PRKDC expression affects mTORC2 signaling, leading to downstream HIF-2α gene activation and tumor cell proliferation, invasion and metastasis (Pang et al. 2025). POLR1C mutations may cause leukodystrophy in children with motor development delay, gait abnormality and intellectual disability arising from an impact on the central nervous system. However, any association with MB remains to be elucidated (Liu et al. 2024). ADSL is an enzyme in the purine synthesis pathway, known to be upregulated in many cancers and linked to breast and prostate cancers (Liao et al. 2021). The fumaric acid product of ADSL activity has more recently been associated with the promotion of GBM growth (Yin et al. 2024). The 51 genes were cross-validated by LASSO-Cox tenfold and 17 non-zero coefficient genes were used to construct a risk signature and divide patients into high- and low-risk groups, independently of other clinical factors. Stable predictive efficacy was shown in the training (HR = 16.78) and validation (HR = 5.38) sets, indicating suitability for individualized risk assessment. The signature was integrated through a nomogram for quantitative survival prediction in clinical decision-making. However, this model also has its limitations, such as not including all clinical variables, including transition status, in the multivariate Cox analysis evaluating signature independence. The main reason is the limited availability of data in the public database used in the validation set of this study. Some clinical variables have a high missing rate (over 30% in the entire merged queue), and excluding incomplete data samples to retain these variables will greatly reduce the effective sample size, thereby damaging the statistical ability and stability of the model. We recognize that the lack of these factors may potentially bias the apparent independence of the model. For example, transition status and MYC amplification are well-documented adverse prognostic factors in MB; If the high-risk population defined by our features disproportionately includes patients with metastasis or MYC amplification (due to unmeasured confounding factors), the observed association between risk features and survival rates may be overestimated, and the true independence of features from these clinical variables may be masked. Similarly, changes in treatment regimens (such as dose intensive chemotherapy or radiation dose adjustments) may affect survival outcomes, and unknown differences in treatment between different risk groups may further confound the results of the model. Despite current limitations, the consistent performance of this feature in both training and validation sets still supports its potential as a supplementary prognostic tool.

Our drug sensitivity analysis based on cancer cell line-derived datasets (GDSC and CTRP) identified differential drug sensitivity patterns between risk groups: high-risk patients may be sensitive to MEK/ERK inhibitors, while low-risk patients may be sensitive to PLK1/mTOR inhibitors. Studies have shown that MEK/ERK inhibitors (such as Trametinib) and mTOR inhibitors (such as OSI-027) can exert their effects by indirectly regulating the transcriptional process of nucleotide metabolism. The MEK/ERK signaling pathway can activate transcription factors such as c-Myc and E2F, which are upstream regulatory molecules of key enzymes in purine/pyrimidine synthesis (such as thymidylate synthase). Inhibition of their activity can downregulate the transcription levels of the above enzymes, thereby inhibiting nucleotide synthesis in MB cells (Levy et al. 2025). mTOR affects ribosomal RNA transcription by regulating S6K1 signals, indirectly participating in the regulation of translation efficiency of genes related to nucleotide metabolism (Guo et al. 2024). Currently, trametinib has shown activity in preclinical studies of SHH subtype MB. Trametinib can inhibit cell proliferation in MB cell lines with abnormally activated SHH pathway and MEK mutation (such as DAOY cells), and can reduce tumor volume by 40%-60% in patient-derived xenograft models of SHH subtype MB (Borlase et al. 2023) However, mTOR inhibitors have been less studied in Group 3 subtype MB. Only early cell experiments have shown that they have a certain inhibitory effect on Group 3 MB cells with MYC amplification, but this has not yet been verified in in vivo models (Jonchere et al. 2023). We acknowledge that this drug prediction approach has inherent limitations. First, the association between patient risk groups and predicted drug sensitivity is indirect, as it is inferred from cell line data rather than direct patient drug response outcomes. Second, cancer cell line models may not fully recapitulate the biological complexity of primary MB tumors (e.g., tumor microenvironment, genetic heterogeneity, and stromal cell interactions). Future studies should validate these predicted drug responses using MB patient-derived models to better bridge the gap between cell line data and clinical applicability.

Nucleotide metabolism has been underexplored in medulloblastoma and the current study aimed to make up for this oversight. Massive data sets were scanned by cutting-edge bioinformatics technology and roles of nucleotide metabolism in MB heterogeneity and prognosis were assessed. The 17-gene signature is a practical tool for risk stratification and treatment selection, bridging basic research and clinical application to inform a molecular understanding of MB and expose potential treatment strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically investigated the role of nucleotide metabolism-related genes in medulloblastoma and constructed a prognostic risk signature to optimize clinical risk stratification and identify potential therapeutic targets. Through bioinformatics analysis of samples from two cohorts, we first identified 51 nucleotide metabolism-related genes with prognostic significance. These genes are mainly involved in key biological processes such as tRNA aminoacylation, GMP biosynthesis, and integrated stress response—pathways closely associated with MB cell proliferation, genomic stability, and treatment resistance. Among these genes, PRKDC, POLR1C, and ADSL were found to be dysregulated in MB; their abnormal expression is linked to aggressive tumor behavior and poor patient prognosis, highlighting their potential as novel prognostic biomarkers. Using tenfold LASSO-Cox regression cross-validation, we further screened 17 genes with non-zero coefficients to establish the risk signature. This signature was also integrated into a nomogram, which enables quantitative prediction of patient survival and provides a practical tool for individualized risk assessment in clinical practice. Finally, drug sensitivity analysis based on the GDSC and CTRP databases revealed distinct therapeutic sensitivities among different risk groups. Despite the contributions of this study, several limitations remain. First, the training set samples were derived from single-center data, while the validation set relied on public data, which may introduce bias. Second, there is a lack of in vitro and in vivo experiments to verify the causal relationships between prognostic genes, drug targets, and MB progression. Third, this study lacks a larger-scale clinical cohort to validate the utility of the signature in guiding treatment decisions. In conclusion, our findings emphasize the critical role of nucleotide metabolism-related genes in MB prognosis and provide a clinically applicable risk signature as well as subtype-specific therapeutic recommendations. Future work will focus on validating the signature in multi-center prospective cohorts, verifying drug efficacy using MB patient-derived models (PDXs/organoids), and exploring the molecular mechanisms by which prognostic genes regulate MB drug sensitivity—ultimately translating these findings into improved clinical management of MB patients.
