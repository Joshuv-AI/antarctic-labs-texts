Medical students’ attitudes, usage patterns, and associated factors with DeepSeek adoption in education: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Wei Liu, Lei-Lei Cao, Xiao-Yu Chen, Hai-Feng Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the growing integration of generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) into healthcare, the DeepSeek large language model has emerged as a versatile tool for medical students, offering adaptive learning solutions tailored to various medical scenarios. However, the adoption of AI in medicine also raises ethical concerns that require warrant consideration. This study aimed to investigate medical students’ usage patterns, attitudes, and influencing factors related to DeepSeek.MethodsIn July 2025, a cross-sectional survey was conducted using random sampling among 1000 medical students from institutions affiliated with Anhui Medical University. Based on previous AI attitude scales, a validated self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data on students’ attitudes, DeepSeek usage patterns.ResultsAmong the 937 valid responses (response rate: 93.7%), 874 participants were aware of DeepSeek prior to the survey and 765 had used it. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the questionnaire was 0.83. 42.0% of students doubted the accuracy of information provided by DeepSeek, 48.6% were apprehensive about potential plagiarism accusations, and 43.0% worried about over-reliance on AI. Additionally, 46.6% found DeepSeek interesting and appealing, with 37.7% expressing enthusiasm for learning new AI technologies. Several factors were significantly associated with usage experience, including being female [β = 6.1(1.5–10.7), (p = 0.031)], holding a Master’s degree [β = 10.5(2.1–18.1), (p = 0.044)], and engaging in clinical [β = 7.4(1.2–13.6), (p = 0.021)] or basic research [β = 9.2(2.9–15.4), (p = 0.012)]. Regarding attitudes, significant predictors included a Master’s degree [β = -2.9(-6.2 to-0.5), (p = 0.011)], having a stomatology background [β = 8.0(2.3–13.7), (p = 0.035)], engaging in clinical [β = -2.6(-5.0 to -0.1), (p = 0.036)] or basic [β = -2.7(-5.2 to -0.3), (p = 0.042)] research.ConclusionsAmong the medical students surveyed in this study, 94.8% reported that they would be willing to use and recommend DeepSeek, but they maintain a cautious attitude toward its privacy, reliability, and dependency. Despite these concerns, 90.2% of participants said they would be willing to use DeepSeek to help them complete their tasks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09918-w.Keywords: Medical students, DeepSeek, Medical education, Usage behavior, Attitude study

Introduction

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) has driven significant advancements across healthcare. Large Language Models (LLMs) such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, and Microsoft’s Copilot demonstrate considerable potential in clinical decision support, medical education, and research [1]. These technological breakthroughs are reshaping medical education models. By leveraging natural language interaction and specialized knowledge output capabilities, LLMs have become essential tools for medical students in both learning and research [2]. Recent evaluations further demonstrate that LLMs, including DeepSeek, achieve performance levels comparable to peer AI systems across healthcare education and quality-management domains, supporting their role as supplementary knowledge and decision-support tools in professional training contexts [3]. Studies have shown that LLMs perform exceptionally well in standardized medical examinations. Their competence covers not only general medical knowledge but also various medical specialties, and their performance in multidisciplinary professional examinations is comparable to that of medical practitioners [4].

As an innovative technological force, AI is exerting a profound impact on higher education, demonstrating multidimensional value in supporting student development. It not only provides students with efficient tools for idea exploration and personalized assessment support for exam preparation but also enhances their technological literacy through interactive engagement [5, 6]. Particularly in practice-oriented fields like nursing, AI fosters critical thinking and innovative potential through highly realistic virtual scenarios and immersive simulation training systems. As technology evolves, these intelligent platforms allow students to repeatedly refine their clinical skills and complex decision-making abilities, delivering learning experiences that combine professional depth with real-world challenges [7].

However, the widespread adoption of generative AI also introduces a series of urgent challenges. A primary concern is the risk of students becoming overly reliant on the technology, which may impede the development of their independent learning abilities and critical thinking [8]. Additional issues include potential biases, cognitive distortions, and accuracy risks in AI-generated content, compounded by academic integrity crises such as the proliferation of plagiarism, as well as data security and ethical dilemmas related to the collection and use of students’ personal health information [9]. How students understand and engage with AI technology will not only impact their current learning adaptation but also shape future human-machine collaboration models in clinical practice [10]. Therefore, accurately assessing medical students’ cognitive attitude and experiences with AI is essential for ensuring both educational quality and professional ethics.

DeepSeek is an AI platform based on deep learning and natural language processing (NLP) technologies. Utilizing a highly efficient Mixture of Expert (MoE) architecture and multimodal data fusion capabilities, along with a significant reduction in training costs (over 90% lower than comparable models), DeepSeek achieves performance on par with models such as OpenAI’s GPT−4o-mini [11]. It can rapidly and accurately extract valuable insights from large-scale datasets, delivering intelligent information retrieval and analytical solutions. These advanced capabilities open up new possibilities in healthcare, providing effective support for both physicians and patients [12]. In parallel, the rapid emergence of open-source generative AI models such as DeepSeek is reshaping the global technological landscape by accelerating innovation diffusion, expanding research accessibility, and redefining how advanced computational tools are integrated into academic and clinical knowledge environments [13]. Studies suggest that DeepSeek may play an important role in medical education assessment, particularly in reducing workload through initial screening or providing immediate formative feedback [14, 15].

Existing research shows significant regional variations in students’ knowledge, attitudes, and usage of AI [16–18]. However, there remains a lack of insights into Chinese medical students’ cognitive attitude and experiences specifically regarding DeepSeek. In this context, the present study seeks to investigate the views, cognition, and usage patterns of Chinese medical students toward DeepSeek. A deeper understanding of their attitudes and concerns will assist medical schools in developing targeted AI education strategies and effectively integrating platforms like DeepSeek into medical education and practice.

To achieve this, we examined differences in attitudes and usage patterns toward DeepSeek among students at Anhui Medical University based on various demographic characteristics, and employed regression analysis to identify predictors of these attitudes and usage patterns.

Methods

Research design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in July 2025 among medical students across different grades and majors at Anhui Medical University, China, using a stratified cluster random sampling method with a standardized study procedure. The target population consisted of full-time students enrolled in all medical-related majors and academic years at the university. To ensure balanced and random sample coverage, the overall population was first stratified by academic level and major category. Classes were then randomly selected as the primary sampling unit using a random number table, and all students in the selected classes were invited to participate. However, relatively few master’s and doctoral students participated, as they do not attend classes in a centralized manner.

Participants were excluded if they submitted questionnaires with completely missing core information, provided invalid responses with logical errors or regular answering patterns, or failed to complete informed consent confirmation. A total of 1000 questionnaires were distributed; however, 10 participants provided no information in the questionnaire. Additionally, 53 participants who had never heard of DeepSeek were excluded from the study. Finally, 937 valid questionnaires were collected, yielding a response rate of 93.7%.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the biomedical ethics committee of Anhui Medical University (ethical approval no.: 81251062). All questionnaire data were collected in a fully anonymous manner; no personally identifiable information was collected throughout the survey, thereby eliminating the risk of participant identification. All collected data were encrypted and stored on a secure device dedicated to the research team, with access restricted to authorized core research members. The data were used exclusively for statistical analysis in this study and will not be disclosed, shared, or transferred to any third party.

According to calculations using the Raosoft® online sample size calculator, the recommended minimum sample size was 377 students, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 50% response rate. Therefore, the actual number of enrolled participants meets the minimum requirements to ensure sufficient statistical power and sample representativeness.

Questionnaire investigation

The TAME-ChatGPT tool has proven to be a reliable, effective and practical instrument for assessing university students’ attitudes towards ChatGPT. As DeepSeek is also a large language model, this study adopted the scale to explore medical students’ attitudes and usage experiences with DeepSeek [19, 20].

The questionnaire consisted of two sections. The first section gathered demographic information, including age group, gender, educational level, major, and research field (clinical research, basic research, science education, undetermined, or others). The second section assessed participants’ experiences with and attitude toward DeepSeek. Two screening questions were included: “Had you heard of DeepSeek before this research?” Responding “No” ended the survey. Responding “Yes” led to the next question: “Had you used DeepSeek before this research?” Responding “No” directed participants to the cognition and attitude scale (13 items), while responding “Yes” directed them to the usage patterns scale (25 items). A 5-point Likert scale was employed, ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to assess the internal consistency of items related to usage experience and perception, yielding a value of 0.83, indicating good reliability. For the 13-item Cognition and Attitude Scale, the theoretical total score ranged from a minimum of 13 (all items scored as 1) to a maximum of 65 (all items scored as 5). For the 25-item Usage Pattern and Experience Scale, the theoretical total score ranged from a minimum of 25 (all items scored as 1) to a maximum of 125 (all items scored as 5). The total score of each scale was calculated as the sum of scores for all completed items, and the mean item score was calculated as the total score divided by the number of corresponding items. A higher total score or mean item score indicated a higher level of positive recognition, acceptance, and favorable evaluation of DeepSeek’s application in medical education scenarios. Key items are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

To ensure the validity and reliability of the content, the questionnaire was reviewed by three experts in relevant fields. Based on their feedback, minor adjustments were made to improve the relevance and wording of certain sections. The questionnaire covers academic engagement, usage of DeepSeek, and attitudes toward its application in the medical field. Confirmatory Factor Analysis was employed to assess the structural validity of the questionnaire. The fit indices improved as follows χ2/df = 288.28/60 = 4.80, p < 0.001, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.032, CFI = 0.965, and TLI = 0.954. Subsequently, a pilot test was conducted with 30 medical students, during which completion times and any encountered issues were recorded to assess comprehensibility and usability, further refining the questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Survey data were entered into a database created using Epidata 3.1. During data entry, predefined logical checks were applied to validate variable ranges, and a dual-entry procedure was used to ensure accuracy. After the database was established, preliminary data analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics, their experience with DeepSeek, and their cognitive attitudes toward the platform. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Univariate analyses were conducted to assess data normality and the presence of outliers. Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal Wallis’s H tests were used to compare mean scores of usage experience and perception across different demographic groups. All categorical independent variables were converted into dummy variables for regression analysis, with the most representative category set as the reference group. Specifically, gender was coded as a binary dummy variable (reference group: male; female = 1). Educational level was coded as (reference group: undergraduate; master’s = 1, doctoral = 2). Major was coded as (reference group: clinical research; stomatology = 1, public health = 2, traditional chinese medicine = 3, others = 4). Research areas were coded as (reference group: clinical research; basic research = 1, science education = 2, undetermined = 3, others = 4).

Multiple linear regression (MLR) models were employed to identify factors associated with DeepSeek usage experience and perception, as both outcome variables were continuous in nature. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, which aimed to examine the independent associations between multiple independent variables and continuous outcomes, multiple linear regression is the most classic and widely accepted statistical method for this research scenario.

First, univariate linear regression analyses were performed separately for each candidate independent variable to estimate crude associations with the outcome variables, thereby avoiding model overfitting. Second, during the multivariate model construction phase, all variables that passed the pre-screening were simultaneously entered into the final model using the enter method, rather than the stepwise regression method. The enter method allows for the complete retention of all potentially important variables based on the theoretical framework, avoiding the variable selection bias and result instability that may be introduced by stepwise regression. For regression analyses, unstandardized regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for β, and p-values were reported. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 937 participants were included in this study. Their demographic and academic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among them, 349 (37.2%) were male and 588 (62.8%) were female. Regarding age distribution, 51.9% of participants were under 20 years old, while 47.5% were between 20 and 29 years old. The majority (91.5%) were undergraduate students. In terms of academic focus, 42.0% majored in public health, 16.4% in clinical medicine, and 16.0% in basic research. Regarding familiarity with DeepSeek, 874 (93.3%) reported having heard of DeepSeek, and 765 (81.6%) had used it prior to the survey. The mean score for self-reported DeepSeek usage experience was 86.6 ± 10.4, and the mean score for awareness of DeepSeek was 42.8 ± 5.4.

DeepSeek user experience

User experience with DeepSeek was assessed by calculating the scores of 25 items. A total of 394 participants (42.0%) expressed concerns about the reliability of information provided by DeepSeek, while 455 participants (48.6%) worried that its use might lead to accusations of plagiarism. On the other hand, 509 participants (54.3%) believed that DeepSeek helped save time in information retrieval, and 431 (46.0%) felt it reduced the time and effort required for assignments. Additionally, 320 participants (34.2%) reported frequently using DeepSeek to complete homework.

Cognition and attitude toward DeepSeek

Cognition and attitudes toward DeepSeek were derived by calculating scores across 13 items. A total of 433 participants (46.2%) expressed concern that overreliance on DeepSeek might diminish critical thinking skills, and 403 (43.0%) feared excessive dependence on AI technology in general. Meanwhile, 395 participants (42.2%) believed that DeepSeek benefits academic research, and 437 (46.6%) found it appealing and engaging.

Comparative analysis of DeepSeek usage experience and attitude across demographic groups

As shown in Table 2, the Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis H test revealed statistically significant difference in usage experience scores across gender, educational level, and research field. Specifically, participants with an undetermined research field exhibited significantly lower usage experience scores than those in clinical research and basic research. Undergraduate students reported lower usage experience scores than postgraduate students, while female participants scored higher than male participants.

Discussion

This study investigated medical students’ acceptance, usage experience, and cognitive attitudes toward DeepSeek against the backdrop of rapidly advancing AI. The findings revealed that the majority of medical students (93.3%) were aware of DeepSeek, with 81.6% having used it. Usage experience varied across gender, educational level, and research field, with female students, master’s degree candidates, and those engaged in clinical or basic research reporting more favorable experiences. Similarly, cognitive attitude toward DeepSeek was influenced by educational level, specialty, and research field; master’s degree candidates, stomatology students, and those involved in clinical or basic research held more positive attitudes toward the platform.

The high usage rate of DeepSeek among medical students fundamentally stems from its precise alignment with the technical demands of medical education [21]. Its open-source, free-to-use model lowers the economic barrier to access, while the efficient computing enabled by its MoE architecture meets the frequent need for information retrieval and data processing in medical studies [22]. Consistent with our findings, a study of 305 dental students in Palestine revealed that 77% had basic knowledge of AI and 71.5% understood its applications in their field [23]. Another study on Chinese medical students’ awareness of ChatGPT revealed that 62.9% of medical students had used it in medical research [24]. This reporting rate is slightly lower than that in our study, potentially because DeepSeek promotes continuous learning by leveraging publicly available open-source datasets, thereby enhancing adaptability to evolving medical knowledge and scientific reasoning [1, 25].

Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of students expressed concerns about using DeepSeek. Specifically, 42.0% of participants questioned the reliability of information it provides, 48.6% feared being accused of plagiarism, 46.2% worried that overreliance might erode critical thinking skills, and 43.0% were apprehensive about becoming overly dependent on AI technology. Among the participants, some expressed reservations about the reliability of artificial intelligence technology. The most prominent concern centered on the potential risks of applying AI in clinical diagnosis and decision-making, as well as in safeguarding the originality of academic research. To date, a number of international studies have documented similar concerns, reflecting widespread apprehension across society regarding the opacity of AI algorithms and the potential for their misuse or erroneous application [26, 27]. In China, increasingly stringent academic misconduct regulations have been rolled out in recent years, where AI-assisted plagiarism is explicitly recognized as a form of academic misconduct and can lead to severe penalties including degree revocation. These findings align with previous research. A Saudi study revealed participants expressed concerns about ChatGPT regarding plagiarism, academic integrity, and diminished critical thinking skills [28]. Research by Hammad et al. and Salvagno et al. similarly indicated participants viewed plagiarism as the primary ethical risk in AI-assisted academic work, and that using AI tools could undermine human critical thinking and originality [29, 30].

At the same time, participants recognized several benefits of DeepSeek. Over half (54.3%) of participants believed it saved time in information retrieval, 46.0% felt it reduced effort required for coursework, 42.2% regarded it as beneficial for academic research, and 46.6% found DeepSeek appealing and interesting. The capacity of AI to summarize research and generate content rapidly was particularly valued. These findings align with studies by Abujaber et al. and Naamati-Schneider et al., which highlight the role of AI tools in enhancing academic productivity [31, 32]. They are also consistent with a Chinese study on nursing undergraduates, in which participants viewed AI as essential for healthcare advancement and capable of improving the quality and efficiency of nursing practice [33]. The heavy academic workload of Chinese medical students drives a strong demand for AI tools.

Medical students’ ambivalent attitude of “acknowledging efficiency while worrying about risks” reflects the unique demands of their discipline [21, 34]. Medicine requires extreme precision, which conflicts with the known “hallucination” issues in AI-generated content and the field’s low tolerance for error [35]. This tension often leads to a pattern of “use without full trust.” Furthermore, concerns about academic integrity and privacy protection arise from the stringent ethical standards governing medical practice. This contradiction is not unique to DeepSeek but represents a common challenge for all medical AI tools, requiring solutions through both technological optimization and institutional regulation [36–38]. A narrative review on the ethical aspects of implementing generative AI in medical education further highlights the need for structured governance frameworks, transparent accountability mechanisms, and formal ethical guidance to safeguard academic integrity, learner autonomy, and data protection [39].

Analysis on factors influencing experience and perception revealed that female students, master’s degree candidates, and those engaged in clinical or basic research reported better user experience. Meanwhile, master’s degree candidates, stomatology majors, and those involved in clinical or basic research demonstrated heightened cognitive engagement with DeepSeek. In the present study, female students constituted a larger proportion of the sample, and female gender was significantly and positively associated with both usage experience and cognitive attitudes toward DeepSeek, which may be related to their higher sense of responsibility or engagement [23, 40]. In general, the depth of academic literacy and proficiency with digital tools are key predictors of AI adoption in healthcare education [41–43]. Consistent with this perspective, educational research has demonstrated that students with stronger digital competencies and prior exposure to intelligent learning technologies exhibit higher acceptance levels, deeper engagement, and more effective academic utilization of AI-supported educational platforms [44]. These findings indicate that the greater students’ academic exposure and familiarity with AI tools, the stronger their confidence and practical ability in using AI [41]. Senior students generally exhibit a higher level of understanding and acceptance of AI technologies in academic settings [42]. Meanwhile, students with higher academic attainment and greater exposure to digital tools are more likely to adopt and apply AI in medical education, and these two factors also serve as key predictors for the adoption of AI in this field [43]. Engagement with AI also varies significantly by academic level: senior students tend to use more advanced AI functionalities, likely because their academic tasks grow more complex and demand higher digital literacy, reflecting an adaptive selection of technological tools as specialization deepens [45].

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first study in China to investigate medical students’ experiences and cognition regarding the use of DeepSeek. By examining how factors such as different specialties and educational levels influence perceptions of DeepSeek, it aims to better integrate AI into medical education. However, this study has the following limitations. First, this study employed a cross-sectional design, lacking verification from longitudinal or behavioral outcomes. As self-reported questionnaires were used, the results may be affected by reporting bias and recall bias. Second, participants were recruited from a single university with a high proportion of undergraduates, resulting in a non-representative sample and limiting the generalizability of the findings. Third, measurement bias may have been introduced by directly adopting tools validated against ChatGPT without full re-validation for DeepSeek. Fourth, potential residual confounding may exist due to prior AI training or institutional exposure. Future research should employ prospective designs to further explore medical students’ experiences and cognitive attitude toward DeepSeek and extend the investigation to include multiple medical schools.

Regarding the high R2, several factors should be noted. First, all five independent variables were selected on the basis of well-established psychosocial theories and prior empirical research on AI acceptance among medical students. These variables represent theoretically core predictive factors that are strongly and consistently associated with students’ AI usage behavior, ensuring strong intrinsic predictive validity. Second, the model was estimated using a large sample size of 937 participants. This sufficiently large sample size helps reduce random noise and sampling bias, stabilizes model estimation, and consequently improves the model explanatory degree. Third, the dependent variable in this study is a composite score derived from multiple validated scale items, with demonstrated good internal consistency and test-retest stability. Compared to single-item indicators, such aggregated multi-item measures can substantially increase model interpretability and lead to a higher and more reliable R².

Conclusions

This cross-sectional survey analyzed the usage status, experience, and perceptions of DeepSeek among 937 medical students in a medical education setting based on self-reported data. The results showed that while most participating medical students positively recognized DeepSeek for its open-source and high-efficiency features, they also held widespread concerns about the tool’s information accuracy, academic ethical compliance, and privacy security. Between-group differences in usage experience and perceptions were observed across gender, specialty, educational level, and research field. Based on these findings, targeted AI literacy cultivation, scenario-based optimization of the tool, and clear application specifications could address the core concerns of current medical students regarding generative AI tools, and provide a reference for its standardized application in medical education.
