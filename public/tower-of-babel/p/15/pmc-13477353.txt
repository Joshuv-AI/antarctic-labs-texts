Associations of Physical Activity With Mortality Across Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic Syndrome Stages: Evidence From NHANES 1999 to 2018
Authors: Yangjie Xiao, Cheng Cheng, Le Zhang, Jinling Hu, Lei Wang, Cong Shang, Song Gao, Bing Ma, Hao Sun, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome integrates cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic dysfunction and is associated with high mortality. Stage‐stratified evidence linking physical activity (PA) to mortality remains limited.MethodsWe analyzed 19 930 adults with CKM stages 0 to 4 from the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, 1999–2018), with mortality follow‐up through 2019. PA was assessed by duration (minute/week) and volume (metabolic equivalent of task‐min/week) across recreational, transport, and occupational domains. Survey‐weighted Cox models with restricted cubic splines characterized dose–response associations, defined restricted cubic spline‐derived reference ranges, and tested interactions.ResultsDuring follow‐up, 2759 deaths occurred (997 CKM related). Mortality increased sharply with CKM stage (all cause: 2.7% in CKM 0–1, 7.0% in CKM 2, and 33.5%–39.2% in CKM 3–4; CKM‐related: <2% in CKM 0–2 and 14.2%–15.5% in CKM 3–4). Higher PA was associated with lower mortality across stages, with the largest absolute differences in CKM 3–4. Associations were nonlinear and L‐shaped. In CKM 3–4, total PA near the reference range (≈500 min/week or ≈2000 metabolic equivalent of task‐min/week) was associated with lower all‐cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR], 0.745–0.700) and CKM‐related mortality (HR, 0.739–0.690), whereas recreational PA (≈250 min/week or ≈1000 metabolic equivalent of task‐min/week) showed the strongest inverse associations (all‐cause HR, 0.740–0.702; CKM‐related HR, 0.568–0.550). Occupational PA displayed a U‐/J‐shaped pattern. Interactions were strongest for renal dysfunction, particularly proteinuria.ConclusionsModest, attainable PA—especially recreational activity—was associated with lower all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality, with the greatest differences in CKM stages 3 to 4.Keywords: cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic syndrome, mortality, NHANES, nonlinear dose–response, physical activitySubject Categories: Lifestyle, Exercise

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

In a nationally representative cohort spanning cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic stages 0 to 4, we identified stage‐stratified, nonlinear dose–response relationships between physical activity (duration and volume) and both all‐cause and cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic‐related mortality, with data‐driven inflection‐point reference ranges (for descriptive stratification, not exercise prescriptions) around ~500 min/week or ~2000 metabolic equivalent of task‐min/week for total physical activity and ~250 min/week or ~1000 metabolic equivalent of task‐min/week for recreational physical activity, and a U‐/J‐shaped pattern for occupational physical activity (most evident in cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic 3).

Cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) syndrome has recently been proposed as a comprehensive framework that captures the intertwined pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes. 1 , 2 These conditions frequently coexist in clinical practice, and their overlap substantially amplifies the risk of adverse outcomes. 3 Recent epidemiological data show that CKM prevalence is steadily rising; nearly 90% of adults meet the criteria for stage 1 or above, and about 15% progress to advanced stages associated with multiorgan damage and increased mortality. 3 , 4 Large cohort studies consistently demonstrate a steep risk gradient across CKM stages: compared with stage 0, individuals with stages 3 to 4 CKM exhibit a 2‐ to 3‐fold higher risk of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, as well as a markedly higher incidence of kidney failure and cardiometabolic events. 5 , 6 , 7 This evidence positions CKM as more than an aggregation of risk factors; it represents a progressive multimorbidity construct with major prognostic implications for global health.

Physical activity (PA) is one of the most effective lifestyle exposures, with extensive evidence supporting its role in improving long‐term outcomes across cardiovascular, metabolic, and renal disorders. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 In the general population, sufficient PA is associated with 20% to 30% lower all‐cause mortality and up to 35% lower cardiovascular mortality, with clear dose–response associations. 8 Disease‐specific studies reinforce these benefits: in type 2 diabetes, longer PA duration is associated with 19% to 31% lower mortality; 9 in hypertension, moderate‐intensity PA corresponds to mortality risks comparable to or below those of inactive individuals with normal blood pressure; 10 and in CKD, higher PA levels are associated with improved vascular function and delayed progression to end‐stage kidney disease. 11 , 12 Together, these findings highlight PA as a cornerstone exposure associated with lower premature mortality across diverse high‐risk populations.

Despite this evidence, most studies have examined PA in isolation with respect to a single disease entity—CVD, diabetes, or CKD—without addressing their frequent coexistence. 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 , 13 In practice, patients rarely present with a single risk factor; instead, combinations of metabolic dysfunction, cardiovascular impairment, and renal decline converge, contributing to the poor outcomes that CKM seeks to capture. 2 , 5 Yet the relationship between PA and prognosis has not been systematically evaluated within the CKM framework. Whether PA–mortality associations differ across CKM stages, and what duration and volume levels are associated with lower risk, remains unclear. A recent study showed that moderate PA was associated with a lower risk of developing early‐stage CKM, whereas excessive activity offered diminishing benefits. 14 However, that work focused on incident CKM rather than prognosis; to date, CKM‐oriented PA studies have largely emphasized CKM development, progression, or stage severity, 14 , 15 , 16 with limited quantification of PA in relation to stage‐stratified mortality.

In prognostic research on CKM, PA has largely been embedded within composite health scores, obscuring its specific contribution. The American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 incorporates PA as 1 of 8 core metrics of cardiovascular health. Higher Life's Essential 8scores are associated with substantially lower all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, with behavioral components such as PA making a prominent contribution. 17 , 18 Beyond Life's Essential 8, other integrative indices—including the earlier Life's Simple 7, 19 the oxidative balance score, 19 DNA‐methylation age acceleration, 20 and multidimensional frailty indices 19 —have also embedded PA as a central element. However, PA has usually been analyzed as 1 component of composite health scores, leaving its independent and domain‐specific dose–response associations with CKM outcomes unclear.

To address this gap, we analyzed data from the US NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey), a nationally representative cohort with long‐term mortality follow‐up. PA was quantified by duration and volume, and its associations with all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality were evaluated across stages 0 to 4. By characterizing nonlinear dose–response patterns and describing risk‐associated ranges across stages, our findings provide CKM‐focused evidence on the prognostic associations of PA with mortality and support stage‐informed counseling in multimorbid populations.

METHODS

All data underlying this article are publicly available from NHANES (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm). Analytic code and study materials are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

The NHANES is a nationally representative survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. It uses a stratified, multistage sampling design to assess health and nutrition in the US population. For this study, 10 NHANES cycles (1999–2018) were analyzed. Eligible participants were adults aged ≥18 years who met criteria for CKM syndrome. Participants with missing data on PA, CKM‐related clinical assessments, or mortality follow‐up were excluded. The final cohort included 19 930 individuals (see Figure S1 for selection details). The study protocols received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Definition of CKM Stages

CKM syndrome was classified into 5 stages (0–4) based on criteria from the American Heart Association and Aggarwal et al., 2 , 4 adapted to NHANES data as previously described. 21 Stage 0 included individuals with normal body weight (body mass index [BMI], 18.5–24.9 kg/m2) and normal waist circumference (<88 cm in women and <102 cm in men) who did not meet criteria for other stages. Stage 1 comprised those with overweight or obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m2), abdominal obesity (waist ≥88 cm in women or ≥102 cm in men), or prediabetes (fasting glucose 100–124 mg/dL or hemoglobin A1c 5.7%–6.4%). Stage 2 included participants with metabolic risk factors such as triglycerides ≥135 mg/dL, hypertension, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome, or those with intermediate‐to‐high risk CKD according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria. 22 Stage 3 encompassed individuals with very high‐risk CKD per Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes or elevated predicted 10‐year CVD risk (Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events equations). 23 Stage 4 included participants with self‐reported CVD (coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, heart failure, or stroke). CKD risk was determined by cross‐classifying estimated glomerular filtration rate and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes prognostic grid (low, moderate, high, and very high risk; Table S1). 22 Intermediate‐to‐high risk CKD was defined as Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes grid categories of moderate or high risk, whereas very high‐risk CKD corresponded to the very high risk category. Comprehensive CKM staging criteria are provided in Tables S1–S4. 2 , 22 , 23

Physical Activity Assessment

Self‐reported PA was collected during the examination visit using 2 instruments by survey waves: a 30‐day recall questionnaire (1999–2006) and the World Health Organization Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (2007–2018). 24 , 25 Both instruments recorded frequency, duration (minutes), and intensity across 3 domains—work/household, transport (walking/cycling), and recreational activity—though labels differed slightly before 2007 (eg, “tasks around home/yard” for work; “leisure‐time activity” for recreation).

For 1999 to 2006, respondents reported domain‐specific activity in the prior 30 days at moderate or vigorous intensity; weekly minutes were derived as (frequency × duration) ÷ 4.33 (weeks per month). For 2007 to 2018, Global Physical Activity Questionnaire captured days/week and min/day for activities performed in ≥10‐minute bouts in a typical week; weekly minutes were computed as (days×minutes).

Following NHANES guidance and the Compendium of Physical Activities, standardized metabolic equivalents of task (MET) values were assigned to domain–intensity combinations 26 , 27 : vigorous work=8.0, moderate work=4.0, walking/cycling for transport=4.0, vigorous recreation=8.0, and moderate recreation=4.0. For each activity, MET‐min/week=weekly minutes×assigned MET value, then summed across domains and intensities.

Two primary, cross‐cycle PA metrics were analyzed across all survey waves: total PA duration (min/week) and total PA volume (MET‐min/week). This cross‐cycle PA metric analysis has been commonly used in long‐span NHANES PA studies. 24 , 25 , 28 To document cross‐cycle comparability, we compared the distributions of PA metrics between 1999 to 2006 and 2007 to 2018 (Table S5) and visually assessed the comparability of the (continuous) PA metrics across the 2 periods by generating kernel density plots (Figure S2). Our comparison identified variations in PA metrics between the 1999 to 2006 and 2007 to 2018 survey cycles. However, no major deviations in distribution characteristics were evident based on the density plots, indicating that the data retain a basic level of comparability. This supports the use of consistent metric definitions across periods for the main analyses. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the inherent limitations in cross‐cycle comparisons, which we discuss in more detail in the Limitations section.

Mortality Ascertainment

Participants were followed from the date of NHANES enrollment until death or December 31, 2019, whichever came first. The primary outcomes were all‐cause mortality and CKM‐related mortality. Causes of death were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/datalinks/mortality‐public.htm). All‐cause mortality was defined as death from any cause. CKM‐related mortality comprised 3 categories: (1) CVD mortality, including deaths due to rheumatic heart disease, hypertensive heart and kidney disease, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, or cerebrovascular disease (ICD‐10 codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51, I60–I69); (2) diabetes‐related mortality (E10–E14); and (3) kidney disease–related mortality (N00–N07, N17–N19, N25–N27).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design by applying Mobile Examination Center weights, according to the official NHANES analytic guidelines (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/Weighting.aspx) and with detailed information provided in the Table S6. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation under the missing‐at‐random assumption. Five imputed data sets (m=5) were generated using the mice package in R with the random forest method (method=“rf”), which does not require distributional assumptions and can accommodate mixed variable types. Each imputation was performed using 20 Gibbs‐sampling iterations. The extent of missingness was summarized in Table S7. The adequacy of the imputation procedure was evaluated using convergence trace plots (Figure S3), density plots (Figure S4), and strip plots (Figure S5), which supported satisfactory convergence, stability, and plausibility of the imputations. Estimates from the imputed data sets were pooled using Rubin's rules.

Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the Rao–Scott chi‐square test. Continuous variables were presented as means with 95% CIs, and group differences were assessed using Taylor series‐based variance estimation. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models with restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions were used to assess the associations and dose–response patterns of total and recreational PA, quantified by duration and volume, with all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality, and to identify PA levels associated with lower mortality. Before model fitting, the assumptions of the Cox model were evaluated. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests and scaled Schoenfeld residual plots, with no significant violations detected (all P>0.05; Tables S8–S11 and Figure S6). Martingale residuals were examined to assess the functional form of covariates and did not indicate substantial model misspecification (Figure S7). Deviance residuals were used to identify influential observations, and no systematic strongly influential points were observed (Figure S8).

We used the fitted spline curves to identify cohort‐specific RCS‐identified inflection points (ie, where the slope began to attenuate or level off) and treated the corresponding values as reference values for stratified presentation; these data‐driven values summarize the observed dose–response shape and should not be interpreted as evidence‐based targets, guideline thresholds, or exercise prescriptions. Covariates included sex, age, BMI, race, ethnicity, family income‐to‐poverty ratio (0–1.0, 1.1–3.0, >3.0), educational attainment (less than high school, high school/equivalent, college or above), marital status, smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption, and Healthy Eating Index score (quartiles). The knots positions of RCS models followed Harrell's recommended default quantile method: 3 knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles; 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th; 5 knots at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles. The model exhibiting the lowest Akaike information criterion was chosen, as this criterion optimally balances goodness‐of‐fit with model complexity, thereby providing a parsimonious and reliable representation of the underlying relationship.

Weighted Cox models were then used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for mortality associated with total and recreational PA below and above the identified levels and, within level‐defined strata, to assess associations with all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality. For comparability, the level was normalized to 1.0, and all other activity levels were divided by this value and expressed as relative multiples. The crude model included no adjustments; Model 1 adjusted for sex, age, BMI, race, ethnicity, family income‐to‐poverty ratio, education level, and marital status; Model 2 further adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, and Healthy Eating Index score. To verify robustness, we conducted sensitivity analyses (restricted to participants with complete covariates and those with ≥3 years of follow‐up, n=12 344; and to CKM stages 3–4, n=2734) and subgroup analyses. Subgroups (sex, age, BMI, race, ethnicity, income, education, marital status, smoking, alcohol use, diet quality) were analyzed with the fully adjusted model, and results were summarized as forest plots.

Weighted Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to illustrate survival differences across PA‐level groups. Additionally, 3‐dimensional heat maps derived from Cox models were used to visualize interactions of recreational PA volume with CKM components (history of myocardial infarction, waist circumference, fasting glucose, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic/diastolic blood pressure, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio) in relation to all‐cause mortality. All analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1). A 2‐sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 19 930 participants were included (mean age 46.43 years [95% CI, 45.99–46.87]; 50.55% women). During follow‐up, 2759 deaths occurred, including 997 CKM‐related deaths. Cumulative all‐cause mortality rose from 2.7% in CKM 0 to 1 to 7.0% in CKM 2 and to 33.5% to 39.2% in CKM 3 to 4, representing an approximately 15‐fold higher risk compared with CKM 0. CKM‐related cumulative mortality showed a similar pattern, increasing from <2% in CKM 0 to 2 to 14.2% to 15.5% in CKM 3 to 4. Baseline characteristics—including sex, age, BMI, socioeconomic variables, lifestyle factors, and PA—differed significantly across stages (Table 1).

Dose–Response Relationship Between PA and Mortality Outcomes

Total PA and All‐Cause Mortality

RCS models showed a significant nonlinear association between total PA (MET‐min/week) and all‐cause mortality (P for overall <0.001–0.326; P for nonlinearity 0.005–0.646) (Figure 1). The curve was L‐shaped: the HR declined from 1.0 at baseline to ∼0.6 to 0.7 within 0 to 500 MET‐min/week; between 500 to 2000 MET‐min/week, the HR stabilized around 0.5 to 0.6. Beyond 2000 MET‐min/week, the association leveled off, with HR near 0.5 and only minor variability. Stratified analyses showed comparable patterns across CKM risk categories. In CKM 0, 500 MET‐min/week corresponded to an HR <0.7. Although baseline risk rose with higher CKM stages, the inverse association with PA persisted, with the lowest HRs near 2000 MET‐min/week.

Differential Effects by PA Domain

Walking or cycling also showed no significant association with mortality (P for overall 0.134–0.990; P for nonlinearity 0.194–0.968), though the inverse‐association curve was flatter than for total PA. Recreational PA displayed a clear inflection pattern (P for overall <0.001–0.285; P for nonlinearity 0.030–0.524): <1000 MET‐min/week, risk declined steeply, then the association plateaued. By contrast, work‐related PA followed a U‐ or J‐shaped curve (P for overall 0.003–0.652; P for nonlinearity 0.013–0.582): 500 to 1500 MET‐min/week was linked to lower mortality risk (HR=0.4–0.6), whereas >2000 to 2500 MET‐min/week was associated with higher risk, particularly in CKM 3 (Figure 1).

Combined Effects of Duration and Volume

When PA was evaluated by duration (min/week), similar nonlinear patterns were observed across PA domains. The steepest decline in HR occurred within ∼500 min/week of total activity, aligning with the 500 to 2000 MET‐min/week range. For recreational activity, the lowest HRs clustered within ~75 to 250 min/week (Figure 2).

Nonlinear Association Between PA and CKM‐Related Mortality

Among CKM stages 2 to 4, RCS analyses demonstrated a significant nonlinear association between PA and CKM‐related mortality (Figures S9 and S10). Total PA was inversely associated with risk (P for overall <0.001–0.013), with pronounced nonlinearity in CKM 3 to 4 (P for nonlinearity 0.001–0.004). Two activity levels associated with lower risk emerged: <500 min/week was associated with the steepest decline in mortality risk; 500 min/week to 2000 MET‐min/week showed a slower, stable decline; >2000 MET‐min/week, additional differences were minimal. Recreational PA showed a stronger inverse association, reaching significance in CKM 3 to 4 (P for overall 0.003–0.016). RCS‐identified inflection points were observed around 250 min/week and 1000 MET‐min/week: <250 min/week, the inverse association was greatest; >250 min/week, the association plateaued; at 1000 MET‐min/week, HRs were 0.3 to 0.6.

Limited Additional Differences Beyond the Reference Values

Above the reference values, further increases were not linked to additional risk reduction. For ≥500 min/week, more duration was not significantly associated with lower risk (all‐cause: HR, 1.076 [95% CI, 0.992–1.167], P=0.076; CKM‐related: HR, 1.062 [95% CI, 0.918–1.230], P=0.418). For ≥2000 MET‐min/week, additional volume was associated with a slight rise in all‐cause mortality (HR, 1.075 [95% CI, 1.019–1.134], P=0.008), with no significant change in CKM‐related mortality. Sensitivity analyses (Table S13) confirmed the robustness of these findings. Subgroup analyses showed consistent inverse associations across most populations (Figures S12–S19).

Three‐Dimensional Interaction Effects

Three‐dimensional analyses identified significant interactions between recreational PA volume and multiple CKM components for all‐cause mortality (Figure 5). The interaction with BMI (P=0.043) indicated a steeper inverse association in obesity; waist circumference (P<0.001) suggested a more pronounced nonlinear inverse association in central obesity. Fasting blood glucose (P=0.023) was consistent with greater differences at moderate volume in abnormal glucose metabolism, whereas triglycerides (P=0.032) suggested that elevated triglycerides were associated with lower risk primarily at moderate volume. Interactions with high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (P=0.67) and diastolic blood pressure (P=0.44) were not significant. Systolic blood pressure (P=0.007) indicated a stronger inverse association in hypertension, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (P=0.006) supported the lowest HRs at moderate volume with impaired renal function. The strongest interaction was with urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (P<0.001), indicating the most pronounced dose–response gradient in proteinuria.

DISCUSSION

This study addresses a practical and clinically relevant question: what duration and volume levels are linked to more favorable survival across the CKM spectrum. Using a nationally representative cohort with long‐term follow‐up, we identified PA ranges associated with lower risk—∼500 min/week or 2000 MET‐min/week for total PA and ≥250 min/week or 1000 MET‐min/week for recreational PA—and compared these associations across CKM stages. We observed nonlinear, L‐shaped dose–response patterns; most of the lower risk accrued below these risk‐associated ranges, and additional activity was associated with only marginal differences. Importantly, although PA showed inverse associations across all stages, absolute and relative risk differences were greatest in CKM 3 to 4, suggesting potential clinical relevance in those most vulnerable.

Extensive published clinical and population‐based evidence links PA to lower mortality in single‐disease settings, 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 , 13 but CKM‐specific evidence remains comparatively new and heterogeneous. Much of the CKM‐oriented literature has focused on CKM development, progression, or stage severity rather than prognosis. For example, NHANES analyses suggest that higher moderate‐to‐vigorous PA is associated with a lower likelihood of incident early‐stage CKM (stages 0–2), 14 and cross‐sectional studies have related moderate‐to‐vigorous PA and sedentary time to lower odds of advanced CKM stage. 16 However, these designs do not evaluate mortality or provide stage‐stratified survival estimates. Prognostic studies more often frame PA within composite cardiovascular health or lifestyle indices (eg, Life's Essential 8), supporting the clinical relevance of health behaviors across CKM stages but not isolating PA‐specific dose–response patterns or activity domains. 17 , 18 , 29 , 30 Device‐based analyses in large biobanks extend this evidence, yet typically summarize overall activity without detailing domain‐specific patterns or CKM‐related mortality across the full CKM spectrum. 31 Recent accelerometer‐based NHANES studies have examined light PA and weekend‐warrior activity patterns across CKM stages. However, they focused on device‐measured intensity or timing rather than on domain‐specific PA duration and volume, and they did not jointly assess all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality across CKM stages 0 to 4. 32 , 33 Expert reviews emphasize lifestyle intervention throughout the CKM continuum but do not provide stage‐stratified cohort modeling that links quantified PA volume or duration to mortality outcomes. 34 , 35 In this setting, our NHANES analysis evaluates PA as a stand‐alone exposure—by duration and volume, overall and by domain—across CKM stages 0 to 4 for both all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality using nonlinear dose–response modeling; the findings should be interpreted as prognostic associations rather than exercise prescriptions.

We observed a steep mortality gradient across CKM stages, consistent with prior large‐scale cohorts. 5 , 6 , 36 , 37 Mortality rose from <3% in CKM 0 to 1 to nearly 40% in CKM 3 to 4, paralleling findings from Asian cohorts where each additional CKM component was associated with higher mortality and shorter life expectancy. 6 In our domain‐specific analyses, PA showed a robust inverse association across CKM stages, and the pattern was domain‐dependent: recreational PA showed the clearest inverse association, whereas occupational PA followed a U‐ or J‐shaped pattern. At low‐to‐moderate levels, occupational activity may simply reflect regular movement and less sedentary time. At very high workloads, however, risk increased—consistent with the PA paradox. 38 , 39 , 40 Prior studies have likewise reported that high occupational activity does not necessarily confer the survival benefits observed for leisure‐time activity. 38 , 39 This may reflect that high occupational PA is not simply “more exercise.” In many settings, it consists of prolonged, repetitive, or static work (often involving heavy lifting or long standing), performed at a fixed pace with limited autonomy and insufficient recovery, which may sustain hemodynamic load and fatigue and limit cardiorespiratory adaptation compared with structured leisure‐time activity. 39 , 40 , 41 In addition, very high occupational PA often co‐occurs with workplace and socioeconomic factors (eg, shift work, heat exposure, psychosocial stress, and environmental hazards) 40 , 41 that are not fully captured in NHANES, making residual confounding plausible at the upper end. Clinically, high occupational PA should not be assumed to confer the same benefits as structured recreational activity.

Our interaction analyses suggest that the inverse association between PA and mortality was stronger among participants with less favorable cardiometabolic–renal profiles (older age, excess adiposity/central obesity, dysglycemia, elevated blood pressure, hypertriglyceridemia, and reduced kidney function). Among renal markers, urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio showed the strongest interaction signal, with a clearer gradient in the presence of proteinuria. Among individuals with lower baseline activity, greater PA volume was associated with meaningfully lower all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality after full adjustment, consistent with prior dose–response work showing that the largest relative gains occur when moving from inactivity to modest activity. 42

Notably, the larger inverse associations observed in CKM stages 3 to 4 warrant emphasis. Because baseline absolute risk is higher in advanced CKM, modest differences in activity or fitness can translate into larger absolute survival differences. One biologically plausible explanation is that advanced CKM and cardiorenal disease may result from converging maladaptive pathways, including neurohormonal activation (eg, the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system), oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and maladaptive epithelial‐ and endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transitions, which collectively promote endothelial dysfunction, vascular and cardiac remodeling, and fibrosis across the heart–kidney axis. 43 , 44 , 45 These maladaptive pathways function within a tightly interconnected, multiorgan network, where neurohormonal, oxidative, and inflammatory signals mutually amplify cellular stress responses, driving progressive cardiac and renal fibrosis and endothelial dysfunction, and thereby heightening vulnerability in advanced CKM stages. 1 Recent CKM‐focused studies and preclinical models further support the role of inflammation, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system‐related biomarkers, and oxidative balance in CKM pathophysiology and the risk associated with advanced stages. 46 , 47 , 48

Emerging evidence indicates that dysregulation of gut microbiota exacerbates oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, contributing to renal tubular injury and interstitial fibrosis in CKD and cardiorenal syndrome. 43 , 45 Local podocyte mechanisms, including aryl hydrocarbon receptor ‐mediated stress signaling, contribute to enhanced cellular stress and renal vulnerability in proteinuric CKD. 49 Gut microbial dysbiosis can further modulate renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system activity and activate TGF‐β (transforming growth factor–beta)/mothers against decapentaplegic homolog (Smad) and Wnt (wingless/integration‐1)/β‐catenin signaling, amplifying endothelial and renal dysfunction, thereby highlighting key molecular mechanisms underlying advanced CKM progression. 50 Dysregulated noncoding RNAs, including microRNAs and long noncoding RNAs, have also been implicated in cardiorenal remodeling and may interact with inflammatory, fibrotic, and metabolic signaling pathways, adding an additional layer of regulation in advanced cardiorenal disease. 43 Similarly, CKD is linked to impaired intestinal barrier integrity, which increases systemic exposure to endotoxin and gut‐derived uremic solutes, sustaining chronic systemic inflammation. 51 These processes are particularly relevant in patients with proteinuria, where renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system/reactive oxygen species signaling and gut‐derived metabolites have been implicated in kidney injury pathways. 52

Mechanistic studies suggest that modulation of these maladaptive pathways, including pharmacologic interventions such as statins, can attenuate epithelial‐ and endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transitions, suppress proinflammatory cytokines, reduce reactive oxygen species production, and enhance protective mediators, potentially amplifying beneficial cellular responses in high‐risk populations with CKM. 45 , 50 In this setting of heightened hemodynamic and inflammatory stress, physical activity‐related improvements in endothelial function, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory tone may exert more pronounced benefits, 53 , 54 , 55 potentially acting in concert with systemic maladaptive pathways, helping to contextualize the steeper survival gradients observed in CKM stages 3 to 4 and in participants with proteinuria.

At the same time, these gradients should be interpreted cautiously. Reverse causation is plausible: participants with more advanced disease, frailty, or subclinical symptoms may reduce activity, so lower PA may partly reflect underlying illness rather than a causal effect of PA. Higher PA may therefore also be a marker of better functional capacity and overall health status. Selection may also contribute: participants able to sustain higher activity may represent a more resilient subgroup with better functional reserve and therefore lower mortality. This pattern may be more apparent in frailer or higher‐risk groups. 56 For clinical context, current recommendations for adults remain 150 to 300 min/week of moderate‐intensity (or 75–150 min/week vigorous‐intensity) aerobic activity, plus muscle‐strengthening activities on ≥2 days/week. 57 , 58 Relatedly, the activity levels highlighted here (e.g., ~500 min/week total PA and ≥250 min/week recreational PA) are data‐driven inflection points from the fitted curves and are intended as risk‐associated reference ranges for descriptive stratification—not new guideline targets, causal thresholds, or exercise prescriptions.

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations. First, PA was self‐reported and assessed only at baseline, introducing recall and social desirability bias and precluding assessment of changes over follow‐up. PA was measured using 2 instruments across NHANES cycles (30‐day recall in 1999–2006 and Global Physical Activity Questionnaire in 2007–2018); we followed established methods used in previous long‐span NHANES analyses to assess PA across the 2 periods. Although PA distributions were broadly similar across periods, residual misclassification of PA duration and volume may remain and could influence the estimated dose–response shape and the exact location of RCS‐derived inflection points, particularly at the extremes of activity. Second, CKM stage was assigned at baseline, and we could not capture stage transitions or short‐term changes in metabolic or renal status during follow‐up; thus, some stage misclassification over time is possible. Third, residual confounding and reverse causation remain concerns. Although we adjusted for many covariates, participants with more advanced disease, frailty, or subclinical symptoms may be less active and also have higher mortality. Our sensitivity analyses restricting the sample to participants with complete covariates and to those with ≥3 years of follow‐up reduced, but did not eliminate, this concern. Time‐updated information on disease severity, incident diagnoses, and cardiometabolic treatments was not available, so time‐dependent confounding could not be modeled. Fourth, selection and survivorship biases are possible. NHANES excludes institutionalized populations, and exclusions related to missing PA data or mortality linkage may further limit generalizability, particularly to frailer or very high‐risk groups. In addition, because the analytic sample may overrepresent non‐Hispanic White participants relative to other racial or ethnic groups, the applicability of our findings to more diverse populations or settings outside the United States may be limited. Fifth, misclassification of outcomes and exposures is also possible. Cause‐specific deaths were determined by registry linkage and may be misclassified, particularly for CKM‐related mortality. Moreover, domain‐specific PA (occupational, transport, recreational) relied on self‐categorization and fixed MET assignments, which may not fully capture domain‐specific intensity. Finally, the mechanistic explanations discussed above are intended to provide biological context and could not be tested directly in this observational analysis. The reported PA inflection points should also be interpreted as descriptive summaries of nonlinear associations in this cohort, rather than causal thresholds or clinical targets. Because multiple PA metrics, domains, and outcomes were examined, chance findings are possible. Estimates at very high PA levels were also less stable because of smaller subgroup sizes. Independent confirmation in more diverse cohorts, using device‐based PA assessment and time‐updated CKM staging, is needed.

CONCLUSIONS

Higher PA was associated with lower all‐cause and CKM‐related mortality across CKM stages, with the most significant risk differences accruing at modest, attainable levels. The greatest absolute differences were observed in CKM stages 3 to 4, where stronger inverse associations were found among participants with less favorable cardiometabolic and renal profiles. Dose–response curves were nonlinear; recreational PA showed the clearest inverse association, whereas occupational PA displayed a U‐/J‐shaped pattern. These findings are observational; the reported inflection points should be interpreted as cohort‐specific, risk‐associated ranges rather than prescriptive exercise targets. Future studies using device‐based PA assessment and time‐updated CKM staging are needed to validate these findings and refine stage‐specific counseling approaches.
