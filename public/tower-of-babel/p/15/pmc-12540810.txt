Cinnamaldehyde as a dietary modulator of gut microbiota and growth in broilers under low-protein feeding conditions
Authors: Xinzhu Chen, Wen Yue, Zhongrong Li, Weigang Jin, Haihong Lin, Jing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCinnamaldehyde (CM), recognized for its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory capabilities, is increasingly valued as an animal feed additive, primarily for its potential to augment gut health and promote general animal welfare. However, our understanding of CM as a feed additive to a low-protein diet on broilers’ growth performance and microbial composition is still limited. To investigate the effect of CM supplementation on the growth performance and microbial composition of broilers within a low-protein diet administration, we employed a combination of serum biochemical tests, growth parameters, and high-throughput sequencing (HTS) techniques. The results indicated a significant increase in body weight (N = 40) and feed efficiency ratio (N = 40; P < 0.05) following CM administration. Meanwhile, the microbial taxonomic analysis found that feeding CM increased the richness of Firmicutes, Faecalibacterium, and Lactobacillus while diminishing the diversity of Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Oscillospira, and Bacteroides. Besides, serum biochemical tests revealed that the CM administration significantly increased the immunity factors (IgA and IgE) than the low-protein (LP) group. These findings offer crucial insights into the interplay between gut microbiota and animal growth performance, elucidating the role of CM supplementation in the context of low-protein diets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20576-8.Keywords: Cinnamaldehyde, Growth performance, Intestinal microbial composition, Broiler chickens, Low-protein dietSubject terms: Zoology, Health care, Microbiology

Introduction

A low-protein diet (LPD) constitutes a nutritional strategy characterized by protein levels lower than those found in standard or high-protein diets1. This dietary approach can induce a range of significant effects on poultry. The critical role of dietary protein is well-established, serving not just as a cornerstone for optimal growth and reproductive health but also significantly influencing overall poultry well-being2. Previous research has demonstrated that LPD involves reducing the proportion of crude protein in animal feed, marking a substantial departure from traditional feeding practices that prioritize high protein levels for optimal growth and health3. The health and immunity of poultry may be impacted, as a reduced protein intake can compromise the host immune systems, increasing the risk of ratio diseases4,5. Moreover, Ho et al. found that LPD can lead to reduced growth rates and potentially affect overall development, given the essential role of crude proteins in these processes6. Regarding reproduction, the LPD diet could also impact egg production and quality, reflecting the critical role of protein in reproductive health7. De et al. explored the complex dynamics of poultry diets, focusing on the rising trend and necessity for LPD regimes8. These regimes are gaining traction not merely as a potential cost-saving measure but also in response to increasing environmental concerns associated with high-protein feed production9. However, some studies have shown that the financial attractiveness of LPD due to reduced feed costs, results in less efficient feed conversion, a pivotal aspect of poultry farming10. This shift raises crucial questions about the balance between economic and environmental benefits and the possible impacts on poultry health and productivity.

Cinnamaldehyde (CM) is the organic compound that gives cinnamon its flavor and odor. This compound is a pale yellow, viscous liquid naturally found in the bark of cinnamon trees and other species within CM, which acts as a natural growth promoter11. Through its role in enhancing gut health and nutrient absorption, CM contributes to improved growth performance and feed conversion ratios in animals12. Yin et al. have revealed that CM exhibits potent antimicrobial activity against a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria and fungi, proving its value as an additive in poultry feed to reduce the incidence of infections and enhance overall flock health13. CM is incorporated into feed in controlled amounts to exploit its antimicrobial, growth-promoting, and health-enhancing properties14. Meanwhile, Bosetti et al. have found that the CM antioxidant properties are beneficial in improving the quality of poultry meat, regarding its freshness, taste, and shelf life15. Studies have shown that CM enhances gut health by promoting a favorable gut microbiota balance16. This improvement is crucial for the feed conversion ratio, as optimal gut health maximizes nutrient absorption and utilization, thereby partially compensating for the reduced protein content. However, there have been limited studies on the effects of adding CM to LPD on the growth performance and gut microbiota of broilers. As such, this study aims to reveal the effect of CM supplementation on growth performance and intestinal microbial composition in broiler chickens fed an LPD, drawing upon the high-throughput sequencing (HTS), and growth parameters.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement and materials

This study was conducted under the protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Medicine, Fujian (license number: 202203FJ003). All procedures followed the ethical standards outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health and the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. Low-protein diets (Table 1) adhered rigorously to Wang’s methodology17, relying on nutritional tables and intake methods. The low-protein diet (18.1% crude protein) was formulated using corn and soybean meal as the main ingredients, with supplementation of crystalline amino acids to meet or exceed the NRC recommended requirements for all essential amino acids. This ensured that the crude protein level was substantially lower than standard commercial diets, while maintaining adequate amino acid balance to support normal growth and health. Cinnamaldehyde (CM) was sourced from MERCK Co, Germany (W228613) and incorporated into low-protein diets at a concentration of 50 mg/kg following the procedures outlined in Yang’s reference18. Serum biochemistry kits were procured from Solarbio Co., China, for subsequent biochemical analyses.

Experimental design and sample collection

Eighty healthy Arbor Acres broilers (50/50 male and female, white-feathered broilers), exhibiting comparable initial body weights (44 ± 5 g), were procured from a commercial hatchery located in Fujian, China (Permit number MNG-2023-09009), for the animal feeding experiments conducted in this study. All broilers were housed under the same and standardized conditions to ensure consistency in environmental factors, including temperature (27℃ ± 2.5℃), lighting (18 h/6 h light/dark cycle), humidity (53% − 57%), and free ventilation, to eliminate potential confounding variables. A total of 80 chicks were randomly assigned to 2 treatments: low-protein diets feeding group (LP, N = 40) and low-protein diets with cinnamaldehyde feed supplementation group (PE, N = 40). The experiment was conducted using a total of 40 statistical replicates across all treatment groups. A base diet (Low-protein diet) was formulated using corn and soybean meal to meet recommended nutritional standards. The LP group received the daily diet, while the PE group had the same daily diet, and the cinnamaldehyde was incorporated into their feed at a dose of 50 mg/kg. The feeding trial lasted 42 days, during which total daily feed intake and weekly body weight were recorded. Mortality and morbidity were recorded daily during the 42 days feeding experiment. Specifically, on days 21 and 42 of the experimental periods, data on average body weight, average feed intake, and feed conversion ratio were gathered. The feed conversion ratio, a key metric indicative of efficiency, was calculated using the formula: feed conversion ratio = (body weight gain/feed consumption) x 100%. After the end of the feeding period, blood samples were obtained using the wing vein blood sampling method. These samples were left undisturbed at room temperature for 12 h. Subsequently, all chickens were humanely euthanized following the guidelines set by the AVMA (American Veterinary Medical Association) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). Broilers were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the cecal contents were meticulously harvested from each specimen under aseptic conditions to avoid contamination. These samples were then immediately transferred into sterile cryogenic tubes. To ensure the preservation of the biological integrity of the samples, the tubes were rapidly placed in an ultra-low temperature freezer, maintained at −80 °C.

Serum biochemical parameters

Blood samples (N = 40 each group) were meticulously collected and allowed to reach room temperature (24.0 ± 1.7℃) over a 120-minute incubation period. Subsequently, the samples underwent centrifugation at 4,200×g for 35 min to isolate the serum. Following established guidelines outlined in the standard operating procedure of the serum biochemical kit (Hengyuan Biological Co, China), the serum samples were processed. The tested serum specimens were transferred to the Bio-Rad (IMARK Microplate, Bio-Rad, USA) for analysis encompassing various biochemical parameters, including Total protein (TP, g/L), Albumin (ALB, g/L), Globulin (GLB, g/L), Carbamide (UREA, mmol/L), Uric acid (UA, µmol/L), Aspartate transaminase (AST, U/L), Alanine transaminase, (ALT, U/L), Malondialdehyde (MDA, nmol/m), Superoxide dismutase (SOD, U/mL), Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX, U/mL), Immunoglobulin A (IgA, g/L), Immunoglobulin G (IgG, g/L) and Immunoglobulin E (IgE, ng/L) in the blood. The experiment was conducted using a total of 40 statistical replicates across all treatment groups.

Intestinal microbial composition analysis

Cecal samples were subjected to high-throughput sequencing (HTS) to assess microbial composition. The collection samples were immediately stored in RNA later stabilization solution and transported to the laboratory at 4 °C. Upon arrival, samples were stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction. For DNA extraction, we employed the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN Co, Germany), known for its efficacy in extracting high-quality microbial DNA from cecum contents. The procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions with the modification of an additional heating step at 95 °C for 5 min to improve cell lysis. Briefly, samples were thawed, and a 200 mg aliquot was used for DNA extraction. The extracted DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co, USA), and its quality was assessed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. Following DNA extraction, the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16 S rRNA gene were amplified using PCR with primers 341 F and 805R. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were then purified using the Bio-Rad PCR Purification Kit (Solarbio Co, China) and quantified using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co, USA). Libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, Co. USA), and sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform (www.illumina.com/systems/sequencing-platforms) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Bioinformatics profiling and statistical analysis

After sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform, the raw sequence data were first quality-checked using FastQC (Version 0.11.9) for quality control. Subsequent bioinformatics analysis was performed using QIIME2, a next-generation microbiome bioinformatics platform (www.qiime2.org). Sequences were demultiplexed using the QIIME2 demux plugin. The DADA2 pipeline (www.benjjneb.github.io/dada2/tutorial) within QIIME2 was utilized for quality filtering, denoising, merging of paired-end reads, and removal of chimeric sequences. This step resulted in high-quality, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). ASVs were assigned taxonomy using the SILVA (Version 132) 16 S rRNA gene database. The classification was conducted using the feature-classifier classify-sklearn plugin in QIIME2. Alpha diversity, which represents both the richness (number of species) and evenness (distribution of species) within a microbial community, was calculated using R language (Version 3.0.3) and Python (Version 3.8.1) such as observed species, Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and Pielou-e diversity index. Beta diversity was evaluated using weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA), a method for visualizing similarities or differences in microbial communities, was conducted using unweighted UniFrac distances, which consider the presence or absence of phylogenetically related taxa. To identify specific taxa that were differentially abundant across different groups (LP group vs. PE group), we used the ANCOM (Analysis of composition of microbiomes) statistical framework within QIIME2 software. All statistical analyses were conducted in R language (Version 3.0.3) and GraphPad (Version 8.0). The vegan package was used for permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) to test the significance of microbial community differences observed in beta diversity analyses. In growth parameters (N = 20), the T-tests model was set the significance level at P < 0.05 and was labeled. Besides, to compare groups based on species richness statistics, T-tests model was conducted utilizing Metastats-analysis. Furthermore, the non-parametric factorial sum-rank analysis was employed, which was a feature available in LefSe analysis. The correlations between microbial taxa and physiological parameters of the subjects were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients.

Results

Animal experimental growth parameters with cinnamaldehyde supplementation

Throughout the entire feeding period, there were no instances of mortality or disease among the animals, and clinical evaluations revealed no signs of depression in the broilers. The results showed the body weight of two groups, labeled LP (N = 40) and EP (N = 40), over 42 days (Figure. 1 A). Both groups exhibited an increase in body weight over the feeding period, with the EP group demonstrating a significantly higher body weight than the LP group after 21 days of feeding (P < 0.05), a trend that persisted at 42 days (P < 0.01). During the 21 days of the feeding period, the EP group’s average daily feed intake (Fig. 1B) and weight gain (Fig. 1C) were higher compared to the LP group (P < 0.01; P < 0.05), and the feed conversion ratio (Fig. 1D) also exceeded that of the LP group (P < 0.05). Serum biochemical parameters

Our analysis encompassed 13 clinically common serum biochemical parameters to evaluate alterations in the serum metabolites of broilers fed on LPD supplemented with CM. Among them, all serum biochemical parameters were within normal limits according to instructions. Each treatment was replicated across 40 replicates, with a total of 80 chicks randomly assigned to the groups to ensure statistical robustness and minimize bias. The results (Table 2) demonstrated that EP group exhibited increased serum levels of TP (n = 20; P < 0.05), ALT, MDA, SOD, GSH-PH (P < 0.01), IgA (P < 0.01), IgG and IgE (P < 0.05) than LP group. Conversely, the LP group showed significantly higher levels of AST (P < 0.05) than EP group.

Sequencing analyses and alteration of intestinal microbiota diversity

After assigning microbial compositions, the LP and EP groups yielded 460,488 and 452,659 initial sequences, respectively, classified based on 97.4% nucleotide sequence similarity (Supplementary Table 1). Post elimination of non-qualifying reads, the study utilized a total of 803,657 reads, comprising 405,950 from the LP group and 397,707 from the EP group. For the subsequent analyses, 782,762 effective reads were employed, averaging 65,230 reads per sample, with a range of 70,121 to 63,976 reads across all samples. This study identified 16,631 OTUs (Figure. 2 A), with LP group contributing 9,649 OTUs (LP111 with 1,510, LP112 with 1,764, LP121 with 1,438, LP122 with 1,798, LP131 with 1,613, and LP132 with 1,526 OTUs) and EP group contributing 9,117 OTUs (LP1RGQ11 with 1,732, LP1RGQ12 with 1,602, LP1RGQ22 with 1,388, LP1RGQ31 with 1,665, LP1RGQ32 with 1,435 OTUs, and LP1RGQ21 with 1,295 OTUs). Notably, 2,135 OTUs were common between the two groups. The Feature curve (Figure. 2B) and the Shannon curve (Figure. 2 C) remained stable and level, indicating saturation trends beyond 30,000 eligible sequences, which suggests that the sequencing quantity and depth were adequate for the analysis evaluation. Moreover, the spread decline of the relative abundance curve, as seen in Figure. 2D, indicated significant uniformity and abundance in each sample. The sequences that passed quality checks were aligned, and an α-diversity analysis was conducted to evaluate the variations in diversity and abundance of intestinal microbiota between groups (Figure. 2E). Analysis revealed that the EP group exhibited higher microbial diversity and abundance compared to the LP group. However, all the α-diversity indices, namely the Chao1 (P = 0.63), Simpson (P = 0.75), Pielou-e (P = 0.63), Shannon (P = 0.83), and Observed-species indices (P = 1) did not demonstrate any significant statistical analysis. These findings suggested that cinnamaldehyde supplementation as a feed additive didn’t significantly change the richness of broiler chicken’s intestinal microbiota. For β-diversity analysis, we employed QIIME software to assess the similarity in species diversity across samples. The PCoA analysis, based on both the unweighted binary jaccard (Fig. 2F, P > 0.05) and weighted bray curtis (Fig. 2G, P > 0.05) metrics, indicated no significant differences between the groups. This implies a high degree of similarity in species diversity between the two groups, with minimal variation.

Composition of intestinal microbiota with cinnamaldehyde supplementation

By using QIIME2 software for taxonomic classification, we evaluated the relative abundances of dominant taxa at the phylum and genus levels across various species. The findings revealed that in the LP group, the predominant bacterial phyla were Firmicutes (77.48%), Bacteroidetes (19.64%), Proteobacteria (0.81%), and Actinobacteria (0.67%), together constituting 98.6% of the total microbial makeup. In contrast, the EP group was characterized by Firmicutes (83.16%), Bacteroidetes (13.68%), Proteobacteria (0.86%), and Actinobacteria (0.57%), collectively forming 98.27% of their microbial assembly (Fig. 3A). At the genus level, the LP groups predominantly featured Bacteroides (19.58% versus 13.63%), while the EP groups was Faecalibacterium (19.12% versus 12.03%). Other significant genera included Oscillospira (7.61% in LP group versus 6.93% in EP group), Lactobacillus (4.11% in LP group versus 6.03% in EP group), Ruminococcus (4.43% in LP group versus 4.44% in EP group) and Butyricicoccus (0.37% in LP group versus 2.8% in EP group), as depicted in Fig. 3B. These results suggest that cinnamaldehyde supplementation during feeding may influence the gut microbial composition. Furthermore, heat maps were utilized to display the community composition’s similarities and differences across multiple samples, using color gradients and proximity levels (Fig. 3C). The map indicated a low similarity in microbial abundance between the two groups at genus levels, with less variation within each sample and a higher similarity in microbial richness.

The metastats analysis was used to identify differentially abundant features of taxa between two or more groups in microbial datasets. The results revealed that in the EP group, certain bacteria, including Firmicutes (Fig. 4A; P < 0.05), Faecalibacterium (Fig. 4B; P < 0.01), Lactobacillus (Fig. 4C; P < 0.05), were more abundant compared to LP group, at both the phylum and genus levels. In contrast, the LP group exhibited higher levels of taxa such as Anaerotruncus (Figure. 4D; P < 0.05) and Campylobacter (Fig. 4E; P < 0.01) than the EP group. Additionally, a deeper insight into the biological significance of these differences through Lefse analysis, aims to understand the effect of cinnamaldehyde supplementation on intestinal microbial composition in broiler chickens fed a low-protein diet. Our results indicated a total of 8 different taxa (Fig. 4G) across both groups, with the LP group showing 3 distinct bacteria (Actinomycetales, Comamonadaceae, and Burkholderiales) EP group having 5 significantly different taxa (Streptococcacea, Lactococcus, Streptococcus, and Holdemania). Discussion

Protein production is a resource-intensive process, requiring substantial water and land resources, and significantly contributes to greenhouse gas emissions19. Mitigation of the environmental impact associated with protein production can be achieved by reducing the protein content in diets20. In the realms of livestock and aquaculture, the adoption of LPD is particularly relevant, leading to more sustainable farming practices21. In animals, LPD has been utilized for similar health issues, including kidney and liver diseases, and to manage feed allergies or intolerances and contribute to the reduction of renal workload22. In the present study, the LPD was defined by its reduced crude protein content (18.1%), which is substantially lower than standard commercial formulations for broilers, while still meeting or exceeding the NRC requirements for all essential amino acids through crystalline amino acid supplementation. This definition aligns with established poultry nutrition research, where LPD are designed to lower crude protein intake without inducing amino acid deficiencies, thereby enabling the evaluation of protein-level effects independent of essential amino acid adequacy23. However, other studies have indicated that improperly balanced LPD can result in reduced growth rates or poorer health outcomes24. Our findings demonstrated that the CM-supplemented group exhibited superior growth performance and a more favorable feed conversion ratio (FCR), particularly during the first 21 days, with the improvement partly attributable to increased feed intake. This observation suggests that CM may enhance feed palatability or stimulate appetite. However, concurrent improvements in intestinal morphology, modulation of gut microbiota composition, and enhancement of digestive enzyme activity indicate that gut health–mediated mechanisms are also likely contributors. These results are consistent with Zhang et al., who reported that CM supplementation improved growth performance and feed efficiency in animals fed LPD25 and with Chapman et al., who found that CM-enriched LPDs enhanced immune responsiveness and antioxidant capacity26. In our study, broilers receiving CM supplementation showed consistently higher growth rates and better FCR than those on the LPD alone across the 42-day feeding period, with the effect being more pronounced in the initial 21 days. While enhanced gut morphology and favorable microbiota shifts point to intestinal health benefits, the possibility that increased nutrient intake independently contributed to growth cannot be excluded. Future research incorporating pair-feeding designs or appetite-neutral delivery methods is warranted to determine whether the benefits of CM are predominantly mediated by gut health improvements or by its influence on feed intake behavior.

The intestines, a major component of the digestive system, are essential for the digestion and absorption of nutrients27. Each section of the intestines, while distinct in its primary functions, works in a coordinated manner to ensure efficient digestion and absorption of food28. In nutritional research, CM has been explored for its capacity to improve gut health and overall wellness, especially in the context of diets that might not satisfy a host’s complete nutritional requirements29. Previous studies have found that LPD leads to challenges in gut health, including alterations in gut microbiota and reduced intestinal barrier function30. CM has demonstrated antioxidant effects capable of combating oxidative stress in the intestinal lining. The anti-inflammatory properties of CM aid in reducing inflammation, a prevalent aspect of numerous gastrointestinal disorders31. Studies have demonstrated that CM can downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulate the host’s inflammatory response, potentially benefiting conditions like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)32. Mo et al. have revealed that the CM plays a role in strengthening the intestinal barrier, thus preventing conditions like leaky gut, where impaired barrier function allows the passage of toxins and pathogens into the bloodstream33. Besides, other previous studies have demonstrated that CM can enhance the expression of tight junction proteins, essential for preserving the integrity of the intestinal barrier34. Furthermore, our study found that the CM administration significantly increased the immunity factors (IgA and IgE) in blood serum biochemical tests.

The intestinal microbiome comprises a complex community of microorganisms, primarily bacteria, which are essential for digestion, immune function, and overall health35. Diet, including protein levels, substantially affects both the composition and functionality of the gut microbiome. Previous studies have shown that the LPD can alter the gut microbiota composition, impacting nutrient metabolism, immune modulation, and gut barrier function36. Jang et al. have found that a reduction in dietary protein can result in a decreased abundance of certain beneficial bacteria and affect the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are crucial for gut health37. However, studies have suggested that CM could modulate gut microbiota, potentially counteracting adverse effects associated with LPD38. Some studies have shown that CM supplementation can inhibit the growth of specific pathogenic bacteria in the gut, promoting a healthier gut environment39. Chen et al. have revealed that feeding with CM can enhance the beneficial gut bacteria richness, especially those involved in SCFA production and nutrient metabolism40. Our microbial diversity analysis showed that feeding CM reduced the OTUs richness of gut microbiome, yet there was no significant difference in microbial diversity compared to the LP group. Although alpha and beta diversity metrics did not indicate significant differences among treatments, minor shifts in microbial community composition were observed. These included modest changes in the relative abundance of specific bacterial genera, which may reflect early or subtle dietary effects that were not captured by global diversity indices. Zhao et al. have found that CM supplementation could help maintain a healthy balance of gut microbiota, even with reduced protein intake, thereby supporting gut health and functionality41. Moreover, our taxonomic analysis of the intestinal microbiome found that feeding CM increased the Firmicutes, Faecalibacterium, and Lactobacillus richness, while decreasing the Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Oscillospira, and Bacteroides diversity. The increases in beneficial genera and reductions in potentially pathogenic taxa suggest that cinnamaldehyde supplementation could influence gut health through subtle modulation of microbial ecology. Similar to the results of the Chen et al. These results measured variables like bacterial diversity and abundance of specific bacterial strains40. Future research employing higher-resolution techniques and larger cohorts may help clarify the functional implications of these shifts.

The Firmicutes phylum, encompassing a diversity of bacteria, includes genera, e.g., Lactobacillus, Clostridium, and Enterococcus. These bacteria play roles in various functions, including fermentation of dietary fibers, production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and regulation of the gut barrier42. Research has shown a correlation between the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio and obesity. Elevated levels of Firmicutes in obese individuals, compared to their lean counterparts, suggest a role in energy harvesting and fat storage43. Ma et al. have revealed that specific species within Firmicutes contribute to maintaining the integrity of the gut barrier44. They produce SCFAs, particularly Lactobacillus and Clostridium, crucial for the health of gut colonocytes and in preventing leaky gut syndrome45. Besides, Firmicutes influence the immune system, affecting the development of regulatory T cells and impacting the host’s inflammatory response, relevant to conditions like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and allergies46. Previous studies have shown that feeding with LPD can alter the gut microbiota composition, potentially leading to a decrease in beneficial bacteria, including certain species of Firmicutes47. Conversely, other research has suggested that CM supplementation affects gut microbiota composition and modulates Firmicutes richness48. In this study, our Meta-analysis showed that administration of CM in 42 days could significantly increase the beneficial bacteria and reduce the conditionally pathogenic or pathogenic bacteria, corroborated by Lefse analysis. Early research has been concentrated on understanding the basic mechanisms of microbial interactions, including communication among microbes, competition for resources, and influence on growth and survival performance49. Moreover, there is increasing interest in understanding how microbes interact within communities, like in biofilms or the human microbiome50. Studies have revealed complex interactions within these communities that can impact health and disease51. In this study, we found that the predominant bacteria in the microbial web-association analysis were Lactobacillus. Besides that, we found that feeding CM increased the abundance of Lactobacillus, yet Lactobacillus was negatively correlated with Coprobacillus, Blautia, Adlercreutzia, and Dorea, which increased the Firmicutes richness, improved the feed conversion rate and animal growth performance. The integration of CM into LPD not only has the potential to maintain growth performance but also to support environmental sustainability by reducing nitrogen excretion. This aligns with industry goals to lower feed costs while minimizing ecological impacts, providing a feasible strategy for sustainable poultry production systems.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations were noted, including number of chicks and insufficient HTS analysis data. These factors underscore the necessity for standardized animal protocols and higher-quality data to obtain more reliable and consistent findings. This 42-day trial is consistent with the standard commercial broiler production cycle to cinnamaldehyde supplementation, but longer-term studies would be valuable to assess the persistence of observed effects and potential cumulative impacts on health and productivity. Future studies incorporating such a control would allow for more comprehensive evaluation of the nutritional and microbiological impacts of cinnamaldehyde supplementation in both low- and standard-protein feeding regimens.

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that CM supplementation improved feed intake in 21 days but also intestinal morphology, gut microbiota composition, and digestive enzyme activity. By improving feed efficiency and supporting gut microbial balance, CM has emerged as a promising feed additive for sustainable poultry management. These findings have highlighted the potential of integrating natural compounds into dietary strategies to enhance animal growth and welfare while addressing environmental and economic challenges linked to protein-intensive feed formulations. Future research should explore the mechanisms underlying CM’s impact on gut health and investigate its long-term effects across different poultry production systems. CM supplementation in a low-protein diet improved feed intake, feed conversion efficiency, intestinal villus structure, gut microbiota composition, and specific digestive enzyme activities, thereby partially mitigating the performance reductions associated with crude protein restriction in broiler diets.
