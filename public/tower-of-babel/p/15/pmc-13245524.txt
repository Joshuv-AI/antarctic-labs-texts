Closing the Implementation Gap: New Models of Care to End Chronic Liver Disease
Authors: Fernando Bril, Fasiha Kanwal, Kenneth Cusi
Published: 2026

Chronic liver disease (CLD) represents a major and growing public health challenge in Europe, driven largely by the convergence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), and viral hepatitis as outlined in the four manuscripts published in The Lancet Regional Health – Europe about ending CLD as a public health threat in Europe.1, 2, 3, 4 MASLD alone affects approximately one-third of the adult European population and is second only to ALD as a leading cause of cirrhosis.5 Europe remains the region with the highest levels of alcohol consumption per capita, contributing substantially to liver-related mortality.3 Finally, despite effective vaccine for hepatitis B virus infection and safe treatments for both hepatitis B and hepatitis C virus infections, millions of individuals in the region continue to live with chronic infection, and progress toward elimination targets set by the World Health Organization remains uneven.

It is evident based on the manuscripts included in The Lancet Regional Health – Europe Series that there is a marked heterogeneity across countries in policy implementation, healthcare infrastructure, resource allocation, and access to care, which contributes to wide disparities in liver disease outcomes. Similar epidemiological trends are observed in the United States, with increasing prevalence of MASLD-related fibrosis6 and rising mortality from ALD.7 While some underlying system challenges may diverge in certain domains, both Europe and U.S. share many important commonalities. In the United States, high healthcare costs and reliance on an insurance-based model create substantial barriers to healthcare access, particularly for preventive services and longitudinal care, limiting early detection and continuity of care.8 Indeed, while the United States spends nearly twice as much on healthcare as other high-income countries, cost-related barriers to care are significantly higher than in European countries.9 In contrast, European healthcare systems are largely grounded in universal coverage and offer broader population-level access. However, both share inconsistent policy prioritization, fragmented implementation of guidelines, and variable resource allocation across regions, as described throughout the Series.

The implementation gap (Fig. 1) arises across multiple levels, from lack of population-wide policies to shortcomings in individual patient care. At the population level, governments have been slow to recognize CLD as a major public health problem and implement effective interventions targeting its key drivers, such as alcohol, physical activity, or processed foods. Strong evidence supports alcohol pricing policies, marketing restrictions, and availability controls, yet their adoption across Europe remains inconsistent and often undermined by competing commercial interests.3 Similarly, food environments that promote obesity and metabolic dysfunction remain largely unregulated, contributing to the increasing burden of MASLD.1 Further contributing to this issue, liver disease is frequently absent from national non-communicable disease (NCD) strategies, reflecting a lack of political will and prioritization despite its substantial health and economic impact. At the clinical level, although multiple guidelines exist to guide clinicians in hepatology, endocrinology, and primary care settings,10, 11, 12, 13 their implementation and uptake remain uneven. Furthermore, healthcare systems across Europe, similarly to the United States, remain fragmented, with care often delivered within specialty silos. As shown in Fig. 1, MASLD, ALD, and viral hepatitis are frequently managed independently and by different specialists, despite shared risk factors and overlapping patient populations. This fragmentation limits opportunities for early detection, coordinated management, and comprehensive risk reduction. Integrated liver health initiatives will be needed to reverse current patient and physician unawareness about CLDs and facilitate implementation of current guidelines. This is especially true for MASLD where liver disease is often associated with obesity and diabetes and requires life-long intervention. Increased awareness as well as improved non-invasive tests (NITs) and risk stratification algorithms have transformed the diagnostic landscape of MASLD in recent years. Yet meaningful progress will hinge more than anything on implementation of effective models of care and of healthcare policies to overcome systemic and structural barriers that enable long-term change, as carefully outlined by Lazarus et al.1 and Pugliese et al.2 in The Lancet Regional Health – Europe Series. Improving implementation will require structured multidisciplinary models of care, automated laboratory and electronic health record testing pathways, improved availability of second-line tests, community-based digital care (i.e., telemedicine for rural and low-resource areas), and reimbursement structures that facilitate patient care.14 Elimination of viral hepatitis would require rapid scale-up of active case-finding and treatment expansion and require political and financial commitment across Europe. The outlined priorities for policy reform, stigma reduction, and youth-focused prevention hold substantial promise to reduce alcohol-related harms. Embedding liver health within broader NCD strategies now would ensure sustained visibility and integration into preventive care.

Taken together, our current care for CLDs suffers from multi-level implementation gaps that span across patient/physician awareness, diagnostic tools, and healthcare policy and delivery. Importantly, many of these challenges are not unique to Europe. Similar barriers exist in the United States and everywhere else. However, differences in healthcare financing and public health infrastructure may influence how these barriers manifest and are addressed. Integrated frameworks that combine population-level policies, early detection strategies, and multidisciplinary care substantially reduce liver-related morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Ultimately, bridging this implementation gap – the last mile of translation from evidence to practice – may have the largest near-term impact on the burden of CLD in Europe and the rest of the World.
