Longitudinal Changes in the Serum Pepsinogen I/II Ratio With Progression of Gastric Atrophy
Authors: Soie Chung, Jung Kim, Ji Hyun Song, Jooyoung Lee, Sun Young Yang, Eun Young Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study evaluated longitudinal changes in the serum pepsinogen (PG) I/II ratio according to endoscopic gastric mucosal atrophy.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 21,265 participants who underwent endoscopy during health checkups between 2015 and 2023. Longitudinal changes in PG I/II ratio were examined in 2217 subjects with ≥ 2 endoscopies using linear mixed‐effects models. Serum PG I, PG II, and Helicobacter pylori IgG were measured. The AG severity was graded endoscopically according to Kimura–Takemoto classification.ResultsThe PG I/II ratio decreased stepwise with advancing Kimura‐Takemoto stage. In longitudinal analyses of 2217 participants with repeated endoscopy, within‐subject declines in the PG I/II ratio paralleled progression of endoscopic atrophy, with greater reductions in more advanced stages (No atrophy: reference; C2: −0.383; C3: −1.150; O1: −1.412; O2: −2.101; O3: −2.007; all p < 0.001).ConclusionsWithin‐subject declines in the PG I/II ratio were observed with progression of gastric atrophy. Serial measurement of the PG I/II ratio may be useful for monitoring the progression of gastric mucosal atrophy, particularly in patients for whom repeated endoscopic evaluation is not feasible.Keywords: atrophic gastritis, Helicobacter pylori, kimura‐takemoto classification, pepsinogen

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a major global health burden and is closely associated with chronic Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection [1, 2]. According to Correa's cascade, chronic HP‐induced inflammation can progress to atrophic gastritis (AG), intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and eventually gastric cancer [3]. Therefore, early identification and monitoring of premalignant gastric lesions are essential for effective prevention and timely intervention.

Endoscopic assessment is the standard method for evaluating gastric mucosal atrophy. The Kimura–Takemoto (K–T) classification is widely used in clinical practice and has been shown to correlate with histological staging systems. However, repeated endoscopic examinations are invasive and may not always be feasible in large‐scale screening programs or in certain clinical settings.

Recently, serum pepsinogen (PG) levels are widely used to predict atrophic gastritis (AG) [4]. Serum PG I and II are precursors of the protease pepsin, secreted into the gastric lumen: PG I exclusively by chief and mucous neck cells in the fundic glands, and PG II by both fundic and pyloric glands [5]. In advanced atrophic gastritis, PG I levels decline while PG II increases, leading to a reduced PG I/II ratio. Blood PG measurements have therefore been introduced as non‐invasive biomarkers for atrophic gastritis, with common cutoffs of PG I ≤ 70 ng/mL and/or PG I/II ≤ 3 [6].

Importantly, data on longitudinal changes in PG I/II ratio in relation to endoscopic atrophy progression are limited. Understanding intra‐individual changes over time may provide additional clinical value beyond cross‐sectional assessment. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate longitudinal changes in serum PG I/II ratio according to endoscopic gastric mucosal atrophy in a large health screening cohort.

Methods

Study Population

The inclusion criteria for this retrospective single‐center study were participants who underwent comprehensive health examinations, including esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and serum PG between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2023. The EGD results were retrospectively reviewed. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. H‐2401‐095‐1503), with a waiver of informed consent granted for this retrospective study. Participants with a history of gastric cancer, a tumor detected on endoscopic biopsy, or an insufficient biopsy specimen were excluded from the study. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 21,265 participants were finally enrolled in the study (Figure 1). Among them, 2217 participants underwent endoscopy more than twice and were included in the longitudinal analysis.

Flowchart of participant inclusion and exclusion. MALT, mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue.

Endoscopic Assessment

The grade of gastric mucosal atrophy was estimated according to the location of the endoscopic atrophic border (EAB), which was recognized by distinctive differences in the visible capillary network, color, and height of the gastric mucosa in a non‐overdistended stomach, as reported by Kimura and Takemoto [7] and Liu et al. [8]. The extent of AG was categorized into six grades (C1 to O3) according to the K‐T classification, which includes two main types: closed type (C‐type, C1–C3) and open type (O‐type, O1–O3). In C1, the EAB is limited to the antrum; in C2, it is at the angle or lower corpus; in C3, it is at the upper corpus. The EAB is parallel to the vertical axis of the stomach and is on the lesser curvature in O1, anterior and posterior walls in O2, and greater curvature in O3.

All EGD procedures were performed by senior endoscopists with at least 5 years of professional experience. At Seoul National University Hospital Gangnam Center, all endoscopists have undergone training to objectively and accurately assess the severity of endoscopic AG, and significant efforts have been made to minimize inter‐observer variability, reaching kappa values of 0.76–0.78 for concordance in the diagnosis of AG [9]. All upper gastrointestinal endoscopies were performed using a conventional white‐light video endoscope (GIF‐H260/H290 series endoscopes; Olympus Optical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) as part of a comprehensive health check‐up program to assess AG levels based on the K‐T classification. The severe AG was defined as K‐T classification stages O2 and O3, as in previous studies [10, 11].

Serum Pepsinogen and HP Immunoglobulin (IgG) Tests

Blood samples were collected after a 12 fast. Serum samples were collected in tubes containing a clot activator and serum gel separator. Centrifugation was performed at 3000 rpm for 10 min within 30 min. All analyses were performed immediately after centrifugation. The PG I and PG II levels were measured [12] on the Architect C16000 (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA) using latex agglutination turbidimetric immunoassay reagent (HiSens Pepsinogen‐I LTIA and HiSens Pepsinogen‐II LTIA, HBI, Anyang, Korea), which was validated in previous studies [12, 13]. HP IgG levels were analyzed using the Immulite 2000 (Siemens AG, Erlangen, Germany).

HP Assessment

For additional analyses, HP positivity was defined as a positive result on Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [13C]‐urea breath test (UBT), or rapid urease test (RUT). In addition, subjects without documented HP diagnostic test results but diagnosed with HP gastritis based on histologic findings from diagnostic endoscopic biopsy were also classified as HP positive.

PCR was performed using dual‐priming oligonucleotide‐based multiplex PCR (Seeplex H. pylori ClaR ACE Detection PCR, Seegene Inc., Seoul, Korea).

Eradication status was primarily assessed using the 13C‐UBT, with RUT used in a limited number of cases as an alternative. For 13C‐UBT, breath samples were collected before and 20 min after administration of 13C‐urea and analyzed using an infrared spectrometer. A delta 13CO2 value of ≥ 2.5‰ was considered positive according to the manufacturer's instructions. For RUT, a single biopsy specimen from the greater curvature side of the lower body was obtained (CLO test; Delta West, Bentley, WA, Australia), and a typical color change within 24 h was regarded as positive.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA) and MedCalc version 20.211 (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium). All statistical outcomes were based on two‐sided tests, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For continuous variables, data were expressed as the median and interquartile range when the distribution was not normal according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (p < 0.05); otherwise, they were expressed as the mean ± SD. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Pepsinogen (PG) levels were compared among AG groups according to the K‐T classification using ANOVA. A linear mixed‐effects model was applied to evaluate longitudinal changes in the PG I/II ratio among subjects with ≥ 2 endoscopic examinations. For additional analyses, the diagnostic performance of the PG I/II ratio was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). The optimal cut‐off values for AG and severe AG were determined by maximizing Youden's index. In addition, a mixed model was used to assess whether changes in the PG I/II ratio before and after eradication therapy differed according to the outcome of HP eradication.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The baseline clinical characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. The mean age (SD) of the participants was 54.9 (9.6) years. Of the 21,265 participants, 1926 (9.1%) had a family history of gastric cancer and 4599 (21.6%) had a history of HP eradication. A total of 11,489 (54.0%) participants had AG and were categorized according to the K‐T classification as C1: 1566 (7.4%), C2: 3805 (17.9%), C3: 3166 (14.9%), O1: 1904 (9.0%), O2: 742 (3.5%), and O3: 306 (1.4%). A total of 1289 participants had undergone HP PCR, biopsy, RUT testing, with 72.2% positive results. Among the 21,265 participants, 8503 (40.0%) had positive results for the HP IgG test. The mean (SD) values for PG I, PG II, and PG I/II ratio were 59.6 (33.5), 13.8 (9.4), and 4.9 (1.9) μg/L, respectively.

Longitudinal Changes

Among the study participants, 2217 underwent upper endoscopy at least twice during the observation period. The median follow‐up duration was 4.0 years (range, 0.6–9.1 years). Of these, endoscopy was performed six times in 3, five times in 10, four times in 37, and three times in 244 individuals. The distribution of the PG I/II ratio according to endoscopic findings in subjects with ≥ 2 examinations is presented in Table S2. Overall, the table demonstrates a decrease of the PG I/II ratio according to the atrophic severity across repeated endoscopic examinations. However, a slight increase in mean PG I/II ratios of O1, O2, and O3 at the second and third examinations compared to those of the first examination was observed. To rule out the effect of HP eradication on the PG I/II ratio, we selected only participants without a prior eradication history and presented their mean PG I/II ratio values according to the K‐T classification in Table S3–1. In this subgroup, a consistent decrease of PG I/II ratio according to K‐T stage across repeated endoscopic examinations was observed. We also performed subgroup analysis in the patients with HP IgG positivity at the first endoscopic examination and who underwent HP eradication therapy between the first and second endoscopies (Table S3–2). The mean PG I/II ratios showed a tendency of increase in second or third examinations within the same AG grade.

In the cohort of 2217 subjects with ≥ 2 endoscopic examinations, a linear mixed‐effects model was used to evaluate within‐subject changes in the PG I/II ratio as atrophic gastritis progressed, with “no atrophy” set as the reference category (Table 3). Changes in endoscopic staging were significantly correlated with alterations in the PG I/II ratio (p < 0.001). Progression to stages C2, C3, O1, O2, and O3 was associated with mean decreases in the PG I/II ratio of 0.383, 1.150, 1.412, 2.101, and 2.007, respectively (all p < 0.001). Although progression from no atrophy to stage C1 corresponded to a mean decrease of 0.101 in the PG I/II ratio, this change did not reach statistical significance. As the disease progresses from stages C1–O2 to C2–O3, the least squares mean PG I/II ratio decreases, as shown in (Table S4).

Diagnostic Performance of the PG I/II Ratio According to H. pylori ‐Related Factors

The diagnostic performance of the PG I/II ratio varied according to HP‐related factors. Stratification by prior HP eradication history provided more consistent discrimination for both AG and severe AG than stratification by HP IgG serostatus (Table 4 and Figure S3). In particular, participants without prior HP eradication showed higher AUCs than the overall cohort. In contrast, analyses based on cross‐sectional HP status (infected, exposed, and naïve) showed less consistent performance, indicating heterogeneity across groups (Table S5 and Figure S4).

Discussion

In our study, we demonstrated that the serum PG I/II ratio decreases progressively with worsening gastric mucosal atrophy and reflects longitudinal intra‐individual changes. These findings support the role of PG I/II ratio as a non‐invasive biomarker for monitoring atrophic progression.

By analyzing 2217 subjects who underwent at least two endoscopies, we showed that serial measurements of PG I/II ratio accurately reflect mucosal deterioration over time. Thus, in repeat‐screened individuals, comparing successive serum PG I/II ratios may provide a convenient, noninvasive means to closely monitor gastric mucosal changes. To our knowledge, few studies have evaluated longitudinal intra‐individual changes in the PG I/II ratio in relation to endoscopic progression of gastric mucosal atrophy. Previous studies have mainly focused on examining the relationship between PG concentrations and the histological degree of atrophic gastritis at single time points, without following patients over extended periods. Our research addresses this significant knowledge gap by showing that regular measurement of the PG I/II ratio may function as a useful biological marker for monitoring how gastric mucosal atrophy naturally evolves over time. From a clinical perspective, these results suggest that repeated PG I/II measurements may serve as a practical, non‐invasive approach to monitor gastric mucosal status, particularly in screening settings where repeated endoscopy is not always feasible.

HP related factors, including eradication history, may influence PG dynamics and affect diagnostic interpretation. As eradication can alter gastric mucosal physiology, consideration of treatment history is important when interpreting PG I/II ratio. These analyses were therefore included as additional analyses. In particular, classification based on cross‐sectional HP status may introduce biological heterogeneity, as individuals with prior eradication and past exposure are combined, leading to reduced clinical interpretability. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution. In the additional analyses, the PG I/II ratio increased in patients with successful HP eradication compared with those with eradication failure, consistent with previous report [14]. Given that some studies have shown histologic or endoscopic improvement of gastric atrophy after successful eradication [15, 16], it is plausible that the PG I/II ratio may serve as a prognostic marker reflecting mucosal recovery, warranting further investigation linking serologic changes with mucosal atrophy. The PG I/II ratio also showed higher diagnostic performance in participants without prior eradication history, whereas stratification based on HP IgG seropositivity provided no additional benefit, likely due to the persistence of IgG positivity after eradication [17]. Notably, the diagnostic performance markedly decreased in the HP exposed group (AUC 0.531), likely reflecting substantial biological heterogeneity introduced by combining individuals with prior eradication and those with past exposure. These findings highlight the limitations of cross‐sectional HP classification and underscore the importance of considering eradication history in PG‐based assessment.

This study has several limitations. First, gastric mucosal atrophy was assessed using endoscopic findings based on the K–T classification rather than histological confirmation. Although this may be considered a limitation, previous studies have demonstrated good correlation between endoscopic and histological atrophy [18, 19], and the Kimura–Takemoto classification provides a practical and reproducible approach in large‐scale screening settings [20]. In addition, a study conducted at our institution showed that targeted training in the K‐T classification can substantially improve interobserver agreement, even among physicians with varying levels of endoscopic experience [9]. Second, as this was a retrospective study conducted in a real‐world health check‐up cohort, diagnostic methods for HP infection and eradication confirmation were not fully standardized. Although eradication status was predominantly assessed using urea breath test, some variability in testing methods may have introduced potential misclassification. Third, pepsinogen levels may be influenced by demographic and lifestyle factors, and therefore caution is needed when extrapolating these findings to other populations. Finally, this study focused on AG and did not evaluate the relationship between pepsinogen levels and other premalignant or neoplastic conditions, such as intestinal metaplasia or gastric neoplasia, which warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, the serum PG I/II ratio reflects longitudinal changes in gastric mucosal atrophy and may serve as a practical non‐invasive biomarker for monitoring atrophic progression. These findings suggest that the PG I/II ratio may serve not only as a diagnostic biomarker but also as a dynamic indicator of gastric mucosal change over time. In screening settings, serial measurement of the PG I/II ratio could provide a practical, non‐invasive approach for monitoring atrophic progression and identifying individuals who may benefit from closer endoscopic surveillance. This longitudinal perspective may help refine risk stratification strategies for gastric cancer prevention.
