Impact of Clostridium butyricum HADIG-CB003 dietary supplementation on the gut microbiota of Kunming mice
Authors: Yilin Pang, Xianxian Xu, Daizhi Li, Yue Ge, Jiamin Liang, Xiaojun Xiong, Shaohuai Wen, Xiaokai Li, Yueqiu Wang, Xiongying Li, Guoqiang Tan, Yuanshan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClostridium butyricum is a key anaerobic probiotic due to its ability to produce butyric acid and form spores. In this study, four strains were isolated from pig feces using tryptose sulfite cycloserine (TSC) plates and specific primers sequentially. The optimal strain, C. butyricum HADIG-CB003, identified via gas chromatography and enzyme tests, produced 1.54 g/L of butyric acid, with cellulase and amylase activities of 20.88 ± 1.27 U/mL and 16.04 ± 0.46 U/mL, respectively. In reinforced clostridial medium, it produced 2.48 L of gas per liter, with hydrogen making up 59.68% (1.48 L). The adhesion rate to porcine intestinal epithelial cells (IEC-J2) was 9.16% in vitro. Twelve 3-week-old male Kunming mice were split into two groups and fed a basal diet with or without 1 × 1010 CFU (colony-forming unit)/kg HADIG-CB003 for 2 weeks. The HADIG-CB003 diet did not significantly affect body weight or the feed-to-gain ratio but reduced harmful bacteria like Salmonella enterica and increased beneficial gut bacteria, elevating the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio (p < 0.05). Moreover, the HADIG-CB003 diet significantly increased fecal butyric acid levels and decreased microbial species richness (Chao1 index) in the cecal contents of the mice. The study shows that a TSC double-layer plate, PCR screening, and 16S rRNA sequencing effectively screen C. butyricum in pig feces. Introduction

Over the past two decades years, as the field of animal husbandry has transitioned into the “post-antibiotic era,” numerous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of probiotics as a substitute for feed antibiotics (Popov et al. 2021; Wickramasuriya et al. 2021). Probiotics, which are live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host when administered in adequate amounts (Hill et al. 2014), have garnered attention. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacteria, which dominate the normal microflora of the animal digestive tracts, have been the earliest and most commonly used probiotics in animal production (Liu et al. 2020). However, these probiotics are sensitive to temperature and have limited shelf-life at ambient conditions (Klu et al. 2012; Perdana et al. 2014). For instance, the survival rate of Lactobacillus plantarum WCFS1 falls below 95% when subjected to high-temperature spray drying (outlet temperatures > 70 ℃) (Perdana et al. 2014). In contrast, Clostridium butyricum exhibits greater resistance to stress factors such as high temperature, stomach acid, and bile salts due to its ability to form endophytic spores (Li et al. 2021). Additionally, C. butyricum promotes the growth of beneficial gut probiotics like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium while inhibiting pathogenic bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and colibacillosis-inducing Escherichia coli (Imase et al. 2008; Yang et al. 2012). This bacterium also secretes various digestive enzymes that produce beneficial substances like butyric acid and hydrogen through the anaerobic fermentation of carbohydrates such as starch and cellulose (Lo et al. 2022; Lu et al. 2022; Stoeva et al. 2021; Zou et al. 2021).

There is existing research on the effects of supplemental C. butyricum on the productivity of weaned piglets (Liang et al. 2021; Tang 2024; Zhang et al. 2018), broilers (Abdel-Latif et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2024), and aged laying hens (Huang et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2020). For example, Zhang et al. (2018) found that dietary C. butyricum led to a shift in the fecal microbiota composition and increased the population of acetic acid-producing bacteria in weaned piglets. Additionally, Kong et al. (2011) demonstrated that administering C. butyricum orally at concentrations of either 1 × 106 or 1 × 108 CFU (colony-forming unit)/mouse/day for 14 days led to a significant increase in populations of Bifidobacterium spp. and Lactobacillus spp. in the ceca of male BALB/c mice. The ratios of these beneficial bacteria to Clostridium perfringens also significantly increased, compared to the control group (p < 0.01). However, there are few reports on applying autonomously isolated C. butyricum in animal experiments, especially in mice.

The purpose of this study is to summarize a set of rapid screening methods for excellent characters of C. butyricum, and evaluated the impact of C. butyricum supplementation on the growth performance and intestinal flora of Kunming (KM) mice over a 2-week period, so as to provide support for how soon feeding C. butyricum can begin to have beneficial effects on hosts. We isolated four strains of C. butyricum from fattening pig manure using TSC (tryptose sulfite cycloserine) double-layer plates, specific identification primers, and 16S rRNA sequencing. The most effective strain, HADIG-CB003, was selected based on enzyme activity tests and acid production capabilities. Subsequently, we assessed the effects of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 on the growth performance, serum parameters, and intestinal microbial community of KM mice. In summary, these findings suggest that C. butyricum exerts beneficial effect on the host as a dietary supplement by modulating the intestinal microbiota.

Materials and methods

Preliminary screening of C. butyricum from pig manure

Five grams of fresh feces from healthy finishing pigs (Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire) was weighed and then diluted tenfold to concentrations of 10−5, 10−6, and 10−7 using sterile saline. Next, 500 µL of these fecal dilutions were dispensed into the base of Petri dishes. The TSC submedium (Harmon et al. 1971) was subsequently poured over the fecal dilutions. Once the submedium solidified, a layer of upper TSC medium containing gelatin (20 g/L) was added, and the dish was sealed with its lid. Following the complete solidification of the media, the dish was incubated at 37 ℃ overnight to enable the counting of black colonies. C. butyricum JCM 1391, also known as ATCC 19398 and NCTC 7423 (Shin et al. 2020), was obtained from the Guangdong Microbial Culture Collection Center (GDMCC) in Guangzhou, China. C. butyricum JCM 1391 was employed as a positive control strain due to its initial discovery and application in Japan, as well as its commercialization as a probiotic for human health in the Chinese market.

Specific colony PCR for secondary screening of C. butyricum

Large black colonies on the TSC double-layer plates were picked using a micropipette tip and inoculated into reinforced clostridial medium (RCM), filling over 90% of the liquid medium volume. These samples were then incubated at 37 °C overnight under aerobic conditions. Strains exhibiting small white bubbles on the medium surface and displaying microbial characteristics consistent with Gram staining were further examined using an oil-immersion microscope. Only strains with morphological features indicative of C. butyricum were selected for further analysis. For PCR amplification, the specific primer set CBu21F/CBu1023R (Yi et al. 2020) was used, as outlined in Supplementary Table S1. Briefly, the 20 µL PCR amplification system included 1 µL of genomic DNA templates (10 ng), 10 µL of 2 × Taq Master Mix (Dye Plus) from Vazyme Biotechnology Corporation (Nanjing, P.R. China), 0.4 µL of each primer (20 µM), and 8.2 µL of double-distilled water (ddH2O). The reaction started with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 53 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Strains yielding the correct PCR product size (1022 bp) were then diluted tenfold and streaked onto RCM plates for further culturing until pure colonies of C. butyricum were isolated. The RCM medium was composed of 10 g/L beef extract, 1 g/L soluble starch, 3 g/L yeast extract, 5 g/L glucose, 10 g/L peptones, 3 g/L sodium acetate, 5 g/L sodium chloride, 0.5 g/L L-cysteine hydrochloride, and 20 g/L agar (added for solid medium). This medium was sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C for 15 min (Gong et al. 2021).

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

Molecular identification was conducted through 16S rRNA sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from C. butyricum using a FastPure Bacteria DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The 16S rRNA gene was then amplified with the primer set 27F/1492R, as specified in Supplementary Table S1 (Reuben et al. 2019). The PCR amplification was carried out in a 20 µL reaction mixture that included 10 ng of genomic DNA and 20 µM of the primer. The reaction was initially subjected to a pretreatment at 96 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 35 amplification cycles. Each cycle consisted of three steps: denaturation at 96 ℃ for 20 s, annealing at 62 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 90 s. The purified PCR products were then sequenced by BGI Corporation (Shenzhen, China) and analyzed using the BLAST program available from NCBI (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). DNA analysis was conducted using Vector NTI software (Rahnama et al. 2016), and the phylogenetic tree was generated using the neighbor-joining algorithm in the MEGA7 software package (Kumar et al. 2016).

Comparison of acid production ability

The concentrations of butyric and acetic acid in the fermentation broth (prepared in RCM medium) from each strain were quantified using gas chromatography. A 1 mL aliquot of C. butyricum fermentation broth was centrifuged in 2-mL Eppendorf tubes at 8000 rpm for 5 min. Afterward, 900 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube, to which 100 µL of 25% metaphosphoric acid was added. The mixture was allowed to react at room temperature for 3–4 h, followed by a second centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was then filtered using syringe filters with a pore size of 45 µm and injected into an Agilent 6890 N gas chromatograph. This system was equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID), a high-resolution gas chromatography column (DB-FFAP, 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm), and an N10149 automatic liquid sampler (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The amount of acetic acid, and butyric acid in each sample was quantified using external calibration curves.

Comparison of extracellular carboxymethyl cellulase activity

Cellulase activity was assessed using 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS), following the method outlined by Hussain (2017). Various strains of C. butyricum were cultured in RCM medium and incubated at 37 ℃ for 12–14 h. Following incubation, the cultures were centrifuged at 4 ℃ and 5000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of supernatant from each C. butyricum strain was combined with 1.5 mL of deionized water and 3 mL of carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), solubilized in a sodium acetate-acetic acid buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 4.5). This mixture was then incubated at 40 ℃ for 30 min. To inactivate the enzymes, the samples were immediately boiled in a water bath for 10 min. Three milliliters of DNS reagent was added, and the mixture was boiled for another 10 min before being rapidly cooled in water. An additional 17 mL of deionized water was added, and the mixture was thoroughly mixed. The absorbance was measured at 550 nm. Standard glucose curves were prepared using a range of glucose concentrations (0.008, 0.016, 0.024, 0.032, 0.04, and 0.048 mg/mL). Initially, a specific volume of gradient-concentration glucose was diluted to 2 mL with deionized water. Next, 3 mL of DNS solution was added to each tube, followed by shaking. The tubes were then boiled in a water bath for 10 min and cooled to room temperature. The subsequent steps of the experimental procedure remained consistent with those described earlier. Finally, the cellulase production by C. butyricum was estimated using a glucose calibration curve. One unit (U) of cellulase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 mg of glucose every 30 min under the specified reaction conditions.

Comparison of extracellular amylase activity

The activated seed cultures from five isolates of C. butyricum were inoculated into fresh, sterile RCM medium at a 5% concentration and incubated at 37 ℃ for 12 h. The OD600 value of each fermentation broth sample was then measured. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4 ℃, and the clear supernatant was collected as the enzyme solution. For the substrate solution, 1 mL of 1% soluble starch was mixed with 0.25 mL of pH 6.0 citrate-sodium citrate buffer (0.1 mol/L). This substrate buffer was preheated at 50 ℃ for 5 min before adding 0.2 mL of the enzyme solution. The mixture was then incubated at 50 ℃ for 10 min. To terminate the reaction, 0.1 mL of HCl was added immediately. Next, 0.35 mL of DNS staining solution (Thongprajukaew et al. 2014) was added, consisting of 10 g/L 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid, 16 g/L NaOH, and 300 g/L potassium sodium tartrate tetrahydrate. This was boiled for 5 min, rapidly cooled and mixed with 2.1 mL of deionized water. For the control group, an equal volume of deionized water replaced the enzyme solution, with three replicates per group. The absorbance of each sample was measured using a Hitachi U3900 UV–vis spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at 540 nm. Amylase activity for each strain was calculated based on a maltose standard curve, ranging from 0.125 to 1 mg/mL. One unit of amylase activity (U) was defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 mg of maltose every 10 min under the given conditions.

Additionally, 100 µL of each fermentation broth was pipetted onto the center of an RCM agar plate containing 0.5% soluble starch. After the medium absorbed the fermentation broth, plates were placed in an anaerobic chamber and incubated at 37 ℃ for 48 h. Subsequently, the surface was coated with a layer of iodine solution, and the diameter of the resulting transparent zone was measured (Kim et al. 2011).

Assessing the hydrogen production performance of C. butyricum using gas chromatography

First, the gas produced by C. butyricum was collected in a gas sampling bag, and its volume was measured using a glass syringe. The composition of the gas was analyzed using a gas chromatograph (GC456) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) (Scion, Goes, China). Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas with a flow rate of 30 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 50 ℃, the sample inlet at 120 ℃, and the detector outlet at 175 ℃. The standard gas mixture used for calibration included methane (74.600%), ethylene (0.993%), oxygen (0.501%), carbon dioxide (5.210%), ethane (1.020%), acetylene (0.101%), hydrogen (1.000%), and the remaining balance was nitrogen.

Medium for inducing spore formation in C. butyricum

A liquid fermentation medium was formulated to induce spore formation in HADIG-CB003. This medium consisted of 2% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 0.3% lactose, 1% corn starch, 0.1% L-cysteine hydrochloride, 0.3% K2HPO4, 0.15% KH2PO4, 0.2% sodium citrate, 0.04% MgSO4•7H2O, and 0.1% Tween-80, adjusted to a pH of 7.0. In a 1 L jar, 30 mL of seed medium (RCM) containing HADIG-CB003 was inoculated into 1 L of this prepared medium. After 12 h of anaerobic fermentation at 37 ℃, an additional 0.035% of MnSO4•H2O was introduced. The pH was then adjusted to 6.5 using a NaOH solution, and the mixture was incubated for another 12 h at 37 ℃.

Experimental study on the tolerance of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 to artificial gastric fluid, artificial intestinal fluid, and bile salts

To prepare artificial gastric juice, dilute 1 mol/mL hydrochloric acid with water, adjust the pH to 2.5, add 1 g of pepsin per 100 mL, mix, and filter sterilize using a 0.2-μm filter. For artificial intestinal liquid, dissolve 6.8 g of KH2PO4 in water, adjust the pH to 6.8 with 0.4% NaOH, add 1 g of trypsin per 100 mL, mix, and filter sterilize with a 0.2-μm filter.

Artificial gastric juice tolerance was studied by centrifuging late-growth culture, washing it with PBS, and re-suspending it. The culture was then inoculated at 107 spores/mL in artificial gastric juice, mixed, and incubated at 37 ℃. Viable bacteria counts were taken every hour for 3 h, with three replicates per sample; Study on artificial gastric fluid tolerance: Bacteria exposed to artificial gastric fluid were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, washed twice with PBS, re-suspended, and inoculated with 107 spores/mL. The experimental procedure mirrored the study on artificial gastric fluid tolerance; Tolerance test to bile salts: Porcine bile salt was incorporated into RCM liquid medium at concentrations of NC, 0.10%, 0.20%, 0.30%, and 0.40%, then sterilized at 121 ℃ for 15 min. Strains were inoculated at 5% and cultured anaerobically, with bacterial growth observed in triplicate for each sample.

Investigation of the adhesion rate of C. butyricum to porcine intestinal epithelial cells

Pig intestinal epithelial cells (IPEC-J2) were obtained from the BeNa Culture Collection in Xinyang, China. They were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM-H) with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The cell lines were verified to be mycoplasma-free prior to use. The adhesion rates of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 and C. butyricum JCM 1391 to IPEC-J2 cells were measured using the TSC double-layers plate. IPEC-J2 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/mL and incubated. Once cell density reached 80%, the medium was changed to DMEM-H without antibiotics. The experimental group received 500 µL of 1 × 108 CFU/mL C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 solution per well, while the control group received the same volume and concentration of C. butyricum JCM 1391. Each group had three wells and was cultured for 3 h. Post-incubation, the medium was discarded, and wells were washed three times with DMEM to remove non-adhered bacteria. Then, 100 µL of 1% Triton X-100 was added to each well and mixed for 8 min at room temperature to release C. butyricum. The lysis was stopped by adding 0.9 mL of PBS and mixing thoroughly. The suspension was diluted 10,000 times, and 100 µL of the diluted suspension was used to count viable C. butyricum on a TSC double agar plate. The adhesion rate (%) of C. butyricum was calculated as follows: (post-Triton concentration/pre-Triton concentration) × 100.

Quantification of total live bacteria and spores

The total counts of live bacteria and spores were determined using the TSC double-layer plate method, as previously described (Li et al. 2020). The specific procedure is outlined in the section titled “Preliminary screening of C. butyricum from pig manure.”

Experimental design for animal studies

Twelve male KM mice, averaging 13 ± 1 g and aged 3 weeks, were obtained from Hunan Sileike Jingda Co. (Changsha, China). These mice were housed in plastic-bottomed cages with meshed stainless-steel tops under a controlled 12-h light–dark cycle. The ambient temperature was maintained between 25 and 27 ℃, and humidity levels were controlled at 40–60%. Mice had unrestricted access to food and water. The animals were divided into two groups, each comprising six mice: a normal control (NC) group, which was given only tap water and a standard basal diet for 12 days, and a C. butyricum (CB) group, which received a basal diet enriched with HADIG-CB003 at a concentration of 1 × 1010 CFU/kg feed for the same duration.

To prepare the C. butyricum-supplemented base diet, the standard mouse food was first crushed. Then, spores of HADIG-CB003 and its fermentation broth were mixed in to achieve a spore concentration of 1 × 1010 CFU/kg. This mixture was moistened with 10% sterile water and blended thoroughly. For the NC group, an equivalent volume of sterile water was added to the basal diet. The mixed feed was then shaped into compact strips of about 10 cm using a 20-mL sterile syringe. These strips were dried overnight in an oven set at 65 ℃ and stored at room temperature until fed to the mice.

Throughout the experimental period, each mouse’s body weight, food consumption, and water intake were recorded at 4-day intervals. After a 12-h fast, fresh feces were collected from all mice to measure butyrate and acetate via gas chromatography. Concurrently, blood was collected from 12 mice through eye extraction. The blood samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, then chilled at 4 °C overnight to separate the serum. The next day, serum was isolated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min and stored at − 20 °C. Serum parameters were tested by the Hunan Anshengmei Drug Research Institute Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China). Cecal contents were collected and sent to Chengdu Rhonin Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China) for quantitative microbiological analysis.

Analysis of cecal microorganism diversity

Total DNA was extracted from cecal contents using a Zymo Research BIOMICS DNA Microprep Kit (ZYMO RESEARCH, Orange County, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. For the PCR amplification of full-length bacterial 16S rRNA genes, the 8F-1492R primer set was utilized. The 25 µL reaction mixture included 0.5 µL of MegaFi Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Applied Biological Materials, Vancouver, Canada), 5 µL of 5 × MegaFi Buffer, 0.5 µL of 10 mM dNTPs, 0.5 µM of each primer, and 20 ng of template DNA. The thermocycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 ℃ for 30 s, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 ℃ for 5 s, annealing at 54 ℃ for 14 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 45 s; this was concluded with a final extension at 72 ℃ for 2 min. The PCR amplicons were purified using the Zymoclean Gel Recovery Kit (ZYMO RESEARCH, Orange County, CA, USA). Subsequently, the final sequencing library was prepared using the Nanopore 16S Barcoding Kit, Ligation Sequencing Kit, and Flow Cell Priming Kit, and sequenced on a Nanopore GridION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, England) (Curry et al. 2022; Cuscó et al. 2018; Santos et al. 2020).

The generated data were subjected to bioinformatic analyses (Cuscó et al. 2018). A community feature table, similar to an OTU (operational taxonomic units) table, was constructed at the species level using an R script available on the associated GitHub repository (https://github.com/vesperlight2/Kraken2OTUtable). Alpha diversity was evaluated through Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity indices. Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on species-level OTUs and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on unweighted UniFrac distances. Differences in microbiota composition between the two groups were visualized using the ggplot2 R package (Moraes et al. 2020). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was conducted to identify significant differences in microbiota structure between the groups (Tian et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021b, 2018).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Student’s t test and one-way ANOVA, utilizing GraphPad Prism 5 software (GraphPad, San Diego, USA) for Windows. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Isolation and identification of C. butyricum from pig manure

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, we successfully isolated black colonies of Clostridium from pig manure. Approximately ten individual black colonies were selected and cultured in RCM medium. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. S1, strains that developed small, white, dense bubbles on the surface of the medium following overnight incubation were chosen for further study. Four such cultures were selected for additional analysis based on microscopic examination. Specific primers were employed for the PCR identification of the five isolates, which included the positive control strain C. butyricum JCM 1391, as seen in Fig. 1B. The PCR products generated had a size of approximately 1022 base pairs (bp), confirming that the four isolates were C. butyricum. These were subsequently designated as HADIG-CB001-004. Further, agarose gel electrophoresis analysis, as shown in Fig. 1C, indicated that all five isolates produced positive bands with sizes of approximately 1500 bp. These PCR products were submitted to BGI for strain identification, and BLAST sequencing results corroborated that all five isolates were indeed C. butyricum. The morphology of the single colony, strain, and phylogenetic tree of isolate HADIG-CB003 is presented in Fig. 1D–F.

Examination of the biological characteristics of C. butyricum

As indicated in Supplementary Fig. S2B-F and Supplementary Table S2, the levels of butyric acid and acetic acid in C. butyricum’s fermentation broth were approximately 1.6 g/L and 3.2 g/L, respectively. Among the five C. butyricum isolates, strain HADIG-CB001 exhibited the highest acid production; however, no significant differences in acid-producing capabilities were noted among the remaining isolates. Figure 2 A reveals that the extracellular cellulase activity in HADIG-CB003’s fermentation broth was significantly stronger than that of the control strain C. butyricum JCM 1391 (p < 0.01) and other C. butyricum strains. Moreover, HADIG-CB003 displayed the highest extracellular amylase activity among the five C. butyricum isolates (Fig. 2B), registering 16.19 ± 0.43 U/mL. This activity was further confirmed by the formation of a clear hydrolytic zone on a starch agar plate (Fig. 3A). As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. S3 and Fig. 3B–C, C. butyricum produced gas at a volume approximately 2.5 times that of the medium. Gas chromatography detected that the composition of the total gas volume included 56.97% hydrogen, 0.77% oxygen, and 6.56% carbon dioxide (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that HADIG-CB003 has the capability to produce hydrogen, similar to other C. butyricum strains, while maintaining extremely low levels of oxygen in the culture system. Additionally, the spore count for HADIG-CB003 reached up to 2.3 × 108 CFU/mL, with a spore formation rate of 63.2%. A microscopic image of C. butyricum spores is displayed in Fig. 3D. Based on these characteristics, HADIG-CB003 was selected as the strain with the most optimal biological properties in this study. Moreover, C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 has been stored in China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) with the storage number CGMCC No. 27853.

C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 and its spores exhibit significant stress resistance

As illustrated in Fig. 4, a 3-h incubation period in artificial gastric and intestinal juices did not significantly impact the spore survival rate of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003. Furthermore, as detailed in Supplementary Table S3, a 0.1% bile salt concentration exerted minimal influence on the growth of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003. In contrast, a 0.2% bile salt concentration markedly inhibited its growth, while concentrations of 0.3% and 0.4% severely suppressed the growth of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003. Additionally, as demonstrated in Supplementary Table S4, the adhesion rate of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 to IEC-J2 cells is 9.16%. In comparison, the adhesion rate of the control strain C. butyricum JCM 1391 to IEC-J2 cells is 10.64%. Statistical analysis shows no significant difference between the adhesion rates, indicating that C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 from pig feces might effectively colonize pig intestines and act as a probiotic.

Supplementing KM mice with C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 raised fecal butyric acid and blood glucose levels while decreasing cecal microbiota diversity

Figure 5 indicates that there are no significant differences in body weight and feed-to-growth ratio (F/G) between the KM mice supplemented with CB and those in NC group. Figure 6 A shows that the CB group had a 102.63% increase in fecal butyric acid compared to the NC group (p < 0.05), with no significant change in acetic acid levels. Serum analysis revealed that only GLU (glucose) levels differed between the groups, with the CB group showing a 53.80% increase (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6D). Other liver function markers (ALP, alkaline phosphatase; ALT, alanine aminotransferase; AST, aspartate aminotransferase; ALB, albumin) and blood lipid markers (TG, triglycerides; TC, total cholesterol) showed no differences. After generating the OTU abundance matrix, we analyzed both alpha and beta diversities of the intestinal microbiota in the two groups of KM mice. Various indices such as Chao1, PD, Shannon, and Simpson were employed to measure species abundance and diversity. As illustrated in Fig. 7A–D, the Chao1 index in the C. butyricum-supplemented group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.05).

Furthermore, the Bray–Curtis distance and the Unweighted UniFrac algorithm were utilized for principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) statistics. Dietary supplementation of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 alters cecal microbiota composition

To ascertain the abundance information for each group at various classification levels, we used the OTU abundance table (Supplementary Table S5) and annotated classification information table (Supplementary Table S6). We then converted this data for each classification level—such as phylum, genus, and species—to generate a table displaying the relative abundance of each sample at each level (Supplementary Table S7). Furthermore, to visually represent the average abundance of the most abundant taxa (top 20) for each sample group, we averaged the data for the groups and presented it in a bar chart (Fig. 8A–C).

The microbiota composition changed after CB supplementation in KM mice. A–C The relative abundance of the phylum (A), genus (B), and species (C) of cecal microbiota present in KM mouse of both the NC and CB groups. D Effects of CB supplementation on Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio of gut microbiota in KM mice compared with NC group. E, F Changes in relative abundance of the top 40 in genera and species taxa. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01

After supplementing the basal diet of KM mice with C. butyricum for 12 days, we observed changes in bacterial abundance at the phylum level when compared to the NC group. Specifically, the abundance of Proteobacteria in the CB group increased by nearly 36%, whereas the abundances of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Tenericutes decreased by approximately 34%, 70%, and 72%, respectively (Fig. 8A). These results indicate that supplemental C. butyricum led to an increase in the abundance of Proteobacteria and a decrease in the abundance of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Tenericutes at the phylum level. Intriguingly, Fig. 8D reveals that compared to the NC group, supplementation with C. butyricum significantly reduced the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio (p < 0.05).

At the genus level, compared to NC group, the CB group showed a significant increase in the percentage of Pseudomonas—up by nearly 317% (p < 0.01, Fig. 8B). Specifically, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas peaked at 44% in the cecum of KM mice supplemented with C. butyricum. Conversely, the abundances of Sphingomonas, Helicobacter, Bacteroides, Escherichia, Blautia, Mycoplasma, Lachnoclostridium, and Salmonella decreased by approximately 63%, 55%, 77%, 90%, 55%, 70%, 47%, and 72%, respectively. Other genera experienced a decrease of nearly 13%.

In the cecum of KM mice receiving a C. butyricum supplement, Pseudomonas syringae had the highest abundance at 9%, followed by Pseudomonas chlororaphis and Sphingomonas hengshuiensis. In contrast, in the NC group, the abundances of P. syringae, P. chlororaphis, Pseudomonas putida, and Pseudomonas sp. CC6-YY-74 increased by nearly 324% (p < 0.01), 313% (p < 0.01), 257% (p < 0.01), and 298% (p < 0.01), respectively. Abundances of S. hengshuiensis, E. coli, Helicobacter typhlonius, Helicobacter hepaticus, Sphingomonas sp. LK11, Bacteroides vulgatus, Salmonella enterica, and Blautia sp. N6H1-15 dropped by approximately 69%, 90%, 59%, 51%, 41%, 91%, 72%, and 66%, while other genera increased by nearly 6.4%. Moreover, over 90% of bacterial species did not receive focused analysis and warrant further study, as shown in Fig. 8C.

To provide a more comprehensive view of species groups (top 40) and the data distribution characteristics within the sample groups, we conducted a community composition analysis using a boxplot, as depicted in Fig. 8E. At the genus level, we observed significant changes in the CB group relative to the NC group. Specifically, the relative abundances of Parabacteroides, Staphylococcus, and Alistipes decreased significantly by about 78% (p < 0.05), 97% (p < 0.05), and 62% (p < 0.05), respectively. In contrast, the relative abundance of Variovorax increased significantly, by approximately 465% (p < 0.01). At the species level, when comparing the CB and NC groups, we found notable increases in the abundances of various Pseudomonas and Variovorax species. Specifically, the abundances of Pseudomonas protegens, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Pseudomonas fluorescens, Pseudomonas koreensis, Pseudomonas sp. M30-35, Pseudomonas stutzeri, Pseudomonas mandelii, Pseudomonas sp. K2W31S-8, and Variovorax sp. PMC12 surged significantly—by roughly 400% (p < 0.05), 324% (p < 0.05), 262% (p < 0.05), 400% (p < 0.05), 278% (p < 0.05), 350% (p < 0.05), 407% (p < 0.05), 338% (p < 0.05), and 467% (p < 0.01), respectively. Conversely, the abundance of Staphylococcus nepalensis was significantly reduced, by nearly 99% (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8F).

Discussion

TSC plates are traditionally employed for enumerating C. perfringens colonies (Adams and Mead 1980). In a similar vein, our study observed that C. butyricum formed distinct black colonies when cultured on a TSC double-layer plate. This is because Clostridium species can utilize the sulfur in sodium pyrosulfite to generate hydrogen sulfide, which subsequently reacts with ferrous ions present in ammonium ferric citrate to form black ferrous sulfide. The TSC medium also contains D-cycloserine, which effectively inhibits the growth of Gram-negative and other non-Clostridium bacteria. To generate anaerobic conditions in the sub-medium, gelatin was added to the upper layer of the TSC plate (Fig. 1A). In addition, the RCM used in this study was formulated with L-cysteine hydrochloride and trace amounts of agar to further promote anaerobic conditions. If the liquid content in the conical flask exceeded 90%, employing high-pressure steam sterilization and sealing the flask with tin foil aided in maintaining an anaerobic environment. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. S1, C. butyricum displayed turbid growth in RCM medium, due to its ability to produce hydrogen gas that accumulates as small, dense, white bubbles at the top of the medium, further aiding in air insulation, while the fermentation process of C. perfringens results in the production of carbon dioxide and is known for causing stormy fermentation (Kato et al. 2009).

Yi et al. (2020) developed a highly efficient PCR-based screening method that utilizes species-specific primers for C. butyricum. This approach showed a remarkable increase in success rate for identifying C. butyricum strains, from 0.61% using traditional trypticase sulfite neomycin (TSN) selective medium, to 81.91% (Yi et al. 2020). The primer set, CBu21F/CBu1023R, was designed to target conserved regions in C. butyricum-specific genes, enabling the specific identification of C. butyricum while differentiating it from 12 other Clostridium species (Yi et al. 2020). In the present study, this primer set (Supplementary Table S1) was employed for the secondary screening of C. butyricum. Subsequent 16S rRNA sequencing confirmed that all five of the isolated strains were indeed C. butyricum (Fig. 1B–C). Similarly, Nakanishi et al. (2005) used PCR primer sets CBISF/CBISR and B2F/CB23R to successfully distinguish C. butyricum from C. perfringens, achieving a 100% positive screening rate for the beneficial C. butyricum strains.

As indicated in Supplementary Table S2, the acid-producing capabilities of HADIG-CB003 were comparable to those of the positive control strain, C. butyricum JCM 1391. This suggests that the C. butyricum strains identified in this study may have similar probiotic potential to the JCM 1391 strain. Interestingly, the acetic acid levels in the fermentation broth were notably higher than those of butyric acid (Supplementary Table S2), which contrasts with the typical 2:1 ratio of butyric acid to acetic acid found in such broths (Zhang et al. 2009). We hypothesize that this discrepancy could be related to the 3 g/L sodium acetate content in the RCM medium. Previous research by Vandák et al. (1995) demonstrated that C. butyricum, when fermenting a medium consisting of 50 g/L whey and either 5 g/L yeast extract or 50 µg/L biotin, could yield butyric acid levels as high as 19 g/L. Additional studies by Zhang et al. (2009) reported butyric acid yields of 7.3 g/L and 20 g/L for batch and fed-batch fermentations of C. butyricum S21, respectively. Similarly, butyric acid yields of 12.25 g/L and 16.74 g/L were observed in batch and fed-batch fermentations of C. butyricum ZJUCB (Zhang et al. 2009). These findings suggest that the butyric acid-producing capacity of C. butyricum is significantly influenced by both the composition of the fermentation medium and the fermentation method employed. Therefore, evaluating the acid-producing capacity of various C. butyricum strains in different hosts is essential. Optimizing the medium for both butyric acid and spore production can provide a theoretical foundation for expanding the application potential of C. butyricum.

As indicated in Fig. 2, the strain HADIG-CB003 demonstrated superior performance in the production of extracellular cellulase and amylase. This is in line with the findings of Ye et al. (2022), who reported that the cellulase activity in a high-dose group of weaned Ira rabbits (receiving a basal diet supplemented with 1.2 × 108 CFU C. butyricum per kg) was 50.14% and 90.13% higher at 53 and 67 days, respectively, compared to the control group (p < 0.05). Similarly, Shah et al. (2022) found that the C. butyricum CBT-1 strain exhibited enzymatic activities of amylase, cellulase, and peroxidase. In batch fermentation experiments, CBT-1 yielded the highest hydrogen production rates—3.06, 2.67, and 2.46 mol/mol H2 from glucose, starch, and cellulose, respectively (Shah et al. 2022). Huang et al. (2021) also contributed to this body of research, noting that, compared to a control group of New Zealand rabbits fed a basal diet for 6 weeks, a diet enriched with 1 × 109 CFU C. butyricum per kg improved the activity of digestive enzymes, specifically amylase and trypsin, in the rabbit’s ileum. Liu et al. (2024) demonstrated that C. butyricum CBX 2021 adhesion enhanced the growth of IPEC-J2 cells. Transcriptome analysis showed this adhesion upregulated G protein-coupled receptors, suppressed the Notch signaling pathway, and reduced pro-inflammatory molecules. Given these findings, it is crucial to screen C. butyricum strains for both high butyric acid yield and enzymatic activity to assess their potential as probiotics. Additionally, as demonstrated in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S3-S4, C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 can adhere to porcine intestinal cells and withstand artificial gastric fluid, intestinal fluid, and bile salts, aligning with previous studies (Feng et al. 2025; Liang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). Thus, it shows promise for use in probiotic products.

Prior research has indicated the beneficial effects of supplementing weaned piglets with C. butyricum ZJU-F1 at a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/kg of basal diet. This supplementation notably elevated the levels of various short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyric, propionic, and acetic acids, in the piglets’ ceca. It also enhanced intestinal function and positively influenced the TLR-2-MyD88-NF-kB signaling pathway (Fu et al. 2021). Additionally, multiple studies have illustrated that C. butyricum and C. butyricum-based probiotics have a substantially positive impact on the growth performance and gut microbiota in various animals, including broilers (Abdel-Latif et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2012), crayfish (Foysal et al. 2019), weaning rex rabbits (Liu et al. 2019), and large yellow croaker (Yin et al. 2021). In our study, we also explored the potential benefits of HADIG-CB003 in vivo, finding both parallels and discrepancies when compared to the existing literature.

Similarly, the CB group showed a significantly higher fecal butyric acid content than the NC group (Fig. 6A), indicating that C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 may colonize the mouse intestines and enhance SCFAs levels. Notably, this study represents the inaugural report indicating that a 12-day treatment with C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 can significantly elevate serum glucose levels in mice, while exerting no discernible impact on TG and TC levels. This finding appears to contradict earlier studies suggesting that C. butyricum ameliorates blood glucose levels in cases of type II diabetes mellitus (Jia et al. 2017; McMurdie et al. 2022; Tayyib et al. 2024). Future research involving a larger sample size is warranted to validate these findings and further investigate their potential biological implications.

As illustrated in Fig. 7A–D, the addition of supplemental HADIG-CB003 notably decreased the species diversity of the cecal microflora in KM mice. We hypothesize that this effect may be attributable to C. butyricum’s capacity to produce butyric acid, acetic acid, and other metabolites, which exert both bacteriostatic and intestinal regulatory effects (Clarke and Morris 1976; Wang et al. 2021a). Staphylococcus nepalensis is recognized as a food-borne pathogen (Nguyen Thi Anh et al. 2022), while Bacteroides vulgatus is an opportunistic pathogen in the human intestine (Kumar and Sivaraman 2011; Mills et al. 2022). Intriguingly, our study is the first to show that supplementing with C. butyricum considerably reduces the presence of these two pathogenic bacteria in the cecum of KM mice, as evidenced in Fig. 8C and F. In alignment with prior research (Yang et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2020), our data, presented in Fig. 8, reveal that supplemental C. butyricum effectively diminishes the abundance of harmful bacteria such as E. coli, H.typhlonius, H.hepaticus, and S.enterica in the cecum of KM mice. Previous studies have also indicated that the gut microbiota in obese animals and humans exhibits a higher Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio compared to those with normal weight (Ley et al. 2006; Magne et al. 2020; Turnbaugh et al. 2006). Wang et al. (2021a, b, c) reported that, in cecal contents, CB increased the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes, and CB increased the laying performance and egg quality of Lingnan yellow-feathered breeder hens. In line with this, our results, as presented in Fig. 8D, suggest that C. butyricum supplementation can potentially promote weight gain in KM mice by increasing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio. Although Fig. 5A showed no significant difference in body weight between CB group and NC group, we speculated that it might be because, after 2 weeks of feeding, the mice had become sexually mature, their body weight no longer increased linearly. Similar to our research results, Liang et al. (2021) Showed that supplementation with C. butyricum for 21 days could significantly improve the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes in the rectum of weaned piglet but had no significant effect on the growth performance and diarrhea rate.

In summary, C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 appears to facilitate intestinal development in KM mice by colonizing their intestines and significantly elevating butyric acid concentration in the gut, thereby providing enhanced energy to colonocytes (Agarwal and Schimmel 1989) and epithelial cells in the large intestine and terminal ileum (Biagi et al. 2007). Concurrently, the marked increase in butyric acid levels, alongside a notable reduction in microbial species richness within the CB group, may be intricately linked to the decreased prevalence of pathogenic bacteria such as H.hepaticus and S.enterica in the cecal contents of KM mice. Furthermore, the secretion of amylase and cellulase by C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 contributes to improved feed utilization in KM mice. Consequently, C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 holds potential as a beneficial feed supplement for enhancing the health of KM mice and livestock. Additionally, due to the small size and short sexual maturity cycle (7 to 8 weeks) of KM mice, they present a more cost-effective model for investigating the probiotic potential of novel strains compared to the higher feeding costs associated with pigs and poultry.

Additionally, it is important to note that supplementing with C. butyricum also led to a significant increase in the abundance of the human pathogens P.aeruginosa (Jurado-Martín et al. 2021) and P.stutzeri (Lalucat et al. 2006) in the cecal contents of KM mice. Previous studies have shown extracellular products-mediated interspecific interaction between P.aeruginosa and E.coli (Yuan et al. 2021). Sun et al. (2022) reported that branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) biosynthesis pathways involve in syntrophic cooperation between Bacillus velezensis and P. stutzeri. Although interactions between these two human pathogens and C. butyricum have not been reported, it is necessary to explore whether there is a similar interaction mechanism in the future. In addition, we hypothesize that this outcome, as well as the impact of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 on the gut microbiota, may be influenced by potential confounding factors, including the dietary composition, batch variations, housing conditions, individual mouse variations, and intestinal content collection sites. For example, Weng et al. (2024) discovered that gut microbiota imbalance caused by a humid heat environment can worsen anxiety in mice by disrupting bile acid metabolism and increasing neuroinflammation, suggesting that probiotics like Lactobacillus murinus could be a potential treatment. Weldon et al. (2015) noted significant differences in microbiota diversity among mice from various locations, correlating with factors like body weight, age, body-mass index (BMI), leptin levels, and infections. Future research should explore the impact of C. butyricum HADIG-CB003 on intestinal health and growth in different animal breeds and environments.
