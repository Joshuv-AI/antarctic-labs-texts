Pull–Push Interaction between Ions and Synthetic Polymers in All-Fiber Triboelectric Nanogenerators for Wireless Healthcare Applications
Authors: Hao Duo, Yang Liu, Haitao Wang, Hiroaki Sakamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Enhancing the charge density output at the molecular scale within confined tribolayer regions is a promising strategy for optimizing triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) performance. This study explores the molecular-level enhancement of charge density in TENGs through ion–ion and polymer–ion interactions achieved by incorporating KCl into Nylon66 in varying ratios via electrospinning. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the uniform distribution of KCl both inside and on the surface of the Nylon66 nanofibers. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses revealed that KCl disrupts hydrogen bonding in Nylon66, facilitating its integration into the polymer’s molecular chains. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) demonstrated that pure Nylon66 exhibits a relatively low static potential. Within a certain range, increasing the KCl content led to a significant rise in the surface positive potential of Nylon66, thereby markedly enhancing its triboelectric performance. However, when the KCl content was excessive (Nylon66:KCl = 2400:4), KCl particles gradually accumulated on the nanofiber surface. Due to its hygroscopic nature, KCl can slowly absorb moisture from the air, which increases surface conductivity and accelerates charge dissipation, ultimately resulting in a deterioration of the triboelectric performance. The optimized KCl ratio resulted in a significant enhancement, with output voltage and current density increasing by 121.7% and 135.3%, respectively. Additionally, instead of using a conventional metal film electrode, a breathable, flexible, and antibacterial electrode was developed by coating the tribolayer surfaces with silver nanowires (AgNWs). Ultimately, the improved TENG was seamlessly integrated into wireless healthcare systems, offering an innovative communication solution for individuals with speech impairments or mobility limitations. This functionality highlights its potential as a transformative innovation for personalized and accessible healthcare solutions.

Introduction

With the continuous advancement of technology and the rapid development of the Internet of things (IoT), an increasing number of miniature sensors have been applied in fields related to point-of-care (PoC), , smart transportation, , and IoT. , These sensors are characterized by their widespread distribution and abundance. , However, traditional methods rely primarily on batteries and wired power supplies, , which entail significant labor costs and challenges associated with waste battery disposal. , Self-powered devices based on triboelectric nanogenerators (SD-TENGs) represent a promising alternative. They can be activated by irregular and low-frequency pressure, strain, and flexion, as well as actions involving minimal movement such as breathing, , finger bending, , and pulse beats. , However, the inherently low current density of triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) poses a significant challenge, , limiting their application as power sources in miniaturized, portable, implantable, wireless, and wearable electronics. Hence, achieving high current density , facilitates device miniaturization, enhances output performance, , and reduces the energy input required for sensors to produce detectable signals, an essential requirement for TENGs. Thus, developing innovative approaches to enhance the charge density of TENGs is critical to expanding their practical applications. Initial approaches to enhance the performance of TENGs focused on selecting triboelectric materials with significant differences in electronegativity, , as indicated by the triboelectric series, , and modifying the material surface through treatments such as roughening, etching, or coating. These methods aim to alter the state of the triboelectric materials and promote charge transfer at the surface. However, owing to the inherent limitations of the material surface area, the improvement in the triboelectric current density achieved by these strategies was relatively modest. Subsequent research has revealed that the essence of triboelectricity lies in charge transfer, suggesting that facilitating the redistribution and transfer of electrons can significantly enhance TENG efficiency. For instance, physical methods such as leveraging the strength and direction of external magnetic fields have been employed to modulate the charge distribution, thereby improving the charge separation efficiency and enhancing TENG performance. Cheng et al. proposed a theory suggesting that electrons are capable of transferring through hydrogen bonding interactions. , In our previous research, we demonstrated that enhanced hydrogen bonds in the interior of the fiber network structure could improve charge transport; however, the triboelectric output performance arising from ion–ion and polymer–ion interactions at the molecular level has yet to be thoroughly investigated.

Electrospinning is a widely used, simple, and versatile technique for fabricating fibrous nanomaterials with high porosity and large surface areas. , The resulting fibers can range in diameter from a few nanometers to several micrometers, making electrospinning an ideal method for the large-scale production of dielectric materials. , Materials suitable for electrospinning include synthetic polymers such as polyurethanes, nylons, and polylactic acid, as well as natural polymers such as chitosan, cellulose, and gelatin. Additionally, inorganic compounds such as titanium dioxide (TiO2), zinc oxide (ZnO), and composites such as inorganic salts with polymers, metal nanoparticles with polymers, and graphene with polymers can also be used. Among the synthetic materials, Nylon66 stands out owing not only to its exceptional durability, strength, and versatility , but also to the strong tendency to lose electrons and its hydrophobicity, which make it highly attractive as an electron-donating triboelectric material. Further, potassium chloride (KCl), a strong electrolyte, can completely dissociate into K+ and Cl– ions in solution. Under the combined influence of the electric field and frictional force during the process of electrospinning, these ions migrate and redistribute onto the surface and into the inner spaces of the nanofibers. This process results in the formation of localized charges among the molecular chains, which enhances the polarization capability of the material and promotes the electron-donating ability of Nylon66.

In this study, nanofiber mats were prepared by incorporating various concentrations of potassium chloride (KCl) into Nylon66, designed to function as an electron-donating material. Characterization through EDS, FTIR, and XRD tests confirmed the successful integration of KCl within the intermolecular spaces of Nylon66. Triboelectric performance evaluations were conducted using composites of PVDF/MWCNT, made of poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) and multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), as the electron-withdrawing counterpart. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) analysis revealed that pristine Nylon66 possesses a relatively low positive surface potential. Within an optimal range, the incorporation of a small amount of KCl markedly increased the surface positive potential, thereby enhancing the triboelectric output. This enhancement is attributed to the increased surface charge density induced by the ionic species. However, at excessive loadings (Nylon66:KCl = 2400:4), KCl particles tended to agglomerate on the nanofiber surface. Owing to its strong hygroscopicity, KCl gradually absorbs atmospheric moisture, leading to elevated surface conductivity and accelerated charge dissipation, which in turn compromises the triboelectric performance. Under optimized conditions, the nanofiber mats achieved an increase of 121.7% and 135.3% in the output voltage and current density, respectively, compared to pure Nylon66 as a dielectric material. Further, device applicability has been demonstrated by incorporating the enhanced TENG into wireless healthcare systems, offering a novel communication method for individuals with speech impairments or mobility limitations. By generating Morse code signals through simple touch or tapping, SD-TENGs enable the efficient transmission of both routine and emergency messages (Figure ). This breakthrough demonstrates its significant potential as an innovative and transformative solution for accessible and personalized healthcare applications.

Experimental Section

Materials

Nylon 66 pellets, PVDF powder (534 000 g/mol), and 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) were purchased from Meck (Osaka, Japan). N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), formic acid (FA), acetone, and potassium chloride (KCl) were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), with a diameter of 20–40 nm and a length of 1–2 μm, were procured from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). All reagents were of analytical grade and were used as received without further purification.

Nylon66/KCl Nanofiber Mats Preparation

A 5 g solution with a concentration of 6 wt % was prepared by dissolving Nylon66 pellets in a mixture of HFIP and FA, taken in a mass ratio of 9:1. Subsequently, eight additional solutions were prepared by blending pure Nylon66 with KCl in weight ratios of 2400:0, 2400:1, 2400:2, 2400:3, 2400:4, 2400:6, 2400:8, and 2400:10 (supersaturated). All the prepared solutions were stored separately at room temperature prior to electrospinning. For electrospinning, a specific amount of each polymer solution was loaded into a 6 mL syringe fitted with a stainless-steel 25 G needle. The needle was connected to a high-voltage power supply set at 12 kV. A distance of approximately 10 cm between the needle tip and the rotating collector, along with a controlled flow rate of 0.1 mL/h, was maintained throughout the process. The electrospinning process for each sample was performed continuously for 6 h, and the resulting fiber mats (with a thickness of approximately 0.8 mm) were subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 24 h to remove residual solvents.

Preparation of PVDF/MWCNT Fiber Mats

PVDF pellets (20 wt %) were dissolved in DMF at 70 °C under continuous stirring until a clear and homogeneous solution was obtained. After cooling to room temperature, acetone was added to this solution at a weight ratio of 3:7 (acetone:DMF) to optimize the volatilization characteristics of the solution. Subsequently, MWCNTs and SDS were introduced into the solvent at concentrations of 0.01 and 0.04 wt % relative to PVDF, respectively, to prevent aggregation and enhance dispersion. The mixture was then sonicated for 3 h to ensure thorough and uniform dispersion of all components, guaranteeing solution stability and homogeneity for subsequent processing. For the electrospinning process, 5 mL of the prepared solution was loaded into a syringe fitted with a 25-gauge needle and mounted onto a syringe pump at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/h. A layer of nonstick parchment paper was evenly applied to the surface of the rotating collector to facilitate easy removal of the electrospun fiber mats. The needle-to-collector distance was maintained at 10 cm, and a high voltage of 22 kV was applied to ensure efficient electrospinning.

Fabrication and Integration of Silver Nanowire (AgNW) Electrodes

High-quality AgNWs were synthesized according to our previously reported method. To utilize the fiber mat as a filtration medium, AgNWs were deposited on the surface via vacuum filtration. Specifically, 2000 μL of the as-prepared AgNW solution was diluted with absolute ethanol to a final volume of 30 mL. A 55 mm diameter filter paper was placed at the bottom of a flat-bottomed ceramic suction funnel, onto which the previously prepared fiber mat was carefully positioned. During the filtration process, the solvent was drawn into the receiving flask under vacuum, enabling the AgNWs to form a uniform and robust coating on the fiber mat surface.

Characterization and Measurements

Nanofiber mats were fabricated via electrospinning using a Nex101 device (Nex101, Japan). The morphologies of the nanofibers were studied, and the elemental distributions within the nanofibers of the samples were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-6390FMM, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; DX200, SGX SENSORTECH, Switzerland), with all samples coated with a thin layer of osmium prior to imaging. SEM images were captured under an acceleration voltage of 15 kV. The crystalline structure of the samples was examined using wide-angle X-ray diffraction (WAXD; Rigaku Ultima IV, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å) at a scan rate of 1.5°/min. Functional group analysis was performed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Nicolet-6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM; Bruker Dimension Icon, USA) was used to image the surface potential of the materials at the nanoscale and obtain insights into their electrical properties and surface charge distributions. Electrical output characterization was performed by using an oscilloscope (Analog Discovery 2, Analog Devices, Norwood, USA) integrated with a custom-designed setup that included a compressed air-driven machine to apply varying forces. Data acquisition and processing were conducted using Waveform 2015 software (DIGILENT, Pullman, USA). All electrical measurements were performed under identical circuit configurations to ensure consistency.

Results and Discussion

Superficial Morphology Characterization

Figure a illustrates that the pristine Nylon66 nanofibers exhibited smooth surfaces with a consistent diameter distribution (Figure S1a). With the incorporation of KCl, the nanofiber surfaces initially remained smooth at low KCl concentrations (Figures b–e and S2a). However, as the KCl content increases, crystalline KCl spots are observed to appear on the nanofiber surfaces (Figures f,g and S2bc). This suggests that, at lower concentrations, KCl integrates into the polymer molecular chains, enhancing the electrical conductivity of the polymer and leading to nanofibers with more uniform and reduced diameters (Figure S1b–g). Beyond a certain concentration, KCl began to accumulate on the fiber surface. SEM was also used to characterize the nanofibers of PVDF (Figure h) and PVDF/MWCNT (Figure i). Under the influence of the external electric field during electrospinning, MWCNTs generate inductive charges on their surfaces, which enhance the Coulombic forces. This increased electrostatic interaction contributed to the formation of fibers with relatively smaller average diameters (Figure S1h,i) during the electrospinning process. , Finally, the findings demonstrate the successful deposition of a uniform, high-quality AgNW layer on the fibers (Figure j), with an average diameter of 64.1 nm (Figure S1j) and no signs of aggregation. Furthermore, to assess mechanical robustness, the AgNW-loaded dielectric material was subjected to typical deformation scenarios, including bending, friction, twisting, and rolling. Subsequently, the interface between the AgNWs and the nanofiber mats was examined by using SEM, revealing no significant structural changes after deformation (Figure k,l). This stability is likely due to the relative sliding of the AgNWs during mechanical stress, which imparts fiber-like flexibility to the conductive network while maintaining strong interfacial contact with the nanofiber substrate. In addition, the uniform deposition of AgNWs ensures excellent air permeability throughout the entire structure.

Evaluation of Electron-Donating Tribolayer Structure

The FTIR spectrum (Figure a) indicates that the characteristic −OH stretching vibrations are typically observed at 3070 cm–1, whereas C–N bending vibrations and in-plane bending of N–H appear at 1270 cm–1 and 1370 cm–1, respectively. The absorption bands at 2930 cm–1, 1530 cm–1, and 1630 cm–1 correspond to the −CH2 group, N–H bending vibration, and −NHCO group, respectively. These distinct absorption features are characteristic of the molecular structure of Nylon66. , Potassium chloride, an inorganic salt without covalent bonds, does not exhibit any significant characteristic absorption peaks in the infrared spectrum. No notable changes were observed in the infrared absorption peaks before or after the addition of KCl. However, when KCl was incorporated into the molecular chain of Nylon66, a blue shift was observed in the infrared absorption peaks with increasing KCl content. This shift toward lower wavenumbers is attributed to the disruption of hydrogen bonds between the Nylon66 polymer chains caused by the presence of KCl. This conclusion was further corroborated by the XRD analysis (Figure b). Nylon66 exhibits distinct broad peaks at approximately 2θ = 20.26° and 23.40°, corresponding to the (100) and (010)/(110) planes of the α-phase, respectively. These peaks become broader upon the incorporation of KCl, indicating a disruption in the crystalline structure of Nylon66. As the KCl content increases from 2400:0 to 2400:4, a distinct diffraction peak appears at 2θ = 28.42°, corresponding to the (200) crystal plane of KCl. When the KCl content is further increased from 2400:6 to 2400:8, five additional prominent diffraction peakshighlighted within the red rectangle in Figure b and also observed in Figure cemerge at 2θ values of 40.71°, 50.32°, 58.75°, 66.36°, and 73.71°, corresponding to the (220), (222), (400), (420), and (422) crystal planes of KCl, respectively. These peaks are characteristic of the crystalline structure of the KCl nanoparticles , and are attributed to excessive KCl accumulation on the surface of the nanofibers. KPFM was used to analyze the charge distribution of the tribomaterials (Figure S4a–g). As pure Nylon66 functions as an electron-donating triboelectric material, its surface potential map reveals a localized positive surface potential. Notably, as the KCl ratio increases from 2400:0 to 2400:4, the positive surface potential demonstrates a significant, gradual increase. However, when the KCl ratio was further increased to 2400:6, the surface potential shifted to a negative value (Figure d).

Structure Evaluation of Tribolayers with Negative Charge Affinity

The XRD pattern (Figure S5a) exhibited a prominent diffraction peak at approximately 2θ = 26°, along with less intense peaks at around 2θ = 44°, 53°, and 78°, corresponding to the (002), (100), (004), and (110) planes, respectively, , characteristic of typical graphite structures. The XRD spectrum of pure PVDF (Figure S5b) demonstrates no distinct absorption peaks at 2θ = 44° and 78°. However, for the nanofiber membranes obtained by electrospinning the PVDF with MWCNT, prominent absorption peaks (Figure S5c) were observed at these positions. This indicated that the MWCNTs were successfully dispersed within the PVDF matrix. Moreover, the incorporation of MWCNTs significantly enhances the β-phase content of PVDF, as evidenced by the pronounced enhancement of the characteristic diffraction peak at 37°, which is scarcely discernible in pristine PVDF but becomes distinctly intensified in the PVDF/MWCNT composite, thereby contributing to enhanced triboelectric performance.

Each crystalline phase of PVDF exhibits distinct absorbance characteristics in the infrared region, enabling phase identification through FTIR analysis (Figure S6a). The formation of the β-phase in PVDF/MWCNT thin films was confirmed by the absorbance intensities of the bands at 840 cm–1 and 1277 cm–1, which correspond to CF2 stretching vibrations typical of the β-phase. In contrast, the peaks observed at 610 cm–1 and 761 cm–1 are associated with the α-phase of PVDF. , Owing to the limited infrared transmittance of the MWCNT samples, Raman spectroscopy is a powerful tool for characterizing modifications involving carbon-based materials. The peaks at 1348 and 1590 cm–1 of MWCNT (Figure S6b) correspond to the D-band of the sp3 defects of carbon atoms and the G-band of the sp2-bonded carbon atoms, respectively. However, for the pristine PVDF (Figure S6c), the Raman signal was relatively weak, and three distinct bands were observed. The band at approximately 610 cm–1 corresponds to C–C vibrations, while the band at 794 cm–1 is associated with CF2 vibrations, both of which are characteristic of the α-phase in PVDF. Additionally, the band around 2973 cm–1 arises from CH2 symmetric stretching, a feature commonly associated with the β-phase of PVDF. The Raman spectra of the PVDF/MWCNT composite exhibited the characteristic G-band at 1570 cm–1, an intrinsic feature of carbon nanotubes associated with vibrational modes in sp2-hybridized carbon structures, and the D-band at 1324 cm–1, indicative of structural disorder within the carbon framework. Upon incorporation of PVDF, these bands shifted to 1428 cm–1 and 1526 cm–1, respectively, signifying strong interactions between PVDF and MWCNTs. This spectral shift suggests alterations in the electronic structure and bonding environment of the MWCNTs, which are likely driven by intermolecular interactions or modifications in the carbon framework induced by PVDF.

Electric Output Performance Evaluation

The contact-separation mode was employed to assess the key performance parameters of the TENG, including the open-circuit voltage (Ov), short-circuit current (Sc), charge transfer (Ct), and output power (Op). These parameters were systematically evaluated using triboelectric materials with a working area of 3 cm × 3 cm. To ensure consistent testing conditions, the separation distance between the two triboelectric materials was fixed at 5 mm throughout the measurements. When PVDF/MWCNT was used as the electron-accepting triboelectric material, the pure Nylon66-based TENG exhibited an Ov of only 57.4 V (Figure a) and an Sc of 25.0 μA (Figure b), resulting in a calculated power density of merely 1.6 W/m2 (Figure c). Previous studies demonstrated that hydrogen bonding within polymers facilitates electron transfer. The observed results suggest that the addition of KCl disrupts the hydrogen bonding interactions within the molecular chains of Nylon66, thereby reducing the efficiency of electron transfer along the polymer. Consequently, when the mass ratio of Nylon66 to KCl was 2400:1, both the Ov and Sc values demonstrated an obvious reduction, with the calculated power density decreasing by 55.5% compared with that of pure Nylon66. However, as the KCl content increased from a mass ratio of 2400:2 to 2400:4, the KCl content of Ov, Sc, and Op exhibited a notable increase. This increase can be attributed to the ionic bonding between K+ and Cl–, which progressively replaced the hydrogen bonding interactions between the polymer chains. Due to the high electronegativity of oxygen atoms in Nylon66, K+ tends to associate with the oxygen atoms. Consequently, as KCl remains electrically neutral following solvent evaporation, the K+ ions are positioned in proximity to Cl– ions, which are, in turn, drawn closer to the nitrogen atoms. Since the nitrogen atoms in Nylon66 adopt a sp3 hybridization state, , the proximity of Cl– ions to nitrogen atoms repels the lone pair of electrons on the nitrogen atoms. As the KCl content increases, the cumulative effect of Cl– ions from different directions further displaces the lone pair electrons on the nitrogen atoms, resulting in a measurable increase in the electrical signals. The maximum output performance was achieved at a KCl content of 0.167% with an open-circuit voltage (Ov) of 127.9 V (Figure a), a short-circuit current (Sc) of 59.2 μA (Figure b), and a power density (Op) of 8.4 W/m2 (Figure c). However, further increasing the KCl content beyond this level led to a noticeable decline in triboelectric performance. Specifically, when the KCl content increased from 2400:4 to 2400:6, the values of Ov, Sc, and Op decreased by 26.8%, 20.6%, and 42.0%, respectively. This phenomenon is likely attributed to its hygroscopic properties, KCl can slowly take up moisture from the surrounding atmosphere. When characterizing the triboelectric output voltage of nylon66 nanofiber membranes containing different ratios of KCl, it was observed that the influence of environmental humidity40% (Figure S7a), 60% (Figure S7b), and 80% (Figure S7c)became significantly more pronounced when the KCl content exceeded the ratio of 2400:4 (Figure S7d). To further investigate the voltage (Figure d–f), current (Figure g–i), and power (Figure j) sensitivity of the prepared SD-TENG in response to applied frequency and force, we selected a composite of Nylon66/KCl (2400:4) as the electron-donating triboelectric layers. The performance of the materials was systematically assessed under varying frequencies (1, 2, and 3 Hz) and pressures (30, 60, 90, and 120 N). The results revealed a strong positive correlation between the applied pressure and the resulting voltage output. Higher pressures significantly enhanced the voltage generation, highlighting the TENG’s sensitivity to dynamic loading conditions. Similarly, an increase in frequency led to notable improvements in the current and charge transfer. This enhancement can be attributed to the expansion of the effective contact area between the triboelectric layers under higher pressures, which facilitates improved charge generation. Additionally, higher frequencies accelerated the charge transfer process, resulting in increased output performance. Notably, the Nylon66/KCl composite (2400:4) exhibited superior performance, achieving a maximum voltage of 322.6 V, a current density of 180.0 mA/m2, and a power density of 58.1 W/m2 at a pressure of 120 N and a frequency of 3 Hz, significantly outperforming pure Nylon66.

Practical Application

The substantial output power density of SD-TENGs demonstrates their strong potential as efficient energy sources. To highlight its practical applicability, we conducted an initial demonstration by successfully powering 124 blue LEDs (Figure a) arranged within an area of 3 × 3 cm2. In previous studies, self-powered wearable sensors based on triboelectric nanogenerators were developed, which could be integrated into wearable fabrics to enable nonverbal patients to communicate using Morse codes , by translating voltage peaks into messages. The system captures both physical and emotional states, providing critical information to caregivers, and is accessible to patients with limited mobility or cognitive functions. However, a limitation of the device lies in its reliance on wired connections to transmit the collected signals to the oscilloscope, where they are displayed, resulting in reduced convenience. To address this issue, we developed a wireless Bluetooth-based system for signal acquisition, display, and transmission (Figure b). The device is compact, with dimensions comparable to those of a standard wristwatch (Figure c), and is capable of real-time transmission and display of the three operational states of a contact-separation mode triboelectric nanogenerator (Figure d and e): static (Figure f), contact (Figure g), and separation (Figure h). Notably, the system demonstrated a signal transmission rate of 2226 B/s at a given moment.

Nonverbal patients and those with limited mobility often face considerable challenges in expressing their needs and emotions, resulting in frustration and a reduced quality of life. Traditional communication methods, including speech-synthesis devices and manually operated systems, are frequently inadequate or inaccessible to individuals with severe physical disabilities. In this study, we integrated the fabricated SD-TENG with a signal conversion and transmission device, enabling finger movements, such as light taps and firm presses, to be converted and displayed via an electronic module (Figure i). Figure S6a illustrates the software interface prior to connection, whereas Figure S8b illustrates the interface after successful integration with the electronic module (transmission speed: 2.36 KB/s). Using Morse code, as depicted in Figure j, all letters and numbers of the English alphabet can be represented through two simple pressing forces of different intensities. Accordingly, SD-TENG can be classified into three states: idle or no action (Figure k), light pressing (Figure l), and firm pressing (Figure m). When applied as a communication tool, SD-TENG effectively facilitates the accurate transmission and representation of any desired letter or number. SD-TENG serves as a multifunctional device with potential applications in healthcare. It offers an efficient communication method for individuals with speech impairments or limited mobility by enabling the generation of Morse code signals through simple touch or tapping, thereby facilitating the transmission of emergency messages or routine requests. Additionally, it can record and analyze motion or touch signals in real time, converting them into medically relevant data such as movement frequency during rehabilitation or applied pressure intensity. This capability supports remote diagnoses and monitoring by healthcare professionals. Furthermore, SD-TENG could assist in tracking and evaluating daily health parameters, including hand muscle activity and responsiveness, making it a valuable tool for disease screening and rehabilitation assessment. Moreover, it enables gesture-based control of medical devices or electronic equipment, enhancing patient autonomy and improving convenience in daily activities. These features underscore its potential as an innovative, self-powered solution for personalized healthcare and remote monitoring.

Conclusion

This study introduces an innovative strategy to enhance the performance of TENGs by leveraging the pull–push interactions between Cl– ions and nitrogen atoms in Nylon66 at the molecular scale, facilitated by sp3 hybridization. This mechanism significantly enhanced the electron-donating properties of Nylon66 at the molecular scale, leading to a remarkable improvement in triboelectric performance, with a 121.7% increase in output voltage and a 135.3% increase in current density compared to pure Nylon66. However, when the KCl content in Nylon66 surpasses a critical threshold (Nylon66:KCl = 2400:4), excessive KCl agglomerated on the nanofiber surface, where its hygroscopic nature promoted moisture adsorption, elevated surface conductivity, and expedited charge dissipation, ultimately limiting triboelectric performance. Moreover, the practical utility of this enhanced TENG was demonstrated in wireless healthcare systems by generating Morse code signals through simple touch or tapping. This application highlights its potential as a multifunctional, self-powered device capable of facilitating effective communication among individuals with speech impairments or mobility limitations.
