Post-translational modifications in the insoluble fraction of platelet-poor plasma distinguish pre-COVID-19 Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome, Long COVID, and Long COVID-POTS: a preliminary study
Authors: Renata M Booyens, Mare Vlok, Cecile Bester, Rashmin Hira, M Asad Khan, Douglas B Kell, Satish R Raj, Etheresia Pretorius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-COVID-19 Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (PC-POTS), Long COVID with initial orthostatic hypotension (LC-IOH), and Long COVID-POTS (LC-POTS) are primarily chronic post-viral conditions with debilitating symptoms, despite routine laboratory findings often being normal. We previously identified amyloidogenic fibrinaloid microclot complexes (FMCs) in the insoluble fraction of platelet-poor plasma (PPP-IF) in Long COVID, but it remains unclear whether similar abnormalities occur in PC-POTS and LC-POTS, or whether post-translational modifications (PTMs) contribute to disease mechanisms.MethodsPlatelet-poor plasma from healthy controls (n = 18), PC-POTS (n = 20), LC-IOH (n = 19), and LC-POTS (n = 18) participants underwent proteomic analysis of the PPP-IF using double trypsin digestion and data-independent LC-MS/MS. Differential protein abundance, PTMs, and amyloidogenicity were compared across groups.ResultsAlthough few proteins were significantly dysregulated, extensive disease-specific PTM alterations were identified across coagulation, immune, and metabolic pathways. LC-IOH demonstrated prominent AGE- and oxidation-related PTMs on fibrinogen subunits, particularly fibrinogen alpha chain (FIBA), resembling diabetic glycation profiles. PC-POTS showed fewer fibrinogen PTMs but marked metabolic and immune dysregulation, including oxidised apoA1 and apoB and complement-associated proteins (C3, C4A/B, IC1), IGG1, and A2MG. LC-POTS shared coagulation-related features with LC-IOH and immune-related features with PC-POTS. Many dysregulated peptides were highly amyloidogenic in silico, consistent with β-sheet-rich FMCs. PTM dysregulation was substantially greater than protein-level changes.ConclusionsThis study provides the first evidence that PTMs within the PPP-IF has the potential to distinctly differentiate PC-POTS, LC-IOH, and LC-POTS. Because PC-POTS samples predated SARS-CoV-2, their PTM signatures reflect intrinsic disease biology independent of COVID-19. PTM profiling revealed pro-coagulant fibrinogen changes in LC-IOH and LC-POTS, metabolic-oxidative dysregulation in LC-IOH and PC-POTS, and immune dysregulation in PC-POTS and LC-POTS. These abnormalities were not detectable by routine assays and may contribute to microvascular dysfunction. The findings support disease-specific biomarker development and targeted therapeutic strategies across autonomic and post-viral disorders.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03296-9.Keywords: Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome, Long COVID, Post-translational modifications, Proteomics, Coagulation

Research insights

What is known currently about this topic?

Long COVID and POTS are debilitating conditions with similar symptoms and symptom-based diagnoses. Previous proteomic studies focused on protein abundance, and the role of PTMs are unclear.

What is the key research question?

Do PTMs in PPP-insoluble fraction distinguish pre-COVID-19 POTS, Long COVID, and Long COVID POTS?

What is new?

PTMs distinguish PC-POTS, Long COVID, and LC-POTS.

Long COVID showed pro-coagulant PTMs; PC-POTS showed immune-metabolic PTMs.

PTM dysregulation explained severe symptoms despite normal routine blood tests.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

PTM profiling may enable biomarker-guided diagnosis and targeted therapy.

Background

Post Acute COVID-19 sequelae (PASC), or Long COVID, and Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) are chronic, debilitating, and primarily post-viral diseases [1]. Long COVID is characterized by persistent symptoms after acute COVID-19 infections, affecting 10–26% of cases [2, 3] across respiratory, systemic, musculoskeletal, neurological, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, psychological, and dermatological categories [2, 4]. POTS involves autonomic dysfunction and an abnormal heart rate increase (> 30 bpm) upon standing, predominantly in females (> 90%), often causing light-headedness or fainting [5–8].

Both Long COVID and POTS affect multiple physiological systems [9–11] but precise pathologies remain uncertain, and diagnoses are mostly symptom-based [12]. Despite severe symptoms, routine blood analyses often show normal protein levels, leading clinicians to treat symptoms rather than underlying causes.

In 2020, we identified dense amyloid-like deposits, called “microclots” in acute COVID-19 and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) patients, implicating coagulation dysfunction [13]. In 2021, these deposits were observed in Long COVID blood, affecting the microcirculation [14]. We now refer to these microclots as fibrinaloid microclot complexes (FMCs) [15]. The insoluble fraction of platelet-poor plasma (PPP-IF) consists of FMCs, which are aggregates of inflammatory proteins and fibrinogen (present in both their normal and amyloid states), as well as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and cellular debris. These cellular areas can be prothrombotic and can act as seeding areas for FMCs [15, 16]. Although healthy individuals may also contain FMCs within their PPP-IFs, the composition of PPP-IFs in patient groups may differ and could potentially explain severe symptoms [17] despite these pathological features being undetectable by conventional assays.

Following the COVID-19 pandemic, a positive correlation between Long COVID and POTS incidences has been observed [18–21] suggesting shared pathophysiology and raising the possibility that pre-COVID-19 POTS (PC-POTS) and Long COVID POTS (LC-POTS) may exhibit FMCs [22]. To our knowledge, the FMCs in PPP-IF of PC-POTS and LC-POTS have not yet been investigated, although it is known that many other diseases involving POTS and FMCs share comorbidities [22], and FMCs provide a mechanistically straightforward physiological explanation for POTS [22].

Previously, we conducted proteomics on FMCs in Long COVID to identify abundant proteins [23]. Systematic reviews indicate that potential biomarkers reflect multiple mechanisms, and no concise profile supports a diagnostic criterion [24]. Proteomics studies in POTS are limited, with mass-spectrometry analyses calling for further investigation of molecular mechanisms [12].

Given that conventional protein analyses fail to fully explain disease pathophysiology, we investigated post-translational modifications (PTMs) as a potential mechanism. PTMs, enzymatic or non-enzymatic, can be reversible or irreversible; oxidative and metabolic stresses often induce irreversible non-enzymatic PTMs [25]. PTMs on fibrinogen influence clot formation and degradation, contributing to pathological progression [26]. Previous studies of Long COVID PTMs focused only on soluble plasma proteins, thereby missing PTMs of proteins entrapped in the FMCs in the PPP-IF, potentially explaining severe symptoms despite normal lab values [27]. We note, too, that the proteome of FMCs differs substantially from that of normal clots, being highly enriched in amyloid(ogenic) proteins [28, 29]; the same is true for [30] the macroclots observed in [31] ischaemic stroke.

We thus hypothesized that by examining the PPP-IF, we would observe PTMs on proteins within FMCs which should reveal disease-specific molecular changes that could reflect pathology even when conventional protein levels appear normal. To test this, our cohort included healthy controls, PC-POTS, Long COVID with initial orthostatic hypotension (LC-IOH), and LC-POTS patients. The PPP-IF with FMCs was obtained by a double trypsin digest and LC-MS/MS proteomics. Analyses included protein profiling, biomarker discovery, and PTM evaluation.

Methods and materials

Methods and materials

Inclusion criteria for long COVID and healthy participants

Participants for Long COVID and healthy participants (controls) of ages between 18 and 80, male or female and who were able to sign and understand informed consent documentation were included. Participants with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test and who experience COVID-19 symptoms that lasted 12 weeks or longer after the confirmed infection were included in the Long COVID group. Participants who did not have a proven or suspected positive PCR SARS-CoV-2 test were included as controls. The status of their SARS-CoV-2 test was self-reported.

Inclusion criteria for postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome participants

Participants diagnosed with POTS within the age bracket of 18–60 years old, who were able to attend the Calgary Autonomic Research Clinic in Calgary and who are residents of Canada were included. Lastly, only participants whose sample were obtained before the COVID-19 pandemic were included in the PC-POTS group.

Exclusion criteria for all participants

Participants were excluded if they could not provide informed consent, or if they could not safely withdraw from medicine use that could make test interpretation difficult or impossible. Other factors which in the investigator’s opinion would prevent the participant from completing the protocol, including poor compliance during previous studies or an unpredictable schedule.

Diagnostic criteria

Long COVID sub-groups

Participants who met the inclusion criteria for Long COVID were further sub-divided into Long COVID patients with initial orthostatic hypotension (LC-IOH) or Long COVID with Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (LC-POTS). LC-IOH was diagnosed when a transient drop in SBP of > 40mmHg within 15s of standing, followed by recovery within 60s of standing, was observed as described in the “Consensus statement on the definition of orthostatic hypotension, neurally mediated syncope and the postural tachycardia syndrome” [32]. LC-POTS were diagnosed when a HR increase > 30 bpm within 5-10 min of standing in addition to chronic symptoms were observed.

POTS

Physician diagnosis of POTS as defined by the Canadian Cardiovascular Society Consensus Statement [33] and sustained, excessive orthostatic tachycardia ≥ 30 bpm within 10 min standing, absence of orthostatic hypotension (> 20/10 mmHg decrease) within 3 min standing and chronic orthostatic symptoms that improve with recumbence.

Ethics and informed consent

Human samples used in this research were obtained from the University of Calgary where they had been collected under approvals from The Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (CHREB), University of Calgary (REB21-1188 and REB15-2434).

The transfer of samples from the University of Calgary to the University of Stellenbosch was conducted under a formal Material Transfer Agreement (MTA) (Contract number: S009247). The MTA governed the conditions of sample transfer, use, storage, and confidentiality, and confirmed that all materials were provided in accordance with the original ethics approval. All samples were received in de-identified form and were used solely for the purposes described in this study. No new samples were collected specifically for this study.

Sample collection

Samples were drawn by phlebotomists and were centrifuge within 30 min of the blood draw at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and frozen at -80 °C within 30 min after centrifugation. Samples underwent 2 freeze-thaw cycles i.e. samples were thawed for a second time when proteomics experiments were conducted.

Sample preparation

A 19× dilution of platelet-poor plasma (PPP) was prepared using 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate (AmBic). AmBic is a volatile buffer that does not form crystals during the drying process, which is often observed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and it functions at the optimal pH − 7.8–8.2. The solution was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and the remaining sample was subjected to a double trypsin digestion.

Firstly, the more soluble fraction was digested using a low concentration of trypsin at a ratio of 1:5000 protease: protein concentration (final trypsin concentration 0.574 ng/µL). This was followed by overnight incubation at room temperature. The next day, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min.

This was followed by a reduction step. The supernatant (92 µL) was removed, and the remaining 8 µL along with the pellet was incubated with ~ 5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) (denaturing buffer; pH 8). The samples were incubated in a heating block at 60 °C for 45 min, after which they were cooled to room temperature. S-methyl methanethiosulfonate (MMTS) was added to a final concentration of 10 mM to block free cysteine residues and prevent disulfide bond reformation.

The second digestion was performed at a 1:50 protease: protein ratio (final trypsin concentration of 6 ng/µL), in 40mM TEAB with 10% methanol (MeOH) as a denaturing solvent. This mixture was incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. To precipitate the trypsin, trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added to a final concentration of 1%. The mixture was briefly vortexed and then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was removed, dried, and used for peptide quantification as a standardization step prior to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

Sample analysis on the EvoSep TimsTOF Pro-2

Samples were analyzed by data-independent acquisition liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (DIA LC-MS/MS) using an EvoSep One LC system coupled to a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer equipped with trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS). Samples were diluted in 5% formic acid and loaded onto EvoTip Pure tips (Evosep ApS, EV2011) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, then stored in 0.1% formic acid until analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed using standard EvoSep gradients of 100, 60, 30, and 15 samples per day (SPD). Mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). Separation was performed using EvoSep Performance columns (8 cm × 150 μm, 1.5 μm C18 packing) for the 100 and 60 SPD gradients and EvoSep Performance columns (15 cm × 150 μm, 1.5 μm C18 packing) for the 30 and 15 SPD gradients. The columns were coupled to a Bruker timsTOF Pro 2 via a 20 μm silica emitter (Bruker CaptiveSpray ZDV Sprayer 20) and maintained at 40 °C using a Bruker Column Toaster. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in diaPASEF mode. Raw Bruker files were processed using FragPipe v24.0 incorporating MSFragger v4.0, diaTracer v2.2.1, and DIA-NN v2.0.2.

Statistical analyses

A spectral library was generated during the Fragpipe analysis as part of the DIANN workflow with 1% false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff. This library.tsv file was imported into Skyline. The database search was performed with a precursor charge range of 1–4, while fragment ions were considered at a maximum charge state of 1. Trypsin was specified as the proteolytic enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages per peptide. The precursor mass tolerance was set to ± 15 ppm for the initial search, with a refined (true) tolerance of ± 20 ppm, and the fragment ion mass tolerance was set to ± 20 ppm. Spectral matching was conducted using b- and y-type fragment ions, requiring a minimum of four matched fragment ions for peptide-spectrum match (PSM) reporting, with at least two matched fragments required for inclusion in statistical modelling. For spectral preprocessing, the top 150 most intense peaks per spectrum were retained, with a minimum requirement of 15 peaks per spectrum and an intensity threshold set at 1% of the base peak.

Group comparisons were performed within Skyline, and adjusted p-values (Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons) as well as fold changes were extracted. Statistically significant peptides (adjusted p-value < 0.05) identified in the initial analysis were used to generate a list of proteins. All peptides of the selected proteins were subsequently validated by confirming that at least four fragment ions co-eluted within the same retention time window. Following validation, protein- and peptide-level comparisons were conducted for the established protein list. Fold changes between groups were calculated, and log₂ fold change (log₂FC) values were derived. Adjusted p-values were transformed to –log₁₀(p-value) for volcano plot visualization. Significance thresholds were set at |log₂FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05, with features exceeding both thresholds classified as significantly differentially abundant. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.14.2 with the SciPy library. Data visualization was conducted using the Matplotlib and Seaborn packages.

Proteomic analysis of the PPP-IF revealed minimal protein differences

Proteins that were dysregulated in at least one disease group relative to controls were included in comparisons between disease groups. Only three proteins showed dysregulation at the protein concentration level (Fig. 2). ZA2G was downregulated and MYH9 was upregulated in LC-IOH compared to controls. Additionally, ZA2G was downregulated in LC-IOH when compared to PC-POTS and LC-POTS (i.e. ZA2G is higher in PC-POTS and LC-POTS than in LC-IOH). IC1 was downregulated in PC-POTS relative to controls. There is extensive PTM dysregulation within the PPP-IF

The proteomics analysis which included PTMs revealed 29 unique base peptides which showed significant differences between controls and at least 1 disease group and 58.62% of the base peptides were amyloidogenic according to Amylogram (results in Supplementary Table S3.1 and S3.2), i.e. had AmyloGram scores greater than 0.5. Furthermore, these 29 base peptides originating from 16 proteins, included 64 uniquely modified peptides and revealed multiple significant differences between groups (Fig. 3; Table 4). Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether the peptides with PTM of proteins within the PPP-IF distinguish PC-POTS, LC-IOH and LC-POTS at the molecular level. The PC-POTS samples were collected before the COVID-19 pandemic, enabling us to define the baseline molecular signatures of classic POTS entirely independent of SARS-CoV-2 exposure. The LC-POTS group were all diagnosed with POTS after an acute COVID-19 infection. The pre-pandemic POTS cohort therefore provides a true reference for classical POTS biology, allowing us to distinguish molecular features that originate from established POTS mechanisms from those newly introduced or amplified by Long COVID in individuals who later developed POTS symptoms. The composition of the PPP-IF was achieved with proteomic analysis and revealed that widespread dysregulation of PTMs far exceeded changes observed at the level of protein abundance. This suggests that PTMs, rather than protein concentration, more accurately reflect the underlying disease biology.

Coagulation-related PTMs and FMC structural biology

Several fibrinogen peptides displayed disease-associated PTM patterns, underscoring the central role of coagulation proteins in PPP-IF composition. In LC-IOH, increased advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) modifications on FIBA peptides align with known glycation patterns observed in diabetes [35]. Furthermore, AGEs result in the formation of covalent cross-links, which may help explain the robust, fibrinolysis-resistant nature of FMCs within PPP-IF in this group [36]. Conversely, PC-POTS showed reduced modification of selected FIBG peptides, which may reflect structural shielding of these sites once fibrinogen is incorporated into compact FMCs [37] or it may be the age differences between the cohorts with the PC-POTS group being significantly younger than the controls. Oxidation, deamination and pyroglutamate (PYD) formation of fibrinogen subunits could collectively suggest altered clot architecture across diseases, with implications for fibrinolysis, clot permeability, and microvascular obstruction, which was also seen in the LC-POTS group. The presence of amyloidogenic peptides in multiple fibrinogen regions supports the concept that FMCs arise from misfolded, β-sheet-rich aggregates rather than classical thrombin-driven fibrin [38].

At the protein level, MYH9 is upregulated in LC-IOH alongside peptides of ITA2B. These results are consistent with PTM changes indicative of dysregulated anticoagulant function and could allude to altered platelet–fibrinogen interactions [39, 40]. The latter could also apply to the PC-POTS group as dysregulation of ANT3 peptides was observed within that group. Collectively, these findings possibly indicate that the coagulation system is particularly disrupted in LC-IOH and LC-POTS (Fig. 4), with FMC-associated PTMs potentially amplifying microvascular dysfunction.

Immune and complement system dysregulation

PTMs in proteins involved in complement activation and innate immunity has the potential to further differentiate the disease groups. C3, C4A/B and IGG1 peptides displayed distinct PTM profiles in PC-POTS compared to controls, suggesting altered complement turnover and immune activation [41]. Downregulation of IC1 in PC-POTS parallels patterns seen in complement/contact activation disorders such as hereditary angioedema, which share clinical overlap with PC-POTS, including hypotension, autonomic instability and inflammatory symptoms. Additionally, the complement system plays a central role in mast cell activation and allergic inflammation [42], and several clinical studies have reported substantial overlap between mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) and POTS [43].

A2MG showed multiple oxidation- and deamination-based PTMs, particularly in PC-POTS and LC-POTS. Given A2MG’s role in trapping proteases and binding cytokines, these modifications could alter inflammatory signalling [44, 45]. The pattern observed for ITIH4 (reduced oxidised forms in PC-POTS despite its identity as an acute-phase reactant) may indicate an oxidative environment that selectively preserves functional ITIH4, potentially contributing to sustained inflammatory signalling [46]. Collectively, these findings could suggest distinct immune-modulatory landscapes across diseases, especially in PC-POTS and LC-POTS groups.

Metabolic pathway dysregulation in PC-POTS and LC-POTS

PTMs in lipid-handling proteins were prominent in PC-POTS and LC-POTS. Oxidised and deaminated variants of apoA1 and apoB were consistently upregulated in PC-POTS relative to both controls and LC-IOH. This pattern resembles PTM signatures observed in T2DM, where oxidised apolipoproteins contribute to vascular dysfunction and altered lipid metabolism [47, 48]. ZA2G downregulation in PC-POTS and LC-IOH aligns with insulin resistance pathways and potentially supports a metabolic contribution to symptoms [49, 50]. These findings could indicate that PC-POTS is characterized by a pronounced metabolic-oxidative PTM profile. Moreover, because the metabolic dysregulation observed in LC-IOH - specifically AGE- and oxidation-mediated modifications of fibrinogen - mirrors that seen in T2DM, the LC-IOH group likewise exhibits dysregulation of metabolic pathways (Fig. 4). Although two participants had pre-existing type 2 diabetes, these individuals were retained in the analysis as diabetes represents a biologically relevant metabolic comorbidity that may act as a predisposing or interacting factor in post-acute disease states. Exclusion of such cases could therefore introduce bias by removing clinically meaningful heterogeneity inherent to the LC-IOH cohort. Importantly, the observed metabolic signature was consistent across the group and was not driven by these individuals. This was confirmed with a repeat of the analysis where they were indeed removed from the cohort.

Cross-disease integration and mechanistic interpretation

Synthesising these data, LC-IOH appears to be predominantly characterised by PTM dysregulation of coagulation proteins, particularly fibrinogen, with downstream effects on both coagulation and metabolic pathways. In contrast, both PC-POTS and LC-POTS exhibit more pronounced PTM alterations within immune pathways; PC-POTS shows secondary involvement of metabolic modifications, whereas LC-POTS demonstrates secondary dysregulation of coagulation pathways. The increase in AGE-modified FIBA peptides in LC-IOH mirrors glycation patterns in diabetes; a convergence with notable clinical relevance, given that metformin reduces the risk of developing Long COVID [51]. The downregulation of FIBG modifications in PC-POTS suggests structural differences in FMC architecture that may shield specific fibrinogen sites from modification, although the age difference between the cohorts could also be contributing factor. The high amyloidogenicity of many dysregulated peptides further reinforces the nature of FMCs as aggregated, amyloid-prone complexes. Importantly, the differential modification of individual peptides within the same protein indicates site-specific PTM non-enzymatic modifications rather than uniform protein-level changes, offering a potential molecular explanation for why routine soluble plasma assays often fail to capture disease pathology.

Clinical relevance and implications for biomarker development

Samples from LC-IOH patients exhibited widespread PTM alterations in coagulation-related proteins, particularly AGE- and oxidation-based modifications of fibrinogen subunits, suggesting a strong pro-coagulant signature, whereas PC-POTS presented a predominantly metabolic-oxidative PTM pattern affecting apolipoproteins and pointing to metabolic dysregulation. Secondarily, in PC-POTS it affects the immune system through IGG1 and complement systems proteins. Interestingly, both conditions share features commonly associated with diabetes, such as protein glycation and oxidative stress. LC-POTS shares features with LC-IOH in the coagulation pathway, and with PC-POTS in immunity (Fig. 4). Together, these findings highlight PTMs within the PPP-IF as a mechanistically rich and clinically relevant domain of investigation. By analysing the PPP-IF, we have uncovered biochemical perturbations invisible to standard blood tests, offering a potential explanation for persistent and debilitating symptoms despite “normal” laboratory values. PTM signatures, especially those involving fibrinogen, complement proteins, and apolipoproteins, may hold utility as diagnostic or stratification biomarkers, and the distinct PPP-IF associated PTM patterns observed across LC-IOH, PC-POTS and LC-POTS could inform disease-specific therapeutic strategies, including interventions targeting glycation pathways, oxidative stress, or complement activation.

Limitations and future directions

The PPP samples underwent two freeze-thaw cycles which could induce PTM’s and protein degradation, however this limitation was shared equally by all samples which was subjected to identical conditions. This study focused exclusively on the PPP-IF; parallel analysis of soluble proteins would clarify how PTMs partition between fractions. Larger cohorts are needed to validate disease-specific PTM signatures and to correlate molecular findings with symptom severity. Furthermore, the entire cohort are from Canadian residents who visited a single clinic and therefore the generalization of the findings might be limited. The use of Amylogram remains an in silico predictive tool and the results obtain from the model should be used with caution, it still provides a useful framework and potentially aid in disease and FMC characterisation. Functional assays are required to determine how individual PTMs affect protein activity, fibrinogen structure, or complement regulation. Including a T2DM cohort in future studies will be essential, given the strong overlap between LC-IOH and diabetic PTM patterns. Future work could include a data-dependant analysis supplementary to the data-independent analysis and a focus on the functional implications of specific PTM’s in relation to the protein and the modification site of the protein.

Conclusion

This study provides the first direct evidence that PTMs within FMC rich PPP-IF has the potential to distinctly characterize PC-POTS, LC-IOH, and LC-POTS. Since the PC-POTS samples predate the COVID-19 pandemic, the PTM patterns represent intrinsic POTS features rather than SARS-CoV-2 consequences, enabling the contributions of classical POTS biology and Long COVID dysregulation to be disentangled (Fig. 5).

A schematic figure summarizing the proposed mechanistic relationship between PTMs, fibrinaloid microclot complexes (FMCs), coagulation, immunity, and metabolic physiology phenotypes

PTM profiling reveals distinct yet overlapping biochemical signatures invisible to standard blood tests: LC-IOH exhibits pro-coagulant fibrinogen modifications with metabolic-like dysregulation. PC-POTS and LC-POTS shows predominantly immune-oxidative disruptions, with PC-POTS also showing metabolic dysregulation and LC-POTS include oxidation-modifications effecting coagulation. These PTM landscapes highlight potential avenues for biomarker-based diagnosis, patient stratification, and therapeutic strategies targeting glycation, oxidative stress, and complement pathways.

The consistent presence of amyloidogenic peptides across groups underscores FMCs within PPP-IF as β-sheet-rich, fibrinolysis-resistant aggregates contributing to microvascular dysfunction. Minimal protein-abundance differences alongside substantial PTM dysregulation suggest a possible mechanistic explanation for severe symptoms despite normal routine lab findings.

Overall, these findings offer a unifying framework and the first provable hypothesis for shared and divergent mechanisms in post-viral and dysautonomic conditions, establishing a novel and foundational avenue for biomarker-research, which could lead to biomarker-driven diagnostics and personalized therapeutics across the spectrum of autonomic and post-viral disorders.
