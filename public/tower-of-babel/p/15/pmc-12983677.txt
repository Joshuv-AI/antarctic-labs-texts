Tai Chi interventions for older adults living in aged care homes in Sri Lanka: a study protocol for a single-arm pretest-posttest feasibility study
Authors: Sarath Rathnayake, Indrajith Liyanage, Jeewanthika Ekanayaka, Esther Liyanage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe institutionalisation of older adults has become increasingly common in Sri Lanka due to recent socioeconomic changes. Tai Chi is a traditional Chinese martial art that provides numerous physical and psychological health benefits. Despite its proven effectiveness, Tai Chi is a relatively new intervention in Sri Lanka, with no local research on its feasibility and efficacy among older adults. This study aims to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of Tai Chi on physical function and fall risk, lung function, depression, pain and health-related quality of life (HRQOL) in older adults residing in aged care homes in Sri Lanka.Methods and analysisA single-arm pretest-posttest feasibility study will be conducted among older adults aged 60 and over (n=40) living in aged care homes in Kandy District, Sri Lanka. A certified Tai Chi instructor will deliver two 60 min sessions of the Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention programme per week over a 12-week period in aged care home settings. Acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, adaptation, integration, expansion and study limitations will be assessed to determine feasibility. Baseline and post-12-week Tai Chi programme outcomes will be assessed, focusing on physical function and fall risk, lung function, pain, depression and HRQOL. Additionally, field notes will be taken during the intervention, and a post-intervention follow-up group discussion will be conducted. Paired t-tests will be used to assess the effectiveness of the Tai Chi programme. A narrative synthesis will be used to analyse qualitative data.Ethics and disseminationThe Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka, approved this study (No.: 2024/EC/34). Written informed consent will be obtained from each participant before data collection. The findings will be presented at national and international research conferences and published in a reputed journal.Trial registration numberThis study was registered with the Sri Lanka Trial Registration (SLCTR/2025/025) at https://slctr.lk/trials/slctr-2025-025 (date: 26 June 2025).Keywords: Aged, Feasibility Studies, Health Services for the Aged, Nursing Homes, Psychosocial Intervention, Quality of Life

Introduction

Sri Lanka is experiencing rapid population ageing and is among the fastest-ageing countries in the developing world, particularly in South Asia. According to the most recent census data, the older population in Sri Lanka is 2 524 570 (12.4%)1 and is projected to be 25% by 2040.2 The Asian Development Bank recognises Sri Lanka as ‘Growing Old Before Becoming Rich’, highlighting the current challenges faced by the country in providing care for its ageing population.3 The breakdown of traditional extended family systems, smaller family units, urbanisation and migration4 5 added additional challenges to caring for older adults within the family system, and there is a growing trend towards establishing aged care homes in the country. According to the National Secretariat of Elders, Sri Lanka,6 429 aged care homes house 9744 residents in Sri Lanka. Consequently, it is crucial to focus on enhancing their health, well-being and quality of life (QOL).

In Sri Lanka, there is no well-established care system for older adults living in aged care homes, and registered nurse services are lacking.7 Recent literature supports that current human and physical resources are inadequate to meet the demanding needs of residents in aged care homes.4 Moreover, physical and psychological health issues, including depressive symptoms, pain, sleep disorders, loneliness and falls, are more prevalent among residents,8,11 and poor QOL is significantly higher in institutionalised older adults compared with community-dwelling older adults in the country.12 Inadequate quality of care is evidenced by insufficient financial support and medications, a lack of qualified staff and poor referral systems.4 Economically underprivileged older individuals who are unmarried are more likely to become institutionalised in Sri Lanka,12 which adds additional challenges when providing care for them. Additionally, there is a limited understanding of interventions that assess the impact on QOL among older adults living in aged care homes in Sri Lanka.4 Consequently, the need for effective interventions to enhance the health, well-being and QOL of older adults living in aged care homes becomes increasingly important in the Sri Lankan context.

Tai Ji Quan, also referred to as Tai Chi, is a part of traditional Chinese Wushu (a martial art) and is practised for self-defence, holistic well-being and fitness enhancement.13 Tai Chi includes a series of individual movements connected in a slow, smooth and continuous way and is characterised by graceful, dance-like features that incorporate elements of balance, strength, flexibility, relaxation and body alignment.14 The popular Tai Chi styles are Chen, Yang, Wu, Wu/Hao and Sun. Tai Chi is highly recognised as a body-mind exercise14 that combines physical movements, meditation and deep breathing to enhance both physical and psychological health.15 16 The health effects of Tai Chi have been widely examined, and a recent umbrella review reported multidimensional benefits of Tai Chi, including physical and psychological health and QOL improvements, especially for conditions like Parkinson’s disease, fall risk, knee osteoarthritis, low back pain and cerebrovascular and cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension.17 Similarly, health effects of Tai Chi in older adults have also been established, and a recent umbrella review with re-meta-analysis reports positive outcomes in physical function, mobility, pain, psychological distress and depressive symptoms, anxiety, cognitive functions, mental speed and attention, learning ability and verbal fluency, executive function and QoL of older adults.18 Consequently, Tai Chi can effectively improve health, well-being and QOL among older adults.

Tai Chi is a relatively new intervention in Sri Lanka, and there are no reported local studies examining its feasibility or effectiveness among Sri Lankans, including older adults. Physical inactivity is prevalent among older Sri Lankans,19 20 underscoring the need for targeted physical activity programmes for older adults that focus on muscle strength, balance and pain management.19 With prevailing health problems like depression,8 20 catastrophising pain and sleep disorders,8 and loneliness,10 improving psychological health is also mandatory. According to a recent study by Wijesiri et al, loneliness is experienced as a form of coping with inner pain caused by feeling abandoned by family networks and confined to care homes.10 Consequently, loneliness-alleviating interventions are needed to improve engagement and social participation.10 With the increasing number of institutionalised older adults in Sri Lanka and the promising effects of Tai Chi as a body-mind exercise on their health and well-being, understanding the feasibility and potential benefits of Tai Chi for this population is important for improving their QOL. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of a Tai Chi intervention, with a particular focus on physical function and fall risk, depression, lung function, pain and health-related QOL (HRQOL) among older adults residing in aged care homes in Sri Lanka. To the authors’ knowledge, this will be the first interventional study conducted in Sri Lanka to employ a Tai Chi intervention for older adults. Conducting a feasibility study alongside an assessment of effectiveness will help identify the practicality of the programme, including its efficacy and implementation barriers and challenges; therefore, the findings will be valuable for planning future large-scale studies and for integrating Tai Chi interventions into aged care services in Sri Lanka.

Hypothesis

The hypothesis for the limited efficacy testing is that participation in the 12-week Tai Chi programme will reduce fall risk, improve lung function parameters, reduce pain, decrease depressive symptoms and improve HRQOL among older adults living in aged care homes in Sri Lanka.

Aim

This study aims to examine the feasibility and effectiveness of a 12-week Tai Chi programme on physical function and falls risk, pain, depression, lung function and HRQOL among older adults residing in aged care homes in the Kandy District, Sri Lanka. As this is a feasibility study, our aim is to assess the acceptability, feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of the Tai Chi intervention rather than to establish a definitive causal effect; a single-arm design was selected. This design reduces participant burden, particularly in aged care homes, as the intervention can be integrated into daily routines without imposing significant demands,21 thereby enhancing participation. Additionally, in the selected setting, most aged care homes have fewer than 20 residents, making randomisation difficult to implement.

Materials and methods

Study design

A single-arm pretest-posttest design (ie, a quasi-experimental study without a control group) will be used. This approach will help researchers to compare the selected outcomes of participants before and after the Tai Chi intervention.22 We followed the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials.23

Setting

This study will be conducted in a purposively selected aged care home/s in the Kandy District, Sri Lanka. Kandy is a district in the Central Province, one of the nine provinces in Sri Lanka. It consists of 20 aged care homes.6

Participants

In this study, older adults aged 60 and over residing in aged care homes in Kandy District who did not participate in any formal regular exercise programme during the last 6 months and agreed to participate in the Tai Chi programme twice a week for 12 weeks will be included. Residents who are severely depressed, already diagnosed with dementia or neurodegenerative disorders, bedridden, presenting with severe hearing or visual impairment or musculoskeletal disorders, and diagnosed with cardiovascular diseases and who cannot understand the Sinhala language will be excluded. In the intervention, instructions will be provided in Sinhala, one of the official languages in Sri Lanka. Older adults will be screened for cognitive functions using the Sinhala Version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).24 Permission will be obtained from the developers before using the Sinhala version of MMSE. The cut-off score for cognitive impairment will be taken as 17.24 A score of 17 or below will be excluded. Usually, pilot trials aim to test trial procedures and processes. As this is a pilot feasibility study, formal power calculation was not conducted. This study will assess the acceptability and feasibility of the Tai Chi intervention. As a part of the feasibility study, preliminary effectiveness will be assessed. As pilot studies are not confirmatory trials and are not necessarily hypothesis driven, formal statistical power calculations are not essential.25 As a general rule, the minimum sample required for a pilot intervention is 30.26 In this study, 40 older adults will be included to account for possible dropouts. A recent systematic review supports the use of 30–50 participants in feasibility studies.27 Participants will be screened until the required sample size is achieved.

Intervention

The intervention is based on the Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention (TCAFP) programme developed by Tai Chi for Health Institute, an evidence-based programme designed to enhance balance, mobility, strength and flexibility, while reducing pain and the risk of falls.28 TCAFP is derived from Sun-style Tai Chi, which has been adapted by eliminating high-risk movements to ensure ease of learning and safety.29 The programme emphasises breathing techniques and awareness of weight transfer, making it suitable for older adults with or without arthritis, particularly those at risk of falling.28

One of the delivery options for TCAFP is two 60 min sessions per week for 8 weeks.28 TCAFP guidelines30 recommend 8–12 weeks, and a recent systematic review reports that Tai Chi studies commonly use 60 min sessions, two to three times per week, for 8–12 weeks.31 In this study, a certified Tai Chi for health instructor will conduct 60 min two sessions of TCAFP per week for 12 weeks at aged care homes. The Stepwise Progressive Teaching Method will be used to teach the programme to older adults.30 A standardised session will be conducted for all the participants. The Tai Chi training session mainly comprises three components: warm-up exercises, Tai Chi form practice and cool-down exercises.30 Based on the TCAFP guidelines, the Tai Chi intervention in this study will be structured to include participant welcome, warm-up exercises, Tai Chi practice and cool-down sessions.30 In the first few weeks, more time will be spent on teaching the warm-up exercises. The warm-up component is designed to promote mobility and flexibility across key joints, including the neck, shoulders, spine, hip, knees and ankles. Each joint will be engaged through two distinct exercises, with each movement repeated three times to facilitate gradual adaptation and effectiveness. Any difficulties or deviations will be recorded. During the initial four sessions, a selected set of warm-up exercises will be introduced to ensure a progressive approach to movement training. If needed, participants may perform the exercises while seated or using a chair for support, ensuring accessibility and safety. The Tai Chi form practice component will include nine fundamental movements: Commencing Movement, Opening and Closing Hands, Single Whip (Right), Waving Hands in the Cloud (Right), Opening and Closing Hands, Single Whip (Left), Waving Hands in the Cloud (Left), Opening and Closing Hands, and Closing32 and these forms will be gradually introduced, at least one per week (one per two sessions), and all forms are planned to be introduced by the sixth week; however, any deviations will be recorded. The cool-down session includes relaxation, encouragement and reminders for the next session.30 A personal chair will be provided for participants during the session. They can sit while learning Tai Chi activities if they become tired or experience adverse effects. While the instructor conducts the Tai Chi programme, research staff will observe the older adults for any adverse effects, including falls or fall risk, and keep records. Any adverse effects will be reported to the aged care home’s administration, and first aid will be provided where necessary. Additionally, one research assistant will take field notes (online supplemental material A) for each session, including participants’ attendance, behaviours and any concerns related to the intervention.

Measurements

Feasibility assessment

To evaluate the feasibility, the programme’s acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, adaptation, integration, expansion, study limitations and limited efficacy testing33 34 will be considered. According to Bowen et al,33 acceptability focuses on how the intended recipients, such as target individuals and those involved in programme implementation, react to the intervention. Demand can be assessed by estimating the amount of intervention that will be used or by documenting the use of selected intervention activities. Implementation is evaluated by determining whether the intervention is executed as planned and recommended. Practicality examines how well an intervention can be delivered when resources, time or commitment are limited. Adaptation includes modifying programme contents or procedures to suit a new context or situation. Integration involves the necessary modifications to integrate a new programme into an existing infrastructure or system. Expansion examines whether an existing successful intervention will be effective with a different demographic or in a different setting. Limited-efficacy testing involves assessing the efficacy of an intervention in a limited manner. In this study, feasibility and study limitations will be identified based on participant attendance, the research staff’s notes (online supplemental material A) on each session, instructor’s feedback and follow-up group discussions with participants (online supplemental material B). Acceptability will be explored through follow-up focus group discussions that will assess participants’ satisfaction with the delivered programme. In addition, overall satisfaction will be assessed in the post-test survey using a single Likert-type question: “Overall, how satisfied are you with the Tai Chi programme?” with response options ranging from ‘highly satisfied, satisfied, neutral, dissatisfied, to highly dissatisfied’. Demand will be assessed using participants’ attendance rates and the number of adverse events reported during the intervention. Implementation will be assessed by documenting any deviations from the TCAFP protocol. Practicability will be evaluated based on participants’ progress in learning Tai Chi activities. Adaptation and integration will be assessed by recording any changes or modifications made to the programme content or procedures to suit the Sri Lankan context. Expansion will be examined by identifying and documenting potential barriers and challenges encountered, to inform the future scale-up of this intervention to other settings in Sri Lanka. To examine limited efficacy, a single-arm pretest-posttest study design will be employed.

Outcome measurements

A research team will measure outcomes at the baseline and after completion of the 12-week Tai Chi programme. In this study, physical function and fall risk, lung function, pain, depression and HRQOL will be assessed.

Physical function

Grip strength, flexibility, balance and mobility will be assessed.

Grip strength: the most commonly performed static strength test is the measurement of grip strength using a handgrip dynamometer.35 Grip strength in both hands will be measured with a digital hand dynamometer (0–90 kg; Camry, EH101).

Grip strength: the most commonly performed static strength test is the measurement of grip strength using a handgrip dynamometer.35 Grip strength in both hands will be measured with a digital hand dynamometer (0–90 kg; Camry, EH101).

Flexibility assessment: the flexibility of the lower body will be assessed by the Chair Sit and Reach Test (CSR).36 In the CSR, participants will be instructed to sit at the front edge of a chair and extend one leg straight out in front of the hip while keeping the other leg bent and slightly to the side. The measurement for the score will be recorded to the nearest half inch, representing the distance reached in either a negative or positive score. It will also note which leg will be used for the measurement. Based on the gender and age group, norm values for CSR will be taken into account.37 The upper body flexibility will be assessed using the Back Scratch test.37 The following instructions will be given.37 The test will be performed in a standing position, and older adults will be instructed to position one hand behind their head and over their shoulder, reaching as far down the centre of their back as possible. The palm should touch the body, with fingers pointing downwards. With the other arm behind their back, the palm should face outwards and fingers upwards, reaching up as far as possible to try and touch or overlap the middle fingers of both hands. A research assistant will guide the individual in aligning their fingers and measuring the distance between the tips of their middle fingers. If their fingertips touch, the score is zero. If they do not touch, the distance between them is measured (a negative score). If they overlap, the overlap distance is measured (a positive score).29 One attempt will be carried out on each side (right and left arm over), and the result will be recorded to the nearest half inch.

Fall risk: fall risk will also be evaluated using the TUG test, with time recorded via stopwatch, while scores exceeding 12 s will be used to identify older adults at increased risk of falling.40

Lung functions

Spirometry will be used to assess lung function using a standard protocol,41 performed by the same technician and using the same machine. Parameters will include forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), percentage predicted forced vital capacity (%FVC) and FEV1 percentage predicted normal values (FEV1%).

Pain

The pain level of older adults will be assessed by the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS).42 The NRS is an 11-point, unidimensional numeric scale that measures pain severity during the last 24 hours and ranges from 0 to 10 (0 being the absence of pain and 10 being the worst pain).42

Depressive symptoms

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) will be used to assess depressive symptoms.43 The 15-item shorter version of the GDS has been widely used as a time-saving method.43 44 In this study, the Sinhala version of the GDS-1535 will be used, with prior permission from the translators. The score ranges from 0 to 15. Based on a previous study conducted among older adults living in aged care homes in Sri Lanka, the cut-off scores of 0–4 will be considered normal, 5–8 will indicate mild depression, 9–11 will indicate moderate depression and 12–15 will indicate severe depression.8 20

Health-related quality of life

Euro Quality of Life Questionnaire (EQ5D)45 will be used to assess HRQOL with prior permission. This tool assesses the individual’s health in five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression.45 EQ5D consists of five questions where the users must mark their response on the Likert scale, and one question where the user must mark their response on the Visual Analogue Scale.45 Each domain of study is rated on a Likert scale, with level 1 representing ‘no problem’ and levels 2, 3, 4 and 5 representing a ‘slight problem’, a ‘moderate problem’, a ‘severe problem’ and an ‘extreme problem or unable to do’, respectively. Accordingly, each domain can be represented by a numerical value ranging from 1 to 5. The overall health status of older adults will be represented by five domains (each domain representing a numerical value (1–4)) or by a country-specific index value set identical to Sri Lanka.46 In this study, the Sinhala version of EQ5D will be used with prior permission.47

Moreover, sociodemographic data, including age, gender, marital status, education level, ethnicity, religion, months residing in an aged care home and primary income source, as well as height, weight and the presence of chronic medical problems, will be recorded. Height will be measured using a standard stadiometer. The participant will be asked to stand on the stadiometer footplate against the stadiometer ruler without shoes and hats. The person should be in an erect position with their arm hanging by their side, relaxed shoulders and straight legs. They will be advised to keep their head on the Frankfort plane.48 The participant will be requested to hold their breath while taking the measurement. The headpiece will be lowered until it touches the head’s crown, compressing the hair. One attempt will be carried out. The measure will be read with the examiner’s eye parallel to the headpiece and to the nearest 0.1 cm. Weight will be measured using a calibrated electronic weighing scale positioned on a flat surface. The older person will be instructed to step onto the weighing platform while wearing minimal clothing and with empty pockets. They must stand still on the platform, hand hanging by the side, feet together, looking forward. One attempt will be carried out. The reading will be taken to the nearest 0.1 kg.

Data collection

Necessary permission to access old-age homes will be obtained from the National Secretariat for Elders, Sri Lanka. After obtaining permission to collect data from the manager of the aged care homes, one member of the research team will invite older adults. Then, written information sheets will be delivered and written informed consent will be obtained. Screening will be conducted based on the inclusion criteria to identify potential participants. Face-to-face interviews will be conducted to collect sociodemographic data, as well as depressive symptoms, pain and HRQOL. The trained research staff will measure height, weight, grip strength, balance, flexibility and mobility. A physiotherapist specialised in lung function testing will conduct the tests. That data will be collected at the beginning and end of the intervention.

With support from the aged-care administration, a suitable venue (a common room/hall) at the aged-care homes will be selected. A certified Tai Chi instructor will conduct the Tai Chi intervention, with research assistants providing support. Research staff will obtain field notes for each session. Participants will receive a snack following each session. At the end of the programme, a group discussion will be conducted.

Data analysis

Data will be analysed using SPSS software V.26. Data will be entered into an Excel spreadsheet and then uploaded to SPSS. Descriptive statistics will be used to characterise the sample and describe the outcome variables. Paired t-tests will be used to assess the effectiveness of the Tai Chi programme on physical functioning and fall risk, lung function, pain, depression and HRQOL of participants of the intervention group. For the paired t-test analysis, only participants with complete pre-intervention and post-intervention data will be included. All outcome variables will be analysed at the 0.05 significance level. Qualitative data will be analysed through a narrative synthesis.

Data management and monitoring

IL and JE will be responsible for participant recruitment, data collection, data management and maintaining confidentiality. Two independent researchers will enter de-identified data with a code number (Participant1) into an Excel spreadsheet, then upload it to SPSS and save it in the OneDrive of the PI’s (JE) password-protected computer (JE). All computers will be password protected with a strong password. Researchers only access electronic systems and data. The data generated through this study will be available on request from the PI (JE). Given the feasibility nature of this single-arm, low-risk intervention study, a formal Data Monitoring Committee was not established. PI will oversee the participant safety during the intervention. Any adverse events will be reported to the Research Ethics Committee as required.

Patients and public involvement

In the design of this protocol, there was no patient or public involvement. In this feasibility study, older adults residing in aged care homes(s) in the Kandy District, Sri Lanka, will be recruited.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka (No. 2024/EC/34). The trial was registered with the Sri Lanka Clinical Trials Registry (No. SLCTR/2025/025). One of the researchers will provide participants with a detailed explanation of the project. Written information will be provided to each participant prior to data collection. The research assistant will read the information sheet to any participants who are unable to read. Written informed consent will be obtained from each participant by one of the members of the research team prior to participation (online supplemental material C). Anonymity and confidentiality will be assured. In data sharing and dissemination, this study will use de-identified data, including field notes from each session, group discussion transcripts and sociodemographic information. Names, contact information and other established identifiers will not be included. Participants can withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. They will ensure that the care provided in aged care homes is not affected by such a decision. All collected data will be stored in a locked cupboard, and soft copies of the entered data will be stored on a password-protected computer. Data will be accessed only by the research team. After 5 years, data collection forms will be destroyed. Anonymised data will be used to disseminate findings. Any amendment to the protocol will be communicated to the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka, and the Sri Lanka Clinical Trial Registry.

Dissemination of findings

The findings of this study will be shared with the National Secretariat for Elders in Sri Lanka and presented to the relevant aged care homes. Additionally, the findings will be presented at national or international conferences, research sessions or symposiums and published in a reputable journal.

Discussion

Through this study, we aim to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of Tai Chi interventions among older adults living in aged care homes in Sri Lanka. Although Tai Chi interventions are new to Sri Lanka, they have shown promising effects in improving the health, well-being and QOL of participants, including older adults, in the global context.

Most Tai Chi interventions for older adults have been conducted in East Asian countries (eg, China, South Korea, Hong Kong),49 50 where Tai Chi is culturally embedded and well recognised. These countries are equipped with established community exercise infrastructures and trained instructors. Additionally, many studies have been completed in high-income countries (USA, Germany, Spain, Australia, Italy),49 50 where aged-care services are well resourced. These studies support the clinical effectiveness of Tai Chi across several outcomes. In contrast, this proposed study will be conducted in aged care homes in Sri Lanka, a lower-middle-income setting located in the South Asian Region, with a primary focus on assessing the feasibility of the intervention.

In Sri Lanka, there are no well-established care systems to provide community-based care for older adults or residential aged care facilities.7 Additionally, community or aged care home-based physical activity programmes are limited, and Tai Chi remains largely unfamiliar. The implementation of a Tai Chi intervention in Sri Lanka may be influenced by several context-related challenges. The introduction of Tai Chi may be hindered by the general unfamiliarity with this practice in the country. The limited availability of trained Tai Chi instructors and trained care staff may represent additional barriers. This study will require adapting training materials into local languages and simplifying instructions. Moreover, the availability of physical facilities within aged care homes, as well as financial and organisational constraints, may limit the long-term sustainability of the intervention.

However, Sri Lanka presents important opportunities to adapt Tai Chi interventions and to extend prior findings. Sri Lanka is one of the most rapidly ageing countries and is recognised as a lower-middle-income country. There is a growing trend towards the expansion of aged care homes across the country; therefore, sustainable strategies are needed to improve the health and well-being of older people living in these facilities.7 12 Evidence from other developing countries, for example, a quasi-experimental study conducted in Indonesia, demonstrated a reduction in fall risk among older adults with cataracts following a Tai Chi intervention,51 while another quasi-experimental study conducted in India reported that Tai Chi intervention is beneficial in managing stress among older adults,52 suggesting that such interventions can be adapted for low-income countries. Therefore, this study will provide a comprehensive understanding of the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of Tai Chi intervention in the Sri Lankan context. Consequently, the findings will be of interest to healthcare professionals and managers of aged care homes, informing the design of future studies aimed at developing age-friendly interventions for older adults in Sri Lanka.

Limitations of this study

This will be a pilot feasibility study, which poses a major limitation when assessing the effectiveness of the intervention. However, it will be appropriate for examining the potential to plan future Tai Chi interventions for older adults in Sri Lanka. A single-arm pretest-posttest design without a control group has been selected, which limits causal inference regarding the effects of the intervention. As a pilot study, the sample size is small, further reducing statistical power and limiting the generalisability of the findings. Participants will be recruited from a purposively selected aged care home in a single district of Sri Lanka, and only older adults who speak Sinhala will be included. These methodological limitations will restrict applicability to other settings and populations. Outcomes will be measured only at the completion of the 12-week intervention, and the absence of follow-up will prevent assessment of the long-term sustainability of the intervention effects.
