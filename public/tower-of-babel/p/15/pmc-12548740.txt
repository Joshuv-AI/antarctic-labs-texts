Deployment Exposures, Respiratory Symptoms, and the Limits of Resting Lung Function
Authors: Lauren M. Zell-Baran, Cecile S. Rose
Published: 2025

After the months-long buildup and combat that marked the 1990–1991 first U.S. Gulf War in Kuwait and Iraq, smoke from oil well fires emerged as a defining inhalational exposure. The United States entered the war in Afghanistan in 2001 and invaded Iraq in 2003. Shortly thereafter, smoke from burn pits emerged as the most notorious exposure in the post-9/11 Southwest Asia Theater of Military Operations (SWATO).

Over time, reports grew of persistent and often disabling respiratory symptoms among first and second Gulf War veterans. By the time of the drawdown of U.S. troops from SWATO, a spectrum of deployment-related respiratory diseases had been identified, including asthma and distal lung injury with bronchiolitis. But many affected veterans had normal resting lung function. Some in the pulmonary and military medical communities were skeptical that, absent severe functional abnormalities, persistent respiratory symptoms could reflect significant exposure-related health outcomes.

Little is known about the longitudinal respiratory health outcomes in deployed veterans with persistent respiratory symptoms (1, 2). In this issue of AnnalsATS, Zeng and colleagues (pp. 1664–1673) report on the utility of resting pulmonary function testing (PFT) in predicting risk for developing adverse lung health outcomes among veterans with preserved spirometry and total lung capacity (3). Using data from the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs Airborne Hazards and Open Burn Pit Registry, they analyzed findings in 1,879 veterans (average age, 37 yr) with preserved PFT findings after deployment. They found that diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DlCO) was the strongest predictor of later restrictive lung function impairment and for a diagnosis of parenchymal lung disease.

We commend the authors on their sophisticated statistical analysis methods (including exploratory machine learning analysis followed by confirmatory regression modeling) and the breadth of clinical outcomes assessed. Linkage of registry symptom and exposure data to health outcomes data from the Veterans Health Administration’s Informatics and Computing Infrastructure, including clinical diagnoses based on International Classification of Diseases codes, medications prescribed, postdeployment PFT results, and healthcare utilization, was particularly noteworthy. There are several key points from the study that warrant further comment.

First, resting PFT appears to be an insensitive clinical tool in this younger occupational cohort. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (4), multiple breath washout testing (5), and oscillometry (6) may be more sensitive in diagnosis, functional assessment, and longitudinal follow-up of veterans with persistent respiratory symptoms, and the predictive value of these clinical tools deserves further examination.

Furthermore, as the authors acknowledge, resting PFT without predeployment or baseline testing is a major limitation in the assessment of postdeployment adverse respiratory health outcomes. This limitation persists despite recommendations to the U.S. Department of Defense from National Academies committees and others in the medical scientific community to conduct predeployment testing in high-risk military personnel at a minimum (7). The study authors attempt to circumvent this challenge by including only veterans having PFT before enrollment in the registry examination, excluding roughly 20% of their potential sample. Given that PFT was likely obtained for evaluation of symptoms and not from all deployed veterans, eliminating those who had undergone PFT after registry enrollment may not adequately address this limitation.

A focus on veterans with preserved spirometry and total lung capacity is useful in understanding their persistent respiratory symptoms, but it would be helpful in addition to examine longitudinal outcomes in the larger subset of this study population with findings of restriction, obstruction, or mixed physiology on initial PFT. For example, Zell-Baran and colleagues found that previously deployed veterans with both large and small airway disease had the lowest longitudinal lung function (1). By examining both groups, Zeng and colleagues could assess whether the same PFT parameters are useful for monitoring those with impairment and those without impairment on initial PFT, as well as whether these parameters are similarly predictive of poor longitudinal outcomes.

Previous studies have found a limited signal for isolated reduction in DlCO as a clinically useful tool in symptomatic veterans. This may be due in part to differences in the reference sets used for DlCO interpretation. For example, in our 2020 study (8) of symptomatic veterans, we used Crapo prediction equations (9) and found that 18% (19 of 104) had reduced DlCO; in 2024 (1), we used the Global Lung Function Initiative prediction equation (10) and found that less than 1% (1 of 219) of our cohort had reduced DlCO. It is unclear in the present study whether DlCO findings were adjusted for race and which reference set was used (11). Selection of the reference set can dramatically alter results, and clinicians need to consider this when assessing DlCO trends in individual patients over time. Nevertheless, the study supports the regular use of DlCO in evaluating and monitoring those with deployment-related respiratory symptoms.

The authors did not observe an association between DlCO and either cumulative deployment length or time deployed near an open air burn pit. Yet, as they point out, SWATO deployment is associated with exposure to multiple inhalational hazards besides burn pits, including particulate matter from sandstorms; combat; local industries; diesel exhaust; and job-specific vapors, gases, dusts, and fumes. Deployment exposures also differ substantially on the basis of deployment location, timing, job duties, service branch, and rank. For example, we observed significantly more abnormal lung clearance index scores, a measure of global ventilation inhomogeneity, among symptomatic veterans who reported frequent exposure to explosive blasts during deployment (5). Despite the lack of predeployment spirometry in our symptomatic cohort, we also found that forced expiratory volume in 1 second, forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity ratio, and forced expiratory flow, midexpiratory phase, significantly declined for each additional month of deployment (1). Furthermore, we found that veterans with combat-related occupational specialties were more likely to undergo evaluation for persistent respiratory symptoms (12). Consideration of multiple exposures beyond just reported time deployed near a burn pit may provide additional insight into risks for long-latency chronic diseases of the airways and interstitium.

In summary, this study adds to the growing literature on risk for significant exposure-related respiratory health outcomes in those with normal resting pulmonary function and helps address concerns among veterans and their caregivers about persistent if sometimes poorly understood symptoms.
