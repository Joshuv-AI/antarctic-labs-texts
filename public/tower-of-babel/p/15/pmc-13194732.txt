Evaluating radiation protection knowledge and practices in operating room personnel: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xin Peng, Jing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although radiation hazards are prevalent in the operating room, making radiation protection critical for occupational safety, the current status of radiation-related knowledge and compliance with radiation safety practices remains suboptimal in many operating rooms. This study aimed to evaluate the current status and identify the influencing factors of radiation protection knowledge, safety practices, and compliance among operating room personnel. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among operating room personnel in hospitals at various levels in Hunan Province, China, using the Healthcare Professional Knowledge of Radiation Protection (HPKRP) scale was employed to assess radiation-related knowledge and a self-designed questionnaire to evaluate participants’ radiation protection compliance. Collected data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. A total of 381 participants were surveyed, including 349 operating room nurses (91.6%), 13 anesthesiologists (3.41%), and 19 surgeons (4.99%). Among these participants, 206 (54.07%) participants had received radiation-related training and only 27.8% routinely used at least one type of personal protective equipment (PPE) during potential radiation exposure. Multivariate regression analysis indicated tertiary hospitals, departments with standardized radiation protection management, and radiation-related training were independent factors associated with both superior radiation-related knowledge and better radiation protection compliance. Higher knowledge scores (OR = 2.366, p < 0.001) and physicians (OR = 4.845, p = 0.001) were independently associated with better radiation protection compliance. This study demonstrated that radiation relative knowledge and protection compliance need to be strengthened among physicians and operating room nurses. Standardized training and radiation protection management should be adopted according to specific needs of different hospitals and staff. These results offer meaningful evidences for the optimization of occupational radiation protection management and policy-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47272-5.

Introduction

In recent years, ionizing radiation has been increasingly applied in surgical procedures1. It is not merely employed as adjunctive imaging modalities in surgery, but also become a therapeutic modality-such as intraoperative radiotherapy- that have implemented for many clinical conditions2. The advances improve therapeutic efficacy and accuracy. However, it also increases the risk of occupational exposure among medical staff in operation room3. Ionizing radiation can impair the circulatory and immune systems, increasing the risk of cancer and accelerating the growth of cancer cells. It may also damage the central nervous system, leading to, headaches, anxiety, and panic4. With the rapid development and increasing use of fluoroscopy, operating room nurses and surgeons are at greater risk of occupational radiation exposure. Studies have shown that the incidence of radiation-related cancer among hybrid vascular room surgeons and nurses is 2355 and 795 per 100,000 people, respectively5. Thus, radiation safety in operating rooms, has become an important priority.

Despite the high risks associated with radiation exposure, radiation-relative knowledge and compliance with protective measures remain severely inadequate3,6. A study reported that 33.3% of participants did not regularly use any protective equipment, 68.3% most commonly used lead aprons, 52.1% used thyroid shields, and only 10.6% of surgeons indicated regular use of dosimeters7. This may be attribute to lack of adequate radiation protection training and education. A national survey in UK reported that 38% of orthopedic surgeons had not received formal training on radiation safety, and most reported limited knowledge of basic radiation principles, legislation, and practices, posing additional risks to both patients and healthcare workers8. Other studies also imply effective radiation protection education improve the understanding and appliance about radiation protection9. However, the radiation protection status of operating room staff across different hospital levels in China remains poorly evaluated, and comprehensive analyses of factors associated with radiation protection knowledge and compliance among such personnel are scarce.

In this study we aimed to performed a cross-sectional survey targeting operating room nurses, surgeons, and anesthesiologists from hospitals of various levels in Hunan Province, China. Based on these data, we conducted a rigorous analysis of the current status and independent influencing factors associated with radiation protection knowledge and compliance among operating room personnel.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was employed.

Participants and setting

Participants included operating room nurses, anesthesiologists, and surgeons from hospitals of various levels. A purposive sampling method was used. Inclusion criteria were: participants with potential occupational radiation exposure during daily or occasional clinical duties in operating theaters, aged 18 years or older, and held a valid nursing or medical license. Exclusion criteria were: clinical interns or trainees. Invitations and questionnaires were distributed to nurses between February and March 2024. The response rate was 76%.

Instrument

In this study, we used the Healthcare Professional Knowledge of Radiation Protection (HPKRP) scale10, which has been widely validated and employed in previous studies to evaluate radiation protection–related knowledge among various healthcare personnel11,12. The scale consists of three dimensions: radiation physics, biology, principles of radiation use (12 items); radiation protection (13 items) and guidelines for safe ionizing radiation use (8 items). Participants rated their knowledge using a 10-point Likert scale (1 = no knowledge, 10 = complete knowledge), with total scores ranging from 0 to 330. Additionally, a self‑designed questionnaire was used to evaluate participants’ radiation protection compliance. This questionnaire was revised and improved based on previously published and validated scales13, and consists of a total of 8 items. Background questions covered demographics such as age, work experience, hospital level, occupation, education level, marital and children status, etc. The reliability of the questionnaire was verified using Cronbach’s alpha. Values for all dimensions were greater than 0.9, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Data collection

Participants from various hospitals completed the questionnaire via a web link. All responses were anonymous and processed in accordance with good research practice. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before questionnaire completion. The study was conducted in adherence to good research practice principles, respect for human dignity, and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze background data. Linear regression and logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with radiation-related knowledge and radiation protection compliance, respectively. Univariate analyses were initially conducted to screen candidate variables, and those with a p-value < 0.1 were retained for inclusion in the multivariate model. Bivariate correlation analysis was then used to detect and exclude highly correlated variables. Finally, the remaining independent variables were entered into the final multiple linear regression model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Of the 381 participants, 349 were operating room nurses (91.6%), 13 were anesthesiologists (3.4%), and 19 were surgeons (5.0%). The majority of participants were female (82.7%), with an average age of 34.0 ± 7.89 years and an average work experience of 12.4 ± 7.8 years. Most participants worked in tertiary hospitals (76.1%). Regarding education level, the majority held a bachelor’s degree (75.6%). Radiation protection compliance and perceived major factors

Compliance with personal protective equipment (PPE) was generally poor in this study population. Specifically, only 9.7% of participants reported regular radiation dose measurements (Table 2), while merely 20.2% consistently used lead aprons as protection against potential radiation exposure in a radiation‑risk environment. Conversely, 28.9% of respondents reported never using lead aprons. Similarly, usage rates for other protective gear varied. For instance, 21.8% of participants always wore lead gowns, but 39.11% only wore them occasionally. Other PPE including lead thyroid collars and radiation protection glasses, was also used inconsistently, with a considerable proportion of participants reporting irregular use (Table 2). Although most participants reported that adequate PPE was provided (81.1%) and dedicated management was available in their departments (78.7%), a significant number of participants had experienced accidental exposure to radiation (72.8%). Radiation-related training and knowledge

Among the participants, 51.4% had received standardized radiation‑related training. Most underwent training once per year (38.1%), and the training was most frequently organized by departments (39.4%), followed by hospitals (37.1%). 32.6% of the participants acquire radiation-related knowledge by self-learning (Table 4). We next evaluate the radiation relative knowledge by using a widely used HPKRP scale. The average total score was 175.3 ± 72.7 (out of a full score of 330). The mean item score was 5.3 ± 2.2 (out of a full score of 10). In the radiation use principles category, the mean item score was 5.1 ± 2.2, comparable to 5.5 ± 2.3 in radiation protection and 5.4 ± 2.4 in safe radiation use guidelines category, respectively. Factors associated with radiation protection compliance

To investigate factors associated with radiation protection compliance against the risk of radiation exposure, we defined participants who “always” or “usually” used at least one PPE as having good compliance; otherwise, they were categorized as having poor compliance. Radiation-related knowledge was also categorized into high and low groups using the median as the cutoff. In univariate analyses, age, occupation, hospital level, standardized PPE management, radiation-related training, radiation-related knowledge, and participant children’s status were significantly associated with compliance (p < 0.05). After excluding children’s status due to potential multicollinearity with age (r = 0.634, p < 0.001) identified by bivariate correlation analysis, we conducted multivariate analysis of radiation protection compliance. Following adjustment for confounders, we found that being a doctor, higher hospital level, standardized PPE management in the department, receiving radiation-related training, and higher radiation-related knowledge score were independent factors associated with good compliance (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify influencing factors associated with radiation protection compliance against the risk of radiation exposure. Discussion

This study revealed inadequate knowledge and significant gaps among healthcare professionals regarding radiation protection and its associated hazards, along with a range of suboptimal practices. among the participants, only 27.8% routinely used at least one type of PPE during potential radiation exposure and 72.8% had experienced at least one occupational radiation exposure. Similar challenging situations have been frequently reported in other countries14–16. In our survey, participant response poor compliance may be largely attributed to insufficient radiation-related knowledge and substandard departmental management of radiation protection. To further explore these issues and their underlying causes, we performed rigorous multivariate regression analysis, our findings indicated that tertiary hospital affiliation, departments with standardized PPE management, and radiation-related training were independent factors associated with both superior radiation-related knowledge and better PPE compliance. Higher knowledge scores and being a doctor were independently associated with better PPE compliance.

These findings, consistent with previous research, underscore the necessity of targeted training to enhance adherence to radiation safety protocols9,13,17. Given that higher-level hospitals demonstrated significantly better knowledge and compliance in our study, training is especially critical in primary and secondary hospitals. Studies has consistently shown that implementing radiation protection education and training can significantly improve awareness, enhance protective measures, and reduce the risk of fluoroscopy-related hazards18. Training can help operating room personnel understand the principles, necessity, risks, and potential consequences of radiation exposure, enabling nurses to better clarify when and how to proper use protective measures. This will improve compliance with safety standards and reduce radiation exposure risks19. Our study also indicated participants who received standard training demonstrated significantly higher knowledge levels than those who relied solely on self-learning, which further strengthen the importance of training. We also found that participants with training twice or more per year achieved higher knowledge scores and better PPE compliance. It is imperative to strengthen radiation protection knowledge through standardized training and improve adherence to intraoperative radiation safety guidelines and proper PPE use20.

Our findings highlight the importance of specialized departmental radiation protection management in the operating room. Standardized management includes providing sufficient and easily accessible dosimeters and PPE, clarifying its appropriate indications for use, and implementing standardized procedures for cleaning, disinfection, and maintenance. In this study, many participants also reported that insufficient departmental management was an important reason for poor PPE compliance. A national survey conducted in the UK have reported similarly limited access to safety equipment among surgical trainees8. Furthermore, favorable radiation protection management may improve the utilization of dosimeters and routine monitoring among healthcare workers in hospitals. A recent study in Japan demonstrated that strict regulatory changes significantly improved the deployment of dosimeters and eyewear in hospitals21.Previous studies also implied that a favorable administrative environment promoted radiation protection behaviors in emergency room22. Additionally, another study recommended that radiation management be incorporated into the quality assurance program of the radiology department23. A similar management model should also be implemented in the operating room.

In this study, participants from tertiary hospitals achieved higher radiation-related knowledge scores and better PPE compliance than those from secondary or primary hospitals. However, previous studies seem to have reported inconsistent findings. For instance, one study conducted in Japan showed that larger hospitals exhibited better compliance with radiation protection measures21. In contrast, another study from the same group also found that healthcare workers in general hospitals experienced slightly higher radiation doses than those in university hospitals16. This is reasonable because healthcare workers in larger hospitals may receive more training and have greater knowledge than those in smaller hospitals, leading to better compliance. However, due to heavier workloads and more frequent fluoroscopy use, radiation doses may ultimately be higher among staff in larger hospitals. Thus, these disparities across different hospital levels underscore the need for targeted support to ensure uniform compliance with radiation safety standards, as well as improved resource allocation and protective measures in hospitals of all levels.

Previous study reported male and nurses with less than 5 years of experience achieved higher compliance24,25. However, other study indicated no significant association between compliance with radiation safety practices and work experience among operating room staff, which is consistent with our study26. In our study, despite older age (rather than work experience) was associated with better compliance in univariate analysis, no significant difference was observed in multivariate analysis. This may be explained by the fact that older staff had received more training and possessed greater radiation protection knowledge.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the study population was limited, with a significant imbalance between physicians and nurses. Thus, a larger and more balanced sample is warranted in future research. Furthermore, the data were collected via an online survey, which may result in limited accessibility and potential bias between self-reported practices and actual behaviors. Data quality may also be affected by technical issues and misunderstandings of questionnaire items.

Conclusion

This study, through surveying physicians and operating room nurses, revealed a need for enhanced training and specialized departmental management in radiation protection. Future training programs should be tailored to the specific needs of different healthcare institutions and individuals. Our study provides important provides important guidance for occupational protection management and training policy for hospitals at different levels.
