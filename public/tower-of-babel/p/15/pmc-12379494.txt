A case report of SMARCA2-deficient and SMARCA4-preserved lung adenocarcinoma diagnosed by pleural effusion cytology
Authors: Xiaoyue Feng, Ruixia Jie, Fang Peng, Huan lei, Xiaomin Dai
Published: 2025

Background

SWI/SNF (Switch/Sucrose Non-fermentable) is a multi-subunit chromatin remodeling complex that utilizes ATPase hydrolysis to regulate the chromatin structure and control gene expression dynamically [1]. SMARCA2 and SMARCA4, the two core catalytic subunits of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, play crucial roles in modulating a broad spectrum of genetic programs, including cell differentiation and proliferation, actively participates in the development and progression of various malignant tumors [2]. The fifth edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Thoracic Tumors has proposed a newly recognized distinct category of pathological entities, classified as thoracic SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated tumor (SMARCA4-UT) [3]. Additionally, it should be worthy noted that SMARCA4 deletion is also observed in a subset of non-small cell lung cancers, including adenocarcinomas, squamous cell carcinomas, and neuroendocrine carcinomas. In contrast, isolated loss of SMARCA2 expression in tumors is less frequently detected [4]. Here, we present a case of poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma diagnosed via pleural effusion cytology, characterized by the absence of SMARCA2 expression but the presence of SMARCA4 expression, along with aberrant expression of β-catenin and p53. The identification of this subtype of lung adenocarcinoma, characterized by distinct phenotypic features, may contribute to more accurate diagnosis of thoracic tumors with SWI/SNF complex deficiency.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old female patient, previously without significant medical history, presented with the complaints of persistent cough and dyspnea lasting for one week. She denied experiencing hemoptysis, fever, or weight loss. The chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed an irregularly shaped mass measuring approximately 4.6 × 4.5 × 3.0 cm in the dorsal segment of the left lower lobe, suggestive of malignancy (Fig. 1). In addition, there was evidence of left pleural effusion and atelectasis in the adjacent lung tissue. Laboratory tests showed that carcinoembryonic antigen was elevated to 17.0ng/mL (reference: 0–5.00 ng/mL) and carbohydrate antigen 125 was 62.64U/mL (reference: 0–35.00 U/mL).

Ultrasound examination revealed a significant accumulation of pleural effusion on the left side. Subsequently, the patient underwent pleural aspiration, and the pathological analysis was performed. Liquid-based cytological examination of the pleural effusion demonstrated numerous grape-like, three-dimensional clusters of epithelioid tumor cells, characterized by marked cellular atypia and variability in cell size, within a background of lymphocytes, macrophages and reactive mesothelial cells (Fig. 2A). The presence of high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, prominent nucleoli, and condensed nuclear chromatin adjacent the nuclear membrane, resulting in vacuolated nuclei was observed (Fig. 2B). Notably, scattered tumor cells with irregular karyotypes and hyperchromatic nuclei were also identified.

The liquid-based cytological examination of the pleural effusion. A Tumor cells arranged with three-dimensional architecture (Papanicolaou, ×200). B High-grade cytological features characterized by a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, prominent nucleoli and vacuolar nuclei (Papanicolaou, ×400)

The cell block was prepared from residual material. Hematoxylin-eosin staining of the cell block section showed extensive areas of large, tightly packed epithelioid cells without distinct adenoid or papillary structures. The pleomorphic tumor cells exhibited cytological features consistent with those observed in the patient’s liquid-based cytology test. These malignant cells showed high-grade cytomorphology characterized by enlarged, eccentrically positioned nuclei, occasional binucleation, prominent nucleoli, and finely vacuolated cytoplasm (Fig. 3A). A subset of tumor cells exhibited poor cohesion and rhabdoid morphological features. Immunocytochemical analysis revealed that the tumor cells were positive for TTF-1 (while a subset showing negative staining), NapsinA, CK7, Claudin-4, SMARCA4 and P53 (Fig. 3B-D). The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 20%. Notably, β-catenin exhibited an atypical staining pattern, certain tumors cells exhibited β-catenin expression on the cell membrane, while others displayed cytoplasmic and nuclear localization of β-catenin (Fig. 3E). Negative staining was observed for CD56, AFP, CK20, CDX2 and SMARCA2 (Fig. 3F). The cellular morphology and immunohistochemical characteristics are indicative of poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma with isolated loss of SMARCA2.

B TTF-1 staining is positive in a portion of the tumor cells (×200). C-D SMARCA4 and P53 show positive staining in tumor cells (×200). The fiberoptic bronchoscopy revealed partial occlusion of the dorsal segment of the left lower lobe (Fig. 4A). Histopathological examination of a bronchus biopsy from the lesions confirmed poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma, with histological and immunohistochemical characteristics consistent with those observed in the cell block (Fig. 4B). Programmed cell death-Ligand (PD-L1) expression was detected in less than 1% of tumor cells. Additionally, next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis identified a non-frameshift deletion in exon 19 of the EGFR gene among eighteen genes associated with lung cancer development. Based on these findings, osimertinib was selected as the targeted therapy. The patient remained clinical stability for a duration of seven months following the initial presentation.

Discussion and conclusions

Previous studies have demonstrated that loss of expression of the SWI/SNF complex correlates with aggressive clinicopathological features in various types of cancer, including breast cancer [5], ovarian cancer [6], endometrial cancer [7, 8], renal cancer [9], colorectal cancer [10], gastric cancer [11], pancreatic cancer [12, 13] and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [14]. SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 are the two core catalytic subunits of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex. The SMARCA4 gene, located on chromosome 19p13.2, encodes the BRG1 protein, while the SMARCA2 gene, located on chromosome 9p24.3, encodes the BRM protein [15]. Both proteins are essential for the functionality of the SWI/SNF complex [16]. They exhibit approximately 75% structural homology and possess comparable ATPase and helicase activities, although their functions are not entitirely identical [17]. In the thoracic regions, SMARCA4 deficient has been identified in two subtypes of tumors: thoracic SMARCA4-UT and NSCLC [14]. Some studies have also suggested that the loss of SMARCA2 expression is significantly correlated with high nuclear grade, aberrant P53 expression, and an elevated proliferation index, serving as an independent prognostic marker for poor prognosis in NSCLC [18]. The progressive downregulation of SMARCA2 reflects the malignant progression and deterioration of the tumor [19], although isolated loss of SMARCA2 expression is infrequently observed. A comprehensive review of the relevant English literature reveals a paucity of reports regarding the cytological diagnosis of SWI/SNF-deficient lung tumors in pleural effusion. We present a case of lung adenocarcinoma characterized by SMARCA2 deficiency and SMARCA4 expression, which has not been previously documented using cytological diagnostic methods.

In the present case, liquid-based cytological examination of the pleural effusion revealed a cellular composition characterized by highly pleomorphic epithelioid and rhabdoid tumor cells. These cells were observed in aggregated clusters or with loose adhesion, the cytomorphology and the arrangement of these tumor cells were consistent with the characteristics of malignant pleural effusion. Differential diagnoses considered included poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma, poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, large-cell carcinoma, thoraic SMARCA4-UT, sarcomatoid carcinoma and mesothelioma. It is challenging to ascertain the origin or type of tumor cells based solely on cytological morphology, which represents one of the significant difficulties in cytopathological diagnosis. Therefore, the preparation of cell blocks and immunohistochemical examination are indispensable. Immunocytochemical analysis of the cell block showed that tumor cells with positive reactivity to Napsin A, CK7, Claudin-4, and SMARCA4. Additionally, some tumor cells exhibited TTF-1 expression while concurrently showing loss of SMARCA2. The comprehensive analysis ultimately confirmed a diagnosis of poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma with isolated loss of SMARCA2 expression. Although the subsequent bronchial biopsy tissue contained fewer tumor components, the pathological findings were consistent with the cytological results. This case highlights the importance of cytological analysis of pleural effusion in achieving a definitive diagnosis when biopsy tissue is limited or unavailable, which holds significant clinical value. In this case, both aberrant P53 expression and reduced TTF-1 expression in some tumor cells were observed, further supporting the hypothesis that SMARCA2 deficiency may induce morphological alterations, changes in cell viability, and dedifferentiation of tumor cells, consistent with the findings reported by Sun S et al. [18].

Tessier Cloutier B et al. [20] and Michal et al. [21] reported that SWI/SNF subunits were frequently absent in undifferentiated carcinomas, particularly those with rhabdoid morphological features, such as those originating from gastrointestinal and pancreatic cancers. In this case, we observed a subset of tumor cells exhibiting poor adhesion and rhabdoid morphology, characterized by abundant and finely vacuolated cytoplasme, enlarged and eccentrically positioned nuclei, and prominent nucleoli. Notably, certain tumor cells demonstrated aberrant expression of β-catenin in both the cytoplasm and nucleus. To date, there has been no literature reporting on the expression of β-catenin in SMARCA2-deficient lung adenocarcinoma, and studies investigating the association between the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and SMARCA2-deficient NSCLC, as well as undifferentiated carcinoma of other systems remain limited. Therefore, multi-patient cohort studies are essential to further elucidate these relationship.

Matsubara D et al. [22]showed that SMARCA4 deletion was specifically associated with the progression of EGFR wild-type tumors, but not EGFR-mutant tumors. Herpel E et al. [4] corroborated this finding in a clinical case study, noting an association between SMARCA4 deletion and EGFR wild-type status. However, Sun S et al. reported no significant difference in SMARCA2-negative expression between EGFR mutant and wild-type NSCLC in a cohort of 569 cases [18].This suggests that despite high homology, SMARCA2 and SMARCA4 exhibit distinct functional roles, with SMARCA2 potentially representing an independent genetic factor unrelated to EGFR mutations in NSCLC [18]. In our case, the presence of EGFR mutations provides an opportunity for targeted therapy. Additionally, Sun S et al. [18]observed that SMARCA2-negative expression was associated with PD-L1 positive expression, indicating potential efficacy of immunotherapy using immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Regrettably, the PD-L1 test result for our case was negative. Given the rarity of isolated SMARCA2 deficiency, we believe that a comprehensive evaluation of PD-L1 status and the determination of the optimal treatment strategy for this specific tumor type require the accumulation of additional clinical cases. Studies have shown that Cisplatin and inhibitor of Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 histone methyltransferase hold promise as therapeutic agents for malignancies with concurrent loss of both SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 [23]. The development of selective and highly efficient small-molecule ATPase inhibitors represents a more direct strategy for inhibiting SMARCA2 function and treating tumors harboring SMARCA4 mutations via synthetic lethality mechanisms [24]. Despite the high degree of homology between SMARCA2 and SMARCA4, existing evidence indicates that these drugs demonstrate limited efficacy in treating isolated SMARCA2-deficient tumors [25]. It is important to emphasize that in cancer therapy, directly inhibiting SMARCA2 without considering specific contexts may not be appropriate. The choice of inhibitors or agonists for SMARCA2 should be determined based on the distinct characteristics of various cancers [26]. Although the EGFR mutation was identified in this case, the loss of SMARCA2 expression did not alter the clinical treatment approach. Nevertheless, the importance of this report resides in its reinforcement of the notion that NSCLC is not solely associated with oncogenic mutations, but may also involve inactivating alterations in tumor suppressor genes. Furthermore, considering the well-established tumor suppressor role of SMARCA2, its selective loss may contribute to tumor dedifferentiation, progression, and metastasis [2]. Therefore, the identification of lung adenocarcinomas exhibiting isolated SMARCA2 deficiency holds important clinical implications.

In conclusion, we present an unusual case of poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma diagnosed via cytological examination of pleural effusion, characterized by SMARCA2 deficiency and SMARCA4 preservation. Cytopathological analysis reveals that the tumor cells exhibit epithelioid and rhabdoid morphology tumor cells with high-grade morphology. Due to its rarity and the absence of distinct clinical manifestations or morphological features, accurate identification and diagnosis pose significant challenges. Therefore, the preparation of cell blocks and adequate immunocytochemical staining are valuable and reliable tools for cytological diagnosis of such rare cases.
