High‐Fidelity Measurement‐Based Detection Values and Roles of Three‐Dimensional Joint Space Width in Evaluation of Knee Osteoarthritis
Authors: Elena Tahir, Zhi Zhang, Dangfeng Zhang, Hussnain Tahir, Huida Wang, Xing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective3D joint space width (JSW) mapping quantifies structural narrowing in knee osteoarthritis (OA), but the effect of surface mesh resolution on measurement reliability and precision remains unclear. This study assessed how mesh resolution influences 3D JSW measurements, whether effects differ by metric and OA severity, and which resolutions best preserve OA group‐level discrimination and subject‐level agreement.MethodsKnees with medial knee OA (KL grades 0–4) were analyzed. For each knee, 3D JSW maps were generated on a 0.1 mm reference mesh and seven coarser meshes (0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 mm). Minimum and median JSW (minJSW, medJSW) were calculated within a standardized medial region and compared to the reference. Systematic error, agreement (Bland–Altman), and subject‐level interchangeability were evaluated across resolutions for the overall cohort and early versus advanced OA subgroups. Absolute‐error thresholds were set at 0.2 mm (sensitivity) and 0.5 mm (clinical significance). Mesh‐by‐severity interactions were analyzed using mixed‐effects models, and group‐level discrimination was assessed at each resolution.ResultsSeventy‐two knees from 54 patients (mean age 65 ± 10.4 years; 48.6% advanced OA) were included. The 0.1 mm reference mesh had excellent geometric fidelity (RMS deviation 0.0011 ± 0.0010 mm) and high vertex density (median 118,300 vertices), which dropped to 14,848 at 0.3 mm, 5033 at 0.5 mm, and 72 at 4.0 mm. From 0.1 to 4.0 mm, medJSW increased from 4.07 ± 0.77 mm to 5.14 ± 0.96 mm and minJSW from 1.98 ± 0.81 to 2.34 ± 0.81 mm, converging at 0.3 mm. Subject‐level agreement for medJSW declined beyond 0.3 mm: at 0.5 mm and coarser meshes, ≥ 44.4% of knees exceeded the 0.2 mm error threshold; at 0.7 mm and coarser meshes, ≥ 12.5% exceeded the 0.5 mm threshold. In contrast, minJSW remained within the 0.5 mm clinical threshold through 1.0 mm. Early OA showed a larger absolute medJSW inflation (p = 0.023), whereas advanced OA had greater relative minJSW error (p < 0.05). Group‐level OA discrimination remained preserved at all resolutions (p < 0.001), while subject‐level agreement deteriorated at coarser meshes.ConclusionsMesh resolution significantly influences 3D JSW measurements, with effects modulated by selected metric, disease stage, and level of analysis. Using a rigorous 0.1‐mm reference mesh, specific thresholds were established: a 0.3‐mm mesh provided the most consistent performance across all conditions; a 0.5‐mm mesh may be acceptable for medJSW under a less stringent error tolerance; and up to 1.0 mm sufficed only for identifying the narrowest point (minJSW). At coarser resolutions, baseline JSW overestimation can be large enough to alter perceived structural severity of medial compartment narrowing, carrying risks for clinical misinterpretation and treatment decisions.

Introduction

Joint space width (JSW) remains the most widely used quantitative metric for assessing structural severity in medial knee osteoarthritis (KOA) [1]. Recent three‐dimensional (3D) imaging studies suggest that sub‐millimetric differences, on the order of 0.1–0.5 mm, in the tibiofemoral joint structure can be clinically meaningful [2, 3, 4]. Such differences can inform the risk of future knee replacement [5], reflect full‐thickness cartilage integrity [3], and even aid in interpreting postoperative joint status [6]. Detecting structural differences at this scale in clinical practice, however, is not always straightforward. It requires measurement techniques that are not only precise and reproducible, but also sensitive to the specific anatomy and disease state of each joint. Without sensitivity to small effects, clinically important structural differences may go undetected.

Currently, radiographic JSW measurements cannot consistently meet this demand. Their reliability depends heavily on joint positioning, knee flexion angle, and X‐ray beam alignment [1]. Moreover, the inherently complex 3D geometry of the knee is compressed into a single planar view, obscuring the spatial distribution and extent of structural damage within the joint space. This limitation is particularly problematic in medial KOA, where cartilage loss is frequently focal and spatially heterogeneous [7]. Some studies have even shown that the measurement error of radiographic JSW can reach up to 1 mm [8], a magnitude that equals or exceeds the sub‐millimetric differences considered clinically meaningful.

Computed tomography (CT)‐based 3D JSW mapping can address many of these limitations. By reconstructing subject‐specific bone surfaces from volumetric CT scans, it enables joint space measurements to be sampled across the entire articulating surface rather than at a single projected plane [9]. Prior work has shown that 3D JSW metrics can differentiate knees across osteoarthritic (OA) severity levels and are more sensitive to structural changes than conventional radiographic measures [1, 2].

Despite these advantages, a key challenge remains. Although 3D JSW analysis produces dense, surface‐wide maps of inter‐bone distances, clinical interpretation and statistical analysis still rely on summary measures. Minimum, mean, and median JSW remain the most commonly reported structural endpoints [2, 4, 9, 10, 11]. While reducing data in this way facilitates comparisons across individuals, time points, imaging modalities, and disease groups, it also introduces an important dependency: the measured JSW values reflect not only the anatomical state of the joint but also the characteristics of the 3D surface representation used to generate them.

This dependency arises from how joint surfaces are computationally modeled. To calculate 3D JSW, segmented bones are approximated using triangle meshes composed of interconnected triangular faces [2]. Mesh resolution, typically defined by triangle edge length (or grid size), directly influences both the geometric fidelity of the reconstructed surface and the density of points used to compute inter‐surface distances (i.e., the effective sampling resolution).

Triangle meshes are standard in computer graphics and widely used in quantitative 3D joint imaging across multiple anatomical sites, including the hip, knee, ankle, wrist, and hand [9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Their effects are well established in finite element analysis studies [17]. However, mesh resolution and point distribution for 3D JSW mapping remain poorly standardized, and optimal parameters have yet to be established. In knee imaging JSW studies, for example, uniform triangular meshes are commonly used, but edge length is often unreported. Even when sampling density is specified, it varies widely, from fewer than 500 to more than 50,000 surface points [2, 4, 11, 18, 19], and no consensus threshold exists to guide this choice. A reported total of 2000 surface points, for example, could represent either a fine mesh over a small, tightly defined subregion or a coarse mesh applied to a larger compartmental surface. This ambiguity makes reproducibility and cross‐study comparisons difficult as observed differences in 3D JSW may reflect true biological variation or simply inconsistencies in mesh resolution and region of interest (ROI) definition.

The influence of mesh resolution may be especially important to consider in advanced medial KOA. In late‐stage disease, joint surfaces deviate from smooth, healthy contours and instead exhibit focal cartilage loss, subchondral remodeling, surface flattening, and osteophyte formation. Coarse meshes may inadequately capture these structural features, for example by bridging narrow defects or smoothing osteophytes, thereby altering local JSW measurements. Whether sensitivity to mesh resolution varies across disease stages remains unclear, as does whether the irregular geometry of advanced OA requires finer sampling. This is a timely question, as recent evidence from a study using simplified primitive models (inclined planes and ball‐and‐socket geometries) adaptively meshed at three edge lengths (0.5, 1, and 2 mm) suggested that distance measurement error increases as uniform joint gaps narrow [20]. However, whether similar effects occur in the highly complex and spatially heterogeneous joint space of the human medial knee has not been systematically investigated.

The choice of summary metric adds another layer of complexity. Finite element studies of a single healthy foot, generating 125 model variants across five mesh sizes (ranging from 1.5 to 6.0 mm), indicated that sensitivity to mesh resolution depends on the specific output variable; and for peak contact pressure, their models converged at an element size of 2.5 mm [21]. It is not known whether a similar principle applies to 3D JSW, and in particular whether single‐point metrics (e.g., minimum JSW) and distribution‐based metrics (e.g., median JSW) are affected to different degrees. Furthermore, for large cohort studies, evidence from cortical bone mapping (CBM) suggests that measurements are robust provided the mesh vertices are within approximately 2 mm of the true cortical surface, a tolerance demonstrated in the proximal femur [22]. Whether a similar resolution tolerance is applicable to 3D JSW measurements in the medial knee, and whether a resolution adequate for group‐level analysis may still preserve subject‐level agreement are questions that become critical when evaluating an individual for treatment eligibility.

To date, no study has systematically evaluated the effect of mesh resolution on CT‐derived 3D JSW in KOA. Accordingly, the present study investigated the influence of surface mesh resolution on CT‐derived 3D JSW measurements in medial KOA, examined whether these effects differ according to summary metric and disease severity, and identified mesh resolutions that remain acceptable relative to a high‐resolution reference mesh. The core question was whether mesh resolutions that preserve OA group‐level discrimination also preserve subject‐level agreement with the reference mesh, a distinction that is essential for both population‐based research and personalized clinical care.

Methods

Study Cohort

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval no.:XJTU1AF2022LSK‐114). Given the retrospective design and use of de‐identified clinical imaging data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the approving committee.

Knee CT examinations performed between May 2021 and October 2024 were identified from the institutional imaging archive and screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: (1) radiographic evidence of medial compartment‐predominant OA or radiographically normal knees; (2) availability of thin‐slice clinical CT (≤ 1.0 mm slice thickness) acquired in a non‐weight‐bearing position; (3) availability of weight‐bearing radiographs within 90 days of CT for KL grading; and (4) complete visualization of the tibiofemoral joint without considerable motion or metallic artifact. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior major knee surgery (e.g., arthroplasty, osteotomy, or ligament reconstruction); (2) inflammatory arthropathy or metabolic bone disease affecting joint architecture; or (3) image quality unsuitable for volumetric analysis.

The final cohort comprised 72 knees from 54 patients (mean age, 65.0 ± 10.4 years; range, 36–87 years; 73.6% female) with radiographically normal to advanced medial KOA (KL grades 0–4). In cases where both knees from the same patient were eligible, each knee was treated as a separate analytic unit, and within‐subject correlation was accounted for in comparative analyses.

Image Segmentation and Bone Surface Reconstruction

CT volumes with a 1.0‐mm slice thickness and a 0.5‐mm reconstruction interval (50% slice overlap) were reconstructed with a high‐spatial‐frequency bone kernel to enhance cortical edge definition. Semi‐automatic segmentation of the distal femur and proximal tibia was then carried out in Materialize Mimics (version 21.0; Materialize, Leuven, Belgium) following grayscale windowing to optimize bone‐soft tissue contrast. A global Hounsfield unit threshold (Bone CT preset) was applied first, then refined locally through intensity profile‐guided calibration, with manual correction applied where needed. Bone isosurfaces were subsequently generated and exported in STL format.

Surface Mesh Generation and Resolution Control

All reconstructed bone surfaces were processed using Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). Upon import, each isosurface was inspected for topological defects, including degenerate faces, non‐manifold edges, and self‐intersections, and repaired using the software's auto‐repair function before any remeshing operations.

Isotropic remeshing was performed under identical settings for all surfaces to ensure comparability, with “Preserve Sharp Edges” and “Use Existing Boundaries Only” enabled. Each surface was then represented as a uniform triangular mesh, and mesh resolution was varied systematically by adjusting the target edge length.

For each knee, a high‐resolution reference mesh with a target edge length of 0.1 mm was generated (Figure S1). This mesh served as the subject‐specific baseline and provided a numerically dense digital representation of the segmented joint surface. Seven additional test meshes were also generated with target edge lengths of 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 mm. These mesh sizes were chosen to span the range of resolutions commonly used in knee modeling studies [2, 6, 18, 19, 23, 24, 25]. To avoid cumulative simplification errors, each test mesh was generated directly from the original segmented surface using the same remeshing workflow, rather than from a progressively coarsened model. This process produced a total of 576 femur–tibia mesh pairs, resulting in 1152 individual digital models for analysis.

ROI Definition and Extraction

The ROI was defined through a stepwise, anatomically constrained workflow to ensure consistent coverage of the medial compartment across all knees. A subject‐specific 3D bounding box was used to establish the anteroposterior and mediolateral extent of the medial tibial plateau. The medial intercondylar eminence served as the fixed lateral landmark, while the outer cortical rim defined the anterior, posterior, and medial borders. Within this bounding box, a closed B‐spline contour was fitted and refined in multiple planes to conform to the individual plateau geometry.

The final tibial ROI encompassed the medial subchondral surface and the articular transition toward the medial tibial spine, while excluding extra‐articular cortex and exophytic growths beyond the articular rim. To avoid confounding, marginal osteophytes extending > 2 mm beyond the native bone margins were excluded [26], as they represent peripheral bony remodeling and horizontal expansion rather than vertical joint‐space loss. Conversely, central osteophytes were retained, as they are strongly associated with full‐thickness cartilage defects that directly impact the weight‐bearing articular surface geometry [27]. In cases of advanced OA where marginal osteophytes obscured the native plateau boundary, the contour was guided by the continuity of the residual plateau surface. Denuded or severely narrowed regions were retained provided they remained within the native articular surface.

An opposing femoral patch was identified by extruding the tibial ROI boundary toward the medial femoral condyle. These paired ROIs were extracted as discrete surface patches and served as the initial ROI template from the original segmented model. This template was then mapped onto the dense 0.1 mm reference mesh and refined by projecting local normals from the tibial surface toward the femur. Regions lacking a direct femoral counterpart along the projection path were excluded, ensuring the final ROI represented only the true articulating interface. The refined ROI template was defined once per knee and applied without modification to all coarser mesh resolutions, maintaining spatial consistency throughout the analysis.

JSW Computation

3D JSW was defined as the minimum Euclidean distance from each tibial vertex to the opposing femoral surface within the finalized paired ROI. Distances were computed in MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) using accelerated closest‐point queries, with identical calculations applied across all mesh resolutions. In accordance with established 3D JSW protocols, distances greater than 10 mm were excluded as non‐articular outliers [2, 9, 19, 28].

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measures were minimum JSW (minJSW) and median JSW (medJSW). MinJSW was defined as the smallest retained tibia‐to‐femur distance and was used as a focal single‐point metric reflecting the narrowest point of joint‐space narrowing. MedJSW represented the central tendency of all retained tibia‐to‐femur distances within the finalized paired ROI and was used as a distribution‐based metric reflecting overall structural narrowing across the compartment.

To examine whether mesh‐resolution effects differed by disease severity, knees were stratified into early OA (KL 0–2; n = 37) and advanced OA (KL 3–4; n = 35) subgroups.

Analyses were performed at three levels: (1) cohort level, to characterize the overall effect of mesh resolution on JSW measurements; (2) subgroup level, to compare these effects between early and advanced OA; and (3) individual‐knee level, to determine whether measurements from coarser meshes remained sufficiently close to the 0.1‐mm reference mesh for subject‐level interpretation.

Agreement, Interchangeability, Discrimination, and Secondary Analyses

Individual‐Level Agreement and Interchangeability

Agreement between coarser meshes and the 0.1‐mm reference mesh at the individual‐knee level was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis. The difference was defined as: (1) ΔJSWm,i=JSWm,i−JSWref,i where JSWm,i is the computed JSW for knee i at mesh edge length m, and JSWref,i is the corresponding JSW from the 0.1‐mm reference mesh.

For each mesh resolution, mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA; mean bias ± 1.96 × SD of the differences) were calculated to quantify fixed bias and dispersion relative to the reference. Proportional bias was assessed by regressing the differences on their means, and overall concordance was summarized using Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC).

Relative percent error (RPE) was calculated for each knee to further characterize the magnitude of individual‐knee error relative to the reference value: (2) RPE=JSWm,i−JSWref,iJSWref,i×100% RPE values were then summarized at the cohort and subgroup levels for interpretation. Interchangeability was additionally evaluated using absolute error relative to the reference mesh. For each resolution, the proportion of knees in which the absolute error (∣ΔJSW∣) exceeded two predefined thresholds was recorded in the overall cohort and in OA severity‐stratified subgroups. The lower threshold (0.2 mm) was used to identify errors likely to affect sensitivity to subtle differences, whereas the higher threshold (0.5 mm) was used to identify errors likely to be clinically consequential. These thresholds were selected to assess whether mesh‐induced measurement error remained small relative to clinically relevant change thresholds reported in prior OA studies [2, 3, 10, 12].

OA Severity Discrimination

The ability of 3D JSW measurements to distinguish disease severity across mesh resolutions was assessed by comparing minJSW and medJSW values between early OA (KL 0–2) and advanced OA (KL 3–4) knees at each mesh resolution using Mann–Whitney U‐tests. p values were adjusted across mesh resolutions using the Holm method, and effect sizes were quantified using rank‐biserial correlation coefficients.

Geometric Surface Deviation

The geometric effect of remeshing relative to the original segmented isosurface was quantified using 3D point‐to‐surface deviation, summarized as root mean square (RMS) surface deviation. Vertex count within the ROI was also recorded to characterize the reduction in surface sampling across mesh sizes.

Processing Time and Feasibility

Computational feasibility was evaluated by recording wall‐clock time on a standardized mobile workstation (Intel Core i7‐12700H CPU, 40 GB RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU). These measurements were used to determine whether computational efficiency materially influenced practical mesh selection.

Reliability, Quality Control, and Blinding

Measurement Reliability and Precision

Intra‐observer repeatability was assessed by having the primary operator repeat the full image‐processing pipeline, including segmentation, after a washout period of at least 7 days. Inter‐observer reproducibility was assessed by a second independent operator processing the same knees. Reliability was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for intra‐observer agreement and ICC for inter‐observer agreement. The small detectable difference at the 95% level (SDD95) was defined as 1.96 × SD of the differences. The standard error of measurement (SEM) was also calculated.

Quality Control and Blinding

KL grading (0–4) was performed independently on standard weight‐bearing radiographs by two musculoskeletal radiologists blinded to subject identifiers and computational data; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Image analysis, segmentation, and ROI definition were performed by an orthopedic surgeon with over 4 years of experience in 3D reconstruction, and the resulting segmentations were independently reviewed by a musculoskeletal radiologist. For each knee, the reference mesh underwent visual and quantitative quality control, including overlay on the source CT data to confirm continuous cortical bone tracking and 3D deviation analysis against the segmented isosurface. To avoid registration bias, all JSW calculations were performed in the native CT coordinate system. Test mesh resolutions were assigned random anonymous codes to ensure blinded analysis, and the groups were unmasked only after all measurements had been finalized.

Statistical Analysis

To evaluate the statistical sensitivity of the sample for detecting discretization‐related effects, a sensitivity analysis was performed in G*Power (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) using a repeated‐measures framework (α = 0.05; 1 – β = 0.95). With 72 knees, the study was sensitive to a small effect size (f = 0.087).

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality and homoscedasticity were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively; nonparametric methods were applied when assumptions were violated. All tests were two‐sided with α = 0.05. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as n (%). For rank‐based sensitivity analyses, within‐subject effects were tested using the Friedman test with Kendall's W as the effect size; significant omnibus tests were followed by Nemenyi post hoc pairwise comparisons. Effects of mesh resolution and its interaction with KL grade were analyzed using mixed‐effects models with a random intercept for each subject (Satterthwaite approximation; bootstrap 95% CIs).

Results

Geometric Effects of Mesh Resolution

The 0.1‐mm reference meshes showed high geometric fidelity relative to the original segmentations, with all surface points within a ±0.05 mm tolerance and a mean RMS surface deviation of 0.0011 ± 0.0010 mm. At coarser mesh resolutions, geometric deviation increased and surface sampling density decreased (Figure 1). RMS surface deviation rose from 0.0011 mm at the 0.10‐mm reference mesh to 0.1268 mm at the 4.0‐mm mesh. Median vertex density within the ROI decreased from 118,300 vertices (IQR, 30,091; range, 86,675–199,760) at 0.10 mm to 14,848 (IQR, 4030; range, 10,009–27,368) at 0.3 mm, 5033 (IQR, 1376; range, 3671–9659) at 0.5 mm, 1249 (IQR 345; range, 957–2356) at 1.0 mm, and 72 (IQR, 20; range, 55–145) at 4.0 mm. The overall right‐skewed distribution of JSW values was maintained across mesh resolutions (Figure 2).

Cohort‐Level and Subgroup‐Level Effects of Mesh Coarsening on JSW

Across the full cohort, medJSW increased by 1.07 mm, from 4.07 ± 0.77 mm at the 0.10‐mm reference mesh to 5.14 ± 0.96 mm at the 4.0‐mm mesh. Over the same range, minJSW increased by 0.36 mm, from 1.98 ± 0.81 mm to 2.34 ± 0.81 mm (Table 1; Figure 3A,B).

To further quantify these relationships, mixed‐effects models estimated the rate of JSW inflation per unit increase in mesh edge length. For medJSW, the inflation rate was 0.303 mm per 1‐mm increase in mesh edge length in KL 0–2 knees (95% CI, 0.283–0.326; p < 0.001). This rate was significantly lower in KL 3–4 knees (interaction: −0.035 mm/mm; 95% CI, −0.065 to −0.006; p = 0.023), resulting in an estimated slope of 0.268 mm/mm. Conversely, the rate for minJSW was 0.081 mm/mm in KL 0–2 knees but was significantly higher in KL 3–4 knees (interaction: +0.023 mm/mm; 95% CI, 0.011–0.034; p < 0.001), corresponding to an estimated slope of 0.104 mm/mm.

OA Severity Discrimination Across Mesh Resolutions

At each mesh resolution, KL 3–4 knees had lower JSW values than KL 0–2 knees (all Holm‐adjusted p < 0.001). Rank‐biserial correlation decreased from 0.76 to 0.65 for medJSW and from 0.86 to 0.78 for minJSW as mesh size increased (Figure 5).

Agreement for Minimum JSW Across Mesh Resolutions

For minJSW, meshes up to 1.0 mm showed limited deviation from the 0.10‐mm reference (Figure 6). Within this range, fixed bias was 0.01–0.06 mm, SDD95 was 0.049–0.075 mm, and CCC ranged from 0.995 to 1.000 (Table 3). At 1.0 mm, RPE was 3.0% in the overall cohort. Across these resolutions, no knee exceeded the 0.5‐mm threshold, and only one of 72 knees (1.4%) exceeded the 0.2‐mm threshold (Table 4). Positive proportional bias was observed throughout this interval (slope, 0.0259–0.1834 mm/mm; all p < 0.001).

In subgroup analyses, RPE in KL 0–2 knees increased from 0.4% at 0.3 mm to 1.9% at 1.0 mm, with no knees exceeding either absolute‐error threshold. In KL 3–4 knees, RPE increased from 0.0% to 5.9%, and one knee (2.9%) exceeded the 0.2‐mm threshold at 1.0 mm. Bland–Altman plots for minJSW in early and advanced OA knees are shown in Figure 7.

At 2.0 mm, 14 of 72 knees (19.4%) exceeded the 0.2‐mm threshold, whereas none exceeded the 0.5‐mm threshold (Figure 9). Fixed bias was 0.14 mm, SDD95 was 0.117 mm, proportional bias was 0.2536 mm/mm (p < 0.001), and CCC was 0.979. Threshold exceedance was more frequent in KL 3–4 knees than in KL 0–2 knees (28.6% vs. 10.8%), with RPE values of 11.9% and 5.0%, respectively.

Further increases were observed at 3.0 and 4.0 mm. At 3.0 mm, 40 of 72 knees (55.6%) exceeded the 0.2‐mm threshold and three knees (4.2%) exceeded the 0.5‐mm threshold; fixed bias was 0.25 mm, SDD95 was 0.215 mm, and CCC was 0.936. At 4.0 mm, fixed bias increased to 0.36 mm, the upper 95% LoA reached 0.84 mm, and CCC decreased to 0.868; 53 of 72 knees (73.6%) exceeded the 0.2‐mm threshold and 17 knees (23.6%) exceeded the 0.5‐mm threshold. Overall RPE at 4.0 mm was 18.2%, with subgroup values of 12.4% in KL 0–2 knees and 29.9% in KL 3–4 knees.

Agreement for medJSW Across Mesh Resolutions

For medJSW, the smallest differences from the 0.10‐mm reference mesh were observed at 0.3 mm (Figure 6). At this resolution, fixed bias was +0.07 mm, SDD95 was 0.073 mm, and CCC was 0.994 (Table 5); overall RPE was 1.7%, with one of 72 knees (1.4%) exceeding the 0.2‐mm threshold and none exceeding the 0.5‐mm threshold (Table 6). RPE values were similar in KL 0–2 and KL 3–4 knees (1.5% and 1.7%, respectively).

At 0.5 mm, 32 of 72 knees (44.4%) exceeded the 0.2‐mm threshold, although none exceeded the 0.5‐mm threshold (Figure 9). Fixed bias was +0.17 mm, SDD95 was 0.079 mm, and CCC was 0.968. The corresponding proportions were 45.9% in KL 0–2 knees and 42.9% in KL 3–4 knees, with RPE values of 3.7% and 4.6%, respectively. Proportional bias at this resolution was not statistically significant (0.0237 mm/mm; p = 0.121).

At 0.7 mm, 48 of 72 knees (66.7%) exceeded the 0.2‐mm threshold and nine knees (12.5%) exceeded the 0.5‐mm threshold. Fixed bias increased to +0.29 mm, SDD95 to 0.122 mm, and CCC decreased to 0.912; proportional bias became significant (0.0562 mm/mm; p = 0.034). In subgroup analyses, the proportions exceeding the 0.2‐mm threshold were 73.0% in KL 0–2 knees and 60.0% in KL 3–4 knees, whereas the corresponding proportions exceeding the 0.5‐mm threshold were 18.9% and 5.7%. Bland–Altman plots for medJSW in early and advanced OA knees are shown in Figure 8.

From 1.0 mm onward, fixed bias and threshold exceedance increased further, while concordance decreased. At 1.0 mm, 30 of 72 knees (41.5%) exceeded the 0.5‐mm threshold. This proportion increased to 70.8% at 2.0 mm (51 of 72 knees) and 86.1% at 4.0 mm (62 of 72 knees). By 4.0 mm, fixed bias had reached +1.08 mm, the upper 95% LoA reached 2.10 mm, and CCC had decreased to 0.461. Overall RPE at 4.0 mm was 26.3%, with subgroup values of 24.6% in KL 0–2 knees and 28.8% in KL 3–4 knees.

Representative spatial maps and corresponding distance histograms showed that mesh coarsening first affected the narrowest‐distance regions (Figures S2 and S3). In both OA subgroups, the number of surface vertices in the smallest JSW bins decreased, with corresponding redistribution of surface area toward adjacent wider bins.

Processing Time

The complete CT‐to‐3D JSW workflow required less than 10 min per knee. Semi‐automatic segmentation accounted for most of the total processing time and required longer in advanced OA than in early OA (8.38 vs. 5.59 min). Automated JSW computation remained below 1 min across mesh resolutions, requiring 2.84 s at 0.3 mm and 1.52 s at 0.5 mm.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated how surface mesh resolution influences CT‐derived 3D JSW measurements in medial KOA. Specifically, it examined whether sensitivity to mesh resolution varied by summary metric, OA severity, and level of interpretation, and whether deviations from the 0.1 mm reference resolution exceeded clinically meaningful thresholds.

Establishment of Fine Mesh Resolution

The importance of systematic mesh/grid convergence analysis is well established in computational biomechanics. For instance, Smith et al. [29] evaluated the influence of cartilage mesh density on predicted model outputs in a dynamic healthy knee model. Their work demonstrated that coarser meshes deviated from a fine‐resolution reference, but all metrics converged within 1% once sufficient density was reached [29]. Similar approaches have been widely applied across joints, tissue types, and quantitative 3D metrics, highlighting the broad applicability of convergence‐based methods [20, 21, 25, 30, 31]. Importantly, the need for convergence analysis is independent of the specific output metric (e.g., contact pressure, stress or JSW) and 3D imaging modality (CT/CBCT or MRI) [20, 21, 29, 31]. This reinforces the view that mesh resolution is a fundamental determinant of surface model fidelity.

Following these principles, the 0.1 mm mesh/grid (> 100,000 vertices per ROI) served as a fine‐resolution numerical reference to quantify surface discretization error. Operating as a densely sampled surface approximation, the fine‐resolution reference far exceeded the vertex density of coarser mesh grids, and measurements converged at the 0.3 mm resolution (14,848 vertices per ROI) for all tested conditions, including metrics (minJSW, medJSW), disease severities (KL 0–4), and levels of analysis (group‐ and subject‐level) for full medial compartment analysis. By establishing this rigorous baseline, the present study translates resolution convergence principles from computational biomechanics to CT‐based 3D‐JSW mapping, providing the first systematic evaluation of mesh resolution thresholds for clinical joint space quantification.

Beyond the 0.3 mm converged model, coarser grids progressively increased both focal (minJSW) and compartment‐wide (medJSW) JSW values, with substantially larger effects on medJSW than on minJSW.

Overestimation Magnitude and Underlying Mechanism of JSW

Coarser mesh grids systematically overestimate JSW, and the magnitude of overestimation increases with larger grid edge length. For medJSW, the fixed bias rises from a negligible +0.07 mm (1.7%) at the converged 0.3 mm resolution to a clinically significant +1.07 mm (26.3%) at 4.0 mm resolution. Within the same resolution range, the bias of minJSW increases from +0.01 mm (0.5%) to +0.36 mm (18.2%) (Figure 3, Tables 4 and 6).

Notably, this effect is modulated by disease severity. For early OA knee joints (KL 0–2) with wider baseline joint space, the absolute overestimation magnitude of medJSW (+1.13 mm) is slightly higher than that of late OA knee joints (+1.01 mm). However, due to the narrower baseline joint space of late OA knee joints (KL 3–4), the same absolute error leads to a larger relative bias (28.8% vs. 24.6%). The vulnerability of focal indicators is more prominent: the relative error of minJSW in late OA (29.9%) is far higher than that in early OA (12.4%), and its increase with disease stage far exceeds that of medJSW. This disproportionate amplification effect indicates that when the joint space has been damaged, the narrowest region is more sensitive to discretization errors. The mixed effects model confirms that disease stage significantly modulates the association between mesh resolution and measurement bias (p < 0.05).

Another key finding is that coarser grids may have extremely high repeatability, but still produce JSW values with systematic bias. For example, the 2.0 mm resolution has excellent repeatability (ICC of medJSW is 0.995, ICC of minJSW is 0.997), but it leads to a large systematic overestimation: compared with the 0.1 mm reference resolution, medJSW is overestimated by +0.81 mm, and minJSW is overestimated by +0.14 mm (Tables 3 and 5). These errors exceed the typical annual joint space loss rate, which is about 0.06–0.28 mm/year based on longitudinal reference data [32]. In addition, the CCC of medJSW drops to 0.587, indicating that high repeatability alone may bring false credibility to measurement results, which are inherently biased due to inaccurate surface representation.

The core mechanism of grid‐induced bias is the mismatch between the geometric complexity of OA joints and computational mesh resolution. As the resolution decreases, the grid edge length increases, and the number of vertices drops sharply (Figure 1). This dimensionality reduction degrades the surface representation effect, forcing the grid to transform from a large number of anatomically conforming small triangles to a smaller number of larger, flatter facets (Figure 2). Coarse grids cannot spatially resolve local features, which is manifested through two different mechanisms: averaging effect and topological feature erasure.

The averaging effect originates from the sharp drop of vertex density in the narrowest region. A larger grid edge length forces the grid to interpolate the gap instead of fitting the underlying anatomical structure, thus systematically underestimating pathological complexity and consistently overestimating JSW. When the resolution is higher than the physical width of anatomical features, topological feature erasure occurs, leading to the disappearance of the feature from the computational model (Figures S2 and S3).

The above two mechanisms act in parallel, but their dominant status varies under different anatomical conditions. In early OA (KL 0–2), the joint space is still relatively wide, and the averaging effect is dominant, which elevates the values of compartment‐level summary indicators such as medJSW, which represent the summary of measurement values at each point of the articular surface. However, in late OA (KL 3–4), the joint space has been severely narrowed, and the main risk shifts to local point‐based indicators. The measurement of minJSW relies on identifying a single vertex in the narrowest region, so once the mesh resolution crosses the topological erasure threshold and all vertices in this region are lost, the minJSW value will show a false increase.

The spatial evidence of these effects is most obvious when the resolution is ≥ 0.7 mm. In a case of late OA knee joint, the number of vertices representing near bone–bone contact (0–0.5 mm) dropped from 114 at 0.1 mm resolution to 0 at 1.0 mm resolution. Similarly, in a case of early OA knee joint, the narrowest JSW region (2–3 mm) was completely eliminated at 0.7 mm resolution. These data indicate that coarse grids systematically and preferentially eliminate the narrowest joint space while elevating the measurement values of adjacent regions. This principle is not unique to JSW mapping, and similar effects have also been confirmed in finite element modeling of the hip joint: geometric simplification artificially reduces peak contact pressure and increases contact area [33].

Clinical Relevance of Mesh‐Induced Errors in JSW Assessment

The clinical relevance of 3D‐JSW measurement errors can be clarified by comparing with the established minimum clinically important difference benchmarks for structural severity. The Osteoarthritis Research Society International–Outcome Measures in Rheumatoid Arthritis Clinical Trials initiative proposes that a 0.5 mm difference in medial JSW measured by X‐ray is a general threshold for judging structural loss [2]. The lower the baseline JSW value, the worse the clinical outcome: for every 0.1 mm difference in JSW, the future risk of receiving knee arthroplasty increases by 14% [10]. Even under optimal fluoroscopic guidance, a measurement difference of at least 0.59 mm is required to exceed the measurement error; without fluoroscopic guidance, this threshold rises to 1.29 mm [8]. Therefore, clinically significant JSW differences on the order of 0.1–0.5 mm are at or far below the detection limit of conventional radiography.

The accuracy of modern 3D imaging is much higher. CT 3D joint space mapping (JSM) with isotropic 0.37 mm imaging can detect JSW differences as low as ±0.1–0.2 mm in the central region of the joint [2, 12]. When using a clinical CT protocol with a slice thickness of 1.0–1.5 mm, validation studies based on high‐resolution peripheral quantitative CT (HR‐pQCT) show that the optimal measurement uncertainty achievable by clinical CT JSM in the above region is also about 0.2 mm [12]. This is comparable to the 1.0 mm slice thickness used in this study. Notably, existing studies have confirmed that a 0.5 mm difference in 3D‐JSW can distinguish each Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade [2], indicating that the 0.5 mm X‐ray benchmark still has clinical validity and structural significance in 3D scenarios.

MRI studies further show that for every 0.1 mm decrease in baseline central medial tibial cartilage thickness, the probability of extensive full‐thickness cartilage loss increases by 20% (OR = 1.20) [3], indicating that clinically meaningful structural signals are inherently focal and small in magnitude. Longitudinal data confirm that the 2‐year change magnitude of MRI cartilage thickness and 3D CT JSW is also on the order of 0.2 mm [4], indicating that submillimeter differences reflect real biological changes. These findings are directly related to 3D JSW assessment, as CT‐based JSW and MRI‐based cartilage thickness have good correlation in both cross‐sectional and longitudinal studies [4]. As described in Section 4.2, coarse grids systematically attenuate such focal signals, especially in advanced disease, and this local attenuation accumulates into the overall bias of summary indicators such as medJSW, highlighting the clinical necessity of high‐precision baseline 3D JSW measurement.

Implications for Orthopedic Decision‐Making

Combined with the above benchmarks, mesh resolution determines whether 3D‐JSW measurement is clinically acceptable. At the coarsest resolution (2.0–4.0 mm), the measurement error is clinically significant. For medJSW, the errors of the vast majority of knee joints exceed both the 0.2 mm accuracy threshold (94.4%–97.2%) and the 0.5 mm clinical benchmark (70.8%–86.1%) (Table 6). Even for minJSW, a large proportion of measurement errors exceed the 0.2 mm threshold (19.4%–73.6%), and at the coarsest resolution, nearly a quarter (23.6%) of the errors exceed 0.5 mm. Although the error decreases at 2.0 mm resolution, the compartment‐level indicators are still severely impaired. Therefore, within the resolution range of 2.0–4.0 mm, the accuracy advantage of 3D imaging is completely offset: the overestimation magnitude of compartment‐level JSW in most knee joints exceeds the boundary of clinical significance, and even the error rate of focal indicators is unacceptable.

These findings have direct implications for studies relying on 3D‐JSW for surgical stratification. Wang et al. [11] reported that the classification performance of tibial subchondral bone area for unicompartmental knee arthroplasty and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is better than that of JSW indicators (AUC 0.71 vs. 0.55). The JSW measurement in that study was based on a 2 mm × 2 mm sampling size, which is consistent with the 2.0 mm grid parameters evaluated in this study. This study found that at this resolution, 94.3% of the samples of late OA knee joints (KL 3–4) had medJSW errors exceeding the 0.2 mm threshold, 68.6% exceeded the 0.5 mm benchmark, and 28.6% of the samples had minJSW errors exceeding 0.2 mm. Sparse sampling is likely the reason for the poor discrimination performance of JSW in that study, suggesting that patient stratification studies need to adopt a finer mesh resolution, which is particularly critical in the advanced disease population for whom surgical decisions need to be made.

When a finer resolution (0.5–1.0 mm) is used, the error rate decreases, but the error of whole‐compartment indicators is still measurable. The minJSW measurement results within this resolution range are basically acceptable: at 1.0 mm resolution, no knee joint has minJSW error exceeding 0.5 mm, and only 1.4% exceed 0.2 mm (the proportion is 0% at 0.5 mm resolution) (Table 4). However, the error of medJSW is still considerable: at 1.0 mm resolution, 81.9% of knee joints have errors exceeding 0.2 mm, and 41.5% exceed 0.5 mm; at 0.5 mm resolution, 44.4% exceed 0.2 mm, and no sample has error exceeding 0.5 mm (Table 6). Therefore, if the clinical focus is on minJSW and the trade‐off of whole‐compartment accuracy is acceptable, the grid parameters in this range are applicable.

Independent research evidence from primitive foot and ankle models has confirmed that coarse grids within this range (edge length 0.5–2 mm) overestimate narrow gaps [20]. Lisonbee et al. [20] reported that the relative error range of 1 mm joint space is 2.52% ± 0.83% at 0.5 mm resolution and 22.36% ± 11.36% at 2.0 mm resolution. In this clinical cohort, the error is also modulated by the baseline gap width, but the pattern is more complex and related to the measurement indicators. At resolutions from 0.5 mm to 2.0 mm, the minJSW error of late OA is 1.5% to 11.9% (RPE value), while the medJSW error is significantly higher (RPE 4.6%–21.1%). In addition, at all resolutions, the relative error of minJSW in late OA is basically more than twice that of early OA (e.g., 1.5 vs. 0.8% at 0.5 mm resolution, 29.9 vs. 12.4% at 4.0 mm resolution). Different from Lisonbee's study, which only used three mesh resolutions and idealized geometric structures, this study confirmed that the results converge at 0.3 mm resolution in a clinical cohort covering KL 0–4, and clarified the finer resolution threshold corresponding to the optimal 3D‐JSW assessment.

Other studies using similar high‐resolution acquisition (isotropic voxel 0.37 mm) but different measurement frameworks further highlight the importance of fine mesh resolution [2, 19]. Segal et al. [19] directly calculated JSW through a dense bone surface mesh, measured the distance between the centers of adjacent triangular elements, and generated about 30,000 measurement values per compartment (corresponding to about 15,000 vertices), with excellent test–retest reliability (ICC 0.90–0.97). In contrast, Turmezei et al. [2] used JSM, only using a mesh of 2000 vertices as the framework, and performed deconvolution sampling on the underlying CT data to generate a distance‐based JSW map; notably, the reproducibility and repeatability for the joint space edges were consistently worse in that study. This indicates that even with high‐resolution imaging, sparse surface representation will reduce measurement fidelity, especially in regions with complex geometric structures or edge regions. This current study found that the measurement results converge at a mesh resolution of 0.3 mm (corresponding to about 14,800 vertices or 30,000 facets per ROI). This data‐driven benchmark is consistent with the sampling density used in high‐reliability studies of whole‐compartment assessment, and still has computational feasibility under conventional clinical CT acquisition.

Feasibility

The results of this study also have important implications for the clinical feasibility of surface‐based 3D JSW mapping. Specifically, the processing workflow of this study is built on real clinical CT acquisition data, without the need for dedicated hardware. The resolution of this imaging protocol (1.0 mm slice thickness, 0.5 mm reconstruction interval) is higher than that of conventional clinical bone scans (usually using 2–3 mm slice thickness) and fully meets the standard range of quantitative 3D joint imaging. Previous studies have shown that a slice spacing of ≤ 1 mm is suitable for long bone surface reconstruction [34], and thicker slices could be close to the clinically acceptable upper limit [35]. Additionally, 50% reconstruction overlap can reduce stair‐step artifacts without additional radiation exposure, thereby improving surface reconstruction fidelity. Importantly, hip JSM studies have confirmed that submillimeter measurement accuracy can be achieved using a clinical CT protocol with a slice thickness of ≤ 1.5 mm, which provides support for the clinical practicability of the workflow of this study [12]. In addition, the baseline minJSW values of the KL 0–2 and KL 3–4 groups in this cohort are highly consistent with the values reported by Fritz et al. [9] using the NWB‐CT protocol, confirming that this study cohort can represent the patient population receiving clinical CT assessment for medial KOA.

When performing whole‐compartment analysis, the processing time of this workflow for a single knee is less than 10 min, most of which is spent on semi‐automatic segmentation: the segmentation time of late OA knee joints (8.38 min) is longer than that of early OA knee joints (5.59 min), because the anatomical geometry of severe cases is more complex and more expert correction is required. Therefore, this time consumption only corresponds to the attributes of the existing segmentation method, not the automated 3D JSW mapping framework itself, which can be adapted to other segmentation strategies, including fully automatic segmentation schemes.

Downstream processing including automatic grid reconstruction and JSW calculation is very fast even with fine resolution (< 1 min). Specifically, JSW calculation only takes 2.84 s (IQR 0.77) at 0.3 mm resolution, and only 1.52 s (IQR 0.26) at 0.5 mm resolution, confirming that fine grids do not bring excessive computational burden to surface distance calculation. Therefore, reducing the grid density can only save very little time, but will compromise the measurement fidelity. Importantly, this single workflow can simultaneously generate multi‐scale complementary structural outputs, including focal JSW measurement values, distribution summary indicators, area measurement values of predefined JSW intervals, quantitative results of whole bone area, and spatial distribution maps of whole‐compartment disease patterns (Figures S1–S3). Such comprehensive output features can achieve detailed 3D structural characterization for individualized patient assessment of medial KOA.

Limitations

While this study has multiple strengths, it also has several limitations. First, the absolute anatomical fidelity of surface reconstruction is still limited by the underlying voxel size, point spread function, and inherent partial volume effect. The high‐resolution protocol adopted in this study can alleviate but not completely eliminate these limitations. Second, this study is a cross‐sectional study, which does not directly evaluate the longitudinal changes of 3D JSW. However, the analysis carried out at the baseline stage clarified the degree of grid‐related bias and emphasized that consistent mesh resolution should be adopted in subsequent longitudinal studies to avoid conflating technical errors with structural changes. Third, although NWBCT can provide a controllable benchmark, the absolute joint space value may change under physiological load. The error thresholds of 0.20 mm and 0.50 mm provide a clinically relevant framework for measurement reliability; extending this study to WBCT is a logical next step, especially since the study found that minJSW measurements are more sensitive to discretization errors in narrower joint spaces. Finally, this study focuses on medial compartment disease, which is the most commonly affected site of KOA. Although the fine resolution threshold established in this study can be generalized to other articular surfaces with high probability, the maximum acceptable grid edge length may vary for different joints such as the hip and shoulder joints. Therefore, before direct application to other anatomical sites, it is recommended to confirm through joint‐specific validation or sensitivity analysis.

Future Prospects

To realize the clinical translation of 3D JSW mapping, optimal CT acquisition parameters (such as slice thickness and reconstruction interval) need to be clarified to ensure high longitudinal accuracy for analysis of all compartments across KL grades 0–4, and validation should be carried out with external anatomical gold standards. Subsequent studies should also evaluate whether the composite clinical model integrating fine‐resolution 3D JSW mapping, local distance threshold, and bone area can improve patient stratification when selecting between unicompartmental replacement or TKA. Combining structural narrowing indicators (such as the surface area with JSW < 2 mm) with subchondral bone markers (such as tibial bone area, osteophyte volume or location) could provide feature characterization with more spatial details than traditional summary indicators. Finally, since ROI definition in most JSW workflows still depends on operators, developing automated deep learning tools to consistently delineate ROI boundaries on the premise of retaining clinically relevant joint features and considering pathological structures such as intercondylar fossa osteophytes or tibial enlargement is essential to achieving reliable cross‐study comparisons, especially for spatial and distributional 3D JSW indicators.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that surface mesh resolution significantly influences 3D JSW measurements, with effects modulated by the selected metric, disease stage, and level of analysis. Notably, the resolution settings that preserve OA group discrimination may still fail to preserve subject‐level agreement. Using a rigorous 0.1 mm reference resolution, specific thresholds were established to balance fidelity and practicality: a 0.3 mm grid provided the most consistent performance across all conditions; 0.5 mm was also acceptable under a less stringent error tolerance for medJSW; and up to 1.0 mm sufficed only for identifying the narrowest point (minJSW). Critically, at coarser resolutions, the overestimation of the baseline JSW estimates becomes large enough to potentially alter the perceived structural severity of medial compartment narrowing, carrying risks for clinical misinterpretation and treatment decisions based on joint space assessment. Therefore, recognizing and controlling for this bias is essential to ensure precise, reproducible, and interpretable 3D JSW assessments, with clear relevance for clinical research, patient stratification, and cross‐study comparability.
