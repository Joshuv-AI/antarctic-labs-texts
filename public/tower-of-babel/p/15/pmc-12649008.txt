Highly stretchable and conductive liquid metal-based multifunctional organohydrogel for behavior monitoring and chronic diabetic wounds treatment
Authors: Lantao Wang, Shuting Peng, Wenxiu Chen, Qingxin Wu, Zhengfeng Lu, Zhengxiao Wang, Yanxin Lu, Yu Gu, Xiaofei Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic wounds are one of the most serious complications of diabetes mellitus caused by impaired blood vessel formation, nerve damage, excessive inflammation, hypoxia, and persistent infections, which severely affects the quality of life of patients. Conductive hydrogel-based flexible epidermal sensors have great potential for personal medical monitoring and diabetic chronic wound therapy. Combining electrical stimulation (ES) therapy with motor activity monitoring can guide clinical practice and thus radically improve treatment outcomes. In this study, a multifunctional conductive Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel flexible sensor with antimicrobial, antioxidant, and biocompatible properties was synthesized using gelatin (Gel) and 2,3,4-trihydroxybenzaldehyde (TBA) as the substrates, and liquid metal (LM) as the functional filler. Its mechanical properties and frost resistance were enhanced by the Hofmeister effect and solvent substitution strategy. The Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel has excellent mechanical properties (1.2 MPa modulus and 400 % strain) and high electrical conductivity (673.76 S/m). The corresponding measurement factor (GF) values were 0.26 (0–50 %) and 2.88 (50–200 %) at 0–200 % strain, thus can sensitively and accurately detect movement behavior of different joints. In addition, the combination of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel with ES promotes diabetic wound healing by coordinating angiogenesis and macrophage M2-type polarization through the VEGF/ERK pathway while maintaining vascular cell-neuronal cell intercellular crosstalk. This study provides a valuable reference for the development of multifunctional conductive organohydrogel sensors for chronic diabetic wound repair and personal health monitoring.Keywords: Liqual metal, Conductive hydrogel, Chronic diabetic wound, Macrophage, Angiogenesis, Neurorestoration

Introduction

Chronic non-healing wounds have long been a major burden on healthcare, especially for diabetic patients [1]. The microenvironment of diabetic wounds, characterized by hyperglycemia, hypoxia, and persistent inflammation, produces continuous oxidative stress, which inhibits multiple signaling pathways. Among them, inhibiting the synthesis of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and phosphorylation of ERK (p-ERK) can hinder angiogenesis [2]. Impaired angiogenesis disrupts neurovascular crosstalk, thereby hindering the nerve regeneration process and also impeding the conversion of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the reparative M2 phenotype [3,4]. The prolonged presence of M1 macrophages leads to persistent inflammation at the wound site, eliminating positive feedback on VEGF expression and further impairing angiogenesis [5,6]. In addition, bacterial infections (such as Staphylococcus aureus) exacerbate inflammatory responses and synergistically impede wound healing [7]. Therefore, a combination strategy promoting angiogenesis, nerve regeneration, and macrophage M2 polarization through the VEGF/ERK pathway for the treatment of diabetic chronic wounds is a promising direction.

During the healing process of diabetic wounds, an endogenous electric field (EF) is generated by the trans-epithelial potential gradient [8,9]. This electric field plays an irreplaceable role in regulating cellular behavior and promoting wound healing and regeneration. Inspired by the endogenous electric field, applying exogenous electrical stimulation (ES) to the wound can effectively accelerate the wound healing process [10]. Existing research has confirmed that exogenous electrical stimulation can increase local microcirculation blood flow and improve the efficiency of oxygen and nutrient delivery to wounds [11]. At the same time, electrical stimulation helps increase VEGF expression and secretion, promotes fibroblast proliferation and migration, thereby significantly improving wound healing efficiency [12]. However, exogenous ES requires the implantation of electrodes near the wound surface to provide current, rather than covering the entire wound surface, which severely affects the therapeutic efficacy of exogenous ES.

Conductive hydrogels with high sensitivity and multifunctional properties are considered to be excellent materials for wearable devices [13], which have received much attention for human behavior monitoring and wound treatment [14,15]. Conductive organohydrogels mimic and enhance the EF at the wound site and can provide a conductive medium for intercellular signaling, which is critical for directing epithelial cell migration and re-establishing the physiological EF that has been interrupted by injury [16]. Conductive hydrogels allow exogenous current conduction, promoting the transmission of external electrical stimulation currents, thereby promoting angiogenesis and the recovery of peripheral nerve function [17,18]. Conventional conductive materials such as metals (Ag, Au, Pt) [18], and carbon nanomaterials [19], limit the application of conductive hydrogels due to their inherent rigidity, dispersion difficulties, interfacial mismatches, and other potential drawbacks [20]. In addition, the high water content conductive hydrogels have limited applicability to extreme environments, especially sub-zero temperatures, reducing their electrical and mechanical properties [21,22]. To solve this issue, glycerol can form hydrogen bonding interactions with water molecules through three hydroxyl groups, thus effectively inhibiting the formation of ice crystals at sub-zero temperatures and preventing water evaporation of the hydrogels [23]. In addition, the conductivity and mechanical strength of hydrogels can be modulated by ions using the Hofmeister effect, which significantly enhances their potential for wearable and biomedical applications [24]. In recent years, liquid metal (LM) has shown great potential in the fabrication of advanced flexible wearable devices due to their high electrical conductivity, excellent deformability, and fluidic properties at room temperature [25]. LM can be viewed as active fillers that can be introduced into the polymer network of hydrogels to improve their mechanical properties [26]. Therefore, the highly stretchable and conductive LM-based organohydrogel can solve the problems of poor conductivity and mechanical properties of traditional conductive hydrogels and enhance their environmental and clinical applicability.

In this study, a novel highly conductive, stretchable and anti-freeze multifunctional LM-based organohydrogel (Gel-TBA-LM) was designed via Hofmeister effect and solvent substitution strategy. The LM was firstly dispersed with 2,3,4-trihydroxybenzaldehyde (TBA) and mixed with Gelatin (Gel), and the incorporation of TBA conferred good antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties to the hydrogel. Moreover, the introduction of TBA with aldehyde and phenolic hydroxyl groups improved the mechanical properties of the hydrogel through Schiff base bonding with the Gel and coordination bonding with LM. Furthermore, soaking the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel in a water/glycerol binary solvent containing (NH4)2SO4 for solvent substitution and salting out not only enhanced the mechanical properties of the organohydrogel, but also maintained their excellent mechanical and electrical stability in extremely cold environments. The resulting Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel has excellent mechanical properties (1.2 MPa tensile modulus, 400 % strain, 9.4 MJ m−3 toughness) stable electrical properties and high sensitivity, and the measurement factor (GF) values of 0–200 % strain is 0.26 (0–50 %) and 2.88 (50–200 %), respectively, with a fast response time of 173 ms. Finally, the sensing properties of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel were verified by detecting human motion. In vitro and in vivo studies have shown that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel sensors can effectively inhibit bacterial infection. At the same time, their binding with ES coordinates angiogenesis and macrophage M2 polarization through the VEGF/ERK pathway, maintains the connection between vascular cells and neurons, and promotes the healing of diabetic wounds. Therefore, multifunctional Gel-TBA-LM conductive organohydrogels are expected to have broad application prospects in bioelectronic materials.

Results and discussion

Design and application of gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

Fig. 1 schematic illustration to sequential synthesis of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel for motion monitoring and diabetic wound healing. LM with melting point at 16 °C was firstly dispersed in TBA solution via sonication, Fig. S1 shows that the TBA-LM solution could be stably stored for at least 12 h, in sharply contrast with LM in water without TBA, which exhibited severe phase separation in 1 h. Then desired amounts of TBA-LM suspension was mixed with 60 wt% Gel solution to prepare Gel-TBA-LM pre-hydrogel suspensions. During hydrogel formation, on one hand, TBA with aldehyde and phenolic hydroxyl groups formed Schiff base reaction and hydrogen bond interaction with Gel, on the other hand, TBA dispersed LM and chelated with Ga3+, which improved the mechanical properties of Gel. This Gel-TBA-LM hydrogel preparation demonstrated that the TBA was utilized as a good interfacial stabilizer involved in encapsulating LM particles and fabricating conductive hydrogels. Finally, Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel was obtained by cooling down the pre-hydrogel suspension followed with soaking in water/glycerol binary solvent containing (NH4)2SO4 for solvent replacement and salting out [27]. The permeable SO42− will enhance hydrophobic effect and the hydrogen bonding on the polymer chain segments [28], and the glycerol molecule contains abundant hydroxyl groups, which can form strong hydrogen bonds with free water molecules, Gel and TBA. Therefore, the designed Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel effectively inhibited the crystallization of water molecules at low temperatures, and exhibited excellent mechanical and conductive properties [29].

Characterization of gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

The short duration of sonication treatment to the LM in this work resulted in the wide size distribution of LM microdroplets. The relatively larger LM microdroplets were settled down and assembled at the bottom of the Gel matrix due to the gravity, as shown in Fig. 2a. Herein, we regarded the surface enriched with LM microdroplets as the lower surface. Fig. 2a shows the SEM characterizations of the lower and upper surfaces of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel. The lower surface shows an enrichment of LM droplets compared to the upper surface. FTIR was used to study the interactions between the components within the hydrogel. As can be seen from Fig. 2b, the characteristic peak at 3309 cm−1 associated with the O-H stretching in Gel-TBA-LM hydrogels shifted, indicating the formation of intramolecular hydrogen bonds [30]. The C-H bending peak shifted from 1446.2 cm−1 to 1443.9 cm−1, which could be an indication of C-H bending due to the increase in bond length and the coordination of Ga3+ with TBA. The spectrum of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel shows a weakened peak at 1500.9 cm−1, which suggests cross-linking of the N-H group in Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel. Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel at 1614.9 cm−1 shows strong stretching peaks which are caused by superposition of C=N and amide vibrations, confirming the formation of Schiff bases. In addition, Fig. 2c clearly shows the remarkable stretching ability of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel. The addition of both TBA and LM increased the tensile length of gelatin without the introduction of the Hofmeister effect, and the tensile length of the hydrogel reached its maximum when both TBA and Hofmeister effect were added.

Mechanical performance of the gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

Favorable mechanical properties are necessary for hydrogels to function in a variety of environments. The influence of different content and proportions of TBA, LM, Hofmeister effect and solvent replacement strategies on the mechanical properties of organohydrogel was performed using tensile tests as a reference index. Due to the synergistic effect of different interactions generated by the organohydrogel, the designed physically and chemically crosslinked Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel have excellent mechanical properties. As shown in Fig. 3a that the obtained Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel had high flexibility and toughness and were easy to stretch, knot and twist. Fig. 3b and c shows the effect of TBA content on the tensile properties of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel. When the concentration of (NH4)2SO4 was chosen to be 30 % and the amount of LM was fixed at 0.1 g/mL, the tensile strain gradually increased and tensile modulus gradually decreased as the content of TBA was increased from 0 wt% to 5 wt%, which was due to the Schiff base reaction and hydrogen bonding forces increasing the internal cross-linking of the hydrogel. When the concentration of TBA was 5 wt%, both strain and toughness reached their maximum values of 426 % and 9.4 MJ m−3, respectively (Fig. S2). However, when a higher concentration of TBA (8 wt%) was introduced, it might lead to structural disorder of the hydrogel and hinder the formation of triple helix-ordered structure in the Gel, which resulted in a continuous decrease in strain, tensile modulus, and toughness of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel [31,32]. Furthermore, the mechanical performance of the organohydrogel was measured with the various mass ratios of TBA to LM. As shown in Fig. 3d and e, with the TBA concentration fixed at 5 wt%, both the stress and tensile modulus significantly increased to 6 MPa and 1.5 MPa respectively as the LM content increased. Specifically, when the mass ratios of TBA to LM reached 1:2 and 1:4, similar strain and elastic modulus values were observed at 426 %, 0.9 MPa and 405 %, 1.2 MPa, respectively. However, it should be noted that the elongation at break of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel decreased with the continuous addition of LM, and the toughness decreased significantly when the mass ratio of TBA to LM exceeded 1:8 (Fig. S3). This phenomenon may be attributed to the over densification of the crosslinked network due to the high LM content, which makes the hydrogel brittle and stiff. Notably, the hydrogels exhibited skin-like modulus (∼1 MPa) when the concentration of added TBA was 5 % and m(TBA):m(LM) = 1:2 or 1:4 (Fig. 3c–e). Subsequently, we chose a TBA concentration of 5 % with m(TBA):m(LM) = 1:4 to investigate the effects of the Hofmeister effect and solvent substitution strategy on the mechanical properties of the hydrogels.

Mechanical performance and fatigue resistance of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel. a) Photographs of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel in (i) initial state, (ii) stretch, (iii) knot and (iv) bending. b) tensile stress-tensile strain curves and c) tensile modulus for different TBA concentrations. d)tensile stress-tensile strain curves and e) tensile modulus for different mass ratio of TBA to LM. f) tensile stress-tensile strain curves and g) tensile modulus for soaking in (NH4)2SO4. h) Tensile stress-tensile strain curves of water, glycerol as solvent and solvent substitution. i) cyclic loading-unloading curves of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel at different strains (100–250 %), and j) Continuous loading-unloading curves at 100 % strain for a total of 100 cycles without stationary intervals.

As shown in Fig. 3f and g, the hydrogels immersed in (NH4)2SO4 had higher tensile strain and elastic modulus like that of skin. In addition to this, the tensile properties and mechanical strength of the hydrogel can be further enhanced by the addition of TBA and LM through Schiff base reaction, coordination, and hydrogen bonding forces. Interestingly, the hydrogels prepared by the solvent substitution strategy containing a 50 % glycerol/water mixture were found to have better mechanical strength and tensile properties compared to those prepared with water as the solvent or with the binary solvent of glycerol/water (50 % glycerol, 50 % water) (Fig. 3h). This may be attributed to the permeable SO42− and abundant hydroxyl groups in the glycerol molecules that enhance the hydrophobic effect and hydrogen bonding of the polymer chain segments, thus improving the mechanical properties of Gel-TBA-LM hydrogels [33]. However, the introduction of excess glycerol can reduce the cross-linking degree of organohydrogel [34]. Ultimately, we chose to use TBA at a concentration of 5 %, m(TBA):m(LM) = 1:4 and combined this with a solvent substitution strategy and the Hofmeister effect for further mechanical stability experiments.

Subsequently, a series of cyclic loading-unloading experiments were conducted to investigate the anti-fatigue and self-recovery properties of hydrogels. Cyclic loading-unloading curves of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel under the tensile strain increases from 100 to 250 % showed a positive correlation between the dissipated energy and strain. Multiple physical interactions within the gel network can effectively dissipate the energy (Fig. 3i). As for the continuous loading-unloading curve with 100 % strain in Fig. 3j, in cyclic stress-strain curves, the apparent hysteresis loops can be clearly observed, indicating a representative energy dissipation ability. The structural rearrangement of the partially entangled molecular chains and some loosely bonded physical crosslinked networks results in a large hysteresis loop in the first cycle. The hysteresis loops almost coincide without displacement and breakage in the following cycles. It is worth noting that after the 5 cycles, the tensile stress is almost unchanged. The great fatigue resistance stems from the dynamic and reversible interactions in the hydrogel network, which are dynamic disruption and reorganization under continuous tensile deformation. Both results indicate that the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel can withstand repeated mechanical stresses and maintain its stable network structure, showing excellent self-recovery and fatigue resistance.

Electrical behaviors of gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

Next, the electrical conductivity of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel were verified. Fig. 4a showed that the hydrogels acted as wires to make the LED bulbs light up. When the hydrogel is in a stretched state, the LED bulbs immediately dim, but returns to its original brightness after unloading the strain. This indicates that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel has excellent electrical conductivity and its resistance changes with increasing strain, which can be used for flexible sensors. The addition of LM endows the hydrogels with high electrical conductivity. Conductivity measurements indicate that the Gel and Gel-TBA hydrogel without the LM presented poor conductivity, however, the electrical conductivity of the Gel-LM and Gel-TBA-LM groups significantly increased with the addition of LM (Fig. S4). Furthermore, Fig. 4b showed that the conductivity of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel increased with the LM content, reaching a maximum conductivity of 673.76 S/m. Finally, combined with the results of mechanical experiments and conductive testing, we chose Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel with m(TBA):m(LM) = 1:4 and 5 % TBA content for the next behavior monitoring and diabetic wound treatment.

Electrical performance of the Gel-TBA-LM composites. a) Circuit in series with Gel-TBA-LM and LEDs. b) Electrical conductivity of Gel-TBA-LM with different content of LM. c) Variation of ΔR/R0 of Gel-TBA-LM with tensile strain. d) Response time and recovery time of Gel-TBA-LM. e) ΔR/R0 signals for index finger bending and straightening. f) wrist bending. g) fist-clenching movement. h) walking. i) neck muscle movements resulted from head movements. j) finger tapping.

The electromechanical behavior of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel is evaluated in Fig. 4c, where the electrical resistance increases rapidly as the hydrogel is stretched, which may be attributed to localized and reversible interruptions in the percolation network during stretching. We assessed the strain sensing sensitivity by calculating the corresponding measurement factor (GF), which varies as a function of tensile strain, namely GF = (ΔR/R0)/Δε. The GF of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel was 0.26 in the strain range of 0–50 %, whereas in the higher strain range (50–200 %), the GF increased to 2.88, which indicated that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel had higher sensing sensitivity over a wider strain range. In addition, the fast response speed of 173 ms also makes it ideal for real-time human motion detection (Fig. 4d). The real-time response of the strain sensor was examined by varying test speeds (0.7 Hz–3.0 Hz) and strains (20 %–100 %) (Figs. S5–S6). It was found that the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel showed a stable and reproducible ΔR/R0 signal, which indicating that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel have good responsiveness and real-time performance as strain sensors. A cyclic stretching releasing test at a tensile strain of 100 % was performed to evaluate the durability of the composite and reliability of the electrical signal. Fig. S7 showed stable waveforms with almost constant ΔR/R0 amplitude, confirming that the sensor had good durability and repeatability.

Diabetic neuropathy frequently leads to sensory and motor deficits, resulting in impaired mobility, gait abnormalities, and balance disorders [35]. Furthermore, neuropathy compromises the ability of individuals with diabetes to perceive wounds, thereby hindering timely and effective feedback and management of injuries [36]. Consequently, real-time monitoring of motor behavior in diabetic patients and the prompt reporting of movement anomalies are critical for effective wound management. The Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel, with its excellent mechanical properties, good electrical conductivity, and high strain sensing properties, show great potential for motion detection. To demonstrate the practical application of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel in wearable sensors, we assembled and integrated Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel strain sensors into different parts of volunteers to evaluate their sensing performance in monitoring body movements. When a finger undergoes multiple uninterrupted bends and recoveries (Fig. 4e), different degrees of finger flexion can be distinguished from the ΔR/R0 signal. Therefore, by monitoring the ΔR/R0 signal, the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel strain sensor can accurately track different degrees of finger bending in real time. In addition, the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel strain sensor can detect a variety of other highly sensitive and reproducible human activities, such as wrists, bending hands, and knees (Fig. 4f–h). In addition, head movements can be effectively monitored by attaching the sensor to the throat (Fig. 4i). Moreover, the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel strain sensor can also respond to pressure, such as finger taps, with repeatable and stable signals (Fig. 4j). These results indicate that the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel sensor has promising applications in health and motion monitoring such as injury joint recovery monitoring, sports posture monitoring, and soft robot motion monitoring.

Self-healing, anti-freezing, swelling, moisturizing and recyclability of gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

The Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel possess good self-healing properties. When the broken pieces were held together, LM droplets can merge on the fracture surfaces and quickly restore the conductivity (Fig. 5a). To test the self-healing efficiency for conductivity, a LEDs lamp was illuminated by a power supply with Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel connected in the circuit. As shown in Fig. 5b, the LEDs lamp was turned off when the hydrogel was cut into two pieces, however, it was lighted up immediately after bringing the divided hydrogels together.

The photograph of a) shows that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel can be remodeled. b) shows that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel can light up small light bulbs after being remodeled. c) Tensile stress-strain curves at −18 °C. d) changes in ΔR/R0 for Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel at different strains, and response time e) at −18 °C.f) Dissolved rise photo. g) Hydrogel swelling property. h) Hydrogel moisturizing properties. i) Photographs illustrating the degradation of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel in aqueous solution of NaOH (2 mol L−1). j) LM recovery rate.

In general, the unique mechanical and electrical properties of hydrogels would be deteriorated when they are stored under cryogenic conditions. A large amount of water in the hydrogel is inevitably frozen in a subzero environment. To overcome this problem, an organic solvent was introduced into the hydrogel to improve its freezing resistance and to extend the operating temperature range of the organohydrogel sensor. As shown in Fig. 5c, the Gel-TBA-LM hydrogel with water as solvent loses its elasticity after stretching when stored at −18 °C for 12 h. However, the organohydrogel were prepared by binary solvent and solvent substitution strategies both showed promising anti-freeze ability, that their tensile properties remained almost unchanged after freezing. Importantly, the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel still maintained good electrical conductivity and sensing properties after being stored at −18 °C for 12h (Fig. 5d). Moreover, Sensor demonstrates fast response even after freezing (631 ms) (Fig. 5e). Furthermore, the antifreeze property was qualitatively evaluated by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and the results were shown in Fig. S8, the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel did not show any exothermic peaks in the temperature range of 25 °C to −20 °C, suggesting the organohydrogel exhibited good antifreeze property. Fig. 5f and g showed that the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel had a high swelling rate (122 %), and Fig. 5h showed that the organohydrogel prepared by the solvent substitution strategy and binary solvent exhibited long-lasting moisturizing properties, that reached initial weights of 0.91 and 0.95 after 48 h of placement, compared to 0.71 for the hydrogel prepared with water as the solvent. The excellent swelling and moisturizing properties can maintain a moist environment for wound healing. In addition, the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel can be quickly dissolved in an alkaline sodium hydroxide solution, allowing for effective recoverability of LM. As shown in Fig. 5i–j, a piece of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel (m(TBA):m(LM) = 1:4) was completely degraded and dissolved in 2 mol L−1 sodium hydroxide solution within 4 h, and the recovery rate reached 97 %.

In vitro antibacterial, antioxidation, and biocompatibility properties

TBA has a similar chemical structure to gallic acid, as a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound, which exhibits various biological activities such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antibacterial activities [37]. Therefore, we hypothesize that the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel also exhibits significant antibacterial activity. To validate this hypothesis, we first determined the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of TBA. The results indicated that the MIC of TBA against the Gram-negative bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) and the Gram-positive bacterium Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) was 0.0625 mg/mL (Fig. S9). Subsequently, co-culture experiments were conducted using different hydrogel matrices with E. coli and S. aureus. Results indicate that compared to the Gel group, the Gel-LM group exhibited significantly reduced bacterial activity, with inhibition rates of 74 % and 71 % against E. coli and S. aureus, respectively. This is attributed to Ga3+ released by LM disrupting bacterial metabolism, thereby killing the bacteria. Furthermore, the Gel-TBA and Gel-TBA-LM groups containing TBA demonstrated more pronounced antibacterial effects, achieving a 100 % inhibition rate (Fig. S10). We further determined the antibacterial properties of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel. Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel was placed on the surface of agar of S. aureus and E. coli. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the size of the zone of inhibition was observed to assess the antimicrobial capacity of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel, which visualizes the spectral antimicrobial activity of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel (Fig. 6a). The diameter of the zone of inhibition of E. coli as well as S. aureus was increased in the hydrogel with the addition of TBA as compared to the control groups.

In addition, as excess ROS induces severe inflammation, they lead to deleterious processes that negatively affect wound healing and tissue regeneration. Therefore, hydrogel dressings with strong ROS scavenging ability can effectively promote for wound healing. The antioxidant properties of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel were determined by evaluating its scavenging effects on 1,1-diphenyl-2-pyridinehydrazine (DPPH) and ABTS radicals. As shown in Fig. 6b–S11, the Gel and Gel-LM hydrogels without TBA exhibited negligible scavenging activity toward DPPH radicals and ABTS. In contrast, the TBA-containing hydrogels Gel-TBA and Gel-TBA-LM exhibited sustained and significant scavenging effects on both radicals, effectively eliminating approximately 99 % of DPPH and ABTS radicals (Fig. 6b–S11). This may be due to the polyphenol groups in TBA that give the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel the potential to trap free radicals with a very favorable antioxidant capacity.

Hemocompatibility is a fundamental requirement for hydrogel dressings. The hemolytic activity test was employed to assess hemocompatibility, the Triton X-100 and normal saline were represented as positive and negative controls, respectively. As shown in Fig. 6c, the hemolysis rate of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel was less than 5 % after 1 h of contact with red blood cells, indicating the existence of good hemocompatibility. At the same time, Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel also had good cytocompatibility, as demonstrated by MTT assay and AM/PI staining assay (Fig. 6d–g). After incubating the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel with three different cells for 5 days, the cells grew well. This further demonstrated the high cytocompatibility of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel (Fig. 6g). These results indicate that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel with excellent antibacterial activity, antioxidative, and biocompatibility, which are a potential dressing for wound healing.

Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel cell migration and antioxidant properties

To illustrate the promotion effect of the magnitude of electrical stimulation on the cells, we also examined the survival rate of cells at different voltages using the MTT method (Fig. S12). The results showed that the DC voltage promoted the propagation and growth of cells. As the voltage increased from 0.5 V to 1 V, the cells showed a certain growth trend. However, when the voltage exceeded 1 V, it would instead cause damage to the cells, thus we chose a 1 V DC voltage for the subsequent experiments. In diabetic patients, hyperglycemia at the wound site damages microvascular endothelial cells, inducing vasodilatory dysfunction and tissue ischemia [38]. Therefore, proliferation and differentiation of HUVECs play a key role in the healing and repair of chronic diabetic wounds. Scratch healing experiments showed that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel could effectively induce cell migration of HUVECs, and the migration rate was further increased after the addition of ES (Fig. 7a and b). Meanwhile, in vitro transwell assay further verified the enhancing effect of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel on the migration ability of HUVECs (Fig. 7c and d). Through the results of neutral red staining test, we found that the combination of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel and ES could effectively increase macrophage phagocytosis (Fig. S13), and rapidly recruit macrophages for phagocytosis and clearance in the early stage of wound healing.

We also investigated the effects of hydrogels on intracellular ROS levels in HUVECs, PC12, and RAW264.7 cells under H2O2 stimulation using the DHE method to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel system as a potential ROS scavenging platform. As shown in Fig. 7e–h, the fluorescence intensity in the H2O2-treated group was much higher than that in the control group, indicating that the cells produced a large amount of ROS after co-culturing with 1 mM H2O2 for 2 h. The fluorescence intensity of the cells treated with the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel was almost at the same level as the control group, which is in line with the results of the DPPH assay. These data demonstrate that the hydrogel effectively scavenges intracellular ROS, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress damage during wound healing.

These results suggest that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel effectively scavenge intracellularly generated ROS, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress damage during wound healing. In addition, Gel-TBA-LM + ES treatment has significant potential in promoting proliferation, migration and differentiation of HUVECs and phagocytosis by macrophages to accelerate chronic wound healing.

Regulation of macrophage polarization and neural axon growth in vitro

Neuropathy is strongly associated with impaired wound healing in diabetic patients. The nerve regeneration capacity of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel was assessed by IF experiments on different groups of PC12 cells (undifferentiated) with neurofilament protein heavy polypeptide (NF) expression. As shown in Fig. 8a–c, the expression level of NF in the Gel-TBA-LM group was significantly higher than that in the control group, and the expression level of NF was further significantly increased after Gel-TBA-LM + ES treatment. The results showed that the strong conductivity of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel could effectively promote the homogeneous distribution of ES among cells and facilitate nerve growth.

Immune dysfunction in diabetic wounds (impeding macrophage M2 polarization) results in a persistent inflammatory environment that delays or prevents wound healing [39]. Persistent inflammation in diabetic wounds is usually associated with a sustained pro-inflammatory effect of M1-type macrophages, which continuously release cytokines such as e.g. inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), while M2-type macrophages promote wound repair by secreting anti-inflammatory factors (e.g. IL-10) [40]. To verify the role of hydrogels in modulating inflammation in vivo, IF analysis of inflammatory markers in macrophages of different phenotypes was performed after co-culture with macrophages. Inflammation was induced by adding LPS to the medium of RAW 264.7 cells before each experiment. IF images showed elevated expression of pro-inflammatory (iNOS) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10) markers in all treatment groups except the control group (Fig. 8b–e). This result suggests that the combined effect of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel and ES recruits macrophages and activates early inflammatory responses by up-regulating pro-inflammatory markers and inducing macrophage polarization towards M1. Meanwhile, further up-regulation of anti-inflammatory markers promotes macrophage polarization from M1 to M2, which ultimately results in the regulation and repair of the immune microenvironment.

Exploration of molecular mechanisms

The crosstalk between angiogenesis, neural repair and immune regulation plays a key role in aspects of diabetic wounding. In diabetic wounds, the hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress microenvironment inhibits the expression of HIF-1α, disrupts the formation of the C-JUN/AP-1 transcription complex, and reduce the synthesis of vascular endothelial growth factor VEGF [41]. The absence of VEGF further reduces ERK1/2 phosphorylation levels and impairs the MAPK signaling cascade, ultimately decreasing endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis capacity [2]. Furthermore, impaired angiogenesis leads to suppressed expression of key neuro-regenerative genes such as GAP43, SYN-1, PSD95, and NF, disrupting axonal growth and synaptic reconstruction, and impairing neuro-vascular interactions. In addition, impaired angiogenesis also affects macrophage recruitment [4]. Inhibition of the VEGF/ERK pathway hinders the conversion of macrophages to the M2 phenotype, thereby reducing the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors (such as IL-10) and losing regulation of vascular endothelial growth factor, further hindering vascular regeneration [42]. The three-dimensional network structure of conductive hydrogels can significantly enhance oxygen diffusion, nutrient delivery, and metabolic waste removal at the wound site [41]. In addition, ES can enhance local microcirculation blood flow and improve the efficiency of oxygen and nutrient supply [11]. The application of conductive hydrogels helps to evenly distribute ES, and this synergistic effect can play a role in the expression of HIF-1α, C-JUN, and AP-1. Therefore, based on the central role of the VEGF/ERK pathway in regulating angiogenesis, nerve repair, and macrophage M2 polarization (Fig. 9a), we believe that combining conductive hydrogels with ES through VEGF/ERK pathway to promote diabetic wound repair is an important therapeutic strategy.

To investigate the mechanism of Gel-TBA-LM + ES combination therapy, the expression of relevant mRNAs in HUVECs, PC12 and RAW264.7 cells was first analyzed by quantitative RT-qPCR. As shown in Fig. 9b, Gel-TBA-LM + ES treatment can effectively increase the expression of HIF-1α, C-JUN, and AP-1. As upstream regulatory factors, they synergistically induce the expression of VEGF and p-ERK1/2, thereby affecting the VEGF/ERK pathway, regulating cell proliferation and migration, and promoting angiogenesis. Among these, the three-dimensional network structure and conductivity of the Gel-TBA-LM group hydrogel facilitated oxygen and nutrient exchange at the wound site and restored the endogenous electric field, thereby promoting the expression of HIF-1α and C-JUN. The application of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogels also promotes the uniform distribution of ES. The synergistic effect of hydrogels and ES enhances cell migration and nutrient transport efficiency, which may also explain the significant increase in VEGFA, p-ERK1, and AP-1 in the Gel-TBA-LM + ES group. In addition, we observed changes in the expression of axon and synapse-related genes (Fig. 9c), with NF, GAP43, PSD-95, and SYN-1 showing the highest expression in the Gel-TBA-LM + ES group, suggesting that the combination of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel and ES has the ability to promote nerve repair. Furthermore, Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel in combination with ES can play an immunomodulatory role by regulating the expression of inflammation-related factors. As shown in Fig. 9 d, the expression of M1-type macrophage-related genes (iNOS, CD 86) was down-regulated, and the expression of M2-type macrophage-related genes (IL-10, CD 206) was up-regulated after treatment with Gel-TBA-LM + ES. This result suggests that the combined effect of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel and ES promotes the polarization of macrophages from M1 to M2, and ultimately achieves the regulation and repair of the immune microenvironment.

We further verified the activation effect of the VEGF/ERK pathway by WB experiments. As shown in Fig. 9e and f, the expression of VEGFA and p-ERK 1/2 was significantly increased in the Gel-TBA-LM group, and Gel-TBA-LM + ES further enhanced this effect, indicating that the synergistic treatment of Gel-TBA-LM + ES can promote the uniform distribution of exogenous ES between cells, further enhance signal transduction and material exchange between cells, and thereby effectively activate the VEGF/ERK pathway.

In vivo diabetic wound healing assays

Based on these in vitro results, the wound healing ability of the organohydrogel was further investigated in STZ-induced type I diabetic SD rats. Fig. 10a showed the macroscopic photographs of wounds in each group at different time points. On day 3, chronic inflammatory infiltrates caused by multiple infections and excessive oxidative stress in the hyperglycemic microenvironment were avoided due to the excellent antimicrobial and antioxidant activity of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel. Compared with the control group, the wound healing speed of the ES group was significantly faster, which is consistent with previous research reports that ES can promote wound healing by guiding and triggering the migration and proliferation of wound healing related cells [43,44]. In the late stage of wound healing, the wound healing rate of Gel-TBA-LM group was higher than that of ES group, confirming that Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel can simulate the natural electric field of the skin and facilitate intercellular electrical signaling, thus accelerating wound healing throughout the process [45]. On day 14, the Gel-TBA-LM + ES group had almost complete wound healing, indicating that the synergistic effect of ES and Gel-TBA-LM could comprehensively accelerate the healing of diabetic chronic ulcer wounds (Fig. 10b).

Epithelialization is an indispensable component and a decisive factor in wound healing. The progress of wound healing and the quality of the regenerated skin were further evaluated by histological analysis (H&E and Masson's trichrome staining) on days 3, 7 and 14 (Fig. 10c and d). H&E-stained images showed faster wound healing and greater epidermal regeneration in the Gel-TBA-LM + ES group (Fig. 10c). The control group still had many inflammatory cells at day 14, and the epidermal thickness was less different compared to day 3(Fig. 10e). From day 3 onwards, the thickness of the wound epidermis was higher in the other groups than in the control group (Fig. 10e). At day 14, the large difference in epidermal thickness between the Gel-TBA-TBA + ES group and the control group suggested that the combined effect of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel and ES accelerated the wound healing process. Masson staining revealed an increase in collagen content in all groups (Fig. 10f). More collagen was deposited in the Gel-TBA-LM group than in the ES and control group, and the Gel-TBA-LM + ES group had the most collagen deposition in days 3, 7, and 14, and the collagen arrangement was also more regular and closer to normal skin (Fig. 10f). The exceptional wound healing efficacy and remarkable safety of the Gel-TBA-LM + ES combination can be attributed to the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel promoting uniform distribution of exogenous electrical signals across the wound surface, thereby eliminating the localized high current density at electrode edges present in conventional setups. Furthermore, the inherent conductivity and high water content of the hydrogel matrix foster a stable electrochemical environment where thermal effects are negligible. Its softness also prevents secondary injury to the wound site. Gel-TBA-LM + ES can further accelerate tissue repair and regeneration by co-regulating the cellular activities and other methods.

Conclusion

In this work, we fabricated Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel through Hofmeister effect and solvent substitution methods using LM droplets as functional fillers. The hydrogel has considerable mechanical properties (4.55 MPa pressure and 426 % strain), electrical conductivity (673.76 S/m), self-healing ability, biocompatibility and anti-freeze properties. The highly sensitive (GF = 2.88) Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel allows for the detection of a wide range of body activities to diagnose relevant activities and diseases. Furthermore, the incorporation of TBA not only involved in dispersing and encapsulating LM particles, enhancing the mechanical properties of organohydrogel, but also conferred good antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties to the hydrogels. To clearly demonstrate the outstanding performance of the Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel, we compared eight key parameters with previously reported LM hydrogel sensors, including tensile stress, conductivity, GF, response time, anti-freezing, antibacterial properties, anti-inflammatory properties, and antioxidant properties. As shown in Fig. S15, our material combines multiple advantages, featuring outstanding mechanical properties, high electrical conductivity, high sensitivity, Anti-freeze, moisture retention, antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant capacity [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]]. In addition, the combination of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel and ES not only effectively enhances the phagocytosis and scavenging ability of M1-type macrophages, inhibits the growth of bacteria in the wound and scavenges ROS, but also promotes the angiogenesis of chronic diabetic wounds through the VEGF/ERK pathway, advances the M2-type polarization of macrophages, and promotes the elongation of nerves, thus promoting the healing of chronic diabetic wounds in an all-round way. In conclusion, this study provides valuable ideas for the development of multifunctional hydrogels for smart health monitoring and offers a promising and innovative therapeutic strategy to promote diabetic wound healing.

Experimental section

Materials

Gallium and indium (99.99 %) were purchased from Changsha Kunyong New Material Co. 2,3,4-Trihydroxybenzaldehyde (TBA) (99 %) was provided by Aladdin Biochemical Technology Company (Shanghai, China). NaCl (99 %), (NH4)2SO4, and dextrose anhydrous were supplied by Shanghai MCLEAN Chemical Company (Shanghai, China), and gelatin (gel strength 300, type A) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Burlington, USA).

Preparation of LM microdroplets

Preparation of LM nanodroplets: 0.2 g of TBA was dissolved in 2 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of EGaIn. The mixture was then sonicated in an ice-water bath (SM-1000C, SHUNMATECH, power 1000 W, amplitude 100 %) to obtain suspensions of LM nanodroplets with different liquid metal contents (m(TBA):m(LM) = 1:2,1:4,1:6,1:8,1:10).

Preparation of the gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

3.6 g Gelatin powder was dissolved in 6 mL DI water by stirring for 60 min at 50 °C. Then, Add an equal volume of TBA-LM nanodroplets suspension. The homogenous precursors were poured into a PTFE mold and maintained at 4 °C for 1 h to form Gel-TBA-LM hydrogels, which were then soaked in water/glycerol binary solvent containing (NH4)2SO4 for solvent replacement and salting out, respectively, at room temperature for 1 h.

Characterization of the gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

The cross-sectional morphology of hydrogels was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Tescan Mira LM) at 15 keV. Samples were freeze-dried with a freeze-dryer for 48 h and then quenched with liquid nitrogen to quench the fracture. A Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet iS20, Thermal Sciences, USA) was used to test the chemical structure of the samples in the range of 4000–500 cm−1. The antifreeze properties of hydrogels of organics were investigated using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, Q2000, USA), in which the samples were weighed 3–5 mg and then placed in a crucible. The temperature was lowered from 35 to −40 °C at a rate of 5 °C min−1. Changes in thermorheological behavior during cooling and heating were recorded.

Mechanical test

The XLW (PC)-500 Intelligent Electronic Tensile Tester was used for mechanical property testing. The samples were of dumbbell type (15 mm × 2 mm × 1.5 mm). Unless otherwise specified, the tensile stress-strain tests were performed at room temperature at a rate of 50 mm/min. Young's modulus was defined as the slope of the tensile curve (0–50 % strain). For cyclic tensile testing of hydrogels, the tensile rate was set at 50 mm/min. The hydrogel was stretched at a constant rate until a specific strain was reached, and then it was unloaded at the same rate until the strain returned to 0.

Conductive performance of flexible strain sensor

Quantitative measurements of the strain sensing range of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel and its resistive sensitivity to different strains were performed, i.e., as a conductor encapsulated in two pieces of medical PU tape and copper foils for fixing the wires at both ends to form a sandwich-like flexible strain sensor. Changes in the real-time resistance signal of Gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel were recorded using a potentiometer (Keithley 7510). The conductivity (σ) equation was calculated as: σ = L/RS, where L, R, and S are the sample length, resistance, and cross-sectional area, respectively.

The strain sensitivity of the flexible sensors under different tensile strain conditions was evaluated using a universal tensile machine and potentiometer. The relative resistance change (ΔR/R0) and the measurement factor (GF) were calculated as ΔR/R0 (%) = (R-R0)/R0 × 100 % and GF = (R-R0)/R0)/ε, where R0 is the initial resistance, R is the real-time resistance and ε is the strain. By mounting the flexible sensor on a healthy male volunteer and monitoring different movements such as frowning, swallowing and joint (wrist, finger, elbow and knee) bending, the potentiometer can clearly show the real-time resistance changes under different activity states.

Self-healing tests

The samples with the same composition were cut into two pieces and the two cross sections of the hydrogel samples were placed back into the mold and transferred into the oven for 6 h. After healing, they were connected to an external power supply and LED bulbs.

Degradation and recycling

For the recycling of conductive fillers (LM), the conductive composite (LM-TBA-LM) was submerged into 10 mL 2 mol L−1 aqueous solution of NaOH. After the complete dissolution of the composite, the resulting slurry was centrifugated at a speed of 6000 rpm. The sediment was rinsed successively with NaOH solution and DI water. Afterward, the sediment was placed in an oven at 60 °C for drying to a constant weight and then its mass was measured for the calculation of recycling ratio.

In vitro antibacterial and antioxidant assessment

For the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) method, overnight cultures of bacteria in LB were diluted to 1 × 107 CFU/mL and mixed with TBA in a gradient from 1 mg/mL. Vancomycin was used as a positive control, and after 24 h of incubation in a biochemical incubator at 37 °C, the OD600 was measured every 2 h using a multifunctional enzyme marker, and the MIC was recorded at the lowest concentration with OD600 < 0.1.

The antibacterial activity of the hydrogel was evaluated using the agar plate count method. First, suspensions of S. aureus and E. coli (106 CFU mL−1) were co-cultured with the hydrogel (400 μL) at 37 °C for 18 h. Following incubation, bacterial solutions were diluted 103-fold. Equal 100 μL aliquots were inoculated onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 7–15 h. After formation of single bacterial colonies, viable colonies were counted to calculate bacterial survival rates.

We chose S. aureus and E. coli to test the antibacterial activity of the organohydrogen by pouring 20 mL of agar into a 9 cm bacterial medium, and then dispersing 100 μL of broth containing E. coli and S. aureus (1 × 105 CFU/mL) on different cured agar media. The hydrogels were then placed on the agar surface. The zone of inhibition of each group of samples was observed after 24 h of incubation.

After cutting the gel into smaller pieces DPPH (100 μM) and different hydrogel samples were dispersed in 3 mL of ethanol solution with stirring and left in the dark for 30 min, then scan at a wavelength of 517 nm using a UV spectrophotometer.

Scavenging of reactive oxygen species by gel-TBA-LM organohydrogel

Intracellular ROS levels in RAW 264.7 cells stimulated with H2O2 were detected by DHE staining. Neutral red phagocytosis assay

Raw264.7 cells were inoculated into 12-well plates, and the cells were treated in different groups after wall attachment. After 3 days of cell culture, each well was rinsed with PBS. Then Neutral Red Stain (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to each well and incubation was continued for 20 min, the supernatant was poured off and rinsed 3 times with PBS to remove phagocytosed Neutral Red particles. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of cell lysis solution (acetic acid: anhydrous ethanol = 50:50) was added to each well and left at room temperature for 2 h. After cell lysis, the absorbance was measured at 540 nm.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA from Raw264.7 cells was extracted with TRLzol reagent and reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Real-time qPCR (RT-qPCR) was carried out using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and finally calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells or tissues were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The samples were washed three times with PBS, incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, and then with secondary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. Finally, the nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI, blocked, and the fluorescence images were observed under a confocal reflectance microscope.

Western blotting (WB) assay

After the cells were treated and cultured according to different groups, total proteins in the cells were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) buffer containing phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were subsequently separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, Epizyme, China) and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, Thermo Fisher, USA) membranes. After incubation with the corresponding primary and secondary antibodies, the protein bands were visualised with the Enhanced Chemiluminescence Kit (ECL, Servicebio, China) and the images were acquired with a chemiluminescence imager. Finally, the band density was quantitatively analyzed by ImageJ software.

In vivo wound healing test

Adult males Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (240–280 g) supplied by Guangdong Animal Medical Experimental Centre (Guangzhou, China) were used in this study. The rats were injected intraperitoneally with 60 mg/kg of streptozotocin (STZ) buffer to establish a diabetic model, and a random blood glucose concentration of more than 16.7 mmol/L after one week was considered diabetic. 48 males diabetic rats were selected and randomly divided into four groups (control group, Gel-TBA-LM group, ES group and Gel-TBA-LM + ES group, 12 rats in each group). Subsequently, rats were anaesthetized by intramuscular injection of Zoletil-50 (50 mg/mL, 0.1 mL/kg), and a model of a full-thickness skin wound infected with S. aureus was established on the back of the rat with a wound diameter of 10 mm. The successful establishment of the infected wound model was indicated by the presence of pus. All animal assays were carried out abided by the standard of the National Regulation of China for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals complied with the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. And all animal experiments in this study were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zunyi Medical University (ZHSC-2- [2024]042).

The ES group and the Gel-TBA-LM + ES group were given 30 min of ES (1 V, DC) per day for the first three days, and the hydrogel was changed daily. After that, the hydrogel was changed every 3 days, and the control group was left untreated. Wounds were photographed on days 0, 3, 7, 14 and quantified using the residual wound (%) calculated by ImageJ software as residual wound (%) = (St/S0) × 100 %, where S0 refers to the original wound area and St refers to the wound area at different time points. Three rats in each group were executed on days 3, 7 and 14, and the skin around the wounds was removed and used for further histological and IF studies after application of 4 % paraformaldehyde. Visceral organs of rats in each group were taken on day 14 for histological staining to assess the in systemic toxicity of the hydrogels. All rats were executed by cervical dislocation after anaesthesia (Isoflurane respiratory anaesthesia).

Statistical analysis

P-values <0.05 were used to evaluate statistical significance, and the data were reported as mean values (±SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the student's test were used where needed to determine statistical significance. The analyses were conducted using SPSSv.13.0.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL), a statistical software. The results of each experiment were independently repeated at least three times.
