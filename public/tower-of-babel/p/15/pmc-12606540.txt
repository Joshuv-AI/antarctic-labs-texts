Prevalence of germline pathogenic variants in 779 patients with metastatic prostate cancer
Authors: Michiel Vlaming, Wouter Koole, R. Jeroen A. van Moorselaar, Eveline M.A. Bleiker, Ellen van de Geer, Hanneke W.M. van Deutekom, Marjolijn J.L. Ligtenberg, Klaartje van Engelen, C. Marleen Kets, Lizet E. van der Kolk, Gina Schijven, Niven Mehra, M. Arjen Noordzij, Richard P. Meijer, Inge M. van Oort, Lambertus A.L.M. Kiemeney, Margreet G.E.M. Ausems
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of germline pathogenic variants (gPVs) in genes associated with female breast cancer in Dutch patients with metastatic prostate cancer (mPCa).Patients and methodsIn this prospective multicentre cohort study (n = 15 centres), germline genetic testing of the genes BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2 and PALB2 was offered to patients with mPCa. We assessed the prevalence of gPVs and compared it to a reference population of 16 823 individuals who underwent genetic testing for non‐oncological conditions. Additionally, we identified factors that increased the likelihood of carrying a gPV.ResultsA total of 779 patients with mPCa underwent germline genetic testing, of whom 46 (5.9%) had a gPV in a DNA damage repair gene. Most gPVs were found in CHEK2 (1100delC variant), ATM and BRCA2, all significantly more prevalent than in the reference population (odds ratios 2.4, 2.8 and 3.1, respectively). The prevalence of gPVs in BRCA1 and PALB2 was not significantly increased. Patients with a second primary cancer, a first‐ or second‐degree relative with breast cancer at age < 50 years, or pancreatic cancer, or Jewish ancestry had the highest likelihood of carrying a gPV.ConclusionWe found that gPVs in CHEK2, ATM and BRCA2 were associated with men presenting with mPCa. Several factors increased the likelihood of carrying such a variant and, in these cases, healthcare professionals should be aware of the need to offer germline genetic testing.

Introduction

Between 3% and 19% of patients with metastatic prostate cancer (mPCa) carry a germline pathogenic variant (gPV) in a cancer predisposition gene [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. These gPVs are detected in particular in the genes BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2, and to a lesser extent in BRCA1 and PALB2, reflecting a key role for the DNA damage repair (DDR) pathway [7]. The prevalence rates of gPVs in those genes differ significantly across studies and vary across ethnic groups; for example, variants in BRCA1/2 are more common in people with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Geographical variation can be seen in the CHEK2 1100delC variant (NM_007194.4), which is uncommon in North America, whereas it is more prevalent in Northern and Eastern Europe [10].

Detecting a gPV in patients with prostate cancer can have important consequences. Multiple studies have shown that PARP inhibitors are effective in metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) patients with a pathogenic germline or somatic BRCA variant [11]. Furthermore, mPCa patients who carry a gPV in BRCA2, have an increased risk of developing breast cancer [12]. Relatives of patients with mPCa can carry the same gPV. The gPVs in the most common genes that are affected in mPCa are associated with increased breast cancer risk in women [13]. There are also associations with ovarian and pancreatic cancer, depending on the affected gene [12]. When a gPV is detected in female relatives, they can opt for intensified breast cancer screening or risk‐reducing surgery and male relatives with a BRCA2 gPV might benefit from prostate cancer screening [14, 15, 16].

Because of these consequences, international guidelines recommend performing germline genetic testing in patients with mPCa [17, 18]. Most studies on the prevalence of gPVs in mPCa have focused on patients from the United States [2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8], while there are only a few studies from Western Europe [1, 6]. Most studies also used large gene panels that include genes that have few or no (or unknown) consequences for patients with prostate cancer or their relatives in the case of a gPV.

In this study, we aimed to determine the prevalence of gPVs in the DDR genes BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2 and PALB2 in a large cohort of unselected patients with mPCa. We also aimed to compare the prevalence against a reference population and to identify patient, tumour and family characteristics that are associated with carrying a gPV.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

This was a prospective cohort study. All patients with mPCa (de novo or progressive after localised disease) were eligible unless they had already undergone genetic testing of the tumour. Patients were included between December 2021 and September 2023 in the DISCOVER study at 15 sites throughout the Netherlands [19]. Eligible patients received genetic counselling from a healthcare professional from the urology or oncology department. These healthcare professionals provided pre‐test counselling and requested genetic testing only after completion of online training (i.e., within a mainstream genetic testing workflow).

The study was registered under number NL9617 in the Overview of Medical Research in the Netherlands. It was reviewed by the Medical Ethics Committee of UMC Utrecht in September 2020, which ruled that the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act did not apply to this study. Every patient gave written informed consent for study participation.

Data Collection

The healthcare professionals completed a short checklist of key information about family cancer history (see Methods S1). In addition, details on the primary prostate cancer diagnosis (TNM stage, PSA value, International Society of Urological Pathology grade of biopsy) and personal cancer history were provided by the Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Organisation (IKNL) through a link with the Netherlands Cancer Registry. After a patient was diagnosed with a gPV, a family cancer history was taken at the Genetics Department, which was reviewed by a researcher (M.V.).

For TNM stage at initial diagnosis, clinical TNM stages were used. If the pathological lymph node status (pN) was known but the clinical lymph node status (cN) was unknown, or if the pN stage showed metastasis and the cN stage did not, the pN stage was used.

Germline Panel Testing

Using blood samples, all patients received germline genetic testing of at least the DDR genes BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2 and PALB2 at one of six participating genetics departments. In some genetics departments, BARD1, BRIP1, RAD51C, RAD51D or HOXB13 were included in the gene panel. Details on the used germline panels are described in Methods S1 and Table S1.

Reference Cohort

A reference cohort was created using whole‐exome sequencing data from individuals who underwent exome sequencing as part of regular healthcare at the University Medical Center Utrecht. These individuals underwent this for various reasons, such as neurological, cardiological or congenital abnormalities or intellectual disability. Individuals who did not consent in their electronic patient dossiers to having their data reused were not included in the reference cohort. Individuals who had received a cancer‐related gene panel test were also excluded. In total, 8940 men and 7883 women, who underwent sequencing between 2020 and 2024, were included. Individual exome data were not assessed; instead, single and multi‐nucleotide variants from only BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2 and PALB2 were aggregated. Possible ‘pathogenic’ or ‘likely pathogenic’ variants were then filtered by Alissa Interpret software, which consults various national, international and local databases such as NCBI ClinVar, HGMD Professional and gnomAD. Subsequently, remaining variants were manually classified in line with American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria and were only included in the analyses if confirmed to be ‘pathogenic’ or ‘likely pathogenic’. For each pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant (gLPV), the number of cases was calculated and added up per gene. Because of the data aggregation, it is possible that individuals were counted twice if they carried two different gPVs or gLPVs within a single gene.

Statistical Analysis

Prevalence rates of gPVs were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cancer characteristics and family history were compared between gPV carriers and non‐carriers using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and an unpaired t‐test for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression models were used for determining the most important predictors of carrying a gPV. To address missing values, we used multiple imputation by chained equations. Patients who could not be linked in the Netherlands Cancer Registry were not imputed due to insufficient values that could be used as references for imputation. Predicted probabilities of carrying a gPV were compared against the actual outcome of carrying a gPV using the area under the receiver‐operating characteristic curve (AUC). To compare the prevalence of gPVs between our cohort and the reference population, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI were calculated. P values <0.05 were taken to indicate statistical significance. All analyses and imputations were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.1.

Results

A total of 779 patients with mPCa gave consent and underwent germline genetic testing. Of those, 46 (5.9%) had a gPV in a DDR gene: CHEK2 (3.0%, all with the1100delC variant), ATM (1.4%), BRCA2 (1.2%), BRCA1 (0.3%) or PALB2 (0.3%; Table 1). One patient was diagnosed with a gPV in both ATM and CHEK2. A distribution of identified gPVs in all tested genes is provided in Table S2.

When the model was restricted to only the variables that were significant or borderline significant in univariable analysis (P < 0.10), the AUC was 0.656. These variables were ‘other cancer diagnosis (except skin cancer)’, ‘breast cancer at age < 50 years in a first‐ or second‐degree relative’, ‘pancreatic cancer in a first‐ or second‐degree relative’ and ‘Jewish ancestry’. The predicted probability of carrying a gPV was 8% or higher when any of the above‐mentioned variables tested positive (Fig. 1). Discussion

In this paper, we report that 5.9% of Dutch patients with mPCa had a gPV in a DDR gene. Similarly to other studies, we showed a significantly higher prevalence of gPVs in CHEK2, ATM and BRCA2 in mPCa patients compared to the prevalence in reference populations [7, 20]. We should therefore regard these genes as being associated with mPCa.

We identified several risk factors that were associated with a high probability of carrying a gPV. These were: other cancer diagnosis; breast cancer at age <50 years in a first‐ or second‐degree relative; pancreatic cancer in a first‐ or second‐degree relative; and Jewish ancestry. In mPCa patients with one or more of these risk factors, the predicted probability of carrying a gPV increased to at least 7.9%, whereas in the absence of risk factors the predicted probability dropped to 4.2%. Based on this low prevalence, we believe that there should be a discussion on whether or not germline genetic testing should be routinely offered to mPCa patients without an additional risk factor in the Netherlands. International guidelines advise germline genetic testing irrespective of patient and family characteristics [17, 18].

In total, 46 families were found with a gPV in a high‐risk or moderate‐risk DDR gene. Finding such a gPV can have serious consequences for female relatives: gPVs in BRCA1, BRCA2 and PALB2 strongly increase breast and/or ovarian cancer risk [12, 13]. When a gPV in these genes is found in families, it leads to cascade screening (i.e., systematic testing of the carrier's relatives). ATM and CHEK2 are moderate‐risk cancer genes. According to Dutch policy, the recommendation for relatives to undergo genetic testing depends on the presence of breast cancer (in the case of an ATM/CHEK2 gPV) or pancreatic cancer (ATM) in their first‐ or second‐degree relatives [21]. In our cohort of 33 patients with an ATM/CHEK2 gPV, breast cancer was present in 18 families and pancreatic cancer in five families (Table S3). Combining those results, we found 19 families with a gPV in CHEK2/ATM (58%) where relatives were eligible for genetic testing. For male relatives, identifying a gPV in BRCA2 is particularly important because of a significantly increased prostate cancer risk, and the benefit from prostate cancer screening [15]. As CHEK2 and ATM are also associated with prostate cancer, discussions have arisen about implementing prostate cancer screening in carriers. However, screening with PSA also carries negative consequences, such as overdiagnosis of indolent tumours [22]. Studies on screening with PSA in CHEK2 and ATM carriers are ongoing (NCT02543905). In CHEK2, we only found the 1100delC variant, a founder variant in Western Europe [10]. Although this founder variant is highly prevalent in the Dutch population (1.3%), the prevalence of gPVs in our mPCa cohort was significantly higher (OR 2.4). In line with our data, a meta‐analysis by Wang et al. [23] did show an increased prostate cancer risk in 1100delC carriers, with an OR of 3.29 (1.85–5.85). Cumulative lifetime risks of developing prostate cancer by age 80 years are 25% for carriers of protein‐truncating variants in CHEK2 [24]. This also supports studies on the efficacy of prostate cancer screening in men with a gPV in CHEK2.

Although increased in our cohort, the prevalence of gPVs in BRCA2 (1.16%) is low compared to other cohorts of mPCa patients, which have shown prevalence rates from 2.7% to 8.6% [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. A clear reason for this low prevalence of gPVs is not known, but we have a few hypotheses. One possibility is that genetic care in the Netherlands is highly accessible for patients with breast and ovarian cancer [25, 26] and these patients frequently harbour gPVs in BRCA1 and BRCA2 [12, 13]; as a result, their relatives are eligible to undergo cascade testing. It can therefore be hypothesised that some men with a gPV in BRCA2 have already been identified previously by cascade screening and were therefore not included in this study. It is also possible that other studies may have been conducted in populations that contained more patients with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry and therefore more BRCA founder variants were identified. In the study by Nicolosi et al. [27], 6.5% of patients had such ancestry, and they accounted for 8.4% of gPVs. However, most studies did not provide further details on background of the tested patients. Another explanation for the low BRCA2 prevalence may be that tumour genetic testing was already performed in patients at high risk of carrying a pathogenic variant. Tumour genetic testing was an exclusion criterion for undergoing germline genetic testing in our study. Upon inquiry with our participating centres, the use of tumour genetic testing varied significantly among centres during the inclusion period and was particularly offered to patients with mCRPC, and much less often to patients with metastatic hormone‐sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC). When patients with mHSPC were compared to those with mCRPC with regard to possible risk factors for carrying a gPV, no indication for pre‐test selection was found for factors such as family history of breast cancer at age <50 years (P = 0.74), family history of pancreatic cancer (P = 0.34), family history of ovarian cancer (P = 0.30), hereditary prostate cancer (P = 0.20), other primary cancer in index (P = 0.49), or Jewish ancestry (P = 0.32). Accordingly, we do not believe that significant numbers of patients at high risk of carrying a gPV would have been excluded from our study and are therefore confident in the validity of the prevalence of gPVs identified in our cohort.

Tumour genetic testing leads to both somatic and germline‐derived pathogenic variants being detected, and therefore identifies more patients eligible for therapies that rely on pathogenic variants. When a pathogenic variant is detected in tumour genetic testing that might be of germline origin, germline testing can be performed. However, tumour genetic testing in cancer has been described as missing up to 7% of relevant gPVs, which could have severe consequences for the patients’ relatives [28]. With the use of only germline genetic testing, it is important to be aware that the variant found can be caused by clonal haematopoiesis, especially in older and extensively treated patients [29]. In our cohort, one clonal haematopoietic variant in ATM with a variant allele frequency of 37% was found and initially assessed as a gPV. This variant was excluded as a gPV in this study.

A strength of our study is that it is the largest study on germline genetic testing in European patients with mPCa; other studies included between 139 and 557 mPCa patients [1, 6]. Additionally, we used a relatively small panel of genes restricted to hereditary breast cancer, unlike the other European studies which used significantly larger panels, with a minimum of 21 genes, including some with unknown associated phenotypes. Since these phenotypes have no known link to prostate cancer, identifying a gPV in one of those genes can create confusion about its clinical implications. This may complicate patient counselling and potentially lead to unnecessary psychosocial impact on the family.

Despite the large cohort of 779 patients, the number of patients with a gPV remained relatively small. This resulted in wide confidence intervals, particularly when comparing against the reference population. To validate our findings, future studies involving even larger cohorts of European patients are necessary. Future studies are also needed to determine whether the risk factors identified also increase the likelihood of detecting a gPV in patients with localised prostate cancer. It is important to note that the risk factors and associated predicted probabilities are all based solely on our cohort (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1). When other cohorts are used, different probabilities will be found. Furthermore, in Fig. S1 multiple risk factors are combined and not all of these combinations exist in our cohort.

In conclusion, the prevalence of gPVs in CHEK2 (1100delC), ATM and BRCA2 was significantly higher in Dutch mPCa patients compared to non‐cancer controls, whereas the prevalence of gPVs in BRCA1 and PALB2 was not increased. Patients with other primary cancers, a family history of early‐onset breast cancer, a family history of pancreatic cancer, or Jewish ancestry were most frequently diagnosed with a gPV. In patients with those risk factors, healthcare professionals should be particularly vigilant in offering germline genetic testing.
