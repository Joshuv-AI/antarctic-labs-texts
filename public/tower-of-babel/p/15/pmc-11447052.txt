Inertial effect of cell state velocity on the quiescence-proliferation fate decision
Authors: Harish Venkatachalapathy, Cole Brzakala, Eric Batchelor, Samira M. Azarin, Casim A. Sarkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Energy landscapes can provide intuitive depictions of population heterogeneity and dynamics. However, it is unclear whether individual cell behavior, hypothesized to be determined by initial position and noise, is faithfully recapitulated. Using the p21-/Cdk2-dependent quiescence-proliferation decision in breast cancer dormancy as a testbed, we examined single-cell dynamics on the landscape when perturbed by hypoxia, a dormancy-inducing stress. Combining trajectory-based energy landscape generation with single-cell time-lapse microscopy, we found that a combination of initial position and velocity on a p21/Cdk2 landscape, but not position alone, was required to explain the observed cell fate heterogeneity under hypoxia. This is likely due to additional cell state information such as epigenetic features and/or other species encoded in velocity but missing in instantaneous position determined by p21 and Cdk2 levels alone. Here, velocity dependence manifested as inertia: cells with higher cell cycle velocities prior to hypoxia continued progressing along the cell cycle under hypoxia, resisting the change in landscape towards cell cycle exit. Such inertial effects may markedly influence cell fate trajectories in tumors and other dynamically changing microenvironments where cell state transitions are governed by coordination across several biochemical species.

Introduction

Cell fate determination is a complex process, underpinned by nonlinear interactions among various biomolecular components that can obscure intuition of key modulators of cell behavior. Energy landscapes can provide graphically intuitive representations of cell decision-making1,2. In this framework, cells are considered as balls rolling on a landscape where each position represents a cell state and the depth at that position represents its potential energy or relative stability. Cell “movement” on the landscape is determined by its initial state, the surrounding topography, and the noisiness of the biomolecular components and interactions within the cell. This framework can also offer quantitative or semi-quantitative descriptions of important landscape features such as valley positions, relative state stability, and mean transition paths and times between these states.

Landscape formalisms have been previously used to understand cell decision-making in contexts such as stem cell differentiation2–5, embryonic development6,7, cell cycle progression8,9, DNA damage responses10,11, and cancer12–15. These studies recapitulate the overall population heterogeneity and dynamics as validated through ensemble-level measurements such as flow cytometry, immunostaining, or single-cell RNA quantification. However, the extent to which individual cell dynamics in vitro or in vivo are faithfully recapitulated on these landscapes is unclear, particularly under external perturbations such as growth factors, cytokines, or stress. Important features such as the contributions of initial position of individual cells and noisiness of movement on this landscape are often obscured due to a lack of single-cell tracking. For example, it is typically assumed that heterogeneity within a valley as well as in phenotype transitions under an external cue are primarily driven by short-term stochastic fluctuations due to intrinsic biochemical noise. However, the validity of this assumption is highly context-dependent as studies show more persistent, but still stochastic, differences in cell state such as epigenetic variation can also lead to divergent responses16,17. Dissecting the specific contributions of these factors is key to engineering cell behavior in different contexts.

In this study, we aimed to understand how both position and velocity regulate cellular movement on an energy landscape under an external perturbation. Here, we focused on the proliferation-quiescence decision in breast cancer dormancy. We chose this clinically important cell decision because it is governed by a well-established but nonlinear protein network implicated in the cell cycle, and therefore we hypothesized important roles of cell position and velocity on the landscape for this system. Furthermore, to our knowledge, the effects of stochasticity—changes in cell state or response due to intrinsic fluctuations in the biochemical processes driving cellular behavior—remain unexplored in this fate decision.

Clinically, cancer dormancy presents a significant challenge in progression-free survival in breast cancer patients even after several years of being in complete remission18. This is underpinned by the reactivation of dormant breast cancer cells that persist post-treatment, often in hypoxic niches such as the bone marrow19–21. These dormant cells arise when typically proliferative tumor cells are faced with stressful conditions, such as hypoxia or nutrient deprivation, causing them to enter a dormant state characterized by quiescence, a reversible non-dividing “resting phase”22. Notably, tumor cells exhibit significant heterogeneity in their abilities to enter a dormant state22–24. This variability is thought to be driven by genetic differences (e.g., estrogen receptor (ER) expression25) and/or cell states (e.g., stem cell-like phenotypes26,27). However, there is heterogeneity in dormancy even among cells with a given dormancy-associated phenotype23,27,28. It is unclear whether this variation is purely due to microenvironmental differences or if there are also stochastic determinants arising from intrinsic biomolecular fluctuations.

Strikingly, heterogeneity in quiescence induction, a core feature of dormancy, has been shown in some contexts to be largely recapitulated by a toggle switch consisting of the cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) Cdk2 and its mutually antagonistic partner p2129–33, a CDK inhibitor shown to be upregulated in some dormant tumor phenotypes20. We thus hypothesized that analyzing variability in quiescence induction under hypoxia through an energy landscape described by this core p21-Cdk2 motif could provide insight into the non-genetic heterogeneity in dormancy induction. Further, as the quiescence-proliferation decision occurs on a high-dimensional landscape and involves several biochemical species, using this established core regulatory motif allows us to perform a commonly applied reductionism9,10,12 and map this complexity onto a better intuitable two-variable landscape. In addition to improved computational and visual tractability, this approach serves to further confirm if the core motif consisting of p21 and Cdk2 is sufficiently informative of quiescence-proliferation decisions on this lower-dimensional landscape.

Applying a trajectory-based energy landscape methodology34, supported by live-cell fluorescence microscopy and single-cell tracking, we mapped the underlying p21-Cdk2 landscape under normoxic and hypoxic conditions in MCF-7 breast cancer cells, finding highly variable quiescence-proliferation cell behavior within the population. We examined the dependence of cell fate on landscape features, surprisingly finding that initial position alone was only partially explanatory of decision-making in this context. We also observed minimal cell fate effects from intrinsic noise in p21 and Cdk2 levels despite the assumed primacy of position and noise in determining cell trajectories on energy landscapes. Instead, we found heterogeneous cell behavior to be explained by a combination of both initial position and cell state velocity. This improvement in cell fate prediction was shown to be due to the additional information encoded in velocity, but usually absent in instantaneous position, such as the epigenetic state and/or other interacting biochemical species (extrinsic noise). Due to this velocity dependence, cells appeared to exhibit “inertia”—a tendency to continue moving along a direction of motion unless acted on by an external force. In this context, proliferative cells preferred to remain proliferative unless the “force” generated by the change in the landscape due to hypoxia was strong enough to change their direction of movement and drive them towards a quiescent state. Overall, velocity provides an intuitive framework to infer cell behavior on an energy landscape, particularly when the position of a cell on the global landscape is partially obscured due to dimension reduction or challenges in measurement. This outlook may aid in designing alternate strategies for engineering desirable cell behavior in contexts such as cancer.

Results and discussion

Single-cell heterogeneity in cell cycle response to hypoxia in MCF-7 cells can be explained by a p21-Cdk2 potential energy landscape

Tumor cells have differential abilities to enter a dormant state depending on genotypic and phenotypic features22,25–27. For example, ER+ cells typically exhibit longer periods of dormancy before relapse than ER− cells do25. However, individual ER+ tumor cells can also differ in their ability to enter a dormant state25,27,28. While this heterogeneity can be due to microenvironmental factors, stochastic fluctuations in the pathways governing dormancy may also contribute. To examine this contribution, we used a hypoxia-mimetic cobalt chloride (CoCl2) platform that has previously been shown to recapitulate the heterogeneity in dormancy capabilities associated with the ER+ MCF-7 and ER− MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell lines in vitro, in addition to single-cell heterogeneity within MCF-7 cells28. This platform provides a uniform microenvironment, minimizing external variability, and can be used to draw conclusions about cell-to-cell heterogeneity due to genetic and epigenetic variability as well as intrinsic noise. Further, the introduction of CoCl2, hereafter referred to as hypoxia, is convenient and more stable compared to conventional hypoxic chambers wherein the normoxic response is established rapidly whenever cells are removed for manipulation and analysis. In addition, in contrast to true hypoxia where GFP variants exhibit reduced fluorescence due to improper folding, CoCl2-based hypoxia can be used with a wider range of non-overlapping fluorophores35,36 allowing for simultaneous marker tracking in live-cell imaging experiments.

Identifying the drivers of heterogeneous dormancy behavior under hypoxia in MCF-7 cells is challenging due to the number of phenotypic features associated with dormancy. However, quiescence, a reversible exit from the cell cycle into the G0 phase, is a conserved feature of cellular dormancy which has been previously shown to be underpinned by a mutually antagonistic p21-Cdk2 toggle switch30–32,37,38. Specifically, p21low/Cdk2high states are proliferative while p21high/Cdk2low states are quiescent (Fig. 1A). Thus, heterogeneity in p21 expression and Cdk2 activity, drivers of quiescence and proliferation, respectively, could be used to understand heterogeneity in dormancy capacities. This hypothesis is supported by previous observations of a mutually antagonistic p21-Cdk2 toggle switch underpinning quiescence induction in single cells29–33. Indeed, by western blotting, we found that MCF-7 cells showed upregulation of p21 along with downregulation of Cdk2 under hypoxia (Fig. 1B). While Cdk2 phosphorylation is the key determinant of its activity, we found a strong positive correlation between the expression levels of Cdk2 and the proliferation marker Ki67 in individual hypoxic and normoxic cells, indicating that Cdk2 expression was a valid metric of the pro-proliferation activity of Cdk2 in this context (Supplementary Fig. 1). We then examined whether the p21 and Cdk2 motif remains explanatory of the quiescence-proliferation decision in this context at a single-cell level. Using immunofluorescence microscopy, we observed significant heterogeneity in p21 and nuclear Cdk2 expression under hypoxia with minimal evident co-expression of p21 and Cdk2, supporting the notion of a mutually exclusive toggle switch between the two proteins (Fig. 1C). We inferred two subpopulations which are defined as p21high/Cdk2low (quiescent) and p21low/Cdk2high (proliferative). Taken together, these results validate the presence of a p21-Cdk2 toggle switch motif that underlies the single-cell quiescence-proliferation decision in MCF-7 cells under hypoxia.

We sought to examine whether such phenotypic heterogeneity can be explained by the underlying energy landscape. To this end, we applied a trajectory-based landscape generation methodology34 to the stochastic version of a quiescence-proliferation model developed by Heldt et al.38. Including the decision-critical species Cdk2 and p21, this comprehensive model comprises 57 reactions with 30 species and 56 kinetic parameters that are part of the G0, G1, and S phases of the cell cycle. Briefly, growth factor stimulation phosphorylates the Rb protein, allowing for dissociation of the inactive Rb:E2F complex. Free, active E2F initiates a strong positive feedback loop by driving its own transcription along with that of Cyclin E and Cyclin A. The latter two proteins form active complexes with Cdk2, leading to further phosphorylation of Rb. Once Cdk2 activity crosses a certain threshold, replication complexes are primed and the cell begins DNA synthesis, signaling S-phase entry. This process is inhibited by p53 through the synthesis of the Cdk2 inhibitor p21. In turn, p21 is inhibited by active Cdk2 through ubiquitin ligases forming a toggle switch that governs quiescence and proliferation. The original model describes the S phase as a transitory state and the G2 phase as the final steady state, so we modified the model to exclude the exit from S phase in order to focus solely on the decision to enter either S or G0 (i.e., the quiescence-proliferation decision).

We simulated this network of biochemical reactions with randomly initialized abundances of total p21 and active Cdk2 using the Gillespie stochastic simulation algorithm. Intrinsic biomolecular noise naturally arises from this algorithm by probabilistic selection of the reaction occurring at each time step. We then sampled points at uniform time intervals from the resulting trajectories to generate a probability distribution, P(p21, Cdk2), that was transformed into a quasi-potential U by taking its negative logarithm (U = -ln(P)). The number of trajectories, sampling interval, and total simulation time were chosen such that the resulting potential surface reached convergence. A region with low potential thus implies that cells within this population are highly likely to exist in the cell state defined by this region; that is, valleys on the landscape represent stable steady states for this system and the relative depths of these valleys represent the relative stabilities of these states. The energy landscape generated for this system (Fig. 1D) shows the existence of two valleys—p21high/Cdk2low and p21low/Cdk2high. These represent the stable steady states of the system as shown by Heldt et al. and correspond to the subpopulations observed by immunostaining where p21high/Cdk2low indicates quiescence and p21low/Cdk2high indicates proliferation. In terms of cell cycle progression, the p21high/Cdk2low state refers to G0, the p21low/Cdk2high state refers to S phase, and the transitory region between these states is the G1 phase.

A previous landscape constructed by Chen and Li9 using the same model but with a different methodology similarly shows stable quiescence and proliferation states. Additionally, an intermediate G1 arrest stable state was shown to exist in certain parameter spaces, resulting in three stable steady states. While this tristability is consistent with the prediction of three distinct subpopulation clusters by unsupervised clustering analysis on single-cell protein expression data39, it is unclear whether these clusters are stable steady states or if they are transitory regions on the landscape due to the snapshot nature of the data and lack of individual cell tracking. Recent time-lapse microscopy and single-cell tracking studies indicate cells do not remain arrested in G1 and instead enter G0 under stress40,41. Therefore, a bistable landscape with a transient G1 state as obtained in this study and in some parameter regions in the aforementioned landscape study better recapitulates previously reported cell behavior. Overall, our results imply that heterogeneity in the quiescence-proliferation decision can be explained by an underlying bistable energy landscape as a function of p21 and Cdk2.

Hypoxia biases the energy landscape towards a quiescent phenotype but still permits heterogeneity that is not fully explained by position or intrinsic noise alone

Having validated the presence of a p21-Cdk2 energy landscape explanatory of cell behavior in this context, we sought to qualitatively map the change in landscape topography when switching from normoxic to hypoxic conditions. From the population-level data of p21 and Cdk2 expression in MCF-7 cells under hypoxia (Fig. 1B), we inferred that cells upregulate p21 and simultaneously downregulate Cdk2. p21 upregulation in this context is likely driven by the stabilization of its transcription factor p53 under hypoxia42. This phenomenon was recapitulated in the model by decreasing the p53 degradation rate. Similarly, simultaneous Cdk2 downregulation can be driven by p53-mediated MYC transrepression, an interaction not modeled in the original computational framework but found to be present in MCF-7 cells43. We modeled this interaction as a reduction in mitogenic signaling by reducing the basal synthesis rate of the transcription factor E2F.

The transition from normoxia to hypoxia caused the cell fate bias on the energy landscape to transform from strongly proliferative to strongly quiescent, as evidenced by the change from a deep proliferative valley at p21low/Cdk2high and shallow quiescent valley at p21high/Cdk2low to a shallow proliferative valley and deep quiescent valley (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, we also observed a small valley at a p21low/Cdk2low state under hypoxic conditions representing a state wherein cells were driven to quiescence primarily by a lack of mitogenic signaling as opposed to increased p21 upregulation (Fig. 2A). Cells from this p21low/Cdk2low state transitioned to the deeper p21high/Cdk2low quiescent valley upon sufficient p21 upregulation due to hypoxia (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). To validate our computational predictions, we used time-lapse imaging on MCF-7 cells engineered to express p21-mVenus from the native CDKN1A (gene encoding p21) genomic locus, DHB-mCherry (a Cdk2 sensor)30, and a H2B-mTurquoise nuclear marker and subjected them to hypoxia through CoCl2 treatment. Using single-cell tracking and fluorescence quantification, we obtained p21 expression and Cdk2 activity levels for individual cells, imaged every 20 min, over a period of 120 h (48 h under normoxia, 72 h under CoCl2). We used these experimental p21 and Cdk2 trajectories to map the p21/Cdk2 phase space occupancies under normoxia and hypoxia. In agreement with our simulated energy landscapes, we observed a reduction in occupancy of the p21low/Cdk2high proliferative state as evidenced by the disappearance of a valley in the p21low/Cdk2high region of the landscape. Simultaneously, there was increased occupancy of the p21high/Cdk2low quiescent state as indicated by the appearance of a valley (Fig. 2B). This change in the potential surface was consistent with our expectation of a strong reduction in the proliferative capacity of cells under hypoxia. We also observed a valley in the p21low/Cdk2low region of the landscape, indicating the presence of cells in such a state under hypoxia, mirroring the computational landscape. While the model predicts that this state is transitory and not a more permanent quiescent state, longer-term experiments (~1–2 weeks) would be required to classify this state with higher confidence in the observations. Interestingly, coexistence of the p21low/Cdk2low and p21high/Cdk2low phenotypes highlights the redundancy in quiescence induction driven by simultaneous inhibition of mitogenic signaling (lower E2F synthesis rate resulting in lower Cdk2 activation) and upregulation of stress signaling (lower p53 degradation rate resulting in higher p21 expression). This redundancy can aid in more robust quiescence induction as well as in the maintenance of this state, particularly if one of the pathways is more prone to noise. There exist analogous cases in proliferative cancer cells where redundancies in governing pathways44,45 can result in maintenance or recovery of a proliferative phenotype.

In general, the cells transited from the proliferative valley to the quiescent valley under hypoxia as evidenced by the decrease in occupancy of the p21low/Cdk2high region of the phase space and simultaneous increase in occupancy of the p21high/Cdk2low region. However, given the presence of multiple valleys in the hypoxic landscape and the distinct cell states observed, we hypothesized that cells would exhibit divergent p21-Cdk2 dynamics when the landscape is perturbed due to hypoxia. Using unsupervised clustering (see “Methods”), we identified four clusters of p21-Cdk2 dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 4). Comparing the clusters, we observed qualitatively different types of behavior for each with respect to the quiescence-proliferation decision immediately after hypoxia induction: (a) Cluster QQ (n = 53)—cells that either entered or started to enter quiescence before hypoxia treatment, remaining quiescent under hypoxia, (b) cluster PQ (n = 95)—initially proliferative cells that entered quiescence under hypoxia treatment, (c) cluster PP (n = 160)—cells that remained proliferative under hypoxia treatment (Fig. 2C). The fourth cluster of cells showed similar behavior to cluster QQ with cells exhibiting high p21 and low Cdk2 levels under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions; classifying the data into three clusters, instead of four, showed that this fourth cluster merged with cluster QQ (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). However, this cluster had significantly fewer cells (n = 6) compared to the other clusters (n = 53, 95, and 160) and, therefore, these cells were removed from analysis in order to provide better statistical significance for inter-cluster comparisons moving forward. We also noted that cells in cluster PP eventually entered quiescence at variable points of time after the first fate decision, either with or without cytokinesis. However, the analysis of such longer-term behavior is outside the scope of this work.

Such divergent quiescence-proliferation behavior of the clusters PP and PQ despite similar initial p21 expression and Cdk2 activity could be reflective of the complexity of the quiescence-proliferation decision. While p21, Cdk2, and the two-dimensional energy landscape involving these species are ultimately reflective of the cell fate in this context, there are several other biochemical species such as Rb and Cyclin D involved in the cell decision-making process whose instantaneous levels are not represented by p21 and Cdk2 alone. The instantaneous levels of all these species determine the initial position of the cells on the multi-dimensional landscape describing cell fate and can lead to divergent cell fate responses even with similar p21 and Cdk2 levels. However, it is impractical to simultaneously obtain temporal information in situ on all the species whose instantaneous levels could affect downstream decision-making. Instead, we relied on the dependence of these various species on cell cycle position and used Cdk2 activity dynamics as a qualitative proxy for cell position on the high-dimensional landscape. Using the relative shape of Cdk2 activity traces aligned to cell division, we classified the cells into either G0/G1/S, S/G2, or M (“Methods”). While there was a difference in the cell cycle position distribution between cluster QQ and PQ as well as between QQ and PP (P < 0.0001 and P < 0.001 respectively; Kolmogorov–Smirnov test), PP and PQ did not have statistically different distributions (Fig. 3C). While our analysis did not use more precise cell cycle tracking markers such as the FUCCI sensors46, we can still conclude that cells that remained proliferative under hypoxia were not solely cells that were in a different phase of the cell cycle.

For cells that divided after hypoxic treatment, one daughter cell was randomly picked for cell fate analysis to avoid multiple counting of the same cell. Including all daughter cells in the analysis did not change the results significantly; the conclusions drawn from the data remained the same (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). In addition, controlling for the approximate cell cycle phase when considering the initial position did not change the conclusions drawn from the results (Supplementary Fig. 6). Overall, our analysis demonstrates that initial position on the landscape, determined either by p21-Cdk2 levels or by the approximate cell cycle position, is unable to fully explain the observed inter-cluster quiescence-proliferation heterogeneity (Fig. 3D).

An additional landscape feature that can lead to differences in cell behavior despite similar initial positions is the presence of intrinsic noise, which can lead to randomness in cellular movement on the landscape and cause divergence in cell fates even with the same initial position47. For the stable steady state observed under hypoxia, intrinsic noise would manifest as stochastic fluctuations from a mean once the system has reached steady state implying that, over a given period, variability in protein expression in the overall population should be similar to intra-cell variability in protein expression. We investigated the contribution of intrinsic noise to system behavior in the computational model by considering two common sources of intrinsic noise: (1) low number of interacting biomolecules and (2) stochastic DNA damage accumulation and repair (Fig. 3E). Computationally, intrinsic noise was implemented by solving the biochemical reaction network using the discrete Gillespie stochastic simulation algorithm, consistent with the simulations used to obtain the energy landscapes in previous sections of this manuscript. In addition, stochastic DNA damage was implemented by incorporating reactions that described the random generation and subsequent p53-dependent repair of DNA breaks. Consistent with our experiment duration, we simulated n = 1000 trajectories for 72 h under the conditions determined for hypoxia in Fig. 2A and measured the coefficient of variation (CV) in p21 expression at steady state (here, 48–72 h). We found that intrinsic biomolecular noise (with and without stochastic DNA damage) led to an overall dataset CV that was similar to the intra-cell CV in this time period. However, when analyzing unsmoothed and background-corrected p21 traces across cluster QQ from 48 to 72 h (the cluster most likely to be at steady state during this time), we found that the CV of p21 expression was much less within cells than across the entire population within this period (Fig. 3F). This discrepancy in noise characteristics between the model and experimental observations implies that the variation in p21 expression and consequently cell fate heterogeneity is likely not primarily driven by intrinsic noise in this system.

Cell state velocity displays an inertial effect on the quiescence-proliferation decision

Previous studies show that flux is explanatory of cell fate in addition to position in non-equilibrium steady-state systems8,10,48. Given the non-equilibrium proliferative steady state of the cell cycle, we hypothesized that cell state velocity—that is, the rate of change of the biochemical determinants of the cell state—may be predictive of cell fate when combined with initial position, especially when the landscape is perturbed. Velocity in this differential equation-based mathematical model is determined by model variables, system parameters, and intrinsic biochemical noise. Since position and intrinsic noise were not fully explanatory of the observed differences, we focused on the effects of system parameters for this analysis. Biologically, these parameters represent critical effectors of cell fate, such as epigenetic regulation and signaling crosstalk, that are not explicitly described by the abundances of the system variables (i.e., the position on the overall multi-dimensional landscape).

Since the cell cycle has a non-equilibrium stable steady state, we used cell cycle velocity as a measure of the average cell state velocity. In the mathematical model, cell cycle velocity was defined as the inverse of time to S-phase entry. We examined the effect of the top ten parameters identified in the local sensitivity analysis performed by Heldt et al.38, finding that modulating these parameters also changed cell cycle velocity (Fig. 4A). The direction of change in cell cycle velocity with respect to change in these parameters was the same as that of the landscape flux as measured by Chen and Li9, highlighting the consistency between these quantities. Further, parameter changes that slowed cells eventually led to quiescence (Fig. 4A). Therefore, for a given fold change in a parameter due to stress, faster cells are more likely to remain proliferative in comparison to slower cells as they are farther away from the quiescent zero velocity state. In our experimental data, we observed a strong correlation between the number of cell divisions in the imaging period 48 h prior to hypoxic treatment and the percentage of cells in cluster PP, validating our computational predictions of an increased propensity to remain proliferative under hypoxic stress in faster cells (Fig. 4B). Consistent with this observation, when examining cells born less than 3 h after a shift to hypoxic conditions, we found that cells in cluster PP had a shorter mother cell cycle time (i.e., had higher cell cycle velocity) compared to cells in cluster PQ (Fig. 4C). While time to S-phase entry (computational measure) and cell cycle duration (experimental measure) are not identical, it has been previously shown that these two quantities exhibit a strong positive correlation, supporting the validity of this comparison49. If the improvement in cell fate prediction is due to the intrinsic information about system parameters (and potentially, other interacting species) encoded in velocity, then cell-to-cell variation in system parameters (extrinsic noise) is likely a significant contributor to cell fate heterogeneity. This extrinsic noise leads to cell-to-cell heterogeneity in the mean steady-state values. Therefore, we hypothesized that normalizing the magnitudes of steady-state p21 expression used in Fig. 3F would lead to a lower dataset CV, significantly attenuating the effects of extrinsic noise and retaining intrinsic noise. Indeed, when we rescaled each individual trace by dividing it by its own maximum, we observed a reduction in an overall CV (Fig. 4D) that agreed with the model predicted effects of intrinsic noise (Fig. 3F). Further, we found that computationally varying Cyclin D abundance along with the synthesis rates of E2F and p21, revealed a negative and positive association of cell cycle velocity with mean p21 expression and mean Cdk2 activity, respectively, which was recapitulated experimentally (Supplementary Fig. 7). Further, as extrinsic noise is thought to be driven by variations in epigenetic states50, which are inheritable, we hypothesized that sister cells would largely exhibit the same cell fate and thus share the same cluster. By examining the cluster identity of sister cells born before hypoxia, we found that there is a high probability (P = 0.64) of sister cells belonging to the same cluster (Fig. 4E). To calculate the statistical significance of this result, we used a permutation test in which we shuffled the cluster identities of all cells in the original dataset while maintaining the proportions of cells belonging to each cluster and examined the probability of sister cells belonging to the same cluster in these randomized cases. From a null distribution generated by 105 different instances of shuffling, we found that less than 0.01% showed a same-cluster probability ≥0.64 with a mean probability of 0.36 for sister cells to be in the same cluster for a dataset with the same composition of cell fate outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 8A). This constitutes a P value less than 0.0001 for our observation with a 1.78 (0.64/0.36) times greater likelihood for cell fate congruence than predicted by chance.

Interestingly, 36% of sister pairs belonged to different clusters (Fig. 4E). We hypothesized this effect to be driven by a combination of extrinsic noise and bistability. Sister cells with parameters close to the cusp of bistability can have divergent cell fates when combined with small changes in system parameters or stochastic dynamics. We examined the phase space of the p21-Cdk2 dynamical system using basal E2F synthesis and p53 stability to represent mitogenic and stress signaling, respectively. With increasing E2F synthesis rates (corresponding to enhanced mitogenic signaling), we observed a transition from monostable quiescence to monostable proliferation bridged by a bistable region (Fig. 4F). For cells transitioning from proliferation to quiescence, parameter values that lie at the cusp between bistability and quiescence would result in more heterogeneous cell fate outcomes when compared to cells that are farther from this region. Considering cells that are sisters at the time of hypoxia induction, we found that prior to and under hypoxia, more pairs shared the same fate, and fewer pairs had divergent fates than expected by random chance (Fig. 4G, H and Supplementary Fig. 8B). This higher-than-expected cell fate congruence was consistent with the notion that there was some determinism (i.e., inheritance) associated with cell fate determination in this context. Based on the observed abundances of sister pair fate (Fig. 4G), we expected most sister cells under normoxia to be farther from the transition from bistability to quiescence on the phase space, with some sister cells at the cusp of this transition. Under hypoxia, we hypothesized this population would shift towards a region with decreasing p53 degradation and E2F synthesis rates (Fig. 4F). Therefore, we would expect a larger number of sister cell pairs to be in the quiescent region as well as the cusp and fewer proliferative sister pairs. In agreement with this prediction, we observed an increase in the number of sister pairs that were both quiescent or had divergent fates and a corresponding decrease in the number of proliferative pairs (Fig. 4H). Overall, these results validate our hypothesis of cell-to-cell heterogeneity being driven by extrinsic noise in this system.

Consequentially, the importance of extrinsic noise observed here implies that steady-state valley width on this energy landscape should no longer be interpreted as the set of states accessible to each individual cell at steady state through intrinsic noise-driven fluctuations. Instead, this width is representative of the set of states that is occupied by the entire subpopulation of cells with that phenotype (here, quiescent or proliferative) and each individual cell is only able to access a small subset of these states through intrinsic noise. Specifically, the magnitude of intrinsic biochemical noise/fluctuations in the species that determine the landscape dictates the region of the energy landscape accessible to each cell. Greater noise leads to better accessibility of the landscape for each individual cell. Further, the p21/Cdk2-derived position and intrinsic noise are no longer fully explanatory of cell behavior in this context because of inter-cell parameter variability. Information about these parameters and, thus, their effect on cell behavior can be represented on the landscape as cell-to-cell differences in their velocity on this landscape. It is also likely that velocity encodes information from additional species that are not directly measured, due to their effect on d[p21]/dt and d[Cdk2]/dt, thereby providing a more comprehensive picture of the cell state. For example, p21 and Cdk2 are influenced by basal levels of endogenous stress and mitogenic signaling through p53 and Cyclin D, respectively29. These are explicitly modeled in the differential equations implying that the dynamics of p21 and Cdk2 encode information about these upstream species that affect cell fate. Under external perturbations, these effects can be more intuitively interpreted as inertia due to the pre-existing cell cycle velocity when the landscape changes. In other words, faster cells have a higher propensity to continue along their path and remain proliferative despite an external force, arising from topographical changes in the landscape due to environmental perturbations, that biases against the direction of motion. In contrast, slower cells are more likely to follow the topography of the newly perturbed landscape and become quiescent.

Nathans et al.40 reported a similar inertial effect reflective of the delay in p21 upregulation post-DNA damage. They showed that cells continued along the cell cycle and entered S phase despite DNA damage in G1 unless the force generated by p21 upregulation was large enough to route the cells to G0. Consequently, they observed that “older” cells that were further along G1 were more likely to enter S phase despite DNA damage as they had less time to upregulate p21 sufficiently to stop entry into the S phase. In contrast, “younger” cells that were earlier in G1 were more likely to enter quiescence. Interestingly, we observed no statistically significant difference in the age distributions of quiescent and proliferative cells relative to hypoxia induction (Supplementary Fig. 9). Cells born earlier, and therefore likely to be further along G1, did not necessarily remain proliferative under hypoxia. This discrepancy could be due to the differences in stresses used in both studies which can result in differing upstream dynamics that subsequently lead to different effects on the same core network. Similar to our observations of longer mother cell cycle durations being associated with quiescence, previous studies have reported that stochastic DNA damage in the mother cell cycle can lead to longer cell cycles and increased propensity for daughter cell quiescence31,51. This effect was shown to be driven by p21 upregulation starting in the M phase of the mother cell. While we observed higher p21 expression in cells that are in the M phase at the time of hypoxic treatment in cluster QQ, there were no statistically significant differences between clusters PQ and PP (Supplementary Fig. 6). Taken together, these findings suggest that the inertial effect of cell cycle velocity observed in our study is more strongly driven by cell-to-cell heterogeneity in parameters. Indeed, simulations using a minimal two-component p21/Cdk2 model showed that heterogeneity in cell fate was primarily a function of parameter variability and not initial p21/Cdk2 abundances (Supplementary Fig. 10). While this minimal model did not include cell cycle progression, and thus, does not show the cell cycle phase-specific dynamics of p21 expression and Cdk2 activity shown in Heldt et al., it recapitulates the general quiescence-proliferation behavior of a cell by using p21 and Cdk2 as proxies for anti- and pro-proliferative factors, respectively.

Given the nature of inertia observed in our study, this velocity dependence is likely not seen on the true higher-dimensional landscape that accounts for all species contributing to cell behavior as well as the processes that lead to epigenomic differences as position would be fully determinative of cell state in that context. However, in addition to challenges associated with computing and visualizing such a high-dimensional landscape, identifying all species associated with a decision is infeasible and any model formulated is necessarily less comprehensive than the biological process being modeled. This limitation implies that, practically, position cannot be fully determinative of cell state despite being increasingly determinative with additional dimensions. In addition, it is also increasingly impractical to experimentally measure the corresponding dynamics of a multitude of species that contribute to fate determination in individual cells. Incorporating velocity offers a more visually and computationally tractable approach to recapitulate the overall cellular position on the more complex high-dimensional landscape underpinning cell behavior and is expected to be informative even in more comprehensive models. Furthermore, this approach mitigates experimental limitations in single-cell dynamic measurements imposed by the paucity of non-overlapping fluorophores required for distinguishing each species being observed. Importantly, velocity information is inherent to single-cell time-lapse microscopy experiments, so no additional experimentation is needed; velocities can be directly extracted from positional time-series data. The increased information gained from such experimental velocity measurements can especially provide better insight into pre-existing cell states defined by parameter variations in highly complex systems16,17,52 and can thereby improve predictability of cell behavior under perturbations. Following our observations in this study, we expect that analyses of trajectories in analogous cell fate decisions would significantly benefit from the experimental measurement of system velocities, particularly under dynamic or transient inputs, providing insight and intuition into system features that are typically obscured in position-centric frameworks.

In the context of cancer dormancy, these results suggest that the velocity of the system (i.e., the epigenetic state of the system) contributes to quiescence-proliferation heterogeneity in individual cells more than intrinsic noise. However, over longer times, we observed that most cells in cluster PP eventually become quiescent after prolonged G2 arrest, with or without cell division (Supplementary Fig. 11), implying that chronic hypoxia can likely overcome the effects of initial velocity/epigenetic state. It is likely that the effects of velocity are more pronounced under transient or fluctuating hypoxia wherein subpopulations of cells can enter quiescence depending on their velocity while others remain proliferative. This hypothesis is challenging to validate using our current experimental system due to the retention of cobalt chloride in cells after washout as well as the misfolding of GFP variants used in live-cell imaging under true hypoxia. However, similar time-dependent phenomena are seen in other systems with toggle switch motifs such as quiescence exit53,54, cell differentiation55, and synthetic genetic circuits56 where heterogeneity in initial biochemical states within the governing bistable switch is amplified leading to divergent phenotypes. In these studies, cells undergo phenotypic transitions at different times leading to variability in cell states under transient or weak cues, similar to cell behavior under early hypoxia observed in our system. In comparison, cells exit quiescence more uniformly or differentiate better under sustained or stronger cues in these examples, analogous to behavior in chronic hypoxia. Together, these lend support for the role of velocity in transient or fluctuating hypoxia in propagating single-cell heterogeneity in dormancy.

Finally, considering a velocity-based perspective can help design strategies for altering system dynamics to engineer cell behavior. For instance, single-cell heterogeneity in cancer cell apoptosis under cisplatin was better explained by the rate of p53 accumulation rather than total p53 accumulated over time57,58. From a landscape perspective, these cells can be imagined as being given an initial cisplatin-driven impulse that directs them to a high-p53 apoptotic state while the landscape itself is biased towards a low-p53 survival state. Cells with high velocities from the initial impulse better resist the underlying landscape that favors a surviving state, resulting in a velocity-dependent cell fate decision in which faster cells undergo apoptosis while slower cells survive. Subsequently, increasing the rate of accumulation (velocity) using a small molecule in these studies reduced the fraction of surviving cells after treatment independent of the total p53 accumulated under treatment. In addition to systems where velocity explicitly plays a role in cell decision-making, this phenomenon is likely to also arise under transient cues where the landscape dynamically changes in response to the presence and absence of the cue. For example, pharmacodynamic effects in a clinical context can lead to changes in the underlying energy landscape during and after drug administration. It would be informative to map the energy landscape under these scenarios and examine the effect of inertia on cell transitions in this context. This information could be further applied to formulate strategies based on exploiting inertia for favorable tumor responses.

In conclusion, this study highlights that the additional cell state information encoded in velocity can be used to better predict cell fate on an energy landscape. This manifests as an inertial effect wherein the cells resist changes in their direction of movement even when the underlying landscape has changed to restrict movement in that direction (Fig. 5). Such velocity-driven inertia can even supersede the effects of initial position on the changed lower-dimensional landscape, as seen in our study. This phenomenon is not limited to the cell cycle and is likely present in most non-equilibrium systems such as the circadian rhythm, p53, and NF-κB networks wherein external perturbations act on cells that are already moving on the landscape as well as systems where there is transience in the cue leading to reversible changes in the underlying landscape. Methods

Human cell lines and culture

MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cell lines (ATCC) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Media (DMEM, 4500 mg/L glucose, MilliporeSigma) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). MCF-7 cells tagged with mVenus fluorescent protein at the endogenous p21 locus and transfected with DHB-mCherry and H2B-mTurquoise (a kind gift from Dr. Sabrina Spencer30) were maintained in RPMI-1640 (MilliporeSigma) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (PenStrep 10,000 U/mL; Life Technologies). Due to the loss of DHB-mCherry and/or H2B-mTurquoise in subpopulations of these cells, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) was used to obtain cells that retained expression of both constructs.

Live-cell microscopy and image analysis

For live-cell imaging, cells were plated on six-well 1.5 glass-bottom dishes (Mattek) at a density of 1.25 × 105 cells per well in transparent RPMI-1640 media (Life Technologies), lacking phenol red and riboflavin, supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) PenStrep. Cells were imaged with a Nikon Eclipse Ti-E inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with an automated stage (Prior) and a custom chamber to maintain 37 °C, 5% CO2, and high humidity. At each time point, images were collected for the brightfield, YFP, Texas Red, and CFP channels using a 20× CFI Plan Apochromat Lambda (NA = 0.75) objective (Nikon) at 20% lamp intensity. The exposure time for each channel was set to be <500 ms. The imaging protocol was paused briefly to add CoCl2 containing media to induce hypoxic conditions and resumed afterward. The ND2 format imaging data was exported as individual tiff files for each channel, time point, and stage position using Bio-Formats command line tools59. Cells were tracked semi-automatically in p53Cinema60 using the nuclear marker H2B-mTurquoise in the CFP channel. p21-mVenus expression for each cell trace was obtained using the getDatasetTraces_fillLineageInformation function while DHB-mCherry nuclear (DHBnuc) and cytoplasmic (DHBcyt) intensities were obtained by the getDatasetTraces_localSegmentation function, both built into p53Cinema. The traces were analyzed using MATLAB as described in the following paragraphs. Briefly, the traces were background subtracted, smoothed, and clustered using dynamic time warping and hierarchical clustering. Scripts used are provided in Code_Inertia_p21Cdk2.zip.

Single-cell trace processing

The individual cell traces obtained from p53Cinema were background-corrected and smoothed for further analysis. p21-mVenus traces were background-corrected by subtracting the minimum value of each individual trace from itself. For DHB-mCherry, the minimum of the cytoplasmic values or the nuclear values for each trace, whichever was lower, was subtracted from both the cytoplasmic and nuclear traces. Normalized Cdk2 activity (Cdk2a) is calculated as DHBcyt/(DHBcyt + DHBnuc). In contrast to the ratio of cytoplasmic intensity to nuclear intensity (DHBcyt/DHBnuc), this scaled measure is more robust to temporal variations in sensor expression observed in our study (Supplementary Fig. 12) while still being representative of Cdk2 activity. However, it must be noted that there appears to be a reduction in dynamic range in untreated cells.

Data processing and clustering analysis

Individual traces were smoothed using a moving-average algorithm in the smoothdata function in MATLAB. Dynamic time warping, dtw function in MATLAB, using a maximum warping distance of five data points (100 min in real-world time) was then applied to calculate pair-wise dissimilarities between traces for both p21 and Cdk2. The Euclidean sum (sum of squares) of the p21 trace distance and Cdk2 trace distance was calculated for each pair of traces. These pair-wise distances were used to perform agglomerative hierarchical clustering (linkage function in MATLAB) with the ‘ward’ algorithm, previously shown to perform satisfactorily for single-cell traces61. The appropriate number of clusters was determined by calculating the Calinski-Harabasz index for different cluster numbers and picking the number with the highest value (evalclusters function in MATLAB); this corresponded to the number of clusters such that the ratio of inter-cluster variance to mean intra-cluster variance was maximized. In other words, on average, data was classified such that points within the same cluster had the highest similarity while being dissimilar to data points from any other cluster. In our dataset, the maximum was obtained for a choice of four clusters. However, one of the clusters consisted of very few cells compared to the other clusters. This cluster was considered as an outlier and discarded.

Cell cycle classification

Cells were classified into G0/G1/S, S/G2, or M phases by the relative Cdk2 activity trend starting from the previous division up to the CoCl2 addition. First, each trace was aligned to the last division before CoCl2 was added to the culture. The traces were smoothed further using a Gaussian algorithm to preserve the general trend while more aggressively removing noisiness. If a trace had a higher value at CoCl2 addition compared to immediately after division, it was assigned to the S/G2 bin; if the value was lower, it was assigned to the G0/G1/S bin. If there was a maximum in Cdk2 activity where Cdk2 initially rose and then eventually came back down, those cells were assigned to the M bin. In addition, if at least 50% of the trace had Cdk2 activity less than a threshold value of 0.38, it was classified as G0/G1/S. This threshold was determined by the central minima of the bimodal distribution of Cdk2 activities, representing the likely discriminant value between high and low Cdk2 activity states.

Density plot generation from experimental data

We selected parts of the p21-Cdk2 time-lapse cell trajectories that were representative of cell fate and not primarily consisting of transitory cells that arise due to changes in the underlying landscape. As the cells were cultured under normoxia, they could be considered non-transitory and therefore, all traces prior to hypoxic treatment were used to generate the normoxic density plot. However, for the hypoxic plots, we discarded cell trace values prior to 52 h (~1.5 times the average MCF-7 cell doubling time) of hypoxia to avoid non-steady-state dynamics during the transition from normoxia to hypoxia. Cobalt chloride treatment to mimic hypoxia

CoCl2 (MilliporeSigma) was dissolved in type I Milli-Q water at a concentration of 10 mM and filtered using a sterile 0.45-µm polyethersulfone (PES) Steriflip 50 mL filter unit (MilliporeSigma). The resulting aqueous solution was directly added to the cell culture media to obtain a final concentration of 300 µM for MCF-7 cells and 100 µM for MDA-MB-231 cells, consistent with a prior study28. Treatment was initiated when the cells began exponential growth, as determined by growth curves (day 2 and day 3 for MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, respectively).

Immunostaining

Cells in a 12-well plate were rinsed 3× with 1 mL DPBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and washed 3× with 1 mL DPBS for 5 min each. Blocking and permeabilization were performed for 1 h in 1 mL blocking buffer—Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) containing 5% (v/v) normal goat serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 (MilliporeSigma). Then, the cells were stained overnight at 4 °C with 500 µL solution of primary antibody diluted in antibody dilution buffer (DPBS containing 1% g/mL bovine serum albumin (MilliporeSigma) and 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100). Antibodies and dilutions are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The cells were then washed 3× with 1 mL DPBS for 5 min each followed by labeling for 1 h at room temperature with 500 µL of the corresponding secondary antibodies diluted in antibody dilution buffer. Cells were again washed 3× with 1 mL DPBS for 5 min each, followed by nuclear labeling for 5 min with 1 mL 300 nM DAPI dissolved in DPBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a 5-min DPBS wash. Finally, 1 mL DPBS was added to the cells before imaging. Samples were imaged using an EVOS FL Auto fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantification was performed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). For imaging in other well formats, the volumes were scaled as per the typical culture media volume for each dish.

Western blotting

For protein extraction, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer mixed with HALT Protease Inhibitor and HALT Phosphatase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Whole-cell lysates were separated on an SDS-PAGE gradient gel (4–15%) and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio-Rad). p21 and Cdk2 were detected in 15 μg of whole-cell lysates. β-actin was detected in 10 μg of whole-cell lysates. The membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk (Bio-Rad) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20 (TBS-T; MilliporeSigma) at room temperature for 1 h. Next, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody diluted in 5% milk or 5% BSA in TBS-T (as per the manufacturer’s protocol), followed by 1× 15-min wash and 2× 5-min washes with TBS-T. Antibodies and dilutions are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The membrane was then incubated with its corresponding secondary antibody diluted in 5% milk in TBS-T at room temperature for 1 h, followed by TBS-T washes as with the primary antibody. Protein expression was detected using SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a ChemiDoc Imager (Bio-Rad). Western blot quantification was performed by ImageLab (Bio-Rad). An equal-sized rectangular region was annotated around the detected protein band in each lane, and the integrated intensity within this box was calculated. This integrated intensity was background-corrected using a global background calculated using a region on the image not containing any protein bands or ladder. The background-corrected values were then normalized to β-actin expression, also calculated with the same processing workflow as described, from each sample to obtain the normalized protein expression values.

Statistical analysis

Western blot data for each protein were analyzed using ANOVA followed by a multiple comparison test in GraphPad PRISM. Data obtained from time-lapse microscopy and single-cell tracking were analyzed using MATLAB. Comparisons of ranks were carried out using a non-parametric version of ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis test. Discrete distributions were compared using a custom MATLAB script implementing the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test, where the statistical significance of the KS statistic was determined by comparison to a distribution of KS statistics calculated from a null distribution of the KS statistic obtained from 104 shuffling iterations of the initial dataset. P values for all comparisons from the main figures are provided in Supplementary Table S6. The statistical significance of same cluster vs. different cluster probabilities for sister cells was calculated by comparison with a distribution of probabilities calculated from 105 shuffling iterations of the cluster identities within the original dataset.

Computational methods

The ODE-based deterministic mathematical model38 was solved using the numerical stiff solver ode23s in MATLAB (Mathworks, Natick, MA). The corresponding reactions and rate constants are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. Rate constant values (Supplementary Table S4) used in this manuscript are the same as those specified in ref. 38. These values were obtained by calibrating the model to quantitatively match cell cycle durations and qualitatively match p21 dynamics, Cyclin E/A protein abundances, and Cdk2 activities of immortalized retinal pigment epithelium cells (RPE-hTERT). For energy landscape generation, the stochastic version of the model in the form of a biochemical reaction network was programmed and solved via the Gillespie algorithm62 in Julia63 using the ModelingToolkit.jl64 and DifferentialEquations.jl65 packages. Further, we removed equations involving DNA synthesis, DNA damage induction and repair, as well as terms describing disassembly of DNA replication complexes post-S phase. Histograms, pseudo-energy landscapes, and parameter phase spaces were visualized using MATLAB. Scripts used are provided in Code_Inertia_p21Cdk2.zip. Simulations for estimating intrinsic noise were performed using COPASI files provided in ref. 38 that implement the model as a set of biochemical reactions.

Pseudo-energy landscape generation

Trajectories from the stochastic model were used to generate the pseudo-energy landscape as described previously34. Briefly, the stochastic version of the model was run for multiple different initial values of total p21 and active Cdk2, each sampled from uniform distributions spanning the variable ranges of interest. The resulting trajectories of total p21 and active Cdk2 abundances were sampled at uniform time intervals to generate a list of cell states accessed by the simulated cells. This list was used to calculate a probability distribution P (p21, Cdk2) of the total p21 and active Cdk2 values from these trajectories. We then converted this distribution into a pseudo-energy landscape using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U=-\mathrm{ln}\left(P\right)$$\end{document}U=−lnP. Any instances of zero probability were reassigned a value equal to the minimum non-zero probability to ensure a bounded (i.e., non-infinite) potential everywhere on the landscape. The resulting potential surface was visualized using the surf function in MATLAB. The number of trajectories (5000), discretization intervals (0.2 min), and simulation time (10,000 min) were determined by examining landscape convergence properties as described previously34. Specifically, additional trajectories, longer simulation times, or shorter sampling intervals beyond the specified values did not change the resulting energy landscape. Scripts used are provided in Code_Inertia_p21Cdk2.zip.

Correlation of average total p21 and active Cdk2 with cell cycle velocity

The average total p21 and average Cdk2 activity for each cell were measured in vitro over the entire cell cycle. However, the model does not cover the entire cell cycle; it does not loop back to G1, but rather stays in G2 indefinitely after S-phase exit. To have a more commensurate comparison, we considered the values of p21 and Cdk2 for 4 h after S-phase exit in the model, measured using DNA concentration thresholds as described in the previous section. This duration is the average eukaryotic G2 phase length66. To qualitatively simulate the effects of biological heterogeneity, the E2F and p21 synthesis rates were varied from the basal values by −0.1 to 0.2 on the log2 scale and 0 to 1 on the log10 scale, respectively. In addition, each parameter pair was simulated 10 times where the initial values for Cyclin D, total Cyclin E:Cdk2, total Cyclin A:Cdk2, and total p21 were sampled from a normal distribution centered around the values in Heldt et al.38 with 20% coefficient of variation. Cell cycle velocity was considered the inverse of S-phase exit time. Quiescent cells were assigned a velocity of zero and their p21 and Cdk2 levels were averaged over the entire simulation length. Average levels of total p21 and active Cdk2 complexes were then measured and plotted as a function of cell cycle velocity.
