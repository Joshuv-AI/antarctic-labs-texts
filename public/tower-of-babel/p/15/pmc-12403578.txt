Differential placebo effect of sham acupuncture for chronic pain: a network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Renhong Wan, Qianhua Zheng, Xiaorui Zeng, Yalan Luo, Luqiang Sun, Shuai Chen, Fangli Luo, Yupeng Zhang, Ziyue Zhu, Xinyun Chen, Yuanyuan Zhao, Ying Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical effects of acupuncture are often underestimated due to the placebo effect of sham acupuncture. While multiple sham acupuncture methods have been used in clinical practice, it is challenging to select the appropriate sham acupuncture technique due to the lack of head-to-head trials.PurposeThe purpose of this study was to compare the differential placebo effects of diverse sham acupuncture interventions, providing evidence-based guidance for selecting optimal placebo controls in clinical practice.MethodPubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Web of Science were systematically searched for randomized controlled trials comparing acupuncture with sham acupuncture for the treatment of chronic non-cancer pain (from inceptions to May 1, 2025). The primary outcome measure was pain variation, while secondary outcome measures included adverse events and the assessment of acupuncture blinding. Frequentist random-effect models were employed to perform indirect treatment comparison meta-analysis, and Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) was utilized to evaluate the level of evidence.ResultsA total of 45 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, involving 7 sham acupuncture methods and 8287 participants. Regarding pain variation, the difference was noted between the waiting treatment group (mean difference 1.86 (95% confidence interval 1.36 to 2.35), SUCRA 97.4%; moderate confidence of evidence) and manual acupuncture group was most pronounced, followed by the difference between the acupoint and no penetration group (mean difference 1.14 (95% confidence interval 0.74 to 1.55), SUCRA 73.6%; low confidence of evidence) and the nonacupoint and no penetration group (mean difference 1.00 (95% confidence interval 0.35 to 1.65), SUCRA 64.8%; low confidence of evidence). Additionally, the rate of adverse events was comparable among the aforementioned sham acupuncture groups, and the blinding procedure was successfully implemented in the trials.ConclusionsThe placebo effect of sham acupuncture is significant, among which the no penetration (acupoint or nonacupoint) groups exhibited the lowest placebo effect. Nevertheless, further head-to-head trials are warranted to obtain direct evidence.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO no. CRD42024535012.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05055-x.Keywords: Manual acupuncture, Sham acupuncture, Placebo, Chronic pain, Randomized controlled trial, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Placebo effect refers to the phenomenon that patients who receive an ineffective treatment"Expect"or"Believe"that the treatment will be effective, resulting in relief of symptoms. In 1995, Henry K. Beecher pointed out that the placebo effect is common, and medicine can be considered effective only if it is proved in a"double-blind"trial that its effect is greater than that of placebo effect [1]. Moreover, the magnitude of the placebo effect may be a crucial factor in determining the outcome of the trial [2]. It can be seen that to evaluate whether medicine or intervention method is effective, it is important to choose the right placebo control.

The past few decades have witnessed an increase in the number of randomized controlled trials investigating the efficacy and safety of acupuncture, and the specificity of acupuncture efficacy has garnered extensive attention. In the study design of acupuncture, sham acupuncture is commonly employed due to its significant placebo effect, which can otherwise lead to an underestimation of the therapeutic effect of acupuncture in clinical practice [3]. To minimize the placebo effect associated with sham acupuncture, several clinical sham acupuncture devices have been developed, such as Streitberger device [4], Park device [5], Takakura device [6], etc. While these devices effectively blind patients, the placebo effect cannot be comprehensively mitigated. Based on the theory of traditional Chinese medicine, the efficacy of acupuncture is contingent upon correct acupoint selection, proper depth, and sufficient stimulation intensity [7]."Deqi", which is generally considered critical for the adequacy of stimulus intensity [8], is avoided in all sham acupuncture techniques. Notably, the high variability in placebo effects between different sham acupuncture techniques largely arises from differences in depth and location [9]. The differences in location and depth have led to a variety of sham acupuncture methods, such as nonacupoint and shallow needling, nonacupoint and deep needling, nonacupoint and no penetration, and acupoint and no penetration [10]. Despite these sham acupuncture methods being widely used in evidence-based research [11–13], the rationale for their selection remains elusive.

The analgesic effects of acupuncture have been validated in numerous clinical and basic research [14, 15]. Nonetheless, the clinical significance of its analgesic effect remains conflicting. The controversy primarily arises from the high placebo effect linked to sham acupuncture, generally resulting in an underestimation of the effects of acupuncture [16, 17]. Although various sham acupuncture theories or devices have been applied in clinical practice, studies objectively evaluating their clinical effects are scarce. Likewise, trials that directly compare different sham acupuncture methods are limited, posing challenges for clinicians to make accurate and evidence-based decisions. Therefore, an indirect comparison meta-analysis was conducted to assess and compare the clinical effects of different sham acupuncture methods. This network meta-analysis evaluated the differential placebo effects of diverse sham acupuncture protocols in chronic pain management, synthesizing evidence to guide optimal placebo control selection in clinical trials.

Methods

We prospectively registered this study to PROSPERO with number CRD42024535012(https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024535012), and followed the PRISMA (preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses) 2020 [18] and extension statement for network meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) [19].

Data sources and searches

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science databases to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing acupuncture and sham acupuncture for the treatment of chronic non-cancer pain. MeSH terms combined with entry terms were used during the search, and the search period ranged between database inception and May 2025 (Appendix 1). Additionally, ClinicalTrials.gov was searched to identify additional studies and screen the citations of relevant reviews and articles.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were determined using the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design) model. Inclusion criteria included RCTs enrolling adults with chronic non-cancer pain (Chronic non-cancer Pain was defined and classified according to the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) criteria [20]) who had undergone acupuncture treatment for at least 4 weeks prior to the study. In order to improve the transitivity and homogeneity of the study, the intervention was limited to manual acupuncture, whereas other forms of acupuncture, such as electroacupuncture and auricular acupuncture, were excluded. Studies that combined multiple acupuncture treatments were also excluded. Sham acupuncture was defined as a placebo acupuncture method for the purpose of control, such as inserting needles into non-acupoints, using non-penetrating needles, or placebo devices. The primary outcome measure was the change in pain intensity from baseline. If the study reported more than one pain scale, the most relevant one was selected, with preference given to the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), given that this is the most commonly used scale in pain research. Secondary outcome measures were adverse events and blinding in acupuncture evaluations.

Study selection

All retrieved studies were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA), and duplicate studies were removed. The screening process consisted of three steps. Firstly, two authors (XR Z and YL L) independently screened their titles and abstracts based on eligibility criteria and excluded irrelevant studies. Secondly, two authors (XR Z and YL L) cross-checked the screening results, and disagreements were resolved by discussion and arbitrated by a third author (RH W). Thirdly, the full text of studies that met the eligibility criteria was further reviewed. Articles were excluded if they lacked sufficient data for conducting the meta-analysis. Studies only available as abstracts or duplicated publications were also excluded.

Data extraction

Two authors (XR Z and YL L) extracted the following information from each included study using a predesigned standardized form: title, author, date of publication, country, participant characteristics, details of the intervention, and outcome measures (mean and standard deviation for continuous data and number of events for dichotomous data). When both per-protocol and intention-to-treat data were reported, preference was given to the latter. If the required data were not available in a published manuscript, attempts were made to obtain information from the authors or the trial registry.

Mean and standard deviation were used to describe changes in pain intensity. Values were calculated using the formula provided in the Cochrane Handbook if the mean and standard deviation were not directly provided in the study [21]. Pain intensity was measured on a 10-cm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS).

Changes in pain intensity during follow-up were also analyzed and classified into short-term (≤ 3 months) and long-term (> 3 months) follow-up intervals. When data from multiple time points were available within the same follow-up interval, the data from the most recent time point within that interval were extracted.

Quality assessment of evidence

Cochrane's Risk of Bias Version 2 (RoB 2) tool was employed to evaluate the risk of bias for each RCT [22]. This tool comprises five components: randomization, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Two authors (XR Z and YL L) independently conducted the assessments, followed by a cross-checking process, with disagreements resolved through discussion. To ensure the robustness of the outcomes, sensitivity analyses were performed using a Bayesian random-effects model. Furthermore, we assessed the robustness of the results by employing an exclusion method stratified by pain type to evaluate the impact of different pain mechanisms on the outcomes. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was constructed to evaluate potential publication bias. Additionally, Egger's test was applied to quantitatively assess publication bias. The Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework was utilized to evaluate the certainty of evidence across six key areas, including within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and inconsistency [23, 24].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

A traditional pairwise meta-analysis was conducted utilizing the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model. The weighted mean difference (MD) in changes in pain intensity between trial groups, as well as the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for adverse events, were calculated. For studies reporting zero events in both groups, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied [25]. Statistical heterogeneity among the pooled data was evaluated using the I2 statistic.

The MVMETA command in STATA 13 software was used to perform multivariate network meta-analyses [26] in a frequentist framework and to generate network diagrams. The node-splitting method was used to assess inconsistency, with a P-value > 0.05 reflecting satisfactory consistency, and the consistency model was used for the analysis. The heterogeneity of network models was evaluated using τ2 [27]. For each outcome measure, both the magnitude and uncertainty of the intervention effects were assessed using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), with higher SUCRA values indicating a lower placebo effect.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Among the retrieved 45,778 studies, 125 trials were included in the full-text assessment, of which 45 trials [28–72] (involving 7 sham acupuncture methods and 8287 patients) met the criteria for inclusion in the meta-analysis. The selected trials were conducted across 10 countries and regions, with sample sizes varying from 17 to 1,160 participants. The duration of interventions ranged from 4 to 13 weeks, and follow-up intervals varied from 4 to 52 weeks. The flow chart is presented in Appendix 2, while detailed baseline characteristics are listed in Table 1 and Appendix 3. The 7 sham acupuncture methods comprised sham laser acupuncture (SLA), nonacupoint and shallow needling (NSN), nonacupoint and deep needling (NDN), nonacupoint and no penetration (NNP), acupoint and no penetration (ANP), acupoint and shallow needling (ASN), and sham electroacupuncture stimulator (SES). To objectively evaluate the placebo effect of sham acupuncture, data from the waiting list (WL) group were also included in the analysis, the definitions of different sham acupuncture are shown in Appendix 4. The type of pain involved chronic widespread pain (fibromyalgia [28, 39, 61, 62]), chronic primary musculoskeletal pain (low back pain [29, 31, 36, 53], epicondylitis [35], rotator cuff tendinitis [42], shoulder pain [44, 54], knee osteoarthritis [30, 43, 46, 52, 57, 58, 60, 67], chronic knee pain [37] mechanical neck pain [66], rheumatoid arthritis [69], chronic neck pain [71], temporomandibular disorders [48]), chronic post-traumatic pain (chronic shoulder pain in individuals with spinal cord injury [33]), chronic primary visceral pain (irritable bowel syndrome [49, 55, 70]), chronic primary headache pain (migraine [32, 47, 64, 68], tension-type headache [40, 41, 51, 65, 72], chronic headache [34, 50]), chronic neuropathic pain (discogenic sciatica [38]), and chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain [45, 56, 59, 63].

Risk of bias, certainty of evidence, and consistency

The RoB-2 evaluation suggested that 26 RCTs were at a low risk of bias in the overall assessment. The remaining 19 studies were rated as some concern given that they did not clearly describe blinding of patients [34] or outcome assessors [29, 31, 33–35, 37, 38, 40, 41, 44, 45, 49, 52–54, 61, 65, 67, 69] (Appendix 5). All 45 studies had a low risk of random-sequence generation bias. Regarding consistency, specifically the consistency between direct and indirect evidence, the findings from the node-splitting method revealed no significant inconsistencies at the local and global levels (Appendix 6). The results of most pairwise comparisons were rated as low or very low confidence after assessing the level of evidence using CINeMA (Appendix 11). Additionally, the funnel plot was asymmetric, suggesting the potential presence of publication bias or the influence of small sample sizes in the research network, which was also confirmed by the results of Egger's test (Appendix 12).

Pairwise meta-analysis

The results of the pairwise meta-analysis and heterogeneity estimates are summarized in Appendix 7. In summary, among all outcome indicators, statistically significant differences were observed between the WL, NNP, ANP, ASN and MA groups. At the end of the treatment period, the ANP group exhibited a lower placebo effect (MD −1.22 (95% CI −1.79 to −0.64, I2 = 96%), excluding the WL group. Similarly, a lower placebo effect within three months of follow-up was noted in the NSN group (MD −0.91 (95% CI −1.30 to −0.52, I2 = 87%), whereas a lower placebo effect after three months of follow-up was identified in the NNP group (MD −1.16 (95% CI −1.67 to −0.65, I2 = 0%). Lastly, the incidence of adverse events was lower in the NSN group compared to the MA group (OR −1.24 (95% CI −1.55 to −1.00, I2 = 18%).

Network meta-analysis

pain intensity

Concerning improvements in pain intensity at the end of treatment, the network meta-analysis incorporated 45 studies [28–72] involving a total of 8287 participants and 7 sham acupuncture methods (Fig. 1). Significant differences in effectiveness in alleviating pain were detected between the WL, ANP, NNP, and NSN groups with the MA group (Table 2, Fig. 2 and Table S9.1). Among these, the WL group (MD 1.86 (95% CI 1.36 to 2.35), SUCRA 97.4%; moderate confidence of evidence) exhibited the most significant difference from MA group, ANP group (MD 1.14 (95% CI 0.74 to 1.55), SUCRA 73.6%; low confidence of evidence), and NNP group (MD 1.00 (95% CI 0.35 to 1.65), SUCRA 64.6%; low confidence of evidence). Overall heterogeneity in the analysis was significant (I2 = 89.7%, τ2 = 0.4364).

Forest plot of pain at the end of treatment

For improvement in pain intensity within 3 months of follow-up, the network meta-analysis included 24 studies [31, 33, 36, 38–41, 43, 46, 48, 49, 52–56, 59, 60, 64–66, 69, 71, 72] involving 3904 participants and 5 sham acupuncture methods (Fig S7.1). Significant differences in effectiveness in alleviating pain were detected between the WL, ANP and NSN groups with the MA group (Fig S10.1, Table S9.2 and Table S10.1). Among these, the WL group (MD 1.47 (95% CI 0.43 to 2.51), SUCRA 87.4%; moderate confidence of evidence) exhibited the most significant difference from MA group, NSN group (MD 0.97 (95% CI 0.55 to 1.38), SUCRA 71.1%; low confidence of evidence), and ANP group (MD 0.78 (95% CI 0.30 to 1.26), SUCRA 53.5%; low confidence of evidence). Overall heterogeneity in the analysis was moderate (I2 = 85.9%, τ2 = 0.3397).

In terms of improvement in pain intensity after 3 months of follow-up, the network meta-analysis included 24 studies [29–32, 35–38, 40, 45–47, 51, 56, 57, 59, 60, 62–64, 66, 67, 71, 72] involving 5782 participants and 7 sham acupuncture methods (Fig S8.2). Significant differences in effectiveness in alleviating pain were detected between the WL, ANP, and NNP groups with the MA group (Table S9.3, Fig S10.2 and Table S10.2). As anticipated, the most significant difference was observed between WL (MD 1.25 (95% CI 0.57 to 1.94), SUCRA 78.5%; very low confidence of evidence) and MA groups, followed by the ANP groups (MD 1.14 (95% CI 0.51 to 1.77), SUCRA 73.0%; low confidence of evidence) and NNP (MD 1.14 (95% CI 0.31 to 1.97), SUCRA 71.3%; low confidence of evidence). The analysis showed overall moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 81.4%, τ2 = 0.2196).

Adverse events

Concerning adverse events, the network meta-analysis included 22 studies [29, 31, 32, 34, 36, 43, 47, 48, 51, 54–57, 59, 60, 63, 66–68, 70–72], covering 5 sham acupuncture methods and involving 6629 participants (Fig. 3). Compared to the MA group, both ANP and WL groups (Fig. 4 and Table 3) demonstrated distinct differences in adverse events. The most pronounced difference was identified between the ANP group (OR 0.13 (95% CI 0.05 to 0.39), SUCRA 88.4%; moderate confidence of evidence) and MA group, followed by WL group (OR 0.18 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.38), SUCRA 83.1%; moderate confidence of evidence). However, differences between these interventions were not statistically significant (Table 3, Fig S9.4 and Table S9.4). The analysis showed overall low heterogeneity (I2 = 1.8%, τ2 = 0.0035).

Patient blinding at end of study

Fifteen studies investigated patient blinding to acupuncture, involving four sham acupuncture techniques: NSN [29, 32, 36, 51, 57, 60, 64], ANP [30, 38, 49, 52, 56], NNP [55, 62] and SES [66]. In these studies, patients were instructed to identify whether they received Chinese acupuncture or another type of acupuncture, and the effectiveness of the blinding procedure was assessed by comparing between-group responses for statistical differences. All studies demonstrated successful implementation of the blinding procedure.

Sensitivity analyses

To further assess the robustness of the results, GeMTC 0.14.3 software based on the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) consistent model under the Bayesian framework was used to conduct sensitivity analyses comparing various sham acupuncture methods with MA. Four chains were utilized for simulation, with the total number of iterations set to 50,000 (the first 20,000 iterations for annealing, followed by 30,000 iterations for sampling). Furthermore, based on differences in the pathological nature of pain, we observed changes in effect sizes of primary outcomes after excluding studies focusing on chronic primary visceral pain, chronic primary headache, chronic neuropathic pain, and chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome, respectively. As demonstrated in Appendix 13, the consistency between the sensitivity analysis findings and the initial analysis findings indicates the robustness of the results.

Subgroup analyses

Next, a subgroup analysis was carried out to compare no penetration sham acupuncture with MA, given that the former exerted the lowest placebo effect. The analysis focused on the effects of different treatment durations, frequency of acupuncture sessions, and types of chronic pain on the outcomes. Moreover, a subgroup analysis was performed to examine the effect of placebo devices in no-penetration sham acupuncture.

The results indicated that, compared to MA, a treatment period of 6 to 8 weeks with over 18 acupuncture sessions had the lowest placebo effect. Interestingly, the placebo device did not significantly influence the outcomes. Nonetheless, it is worthwhile acknowledging that the substantial variability in the number of studies addressing various types of chronic pain has precluded the identification of a clear pattern (Fig S14.1 and Fig S14.2). Furthermore, considering that NSN was the most frequently applied sham acupuncture in the clinical setting, a subgroup analysis was performed on this method. In contrast to previous findings, a treatment duration of below 6 weeks resulted in the lowest placebo effect, whereas a treatment duration exceeding 8 weeks was associated with the highest placebo effect (Fig S14.3).

Discussion

Main finding

This meta-analysis, conducted through indirect comparisons, comprehensively evaluated the placebo effects of seven types of sham acupuncture techniques and MA. Besides, it assessed the placebo effect of sham acupuncture by comparing it with the WL. The results from both direct and indirect comparisons indicated that all sham acupuncture treatments exhibited a significant placebo effect, with moderate confidence in the evidence. After long-term treatment (> 3 months), the placebo effect of no penetration, with acupoints or non-acupoints, was found to be the smallest. In the short term (≤ 3 months), the placebo effect of NSN was the smallest, with low confidence in the evidence. From a safety perspective, the incidence of adverse events associated with these three interventions was second only to that of the waiting list treatment, with no statistically significant differences observed. Additionally, these methods reliably maintained patient blinding.

The placebo effect of acupuncture has historically demonstrated greater efficacy compared to [73, 74] other placebo interventions due to factors such as increased attention and physical contact. By focusing on representative diseases, the findings of previous studies that described that chronic pain is a predominant condition for which acupuncture is widely utilized were validated and extended. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to objectively and comprehensively compare the placebo effects of various sham acupuncture methods on pain alleviation. The results collectively indicated that various sham acupuncture yield different placebo effects. Of note, the placebo effect associated with non-penetrating stimulation was lower and unrelated to acupoints (whether true or sham acupoints). Instead, it was associated with the extent of stimulation, including the duration of treatment and the frequency of acupuncture sessions.

As is well documented, expectation plays a crucial role in the placebo effect of acupuncture. Given that the treatment rituals are more elaborate and impressive, patients tend to have higher expectations regarding the efficacy of acupuncture [74]. Previous studies have identified a strong correlation between patients'expectations of acupuncture treatment and their prognosis [75, 76]. However, it is worth emphasizing that expectancy primarily leads to greater reductions in pain intensity during the initial stages of treatment, with its effects diminishing with increasing numbers of sessions [77]. This study unveiled that exceeding 18 acupuncture sessions reduced the placebo effect, regardless of whether the intervention consisted of non-penetrating or shallow puncture techniques. The situation is more intricate for NSN, where, in addition to the influence of patient expectation, there the irritative effect resulting from skin damage may also play a decisive role. Research has demonstrated that the physiological response to skin damage is related to a decrease in the affective component of pain [78]. This additive effect could have significantly contributed to the positive correlation between the placebo effect and treatment duration. Regarding adverse events, both direct comparisons and subgroup analyses indicated no increase in the frequency of adverse events associated with MA. Furthermore, the safety profile of acupuncture has been widely recognized [79–81]. Therefore, the involved sham acupuncture methods can also be considered safe.

Studies have generally focused on developing methods to optimize patient blinding, leading to the design of various sham acupuncture devices, including the Streitberger [4], Park [5], and Takakura [6] devices. This meta-analysis included studies using the Park device, as well as the Streitberger device and guide tube, which are commonly utilized in clinical practice as sham needle devices. However, the findings indicated that these devices did not significantly influence the placebo effect. This outcome highlights the feasibility of using more economical and convenient sham acupuncture devices in the clinical setting. Noteworthily, regarding the evaluation of blinding, only a limited number of trials in this study reported data on blinding for non-penetrating acupuncture. Nevertheless, a previous head-to-head RCT demonstrated that the blinding efficacy between various non-penetrating acupuncture devices and actual acupuncture is reliable [82].

Implication for practice and research

It is generally recognized that stimulation avoiding acupoints produces the lowest placebo effect. However, this study uncovered that stimulation at the ANP exhibited a higher placebo effect, with only a minimal difference compared to NNP. This finding holds significant practical implications, given that some patients are familiar with the precise locations of acupoints, and using ANP can enhance the effectiveness of blinding. NSN are the most commonly used forms of sham acupuncture in clinical practice. The study signaled that specific conditions must be met to achieve optimal placebo effects. Therefore, reasonable application of these methods is essential. Additionally, our study included only one head-to-head trial comparing sham acupuncture, with most results derived from indirect comparisons. Consequently, further head-to-head trials between sham acupuncture methods are warranted to obtain more robust evidence.

Heterogeneity and Publication Bias

In the meta-analysis of direct and indirect comparisons, moderate to high heterogeneity was observed in some outcomes. Through analysis of the original data, we found that partial studies did not clearly describe blinding of patients or outcome assessors, which may have contributed to methodological heterogeneity. Additionally, differences in population characteristics, pain types, and acupuncture intervention details may have led to clinical heterogeneity. Although we conducted subgroup analyses for specific intervention factors (e.g., treatment duration, total acupuncture sessions) and pain types, limitations in the original studies prevented us from addressing all sources of heterogeneity. Given the unavoidable heterogeneity, we employed a random-effects model for analysis. To minimize clinical heterogeneity, we standardized the pain intensity quantification by converting all measurements to the 10-cm VAS.

Limitations

Nevertheless, some limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. To begin, due to the dose–effect relationship of acupuncture, studies with treatment durations shorter than 4 weeks were excluded, which may have potentially introduced publication bias. Secondly, the majority of the evidence presented in this study was derived from indirect comparisons, highlighting the need for conducting additional head-to-head trials to obtain direct evidence. Thirdly, while this study included 7 sham acupuncture methods, some methods were represented by a limited number of studies, potentially compromising the results. Fourthly, significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies. While subgroup analyses based on treatment courses, total number of acupuncture sessions, types of pain, and sham acupuncture devices were conducted to identify the sources of heterogeneity, none of these factors were found to have a significant impact.

Conclusion

Based on current evidence, non-penetrating sham acupuncture (ANP and NNP) showed the lowest placebo effect, and the magnitude of this effect correlates with treatment duration and the total number of acupuncture sessions. Future direct comparative studies are required to provide more conclusive evidence.
