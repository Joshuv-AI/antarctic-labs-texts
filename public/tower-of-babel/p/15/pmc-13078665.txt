Quantifying and Understanding Healthcare Service Access for Geographically Distributed Communities: The Application of Address‐Level Intelligence
Authors: Vincent L Versace, Neil T Coffee, Anna Wong Shee, Marcus Blake, Sam Quinsey, Sarah Wood, Robert Tanton, Ralf‐Dieter Schroers, Kevin Mc Namara, Matthew Hadfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe population of Australia is unevenly distributed across a vast area. Australians living in rural areas face the ongoing challenge of accessing healthcare services when and where they need them. The application of address‐level intelligence is presented as a viable addition to existing methods that will enhance resource allocation and service planning.ContextCurrent methods for understanding where healthcare need is greatest and guide investment are constrained by the application of existing spatial administrative units. Administrative units tend to be larger in rural settings, masking locally important areas of heterogeneity—i.e., only the ‘average’ condition within a boundary is reported and therefore considered.ApproachThe widespread adoption of address‐level intelligence describing the services available to a community based upon the time it takes to travel to services is offered as a scalable alternative to predefined spatial administrative units. Applied examples of this change are already in practice. These include a system‐level adoption at a large rural health service that spans approximately 300 km and services 250 000 residents, through to national analyses that have contributed to key policy areas in both health and education.ConclusionImprovements in computational power and reduced processing time, coupled with increasingly granular datasets, have seen the adoption of address‐level intelligence gathering momentum. A shift to (1) access measures that account for the service availability in communities rather than relying only on population size and (2) a focus on time to services rather than solely relying on distance are recommended.Keywords: ecological fallacy, health geography, modifiable areal unit problem, rural health, service access, spatial access

Background

Australia is a vast landmass with uneven population distribution and equitable access to healthcare services where and when people need them persists as a longstanding challenge [1, 2]. Access to services is not a one‐dimensional concept and comprises spatial and aspatial factors. Spatial factors influence availability and accessibility (i.e., they are geographic in nature), whereas aspatial factors relate to non‐geographic factors such as affordability, timeliness, accommodation, acceptability, and awareness [2]. The allocation of investment in the publicly funded‐health sector is guided, through necessity, by existing geographically explicit administrative units.

Spatial information (data that includes a spatial reference, such as longitude and latitude) in Australia is typically aggregated to existing administrative units as defined under the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS) [3]. The ASGS classification includes the Main Structure (developed by the ABS and used to describe a broad range of statistics at varying scales), Non‐ABS Structures (geographies not developed by the ABS such as Local Government Areas (LGAs) and Postal Areas), and the Remoteness Structure (categories of remoteness based on their relative access to the five classes of population centres) [3]. Although the application of existing administrative units is useful and may be convenient in some cases (e.g., consistent reporting of aggregated spatial statistics), relying solely on these units falls short of understanding the variability within geographic units given the potential of geo‐computational power that permits much faster processing coupled with the proliferation of available datasets (e.g., the Geocoded National Address File, G‐NAF) [4].

The major drawback of predefined administrative units and aggregated summaries is that they mask potentially important local spatial patterning (i.e., they report the ‘average’ condition within the boundary). In the example presented (Figure 1), access to a general practice is shown to vary depending on how access is defined. Applying an area‐level measure using pre‐existing Statistical Area Level 2 (ASGS‐SA2) units reveals differences between units (ranging 0–10 min up to > 181 min) but shows homogeneity within the units. Once the addresses within the SA2 are overlaid it becomes apparent that access to general practices is far from uniform and consumers within a SA2 unit have very different travel times to services. This dynamic can be exacerbated in rural settings as administrative units tend to get larger as the population becomes sparse and less uniform in density [5]. In all settings, key limitations to applying area‐based measures are the spatial biases that arise as concepts such as the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (where the spatial extent chosen influences the outcome—if you change the unit, you derive aggregations that can change your answer) and ecological fallacy (misleading inferences are made between spatial scales) influence analyses [6]. The onus is then on the user to select the appropriate spatial unit to best match the purpose for the chosen application. But what if there is not an ‘ideal’ spatial unit? Are we bound then by the ‘least worst’ option? The overarching question remains: what is the likelihood that any predefined spatial unit is fit for its intended purpose?

In this Commentary we posit that the adoption of address‐level intelligence in Australia offers a scalable alternative to predefined spatial administrative units for quantifying and understanding healthcare service access [7]. Address‐level approaches have been implemented in Nordic countries, where national registers enable detailed examination of access to health services [8, 9]. To date, applications in Australia have predominantly been localised, limited to specific health service delivery catchments or jurisdictions [2, 10]. Our position is supported by examples from a Local Health Network (based in Victoria, Australia) that has implemented system‐level adoption, through to policy‐relevant health and education applications at national scale.

National Level

The Modified Monash Model (MMM) is used by the Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing to classify areas based upon geographical remoteness and town size (i.e., it is a proxy for access as it is used to guide health workforce investment in rural and remote areas) [11]. Underpinned by the ASGS Remoteness Areas (ASGS‐RA, of which there are five categories), it offers a marginally more nuanced classification with seven categories that define locations across Australia as being metropolitan (MM1), regional (MM 2), rural (MM 3–5), remote (MM 6), or very remote (MM 7) [12]. The MMM has been adopted broadly with a key application being guiding investment in the medical workforce (e.g., as a lever for workforce Distribution Priority Areas, DPAs) [13]. Beyond this, the MMM is used to define the dichotomy between metropolitan areas (MM1) and rural locations (MM2‐7) across several Australian Government programmes. Examples include the Rural Health Multidisciplinary Training Program [12], and more recently to guide funding through selected schemes offered by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) and the Medical Research Future Fund (MRFF) to ensure representation of rural researchers [14].

Moving from high level policy levers to the delivery of medical services, the Australian Government has established Primary Health Networks (PHNs) [15]. Nationwide, there are 31 PHNs and it is commonplace for a PHN catchment area to contain multiple MMM categories. With a remit to assess the needs of their communities and commission health services in their region, part of their planning is contingent upon the preparation of a Needs Analysis for which formal guidance is provided [16]. This advice includes applying the ASGS Statistical Area Level 3 (ASGS‐SA3) for sub‐regions, with specific reference to LGAs for health needs analysis, and also tasks PHNs to consider how boundaries affect their region's health system capacity and performance (e.g., cross‐border utilisation, the influence of central services, and specialists' locations) [16]. The result is spatial units that are too coarse to account for local heterogeneity and therefore likely fall short when assessing need, acknowledging that there are precedents where PHNs have moved beyond LGAs (e.g., looking at socio‐economic position at the ASGS Statistical Area Level 1 (ASGS‐SA1) within LGAs) [17]. Although this offers extra insight as SA1s are designed to maximise the availability of geographic detail, the approach is still constrained to interpreting average data within an existing spatial unit.

State Level

Victoria is Australia's second most populous state (~7 million residents, with approximately 950 000 residents living outside MM1) [12, 18]. In April 2024 the Victoria Government Department of Health launched the Health Services Plan (the Plan). One of the pillars of the Plan was the establishment of Local Health Networks (LHNs) – geographically defined administrative units with complete coverage of Victoria [19]. The LHNs are responsible for both the planning and management of care to meet the population needs of their communities. Across Victoria there are 12 LHNs, with six classed as metropolitan and six classed as regional. Service populations range from 106 000 residents (South West) to 1.2 million residents (South Metro) [20].

The use of large administrative units for the planning and management of health services within states is not uniquely Victorian, nor is Victoria the first Australian jurisdiction to adopt this approach. New South Wales, Australia's most populous state (~8.5 million residents, with approximately 1.9 million residents living outside MM1) [12, 18], first established the Local Health Networks in 2011. Now named Local Health Districts (LHDs), there are six covering the Sydney Metropolitan region and the remaining nine covering rural and regional NSW [21]. Although there is a stated dichotomy of metropolitan and rural and regional LHDs, in practice some metropolitan LHDs include rural areas. For example, the South Western Sydney LHD includes Bowral (~10 000 residents) and Mittagong (~6000 residents), and the Nepean Blue Mountains LHD includes Lithgow (~20 000 residents). Applying the Modified Monash Model classification, Bowral and Mittagong are Large Rural Towns (MM3), and Lithgow is a Medium Rural Town (MM4) [22]. Further complicating questions around access to services is that many large and medium rural towns are surrounded by Small Rural Towns (MM5). This Modified Monash category (MM5) doesn't have defined town boundaries, but rather covers areas between larger towns (MM3, MM4). It is the main interface with Remote communities (MM6), and in some areas shares a boundary with Metropolitan areas (MM1) as well as Very Remote communities (MM7) [22].

The common issue with administrative units is that they can be geographically expansive and therefore homogenise the circumstances and needs of the populations they represent (Table 1). Ideally, any approach to spatial data should be scalable to enable valid comparisons both between and within areas whilst also maintaining the flexibility to aggregate statistics if required.

Grampians Health: The Organisational‐Level Move to Address‐Level Intelligence in a Local Health Network

Established in late 2021, Grampians Health was the first LHN in Victoria [23]. In recognition of the need to better understand geographic access across their newly defined catchment in a range of settings (MM 2–6), a multi‐year strategic investment was made into the establishment of the Centre for Australian Research into Access (CARA) – a partnership with Deakin Rural Health (a University Department of Rural Health). From east to west, there is a 280 km journey by road between the campus in Ballarat to the Edenhope campus near the South Australian border. In addition to better plan and operationalise healthcare service delivery in the region, there was also a shared vision to contribute to policy discussions at the state and national level. Underpinning these efforts was the acknowledgment that previous efforts to describe access have typically been constrained to existing spatial administrative units (e.g., LGAs and postcodes). Strategically the decision was taken by Grampians Health at the organisational level to move towards address‐level intelligence.

At Grampians Health, address‐level intelligence has seen rapid uptake. Emergency Department admissions data (> 750 000 admissions between 2009 and 2022) have been geo‐enriched with information describing distance and time travelled for each admission. In addition, intelligence about availability of primary care locations along the shortest route for non‐urgent presentations has been added to each unit record (i.e., insight into what potential opportunities for early intervention were available to the patient). This has provided insights about where best to invest in primary care within the City of Ballarat, as well as planning for outreach services (e.g., specialty obstetrics clinics in nearby towns to offset emergency presentations). Applications also include a review of existing services (e.g., understanding the geographical distribution of patients requiring palliative care to inform a review of existing service provision) as well as preparation of tenders where Grampians Health can demonstrate a clear comparative advantage to the funding body (i.e., they have a superior understanding of both the geography and demographics of their communities).

The system‐level uptake of address‐level intelligence has been aided by numerous measures. Engagement with the health service executive, primarily the Chief Executive Officer and Chief Medical Officer, led to the original investment and in so doing has signalled strategic intent for the organisation. Further engagement was undertaken with the local IT department to ensure necessary electronic infrastructure was made available, as well as assistance with integration into existing systems. To aid this, a data scientist was employed by Grampians Health to ensure adequate expertise and capacity. In addition to improving Grampians Health's operations, all specific research activities comply with local area ethics and datasets for analysis are geo‐enriched and de‐identified behind the health service firewall to completely protect sensitive data. The methods used to achieve this level of anonymity, yet deliver unprecedented spatial granularity, have been tailored to dovetail with the existing IT infrastructure.

Scalability and Further Policy Applications of Address‐Level Intelligence

At the national scale, CARA has contributed to the Working Better for Medicare Review through a review of the MMM and its influence on DPAs by assessing changing access to GPs services at the address‐level from 2015 to 2023 [24]. This application from the Medicare Review used road distance to nearest general practice, and although correlated, doesn't inform time to nearest practice, nor the type of GP service. Methodological progress has since been made and time metrics can now be applied (Figure 2). Regarding the type of GP service, bulk billing rates (i.e., consultations where the Medicare is billed directly) have declined in Australia—this is not geographically uniform [25]. By accessing national digital health infrastructure, the application of address‐level intelligence permits visualisation of all general practices in Australia compared to those that offer bulk billing only, noting there are practices that offer a combination of services (Figure 3). This type of insight, coupled with the interactive ability to aggregate statistics at varying scales, provides a starting point to develop better policy and service provision at scale [26]. This can be further enhanced through the application of innovations such as a synthetic population to provide socio‐economic data on households and individuals, coupled with Medicare usage data—noting the latter is typically aggregated at much larger spatial units such as ASGS‐SA3 [27, 28, 29].

CARA's work has not been limited to health. Interactive maps of distance from G‐NAF points to primary, secondary, and tertiary education locations were produced for the Regional Education Commissioner's 2023 Annual Report [30]. This work responded directly to the National Regional, Rural and Remote Tertiary Education Strategy (Recommendation Seven) which called for developing an improved geographical classification tool for tertiary education purposes [31]. Understanding inequities for rural Australian's accessing tertiary education is a key consideration in addressing the undersupply of health professionals [32]. Further work around tertiary education access responded to the Australian Universities Accord that recommended a range of higher education initiatives to address student inequities, including the expansion of higher education infrastructure in rural areas, most notably through the Regional University Study Hub (RUSH) program. An interactive application was developed comparing access for all communities to their nearest university campus or RUSH [33]. The utility of this output is to allow policymakers to quantify the impact of location both for existing infrastructure and strategic investment planning—lessons that can be directly applied to health.

To achieve the widespread application of address‐level granularity for policy translation at scale requires adoption at multiple government levels and agencies. In mid‐2025 CARA signed a Research Framework Agreement with the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) [34]. This agreement includes enhancements to the ABS Address Register in the form of a national dwelling‐level sampling frame, a review of the ASGS‐RA [3], and the provision of access measures for key data products such as the Service Accessibility Explorer as part of the Life Course Data Initiative [35, 36].

National health services modelling requires the best available data. CARA is an integration partner with Healthdirect Australia—a government‐funded body (support from Commonwealth and all states and territories) responsible for providing health information, advice and referral for all Australians [37]. As well as obvious practical benefits such as integration of systems and enhanced interoperability, these relationships are also important as they lend credibility to CARA's ability to manage potentially sensitive data in secure environments. This paves the way for increased uptake from departments and organisations yet to adopt address‐level approaches.

Beyond these examples, there is potential for address‐level approaches to enhance our understanding of aspatial challenges wherever geocoded datasets exist with relevant aspatial data. For example, administrative datasets identifying service or product costs at different locations may identify areas with differing price signals (i.e., affordability) and thin markets linked to workforce maldistribution (e.g., the National Disability Insurance Scheme) [38]. Address‐level data can also be used to reveal landscape‐scale patterns, which can guide purposeful sampling for qualitative research to explore variations in health and healthcare quality to better elucidate the processes that underpin spatial patterns.

Conclusion

The adoption of address‐level intelligence is an inevitable paradigm shift in the formulation of health policy and delivery of healthcare services in Australia—improved geo‐computational power, increasingly granular datasets, and embedded data scientists are contributing to progress. This shift is underway as demonstrated by applications within a LHN through to policy‐relevant work at national scale in both health and education. The use of addresses as the base unit of geography enables flexibility for producing spatial aggregate access statistics as required (see Figure 3). This has the potential to both facilitate the transition and allow the ongoing use of administrative units in situations where it is justified as a requirement (e.g., a comparison between two time points). Further unlocking the potential of address‐level intelligence includes the development of a dwelling‐level sampling frame in partnership with the ABS and the overlay of a national‐scale synthetic population, allowing dwelling level socio‐demographic indicators (e.g., age, sex, employment, income and health conditions) to be considered when determining policy settings and service delivery. A shift in focus to time‐based metrics (e.g., 20 min to a GP) from distance‐based metrics (e.g., 10 km to a GP) and a focus to better understanding available services to communities [39] rather than relying upon population size will also increase efficiency of resource allocation.
