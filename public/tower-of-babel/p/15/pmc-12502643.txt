High FGFR4 protein expression, but not FGFR1 or FGFR2, predicts poor prognosis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Marcin Braun, Justyna Durślewicz, Julia Sołek, Zuzanna Nowicka, Aleksandra Zielińska, Paulina Antosik, Dariusz Grzanka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of prognostic and predictive factors for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is limited. Fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs) are emerging as potential therapeutic targets, especially in cases with FGFR2 gene fusions. However, the prognostic relevance of FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4 protein expression in PDAC remains unclear.MethodsImmunohistochemical analysis of FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4 was performed on 99 PDAC and 60 adjacent normal pancreatic tissue samples. Protein expression was quantified using the H-score method and correlated with clinicopathological variables and survival. Publicly available datasets from the GEO repository and the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) were used for pathway enrichment analysis and validation of findings at the mRNA level.ResultsFGFR2 and FGFR4 showed differential expression between tumor and normal tissues, while FGFR1 did not. High FGFR4 protein expression was significantly associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS) in both univariable and multivariable analyses. FGFR2 high expression cases showed a trend towards poor DFS, while FGFR1 had no prognostic impact. In silico analysis confirmed that high FGFR4 mRNA levels are associated with worse DFS. Co-expression and enrichment analysis linked FGFR4 overexpression with developmental, metabolic, and stemness-related processes.ConclusionFGFR4 showed the strongest prognostic association among the FGFR family members studied, with high protein expression correlating with shorter disease-free survival in PDAC patients. These findings underscore the potential of FGFR4 as a biomarker for recurrence risk, while also highlighting the complexity of FGFR-related signaling and its context-dependent clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14976-2.Keywords: PDAC, Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, Fibroblast growth factor receptor, FGFR, Prognosis

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the most lethal malignancies, with a five-year survival rate of less than 10% despite advances in surgical techniques and systemic therapies [1]. The aggressive biology of PDAC, including early metastasis and profound chemoresistance, as well as a characteristically dense stromal microenvironment, highlights the ongoing challenge of identifying reliable prognostic biomarkers and actionable therapeutic targets [2]. The tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly the fibrotic stroma composed of pancreatic stellate cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts, plays a central role in promoting tumor progression, immune evasion, and resistance to therapy [2]. Among the key signalling pathways sustaining the TME, fibroblast growth factor (FGF)–FGF receptor (FGFR) signalling has emerged as a major driver of stromal–epithelial crosstalk and desmoplasia in PDAC [3, 4]. Current clinical trials (OSU-14078, OSU-19041, KIN-3248) investigating FGFR inhibitors in advanced solid tumors have demonstrated striking efficacy in selected PDAC patients harboring FGFR2 gene fusions, underscoring the need to better define the prognostic and predictive significance of FGFR signalling in this malignancy [5–9].

However, the prognostic role of FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4 in PDAC remains controversial. Some studies have associated high FGFR2 or FGFR1 expression with poor overall survival (OS), suggesting a potential link between FGFR-driven signalling and tumor aggressiveness. In contrast, other reports have demonstrated no clear prognostic value for these receptors, or have indicated that FGFR4 overexpression may be associated with more favorable clinical outcomes [10–13]. These inconsistencies may reflect methodological differences, limited cohort sizes, or the absence of molecular stratification in previous studies. Although much of the current evidence is based on retrospective analyses from single institutions, each study contributes to building a more comprehensive understanding of FGFR biology in PDAC. In this context, further investigations—particularly those integrating protein-level assessments with transcriptomic validation—remain essential to delineate the prognostic relevance of FGFR family members and their potential utility in clinical decision-making.

In this study, we aimed to systematically evaluate the prognostic relevance of FGFR family members in a well-characterized PDAC cohort. Protein expression of FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4 was assessed by immunohistochemistry in tumor tissues and adjacent normal pancreatic tissue, and its association with key clinicopathological variables was analyzed. To complement the tissue-based findings, in silico analyses using publicly available transcriptomic datasets were performed to validate the prognostic significance of FGFR expression in relation to disease-free survival (DFS) and OS. This dual approach was designed to strengthen the biological and clinical interpretation of FGFR expression in the context of PDAC progression.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue specimens

This retrospective study included patients who underwent surgical resection for histologically confirmed pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) at the Department of Liver and General Surgery, Collegium Medicum in Bydgoszcz, Nicolaus Copernicus University in Torun (Poland). All patients were treatment-naïve at the time of surgery. Histopathological evaluations were independently performed by two pathologists at the Department of Clinical Pathomorphology of the same institution. Cases with histology other than PDAC were excluded, and patients who died within 30 days after surgery were excluded from survival analyses. Representative formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from tumor areas and matched adjacent normal pancreatic tissue were selected for further analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Bioethics Committee at Collegium Medicum in Bydgoszcz, Nicolaus Copernicus University in Torun (approval no. 342/2020).

Tissue microarrays construction and immunohistochemical staining

Tissue microarrays (TMAs) construction and then immunohistochemical staining of slides with tissue cores were performed as previously described [14, 15]. For the majority of cases, four 2.0 mm cores were sampled from different tumour areas and adjacent normal pancreatic tissues. Section 4-µm thick were prepared from selected paraffin blocks. Immunohistochemical (IHC) reactions were performed using the BenchMark® ULTRA system (Roche Diagnostics/Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-FGFR1 (clone NB100-2080, Novus Biologicals, Colorado, USA; pH 6.0; dilution 1:100), anti-FGFR2 (clone H00002263-M01, Abnova, Taipei City, Taiwan; pH 6.0; dilution 1:600), and anti-FGFR4 (clone M00769-2, Boster Bio, CA, USA; pH 6.0; dilution 1:500). Detection was carried out using the ultraView Universal DAB Detection Kit (Roche Diagnostics/Ventana), following the manufacturer’s protocol and a pre-validated staining procedure. Positive and negative controls were included for each antibody to ensure staining specificity and reliability.

Evaluation of immunohistochemical reaction

Slides were assessed in a blinded fashion using a Zeiss AxioScope 5 brightfield microscope by two observers (AZ, JD), and subsequently reviewed and photographed in digitized form by experienced pathologists (MB, DG) using the PANNORAMIC 1000 FLASH DX scanner along with the SlideCenter and SlideViewer systems (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). Scoring was performed at 100× magnification across the entire slide, with ten representative high-power fields (HPFs) re-evaluated in detail for consistency by the primary observer and independently verified by a second evaluator.

FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4 protein expression levels were semi-quantitatively evaluated using the H-score method. The H-score was calculated by multiplying the percentage of positively stained tumor cells by the corresponding staining intensity, classified as negative (0), weak (1+), moderate (2+), or strong (3+). The resulting values were summed to obtain a cumulative score ranging from 0 to 300. In non-cancerous pancreatic tissue, FGFRs’ expression was assessed exclusively in ductal epithelial cells. Staining was evaluated separately for cytoplasmic, membranous, and, when present, nuclear compartments, although only cytoplasmic and membranous localization was consistently observed and included in the H-score calculation. H-scores were then analyzed for associations with clinicopathological characteristics and patient survival. For statistical analyses, H-scores were categorized as low or high using the upper tertile cut-off for FGFR4, and fixed thresholds of 120 for FGFR2 and 110 for FGFR1, the latter due to its skewed distribution (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In Silico analysis

To assess the prognostic significance of selected genes in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), we utilized the Kaplan–Meier Plotter online tool (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). This platform integrates gene expression and clinical outcome data from publicly available datasets, including GEO, TCGA, and EGA [16]. Specifically, data from 852 patients diagnosed with PDAC were analyzed. Additionally, gene expression and clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (TCGA PAAD) cohort were accessed via cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) [17]. Gene expression was retrieved as mRNA expression z-scores relative to diploid samples (RNA Seq V2 RSEM). Functional gene enrichment analysis was conducted using g: Profiler [18], whilst cell type deconvolution scores from xCell were used for TCGA PAAD cohort samples [19].

Statistical analysis

Continuous data was presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), whereas nominal data as numbers followed by percentages in brackets. After evaluation of distribution’s normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, continuous variables were compared by the Mann–Whitney U-test for two groups or the Kruskal–Wallis test (AKW; with Conover–Inman post-hoc test) for multiple groups, in case of non-normal distribution. In case of normal distribution, Student’s t-test or one-/two-way block ANOVA (with Tukey’s post-hoc test) were used. Differences between categorical variables were evaluated using the Pearson’s chi-squared test. The Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated for correlations. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction in case of multiple comparisons was applied. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as time from a date of curative treatment (usually surgery or end of primary therapy) to the date of first recurrence (local, regional, or distant) or death from any cause, and overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis (or start of treatment) to the date of death from any cause [20]. Both variables were presented using Kaplan–Meier curves, and compared using the log-rank test unless noted otherwise. A multivariable analysis of OS and DFS was performed using Cox’s proportional hazard regression models. K-means clustering analysis, along with heatmap generation, was performed using the ClustVis online tool [21]. The Statistica 13.1 package (Dell Inc., Round Rock, TX, USA) was used. p-values < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

Results

Cohort details

A total of 113 tumor samples were analyzed. Fourteen patients who died within 30 days after surgery were excluded from survival analyses, resulting in a final evaluable cohort of 99 patients. Adjacent normal pancreatic tissue was available for 60 cases (60.6%). Clinicopathological characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

Immunoexpression of FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR4 in PDAC and normal adjacent tissue

Immunohistochemical staining for FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4 was detected in sections of PDAC and adjacent normal pancreatic tissues (Fig. 1). No significant difference in FGFR1 expression was observed between tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 2A). In contrast, FGFR2 expression was significantly higher in PDAC compared to adjacent tissue (p < 0.01; Fig. 2B), while FGFR4 expression was markedly decreased in tumor samples (p < 0.0001; Fig. 2C).

The expression distribution across cancerous tissue was heterogeneous. FGFR2 showed the strongest and most uniform staining, with 38 of 99 tumors (38.4%) reaching the maximal H-score of 300. Using a cut-off of 120, 93 of 99 cases (93.9%) were classified as FGFR2high. FGFR1 displayed the lowest overall staining intensities; across the cohort, 18 of 99 tumors (18.2%) were classified as FGFR1high (cut-off: 110), and 32 of 99 (32.3%) as FGFR4high (cut-off: 170).

Five tumors (5.1%) were triple-negative, lacking expression of all three receptors, while 10 tumors (10.1%) were triple-positive. Co-expression of FGFR1 and FGFR2 was observed in 17 cases (17.2%), FGFR2 and FGFR4 in 32 cases (32.3%), and FGFR1 and FGFR4 in 10 cases (10.1%).

The main cellular compartment for FGFR1 was the cytoplasm, with membrane staining approximately 40% weaker on average. FGFR2 showed comparable cytoplasmic and membranous expression, while FGFR4 staining was predominantly membranous, with cytoplasmic signal approximately 25% weaker. Only scattered cells or small clusters of tumor cells demonstrated nuclear staining for FGFR1 and FGFR2, while such nuclear localization was virtually absent for FGFR4. Full case-level data, including expression intensity, subcellular localization, and clinical annotations, are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR4 expression and clinicopathological associations

No statistically significant associations were identified between FGFR1 or FGFR2 expression and key clinicopathological features including grade, stage, perineural and lymphovascular invasion (Supplementary Table 2). FGFR4 expression showed a weak positive correlation with patient age (R = 0.18, p = 0.059), with no other clinicopathological associations being observed. When analyzed as a binary variable, FGFR4high vs. FGFR4low tumors were more frequently classified as pT3–4 (p = 0.045) and TNM stage III–IV (p = 0.006) (Supplementary Table 3). Patients with available matched-pair normal tissue (60.6% of the cohort) had FGFR expression normalized to adjacent non-tumor tissue, presented both as tumour/normal H-score ratios and absolute differences (Supplementary Table 1). In this subgroup, FGFR4 H-scores were positively correlated with patient age (R = 0.30, p = 0.0499). FGFR2 expression was significantly higher in cases with perineural invasion (p = 0.0292 for ratio, p = 0.0095 for difference) compared to cases without perineural invasion, and it was also higher in low-grade tumours compared to high-grade tumours (p = 0.0444) (Supplementary Table 4).

In survival analysis, FGFR1 expression showed no association with DFS (hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for FGFR1high versus FGFR1low = 0.70 (0.44–1.26), p = 0.756; Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2), while FGFR2high patients had shorter DFS compared to the FGFR2low group, although the difference was not statistically significant (HR (95% CI) = 0.40 (0.14–1.10), p = 0.058; Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2). A FGFR4high status was significantly associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS) when compared to FGFR4low patients (HR (95% CI) = 0.35 (0.23–0.56), p < 0.001, Fig. 3). These findings were consistent when FGFR expression was analyzed as a continuous variable. For FGFR1 H-score, no association with DFS was observed (p = 0.996; HR [95% CI] = 1.00 [0.995–1.005], β = 0.00001). Similarly, FGFR2 H-score was not significantly associated with DFS (p = 0.191; HR [95% CI] = 1.00 [0.998–1.005], β = 0.0021). In contrast, FGFR4 H-score remained significantly associated with shorter DFS (p = 0.002; HR [95% CI] = 1.00 [1.002–1.032], β = 0.0066). The prognostic value of FGFR4 expression remained significant in multivariable analysis after adjustment for clinical variables (Table 2). This association also persisted after including patient age and sex in the model (p = 0.005; Table 2).

Unsupervised clustering based on H-scores for FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4, combined with survival data (deaths and relapses), identified a distinct subgroup of patients enriched for events in cases where tumors co-expressed FGFR2 and FGFR4 (Fig. 4A). To further assess the observed association between FGFR4 protein level and disease-free survival, we analyzed the impact of FGFR4 mRNA levels (microarray data) on overall survival (OS; n = 852) and DFS (n = 150) in patients with pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) using the Kaplan–Meier plotter tool [16]. Interestingly, high FGFR4 mRNA expression (>1 st tercile) was associated with improved OS (HR = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.59–0.84; p < 0.0001; Fig. 5A), but with worse DFS (HR = 2.00; 95% CI: 1.31–3.06; p = 0.0013; Fig. 4B).

In Silico analysis of FGFR4 co-expression and enriched pathways in PAAD

To explore potential molecular mechanisms underlying the association between FGFR4 expression and clinical outcomes, gene co-expression patterns were analyzed using TCGA PAAD transcriptomic data. Among genes showing a significant positive correlation with FGFR4 mRNA levels (FDR < 0.05), 131 genes exhibited a Spearman’s correlation coefficient greater than 0.5, gap junction beta-1 protein (GJB1), hepatocyte nuclear factor-1 beta (HNF-1β), and matrix metallopeptidase 15 (MMP15) among the top 10 co-expressed genes (R = 0.72, FDR < 0.001; R = 0.66, FDR < 0.001; and R = 0.65, FDR < 0.001, respectively), while 18 genes were negatively correlated with FGFR4 expression (Spearman R < − 0.5).

Genes positively co-expressed with FGFR4 were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using g: Profiler [18]. Enriched gene sets included terms related to metabolic processes (small molecule metabolic process, propionate biosynthetic process, GDP-L-fucose biosynthetic process) and developmental and differentiation pathways (endocrine pancreas development, epithelial cell differentiation, digestive system development, embryonic morphogenesis) (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Conversely, analysis of genes negatively correlated with FGFR4 expression revealed that the only significantly enriched biological process was the regulation of pinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

In addition to these associations, FGFR4 mRNA expression showed a weak positive correlation with the cancer stemness index in TCGA PAAD samples (R = 0.240, p = 0.003; Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting a potential link between FGFR4 activity and stemness-related tumor characteristics [22]. To further explore potential biological interactions underlying the association between FGFR4 expression and patient survival, immune and stromal cell type deconvolution data from the TCGA PAAD cohort were analyzed using xCell [19]. Among 64 cell types, the strongest positive correlations with FGFR4 expression were observed for hepatocyte (Spearman R = 0.62, p < 0.001) and effector memory CD4 + T cell (Spearman R = 0.43, p < 0.001) signatures (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, using a well-characterized single-center cohort with matched tumor and adjacent normal tissue, we found that FGFR2 is significantly upregulated and FGFR4 downregulated in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). High FGFR4 expression was associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS), and this relationship remained significant in multivariable analysis. In silico analysis further revealed a complex prognostic pattern, with high FGFR4 mRNA levels linked to longer overall survival (OS) but shorter DFS. Despite its single-center nature, this study offers relevant insight into the clinical and molecular landscape of FGF–FGFR signaling in PDAC and highlights its potential role in translational applications. In contrast to previous studies, which focused on transcriptomic data and experimental models, our work integrates protein-level assessment via immunohistochemistry in a large, clinically annotated patient cohort with transcriptomic profiling, offering a more comprehensive perspective on the prognostic role of FGFRs in PDAC [13].

An aberrant FGFR expression has been described in PDAC for over three decades [23–25]. In vitro studies showed that FGFR1 expression promotes cell migration, invasion and desmoplastic phenotype, which contribute to acquired therapy resistance [3, 26, 27]. Accordingly, in a mouse model, FGFR1 inhibitor treatment reduced tumor progression and significantly prolonged survival [26]. Interestingly, a study by Vickers et al. using xenograft formation in Balb/c mice showed that the effect of FGFR1 may be isoform-specific: FGFR1α overexpression suppressed cell growth and restored cytotoxic responses to chemotherapy, while FGFR1β overexpression promoted tumor growth and conferred chemotherapy resistance [28]. However, in a clinical study of 313 PDAC patients, FGFR1 overexpression was associated with lower tumor grade and better overall survival, and multivariable analysis confirmed FGFR1 as a favorable prognostic factor [29]. In our cohort, FGFR1 expression was predominantly cytoplasmic and relatively low in intensity, and it was not associated with clinicopathological features or disease-free survival.

FGFR2 overexpression in in vitro models has been linked to increased cell proliferation, larger tumor size in xenografts, and earlier onset of liver metastases [30]. In clinical samples, high FGFR2 levels have been associated with shorter overall survival [5, 30]. Furthermore, Stein et al. reported increased FGFR2 expression in cancer cells relative to normal pancreatic ducts, a finding confirmed in our cohort [5]. In our study, tumors showed very strong FGFR2 expression with both cytoplasmic and membranous localization. Subgroup analyses showed higher FGFR2 expression in cases with perineural invasion. In contrast, higher FGFR2 expression was observed in low-grade tumors, however this finding is consistent with our results in breast carcinoma [31]. Patients with FGFR2high PDAC had shorter DFS, with a p-value close to the threshold of significance.

Prior studies have provided conflicting insights into the role of FGFR4 in pancreatic tumorigenesis and progression [11]. FGFR4 activation has been shown to affect cell–extracellular matrix interactions, promoting adhesion to laminin and fibronectin via NCAM-mediated mechanisms [12, 32]. In addition, FGFR4, together with HNF1A, has been implicated in promoting PDAC invasion and metastasis, and its inhibition has shown promising anti-tumor effects in vitro [33, 34]. Our in silico analysis showed that high FGFR4 levels were correlated with genes involved in metabolic processes and developmental transcriptional programs (Supplementary Fig. 3), as well as with a weak positive correlation with the cancer stemness index (Supplementary Fig. 4). These features are also often associated with increased tumor aggressiveness. Motoda et al. reported that FGFR4 expression increases from low-grade to high-grade pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN), suggesting a role in early neoplastic progression. Interestingly, they also demonstrated that stimulation of FGFR4 by its most common ligand, FGF19, exerted a tumor-suppressive effect in PDAC models [12]. In invasive PDAC, FGFR4 expression was enriched in the classical subtype—typically associated with more favorable prognosis—whereas reduced expression was observed in more aggressive, basal-like tumors, a pattern also linked to epigenetic silencing and functional loss of FGFR4 in recent experimental models [13]. Moreover, Motoda et al. found that FGFR4 was expressed in 75.6% of PDAC cases and was associated with a trend toward improved overall survival [12]. These findings collectively support a context-dependent role for FGFR4, with loss of expression in more aggressive tumor phenotypes and enrichment in more differentiated subtypes. These patterns resemble also our previous findings in breast carcinoma, where low FGFRs expression—particularly FGFR2—was linked to more aggressive phenotypes, including the triple-negative subtype [31, 35]. Our findings add to this complex picture, suggesting that FGFR4 may play a dual, context-dependent role—supporting tumor progression in some settings and acting as a suppressor in others, depending on disease stage and subtype [11, 35].

In our cohort, high FGFR4 protein expression was associated with shorter DFS, suggesting a link to earlier recurrence. This adverse prognostic effect was confirmed across univariate and multivariate survival analyses, for both binary and continuous presentations of FGFR4 protein expression data, and was further supported in the subgroup analysis restricted to cases with concordantly high expression in both tumour tissue and relative to matched normal tissue. In contrast, in silico survival analysis based on mRNA expression showed that high FGFR4 levels were associated with longer OS, but also with shorter DFS. This apparent inconsistency underscores the complex and potentially stage- or context-dependent role of FGFR4 in PDAC biology. One possible explanation is that FGFR4 may promote tumor progression in early or classical phenotypes, increasing recurrence risk (reflected in shorter DFS), but its presence may also sensitize tumors to therapies administered after relapse, ultimately extending OS. Furthermore, DFS and OS reflect distinct clinical trajectories—DFS captures recurrence dynamics following treatment, while OS is influenced by a broader range of factors, including post-recurrence treatment, perioperative complications, comorbidities, and even site of distant metastases [36–40]. Variations in patient management after relapse may therefore dilute or obscure the impact of baseline gene expression on OS. Although mRNA and protein expression levels are often correlated, discrepancies due to post-transcriptional regulation or downstream signaling feedback may also contribute to divergent survival associations. Additionally, the dataset used for in silico analysis lacked detailed information on how DFS and OS were defined, as well as on reasons for missing survival data in some cases [16], which may further complicate interpretation. Moreover, there is currently no validated consensus on whether DFS can reliably serve as a surrogate for OS in pancreatic cancer, however most studies suggest that it may be a reasonable approximation in certain contexts [36–38]. A similar divergence between DFS and OS has been described for CA19-9 in PDAC, where associations with perioperative outcomes and post-treatment recurrence differed [41]. According to our results, this observation is most evident for FGFR4, but it may also help explain conflicting findings regarding FGFR1 and FGFR2 in earlier studies, which often focused on OS rather than DFS, as used in our analysis.

This study has several limitations. Reliable data on disease-specific overall survival (OS) were not available for most patients, precluding direct correlation between our protein-level findings and mRNA-based OS analyses. Furthermore, the lack of matched transcriptomic data for FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR4 in patient samples limited the ability to directly compare protein and mRNA expression. Another limitation is potential intratumoral heterogeneity—both spatial and temporal. Although no marked variability was noted within sampled regions in our study, regional differences cannot be excluded. Future studies with whole-slide or spatial analyses may address this issue more comprehensively. The single-center design and moderate cohort size also limit the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, FGFR4 emerged as the most prognostically informative member of the FGFR family in PDAC, with high protein expression associated with early recurrence. In silico analysis highlighted enriched biological programs linked to stemness, metabolism, and immune interaction. The divergent patterns observed between protein-level and mRNA DFS/OS data highlight the complexity of FGFR-related biology. Rather than assigning a fixed prognostic interpretation, our findings indicate that the clinical impact of FGFR expression may depend on disease stage, biological context, and the survival endpoint analyzed. These results provide a framework for more targeted use of FGFR markers in translational and clinical research.
