The hypothalamus is an early site of mitochondrial failure and neuro-immune circuit disruption in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Silvia Scaricamazza, Valentina Nesci, Gianmarco Fenili, Marta Tiberi, Anna Percio, Marco Rosina, Illari Salvatori, Flaminia Riggio, Niccolò Candelise, Luisa Pieroni, Viviana Greco, Valerio Chiurchiù, Alberto Ferri, Cristiana Valle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction is a defining feature of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), emerging early and strongly associated with disease progression and prognosis. While systemic hypermetabolism is well documented, the central mechanisms underlying energy imbalance remain poorly understood. The hypothalamus, a key regulator of whole-body energy homeostasis, has recently been implicated in ALS, but its mechanistic contribution to metabolic failure and disease progression remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed the hypothalamus SOD1-G93A mouse model using proteomics (ProteomeXchange ID: PXD070931), mitochondrial bioenergetic assays, immunofluorescence, flow cytometry, and gene expression to assess hypothalamic mitochondrial function, glial activation, and melanocortin system integrity. Limited analyses in the hFUS model confirmed the presence of key hypothalamic alterations, supporting a shared vulnerability across ALS models. In SOD1-G93A mice, the metabolic modulator trimetazidine (TMZ) was administered presymptomatically to evaluate effects on hypothalamic pathology, metabolic regulation, disease onset, and survival.FindingsWe provide the first evidence that mitochondrial bioenergetic defects arise specifically in the hypothalamus of ALS models before symptom onset. Proteomic profiling revealed dysregulation of mitochondrial pathways, while functional assays confirmed impaired bioenergetics in the hypothalamus. These deficits were accompanied by local pro-inflammatory activation of astrocytes and microglia, mitochondrial dysfunction in glial cells, and early disruption of the arcuate nucleus melanocortin system. Limited analyses in hFUS mice confirmed selective hypothalamic vulnerability.Early TMZ treatment in SOD1-G93A mice specifically restored hypothalamic bioenergetics, normalized local glial activation and melanocortin signaling, delayed disease onset, and extended survival.InterpretationThese findings establish the hypothalamus as an early and selectively vulnerable site in ALS, where region-specific mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to metabolic and neuroinflammatory alterations. Targeting hypothalamic bioenergetics represents a promising therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder in which the selective degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons results in muscle weakness, wasting, and paralysis. The disease typically culminates in respiratory failure and death within two to five years from the first clinical manifestations [[1], [2], [3]]. Although major advances have been made in understanding ALS pathophysiology, its etiology remains complex and multifactorial, and no curative treatments are currently available [[2], [3], [4]]. Among the mechanisms implicated in ALS pathophysiology, energy metabolism dysfunctions are increasingly recognized as key contributors to disease progression [[5], [6], [7]]. Clinical features such as lower premorbid body mass index (BMI), hypermetabolism and rapid weight loss correlates with faster progression and poor prognosis [6,7]. Importantly, these metabolic alterations can emerge years before the neuromuscular symptoms [8,9] and even precede the increase in serum neurofilament light chain in ALS mutation carriers [10], suggesting that they may represent early pathogenic events rather than secondary consequences.

Our previous studies have demonstrated that modulation of whole-body energy metabolism can significantly influence disease progression in ALS mouse models [7,[11], [12], [13]]. Among potential therapeutic approaches, the metabolic modulator trimetazidine (TMZ) improved survival and delayed motor decline in SOD1-G93A mice, an effect associated with preserved mitochondrial activity and energy homeostasis [12]. These findings are consistent with the recent completion of a phase 2a clinical trial [14].

While skeletal muscle and spinal cord are well-established targets of both degeneration and metabolic impairment in ALS, emerging evidence highlights the hypothalamus as a key underappreciated site of vulnerability in the disease [7,15,16].

As the master regulator of energy balance, the hypothalamus integrates peripheral and central signals to control several biological functions including energy homeostasis, food intake and neuroendocrine outputs [17]. In ALS patients, marked hypothalamic atrophy together with TDP-43 aggregation in this region strongly correlate with hypermetabolism, reduced BMI and poor survival [7,[18], [19], [20]]. Beyond structural modifications, emerging data reveals disruptions in specific hypothalamic neuronal populations involved in metabolic regulation, which include reduction in oxytocin-producing neurons within the paraventricular nucleus, loss in orexin-producing and melanin-concentrating hormone neurons within the lateral hypothalamus and imbalance in the melanocortin system in the arcuate nucleus (ARC) [7,15]. Particularly, the melanocortin system, comprising AgRP and POMC neurons in the ARC, tightly regulates the metabolism and is finely tuned by nutrient availability, glial state and mitochondrial activity [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]].

Microglia and astrocytes respond to nutritional cues influencing melanocortin tone and modulate hypothalamic function [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Notably, microglia activation in the ARC of symptomatic SOD1-G93A mice contributes to melanocortin dysfunction and metabolic alterations, while its pharmacological inhibition restores hypothalamic signalling and body weight [26,29].

Mitochondrial dysfunction within hypothalamic neurons and glia correlates with altered feeding behaviour and systemic energy dysregulation [[30], [31], [32]], underscoring the role of hypothalamic mitochondrial efficiency in systemic energy homeostasis.

Interestingly, defects in mitochondria functionalities are a common feature of the tissues considered most susceptible to ALS-related degeneration, such as spinal cord and skeletal muscle [4,[33], [34], [35], [36]]. However, this phenomenon remains largely unexplored in the hypothalamus, an area whose involvement is still poorly understood in the pathophysiology of ALS.

Given the central role of the hypothalamus in energy homeostasis and its emerging vulnerability in ALS, this study aimed to determine whether hypothalamic mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to and potentially drive disease-related metabolic disturbances, thereby representing a potential therapeutic target.

Animals

Only female mice were used for the experimental investigations. Animals from both colonies were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. For all the experiment we used female SOD1-G93A and wild type mice, in our previous studies, we did not observe sex-dependent differences in systemic metabolic alterations, and pharmacological interventions tested in SOD1-G93A mice of both sexes showed comparable efficacy and metabolic responses [12].

TMZ treatment

Female transgenic SOD1-G93A and wild-type mice were treated with TMZ (20 mg/kg), diluted in drinking water starting at 30 days of age (n ≥ 12 per group). As previously described [12], treatment efficacy was monitored by assessing muscle strength using a dynamometer and by recording body weight weekly under consistent conditions and at the same hour to minimize experimental variability. Giving 100% to the first muscular strength measurement, disease onset was defined as the age at which the peak forelimb grip strength of SOD1-G93A mice declined by 20%. The complete hind limb paralysis and the inability to right the body within 30 s when placed in a supine position define the survival endpoint.

Animals aged 70 days were sacrificed for the subsequent in-depth investigations aimed at evaluating how the treatment influences hypothalamic alterations.

Sample preparation for mass spectrometry analysis

Proteins obtained from hypothalamus and cerebellum (used as a control tissue) of 70-day old wild type and SOD1-G93A mice were used for proteomics analysis.

Each protein sample, derived from the whole extracts, was subjected to protein digestion following the Filter-Aided Sample Preparation (FASP) protocol as described by Distler et al. (10.1038/nprot.2016.042). Briefly, 50 μg of proteins resulting from each condition were reduced (DTT 8 mM in 8 M urea buffer and 100 mM Trizma Base), alkylated (IAA 50 mM in 8 M urea buffer and 100 mM Trizma Base) and then digested with trypsin (final trypsin concentration of 0.01 μg/μL) overnight at 37 °C on Microcon Centrifugal Filter Devices (Merck Millipore Ltd., Cork, Ireland).

LC-MS/MS analysis: Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis were performed by an UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano – HPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled with a high-resolution Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Nanospray Flex source (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Specifically, Tryptic peptides (0.5 μg) from each digested sample were loaded into a PepMap RSLC C18 column (2 μm, 100 Å, 50 μm × 15 Cm; Thermo Scientific) maintained at 40 °C and then separated by a 155-minute reversed-phase gradient at 0.3 μL/min flow rate. Eluent A consisted of an aqueous solution of 0.1% FA, while eluent B was ACN with 0.1% FA. The gradient program was set as follows: 0% B and 100% A (min 0-110), 20% B and 80% A (min 110-120), 40% B and 60% A (min 120-125), 90% B and 10% A (min 125-145), 3% B and 97% A (min 145-155).

The separated peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer operating in positive ionization mode (2100 V), with fragmentation performed in a Higher-energy Collision Dissociation (HCD) cell and data acquired in Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode. The MS scan parameters were set as follows: Orbitrap resolution, 120,000; scan range (m/z), 350–1600. The MS/MS scan parameters were: Orbitrap resolution, 15,000. All samples were analyzed in analytical triplicates.

Protein Identification and label free quantification: MS raw data were analysed with the software Proteome Discoverer (v. 2.4.1). The qualitative identification of proteins was obtained by searching all reviewed proteins (Swiss-Prot) within the UniProt Mus Musculus reference proteome (ID: UP000000589, Taxon ID:10090).

The search parameters used were set as follows: parent mass error tolerance automatic 10.0 ppm; fragment mass error tolerance 0.02 Da; minimum of 3 fragment ions matched per protein; minimum of 3 peptides matched per protein; maximum 2 missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteines was set as a static modification, while oxidation of methionines, methionine loss, acetylation of lysines and N-terminal acetylation, were included as dynamic modifications. A false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of less than 1% was applied to the identification algorithm. The expression analysis was performed considering the technical replicates available for each experimental condition assuming each group was an independent variable.

Protein quantification was carried out using label-free quantitative proteomics based on normalized protein intensities across biological and technical replicates.

Differentially expressed proteins were selected using a fold change threshold greater than ±20%, and only proteins with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Protein fold changes were calculated as ratios between experimental groups derived from normalized intensities.

Statistical analysis, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and pathway analysis were performed with the ggplot2 library in R (Wickham H (2016). ggplot2: Elegant Graphics for Data Analysis. Springer-Verlag New York. ISBN 978-3-319-24277-4, https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org.) and OmicScope (10.1038/s41467-024-50875-z.) using Reactome Pathway database (v. Reactome Pathways 2024).

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (Perez-Riverol 2025 0.1093/nar/gkae1011) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD070931.

Bioenergetic analysis on hypothalamic punches

Bioenergetic analyses were performed on brain tissues from 50- and 70-day old SOD1-G93A, and 35-day old hFUS+/+ mice along with their corresponding age-matched control littermates. Coronal brain sections were obtained using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica, Nussloch, Germany) at cutting speed of 0.07 mm/s in cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF: 120 mM NaCl, 3.5 mM KCl, 1.3 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.4 mM H2PO4, 5 mM HEPES, 10 mM d-glucose, pH 7.4) oxygenated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 to maintain the viability and the physiological conditions of the tissue.

To ensure reproducible sampling of the tuberal hypothalamus and hippocampus, 180-μm coronal sections were collected between bregma −1.43 and −2.27 mm, according to the mouse brain atlas (Paxinos and Franklin). For each section, a 0.35-mm biopsy punch was placed bilaterally immediately lateral to the third ventricle and ventral to the fornix, corresponding to the tuberal hypothalamus. Two to three punches per hemisphere were collected from four consecutive sections per animal using a reusable 0.35 mm biopsy punch pen (World Precision Instrument, FL, USA). Each section was kept in ice-cold, oxygenated aCSF to ensure tissue viability. Punches showing visible tissue damage or imprecise localization were excluded prior to analysis. The collected punches were immediately placed in XFe96 Cell Culture Microplate (Agilent Technologies, CA) containing 180 μL of aCSF supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco™, Grand Island, NY and Scotland, UK). After 30 min at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator, the XFe96 cell culture plate containing tissue punches was loaded into the Seahorse analyzer to begin the assay. The drugs were loaded into the XFe96 cartridge ports (A, B, C) at final concentrations: oligomycin, 5 μM (Sigma Aldrich, USA); FCCP, 1 μM (carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone; Sigma Aldrich, USA) with pyruvate, 0.75 μM (Gibco™, Grand Island, NY and Scotland, UK); and rotenone, 1 μM/antimycin A, 1 μM (both Sigma Aldrich, USA).The protocol, modified from Wang et al. [38], consisted of four measurement cycles [186], where each cycle included 2 min of drug injection, 1 min of waiting and 3 min of measurement. Wells in which punches were displaced or showed technical artifacts in the OCR traces were excluded a priori. Furthermore, only punches displaying basal OCR values within the range of 20–200 pmol O2/min were considered for analysis, ensuring consistent tissue quality across experiments.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on brain tissues from 50-, 70-, and 120-day-old SOD1-G93A mice (both treated and untreated) and from 35-day-old hFUS mice, along with their corresponding age-matched control littermates. After anesthetization as previously mentioned, the animals were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). After dissection, the brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C and sectioned coronally with a cryostat to collect 30 μm-thick slices.

To analyze POMC and AgRP neurons, as well as microglia and astrocytes, a free-floating immunofluorescence protocol was used. Briefly, sections were blocked with 10% normal donkey serum (NDS) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat#017-000-121) in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies prepared in PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 2% NDS for 48 h at 4 °C with gentle agitation. After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 2% NDS for 3 h at room temperature. Finally, sections were mounted on slides, air-dried, and coverslipped using Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma). The primary antibodies used were Anti-Agouti-Related Peptide (AgRP, Phoenix Biotech, Cat. #H-003-57, 1:500), Anti-Proopiomelanocortin (POMC, Phoenix Biotech, Cat. #H-029-30, 1:500), Anti-GFAP (Novus Biologicals, Cat. #NBP1-05197, 1:500), and Anti-IBA1 (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical, Cat. #019–19741, 1:500). Secondary antibodies included Donkey anti-Rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #A21208, 1:200) and Cy3 AffiniPure F(ab')2 Fragment Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat. #715-166-150, 1:100). Images of the hypothalamus, particularly the arcuate nucleus (ARC), were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope at 10X magnification. Four sections from each animal were used and analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji) software.

RNA isolation and real time qPCR

Total RNA was obtained from the hypothalamus of 70-day-old SOD1-G93A and WT mice (n ≥ 5 for each experimental group) with TRIzol® Reagent (Ambion, Life Technologies) and reverse-transcribed using the ImProm-II™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega). Real-time qPCR was used to evaluate the levels of mRNA expression with a LightCycler 480 SYBR Green System (Roche, ETC). We used the “second derivative max” algorithm of the LightCycler software to calculate the crossing point (Cp) values. We chose the TATA box binding protein as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Dissociation of the hypothalamus and cerebellum

The hypothalamus and cerebellum were collected and immersed in D-PBS with 5.5 mM glucose (cat. N. 14287072, Gibco) at 4 °C. According to the manufacturer protocol, the tissues were immediately dissociated to single-cell suspension by enzymatic degradation and by myelin removal using the adult brain dissociation kit and GentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec.). Briefly, tissues were placed in C-tubes by adding a mixture of Enzyme P and Buffer Z. A second mix containing enzyme A and Buffer Y is then added, with the C tubes tightly closed and attached upside down onto the sleeve of the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters (Miltenyi Biotec.) and the program 37C_ABDK_02 for 30 min. After centrifugation for 10 min at 300 g, the cells were resuspended and filtered to a 70 μm MACS SmartStrainer with 5 ml of cold D-PBS with 5.5 mM glucose. For the debris removal, the samples were resuspended in debris removal solution, gently overlayed in cold D-PBS with 5.5 mM glucose and centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min with full acceleration brake. The two top phases were aspirated and discarded, and cells were resuspended in cold D-PBS with 5.5 mM and ready for cell count and for high-dimensional flow cytometry.

Immunophenotyping by high-dimensional flow cytometry

For immunophenotyping of hypothalamus and cerebellum, single cell suspension was stained on the cell surface in two different panels with PerCP5.5-conjugated anti-CD45 (1:100, Biolegend or BV510-conjugated anti-CD45 (1:100, Biolegend), APC-Vio770-conjugated anti-CD11b/c (1:100, Miltenyi Biotec) or PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD11b (1:100, Biolegend), APC-conjugated anti-ACSA-2 (1:100, Miltenyi Biotec), PE-conjugated anti-CD3 (1:100, Miltenyi Biotec), PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-Ly6C (1:100, Biolegend), PE-CF594-conjugated anti-Ly6G (1:100, Biolegend), BV650-conjugated anti-CD86 (1:100, Biolegend), BV421-conjugated anti-CD163 (1:100, Biolegend), and APC-conjugated anti-TREM2 (1:100, Biolegend), Alexa488-conjugated S100A (1:100, R&D Systems) and PE-conjugated anti-C3 (1:100, BD Biosciences) for 15 min at 4 °C in the dark. After staining, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min and stained intracellularly with rabbit primary anti-iNOS (1:150, Abcam), washed with cold PBS and stained with secondary anti-rat PerCP5.5 (1:150, Santa Cruz) for further 15 min. After staining, cells were resuspended in PBS, ready to be acquired. These markers allowed us to correctly identify the different cell populations. Briefly, cells were first gated as ACSA2+ to identify astrocytes, which were further gated for their expression of either C3 or S100A. Subsequently, ACSA2- cells were gated for Ly6C+ to identify infiltrated monocytes. Next, Ly6C− cells were gated for CD45+ and CD11b+ to identify the total leucocytes/resident immune cells and inside this gate, microglial cells were identified as CD45lowCD11b+ cells, myeloid cells as CD45highCD11bhigh and infiltrated non-myeloid leukocytes as CD45highCD11blow cells. Non-myeloid leukocytes were further gated to identify infiltrated T-lymphocytes as CD3+ cells. The expression of CD86, iNOS, CD163 and TREM2 was further assessed within the microglial and myeloid cell populations. All samples were acquired on a 13-color Cytoflex (Beckman Coulter). For each analysis, at least 0.2 × 106 live cells were acquired by gating on aqua Live/Dead negative cells and then analyzed using the Flowjo analysis software (FlowJo 10.0 Tre Star; https://www.flowjo.com/solutions/flowjo), as reported [39].

Glial cells isolation and bioenergetic analysis

For each bioenergetic analysis, primary glial cells were isolated from seven hypothalamus of 70-day-old wild type and SOD1-G93A mice. Briefly, the cell suspension was first labeled with the Anti-CD11b antibody, resuspended in PBS with 0.5% BSA and loaded onto an MS Column (Miltenyi Biotec), which was placed in the magnetic field of a MiniMACS™ Separator (Miltenyi Biotec). The magnetically labeled CD11b-positive cells (microglia) were retained within the column and eluted as the positively selected cell fraction after removing the column from the magnet. The unlabeled cell fraction was subsequently labeled with Anti-GLAST antibody (ACSA-1) conjugated to biotin to isolate the astrocytic population. Then, superparamagnetic MicroBeads coupled to an Anti-Biotin antibody were applied. Before antibody labeling, nonspecific binding to the Fc receptor was blocked using the FcR Blocking Reagent, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were resuspended in PBS with 0.5% BSA and the cell suspension was loaded onto an MS Column (Miltenyi Biotec), which was placed in the magnetic field of a MiniMACS™ Separator (Miltenyi Biotec). The magnetically labeled GLAST-positive cells were retained within the column and eluted as the positively selected cell fraction after removing the column from the magnet.

For the Mito Stress Test and the Real-Time ATP Rate Assay, isolated glial cells were seeded onto poly-d-lysine–coated Agilent 96-well plate (4 × 104 cells/well) and maintained in a humidified atmosphere (5% CO2, 95% air) at 37 °C for 6 days using DMEM supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, 100 U/ml penicillin G, 100 μg/ml streptomycin and 0.5 μg/ml Amphotericin B and Gentamicin 40 μg/ml. Before testing, the growth medium was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM medium pH 7.4 (supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM glucose, and 2 mM l-glutamine) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C without CO2.

For the Mito stress test, the XFe96 cartridge was loaded with the following drugs at their final concentrations: 2.5 μM oligomycin (Sigma Aldrich, USA); 1.5 μM FCCP (carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone); Sigma Aldrich, USA) and 1 μM rotenone/1 μM antimycin A (both Sigma Aldrich, USA). For Real-Time ATP Rate Assay were used: 2.5 μM oligomycin (Sigma Aldrich, USA); 1 μM rotenone/1 μM antimycin A (both Sigma Aldrich, USA).

Both assays consisted of three measurement cycles, each including 3 min of injection, 1 min of waiting, and 3 min of measurement. Data were analyzed using Wave software (Seahorse Bioscience).

Data analysis

Data are displayed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Kaplan Meier, Student's t-test (two groups comparison), one-way or two-way ANOVA analysis (more than two groups) were performed, following the assessment of normal distribution of values. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Post-hoc analysis was carried out by Bonferroni or Tukey tests. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 software.

Results

Mitochondrial and inflammatory alterations in hypothalamic proteome

Given the growing evidence implicating the hypothalamus in ALS pathogenesis, we sought to determine whether this region might represent a primary site of disease-related dysfunction.

To date, no proteomic studies have specifically investigated the hypothalamus in the context of ALS. Therefore, we performed an unbiased proteomic profiling of hypothalamic tissue from SOD1-G93A mice and their wild type (WT) littermates at 70 postnatal days, corresponding to disease onset [12]. A total of 2346 proteins were consistently identified across all samples, providing one of the first comprehensive proteomic datasets of the ALS hypothalamus. Quantitative analysis revealed a core of significantly modulated proteins showing significant differential expression between SOD1-G93A and WT mice. Reactome pathway enrichment analysis showed a highlighted dysregulation affecting several cellular processes, with notable alterations in mitochondrial and metabolic pathways, including aerobic respiration (R-HSA-1428517), respiratory electron transport (R-HSA-611105), amino acid metabolism (R-HSA-71291), and mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (R-HSA-77289) (Figure 1B). These findings highlight a major impairment of cellular bioenergetics in the ALS hypothalamus.

At the protein level, several components of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), such as COX6A1 and ATP5MJ, were significantly downregulated (Figure 1C, Table 1). These results, further confirmed by Western blot and mRNA expression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1A and B), indicate a compromised oxidative phosphorylation capacity. Conversely, proteins involved in fatty acid metabolism (DBI) and glycolysis (BPGM) were upregulated, suggesting a compensatory metabolic shift toward enhanced glucose utilization. Consistent with this, the mitochondrial antioxidant Thioredoxin-2 (TXN2) was upregulated, likely reflecting an adaptive response to increased oxidative stress (Figure 1C, Table 1).

While inflammatory pathways were not among the top categories emerging from the global enrichment analysis, a focused evaluation of immune-related proteins have been performed.

This analysis revealed altered expression of key mediators, including HMGB1 and N4BP1, both implicated in immune signalling and neuroinflammatory responses. In addition, proteins involved in endosomal and lysosomal trafficking, such as ATP6V0C, PRKCD, and ACTR10, displayed differential expression, suggesting potential activation of degradative and inflammatory mechanisms within the hypothalamus (Figure 1C; Table 1). Alterations in the proteostasis network were also observed, with PSMD12 upregulated and PSMB7 downregulated, pointing to an imbalance in proteasomal function that may exacerbate protein misfolding stress and neuroinflammatory cascades (Figure 1C; Table 1).

Together, these data reveal a complex reorganization of hypothalamic bioenergetics, integrating mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic compensation, and inflammatory activation.

To assess whether the proteomic alterations detected in the hypothalamus reflected a broader CNS pattern, we profiled the cerebellar proteome from the same SOD1-G93A and WT cohorts using the identical workflow and statistical thresholds (FDR <0.05), as described in the method section.

A comparable number of proteins were identified, yet only a limited subset reached statistical significance. The heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 2A) of the top differentially expressed proteins showed only modest differences between genotypes, indicating that differential protein expression in the cerebellum is limited compared to the hypothalamus. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering confirmed genotype-dependent segregation (Supplementary Fig. 3A–D), likely reflecting the underlying biological differences between WT and SOD1-G93A tissues. However, in contrast to the hypothalamus, these differences were not associated with a coordinated pathway-level remodelling, mainly related to mitochondrial and metabolic alterations.

In particular, reactome pathway analysis highlighted a few enriched categories related to cellular processes such as membrane trafficking, proteasome assembly, and regulation of apoptosis, along with metabolic terms including mitochondrial β-oxidation and respiratory electron transport (Supplementary Fig. 2B). However, these enrichments were mild and lacked coherent directionality, with mean fold changes clustering around zero.

Although some mitochondrial and inflammatory pathways appeared among the top-ranked terms, their statistical weight and internal consistency were much weaker than those observed in the hypothalamus. Overall, the cerebellum exhibited subtle and non-coordinated proteomic changes, mainly involving basal metabolic and structural functions, without the strong mitochondrial and immune remodelling that characterizes the hypothalamus at disease onset.

These results uncover an early and region-specific mitochondrial dysfunction in the tuberal hypothalamus, preceding the onset of the disease. As shown in Figure 2B, punches from 50-day-old SOD1-G93A mice display an altered oxygen consumption rate (OCR) profile, while the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), an indirect indicator of glycolysis, remains unchanged. These findings point to a selective impairment of mitochondrial metabolism rather than a global bioenergetic failure. Further analysis revealed a significant reduction in mitochondrial maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity (Figure 2C), suggesting that hypothalamic mitochondria in SOD1-G93A mice are incapable of sustaining higher energy demand long before the onset of the disease. Notably, these deficits are still present at postnatal day 70, coinciding with the onset, at which point ATP production also tended to decrease (Figure 2D,E). Interestingly, the analysis of OCR/ECAR ratio, used as an index of oxidative versus glycolytic metabolism, was consistent with the proteomic data, which reveal an upregulation of glycolytic pathways. The tuberal hypothalamus of 50-day-old (Figure 2G) and 70-day-old (Figure 2H) SOD1-G93A mice exhibited a more glycolytic metabolic profile compared to wild type mice under physiological condition, and a trend under stress condition, as indicated by OCR/ECAR following oligomycin injection, which inhibits ATP synthase and promotes a shift toward glycolytic metabolism.

Notably, these bioenergetic impairments were absent in the hippocampus, used here as a control region (Supplementary Fig. 4A and B), highlighting again a selective vulnerability of the hypothalamus.

Comparable dysfunctions in spare respiratory capacity and maximal respiration were also detected in the tuberal hypothalamus of symptomatic hFUS+/+ mice (Supplementary Fig. 4C). In contrast ATP production showed an increase in hFUS+/+ (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Our data suggest that mitochondrial alterations are not restricted to the SOD1-G93A model but are also shared in another genetic model of ALS. Interestingly, heterozygous hFUS+/− mice, which do not develop symptoms, showed no detectable mitochondrial impairments (Supplementary Fig. 4C), indicating that such mitochondrial alterations are restricted to the pathological condition.

To gain further insight into glial cells, we performed a deep immunophenotyping by high-dimensional flow cytometry on the hypothalamus of 70-day-old SOD1-G93A and WT mice. The percentages of microglia (CD45lowCD11b+) and astrocytes (ACSA2+) were unchanged between groups (Figure 3C); however, both glial cells showed significant differences in the expression of their respective activation and inflammatory markers (Figure 3D–H). In particular, astrocytes of SOD1-G93A mice expressed significantly lower levels of S100A10 (Figure 3D) and no variation in C3 (Figure 3E), suggesting a more reactive and less resting astrocyte phenotype. Moreover, microglia from SOD1-G93A mice displayed a significant increase in pro-inflammatory M1-like markers (iNOS and CD86), together with a non-significant reduction in pro-resolving M2-like markers (CD163 and Trem2) (Figure 3F–H), indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory state.

Such phenotype was also evident in infiltrated CD45highCD11blow myeloid cells, likely macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 6A and B). Other infiltrated cell types such as non-myeloid leukocytes, myeloid leukocytes and T cells did not show any variation in SOD1-G93A, with only monocytes being higher (Supplementary Fig. 6C). Of note, these alterations in microglia and astrocytes phenotype and activation state were only observed in hypothalamus and not in the cerebellum (Supplementary Fig. 7A–E), corroborating the key role of this area.

Given the concomitant presence of mitochondrial dysfunction and gliosis, we next examined the metabolic profile of hypothalamic glia at 70 days of age. Isolated astrocytes and microglia exhibited a trend toward increased percentage of ATP derived from glycolysis and a concomitant decreased of ATP from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) under basal conditions (Figure 3I,L). This shift was underpinned by impaired mitochondrial functionality (Figure 3M,N), a condition previously linked to glial activation [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. This metabolic shift was consistent with our flow cytometry findings, further supporting the notion that mitochondrial deficits foster a pro-inflammatory phenotype in hypothalamic glia.

Selective vulnerability of the hypothalamic melanocortin system

Mitochondria functionalities and glial state within the hypothalamus strongly influence the activity of the neuronal populations composing the melanocortin system in the hypothalamic ARC, which orchestrate the whole-body energy metabolism: POMC and AgRP neurons [[21], [22], [23],25,45].

In agreement with previous observations in SOD1-G86R mice [46], we detected a significant decrease in the number of POMC neurons and an increase in AgRP neuron-positive area in SOD1-G93A mice. Interestingly, these alterations precede the appearance of the symptoms, manifesting at the presymptomatic stage (50 days of age) (Figure 4A) and remaining evident at the onset of the disease (70 days of age) (Figure 4B). Gene expression analyses on 70-day-old SOD1-G93A mice further confirmed alterations in the melanocortin system, including a downregulation of Mc4r mRNA expression (Figure 4C). Similar alterations were observed in symptomatic hFUS+/+ mice (Supplementary Fig. 8B), supporting the notion that melanocortin system disruption is not restricted to SOD1 mutations.

It is noteworthy that in SOD1-G93A mice, hypothalamic bioenergetic and melanocortin alterations emerge at an early stage, preceding both neuromotor dysfunction and the hypermetabolism while occurring in parallel with skeletal muscle metabolic changes (Scaricamazza et al., 2020). Specifically, changes in the melanocortin system are reflected by a reduction of the muscular melanocortin receptor Mc5r mRNA expression in Gastrocnemius (Figure 4E), which is known to promote glycolytic metabolism [47,48]. This finding is consistent with the decreased expression of the adrenergic receptor β2AR (Figure 4F), also implicated in the stimulation of glycolysis. Muscular β2AR together with the hypothalamic Slc12a8 have been previously identified as markers of the hypothalamic–skeletal muscle axis [49]. Interestingly, Slc12a8 is downregulated in our SOD1-G93A mice (Figure 4G), further suggesting a role of hypothalamus in muscular metabolism.

Early TMZ treatment delays onset and extends the survival

In a previous study, we demonstrated that TMZ administered at disease onset slows ALS progression and extends survival of SOD1-G93A mice, improving their energy balance [12].

We hypothesized that TMZ efficacy on energy metabolism might also involve the recovery of hypothalamic dysfunction. Since the hypothalamic alterations are early events in ALS, we treated female SOD1-G93A mice with 20 mg/kg of TMZ in drinking water starting right after the weaning at postnatal day 30 (Figure 5A). Before analyzing the effect of this drug on hypothalamus, we evaluated whether early intervention could also influence disease progression. Remarkably, pre-onset TMZ treatment improved muscular strength (Figure 5B), delayed the onset of 40 days (Figure 5C,D) and prolonged the survival by two weeks (Figure 5E,F). TMZ driven rescue of mitochondrial and immune proteome signatures

To evaluate whether pharmacological modulation of bioenergetic dysfunction could counteract hypothalamic alterations in ALS, we assessed the effects of TMZ treatment in SOD1-G93A mice at disease onset.

The overall proteomic pattern indicated a partial but consistent normalization of the hypothalamic molecular signature following treatment. In particular, segregation between treated and untreated animals was primarily assessed using principal component analysis (PCA). PCA shows a clear separation between TMZ-treated and untreated SOD1-G93A mice along the first principal component, indicating that treatment is a major contributor to the observed variance (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results demonstrate that TMZ treatment induces a consistent global shift in the hypothalamic proteome.

The heatmap (Figure 6A) highlights a distinct clustering of TMZ-treated SOD1-G93A mice, with several proteins previously altered in the ALS condition showing a reversal of expression. The Reactome pathway enrichment analysis (Figure 6B) provides functional insight into these molecular changes, revealing that TMZ predominantly affects pathways linked to energy metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis. Pathways including respiratory electron transport, mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, and glucose metabolism were significantly enriched and exhibited a trend toward upregulation, consistent with a reactivation of bioenergetic processes impaired in the ALS hypothalamus.

Additionally, enrichment of membrane trafficking, cellular response to hypoxia, and regulation of apoptosis points to a broader improvement in cellular stress resilience and proteostasis.

Single protein analysis (Figure 6C) revealed an upregulation of the mitochondrial OXPHOS proteins, including MT–CO1 and MT–CO3, and a concomitant increase of HKDC1, further suggesting an enhanced glucose utilization. Taken together, these results demonstrate that TMZ exerts a selective rescue effect on hypothalamic bioenergetics, improving mitochondrial function, metabolic efficiency and stress adaptation. The observed proteomic changes are consistent with a shift toward a recovery-oriented molecular profile, supporting the concept that targeted metabolic modulation can stabilize hypothalamic function and mitigate early disease-related dysfunction in ALS.

TMZ improves mitochondrial function and neuroimmune balance in hypothalamus

We further assessed the efficacy of the TMZ treatment on mitochondrial function and inflammation, by evaluating the bioenergetic parameters and glial state in the hypothalamus of treated SOD1-G93A mice. TMZ treatment significantly improved mitochondrial performance, as evidenced by the enhanced maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity (Figure 7A), attenuated gliosis (Figure 7B; Supplementary Fig. 10A) and promoted a M1-to-M2 shift in both microglia (Figure 7C,D) and myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. 10B), accounting for its protective and pro-resolving role. These improvements were accompanied by a restoration of the melanocortin system, including the re-establishment of the proper balance of POMC and AgRP neuronal populations (Figure 7E) and the recovery of Mc4r mRNA (Figure 7F).

Remarkably, TMZ was able to restore the balance of the melanocortin system even when administered at the onset of the disease (Supplementary Fig. 10D and E), a stage at which the hypothalamus is already compromised, highlighting its potential translational relevance for early therapeutic intervention.

Discussion

Metabolic dysfunction is a hallmark of ALS, detectable even in presymptomatic mutation carriers and associated with earlier onset and poorer prognosis [10]. Both patients and mouse models consistently show systemic energy imbalance, with increased energy expenditure and impaired ATP production [[6], [7], [8],50]. Modulating energy metabolism significantly impacts disease course: interventions that raise energy demand accelerate degeneration, while energy-supporting strategies delay progression [7,[11], [12], [13],51,52]. These alterations reflect the broader pathophysiological footprint of ALS, extending beyond the motor system. Among the central regulators of systemic energy balance, the hypothalamus emerges as a critical yet underexplored structure. Although structural and functional hypothalamic changes are increasingly reported in patients and mutation carriers [15,[53], [54], [55]], it remains unclear whether they play a primary, causal role in metabolic dysfunction or arise secondarily to other pathological processes.

Using an unbiased proteomic approach, we uncovered a distinct enrichment of mitochondrial metabolism and inflammatory pathways in the hypothalamus of SOD1-G93A mice. These results highlight the hypothalamus as a selectively vulnerable region in ALS, characterized by early metabolic and inflammatory remodelling. The proteomic alterations identified in the hypothalamus closely mirror molecular signatures previously described in the spinal cord, a region traditionally regarded as the primary site of ALS pathology. Several proteomic and transcriptomic studies of the spinal cord have consistently revealed profound mitochondrial dysfunction, altered oxidative phosphorylation, and activation of immune-inflammatory pathways [[56], [57], [58]]. Our findings extend these observations to the hypothalamus, suggesting that the mechanisms underlying metabolic imbalance and neuroinflammation are not confined to motor regions but rather represent a widespread molecular phenotype of ALS. This convergence between hypothalamic and spinal molecular alterations supports the notion that energy metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis are central to ALS pathogenesis, linking peripheral motor degeneration with central metabolic network dysfunction.

Remarkably, these molecular modifications translate into an early and pronounced bioenergetic impairment in the tuberal hypothalamus, preceding both systemic hypermetabolism and the onset of motor symptoms.

These changes emphasize the hypothalamus selective vulnerability and suggest it as an early site of pathological onset. Interestingly, in the hFUS model, despite reduced spare and maximal respiration, ATP-linked respiration is increased. This may indicate that hypothalamic mitochondria in hFUS mice operate closer to their bioenergetic limit under basal conditions, allocating a larger fraction of their respiratory capacity to sustain ATP synthesis. This pattern was not observed in SOD1-G93A mice, suggesting that distinct ALS-related mutations can differently reshape mitochondrial function, while converging on reduced respiratory reserve. Overall, these findings support the notion that hypothalamic mitochondrial impairment is a shared feature across ALS models, although the functional consequences may vary depending on the genetic mutation.

Mitochondrial function and neuroinflammation are tightly interconnected, as the transition of microglia and astrocytes from a resting to an activated state relies on their metabolic reprogramming [40,41,43,44]. Consistent with this framework, we report for the first time a mitochondrial deficit in astrocytes and microglia isolated from the hypothalamus of SOD1-G93A mice. This impairment likely underlies the metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, a hallmark of pro-inflammatory activation [40,41,43,44,[59], [60], [61], [62]]. While such metabolic reprogramming can be physiologically adaptive, in ALS it may enhance glial sensitivity to inflammatory stimuli, thereby establishing a vicious cycle that perpetuates both mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation. These findings are in line with the pro-inflammatory phenotype detected by high-dimensional flow cytometry in astrocytes and microglia, as well as with proteomic evidence for enrichment of inflammatory pathways. Complementing these observations, Seahorse analyses revealed a reduced OCR/ECAR ratio in the hypothalamus of SOD1-G93A mice, indicative of a shift toward glycolytic metabolism. This aligns with the proteomic and inflammatory profile of glial cells, suggesting that astrocytes and microglia may contribute substantially to the local glycolytic environment, potentially influencing the function of nearby neurons.

In this context, we identified the ARC as a particularly vulnerable region within the hypothalamus, where observed mitochondrial deficits and glial activation provide a compelling mechanistic link to the early disruption of the ARC-melanocortin system, composed of functionally antagonistic POMC and AgRP neurons that cooperatively regulate systemic energy balance [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. POMC neurons are especially sensitive to bioenergetic stress [32,63,64], and several studies also suggest that astrocytes and microglial activation may directly contribute to their functional suppression [25,26,28,29,65]. Consistently, in agreement with the findings on SOD1-G86R mice [46], we observed a decrease in POMC neurons and the melanocortin receptor Mc4r expression, alongside an increase in AgRP neurons in both SOD1-G93A and hFUS murine models.

Importantly, neuronal markers of other hypothalamic nuclei remained unchanged, supporting the selective susceptibility of the ARC-melanocortin system to mitochondrial and inflammatory insults. This specific vulnerability likely underpins the systemic energy imbalance in ALS.

Given the central role of the ARC-melanocortin system in systemic energy homeostasis and its selective vulnerability to mitochondrial and inflammatory insults, restoring hypothalamic function represents a potential target in ALS. Previous attempts to counteract metabolic decline with pioglitazone, which was expected to modulate hypothalamic pathways, failed to yield significant benefit, likely due to a generalized reduction in melanocortin tone [46,66]. TMZ is a metabolic modulator known to enhance mitochondrial bioenergetics and attenuate inflammation [[67], [68], [69]]. We previously reported that systemic TMZ treatment, administered at disease onset, prolonged lifespan and improved muscle strength in SOD1-G93A mice by restoring systemic energy homeostasis [12]. A recent open-label Phase 2a study evaluated TMZ in ALS patients, reporting safety and tolerability as primary outcomes and its effect on reducing hypermetabolism as a secondary outcome [14].

Building on these findings and considering the early hypothalamic bioenergetic and inflammatory alterations observed in SOD1-G93A mice, we reasoned that presymptomatic administration of TMZ might prevent or normalize these dysfunctions, thus influencing disease progression. Importantly, this approach restored hypothalamic mitochondrial function, bioenergetic deficits, suppressed astrocyte and microglia pro-inflammatory activation, and normalized melanocortin system markers by 70 days of age. Proteomic analyses corroborated these findings, revealing TMZ-mediated normalization of mitochondrial and inflammatory pathways alongside improved glucose metabolism. These data support a model whereby TMZ acts centrally on hypothalamic bioenergetics to restore metabolic homeostasis, thereby improving systemic energy balance, delaying disease onset by 40 days and extending survival by two weeks in SOD1-G93A mice.

Remarkably, TMZ treatment at disease onset, when hypothalamic condition is already significantly compromised, can restore the melanocortin system in 120-day-old SOD1-G93A mice. These findings highlight the unexpected plasticity of the hypothalamus, even at symptomatic stages, and underscore its translational potential as a therapeutic target for mitochondrial-focused interventions in ALS.

These results expand the current view of ALS from a neuromuscular disorder to a systemic disease rooted in central energy dysregulation, with the hypothalamus emerging as a key site of early susceptibility and potential intervention.

Conclusion and limitation

Our findings highlight the central role of hypothalamic dysfunction in ALS pathophysiology. Although primarily derived from preclinical models, these results are strongly supported by recent patient studies reporting hypothalamic alterations—including morphological changes—at early disease stages and in presymptomatic mutation carriers [15,[53], [54], [55]]. Within this framework, the SOD1-G93A mouse continues to serve as the most robust and widely validated model, faithfully recapitulating key aspects of human ALS [70]. Importantly, similar cellular and molecular alterations observed in the hFUS model further reinforce the broader relevance of hypothalamic dysfunction, not restricted to SOD1 mutations. Nevertheless, although both SOD1-G93A and hFUS mice closely recapitulate key pathological features of ALS (particularly the SOD1-G93A model) they cannot fully represent the genetic and clinical heterogeneity of the disease, including both familial and sporadic forms. As such, their translational relevance should be interpreted within the inherent limitations of preclinical models.

In this study, we report early bioenergetic impairments in the tuberal hypothalamus that contribute to systemic metabolic disturbances observed in ALS mouse models. Importantly, treatment with the metabolic modulator TMZ restored both hypothalamic bioenergetic and morphological integrity, leading to improved whole-body energy metabolism and delayed disease progression. Although the precise molecular mechanisms underlying TMZ effects remain to be fully elucidated, our findings provide proof of concept that reinforcing hypothalamic bioenergetic integrity can confer systemic benefits. Taken together, these results highlight the hypothalamus as a key site of selective vulnerability in ALS and suggest that its targeted modulation may represent a novel therapeutic avenue to modify disease trajectory, warranting further preclinical and clinical investigation.
