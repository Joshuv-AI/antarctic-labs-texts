Astrocytes modulate the effects of cocaine in low frequency neuro-vascular oscillations in mice
Authors: Qiaochu Wang, Yanzuo Liu, Hyomin Jeong, Nora D. Volkow, Yingtian Pan, Congwu Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-frequency oscillations (LFOs) in neurovascular system reflect brain activity and are disrupted by cocaine, likely contributing to its neuropathological effects. However, cellular mechanisms underlying this disruption remain poorly understood. We utilize a multimodal imaging platform (MIP) integrating GCaMP6f-based calcium fluorescence imaging and optical coherence Doppler tomography to investigate how acute and chronic cocaine alter LFOs within the neuro-astroglio-vascular (NAV) network in prefrontal cortex (PFC) of isoflurane-anesthetized mice. Astrocytes are selectively modulated via GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) to assess their roles in regulating LFOs. Results show acute cocaine enhances LFOs and their synchronization within NAV network; GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation, modulating astrocytic Ca2+, abolishes this effect of cocaine. Chronic cocaine increases basal LFOs, but chronic GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation abolishes these effects in astrocytes and CBF, though not in neurons. Interestingly, LFOs’ responses to acute cocaine in chronically cocaine exposed mice concomitantly treated with GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation are similar to those in drug naïve mice, indicating that chronic GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation prevents adaptations to chronic cocaine on NAV network. These findings reveal astrocytes are essential modulators for acute and chronic cocaine-induced enhancement of cortical LFOs in PFC, suggesting astrocytes as a potential therapeutic target to mitigate cocaine-related neurovascular dysfunction.

Introduction

Neurovascular coupling (NVC) is a fundamental physiological process that reflects the temporal and regional linkage between neuronal activity and cerebral blood flow (CBF)1,2 necessary for neuronal tissue to receive adequate blood flow to meet metabolic demands. Neuroimage techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) are commonly used to map brain functional activation and connectivity. Spontaneous low-frequency oscillations (LFOs) in the neurovascular system have been used as indicators of brain activity, including cellular and hemodynamic activities3 and their interactions at rest4 or during forepaw stimulation5, as well as LFOs’ disappearance after cerebral death6. In humans, LFOs measured with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) are strongly correlated with those from blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals obtained from fMRI7.

Cocaine is a highly addictive drug that disrupts NVC at rest and during stimulation, interfering with functional brain regional connectivity. Animal studies using a variety of modalities have documented disruption in NVC, including spontaneous activities at rest. For example, simultaneous measures of cortical field potential (FP) and CBF by single electrode and laser Doppler flowmetry showed that cocaine increased low-frequency neuronal and hemodynamic cortical oscillations in the rat brain8. Concomitant use of functional ultrasound (fUS) and electroencephalography (EEG) to measure CBF velocity (CBFv) and local field potentials (LFPs), respectively, showed faster CBFv responses to cocaine in the cortex compared to LFPs, which was interpreted to reflect that cocaine’s effects in the peripheral nervous system that regulates vascular responses were faster than its neuronal central effects9.

Astrocytes play an important role in maintaining brain health and function, including regulating glutamate, ions such as Ca2+ and K+, and water homeostasis in neuronal tissue10, contributing to vascular tone11, and removing toxins and debris from the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)12. Though various studies have reported the effects of cocaine in neuronal activity and CBF, the role of astrocytes in mediating cocaine-induced disruptions in LFO remains unclear. Astrocytes’ involvement in modulating LFOs has not been well investigated, partially due to technical limitations and the difficulty to simultaneously image cell-specific neuronal, glial, and vascular dynamic signals in vivo with high spatiotemporal resolutions. To address these knowledge gaps, we combined a multimodal imaging platform (MIP) with optical coherence Doppler tomography (ODT) to record LFOs in astrocytic Ca2+ (Ca2+A), neuronal Ca2+ (Ca2+N), and CBF signals in vivo. Combination of MIP capabilities with Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs (DREADDs), Gi, allowed us to evaluate astrocyte-dependent mechanisms underlying cocaine-induced changes in LFOs within the NAV network. We hypothesize that astrocytes regulate LFOs within the NAV network. To test this hypothesis, we examined both acute and chronic cocaine effects on Ca2+A, Ca2+N, and CBF LFOs and evaluated how GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation altered cocaine responses in the NAV network in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) of isoflurane-anesthetized mice. Our results show that astrocytes play an important role in cocaine-induced LFOs enhancement. Specific GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation prevented acute cocaine-induced enhanced neurovascular LFOs and modulated long-term LFO synchronization in the NAV network after chronic cocaine administration. These findings highlight the critical role of astrocytes in coordinating the NAV network and its disruption by acute and chronic cocaine.

Acute cocaine increased LFOs in the NAV network

To investigate acute cocaine effects on NAV LFOs, we first examined changes in Ca2+A, Ca2+N, and CBF LFOs following a single intravenous cocaine injection in cocaine-naïve mice. This experiment served as a reference for subsequent astrocyte-modulation studies; the experiment timeline is provided in Fig. 3A. Figure 3B shows the power spectra of mean Ca2+A LFOs (via FFT over 5-min traces) before and after cocaine, with insets showing the acquired temporal Ca2+A LFO and short-time Fourier transform (STFT) LFO(t) spectra. Figure 3C compares the total Ca2+A LFO spectral power within 0–1 Hz, showing a significant increase with cocaine (before: 27.77 ± 2.99, n = 10; after cocaine: 64.46 ± 7.49, p < 0.001, n = 10). Similarly, Ca2+N LFOs, illustrated in Fig. 3D, E, showed a significant increase (34.88 ± 3.89 to 62.16 ± 9.99; p = 0.012, n = 10) after cocaine. Figure 3F compares representative results of CBF LFOs before and after cocaine, and Fig. 3G shows a significant increase from 1789.82 ± 146.21 (n = 20) to 2240.58 ± 168.99 (p < 0.001, n = 20) after cocaine. GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation blocked acute cocaine enhancement of LFO in the NAV network

Given enhanced Ca2+A LFO responses to acute cocaine exposure, we next examined whether astrocytes modulated cocaine-enhanced neurovascular LFOs. To address this, we activated DREADDs(Gi) by clozapine prior to cocaine administration (Fig. 4A). Figure 4B shows the mean Ca2+A LFOs power spectra before and after cocaine, where the insets show representative Ca2+A LFO changes with time over 100–200 s interval. Comparisons showed no significant difference in Ca2+A LFO before (42.52 ± 2.57, n = 10) and after acute cocaine (44.92 ± 4.49, p = 0.64, n = 10) (Fig. 4C). Similarly, comparisons of the mean Ca2+N LFOs did not differ before (63.40 ± 7.21, n = 10) and after cocaine (62.09 ± 8.02, p = 0.9, n = 10) (Fig. 4D, E); nor did CBF LFOs differ before (1765.27 ± 115.91, n = 20) and after cocaine (1680.85 ± 146.15, p = 0.6, n = 20) (Fig. 4F, G). With GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation, CBF LFO was not correlated with either Ca2+N LFO (r = 0.08 ± 0.08, p = 0.21, n = 10) or Ca2+A LFO (r = 0.12 ± 0.09, p = 0.21, n = 10) before cocaine (Supplementary Fig. 1E1, E2). After cocaine, CBF LFO remained uncorrelated with Ca2+N LFO (r = 0.15 ± 0.08, p = 0.36, n = 10) or Ca2+A LFO (r = 0.11 ± 0.09, p = 0.43, n = 10) (Supplementary Fig. 1E3, E4).

To determine whether GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation itself alters NAV LFO dynamics, we examined it before and after clozapine administration. The experiment timeline and the results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation decreased Ca2+A LFO from 64.46 ± 7.49 (n = 10) to 42.52 ± 2.57 (p = 0.01) and CBF LFO from 2240.58 ± 168.99 to 1765.27 ± 115.91 (p = 0.03, n = 10), but it did not change Ca2+N LFO (63.40 ± 7.21 vs 62.16 ± 9.99, p = 0.92, n = 10).

In order to exclude potential off-target effects of clozapine on cocaine-induced LFO responses, we performed a negative control (NC) experiment using non-DREADDs(Gi) mice pretreated with clozapine (0.1 mg/kg, i.p.) before cocaine administration (NC group: Supplementary Fig. 3B1, D1, F1) and then compared their cocaine response to those observed in the Gi group given clozapine (Gi group: Supplementary Fig. 3B2, D2, F2). Results show that clozapine treatment did not significantly affect NAV LFO responses to acute cocaine, and the LFO power enhancement in the NC group differed significantly to that observed in the Gi group given clozapine. Specifically, there were significant differences between these two groups in total power increases for Ca2+A (NC: 137.05% ± 66.29%, n = 4 mice; Gi: 8.81% ± 13.28%, n = 10 mice; p = 0.01, Supplementary Fig. 3C), Ca2+N (NC: 117.15% ± 35.96%, n = 3 mice; Gi: 0.22% ± 8.05%, n = 10 mice; p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 3E), and CBF (NC: 56.50% ± 7.59%, n = 7 mice; Gi: −3.76% ± 6.23%, n = 20 mice; p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 3G) LFOs. These findings confirm that the reduced cocaine responses in DREADDs(Gi) mice after clozapine treatment (Fig. 4) were mediated by Gi activation rather than the off-target effects of clozapine itself.

To assess whether neuronal activity would be affected by Gi activation without cocaine exposure, we conducted additional experiments to compare the Ca2+N LFOs before and after clozapine injection in DREADDs(Gi) mice (Supplementary Fig. 4). Results show that Gi activation did not significantly change Ca2+N LFO power (before: 35.47 ± 3.84, n = 4 mice; after: 36.73 ± 5.77, n = 4 mice; p = 0.86), indicating that Gi activation did not increase neuronal LFO activity.

To further evaluate the baseline difference of Ca2+N LFO between “before” cocaine conditions in Fig. 3E (baseline for 1st cocaine, i.e., “1st baseline”) and Fig. 4E (baseline for 2nd cocaine, i.e., “2nd baseline”), we pooled data from each animal and compared their FFT power spectra between the “1st” and “2nd” baselines as summarized in Supplementary Fig. 5. Results show that the integrated total power of the two baseline LFOs within 0–1 Hz was significantly higher for the 2nd than the 1st baseline (1st: 32.68 ± 3.65; 2nd: 63.4 ± 7.21; n = 10 mice, p = 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 5C). Similarly, the peak of Ca2+N LFO power spectra was higher for the 2nd than the 1st baseline (1st: 0.57 ± 0.06; 2nd: 0.84 ± 0.13; n = 10 mice), though this difference did not reach significance (p = 0.08) (Supplementary Fig. 5D).

Taken together, these findings indicate that Gi activation did not increase neuronal LFO activity. However, it also shows that the effects from the first cocaine injection on Ca2+N were still affecting the 2nd baseline prior to the 2nd cocaine (i.e., baseline Ca2+N LFO changes in Figs. 3E and 4E). This long-lasting effect of acute cocaine confounds the interpretation of our findings on the effects of GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation on Ca2+N. Discussion

Cocaine is a highly addictive drug that impairs neurovascular function. Specifically, cocaine has been shown to disrupt NVC13,14, alter brain functional connectivity15–18, impair the cerebral vasculature and blood flow regulation19–21, and change local field potentials (LFP)8,22, including their LFOs8. However, prior studies mainly focused on neuronal and vascular activities, with limited investigations focusing on astrocytes. Here, we assessed how cocaine affected LFO signals in neurons, astrocytes, and CBF, by measuring dynamic changes in Ca2+A, Ca2+N, and CBF oscillatory activities before and after acute and chronic cocaine. Our results showed that acute and chronic cocaine increased LFOs in the NAV network (enhancing Ca2+A, Ca2+N, and CBF LFOs). We also showed that GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation prevented the increases in Ca2+A, Ca2+N, and CBF LFOs after acute cocaine and also after chronic cocaine, except for Ca2+N LFO, in which the increase was attenuated but not eliminated. Chronic cocaine exposure with GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation also prevented further LFO increases in Ca2+A, Ca2+N, and CBF to an acute cocaine challenge. These results provide evidence that astrocytes are essential mediators of cocaine’s effects on the NVC after acute and chronic exposures.

At baseline, CBF LFOs showed stronger correlations with Ca2+N LFOs than with Ca2+A LFOs, consistent with our previous findings that astrocytes did not significantly contribute to resting vascular LFOs4. In comparison, acute cocaine significantly increased Ca2+A, Ca2+N, and CBF LFOs and enhanced the correlations between CBF-Ca2+A LFOs. Enhancement of LFOs in the NAV network suggests that acute cocaine not only increases neuronal and vascular oscillatory activities but also involves astrocytes in the neurovascular response. Previous studies had reported that acute cocaine increased LFOs in hemodynamics8,9 and in dopamine neuronal activity23. Our findings extend these observations by showing that, besides neurons and CBF, cocaine affects astrocytes as well. Importantly, the enhanced synchronization in the NAV network suggests that astrocytes, which are less active than neurons in the resting state, become active NAV regulators under acute cocaine exposure, which might reflect cocaine-induced increases in astrocytic Ca2+A activities24. Though the mechanism(s) underlying astrocytic activation by cocaine is unclear, it is possible that they could reflect dopaminergic stimulation, as astrocytes in the PFC express D2 and D1 receptors25. However, they could also reflect noradrenergic stimulation from cocaine26 or its local anesthetic effects, and further studies are needed to clarify this.

In astrocytes, Ca2+ dynamics are dominated by G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-mediated inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate (IP3)-dependent Ca2+ release from intracellular stores. Meanwhile, astrocytic Ca2+ oscillatory activities are evoked by the stimulation of presynaptic axons27,28. These astrocytic networks contribute to neuronal synchronization and the generation of rhythmic neuronal oscillations29. Cocaine elevates extracellular dopamine and norepinephrine levels, which can activate astrocytic GPCRs and enhance intracellular Ca2+ signaling, which has the potential to propagate as oscillatory waves30. These oscillatory waves may modulate GPCR signaling, shifting it to the low-frequency spectra in the NAV network after acute cocaine. Meanwhile, elevated astrocytic Ca2+ can further promote glutamate release, which may activate neuronal N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, facilitating the transition from individual fast neuron firing to synchronized slow neuronal rhythmic activity27–29,31.

In parallel, increased Ca2+ in astrocytes induced by cocaine leads to the release of vasoactive metabolites of arachidonic acid, such as 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE), from astrocytic endfeet onto blood vessels11,30. These vasoactive compounds participate in actions of smooth muscle cells and control vascular tone, thus altering vasomotion. Specifically, under cocaine challenge, the elevated 20-HETE blocks K+ channel and thus contracts vascular smooth cells, which may drive smooth vascular activity toward a low-frequency oscillatory pattern. Together, these astrocyte-driven processes provide a mechanistic framework linking cocaine-induced alterations in neurotransmitter homeostasis to enhanced astrocytic Ca2+ oscillations and downstream low-frequency CBF changes.

To study the role of astrocytes in NAV LFO responses to cocaine, we activated GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) before cocaine exposure to assess their involvement without pharmacological challenge. Results showed that GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation significantly decreased baseline Ca2+A and CBF LFOs, while Ca2+N LFOs persisted, thus it disrupted the correlations between CBF-Ca2+N LFOs. These results corroborate the role of astrocytes in modulating baseline LFOs in the NAV network. GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation also prevented cocaine-induced LFO increases and their enhanced synchronization within the NAV network, indicating that astrocytes not only participate but also modulate neuronal and vascular oscillatory activities under acute cocaine challenge.

While prior studies have investigated the interaction between astrocytes and neural circuits32–34 and the contributions of astrocytes to vasoconstriction35–38 and vasodilation36,38–40 and reported that astrocytes facilitate NVC41,42, there is limited data on how astrocytes affect the changes in neuronal and vascular LFOs induced by cocaine. Our findings fill this gap by assessing astrocytes’ role in mediating neurovascular LFOs. Specifically, the absence of cocaine response from Ca2+N and CBF when GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) were activated compared with non-activation demonstrates that astrocytes are necessary for the amplification and synchronization of the NAV network under acute cocaine exposure. We had previously shown that astrocytes modulate vascular and neuronal responses to acute cocaine43, and the current finding extends it to document their role in cocaine’s enhancement of LFOs in the NAV network.

Though mechanisms are unclear, the intracellular signaling pathways targeted by DREADDs(Gi) could be involved in inhibition of cocaine-induced NAV LFO enhancement. Even though reports have shown that astrocytic DREADDs(Gi) activation can induce transient Ca2+ elevations44,45, accumulating evidence shows that the dominant effect of DREADDs(Gi) activation is the suppression of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-dependent astrocytic pathways that are essential for neurovascular regulation. Specifically, astrocytic cAMP appears to modulate intermittent Ca2+ oscillations46 and cerebral vasodilation47. The discrepancies between findings may result from differences between in vivo and ex vivo conditions, as neurovascular interactions affect astrocytic Ca2+ dynamics and methodological differences, but our imaging approach emphasizes the global cellular activity rather than different compartments from astrocytes (e.g., soma, processes, etc.). Furthermore, in astrocytes, cAMP has been shown to convey intracellular signaling primarily through protein kinase A (PKA), which is upregulated by cocaine48 and might be involved in astrocytic perturbation of the NAV network. Moreover, previous studies showed that inhibition of PKA reduced baseline cocaine self-administration, whereas pretreatment of PKA activator had antagonist-like effects49, indicating that altered cAMP-PKA signaling may contribute to cocaine-induced astrocytic and vascular dysfunction.

Similar to its acute effects, chronic cocaine increased basal LFOs in the NAV network, which is consistent with our prior results of increased synchronization of CBF LFOs after chronic cocaine8. The current findings provide novel insight into the role of astrocytes in these effects. Interestingly, GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation before daily cocaine treatment did not fully eliminate cocaine-induced enhancement of basal Ca2+N LFOs. We interpret this to suggest that while astrocytes modulate cerebral vessels directly50, their effects on neurons are likely indirect. The preserved Ca2+N LFO enhancement under acute or chronic GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation suggests that neuronal modulation by cocaine occurs partly independently of astrocytic signaling, potentially through direct neuronal activation or alternative non-astroglial pathways. Although the mechanisms behind the communication between astrocytes and neurons remain unclear, it is possible that astrocytes modulate neuronal activity by astrocyte-derived ATP, which mediates synaptic transmission51 or G-protein coupled adrenergic signaling, which can control their ability to respond to other neurotransmitters52. In addition, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic signaling might also be involved as chronic cocaine potentiates astrocytic tonic GABA release53, reflecting long-term astrocytic adaptations to cocaine exposure. The GABA increases could activate metabotropic GABAB receptors in astrocytes, which induce intracellular Ca2+ signaling that depends on Ca2+ release from internal stores54. This tonic inhibition has the potential to suppress high-frequency neuronal firing and promote low-frequency synchronization. Thus, enhanced LFOs observed following chronic cocaine may also reflect astrocyte-mediated oscillations.

We observed that in chronic cocaine animals, the response to acute cocaine did not result in further LFO increases in the NAV network. In contrast, in chronic cocaine animals with pretreatments to activate GFAP-DREADDs(Gi), the response patterns to acute cocaine were restored to those of the cocaine-naïve state. This suggests that GFAP-DREADDs(Gi) activation resets “homeostasis” of the LFOs in the NAV network to be equivalent to that in the cocaine-naïve state. Prior studies have shown that chronic cocaine disrupts NVC functions, including decreases in CBFv55,56 that can result in microischemia57; our current findings further indicate that astrocytes contribute to these effects. These results highlight the role of astrocytes in sustaining long-term network adaptations following chronic cocaine exposure. Further investigations into specific molecular pathways will be critical to fully understand how astrocytes support or control the NVC function and their role in cocaine associated neuropathology and addiction.

A limitation of this study is that cellular and CBF LFO signals were imaged in parallel and not simultaneously using separate imaging systems (MIP and ODT, respectively, because of vibration noise of the animal mounting stage at the time). As a result, animals were no longer naïve while performing ODT experiments on Day 1, as they had been given a cocaine challenge on Day 0. Optimization of our current fluorescence-optical Doppler microscope (fl-ODM) system will allow, in the future, simultaneous acquisition of Ca2+A/Ca2+N fluorescence along with 3D CBFv measurements43. Such an integrated approach would provide a more precise temporal correlation between cellular and vascular LFOs. In addition, the efficiency of GCaMP6f might affect the fluorescence intensity signals. However, it is noticeable that our analysis focused on relative signal fluctuations rather than absolute fluorescence intensity, and the correlation coefficient analysis was performed using normalized FFT spectra, thus minimizing the influence of amplitudes variability. Another limitation arises from the use of isoflurane anesthesia, which might depress synaptic transmission in the brain58. To minimize this effect, we decreased the inhalational isoflurane concentration to as low as 1–1.5%. Future studies should develop awake animal imaging protocols to investigate LFOs’ dynamics under more physiological conditions, eliminating the effects of anesthesia and providing insights into the behavioral consequences of cocaine induced changes in the NAV network. Moreover, imaging in this study was restricted to the PFC, and thus, we could not ascertain whether local astrocytic DREADDs(Gi) activation exerted global or systemic hemodynamic effects. To minimize potential confounding from systemic physiology, we continuously monitored cardiac rhythms, respiration, and body temperature during experiments (Small Animal Monitoring and Gating system, Model 1025 L, SA Instruments Inc.). Supplementary Fig. 7A demonstrates a raw ΔCBF time course measured from a small vessel (i.e., capillary). Its full FFT power spectrum (0–12.5 Hz) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 7B, indicating that the frequencies of respiration and heart rate are ~0.9 Hz and ~5 Hz, respectively, which were out of our main LFO signal band (<0.5 Hz). Supplementary Fig. 7C and D show the zoom-in spectra of the full power spectrum. Also, Supplementary Fig. 7E shows that the respiration-related peak was further attenuated by applying the band-pass filter while doing LFO signals analysis, thus resulting in a much weaker signal than for CBF LFO. Although our data support a predominantly local cortical mechanism, future studies combining multi-region imaging and systemic physiological monitoring will be required to fully investigate local versus global contributions.

Our study reveals that astrocytes play a critical role in modulating baseline and acute and chronic cocaine-induced increases in LFOs within the NAV network. Further research is needed to determine if cocaine’s dysregulation of astrocytic activity and NAV integration contributes to compulsive drugs use and neurovascular toxicity. Targeting astrocytic modulation could serve as a novel therapeutic intervention to mitigate cocaine-induced neurovascular toxicity and might also be relevant for mitigating neurovascular harm associated with other chronic drug exposures.

Methods

Animals and experiment design

A total of 31 adult mice (8–12 weeks) with both males and females (male/female = 20/11) were used in this study. These mice included transgenic GFAP-Cre mice (Jackson Laboratory, strain # 024098) and C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice (Jackson Laboratory, strain # 000664). All GFAP-Cre mice and their corresponding controls were Cre-negative littermates generated from the same breeding pairs. The animals were divided into eight experimental groups (detailed group sizes and experimental design are listed in Table 1). During all surgeries and experiments, mice were anesthetized with inhalation of 1–1.5% isoflurane mixed with pure oxygen, and their heads mounted on a stereotaxic frame. Meanwhile, physiological conditions, including respiration, heartbeats, and body temperature, were continuously monitored and recorded (Small Animal Monitoring and Gating system, Model 1025 L, SA Instruments Inc.). Specifically, the body temperature was kept at 36–37.5 °C with the aid of a heating pad, and the respiration was maintained at 60–80 breaths/min. After virus injection and cranial window surgeries, mice were monitored daily for several days to ensure full recovery. At the end of all experiments, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane until loss of pedal withdrawal reflex, followed by transcardial perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Death was confirmed by cessation of heartbeat and respiration before brain collection. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Stony Brook University and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Viral expression of GCaMP6f and DREADDs(Gi) in astrocytes and neurons

To detect astrocytic and neuronal specific Ca2+ fluorescence responses to cocaine and examine whether astrocytes are involved in cocaine-induced LFOs changes, a 1:1 mixture of genetically encoded calcium indicators (GECIs), GCaMP6f, and DREADDs, Gi, was delivered into the mouse PFC by viral injection using a microliter syringe (7001KH, Hamilton). Specifically, 0.5 μl of AAV5.CAG.Flex.GCaMP6f.WPRE.SV40 (Addgene, https://www.addgene.org/100835/) and 0.5 μl of AAV5.GFAP.hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (Addgene, https://www.addgene.org/50479/) were slowly infused at 0.2 μl/min into transgenic mice (GFAP-Cre) to express GCaMP6f in astrocytes through a small hole predrilled in the skull overlaying the PFC [A/P: +2.5 mm; M/L: 0.5 mm; D/V: −0.5 mm]. Similarly, 0.5 μl of AAV5.Syn.GCaMP6f.WPRE.SV40 (Addgene, https://www.addgene.org/100837/) and 0.5 μl of AAV5.GFAP.hM4D(Gi)-mCherry were infused into the same brain region of wild-type mice (C57BL/6) to express GCaMP6f into neurons, as shown in Fig. 1A. For negative control group, only 0.5 μl of AAV5.CAG.Flex.GCaMP6f.WPRE.SV40 or 0.5 μl of AAV5.Syn.GCaMP6f.WPRE.SV40 was injected. The astrocyte specificity of GCaMP6f and DREADDs(Gi) expression is demonstrated in Fig. 2A.

Cranial window implantation

To image the mouse’s PFC, a cranial window was implanted for each animal as described previously59. Briefly, the scalp was shaved and disinfected with 70% alcohol and povidone-iodine, and the skull surface cleaned with hydrogen peroxide to expose the bregma and lambda for localization. A square region (approximately 2.5 × 2.5 mm²) on the PFC was identified and thinned using a ϕ0.8 mm dental drill. The skull was then carefully removed, leaving the dura intact. A 4 × 4 mm² coverslip was placed and sealed with super glue and dental cement for later imaging.

In vivo time-lapse imaging of LFOs

A customized multimodal imaging platform (MIP)60 was used for astrocytic and neuronal Ca2+ fluorescence imaging. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, a blue LED beam (Serial No. 4054, Lumencor light engine) at 488 nm was delivered to the cortex through the cranial window. GCaMP6f fluorescence emitted within 512–535 nm was acquired by a sCMOS camera (Zyla 4.2, Andor, dark offset ~110 (a.u.)) (Fig. 1B), which is triggered via a custom LabVIEW program. Time-lapse images were recorded at a frame rate of 12.5 Hz over 5 min for each LFO post processing.

To image cerebral vascular LFOs, a customized spectral-domain optical coherence tomography56 system was used (Fig. 1C), in which an ultrabroad-band laser (LS2000C, Thorlabs, λ = 1310 nm, λFWHM = 220 nm) was used to illuminate a 50:50 single-mode fiber interferometer (# TW1300R5A2 Thorlabs). The backscattered light from mouse cortex in the sample arm was recombined with the backreflected light in the reference arm and detected by a spectrometer with a high-speed linescan InGaAs camera (GL2048L, Sensors Unlimited). A full field of 3D optical Doppler tomography (ODT) image of CBF (1.875 × 2 × 1 mm3) was acquired at an A-scan rate at 6 kHz to detect minute arteriolar or capillary flows. Each B-scan consists of 14000 A-scans, resulting in a B-scan rate of 2.3 fps. To speed up time-lapse flow imaging for CBF LFO extraction, a line ROI (e.g., white line in Fig. 1D) including its x-y coordinates was carefully selected in the full field ODT image for repeated B-scans, and either the number of A-scans per B-scan (e.g., reduced from 14,000 to 1400) or A-scan rate (e.g., raised from 6k to 17.5 kHz) was selected. For instance, CBF LFOs in Fig. 1E were captured at A- and B-scan rates of 17.5 kHz and of 12.5 Hz, respectively. Figure 2B illustrates the experiment timeline of LFOs imaging. During in vivo imaging, cocaine was injected intravenously (i.v.) through tail vein, and clozapine was injected intraperitoneally (i.p.).

Data processing and quantification

For GCaMP6f-Ca2+ fluorescence images, 5 ROIs (yellow circle in Fig. 1B) were selected within the GCaMP6f-expressing cortical areas, but avoiding major vessels to track the temporal changes in Ca2+A and Ca2+N. For ODT images, CBFv was quantified by phase subtraction method61. The temporal LFOs signals were extracted from a total of 3750 frames (12.5 Hz × 300 s).

The temporal Ca2+A or Ca2+N and CBF LFOs signals were quantified as ΔCa2+ and ΔCBF, respectively, which were normalized by dividing the average signal over 5 min for each ROI. A band-pass filter (0.03–1 Hz, 3rd order zero-phase Butterworth filter) was applied to the raw data to remove high-frequency noise components (Supplementary Fig. 7). The first 25 s of the filtered Ca2+A and Ca2+N signals were abandoned to remove photobleaching artifacts. Spectral analysis was performed using short-time Fourier transform (STFT) and fast Fourier transform (FFT) for further quantification (Fig. 1B, E).

Frequency-dependent data points were smoothed by averaging with a binning size of 5 to improve signal stability for subsequent correlation and linear regression analysis, as shown in unnormalized power spectra (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 6). Data points within the 0.03–0.5 Hz band, which is sensitive to cocaine effects8 and outside physiological signals (Supplementary Fig. 7), were selected for correlation analysis. LFO power spectra were normalized by dividing each spectrum by its total integrated power, as shown in the normalized power spectra in Supplementary Figs. 1B and 6B.

To evaluate the potential hemodynamic artifacts and offset value of the camera to astrocytic/neuronal Ca2+ images, we covered the corner of the cranial window with black paper to simultaneously measure LFO signals from “inside” fluorescent area, “outside” fluorescent area, and “dark” area (covered by black material) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8A. In Supplementary Fig. 8B, the “dark” signal does not exhibit any oscillatory pattern, and its FFT power spectrum is nearly flat (Supplementary Fig. 8C), indicating that the camera offset does not contribute to LFO signals.

Supplementary Fig. 8D shows the ratio of “inside” and “outside” LFO signals in time domain (inside(t)/outside(t)). Its FFT power spectrum is shown in Supplementary Fig. 8E, exhibiting similar peak frequency and spectral profile as original “inside” power spectrum in Supplementary Fig. 8C. Supplementary Fig. 8F shows the subtraction of inside and outside power spectra in frequency domain (inside(f)-outside(f)), which is an alternative way to correct the tissue absorbance on LFOs. Here we compare the “ratio” (Supplementary Fig. 8E) and “subtract” (Supplementary Fig. 8F), showing that they are highly correlated (correlation coefficient = 0.98, p < 0.001). This indicates the efficiency of the correction in minimizing hemodynamic artifacts on astrocytic/neuronal Ca2+ LFOs.

Immunohistochemistry

After in vivo imaging, mice were transcranially perfused with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M PBS overnight. Brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose solution and sectioned into 50μm thick slices.

For immunofluorescence staining to visualize astrocytes specificity of GCaMP expression, brain sections were incubated with a chicken anti-GFP primary antibody (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat # A10262) to enhance GCaMP6f fluorescence, followed by an Alexa Fluor 488 anti-chicken secondary antibody (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat # A78948). Brain sections were further incubated with a rabbit anti-GFAP primary antibody (1:200; Sigma Aldrich, Cat # G9269-25UL), followed by an Alexa Fluor 546 anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat # A10040). To visualize astrocytes specificity of DREADDs(Gi) expression, a rabbit anti-GFAP primary antibody (1:200; Sigma Aldrich, Cat # G9269-25UL) and a 488 anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat # A21206) were used to enhance green fluorescence emission in astrocytes, and no immunostaining was used for DREADDs(Gi). All ex vivo fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Zeiss).

Statistics

Sample size of each experimental group was calculated based on previous publication8. No animals or data were excluded during experiments or data analysis. All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparison between two groups was analyzed with a paired Student’s t-test. For correlation coefficient, the Pearson correlation was calculated. A P value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant. If P values were less than 0.001, they were reported as P < 0.001; otherwise, precise P values were provided. Holm-Bonferroni correction was used to correct for multiple comparisons in Figs. 3 and 4, and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 5. The comparison of significance before and after Bonferroni correction is summarized in Table S1.
