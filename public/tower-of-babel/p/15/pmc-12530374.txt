Rethinking the way we measure access to language-concordant health services for minority language populations: a secondary analysis of publicly available physician and population data in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Patrick E Timony, Christopher Belanger, Arlynn Bélizaire, Antoine Desilets, Alain Gauthier, Sathya Karunananthan, Mwali-Nachishali Muray, Cayden Peixoto, Jonathan Peter Fitzsimon, Leanda Godfrey, Lise M Bjerre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveProviding care in a patient’s preferred language improves health outcomes and patient satisfaction. In Ontario, access to French-speaking physicians (FSPs) is estimated using FSP-to-Francophone population ratios and compared with total physician-to-total population ratios. This approach fails to consider the fact that FSPs also serve non-Francophone patients and that Francophones must compete with the entire population to access FSPs. As a result, this approach inaccurately suggests that Francophones have better access to language-concordant care than Anglophones/Allophones. We propose a novel approach to address this issue, enabling unbiased comparisons of access to language-concordant care across linguistic groups.DesignThis secondary analysis of publicly available data containing linguistic variables for the Ontario population (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census) and for family physicians (FPs) (College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario, January 2024) calculated competition-adjusted ratios and probabilities of accessing language-concordant care.SettingOntario, Canada.ParticipantsCensus and publicly available data on FPs (ie, those providing comprehensive family medicine care to the community) and the Ontario population were obtained.ResultsProvince-wide, the crude ratio of FSPs per 1000 Francophones was 3.46. After adjusting for competition, the ratio of FSP per 1000 population was 0.12, compared with a general physicians-per-1000 population ratio of 1.05. Anglophones/Allophones attached to a FP have a 100% probability of receiving care in English compared with an 11.4% probability for Francophones to receive care from a FSP. Expressed otherwise, Anglophones/Allophones are 8.8 times more likely to receive language-concordant care (ie, care in English) than Francophones.ConclusionsAlthough crude physician-to-population ratios overestimate Francophones’ access to FSPs, competition-adjusted ratios and probabilities demonstrate that they are much less likely to access language-concordant care than Anglophones/Allophones. This novel approach has equity implications for health human resources planning and can be applied to other linguistic minority groups and healthcare providers.Keywords: Primary Health Care, Health Equity, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

Official language minority populations in Canada fare worse on various indicators of health than the general population and are considered vulnerable.1,5 A lack of access to French language services (FLSs) may be contributing to these health disparities,6 with 60% of Francophones reporting a complete absence of health-related FLS and only 34% having received FLS from any healthcare provider.7 Accessing health services in one’s preferred language is associated with better patient experiences,8 greater satisfaction9 and improved health outcomes.10 11 There is, therefore, an important need for Ontario’s six regional French Language Health Planning Entities (FLHPEs), which engage with the Francophone community and help coordinate local FLS,12 to understand the regional supply of and demand for FLS, a process known as health human resources (HHR) planning.13

Physician-to-population ratios are commonly used as a proxy indicator of supply and demand,14 with large ratios suggesting better access. However, such crude ratios have serious shortcomings that make them unsuitable for HHR planning of FLS. Specifically, access to language-concordant physician services for Francophones is often estimated by calculating ratios of French-speaking physicians (FSPs) per 1000 Francophones in a given geographic area and comparing these ratios to those of the general population (ie, non-FSPs (NFSPs) per 1000 non-Francophones).15,17 These crude FSP-to-Francophone ratios are predominantly larger than those for the general population, giving the impression that Francophones have better access to language-concordant care. However, these crude ratios do not account for the fact that FSPs also serve non-Francophone populations. According to the present policy in Ontario of first-come, first-served,18 Francophones must compete with the entire population to access FSPs. While this approach supports equality of access, it undermines equity, leaving many Francophones without primary care in their preferred language.

The objective of this study is to propose a more robust approach to calculating access to language-concordant primary care, which accounts for competition when accessing FSPs. Furthermore, differences in access between rural and urban areas, northern and southern areas and areas with varying densities of Francophone populations will be reported.

Methods

Study setting and context

Ontario was chosen for the present analysis as it is Canada’s most populous province (representing 38.5% of the total Canadian population),19 is home to the largest concentration of Francophones outside Quebec (52.7% of Francophones outside Quebec live in Ontario),19 and, although it is not officially bilingual, French language rights are protected under the French Language Services Act.20

Data sources and collection

The present study is a secondary analysis of data derived from publicly available sources, including population data from the 2021 Canadian census21 and physician data from the Physician Register on the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario’s (CPSO) public website.22 The CPSO is the body that regulates the practice of medicine in Ontario. Since we rely exclusively on information in the public domain for which there is no reasonable expectation of privacy,23 approval from the research ethics board was not required or sought.

Region classification

All analyses were conducted at the level of Statistics Canada’s 2021 census subdivisions (CSDs), which roughly correspond to municipalities (also referred to as communities). There are 577 CSDs in Ontario, which have been aggregated to allow for comparisons between broader geographic regions when presenting the results. Specifically, CSDs were classified based on their geographic location within the Ontario Health Regions (the North was represented by CSDs from the Northeast or Northwest Regions with the remaining regions representing the South), their size based on Statistics Canada’s Statistical Area Classification codes (with CSDs classified as Census Metropolitan Areas and Census Agglomerations representing urban areas and all other classifications considered rural24) and according to the Francophone presence within each CSD (<10%, 10%–24.9% and ≥25%). These classifications reflect those from past research in this field,15,17 allowing comparison between the present analyses and prior work.

Furthermore, to contribute to HHR planning in Ontario, access patterns are provided at the level of the six FLHPEs and at the level of the Local Health Integrated Networks (LHINs). Although the LHINs are no longer active, their boundaries represent meaningful subregions for the FLHPEs.

Population classification

The First Official Language Spoken (FOLS) is a variable derived by Statistics Canada by combining the knowledge of Canada’s two official languages, mother tongue and the language spoken most often at home.25 For the present analyses, Francophones were defined as those whose FOLS was French only. This group was chosen as they likely represent those with limited ability to speak English and thus would be more likely to need and desire health services in French. Those with a FOLS of English only, or of both French and English, represent Anglophones (as this suggests a capacity to speak English), while those with a FOLS that is neither French nor English represent Allophones. These two groups (Anglophones and Allophones) have been combined to represent the general non-Francophone population. An analysis of access to care in non-official languages is beyond the scope of the present study.

Physician classification

The present analysis focuses exclusively on physicians providing comprehensive family medicine care to the community (henceforth referred to as ‘family physicians’ (FPs)). FPs were identified using selection criteria that were iteratively developed through exploratory data analysis, consultation with the authors of previous studies15,1726 27 and expert input from our research team. Details on the exclusion criteria applied and the number of excluded physicians can be found in the results below. Next, physicians’ primary practice locations were converted from addresses to latitude/longitude coordinates, using Google’s geocoding service28 and the R package tidygeocoder,29 and combined to form the various regions described above (north/south, rural/urban, percentage of Francophone population, FLHPE and LHIN). Finally, physicians were classified based on their spoken languages as self-reported during their annual renewal and published on the CPSO website. FSPs were those who self-reported being competent enough to provide care in French, while NFSPs were those who did not report French as a language of competence.

Assumptions

The present analysis relies on the following assumptions. First, language-concordant care occurs when a patient is cared for by a physician who is competent in the patient’s official language of preference. For the purposes of the present study, a patient’s preferred language is assumed to correspond to their FOLS. For Francophones, care in French can only be received from a FSP. However, given that all physicians in Ontario must be able to practise in English, Anglophones/Allophones can receive language-concordant care (ie, care in English) from any physician. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, the official language of preference for non-Francophone patients is assumed to be English (ie, the dominant language). Thus, Anglophones/Allophones represent the general population, or those who, of the two official languages, are more likely to prefer their services in English based on their FOLS. Second, given Ontario’s policy of first-come, first-served,18 all Ontario residents are assumed to have an equal opportunity to access any physician in their region. Third, according to INSPIRE-PHC’s estimates as of September 2023,30 we assumed that 84% of Ontarians across the province were attached to a primary care physician. Finally, we assumed that FP rosters are representative samples of their regions; thus, in a region with 25% Francophones, each FP’s roster would be 25% Francophones, with the remaining patients representing Anglophones/Allophones.

Data analysis

Since both population and physician data represent entire populations (ie, the census contains data on all Ontario residents, and the CPSO data reflects all physicians licensed to practise in Ontario), inferential statistics were not required. Differences in access to language-concordant care are explored by comparing competition-adjusted ratios and calculating the probability of accessing language-concordant care.

Calculating competition-adjusted ratios

We began by calculating crude ratios of FSPs per 1000 Francophones and NFSPs per 1000 Anglophones/Allophones. However, given the limitations with crude ratios previously discussed, competition-adjusted ratios were calculated by comparing the ratio of FSPs per 1000 total population (which reflects the fact that Francophones must compete with Anglophones/Allophones to access FSPs) to the ratio of total physicians per 1000 total population (which reflects the fact that all physicians can provide care in English and the entire population is competing for access to these physicians). These adjusted ratios provide a more realistic representation of the patient’s experience with accessing care.7

Calculating the probability of accessing language-concordant care

Although ratios provide a quick account of potential access in a given area, even adjusted ratios of FSPs to total population are limited, as they do not account for the presence of NFSPs in a region. In other words, the more physicians there are in a region, regardless of their language competencies, the more patients can be served. Therefore, we next calculated the probability of receiving language-concordant care by accounting for the average number of attached Francophone and Anglophone/Allophone patients in a region (based on an 84% average attachment rate for Ontario in 202330) and the total number of FSP and NFSP. The steps outlined in table 1 were used to calculate probabilities. An example of how probability was calculated at the provincial level is available in online supplemental appendix A in the supplementary files.

Results

FP population

Data for all 42 163 physicians listed in the publicly available CPSO physician registry were extracted in January 2024. After applying the exclusion criteria detailed in figure 1, 14 754 FPs were identified. Of these, 1678 (11.4%) were considered FSPs, having self-identified French as a language of competence. The majority (FSP and NFSPs alike) had primary practice locations in urban communities of southern Ontario, where Francophones represent less than 10% of the total population (table 2). However, physicians located in rural, northern and Francophone-dense communities (ie, ≥25% Francophone population) were more likely to be competent in French.

Competition-adjusted ratios

Crude ratios of FSPs per 1000 Francophones (3.46 at the provincial level) are consistently more favourable than the ratio of NFSPs per 1000 Anglophones/Allophones (0.96) (table 2). However, the competition-adjusted ratios of FSPs per 1000 total population are far inferior (0.12 at the provincial level) to the ratios of total FPs per 1000 total population (1.05). These trends are consistent in all geographic groupings explored, including in the north and south, in rural and urban communities and at all levels of Francophone prevalence, as well as in every FLHPE region and every LHIN region (see table 4 in online supplemental appendix B). Furthermore, although crude ratios of FSPs per 1000 Francophones become less favourable as the proportion of Francophones in a given region grows, competition-adjusted ratios become more favourable.

Probability of accessing language-concordant care

Overall, of the 84% of patients attached to a FP, Francophones have an 11.4% probability of being attached to a FSP, compared with a 100% probability that an Anglophone/Allophone will be attached to an English-speaking physician (which is the case in all geographic regions throughout the province) (table 3). In other words, Anglophones/Allophones are 8.8 times more likely (ratio of language-concordant care probability) to receive language-concordant care (defined as being attached to a physician who is competent in their FOLS) than Francophones. The probability of language-concordant care in French is greater (and the ratio of language-concordant care probabilities is consequently smaller) in the north, in rural communities and in regions where proportionally more Francophones reside. Discussion

The novel competition-adjusted access ratios and probabilities introduced here provide a more accurate representation of access to language-concordant care for linguistic minority populations than traditional methods. Although crude ratios of FSPs per 1000 Francophones (3.46 overall) continue to suggest better access for this population, the adjusted ratio of FSP per 1000 population (0.12 overall) is far inferior to the general FP per 1000 population ratios (1.05 overall), suggesting Francophones in fact have worse access to language-concordant care than the general population. This finding corresponds to the Francophone experience when trying to access care in French in Ontario and in many other parts of Canada.7 8 This tendency persists in all regions of Ontario, but differences in potential access are particularly pronounced in the north, in urban communities and in regions where proportionally fewer Francophones reside, such as the region served by the FLHPE3 (representing the Toronto Central, Mississauga Halton and Central West LHINs).

The probability of accessing language-concordant care provides further evidence of the inequitable access to FLS. While 100% of attached Anglophones/Allophones are receiving language-concordant care (ie, care in English), only an estimated 11.4% of attached Francophones are potentially receiving care from a FSP (based on the present assumptions). Overall, Anglophones are 8.8 times more likely to access care in English than Francophones are to access care from an FSP.

From a health planning perspective, the competition-adjusted ratios, probabilities and ratios of language-concordant care probabilities differ greatly between regions. For instance, the lowest probability of accessing language-concordant care in French is found in the Erie St. Clair LHIN at 3.4% (ratio of probabilities: 29.6), whereas the highest probability is found in the Champlain LHIN at 40.7% (ratio of probabilities: 2.5). These findings provide health planners with a more accurate representation of access to FSPs in their region and clearly demonstrate how the realities of Francophone patients and challenges to FLS planning differ between regions.

The present results are consistent with prior research, confirming the inequitable access to language-concordant care in French in Ontario.15,1727 However, contrary to previous reports that Francophones have favourable physician-to-population ratios15, our competition-adjusted ratios and probability calculations demonstrate that Francophones indeed have worse access to language-concordant care throughout the province. Furthermore, past research has concluded that access to FSPs becomes worse as there are more Francophones in a region.15,17 Our present results support findings that the availability/presence of FSPs becomes worse as the percentage of Francophones increases, both in terms of raw numbers and crude FSP-to-Francophones ratios. However, potential access to these FSPs actually improves as the Francophone population grows, both in terms of competition-adjusted ratios and probability of accessing language-concordant care. This can be explained by the fact that there is less competition from Anglophones/Allophones when Francophones represent a larger proportion of the population. In other words, fewer FSPs can care for more Francophones when there is less competition from Anglophones/Allophones. Therefore, our present results suggest that FSPs may be more equitably distributed than originally thought. It should be noted, however, that, although this is a positive trend, the competition-adjusted FSP-to-population ratios are far less favourable than both the crude ratios from previous research and the general FP-to-population ratios presented here.

For the purpose of HHR planning, the average number of patients attached to a FP (ie, the average panel size) used to calculate probabilities can be adjusted to determine how many additional patients each physician in a region would need to take on to provide care to the entire population. Alternatively, while maintaining the average panel size, the number of additional physicians needed in a region to ensure adequate access can be estimated. Both approaches provide evidence of the level of unmet HHR needs and can be used to advocate for recruitment funding.

Applying an equity lens, the difference between competition-adjusted ratios and the ratio of language-concordant care probability is an indicator of access equity. Equity improves as the difference between the competition-adjusted ratios and the general population ratios approaches 0, and the ratio of language-concordant care probabilities approaches 1. Equity can be improved by matching Francophone patients to FSPs (which is the mission of Ontario’s Francophone Community Health Centres32 and should be the best practice standard in all group practice settings), by improving the supply of FSPs (which would require training more Francophone medical students or recruiting and recognising the credentials of internationally educated FSPs) or by providing all physicians (FSPs and NFSPs) with the skills and resources (including access to publicly funded professional interpretation services) needed to proactively identify and care for their linguistic minority patients, a concept known as the active offer of FLS.33 Unfortunately, access to publicly funded professional interpretation services tends to be rare in Ontario outpatient settings.34 Indeed, when there is low access to interpretive services in family practices, the availability of language-concordant physicians is even more important.

Limitations and future directions

Many assumptions made when conducting our analyses carry inherent limitations, which may be overcome in future studies. For instance, the ‘density’ approach used to measure healthcare access assumes that patients have equal access to all services within their region and that they only access services within their region. As such, we did not consider distance to services or that patients may travel to neighbouring regions to access FLS, which is particularly likely in the context of physician shortages.35 Therefore, we propose a complementary distance analysis that considers patients’ proximity to FSPs. We expect to further refine our methods of calculating access by incorporating this information and other factors affecting the ‘fragility-to-stability’ continuum of access to primary care.

Furthermore, the validity of the results obtained in the present study is limited by the accuracy of the data at our disposal. For instance, the physician’s language of competence is self-reported by physicians and is not an accurate representation of the language in which care is provided or of the physician’s actual level of competence. Furthermore, physician counts do not provide details on practice characteristics, including hours worked (ie, ‘full-time equivalent’ (FTE) status), number of patients rostered or whether physicians are accepting new patients. Additionally, population data from Statistics Canada do not allow us to determine the number of residents attached/unattached to a FP. Therefore, many of the assumptions made in the present analysis represent best-case scenarios of potential access to FLS (ie, all physicians are providing comprehensive primary care, are accepting new patients, are fully rostered and FSPs are providing FLS). However, we know this is not the case.36

While some physicians may also be engaged in administration, research or teaching, which would reduce the hours spent in direct patient care and consequently their panel size, others may provide full-time primary care and have a much larger panel size. Although we estimated panel size based on a provincial attachment rate of 84%, this is not a true representation of panel size, as it does not consider regional variations and would thus result in overestimations in some regions, such as urban areas where physicians may also work in an administrative or academic setting, and, potentially, underestimations in other regions, such as rural and northern communities where clinical workloads are heavier.36 37 In addition to measuring the number of physicians and patients, future HHR planning would benefit from determining the actual panel sizes of physicians, the number of physicians who are accepting new patients, the number of potential patients each physician is willing to take on and the number of individuals who are attached/unattached to a FP. Such information, in addition to the methods proposed in this paper, would provide a more accurate estimation of supply and demand for HHR modelling of FLS.

Conclusions

Although crude physician-to-population ratios remain the most commonly used method of measuring supply and demand,14 they are inadequate for estimating access to language-concordant care for languistic minority patients. The competition-adjusted ratios and probabilities proposed in this article better reflect the patient’s experience when seeking care7 8 and provide evidence of the extent to which Ontario Francophones are underserved while highlighting regions that are particularly at risk. Furthermore, although our present analyses do not explore access to language-concordant care for non-official language minority populations, the methods described herein can equally be applied to other language minority groups accessing other healthcare providers in other provinces or countries. These results have implications for HHR planning and could be used by policymakers and health planners to help provide equitable access to health services across the province and improve access to FLS where unmet needs are greatest.
