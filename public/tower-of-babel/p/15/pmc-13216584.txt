Predicting the evolution and persistence of COVID-19 using context-dependent polymorphisms
Authors: John Caraway, Way Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identifying common patterns of polymorphisms across SARS-CoV-2 variants is an essential part of tracking and preventing future pandemics. By examining the large-scale global sequencing effort of SARS-CoV-2, we find that polymorphisms in SARS-CoV-2 are context-dependent and significantly correlated across different variants, with neighboring nucleotides capable of altering polymorphism frequency by an average of 72-fold. Incorporating context-dependent patterns into evolutionary simulations improves the ability to predict polymorphisms in SARS-CoV-2 by 94%, and reveal relatively immutable regions in NSP3, NSP13, and the spike protein that are potential targets for gene therapy. Subdividing SARS-CoV-2 into Persistent and Transient variants reveal that Persistent variants carry an excess of unique polymorphisms in the hand domain of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (p = 0.001). Overall, our work highlights the importance of context-dependent polymorphisms in the evolution of the SARS-CoV-2 genome, associates genetic signatures with variant persistence, and identifies static regions and motifs that can be used to design long-lasting antivirals that rely on sequence specificity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46298-z.

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2, the virus responsible for the COVID-19 disease, is a respiratory virus that was first reported from Wuhan, in the Hubei province of China in December 20191, and rapidly spread worldwide. Between 2020 and 2021, roughly 80 million cases were reported across the globe and by the end of 2022, a total of 704.6 million cases were confirmed globally2. By 2023, based on estimates by the World Health Organization, an estimated 6.7 million people around the world died to COVID-19 related causes2. Over the course of the COVID-19 pandemic, multiple vaccines and antivirals have been developed. SARS-CoV-2 has continually evolved in response to these therapeutics, resulting in an evolutionary arms race which presents an ongoing challenge to limit the spread of this disease.

An enormous effort has been made to understand how SARS-CoV-2 has evolved to evade therapeutics and infect and spread in humans. High-throughput sequencing of different SARS-CoV-2 variants across the globe has generated a massive amount of data to study the evolution of coronaviruses, and SARS-CoV-2 sequencing data and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for each evolved variant have been deposited into large databases such as GISAID3,4. However, outside of a handful of fixed mutations that have arisen in the spike proteins, there remains a limited amount of analysis that has been performed on the spectrum of SNPs in SARS-CoV-2, how to predict the evolutionary trajectory of future variants, and what SNPs may enable variants to persist within the human population.

In this work, we analyze SNP data from ~ 13.4 million SARS-CoV-2 sequences across 14 different variants over a period of ~ 4.5 years. We identify nucleotide contexts where low-frequency and high-frequency SNPs are most abundant, SNP hotspots and deserts where vaccine design and antiviral targeting would be most efficient, and highly specific SNPs that are overrepresented in variants that persist in human populations.

Methods

SARS-CoV-2 datasets and persistence values

The data used in this study are SNPs and metadata from the primary variants (α, β, δ, e, h, g, i, k, l, µ, o, HK.3, Pirola, Kraken, Table 1) of SARS-CoV-2 based on WHO-designations for Variants of Concerns (VoCs) and using clade naming conventions from NEXTstrain5. For this analysis, omicron (o) and its sub-lineages (HK.3, Pirola, and Kraken) are treated as independent and non-overlapping variants, and their sub-variant designations are XBB.1.1.529, BA.2.86, and XBB.1.5 respectively. Data for each variant was accessed from CoV-Spectrum6 during the period where most of the large-scale sequencing was being deposited into the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data (GISAID)3 and the National Center for Biotechnological Information (NCBI)7. CoV-Spectrum is an aggregation and visualization tool that allows easy access to the data from SARS-CoV-2 genomes deposited into either database.

In order to generate the data used in this study, strain names were entered into CoV-Spectrum and all single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were downloaded from January 2020 through June 2024 (last access date: June 21, 2024). This data from GISAID contained information regarding the SNP position with respect to the original reference genome (GISAID sequence: EPI_ISL_402124), which is the first high-quality complete genome assembled for SARS-CoV-23. Sites that contained SNPs that differed from the original reference genome, and the proportion of the sequences that contained a SNP in the sequencing data at that site were used in this analysis3,4,6. To remove false positives based on sequencing and alignment errors, we required that a minimum of 10 sequences indicated that the site contained the same SNP and that SNPs were found at a minimum allele frequency (MAF) of 5 × 10− 5. At this MAF threshold, 10 out of 2 × 105 sequences that differed from the reference genome would be required to call a SNP.

There is a large disparity in the number of sequences for each variant, ranging from ~ 8 × 103 sequences in kappa to ~ 6.7 × 106 sequences in omicron (Table 1). The presence of VoCs in the human population overlap in time (Table 1) and makes it difficult to categorize VoCs into Persistent and Transient variants. Since the beginning of the pandemic there have been a number of prominent VoCs, including Alpha, Beta, Delta, Gamma, and Omicron (along with its subvariants Kraken and Pirola). Alpha, Beta, Delta, and Gamma span a similar time frame (October, 2020 – June, 2022), and Omicron, and its subvariants, have been the most widespread SARS-CoV-2 variant since Delta.

The highly sequenced VoC Alpha was the first dominant VoC in February, 2021, accounting for over 95% of COVID-19 cases. Soon after Beta and Gamma were identified in South Africa and Brazil respectively, and were the major variants infecting the population in those countries8. It could be argued that sequencing efforts in different global regions may bias the categorization of Persistent and Transient strains. For instance, Beta was not common in the U.S. where large-scale sequencing efforts were undergoing, and thus, sequencing numbers for Beta would be lower. However, Beta was never observed in a large proportion of the human population (2.50%) outside of South Africa. Gamma which was extremely prevalent in the Brazilian population, accounting for ~ 10% of all COVID-19 cases was rapidly replaced by the Delta variant9. Thus, while both Beta and Gamma were extremely dangerous variants, neither reached the global maximum population frequency that was observed in the Persistent strains (> 50% of COVID-19 cases in human population).

Given the large disparity in the sampling and severity of different VoCs across the globe, we relied on deposited sequences to determine variant persistence. To calculate variant persistence, we multiplied the maximum frequency of the variant in the human population (as determined by CoV-Spectrum metadata) by the length of time where greater than 10 sequences were submitted to the sequence database over three consecutive weeks (Supp. Fig. S1, Supp. Table S1). We correlated the number of sequences for each variant with the persistence value of the variant (Supp. Fig. S2, p = 8.47 × 10− 7), and find that they are significantly correlated, consistent with the idea that the most frequent variant in the entire human population would be the most likely variant to be sequenced. The five variants that had enough sequencing data to meet the sequence number requirement had persistence values > 250 (α, δ, o, Pirola, Kraken, Table 1), so they were designated as Persistent. The remaining nine variants did not have enough sequencing data to meet the sequence number requirement and had persistence values < 50 (β, e, h, g, i, k, l, µ, Table 1). These nine variants were denoted as Transient variants, and SNP data for each of the Transient variants was pooled and analyzed as a single variant with the additional requirement that SNPs must be present in at least three variants. This ensures that SNPs are not biased towards Transient variants that have more sequences than others in the pool of nine and also removes SNPs that can result from sequencing errors. We note that this approach does remove some SNPs (9492/22687 of SNPs with allele frequency > 5 × 10− 5), namely those that appear at low frequency within Transient variants with low sequence counts. The context-dependent polymorphisms (CDP) of Transient variants with or without the 9492 discarded SNPs were highly correlated (Supp. Fig. S3, ρ = 0.976, p = 1.81 × 10− 95), indicating that the discarded SNPs did not significantly alter the spectrum of analyzed SNPs.

SARS-CoV-2 context-dependent single nucleotide polymorphism frequency

To ensure that our analysis examines SNPs that are expected to be driven by neutral forces10–12, we restricted our analysis to SNPs that arise in 4-fold degenerate sites (4125 sites, Supp. Table S2). Mutations that arise in 4-fold degenerate sites do not result in an amino acid change and are less likely to be under strong selective pressure13–15. In addition, we removed any 4-fold sites that are in overlapping genes in the SARS-CoV-2 genome as they would only be a 4-fold site in one of the open reading frames. 9044 sites are known to be in overlapping reading frames in the SARS-CoV-2 genome (1b, 3b, 7b, 9b, and 9c), and these sites were excluded from analysis (29.5% of the genome, Supp. Table S2), and the remaining dataset contained 3038 analyzable 4-fold degenerate sites.

For each variant, SNPs at 4-fold degenerate sites were categorized into triplets by the SNP type and the immediate 5’ upstream nucleotide and 3’ downstream nucleotide. This generates a matrix in which the rows represent the neighboring nucleotides, and the columns represent a SNP, resulting in context-dependent polymorphisms. For example, the intersection of 5’-G[X > Y]A-3’ and C > U in the matrix indicates a context-dependent polymorphisms from C to U that is preceded by a G on the 5’ end and followed by an A on the 3’ end. It is important to note that there are no 5’-A[X > Y]N-3’ contexts, where N is any nucleotide, as all these contexts are 2-fold degenerate sites.

This results in a 12 × 12 CDP matrix for each SARS-CoV-2 variant that excludes any SNP that is preceded by an A (Fig. 1B). Codon usage bias can affect the number of 4-fold sites that can be analyzed at each triplet, so we normalized the number of CDPs at 4-fold sites by the total number of that context at 4-fold sites in the genome to get the conditional frequency of that CDP (Supp. Table S2).\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{CDPF}_{context}=\:\frac{\sum\:{CDP}_{context}}{\sum\:context}$$\end{document}

In addition to polymorphic data from GISAID, we analyzed mutation data from direct assays of SARS-CoV-2 in vitro16. In this assay, Circle-Seq was used to sequence α, β, δ, g, o and USA (variant designation − 19B) SARS-CoV-2 variants which have accumulated mutations in a laboratory setting. Of these, α, δ, and USA consisted of two replicates sequenced over seven serial passages while β, g, and o consisted of one replicate sequenced a single time. Mutation data was analyzed for each variant independently, as well as all together (total) and a context-dependent mutation (CDM) matrix was generated for each variant (Supp. Table S3). Note that these CDM matrices include 5’-A[X > Y]N-3’ contexts that are absent in the CDP matrices as the CDM assays are not limited to 4-fold degenerate sites.

Following the authors’ approach16, we used the mutation counts and the coverage threshold to calculate the conditional rate for each context. Mutations were categorized as lethal based upon the authors’ designations: (1) premature stop of the transcript or (2) absence of the mutation in SARS-CoV-2 mutation databases: UShER and Ensembl, which would suggest the mutation is unobserved in population data and assumed to be lethal. The minimum sequencing coverage required to consider a lethal mutation was 1, whereas non-lethal mutations required a coverage of at least 1000. This criterion ensures that the mutations are either new lethal mutations or mutations that are not driven to high frequency through natural selection. CDM analysis was performed on the lethal, non-lethal, and combined datasets.

SARS-CoV-2 simulations performance and benchmarking

Three types of simulations were run in which the mutation parameters of the model were adjusted. The Naïve model consists of four parameters indicating the likelihood of a nucleotide to mutate to any other nucleotide (e.g., A > N). The Transition/Transversion (Ts/Tv) model incorporates the type of nucleotide change (e.g., A > U) of a mutation increasing the number of parameters to 12. The contextual model additionally includes the neighboring nucleotides resulting in a 144-parameter model (e.g., CAG > CTG). In addition to model type, we have two distinct datasets in which we can predict mutations: CDP matrices derived from population data (Supp. Table S4) and CDM matrices from in vitro data (Supp. Table S3). Despite CDM analysis having A[X-> Y]N information, these rows were removed from the matrix when simulating to create parity between the CDP and CDM simulation performance.

To identify regions where mutations are likely to occur, 100 simulations were run for each model type and dataset combination. The starting position of the simulation in the SARS-CoV-2 genome was randomized to remove bias towards placing SNPs at sites earlier in the genome. From the starting position, the simulation calculated a random number, compared this random number to the per-site frequencies from each model, and if the random number exceeded the per-site frequency, placed a polymorphism. Polymorphisms were placed along the genome until 1000 analyzable polymorphisms were reached (polymorphisms at sites with 5’A cannot be compared so they are discarded, Supp. Table S2). Due to the size of the SARS-CoV-2 genome (29,900 bp), placing more than 1000 mutations did not increase simulation accuracy (Supp. Fig. S4). Each simulation resulted in a list of polymorphic sites with the original and final states.

To analyze the performance of each model, simulated SNPs were compared to known SNPs within the population. SNPs at sites preceded by 5’A sites were removed from analysis because the CDP model which uses 4-fold degenerate sites cannot predict SNPs at these sites (~ 30% of SNPs removed). Model accuracy was determined by whether the simulation could predict (1) whether a SNP arose at that site (positional match), and (2) whether the SNP was the correct type (contextual match).

The same exact simulation procedures were used to simulate mutations in the genome, except per-site mutation rates from in vitro experiments were used instead of per-site frequency data from sequencing of natural populations of SARS-CoV-2.

Code availability

The data that support the findings of this study are openly available at CoV-Spectrum6, GISAID3, and NCBI7. The code used in this study to calculate the polymorphism frequency, mutation frequency, and run simulations are available on github: https://github.com/Jwcaraway77/cov_context_dependence.

Results

Context-dependent polymorphisms across SARS-CoV-2 variants

SARS-CoV-2 is a positive-strand single-stranded RNA (+ ssRNA) virus which replicates by first creating a whole genome negative-strand RNA template (-ssRNA). The -ssRNA template is translated into +ssRNA copies17(Fig. 1A). Viruses of the order Nidovirales to which SARS-CoV-2 belongs employ a discontinuous viral transcription process to create -ssRNA templates of the open reading frames within the genome18–20. Synthesis is performed by the complex of non-structural protein (NSPs) 7,8, and 12 (RNA dependent RNA polymerase), and supported by NSPs 10,13,14, and 16 which assist in proofreading, fusing, and regulation20–22.

CDP matrices were generated for each variant as well as three groups of pooled variants: All, Persistent, and Transient (Supp. Fig. S5). Each CDP matrix was significantly correlated with each other indicating that there was a single distinct CDP profile across all variants when including the most prevalent C > U and G > A mutations (Fig. 1B; Supp. Fig. S3A, ρ > 0.90; Supp. Table S5, p < 3.76 × 10− 52 for all comparisons), and when excluding them (Supp. Fig. S6, ρ > 0.85; Supp. Table S6, p < 2.16 × 10− 34 for all comparisons). Given the similarity in spectra across variants, downstream analyses were performed using the All matrix which includes data from all 14 VoCs.

The SNP with the highest frequency was C > U regardless of the context. Across all variants, a C > U SNP was observed at 399/407 (98.03%) sites. C > A and C > G polymorphisms are much less frequent than C > U polymorphisms, which is consistent with that observed in direct measurements of mutation rates in SARS-CoV-2 variants (Supp. Table S4). The complementary SNP G > A also occurred frequently at 139/190 (73.16%) sites across all variants.

Both C and G sites in the genome frequently changed to U, as G > U SNPs were observed at 159/190 (83.68%) sites across all variants. However, the complementary SNP C > A only arose at 74/407 (18.18%) sites. If the contextual errors that arise while synthesizing each RNA strand were identical, we would expect the exact same frequency of polymorphisms at every context of complementary polymorphisms. However, we see that this is not the case. G > U SNPs occur ~ 4.6-fold (prop.test, p < 2.2 × 10− 16) more frequently than C > A SNPs regardless of context.

U > G, U > A, G > C, C > G, A > U, and A > C polymorphisms were generally absent from the genome. On average, SNPs are only found in 5% of these sites regardless of context. From the substitution types we can observe that transitions (Ts) are much more frequent than transversions (Tv) at 4-fold sites in SARS-CoV-2 with an average Ts/Tv ratio of ~ 2.289 across the variants analyzed (Supp. Table S7).

We collapsed the matrix to examine the influence of upstream and downstream nucleotides independently and observe that U > C SNPs occur more frequently when flanked by a C on either side (Fig. 2, yellow). Other extreme contextual effects of upstream and downstream nucleotides are apparent in G > A SNPs, with those flanked by a 5’C being ~ 1.6 times more likely than those with 5’U (0.94 vs. 0.6, Fig. 2A, green). Additionally, G > A SNPs are also ~ 3 fold more frequent when followed by a G rather than an A (0.91 vs. 0.35, Fig. 2B, green).

Other types of polymorphisms show less of an effect from neighboring nucleotides. While A > G SNPs are highly influenced by the downstream 3’ nucleotide, changing the upstream nucleotide seems to have little effect on the frequency of polymorphisms. A > G SNPs occur ~3x more frequently when followed by a U instead of C or A (0.75 vs. 0.25 or 0.24, Fig. 2B, blue and is consistent with the overall elevation SNP frequencies at A > G sites with 3’U (Figs. 1 and 2). The opposite effect can be observed in G > U SNPs which vary more in frequency depending on the 5’ nucleotide but change little with respect to different 3’ nucleotides (Fig. 2, red).

CDP versus CDM

The single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) described up to this point are mutations that arise in SARS-CoV-2 and are maintained within the viral population through natural selection or genetic drift. Mutations that arise at four-fold degenerate sites do not change the amino acid sequence, so they are expected to be under limited selective constraint12,15,23. SNPs at these sites are often used as a proxy for evaluating the rate of molecular evolution and developing a rough framework of the mutation spectra. However, mutations that arise in viruses are expected to be under extremely stringent selective constraints from both antivirals and antibodies, and selection at four-fold degenerate sites has been shown to be present in some capacity in all organisms across the tree of life14,15,24, so it may be expected the spectra of SNPs do not reflect the true mutation spectra in viruses.

Fortunately, recent studies which directly sequence SARS-CoV-2 genomes immediately after replication using CIRseq (16), have provided a unique opportunity to compare SNP frequencies that are maintained in the natural population maintained over a long period of time against direct estimates of mutation that arise during viral replication. This comparison will provide a unique opportunity to examine whether context-dependent polymorphisms (CDPs) can be used as a proxy for context-dependent mutation (CDM) spectra in viral organisms. We compared the All CDPs at four-fold degenerate sites in the natural population against 89,709 CDMs from CIRseq and find a significant positive correlation between CDPs and CDMs (Supp. Table S8, ρ = 0.609, p = 3.73 × 10− 16), with or without the dominant C > U mutation type (without C > U mutations, ρ = 0.612, p = 1.62 × 10− 15). The spectrum of CDPs did not correlate well with the spectrum of lethal mutations (15469/89709 lethal, ρ = 0.132, p = 0.115), which is consistent with the idea that lethal mutations will not be observed in population SNP data.

To identify which contexts are driving the relationship between CDP and CDM, we used the All CDP matrix to simulate mutations within the SARS-CoV-2 genome (see methods), and then used the simulation to derive an All CDM matrix. Bootstrap correlations were performed between the CDP and All CDM matrix and we found that the log-log correlation coefficient was 0.570 (+/- 0.005, 95% CI, 100 iterations, Supp. Table S9, log values of zero were assigned 1 × 10− 5, 10-times lower than the lowest observed polymorphism frequency). Using contextual patterns had the highest average correlation between All CDP and All CDM matrices (Supp. Table S9, Context-dependent − 0.570, Ts/Tv – 0.44, Naïve – −0.055), and correlations were robust even when G > A and C > U mutations were removed. Certain contexts showed stark contrast between CDP and CDM, with high mutation rates of C[G > A]C and G[G > C]U in in vitro assays and high observed frequency of U[U > C]U polymorphisms in natural populations (Supp. Fig. S7).

Being able to predict where polymorphisms arise in SARS-CoV-2 is a key part of vaccine development and therapeutics. Most models focus on predicting new polymorphisms that rise to fixation in the spike protein25–27, relying on a limited number of known fixed mutations in the spike. We improve upon these methods by incorporating population SNP data to model the underlying mutation patterns driving site-specific changes. Using this method, we identify regions where we expect low or high SNP frequencies, and contrast this to SNPs from observed population data.

For each evolutionary model (naive, TSTV, context-dependent) 1000 SNPs were placed using per-site frequencies from the All variant spectra, and simulated over 100 iterations (see Methods, Fig. 1B). We used a 90% confidence interval over a sliding window of 500 base pairs (see methods) to identify regions with significantly lower and higher SNP density in the simulations, and found five of these regions with significantly higher simulated SNP densities (q, t,w, x,z) and five regions with significantly lower simulated SNP density (r, s,u, v,y).

We compared the simulated SNP frequency with the observed SNP frequency in the population (All variant, Fig. 3D). We find three regions where the simulation and the observed SNP frequencies in the population are concordant. Regions q and z have high frequencies in both the simulation and the observed data, and region u has low frequencies in both the simulation and observed data.

The simulated SNP frequency is in contrast with the observed frequency in the All variant for regions t and v. Region t is predicted to have a significantly higher SNP frequency in simulations but has a significantly lower SNP frequency in the observed data. Oppositely, region v is predicted to have a significantly lower SNP frequency in simulation but has significantly higher SNP frequencies in the population data in this region. The remaining regions expected to deviate in SNP frequency based on simulations (r,s,w,x,y), do not have significantly higher or lower observed SNP frequency in the All variant.

In addition, we examined all regions where the allele frequency of SNPs in the population (All variant) exceeded a 90% confidence interval using a 100 bp sliding window and merging regions with a gap size fewer than 250 bps (Supp. Table S10). We find a region where > 40% of the region has SNPs that are significantly elevated in SNP frequency, but the number of SNPs in that region is not elevated when compared to the rest of the genome. This region is in the spike protein and starts slightly upstream from region v and encapsulates region v (v1, 22499–24029, Supp. Table S10).

High frequency polymorphisms in the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase associated with variant persistence

The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) is a highly conserved gene in SARS-CoV-2 as it is responsible for synthesis of the +ssRNA strand. SNPs in the RdRp binding domains could alter site-specific mutation rates or replication efficiency as the SNPs could change the binding efficiency of RdRp to different contexts. All SARS-CoV-2 variants are known to have a well-described mutation in the interface domain of the RdRp which differentiates itself from other Coronaviruses (C14408U, Supp. Table S11).

The RdRp of SARS-CoV-2 has three primary components: (1) nidovirus RdRp-associated nucleotidyltransferase (NiRAN), (2) interface, and (3) hand (palm, fingers, thumb). The NiRAN is a highly conserved region among nidoviruses and has been shown to be vital for mRNA capping and aiding RNA synthesis, as well as potentially functioning as an RNA ligase10. The interface subdomain connects the NiRAN to the hand domain and serves as an interaction site for NSP8. Strand synthesis is performed by the hand subdomain which also interacts with NSPs 7 and 8 to stabilize the structure.

When we examine the RdRp of Persistent and Transient variants, we find five nonsynonymous and four synonymous SNPs in the hand domain of variants with high persistence values (α, δ, Kraken, o, Pirola, Table 1). This is a significant excess of high-frequency SNPs (allele frequency > 30%) in the hand region of the RdRp in Persistent variants (finger, palm, thumb, Fig. 4), whether we consider unique or shared polymorphisms (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.00078; Supp. Table S12). Chronologically, every new Persistent variant contains a unique fixed mutation within the hand domain (Fig. 4). G670G was first observed in δ and arose again in kraken and HK.3 either through recombination or a new mutation. N600I and L758L are found at low frequency in Omicron and L758L rose to high frequency within Kraken, Pirola, and HK.3. None of the Transient variants contained a SNP in the hand domain with an allele frequency > 30%. Outside of C14408U, none of the dominant variants had a SNP in either the interface or the NiRAN.

Among the Transient variants and HK.3, we identify four SNPs in the NiRAN, three of which are fixed nonsynonymous changes, while the other synonymous SNP was observed at ~ 30% allele frequency (Supp. Table S12). The SNPs in the NiRAN are all found in variants with low persistence values (e, g, l, Table 1). At the time of writing, HK.3 is known to be a sub-lineage of Omicron and is unlikely to be a dominant variant when compared to Pirola and Kraken.

Discussion

In this work, we analyze the SNPs present within a large-scale dataset of SARS-CoV-2 variants to study the evolution and persistence of COVID-19 in human populations. First, we use data from the massive sequencing effort of SARS-CoV-2 to explore how contextual patterns can drive viral evolution. Second, we subdivide SARS-CoV-2 variants into Persistent and Transient VoCs in an effort to uncover SNP patterns that may be related to strain persistence within human populations. Finally, we simulate the evolution of SARS-CoV-2 using advanced models to identify regions of the SARS-CoV-2 genome that can be used as targets of viral therapy.

Context-dependent polymorphism (CDP) Spectra

Four-fold degenerate SNPs were categorized based upon their local 5’ and 3’ sequence context and normalized by their context count in the genome to generate the conditional CDP spectra (Fig. 1). When we examine the CDP spectra of different VoCs, we find two interesting patterns. First, we find that the context can alter site-specific SNP frequencies, altering SNP frequency by an average of 37.28% across all SNP types (33.03% if we exclude contexts where no SNPs were observed, Supp. Table S13). Second, Persistent and Transient SARS-CoV-2 variants exhibit very similar contextual patterns (Supp. Fig. S5). This suggests that the evolutionary patterns in SARS-CoV-2 are not changing drastically over time, and that the persistence of SARS-CoV-2 variants is not strongly associated with SNP types (Supp. Fig. S3). G > U, U > C, C > U, and G > A dominate the distribution of SNPs and indicate that reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative damage play a large factor in the types of SNPs that arise in SARS-CoV-2. Oxidized cytosines in single-strand RNA form a uracil derivative that can be misread during synthesis, leading to a C > U transition mutation, and is a pattern commonly observed in bacteria28–31. Deamination of adenine forms hypoxanthine, which preferably pairs with C nucleotides resulting in a A > G (or U > C) SNP after replication. G > U (and less commonly G > A) SNPs have been suggested to increase in frequency in SARS-CoV-2 due to the inability of RdRp to synthesize past 8-oxo-rG lesions28, resulting in SNPs at G sites. Oxidation and deamination of SARS-CoV-2 occurs often during its lifespan and interaction with hosts and can be particularly important in the evolution of this virus. First, deamination of Cs can arise through the host-immune response of the mRNA editing enzymes in the APOBEC family. APOBEC1, APOBEC3A, and APOBEC3G convert cytosine to uracil which can prematurely stop replication or alter the stability of the RNA structure28,32,33, resulting in C > U SNPs in the invading virus. Second, SARS-CoV-2 has been linked to the formation of ROS in the cytokines of the host in multiple ways. ORF7a in SARS-CoV-2 has been suggested to induce cytokine storms that rupture the cell to release viral replicates21,28,34, and NSP1 in SARS-CoV-1 has been linked to increased cytokine activity because of the NF-kB antiviral pathway35. These host-pathogen interactions increase the amount of ROS that SARS-CoV-2 is exposed to and are consistent with the SNP types that are observed through the VoCs.

Contextual effects can compound with exogenous mutagenic effects to further increase SNP frequency. The SNPs that are most affected by local sequence context are U > C, G > U, G > A, A > G. G > U SNP frequency is primarily influenced by the 5’ nucleotide (Fig. 2, fold-change 1.26×), while A > G SNP frequency is primarily influenced by the 3’ nucleotide (Fig. 2, fold-change: 3.10×). U > C SNP frequency is heavily influenced by flanking C nucleotides, either upstream or downstream (Figs. 1, 2 and 5’ fold-change: 1.68×, 3’ fold-change: 1.81×, combined fold-change: 3.48×). G > A SNP frequency is influenced by nucleotides on both 5’ and 3’ nucleotides (Figs. 1, 2 and 5’ fold-change: 1.56×, 3’ fold-change: 2.59×, combined fold-change: 5.80×). The frequency of all SNPs involved with exogenous mutagenic effects are highly affected by local-sequence context except for C > U. C > U nucleotides which dominate both the CDP and CDM spectra do not appear to be heavily influenced by local sequence context. Nevertheless, it is difficult to ascertain any contextual influence as C > U sites are saturated with SNPs across all variants and in direct mutation assays (Fig. 1).

We note that some contexts have extremely low SNP frequency and a non-zero number of contexts (Fig. 1). As expected, the frequency of SNPs that can be observed at triplets that involve a stop codon is rare. Only one U[A > C]N SNP is observed at 183 contexts across all VoCs (All), and in vitro mutation data16 also observes a low mutation rate at the same site (7.27 × 10− 6 per site per generation, Supp. Table S3). UAA is the most used stop codon in the SARS-CoV-2 genome (73.33% of genes, 11/15). Motifs that differ between observed SNP frequencies and in vitro mutation data could arise from a number of different evolutionary processes including positive selection, amino acid constraint, or selection at silent sites.

Overall, G > U, C > U, U > C, and G > A SNP types are extremely prevalent across all VoCs and these SNP types are consistent with RNA damage in viruses commonly associated with oxidation and deamination. We find that the 5’ nucleotide has the largest effect on the frequency of the polymorphism, with highly frequent G > A SNPs affected by local-sequence context on both 5’ to 3’ sides and vary largely depending on the context. SNPs that are infrequent in SARS-CoV-2 also rarely mutate in in vitro data (e.g., U[A > C]N), consistent with the idea that mutations at these sites are extremely deleterious and are prime targets for gene therapy.

SNPs vs. mutation

SNPs in SARS-CoV-2 variants are subject to natural selection during evolution, with 4-fold degenerate sites expected to be subject to less natural selection than other sites. However, selection at 4-fold sites can occur, and has been shown to influence SNP frequency at 4-fold sites in different organisms14,23.

To examine the effect of selection on 4-fold sites in SARS-CoV-2 we contrasted CDP spectra with context-dependent mutation (CDM) spectra obtained from CIRseq sequencing of SARS-CoV-2 variants. Mutation data was split into three groups: lethal, non-lethal, and combined (see methods). Both the non-lethal and combined CDM spectra correlate with the All CDP spectra (Supp. Fig. S8; Supp. Table S8, non-lethal: ρ = 0.609, p = 5.53 × 10− 16, combined: ρ = 0.612, p = 3.73 × 10− 16), supporting the idea that CDP can be used as a proxy for direct CDM assays. As expected, the CDM spectra does not correlate well with the CDP spectra when including lethal sites (Supp. Fig. S8; Supp. Table S8, L: ρ = 0.132, p = 0.115) consistent with the fact that lethal mutations will not be observed in population data. Together, we conclude that 4-fold sites in large-scale population viral datasets can be used as a proxy for direct mutation studies, and that the spectra of lethal mutations that could be used as potential therapeutic targets are anticorrelated with the CDP spectra.

Model improvement when incorporating nucleotide context

Most evolutionary models do not incorporate neighboring nucleotide context when simulating polymorphisms, instead only incorporating transition/transversion biases. Recent natural language processing tools to analyze evolutionary patterns have begun introducing contextual patterns into models indirectly at the amino acid level25–27,36. Transformers and large language models can potentially map larger regions of nonlinear epistasis across the genome. Despite this, many of these models are used to predict future VoCs based only on amino acid changes within the spike protein. Our approach instead is to analyze and predict the full breadth of polymorphisms that arise across the entire SARS-CoV-2 genome at silent sites, incorporating information directly from mutational input.

Ability to simulate incoming mutations

Using the SNP frequencies from the All CDP and the CDM matrix, we simulated the evolution of SARS-CoV-2 using different models to determine if including additional parameters improved performance (Naïve, Ts/Tv, and contextual). To create parity between the different types of simulations, polymorphisms preceded by a 5’A were removed from positional and contextual match analysis (see Methods). The context-dependent model resulted in a 94% increase in the number of SNPs predicted when compared to the Naïve model, and a 10% increase when compared to the Transition/Transversion (Ts/Tv) model (MAF 5 × 10− 5). At MAF 5 × 10− 5 the CDP matrix was able to recover ~ 3.4% more polymorphisms when compared to the CDM matrix (Supp. Fig. S9B). Overall, the more complex All CDP matrix performed better than simpler Naïve and Ts/Tv models and also performed slightly better than using direct mutation parameters from the CDM matrix. This suggests that most polymorphisms are determined by mutational parameters, and that with sufficient data, four-fold degenerate sites can be used to accurately model these parameters in viruses.

Simulated regions of interest

We further analyzed the observed SNP data from the population for individual variants and pooled variants that had enough sequences to perform our thresholding (see methods). The frequency of observed SNPs was calculated by averaging the allele frequency of all SNPs present within a sliding 100 bp window for each variant (Supp. Fig. S10). Additionally, the count of observed SNPs was derived instead by averaging the total number of SNPs present within the same windows (Supp. Fig. S11).

Differences between simulated SNP frequencies and actual SNP frequencies in the population data can provide insight into the evolutionary forces operating on different regions of the SARS-CoV-2 genome. Based on the simulation, there are 10 regions within the SARS-CoV-2 genome that are predicted to have a significant excess (q, t, w, x, and z) or significant scarcity (r, s u, v and y) of SNPs (CI = 90, Fig. 3). Of these regions, five of them (q,r,s,t, and u) are in polyproteins ORF1ab which accounts for ~ 70% of the genome. From population SNP data, we observe a significant excess of SNPs in regions q, v, and z, and a significant dearth of SNPs in t and u.

Neutral SNPs that do not have a large impact on SARS-CoV-2 fitness will not be removed through purifying selection. Thus, simulated regions predicted to have high SNP frequencies that are in accordance with regions of high SNP frequencies in the population are regions where there is likely to be limited purifying selection (+/+, Fig. 3C). Both regions q and z, predicted to have an excess of SNPs in simulated data, have a high frequency of SNPs in population data. Regions q and z contain parts of NSP1, NSP2, ORF8, and N proteins. NSP1 is responsible for inhibiting host cell protein synthesis and antiviral signaling. While NSP1 is well conserved between SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 (86% identity), this protein is quite varied between the alpha and beta coronaviruses37–39. NSP2 inhibits cell antiviral signaling and maintains the integrity of the cell during viral replication21,40. ORF8 interacts with proteins IRF3 and MHC-1 proteins to avoid detection from the cell and antibodies that may be present from past infections or vaccination. The nucleocapsid (N) is vital for replication as it encapsulates the viral RNA to stabilize the sequence. Signaling and caspid proteins may be less critical for viral survival and thus are likely to be under relaxed purifying selection.

Regions with a low simulated SNP frequency and a low observed SNP frequency are either regions that are under limited selective constraint or regions that are optimized for replication efficacy and under strong selective constraint (-/-, Fig. 3D). Region u, spanning part of the RdRp and NSP13, both contain a low frequency of observed SNPs. NSP13 is a helicase which is responsible for producing single-stranded RNA from double-stranded DNA. It has been noted that this protein is vital for viral persistence, is a target for antivirals, and is highly conserved across VoCs (600/601 = 99.83%)21,41. The importance of this region for SARS-CoV-2 survival suggests that this region has been optimized for replication efficacy and is under strong selective constraint.

Deleterious SNPs that are detrimental SARS-CoV-2 fitness will be selected against and removed through purifying selection. As a result, regions that are predicted to have high SNP frequencies in simulations but have low SNP frequencies in the population are regions that should be under strong purifying selection (+/-). This pattern can be observed in region t, which spans from the final 3 amino acids of NSP9 through NSP10, NSP11, and the first 35 amino acids of NSP12 (NiRAN region of the RdRp). NSP10 is a multifunctional protein, used to protect against the host cell’s antiviral response proteins, regulating NSP14 which is a proofreader, and assists NSP16 in camouflaging viral RNA as the host cell’s human RNA21. Given the importance of the RdRp and host cell antiviral interactions, it is unsurprising that there are large amounts of purifying selection in this region. In addition, our results show that the SNPs in the first 35 amino acids of the RdRp are absent from Persistent VoCs, consistent with the idea that mutations in this region are likely to be removed by natural selection (Fig. 4A).

In contrast, regions that have low SNP frequencies in simulations but have high SNP frequency in the population are likely to be under strong positive selection (-/+, Fig. 3D). This can be observed in region v, which spans the tail of subdomain-1 (SD1), the furin cleavage site (FCS), and the beginning of subdomain-2 (SD2) (amino acids 655:859). It has been shown that altering the FCS impacts the efficacy of the spike protein interaction with ACE2 in human lung cells42. Given the importance of the spike protein for virality and the strong selective pressure of antibodies and vaccines, it would follow that the spike is under immense selective pressure to evolve.

Region v does not encompass the RBD (spike amino acids 318:541), which is a common target for vaccine development21. as SARS-CoV-2 has evolved a highly specialized RBD to interact with ACE2 and enter the cell43. When the RBD gene is analyzed separately, there is a significant excess of high-frequency SNPs (MAF > 0.9, 15/669, p = 1.06 × 10− 7, binomial test, Supp. Table S14) within the population. This region v1 encapsulates region v and is consistent with strong purifying selection raising the allele frequency of SNPs in this region.

Finally, there are a few confounding regions that are flagged as significantly elevated or depressed in only the simulation or observed SNP frequency. Regions r and s within NSP3 predict a low SNP frequency (-/=) and regions x (+/=) and y (-/=) that contain the envelope (E) and membrane (M) proteins vary largely in expected SNP frequency (Fig. 3A, D). None of these regions have significant changes in SNP frequency within the population data (r, s, x, and y, Fig. 3D). This discrepancy might be attributed to the low GC content of both ORF6 and ORF7b (Supp. Table S2), limiting the number of analyzable polymorphisms placed in this region. Thus, while we do not observe any consistent patterns between the SNP data and the population data, our analysis may not be able to resolve those patterns on this scale.

Together, our simulation provides potential targets for both vaccination and antivirals. mRNA vaccines rely on sequence similarity, so ideal targets of vaccination are regions where there is limited sequence variability in both the simulated and observed datasets which indicates low mutability and/or strong purifying selection. Region u (-/-, 16395–17015, Fig. 3D) at the start of NSP13 is an ideal target for vaccination as it is not expected to have a high SNP frequency and has a low SNP frequency across the VoCs. Ideal regions for antivirals to target are regions where there is strong purifying selection removing SNPs that may be harmful to SARS-CoV-2 fitness. Region t, which includes genes essential for host antiviral evasion and the RdRp is a primary target for antiviral development.

High frequency polymorphisms in the spike and RdRp

Many studies focus on analyzing fixed SNPs in the spike protein and the RdRp as these proteins are responsible for cell entry and replication speed and can be a primary target for mRNA vaccinations or antivirals44–49. Across all VoCs, there are three fixed mutations. The D614G mutation in the spike protein, P323(S/L) in the RdRp, and F106F in NSP3 are ancestral to SARS-CoV-2 and are fixed within all VoCs. It has been suggested that D614G and P323L co-emerged as the former is responsible for increased stability of the region-binding domain and the latter could potentially increase the stability of the NSP8 in the RdRp38,39,50. To this day, it remains unknown as to role that F106F plays in the evolution of SARS-CoV-2.

There is a growing body of evidence that suggests mutations in the RdRp can influence variant persistence. First, RdRp mutations have been shown to alter RdRp activity in vitro by lowering the binding efficiency of RdRp to the RNA strand as well as the catalytic activity of RdRp51. Lower binding efficiency of polymerases can increase replication speed but also increase error rates52. Second, current antivirals (Remdesivir) target the RdRp, and mutations within the RdRp of patients with long COVID-19 have been shown to prevent binding of these antivirals to the RdRp reducing the efficiency of the antiviral53 and also altering SARS-CoV-2 replication speed54. Third, it has been suggested that mutations in the RdRp, namely C14408T, can impact the error rate during replication55,56. Finally, it has been shown that in other viruses, post-translational modification, particularly ubiquitination, can have a significant impact on protein turnover and function57. Polymorphisms in RdRp can impact post-translational modification, and in turn alter RdRp activity and viral efficiency.

In this study, we expand the analysis by subdividing SNPs into Persistent and Transient variants and analyzing SNPs at all allele frequencies. Upon further analysis of the RdRp and other NSPs, we have found that Persistent variants all have high-frequency SNPs within the hand (palm, fingers, thumb), while Transient variants do not.

There are nine SNPs in the hand domain including five nonsynonymous changes and four synonymous ones (Fig. 4, Supp. Table S12). Of these, α has three unique mutations which appear at high frequency (> 90%). There are two mutations in omicron, N600I and L758L, which appear at ~ 30% and ~ 60% allele frequencies respectively. The synonymous mutation L758L rises to fixation with Kraken, Pirola, and HK.3, but the nonsynonymous mutation disappears. G670G in δ and Kraken may arise independently or be the result of a recombination event. While Kraken and HK.3 share fixed mutations completely (G670G, L758L, F766F, and D833A), Pirola only shares L758L and has a unique mutation S772Y (Supp. Table S11). Given the similarity in CDP spectra between each of these variants (Supp. Fig. S3A), it is unlikely that these SNPs have a large impact on the CDP type. Instead, these SNPs likely impact replication speed and efficiency which could be the reason why these variants are persistent in the population.

Conclusion

In summary, our work shows that context can influence SNP frequency at sites by an average of 72× at all sites, and our simulations and analyses provide a deeper understanding of the evolutionary process in SARS-CoV-2. Simulated regions that show high contrast between the expected and observed number of polymorphisms provide insight on regions that are under strong purifying or positive selection. In addition, our simulations identify static regions of the genome as well as static sequence motifs that can be leveraged to design long-lasting antivirals that rely on sequence specificity. Interestingly, our work identifies that Persistent SARS-CoV-2 variants have a significant excess of RdRp hand mutations, and emergent variants that contain these mutations should be prime candidates for vaccine development. Future work involves examining other viruses to determine whether RdRp hand polymorphisms are associated with viral persistence, testing the in vitro infection efficiency of SARS-CoV-2 RdRp with and without hand mutations, and determining if these contextual patterns are consistent across different viruses.
