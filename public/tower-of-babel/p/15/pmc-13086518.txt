Patient Characteristics and Risk of Fall‐Related Injuries Among New Users of Opioids in Denmark: A Longitudinal Descriptive Study
Authors: Juliane Maria Rosendahl, Carina Lundby, Jacob Harbo Andersen, Jesper Ryg, Anton Pottegård, Lotte Rasmussen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo assess the 1‐year risk of fall‐related injuries among new users of opioids in Denmark.MethodsUsing Danish health registers, we identified all new adult users of opioids between 2010 and 2022 (first time use within 5 years). We performed logistic regression and applied marginal comparisons to estimate risk ratios (RRs) associating baseline risk factors to any fall‐related injury and fall‐related fractures specifically. Patients were followed after opioid initiation until fall‐related injury, death, leaving the country, or 1‐year follow‐up, whichever occurred first.ResultsWe identified 1 638 358 unique new users of opioids (54% women; median age 58 years). The 1‐year risk of fall‐related injuries and fall‐related fractures was 8.6% and 3.6%, respectively. The risk of fall‐related injuries was highest during the first weeks following opioid initiation. Increasing age, depression, Parkinson's disease, osteoporosis, and prior fall‐related injuries were all associated with new fall‐related injuries in the multivariable analysis. Prior fall‐related injuries was a predictor of new fall‐related injuries (RR 2.15; 95% CI: 2.12–2.18) and fall‐related fractures (RR 1.35; 95% CI: 1.31–1.40). Prior fall‐related fractures was a predictor of new fall‐related injuries (RR 1.96; 95% CI: 1.93–1.99) and fall‐related fractures (RR 6.47; 95% CI: 6.29–6.65).ConclusionThe 1‐year risk for any fall‐related injuries and fractures was 8.6% and 3.6%, respectively, and highest in the first weeks following opioid initiation. Several important risk factors associated with new fall‐related injuries were identified. Physicians should be aware of patient characteristics related to fall risk when prescribing opioids.Keywords: falls, fracture, injury, opioids, pharmacoepidemiology, registry

Introduction

Opioids are used to treat acute pain, cancer‐related pain, as well as in terminal and palliative care [1, 2]. Their use has increased in most countries from 2015 to 2019 [3], although in Denmark their use decreased from 2017 to 2019 due to a decrease in the use of tramadol [4]. In 2023, 395 000 (6.7% of the Danish population) Danes redeemed at least one opioid prescription [5, 6].

Use of opioids is associated with an increased risk of falls, injurious falls, and fractures [7, 8, 9], which might be related to opioids causing, for example, sedation and decreased attention to the environment [10, 11]. Further, use of various opioids are associated with hypotension, which also increases risk of falls [12].

Studies have shown that the risk of falling is related to the opioid dosage and patient age, with a higher risk during the first few weeks after initiation of opioid treatment [13, 14, 15]. Further, having a history of falls is an important risk factor for new falls [16]. Falls are common among older people, with 30% of people aged ≥ 65 years experiencing a fall every year increasing to 50% among those aged ≥ 80 years [17]. The increased risk of falls among older individuals is particularly alarming since it is a central cause of decrease in functionality, increasing problems with self‐care and usual activities [18], and mortality [19, 20]. Fall‐related injuries and fractures consequently have a significant impact on the patient's life, negatively affecting quality of life [18], while also being expensive for society [21, 22].

There is limited information about the characteristics of new users of opioids in Denmark and identification of who is at particular risk of falling after opioid initiation. Such information is essential to develop initiatives and efforts to prevent falls. Further, the recently published World Falls Guideline for older adults recommends assessing fall history and risk of falls before potential prescription of fall risk increasing drugs (FRIDs) to older people [23], which are medications commonly used among older individuals [24]. With this study, we aimed to describe patient characteristics among new users of opioids in Denmark and to estimate the risk of fall‐related injuries including fractures among those. In addition, we aimed to describe the timing of fall‐related injuries in relation to opioid initiation.

Methods

In this longitudinal register‐based study, we described patient characteristics of new users of opioids among adult Danes and estimated the risk of fall‐related injuries. The timing of these in relation to opioid initiation was described and patient characteristics associated with fall‐related injuries were identified.

Data Sources

We used the Danish National Prescription Registry to identify the study population. Since 1995, this registry contains information about Anatomical Therapeutical Chemical (ATC) code [25], date of dispensing, number of dispensed tablets, and a personal identification number for prescriptions redeemed at Danish community pharmacies [26]. Use of medication during hospital admission is not captured in the register. The Danish National Patient Registry was used to identify hospital diagnoses including diagnoses related to fall‐related injuries. The Danish National Patient Registry holds information about date of hospital admission or contact as well as primary and secondary diagnoses since 1977 [27]. The Danish Civil Registration System was used to give information about an individual's vital status and migration. All individuals living in Denmark have a CPR‐number, which is a unique 10‐digit identifying number. The CPR‐number permits accurate individual‐level linkage between Danish registers [28].

Study Population

The study population consisted of all individuals ≥ 18 years, living in Denmark, who filled at least one opioid prescription for the first time in 5 years (defined as new users), between January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2022. Each individual could only be included once in the study (first eligible prescription fill during study period). The date of filling the opioid prescription was defined as the index date. Prior use of opioids was defined as filling at least one opioid prescription before the 5 years wash‐out period.

Data on individuals were available at least 1 year from index date. Individuals were excluded if they did not have continuous residency in Denmark at least 10 years prior to index date, due to missing information about filled prescriptions in countries outside Denmark.

Opioids

Opioids were defined according to the ATC classification. ATC‐codes used to define opioids can be found in Table S1. Morphine combination products (N02AA51), pentazocine (N02AD01), and nalbuphine (N02AF02) were not included due to their very limited use in Denmark [5].

Fall‐Related Injuries

Our outcome of interest was fall‐related injuries including fractures treated at hospital admission during 1‐year follow‐up after opioid initiation. International Classification of Diseases 10th version (ICD‐10) codes used to define fall‐related injuries and fall‐related fractures can be found in Table S2 [29].

Analysis

We described baseline characteristics of all new users of opioids and the subgroups of new users of opioids who experienced at least one fall‐related injury and those who experienced at least one fall‐related fracture within 12 months after opioid initiation. Characteristics included sex, age at opioid initiation, use of opioids prior to index date, and previous fall‐related injuries or fall‐related fractures. To identify prior use of opioids and previous fall‐related injuries, we used a window of any time available prior to index date. In addition, we identified the top five most prevalent drug classes (4th ATC level), as well as use of other medications including FRIDs, defined according to the Screening Tool of Older Persons Prescriptions in older adults with high fall risk (STOPPFall) [24], using a window of 12 months prior to the index date (Tables S3 and S4). Furthermore, information about pre‐specified medical diagnoses was described using a time window of 10 years prior to the index date. ATC codes for the included medications and ICD‐10 codes for the included medical diagnoses can be found in Table S5. The specific medical diagnoses were included because of their association with increased risk of falls or fall‐related injuries [30, 31, 32, 33, 34].

To estimate the 1‐year risk of fall‐related injuries specifically among new users of opioids throughout the entire study period, we calculated the cumulative incidence of both the first fall‐related injuries and for fall‐related fractures specifically following opioid initiation. Participants were followed after opioid initiation until fall‐related injury, death, leaving the country, or 1‐year follow‐up, whichever occurred first.

We calculated the 1‐year risk stratified by sex, age, use of opioids prior to index date, prior fall‐related injuries, prior fall‐related fractures, and the use of FRIDs. For each stratum, the absolute risk difference and risk ratio were calculated.

We performed logistic regression from which we applied marginal comparisons to estimate risk ratios (RRs) and obtained 95% confidence intervals using the delta method to identify association between experiencing fall‐related injuries or fall‐related fractures and baseline characteristics [35]. We performed both univariate analysis and multivariate analysis. All variables were included in the multivariate analysis.

Supplementary and Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted a supplementary analysis calculating the cumulative incidence, where individuals with an outcome within 7 days following initiation of opioid treatment were excluded. This analysis was conducted to exclude individuals who were potentially prescribed opioids due to fall‐related injuries that was not yet coded as such in the registries as well as individuals with a primary care diagnosis of a fall with a follow‐up appointment at the hospital, from where we leverage data, after redemption of an opioid. We also performed a sensitivity analysis calculating the cumulative incidence, where an individual could be a new user multiple times over the study period if there was a gap of two years or more between two opioid prescriptions. As a second sensitivity analysis, we calculated the cumulative incidence, only including individuals who initiated a second treatment period with opioids. Thus, individuals must have previously undergone opioid treatment, although with a minimum gap of 2 years between the two treatment periods.

Other

All calculations were performed using R 4.3.3. According to Danish law, register‐based studies without human biological material do not require ethical approval [36]. The study was registered at the University of Southern Denmark's inventory (record no. 10.490).

Results

We identified 1 638 358 unique individuals with a new opioid prescription during January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2022. Following exclusions, due to death, migration, event on day of entry, or other reasons, the final analysis included 1 477 585 unique individuals at risk of fall‐related injuries and 1 483 717 unique individuals at risk of fall‐related fractures.

Baseline Characteristics of Study Population

Baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. The median age (IQR) of all new users of opioids was 58 (43–72) years and 54% were women. One‐third of the study population had used opioids prior to index date (39%) and were current users of paracetamol at index date (31%). One in four users had a fall‐related fracture prior to inclusion (24%). Nearly half of the study population used at least one FRID (43%).

Patient Characteristics of Users Experiencing Fall‐Related Injury

Among those with one full year of available follow‐up (n = 1 638 358), 7.8% (n = 127 418) and 3.2% (n = 53 177) of new users of opioids experienced fall‐related injury or fall‐related fracture, respectively, within 12 months after opioid initiation (Table 1). Those experiencing fall‐related injury or fracture were generally comparable to the users who did not experience fall‐related injury or fracture. However, their median age (65 and 67 years, respectively) was greater than the median age for those who did not experience fall‐related injury or fracture. Further, the majority of users who experienced fall‐related injuries or fractures during the follow‐up period had experienced fall‐related injury prior to inclusion (75% and 82%, respectively). Half of users who experienced fall‐related injuries had experienced fall‐related fractures prior to inclusion (50%). Three quarters of users who experienced fall‐related fractures during the follow‐up period had experienced fall‐related fractures prior to inclusion (71%). Additionally, a higher proportion of users who experienced fall‐related injuries and fractures was identified with osteoporosis (10% and 11%, respectively) compared to users who did not experience fall‐related injury or fracture (5%).

1‐Year Risk of Any Fall‐Related Injuries

The 1‐year risk of fall‐related injury (n = 1 477 585) and fracture (n = 1 483 717) after opioid initiation were 8.6% (n = 127 418) and 3.6% (n = 53 177), respectively. The cumulative incidence was lowest among individuals aged 40–49 years (5.2%), increased with age, and was highest among those aged 90+ years (30%). Notable, the 1‐year risk among individuals aged 18–29 years was 8.9%. Fall‐related injuries or fractures prior to index date led to higher risk of fall‐related injury compared to no prior fall‐related injuries or fractures with absolute risk difference of 9.5% (RR = 3.31; 95% CI: 3.27–3.36) and 13% (RR = 3.35; 95% CI: 3.31–3.38), respectively. Prior use of opioids, which was defined as filling at least one opioid prescription before the wash‐out period, did not increase the risk of fall‐related injuries compared to no prior use (Table 2). The rate of fall‐related injuries and fractures was highest during the first weeks following initiation of opioid treatment and subsequently decreased (Figures 1 and 2).

Predictors of Fall‐Related Injuries and Fractures

For the majority of baseline characteristics, the univariate analysis yielded higher RRs for fall‐related injuries and fractures than the multivariate analysis. However, all age groups had greater RRs in the multivariate analysis compared to the univariate analysis. Prior fall‐related injuries were associated with fall‐related injuries (RR 2.15 95% CI: 2.12–2.18) and fall‐related fractures (RR 1.35; 95% CI: 1.31–1.40) during follow‐up. Prior fall‐related fractures were also associated with fall‐related injuries (RR 1.96 95% CI: 1.93–1.99) and fall‐related fractures (RR 6.47; 95% CI: 6.29–6.65). However, prior use of opioids was inversely associated with fall‐related injuries (RR 0.93; 95% CI: 0.92–0.94) and fall‐related fractures (RR 0.80; 95% CI: 0.79–0.82). Finally, most of the specific medical diagnoses were significantly associated with fall‐related injuries (Table 3).

Supplementary and Sensitivity Analysis

The supplementary analysis, excluding individuals with an outcome within 7 days following initiation of opioid treatment, yielded a 1‐year risk for first fall‐related injury or fracture following opioid initiation of 7.2% and 2.7%, respectively. For the sensitivity analyses, the 1‐year risk for first fall‐related injury and fracture following opioid initiation, including both one‐time users and multiple‐use users, was 8.9% and 3.6%, respectively. Including only users initiating a second treatment episode yielded a 1‐year risk for fall‐related injuries and fractures of 9.6% and 3.8%, respectively.

Discussion

In this study of all adult Danes initiating use of opioids, we found that the 1‐year risk of fall‐related injuries and fractures was 8.6% and 3.6%, respectively. The risk of fall‐related injuries was highest within a short timeframe following opioid treatment initiation. Several important factors associated with fall‐related injuries were identified, particularly increasing age, prior fall‐related injuries including fractures, and several specific medical diagnoses.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of this study is the use of the Danish National Health Registries. Accordingly, we used the Danish National Prescription Registry to identify all Danish residents redeeming a prescription on opioids during a time span of 13 years. The registry consists of high quality nationwide data [26], strongly limiting selection bias. Furthermore, we used the Danish National Patient Registry to identify all fall‐related injuries that led to contact with Danish hospitals. However, the study also holds important limitations. First, many falls do not lead to contact with the health care system [37], making it impossible to determine the number of users of opioids who experience a non‐injurious fall or risk factors associated with this. Second, we do not know if the identified fall‐related injuries in fact are caused by opioids or might be due to non‐measured factors. Using ATC‐codes to identify medical diagnoses might result in an overestimation of users of opioids with the specific medical diagnoses due to insufficient information regarding the therapeutic use of the selected medications. Further, using the Danish National Prescription Registry, it is not possible to know if the redeemed prescription is actually consumed by the study population.

Comparison to Other Studies

We found that the 1‐year risk of fall‐related injuries was most pronounced among users aged 90+ years and lowest among users aged 40–49 years. The finding of greatest risk among the oldest age group is consistent with findings from a study investigating the age‐related risk of serious fall events among users of opioids [15]. On the contrary, another study found the risk of fall‐related injuries after opioid initiation to be highest among young adults [13]. However, the study used a case‐crossover design, which means that cases are their own control. In general, falls are common among older adults [17], which could explain the smaller difference in fall events between case period and control period among older adults compared to younger adults. Remarkably, we found a relatively high 1‐year risk among users aged 18–29 years first surpassed by the age group 70–79 years.

We also found that the majority of users of opioids who experienced fall‐related injuries during the follow‐up period had a history of fall‐related injuries and fractures prior to the index date, which aligns with previous findings [16]. Our finding that the risk of falling while using opioids is highest within the first weeks following treatment initiation aligns with findings in other studies [13, 15]. An explanation of the increased risk following initiation could be due to the known side effects of opioids, such as sedation, being most pronounced at treatment initiation [38]. Thus, this well‐known risk factor should be taken into account when considering prescribing opioids, especially when prescribing to people at high age [23], due to the serious consequences of falls [18, 19, 20].

Surprisingly, we found that prior use of opioids was associated with fewer fall‐related injuries. A reason for this could be that those who experienced an event refrained from later initiating opioid treatment again (i.e., “depletion of susceptible”). Another reason could relate to user awareness of side effects of opioids, such as sedation, enabling users to avoid injurious situations. However, in a recent pilot survey of 57 Danish adult users of opioids, we found limited user awareness of opioids' fall risk. Only few users spontaneously reported being aware of this risk, and when asked directly, only one‐third reported awareness [39].

Several important comorbidities such as depression and Parkinson's disease were all found to be associated with fall‐related injuries, which aligns with the results from a systematic review and meta‐analysis including community‐dwelling older people [30]. However, we found lower relative risk estimates compared to the odds ratios reported in the systematic review and meta‐analysis, which could be due to the difference in study population.

In conclusion, old age, history of fall‐related injuries, recent initiation of opioid treatment, and specific medical diagnoses were found to be related to the risk of fall‐related injuries. The risk was highest within a short time frame following opioid initiation. Our findings indicate that physicians should be aware of patient characteristics associated with fall‐related injuries when prescribing opioids in order to prevent falls, especially among older adults.
