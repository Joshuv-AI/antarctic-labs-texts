Numerical study of the thermal performance of a single-channel cooling PV system using baffles and different nanofluids
Authors: T. Lima-Téllez, J.F. Hinojosa, I. Hernández-López, S. Moreno
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The present numerical study reports the performance of a cooling system for solar photovoltaic panels (PV) using different nanofluids (Al2O3, CuO, and ZnO). A novel parallel flow channel with strategically placed baffles was analyzed to improve the heat transfer between the back of PV and the nanofluid. The nanoparticles' Brownian motion and the nanofluid temperature effect were considered. Computational fluid dynamics was used to simulate the interaction between the fluid in motion and panel materials. Various nanoparticle concentrations, Reynolds numbers (18–1800), and solar radiation values (200–1000 W/m2) were examined. The results showed that the nanofluid composed of CuO was the most effective, improving thermal efficiency by 5.67 % compared to pure water in the lowest Re range. A 10 % vol. concentration of Al2O3 reduced temperature by up to 15 % and increased electrical efficiency by 4 % when the Re varied from 18 to 42. However, increasing the Re number and having low solar radiation values decreased the contribution of the nanofluid. Additionally, using baffles in the flow channel improved electrical efficiency by 2 %.

Introduction

The demand for electrical energy is expected to increase significantly due to the growth of emerging economies and the expanding electrification of the global energy system. The electricity demand is projected to rise by around 80 % by 2050 [1]. Consequently, there is an increasing commitment to solar systems, particularly photovoltaic (PV) systems, to supply electrical energy. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global demand for this technology increased by 15 % in 2020 alone. Photovoltaic energy is expected to account for 16 % of total energy production by 2050 [2].

PV systems have disadvantages, such as low conversion efficiency from solar to electrical energy. Although photovoltaic panels absorb around 80 % of the incident solar radiation, only a fraction is converted into electrical energy. This is because, according to the Shockley-Queisser limit, a single-junction solar cell's maximum theoretical efficiency under standard sunlight conditions is approximately 33.7 % [3]. However, commercial photovoltaic panels have not achieved an efficiency greater than 20 % [4,5]. The remaining part is transformed into heat, which increases the cell's temperature and decreases its efficiency. For every 1 °C increase in the standard cell temperature (Tstc = 25 °C), the cell's electrical efficiency decreases by 0.40 %–0.65 %. Therefore, it is very convenient to cool photovoltaic systems to reduce their temperature and thus increase their efficiency.

Different cooling techniques can be used based on passive (which do not use an external source) and active processes (which use an external source). Active systems are generally more effective than passive ones, especially under high temperatures. Because of this, research in recent decades has focused on improving active cooling systems characterized by the circulation of a conventional fluid through the upper or lower part of the PV system. Water is the most used fluid [6], accounting for 36.13 %, followed by air at 27.74 %, as water-based cooling systems are more effective than air-based cooling systems [7,8]. It was tested with flax fibers [9], using encapsulated PCM balls [10], and under different operating conditions [11].

Recently, scientists have focused on enhancing the thermophysical properties of conventional fluids such as water, oil, or glycol. Advances in nanotechnology have shown that this can be achieved by adding nanoparticles (metallic, metallic oxides, and non-metallic oxides) of sizes less than 100 nm to the conventional fluid. Lee et al. [12] first introduced the term nanofluid to refer to a strategy to improve the thermophysical properties, particularly the thermal conductivity of conventional fluids. In recent years, several authors have investigated the application of nanofluids in cooling systems for high and low-temperature systems, such as concentration photovoltaic (CPV) systems and PV systems, with a porous heat sink [13], different metal oxide nanoparticles [14], different conditions of operation [15], thermal hybrid systems [16], with microchannels [17], and diamond nanoparticles suspended in Therminol [18].

The following sections present recent experimental and numerical studies that have evaluated various parameters, such as types of nanofluids, nanoparticle concentrations, and geometries, providing a comprehensive overview of the advancements and challenges in this field.

Experimental studies

Experimental studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of several nanofluids in improving the thermal and electrical efficiency of PV systems. Initially, researchers focused on studying a single nanofluid and comparing its results with a reference fluid, which is predominantly pure water. Nabil and Mansour et al. [19] used CuO nanoparticles (0.2 % vol.) and pure water to cool PV modules. They reported that the operational temperature of the panel decreased by 4.5 °C and 4 °C, respectively, which resulted in an efficiency increase of 2.46 % and 2.2 %. Alktranee et al. [20] employed zirconium oxide (ZrO) nanofluid in a cooling system, achieving a reduction in the photovoltaic module's temperature of 10.2 °C, doubling the reduction achieved with deionized water. Gelis et al. [21] conducted an experimental study and employed the response surface methodology (RSM) to assess the impact of SiO2 composite nanofluids on a PV system's electrical and thermal efficiency. The authors found that the flow rate and volumetric concentration are the most influential parameters on the electrical efficiency of the PV system. In a recent article, Shen et al. [22] experimentally studied the influence of a nanofluid composed of 0.25 % ZnO on hydrogen production through a PV system. The authors reported that a mass flow rate of 0.012 kg/s achieved the highest thermal efficiency and hydrogen production rate.

To increase the contribution of nanofluids compared to a reference fluid, researchers examined the impact of different nanofluid concentrations. Sardarabadi et al. [23] investigated the effect of SiO nanofluids at 1 % and 3 % concentrations and observed increases in energy efficiency of 3.6 % and 7.9 %, respectively, compared to using only pure water. Alktranee et al. [24] investigated using ZrO2 nanofluid at different concentrations with the same setup to reduce the temperature of the PVT system. The results revealed a panel temperature reduction of 10.2 °C with nanofluid and 5.1 °C with deionized water compared to the reference module without cooling. In another study conducted by Alktranee et al. [25], the impact of using TiO2 nanofluid as a cooling medium for a PVT system was evaluated. The study found that circulating TiO2 nanofluid at 0.2 % and 0.3 % volume fractions reduced the PV cell temperature by 14 % compared to the reference PV module. This significant temperature reduction is attributed to the improved thermal conductivity provided by the TiO2 nanoparticles. Kazem et al. [26] investigated the impact of a nanofluid composed of CuO nanoparticles in a mixture of water and ethylene glycol on the efficiency of PV/T systems. They tested nanofluids with mass fractions of 0.1 %, 0.5 %, 1.0 %, and 2.0 % nano-CuO. Using the nanofluid with a 2.0 % mass fraction of Nano-CuO in the PV/T system, a 6.76 % increase in electrical efficiency and a 39.6 % increase in thermal efficiency were observed compared to the base fluid.

In searching for the most effective nanofluid, researchers tested and compared various nanofluids. For example, Al-Shamani et al. [27] examined the impact of nanofluids (SiO2, TiO2, and SiC) and pure water on cooling a PV system. They found that the PV system cooled with the SiC nanofluid had the highest electrical efficiency of 13.52 %, followed by the SiO2, TiO2, and pure water nanofluids. In another experimental study, Jia et al. [28] evaluated the thermal performance of two metal oxide nanofluids used as coolants in a PV system. They found that the Al2O3 composite nanofluid showed better thermal performance than that of TiO2, attributed to the higher thermal conductivity of Al2O3, which is 40 W/m K, compared to 8.95 W/m K of TiO2. Ebaid et al. [29]employed a heat exchanger containing water, TiO2 nanoparticles mixed with a polyethylene glycol and water solution, and Al2O3 nanoparticles in an aqueous solution with cetyltrimethylammonium bromide positioned at the rear of the panel. The results demonstrated that this configuration significantly reduced the average temperature of the photovoltaic cells, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed cooling method.

Several studies have investigated cooling systems with different geometries to improve the efficiency of photovoltaic (PV) modules. In one of them, Menon et al. [30] implemented an experimental coil-type cooling system using CuO nanofluids, which reduced the panel's temperature by an average of 16 °C compared to a PV system without cooling. In another experimental study, Janardhana et al. [31] tested a dual-pass circulation channel that contained a nanofluid with a 0.2 % vol. concentration of SiO2 in distilled water. The results showed that this cooling technique successfully reduced the operating temperature of the panel by 35 °C, thus enhancing its efficiency by 17.3 % compared to a system without cooling. Diwania et al. [32] evaluated a PVT system using water and a nanofluid composed of CuO in a sheet and coil-type cooling system. The authors obtained that the temperature of the PVT system was reduced by 15 °C and 23 °C when they used water and nanofluid, respectively, compared to the reference system (without cooling), which reached 68.4 °C.

Numerical studies

Numerical research offers a theoretical perspective on nanofluids' effectiveness in enhancing PV systems' performance. As in experimental studies, researchers initially concentrated on evaluating a single nanofluid in numerical simulations to understand its impact on photovoltaic (PV) system performance. Salem et al. [33] demonstrated that incorporating nanofluids enhances the heat transfer coefficient. Arifin et al. [34] examined 0.2 % TiO2 nanofluids and water in coil-type PVT systems, finding an efficiency increase of 2.11 % and a temperature reduction of 10 °C, reaching a maximum temperature of 58.5 °C. Azad et al. [35] conducted a parametric analysis of a photovoltaic thermal (PVT) system using MWCNT nanofluids in Dhaka, Bangladesh. The results indicated that thermal efficiency improved by 5.6 % with higher Reynolds numbers, while electrical efficiency decreased with increased solar irradiation. Sreekumar et al. [36] carried out a 3D numerical investigation on a nanofluid-based photovoltaic/thermal (PVT) system. They used a nanofluid composed of MXene in a water base and found that the thermal efficiency increased by about 21 %, while the electrical efficiency only increased by 0.65 %.

Like the experimental studies, these numerical investigations aim to determine the optimal nanoparticle concentration to minimize the panel temperature and improve overall efficiency. Purohit et al. [37] employed nanofluids (Al2O3) in a flat plate collector to cool a PV system. The authors observed a maximum reduction of 4.1 °C in temperature when the nanofluid concentration was 6 %, and the flow was maintained in a laminar regime (Re = 1200). Tian et al. [38] conducted an economic and exergy analysis of 250 W PV and PVT panels using MgO-water nanofluids (0–1% vol.) in a copper tube cooling system. The study projected an investment recovery period of six years for PV and four years for PVT panels. Although the literature highlights the superiority of nanofluids in terms of thermal performance compared to water, Ould-Lahoucine et al. [39] presented more nuanced results. Their numerical research examined the cooling of a PV system that used a nanofluid composed of 2 % TiO2 nanoparticles for cooling. They concluded that the nanofluid system did not offer a significant advantage over the water-based PV system, especially when the nanoparticle concentration was low.

Numerically, researchers have also focused on testing different nanofluids. For instance, Radwan et al. [39] theoretically studied a low-concentration PV system using a microchannel heat sink with nanofluids (Al2O3 and SiC) in other configurations. The authors found that using nanofluids reduced the temperature of the PV system at a higher concentration ratio and lower Reynolds number (Re). Khanjari et al. [40] evaluated using Ag and Al2O3 nanofluids to cool a PV system. It was found that the Ag nanofluid maintains the maximum temperature of the system at 333.4 K, while that of Al2O3 maintains it at 337.1 K. In other studies, the focus has been on improving the geometry through which the nanofluid circulates. Maadi et al. [41] incorporated a wavy strip inside the tubes of a PV system with Al2O3 nanofluids. The authors found that the integrated PVT system with water-based nanofluid and a wavy strip improved electrical and thermal efficiencies by 3.5 % and 12.06 %, respectively, compared to the typical PVT system. Prasetyo et al. [42] modeled various riser configurations in photovoltaic thermal (PVT) collectors to cool PV panels using different nanofluids (TiO2, SiO2, and Al2O3) and pure water. It was found that employing a semicircular configuration with pure water at a flow rate of 0.5 kg/s resulted in higher efficiency than any nanofluids. Despite water being the most used base fluid for nanofluids in the literature, other fluids have also been investigated. A study by Kenfack et al. [43] analyzed a hybrid PVT system employing various heat transfer fluids (HTF) in Cameroon. Their findings revealed that the cottonseed oil/TiO2 configuration achieved a 12.08 % improvement in electrical efficiency compared to conventional systems.

In addition to traditional methods, researchers have used advanced prediction and analysis to assess the impact of nanofluids and other advanced materials on PV/T systems. These studies use mathematical models and AI techniques to optimize performance. Ali H.A. et al. [44] investigated the impact of nanofluids and nano-PCM on the efficiency of PV/T systems. They compared three linear mathematical models and various artificial neural network models to predict the performance of the PV/T system.

On the other hand, various nanoparticles have been tested at different concentrations, and it has been reported that metal oxide nanoparticles, such as Al2O3 and CuO, are the most used for the composition of nanofluids due to their low production costs. According to Mukherjee et al. [45], producing 4 L of nanofluids with a 2 % concentration has a cost of $180 and $380 for Al2O3 and CuO, respectively, while the production of other nanofluids composed of 2 % carbon nanotubes (graphene) ranges from $704 to $5320.

As it was shown in the literature survey, within numerical studies, very few consider the effect of dimensionless heat transfer coefficients in their analysis, mainly focusing on temperature distribution and, to a lesser extent, on the energetic and exergetic performance of the PV system. Moreover, it has been observed that most of these studies do not consider the effect of temperature on the thermal conductivity of nanofluids. Critical aspects, such as the Brownian motion and the size of nanoparticles, are often overlooked due to the use of classical models for thermal conductivity, where nanoparticles are assumed to be stationary in the base fluid. Considering the Brownian motion leads to more realistic simulations and implies that the thermal conductivity of the nanofluid increases as temperature increases.

Regarding the geometries addressed in the literature, most research focuses on sheet-and-tube or spiral-type configurations. Very few consider a design with a single channel with parallel flow or multiple channels, representing an opportunity to continue exploring these alternative configurations. In the few studies that have addressed single-channel designs, elements such as baffles to enhance heat transfer within the system have not been incorporated. This omission opens a significant area for potential improvement in the efficiency of these systems and provides an avenue for further research and development. Exploring a single-channel design is relevant due to the higher contact area between the PV panel and the nanofluid than the sheet-and-tube configuration. Considering the above, the main objective of the present study is to optimize a single-channel configuration for the cooling system of a photovoltaic (PV) panel using nanofluids. A detailed computational fluid dynamics analysis was developed using various metal oxide nanofluids with different volumetric concentrations and inlet flows. A dynamic model that includes the Brownian effect was employed to calculate the effective thermal conductivity of the nanofluids, considering the impact of temperature dependency, nanoparticle size, volumetric concentration, and the properties of the base fluid. Additionally, baffles were strategically integrated into the channel to improve heat transfer between the back of the PV panel and the nanofluid. This approach allows for a deeper analysis of the effect of dimensionless heat transfer coefficients. It provides a correlation for predicting the Nusselt number in the channel for the nanofluid and the reference fluid, which is pure water. It also addresses the temperature fields and flow patterns concerning the electrical efficiency of the PV panel.

Numerical methodology

The general procedure to solve the heat transfer in the thermal system may be summarized in the following steps: (1) The geometry and computational mesh were created using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software, (2) initial guess values of all variables in the thermal system are given, (3) the fields of pressure, temperature, and velocity components were calculated by solving the discretized equations and applying an iterative algorithm (SIMPLE), (3) the fluid or nanofluid properties are updated with the new temperature field in the channel, (4) the convergence criterion was applied, and the process was repeated from step (3) until the convergence criterion was achieved. The computational procedure was implemented using the software ANSYS FLUENT v20, based on the finite volume method (FVM), to solve governing equations. The semi-implicit method for the pressure-linked equations (SIMPLE) iterative algorithm was used for the pressure-velocity coupling. The pressure staggering scheme (PRESTO) was used for discretizing pressure gradients, whereas a third-order scheme (MUSCLE) was applied to discretize convection and diffusion terms. Convergence criteria of 10−6 were used for all equations. Ansys Meshing software generated a structured, quadratic, and non-uniform mesh.

A mesh independence analysis was conducted to determine the optimum computational mesh by focusing on the variation of the Nusselt number along the channel. Five mesh sizes were tested. As can be observed in Fig. 2, for a mesh comprising 133,345 nodes and 131,200 cells, no significant change in the variable was noted, with a maximum percentage difference of 0.29 %. Fig. 3 shows the selected mesh, which exhibits an optimal orthogonal quality of 1, an average aspect ratio of 3.53 ± 5, and a y + factor of 0.116, deemed ideal for laminar flows. Validation

The validation of the simulation model used for this study was carried out in two phases. In the first phase, the PV system model was validated using only the base fluid (pure water) as a coolant. The PV system's thermal performance and electrical efficiency (Fig. 4) at different flow inlet velocities were compared with the numerical and experimental data obtained by Nahar et al. [11]. The thermophysical properties and specifications of the system are detailed in Table 2. Fig. 4 shows good agreement between the results obtained in the present simulation and the data reported in the literature (the mean average percentage difference was 3.4 %), which supports the reliability of the simulation model in the representation of PV systems.Fig. 4(a) Thermal and (b) electrical performance validation of a PV System [11].Fig. 4

(a) Thermal and (b) electrical performance validation of a PV System [11].

The second validation phase focused on modeling the flow of nanofluids under the laminar flow regime, replicating the experimental results of Kim et al. [60]. In their work, the authors studied heat transfer by forced convection in nanofluids composed of Al2O3/H2O at a volume concentration of 3 %. The experimental model consisted of a stainless-steel tube 2 m long and 4.57 mm internal diameter, in which 11 T-type thermocouples were placed. The tube was electrically heated using a direct current source to achieve a heat flow constant, and the inlet temperature of the nanofluid was maintained at 22 °C. Fig. 5 compares the numerical results obtained through the present simulation and the experimental data regarding the local convective heat transfer coefficient, observing agreement between both results. The validation results provide a solid and reliable basis for modeling PV systems and their interaction with nanofluids.Fig. 5Validation of nanofluid flow by comparing with [61].Fig. 5

Conclusions

The present numerical investigation evaluates the thermal performance of a cooling system with a single parallel flow channel for photovoltaic (PV) panels using different nanofluids (Al2O3, CuO, and ZnO). From the results, we obtained the following conclusions:•The concentration of 0.1 vol in the nanofluid produces a more effective reduction in the temperature of the photovoltaic cell, which reaches up to 15 % when the Reynolds number increases from 18 to 42.•As the Reynolds number increases, convective heat transfer intensifies, contributing to the decrease in cell temperature.•The increase in Re from 18 to 42 boosted electrical efficiency by 4 %. This increase is attributed to better heat transfer by convection, which decreases cell temperature and minimizes internal electrical resistance.•Efficiency improved by 1.40 % by increasing the nanofluid concentration from 0 to 0.1 vol.•Increasing the radiation from 200 to 1000 W/m2 decreased the efficiency by 6.5 % for pure water and 5.5 % for the nanofluid. However, nanofluids more effectively attenuated the decrease in efficiency under high solar radiation.•From the evaluation of the thermal performance of three different metal-oxide nanofluids (Al2O3, CuO, and ZnO) at a concentration of 0.1 vol, it was found that all nanofluids surpass pure water in thermal efficiency for all Reynolds numbers considered.•The nanofluid composed of CuO was the most effective, improving efficiency by 5.67 % compared to pure water in the lowest Re range.•Nanofluids, especially with a 10 % vol. Al2O3 concentration, significantly improve photovoltaic systems' thermal and electrical efficiency, especially at low flow rates.•The effect of nanofluids on efficiency decreases as the Re number increases. At higher flow rates, convective heat transfer is naturally more effective, reducing the need for additional improvements provided by nanofluid.•Baffles improve heat transfer in specific channel areas, resulting in a 2 % increase in electrical efficiency due to fluid flow redirection and acceleration.•Even though nanofluids significantly improve photovoltaic systems' thermal and electrical efficiency, future work will focus on combining nanofluids with phase change materials to upgrade the cooling performance.
