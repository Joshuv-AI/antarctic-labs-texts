Tumor-derived RHOA mutants interact with effectors in the GDP-bound state
Authors: Yuan Lin, Theresa A Ramelot, Simge Senyuz, Attila Gursoy, Hyunbum Jang, Ruth Nussinov, Ozlem Keskin, Yi Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRHOA mutations are found at diverse residues in various cancer types, implying mutation- and cell-specific mechanisms of tumorigenesis. Here, we focus on the underlying mechanisms of two gain-of-function RHOA mutations, A161P and A161V, identified in adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma. We find that RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V are both fast-cycling mutants with increased guanine nucleotide dissociation/association rates compared with RHOAWT and show reduced GTP-hydrolysis activity. Crystal structures reveal an altered nucleotide association in RHOAA161P and an open nucleotide pocket in RHOAA161V. Both mutations perturb the dynamic properties of RHOA switch regions and shift the conformational landscape important for RHOA activity, as shown by 31P NMR and molecular dynamics simulations. Interestingly, RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V can interact with effectors in the GDP-bound state. 1H-15N HSQC NMR spectra support the existence of an active population in RHOAA161V-GDP. The distinct interaction mechanisms resulting from the mutations likely favor an RHOAWT-like “ON” conformation, endowing GDP-bound state effector binding activity.Subject terms: X-ray crystallography, Hydrolases, Solution-state NMR

Introduction

RHOA belongs to the RAS-homology small GTPase family and is a key regulator of a variety of cellular functions1. RHOA activity is modulated at several levels, including lipid modification, protein expression, subcellular localization, and association with RHO GDP dissociation inhibitors (RHO GDIs)2,3. Importantly, as in most small GTPases, RHOA transduces intracellular signals by acting as a molecular switch that cycles between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound forms4. Activation of RHOA involves major conformational changes in two flexible regions, Switch I (residues 28–40) and Switch II (residues 60–78)4. This dynamic cycle of GTP-loading/GTP-hydrolysis is essential for proper RHOA signaling and is tightly regulated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs)5.

RHOA dysfunction has traditionally been considered to promote cancer proliferation and metastasis6,7. Aberrant RHOA signaling in cancer usually stems from RHOA overexpression or hyperactivation via deregulation of its regulators8. The role of RHOA as a tumor driver gene has recently received increased attention after several human whole cancer genome sequencing studies identified recurrent RHOA mutations in gastric cancer (GC)9, peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL)10, adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL)11, angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL)12,13, Burkitt lymphoma (BL)14, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)15. Unlike RAS, whose mutations are mostly (98%) found in well-defined hotspots (e.g., G12, G13, and Q61), impair intrinsic and GAP-mediated GTP hydrolysis and/or cause enhanced nucleotide exchange, RHOA mutational hotspots appear highly divergent, vary by tumor type, and display mutation- and tissue-specific biological consequences16,17. For example, in solid tumors, RHOA mutations are often located at the effector/regulator binding surface, such as E40Q in HNSCC and R5Q/W, Y42C, and L57V in GC9. In T-cell leukemia, RHOA mutations are almost always located in the nucleotide-binding pocket. In ATLL, in addition to the most prevalent hotspot, G17V, shared with PTCL and AITL, other previously undescribed RHOA mutations have been identified, including C16R/F, A161P/V, and K118E/Q11. Some RHOA mutations seem to be gain-of-function mutations (e.g., C16R/F, A161P/V, and K118E/Q), whereas others may be loss-of-function or even behave as dominant-negative mutations (e.g., G17V and A161E).

To understand the unique role of distinct RHOA mutations in different tumor types and their seemingly different functions18–20, it is critical to better characterize RHOA mutants, particularly the structural features that lead to their unique behavior. We have focused on two putative gain-of-function RHOA mutations identified in ATLL, A161P and A161V, to study their impact on both structure and function using a combination of biochemical analyses, crystallography, NMR spectroscopy, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Residue A161 is located in the SAK motif, one of the conserved G-motifs across the small GTPase superfamily for guanine nucleotide binding and GTPase activity. Although mutations at A161 in RHOA are not as frequent (i.e. 11 cases of A161V and two cases of A161P documented in the COSMIC database21), mutations at the equivalent position have been found in KRAS (342 cases of A146T, 98 cases of A146V, and 29 cases of A146P), HRAS (two cases of A146T and two cases of A146V), NRAS (24 cases of A146T, four cases of A161V, and one case of A146P), and RAC1 (22 cases of A159V) in the same database. It is therefore important to understand how these mutations confer oncogenicity at the molecular and atomic level. Here, we reported that in RHOA, the A161V and A161P mutations contribute to faster nucleotide exchange via a unique mechanism. We further establish that RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V can bind to effectors even in a GDP-bound state, suggesting that these mutations cause conformational changes that enforce and stabilize an active conformation in the absence of bound GTP.

Results

RHOAA161V and RHOAA161P are fast-cycling mutants with reduced GTPase activity

Throughout this study, RHOA constructs were truncated at residue 181 (ΔC) and contained the F25N mutation, known to stabilize protein expression in bacteria22. C-terminal truncation has little impact on RHOA’s biochemical properties, whereas F25N reduces interaction with the effector (Supplementary Fig. 1). For simplicity, we termed this modified construct RHOAWT. Two lab-generated mutations, F30L and T37A, were included as controls for biochemical analyses. RHOAF30L is a known fast-cycling mutant23 and RHOAT37A, equivalent to HRAST35A, is a constitutively inactive mutant with impaired interaction with effectors24. Slow hydrolyzable GTP analogs such as GMPPNP or GTPγS were used, and no significant difference was detected between these analogs (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Mutations at residue A161 are expected to affect nucleotide-binding kinetics. Indeed, RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V had significantly increased nucleotide dissociation rates (koff) compared with RHOAWT under both Mg2+ (Fig. 1a, b) and EDTA conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Mg2+ is an essential cofactor for small GTPases and removing Mg2+ with EDTA greatly reduces affinity for nucleotides25. RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V also showed significantly increased nucleotide association rates (kon) compared with RHOAWT (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Increased kon and koff are likely coupled events. RHOAA161V had a slightly higher kon, but no change in koff than RHOAA161P (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 3b). RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V had ~9-10-fold higher koff and ~2-3-fold higher kon than RHOAF30L, indicating a more profound perturbation of the nucleotide pocket by A161P/V than by F30L. T37A, on the other hand, reduced kon and koff to ~50% (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Table 1). MBP-tagged functional DH/PH domains of LARG, an RHOGEF, showed increased binding to RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V preloaded with GDP or GMPPNP, in contrast to RHOAWT which only bound to the GEF when EDTA was added to chelate Mg2+ and release nucleotides (Fig. 1c). Both mutants showed reduced binding to LARG when extra nucleotides were supplemented, distinguishing them from dominant negative mutants. In contrast, RHOAF30L displayed a minimal increase in binding to LARG in GDP/GMPPNP-bound forms and RHOAT37A had much reduced complex formation even in the EDTA condition (Supplementary Fig. 3d). The two mutants, particularly RHOAA161P, had complex formation with LARG in the GDP- and GMPPNP-states (Fig. 1c) and less nucleotide loading (Supplementary Fig. 3b), indicating impaired binding to nucleotides.

To measure GTPase activity, we used an assay that relies on the enzymatic conversion of GTP that remains after the GTPase reaction to ATP and the detection of the generated ATP using a luciferin/luciferase combination26. Excess GTP substrate was used and GTP hydrolysis occurred after GDP/GTP exchange, distinct from other single-turnover GTPase assays. 20 mM EDTA was present in the reaction buffer to minimize the effects of the difference in nucleotide exchange rates on evaluating their GTPase ability. RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V displayed ~2-fold reduced intrinsic and ~3-fold reduced p190, a RHOGAP, stimulated GTPase activity compared to RHOAWT (Fig. 1d, e, Supplementary Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Table 2). The hydrolysis impairment could be underestimated as both mutants cycle GDP/GTP faster even in the EDTA condition (Supplementary Fig. 3a). RHOAF30L showed little change. RHOAT37A showed a much reduced intrinsic and GAP-stimulated GTPase activity, consistent with the previous result in RAS27. Thus, RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V are fast-cycling GTPases with increased dissociation and association rates, destabilized nucleotide binding, and reduced GTP hydrolysis activity.

GTP-bound RHOAA161V exhibits significant perturbation and increased state 1, whereas RHOAA161P is similar to RHOAWT in 31P NMR spectra

31P NMR spectroscopy has been used to probe the conformations around bound nucleotides in small GTPases28. When examining the slow hydrolyzable GTP analog GTPγS-bound protein, we previously revealed that RHOA exists in at least two conformational states29. To explore possible perturbations in the nucleotide-binding environment of the A161P and A161V RHOA mutants, we collected 31P NMR spectra of GDP-, GMPPNP-, and GTPγS-bound RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V, as well as RHOAWT, RHOAF30L, and RHOAT37A at 275 K and 298 K. We focused on examining the changes in both the chemical shift values and the shifts in the equilibrium between the conformational states.

When bound to GDP, all three mutants showed relatively unperturbed chemical shifts of β-phosphate, whereas α-phosphate showed a downfield shift (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Table 3). Interestingly, we observed a resonance close to the free nucleotide at the α-phosphate in RHOAF30L-GDP and RHOAA161V-GDP, suggesting a state with an open binding-site conformation near the α-phosphate. This resonance was unlikely from free GDP as there was no (or very little) βGDP resonance. In contrast, free GDP in the same buffer showed comparable resonances at both phosphates. We arbitrarily designated the downfield peak as state 1 and the other as state 2. In RHOAA161P-GDP, state 1 was negligible and its spectrum was similar to RHOAWT-GDP. The A161P and A161V mutations also influenced the 31P resonances of RHOA in complexes with GMPPNP (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Table 3). RHOAA161P exhibited only minor chemical shift changes, whereas RHOAA161V and RHOAF30L were significantly different for all three 31P shifts.

In contrast to the GDP- and GMPPNP-bound RHOA proteins, we observed only minor chemical shift changes in the GTPγS-bound mutant proteins (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 4c, and Supplementary Table 4). Rather, we identified shifts in the conformational equilibrium, with RHOAA161V and RHOAF30L being the most striking. The 31P peaks corresponding to different conformational states coalesced into a single peak at increasing temperatures, indicating that they underwent rapid exchange (Supplementary Fig. 5). When bound to GTPγS, RHOAA161P displayed a pattern similar to RHOAWT (Supplementary Table 4). State 2 is the active state with stronger effector affinity (Fig. 2d), consistent with previously published RHOAWT titration data29. RHOAA161V, on the other hand, displayed a different pattern. The resonance peak of α-phosphate was broadened and likely dominated by state 1, and titration of GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD resulted in a partial shift to state 2 (Fig. 2e). The resonance peak of β-phosphate indicates two or more possible states, with the dominant state most likely inactive and designated as state 1, and the downfield peak possibly active and designated as state 2 (Fig. 2e). Strikingly, we observed three peaks in the γ-phosphate resonance, with the dominant state designated as state 1’ in RHOAT37A and two other peaks as state 1 and state 2 in RHOAWT. The observation of both state 1’ and state 1 supports the hypothesis that the state 1 conformation in RHOAWT differs from RHOAT37A, which could be similar to the inactive HRAS state 1 conformation represented by RAST35S. RHOAF30L showed a pattern similar to that of RHOAA161V (Fig. 2f) and both have more heterogeneous states than RHOAWT and RHOAA161P (Fig. 2c).

RHOAA161V exhibits an enlarged nucleotide-binding pocket, whereas RHOAA161P displays altered nucleotide interaction in crystal structures

To better interpret our biochemical analyses, we solved the crystal structures of RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V in complex with GDP at 1.5 Å and 1.4 Å resolutions, respectively (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 5). Complexed ligands were confirmed with omit maps (Supplementary Fig. 6). We previously determined the crystal structures of RHOAWT in complex with GDP or GMPPNP (PDB IDs 6V6U and 6V6M)29. Similar to RHOAWT-GDP and RHOAWT-GMPPNP, GDP-bound RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V were packed in the space group P21, featuring the characteristic P36-Mg2+ coordination (Supplementary Fig. 7a) instead of the always observed T37 (T35 in RAS) in most small GTPase structures. Compared with RHOAWT-GDP, there was only minimal conformational variation for RHOAA161P-GDP and RHOAA161V-GDP. They all adopted a more “open” conformation for the N-terminal part of Switch I, with the nucleotide and P-loop highly exposed to the solvent. Unsurprisingly, two key residues, Y34 and Q63, adopted positions and interactions similar to those in the previous RHOAWT-GDP structure (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c).

Compared with RHOAWT-GDP, the A161V mutant displayed no change in nucleotide interactions based on the crystal structure. Two dioxane molecules from the crystallization solutions were identified to stabilize the guanine base and ribose in RHOAA161V-GDP (Supplementary Fig. 7e). RHOAA161P, however, displayed clear alterations in nucleotide interactions around the guanine group. In RHOAWT-GDP, RHOAWT-GMPPNP, and RHOAA161V-GDP, two conserved water molecules coordinated a hydrogen-bond network between the guanine base and the main chain of G17 and K118, and the side chain of N117 (Fig. 3b). In RHOAA161P, both water molecules were absent (Fig. 3c); instead, proline formed stacking interactions with the base. In addition, the dioxane molecule perpendicular to the guanine base in RHOAWT-GDP and RHOAA161V-GDP was missing in RHOAA161P-GDP (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Loop L10, where A161 resides, shows ~0.4–0.7 Å displacement in RHOAA161V and RHOAA161P in opposite directions (Supplementary Fig. 7d). The solvent-accessible area of the nucleotide was increased in RHOAA161V-GDP (1273.2 Å2) and decreased in RHOAA161P-GDP (1188.8 Å2), compared with RHOAWT-GDP (1249.1 Å2), suggesting RHOAA161V-GDP has a more open nucleotide-binding pocket.

Although RHOAA161V showed major changes around the phosphates in 31P NMR spectra, we did not observe significant changes in its crystal structure. This is unsurprising as both switch regions participate in crystal contacts in most RHOA apo crystal structures. Crystallization conditions favor a selected conformational state from multiple possible ones that RHOA could adopt in solution. Exploring alternative crystallization conditions may help reveal additional conformational states. Another key feature we examined was the internal mobility of the switch regions. Due to the limitation of crystal structures, this information is only weakly predictive of motions in solution. Both RHOAA161P-GDP and RHOAA161V-GDP crystal structures showed improved electron densities around the Switch I region compared with RHOAWT-GDP (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f), and the modified B-factor plot indicated slightly decreased flexibility in the N-terminus of Switch I (Supplementary Fig. 7g). The Switch II loop shows no change, and the effects of the mutants may have been masked by crystallographic contact.

RHOAA161V displays a more open Switch I, whereas RHOAA161P shows a closed Switch II in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

To complement the crystallographic and NMR analyses, the molecular dynamics (MD) trajectories of GDP-bound RHOAWT, RHOAA161P, and RHOAA161V systems were analyzed. 1 μs MD simulation of each system was performed, and all systems were stable during the simulation (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Several analyses, including root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSF), clustering of trajectories, and distances between selected switch residues and GDP, were performed throughout the simulation trajectories.

Higher RMSF are associated with larger structural fluctuations. According to the backbone RMSF, Switch I showed similar fluctuations for all systems except around E40, which showed higher fluctuations in RHOAA161P (Fig. 3d). In the Switch II region, RHOAA161P exhibited comparably smaller fluctuations, suggesting a possible transition to active-like state 2 in RHOAA161P that was less prominent in RHOAA161V. We also observed large fluctuations in the insert region for all systems, which is involved in activation and effector binding.

To characterize Switch I/II conformations, we calculated the distances between Y34/T37/G62 and GDP (Supplementary Table 6). From the distance distributions of Y34-to-GDP and T37-to-GDP, it can be seen that the mutated systems had more diverse Switch I conformations (Fig. 3e). They had a higher standard deviation of the Y34/T37 to GDP distances, which supports the conformational diversity of Switch I (Supplementary Table 6). Comparing the standard deviations of the distances, Switch I of RHOAA161P was more diverse than RHOAA161V. RHOAA161P had varying Switch I distances, which may indicate a transition between states 1 and 2, whereas RHOAA161V appeared to be in state 1 (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 8b). In contrast to the Switch I conformation, the Switch II conformation of RHOAWT was more diverse, with a large standard deviation in the G62-to-GDP distance (Supplementary Table 6). Switch II of RHOAA161P has a more closed conformation than that of RHOAWT. The RHOAWT, RHOAA161P, and RHOAA161V trajectories were clustered, and the three most populated clusters were analyzed further (Supplementary Table 7). The most populated cluster of RHOAA161P had a more closed Switch I, whereas Switch I of RHOAA161V was more open (Supplementary Fig. 8c), consistent with the crystallographic data.

With a combination of biochemical analyses, 31P NMR spectroscopy, crystallography, and MD simulations, we demonstrated that mutations A161P and A161V have different atomic mechanisms underlying their fast-cycling behavior. A161P alters the interaction with the nucleotide, reduces the fluctuation of Switch II, and barely shifts the conformation equilibrium when GTP-bound. On the other hand, A161V greatly impacts the conformations around the active site, opens the Switch I loop, and shifts the conformation towards the inactive states when bound to GTP.

RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V bind to effectors in the GDP-bound state

To evaluate the effects of RHOA mutations on signaling, we tested the binding activity of RHOAA161P, RHOAA161V, and RHOAWT to two known effectors that contain two different types of RHO binding domain (RBD): RHOTEKIN and ROCK2. We used the canonical in vitro RHOA activity assay with the RBD of RHOTEKIN or ROCK2 tagged to GST and immobilized to beads. Intriguingly, while RHOAWT showed complex formation with RHOTEKIN-RBD or ROCK2-RBD only in the presence of GMPPNP, RHOAA161V and RHOAA161P showed complex formation under both GDP and GMPPNP conditions. The binding of GDP-bound mutants was unlikely to be non-specific, as GST and GST-PAK1-RBD didn’t pull down the complex (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). RHOA proteins without the F25N mutation showed a similar pattern (Supplementary Fig. 9c). In contrast, the equivalent mutation A159V in RAC1 resulted in little binding to PAK1-RBD in the GDP state (Supplementary Fig. 9d, e), indicating that the functional consequence of a particular mutation is GTPase-specific.

The relative amount of complex forming with effectors between the mutants’ GDP- and GTP-bound states was inconsistent in pull-down assays, likely due to structural heterogenicity and impaired nucleotide binding to different extents of RHOA mutants. To quantify, we measured the direct interaction between RHOA and RHOTEKIN-RBD using microscale thermophoresis (MST). While RHOAWT-GDP showed no binding to RHOTEKIN-RBD, as expected, RHOAA161V and RHOAA161P bound RHOTEKIN-RBD even in the GDP-bound state (Fig. 4b). Although the binding curves of both nucleotide states of RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V were fitted the best with a two-state binding model, we do not have evidence that this resulted from two populations. The high KD of both mutants was similar to that of RHOAWT-GMPPNP, indicating that the mutations have no significant effect on effector affinity.

To further confirm the binding interaction, we titrated GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD to RHOAA161V-GDP and collected 31P NMR spectra. Titration resulted in slight chemical shift changes at both phosphates in RHOAA161V-GDP (Supplementary Fig. 10a), supporting the pull-down and MST results. The chemical shift changes were not observed in RHOAWT-GDP titrated with GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD or RHOAA161V-GDP titrated with GST (Supplementary Fig. 10b). However, titration of the effector did not clearly change the ratio of the states in RHOAA161V-GDP; thus, we could not assign a particular state as active or inactive.

RHOAA161V-GDP contains a population with “ON”-like conformation in 1H-15N HSQC NMR

To examine the overall conformational changes caused by the A161V mutation, we collected 1H-15N HSQC spectra for 15N-labeled RHOAWT and RHOAA161V in both GDP- and GMPPNP-bound forms (Supplementary Fig. 11a) and seek evidence of an “ON” conformation in RHOAA161V-GDP. RHOAA161P was not included due to its impaired binding to GMPPNP (Fig. 1c) and low protein yield. Based on previous assignments for RHOAΔC-GDP (BMRB ID 16668), RHOAΔC-GTPγS (BMRB ID 16669), and 1H-15N nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY) spectra, we were able to transfer most of the non-overlapping amide peak assignments to the 1H-15N HSQC spectra (Supplementary Figs. 12–15).

There were some changes in the spectrum of our RHOAWT-GDP protein (ΔC, F25N) compared with the published result of RHOAΔC-GDP (BMRB ID 16668)30, likely caused by the F25N mutation. For example, K18 and T19 in the P-loop and R68, L69, and R70 in Switch II were assigned in RHOAWT-GDP (ΔC, F25N), but not in RHOAΔC-GDP. In RHOAΔC-GTPγS (BMRB ID 16669), a significant number of peak assignments were missing in the P-loop, SAK motif, and switch regions. In RHOAWT-GMPPNP (ΔC, F25N), we were able to assign more peaks in these regions, including G12, D13, A15, C20, F30, Y34, V35, V38, D59, T60, E64, Y66, R68, R70, A161, and K162. This agrees with our previous finding that GMPPNP-bound RHOA had faster exchange rates between states than GTPγS-bound RHOA29, which resulted in sharper peaks. Therefore, we collected the spectra of RHOA proteins in complex with GMPPNP at 298 K to maximize detectable peaks in the flexible regions.

When comparing the 1H-15N HSQC spectra of all four samples, the first obvious difference between RHOAWT and RHOAA161V was in the spectral region of the Arg and Lys side chains (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). RHOAA161V is similar to RHOAWT-GMPPNP in both forms, with more missing sidechains and amide cross-peaks. Based on the normalized intensity plot for non-overlapping assigned HNs in all four samples (Fig. 5a), residues that vary the most in intensity are those located in the P-loop, the switch regions, and around the mutation, with the largest intensity change observed for V35 in Switch I. Like the activated RHOAWT-GMPPNP, RHOAA161V in both states showed reduced intensities in these regions compared with RHOAWT-GDP, indicating slower timescale motions.

Chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) of the assigned HNs were calculated to allow detailed analysis of conformational changes (Fig. 5b). We first compared RHOAWT vs. RHOAA161V in the same nucleotide-bound state (Fig. 5b, yellow and purple dots; Supplementary Fig. 16a, b). As expected, the A161V mutant did not exhibit an A161 amide peak in the spectrum. Other changes around the site of the mutations included shifted resonances for residues either flanking (S160 and T163, containing the SAK motif) or lying close to the mutation (residues G116, K119, D120, L121, and R122, containing the N/TKxD motif). The largest CSPs between RHOAWT and RHOAA161V were observed in the P-loop and the switch regions. Combining the intensity and CSP analyses (Fig. 5a, b), it is clear that a single A161V mutation influences the dynamics and conformation of the P-loop and switch regions. This is not surprising, as the SAK and N/TKxD motifs are known to have long-range allosteric effects on small GTPase activity31.

We further examined the key residues that underwent conformational changes in RHOAWT upon nucleotide exchange and the effect of A161V. CSP comparisons of RHOAWT-GDP vs. RHOAWT-GMPPNP showed that most of the changes occurred in amides located in the P-loop and preceding region, the switch regions, and amides in the α3/L7 region (Fig. 5b, green dots; and Supplementary Fig. 16c). The α3/L7 region (residues 90-110) is known to drive switch region motion32. Similar changes were observed for the CSPs between RHOAA161V-GDP and RHOAA161V-GMPPNP (Fig. 5b, blue dots; and Supplementary Fig. 16d). Interestingly, when comparing all four samples for these key residues, we found that A161V affected most of the key residues. Although the CSPs of RHOAA161V-GDP vs RHOAWT-GMPPNP (Supplementary Fig. 17a, cyan dots) were generally larger than the CSPs of RHOAA161V-GDP vs. RHOAWT-GDP (Supplementary Fig. 17a, yellow dots), RHOAA161V-GDP displayed decreased intensity for residues in the switch regions, similar to RHOAWT-GMPPNP (Fig. 5a). Most of the observable Switch II residues moved in the direction of RHOAWT-GMPPNP, including T60, E64, Y66, R68, S73, and Y74 (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Q29 was missing in both GDP- and GMPPNP-bound RHOAA161V, differing from a previous study in which the Q29 peak shifted with different nucleotides30.

A particularly interesting observation was the amide cross-peak of V38 (Fig. 5b, c). This peak shifted significantly between GDP- and GMPPNP-bound RHOAWT, making it a good indicator of active versus inactive states. Interestingly, there are two peaks for V38 in GDP-bound RHOAA161V with shifts similar to those of the two states, indicating that it has two distinct populations. In a model of the RHOA/RHOTEKIN-RBD complex generated by AlphaFold233,34, V38 is located at the interaction surface (Supplementary Fig. 18a). Another example is S160 (Fig. 5c). In addition, we observed NOESY differences in V35 HN (Supplementary Fig. 18b). The chemical shifts of the Y34 Ηβs and V35 methyl groups differed between RHOAWT and RHOAA161V, indicating changes in the chemical environment of the Switch I region. Further investigation is warranted to fully understand the implications of these observations.

We also collected 1H-15N HSQC spectra with the RHOA effector, RHOTEKIN, titrated at increasing ratios of RHOA: RHOTEKIN of 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2. Supplementary Fig. 19a shows the comparison of the spectra of each protein alone or with 2x RHOTEKIN titrated, except for RHOAA161V-GMPPNP, which was titrated with 1x RHOTEKIN, as the 2x RHOTEKIN titration sample had low-intensity peaks (see Fig. 5d). In contrast to RHOAWT-GDP, which showed the least peak intensity changes after titration with RHOTEKIN, the other three samples showed an increased peak intensity reduction (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 19b). At residues V38 and S160, titration of RHOTEKIN to RHOAA161V-GDP showed a significant intensity reduction for the corresponding “active” peaks similar to that of GMPPNP-bound RHOAA161V (Fig. 5e, f), further supporting the conclusion that RHOAA161V-GDP has an active population capable of directly interacting with effector proteins.

Discussion

In this study, we have examined mutational hotspot A161 of RHOA, which lies within the SAK motif, a highly conserved key element for nucleotide binding in small GTPases. A COSMIC database21 search revealed at least 11 cases of A161V and two cases of A161P of RHOA mutations in the T cell leukemia/lymphoma patients, whereas equivalent mutations in KRAS, NRAS, and RAC1 appear more frequently. We have applied a combined biochemical, MD simulation, and structural biology approach to reveal the distinct activation mechanisms of these two oncogenic mutant forms of RHOA. Moreover, we have found that these RHOA mutants contain unique conformations and display more than the expected fast-cycling phenotype, capable of interacting with effectors independent of GTP binding. The derived mechanism may have broad implications beyond RHOA, potentially applicable to other GTPases, adding to the well-known mechanism of action of mutations like KRAS-G12C which has become targetable after decades of mechanistic studies.

Both RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V displayed increased binding to RHOGEF and an increased rate of nucleotide exchange. The two mutants drive fast cycling via different mechanisms. RHOAA161P had an altered interaction with the guanine nucleotide base in the crystal structure but had only small conformational changes around the nucleotide phosphate groups (GDP or GTP) based on 31P NMR spectra. RHOAA161V, as well as RHOAF30L, on the other hand, displayed significant conformational changes based on the interpretation of the 31P resonance shifts in both GDP- and GTP-analog-bound states – RHOAA161V-GDP and RHOAF30L-GDP showed a state with open nucleotide-binding pocket and RHOAA161V-GTPγS and RHOAF30L-GTPγS shift towards some inactive states with open Switch I. Moreover, RHOAA161V had an increased solvent-accessible area near GDP in the crystal structure, and subsequent MD simulations supported a more open Switch I. Consistently, GTPγS-bound RAC1 F28L showed an increased solvent-accessible area for the nucleotide (1084.75 Å2, PDB ID 4GZM)35, than GMPPNP-bound RAC1 WT (896.09 Å2, PDB ID 3TH5)36. Another fast-cycling mutant, KRAS4BA146T (A146 in KRAS is equivalent to A161 in RHOA), exists in an open conformation with a pronounced extension of Switch I and β2 away from the body of the protein and loses Mg2+ coordination when GDP-bound (PDB ID 6BOF)37.

Strikingly, we found that both RHOAA161P-GDP and RHOAA161V-GDP interacted with effectors. There are several lines of supporting evidence: (1) effector pull-down assays, (2) 31P NMR of RHOAA161V-GDP titrated with GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD, (3) 1H-15N HSQC spectra of RHOAA161V-GDP with/without RHOTEKIN-RBD. This effector binding in the GDP state seems unrelated to the conformation equilibrium of RHOA-GTP. With mutations reducing affinity around the guanine base (like A161P) or opening the nucleotide-binding pocket (like A161V), GDP could slide and fill the phosphate binding pocket, mimicking GTP binding. However, this cannot explain why RAC1P29S and RAC1A159V do not behave the same. It is also possible that those mutations, especially A161V, which shows slower timescale motions in NMR, increase the energy barrier between the GDP-bound states, trapping RHOA in more conformational states, including inactive and active states. Indeed, both GDP-bound RHOAA161V and RHOAA161P are more structurally heterogeneous than RHOAWT, based on MD simulations.

Some GDP-bound small GTPase proteins have been observed to bind to downstream effectors. For example, RAS-GDP interacts with mutants of RAF38. Switch I, but not Switch II, in the RAS-GDP/RAF mutant complex, was found in a conformation similar to RAS-GTP39. In addition, there are two interesting cases in the PDB databank that GDP- and GTP analog-bound RHO adopt similar conformation when complexed with the same partner protein: RAC1-GDP or RAC1-GMPPNP with ARFAPTIN40, and RHOA-GDP or RHOA-GTPγS with TRPV441. In both cases, Switch I adopts an open conformation. RHOE mutants G23V and T25N in Dictyostlium, the equivalent of RHOA G17V and T19N, induced AKT phosphorylation in cells and directly activated mTORC2-mediated AKT phosphorylation in a GDP-bound form in vitro42. The possibility that RHOA G17V could be active in the GDP-bound form is interesting and can explain why seemingly gain-of-function mutations (A161V, A161P, C16F, and C16R) and loss-of-function mutations (G17V and A161E) can co-exist in the same cancer type – they all lead to signaling events. The activation of mutants in the GDP-bound form is likely not specific to RHOA. Mutations resulting in an “active” state have also been suggested by an MD study using principal component analysis (PCA) of KRAS4BG12V-GDP43. It visits the active state more frequently than KRAS4BG12D-GDP44. Our studies of the tumor-derived RHOA mutations highlight the possibility that GDP-bound mutant GTPases are also critical signaling molecules and targeting them in the GDP-bound state is equally important. Further exploration of RHOA in structural dynamics and its differences from other RHO GTPases and RAS could shed light on the unique mechanisms of widespread mutations in RHOA.

Decoding the molecular dynamics and conformational equilibrium of RHO GTPases to predict their biological properties is challenging because of their inherent conformational heterogeneity. A general principle cannot be established yet based on current studies. Increased state 1 in the GTP-bound state does not correlate with reduced affinity for effectors, nor increased nucleotide exchange. Nonetheless, oncogenic mutants, even at the same residue, were characterized by distinct conformational landscapes. These changes are almost impossible to address in vivo and therefore, in this study, we relied heavily on biophysical in vitro methods and computational MD simulations. The importance of studies on conformational space and dynamics was exemplified by the successful development of KRAS-G12C covalent inhibitors, which eventually led to the FDA-approved sotorasib (AMG510) and adagrasib (MRTX849)45,46. The targeted pocket, as well as other main ligand-binding pockets on RAS, are not readily observed in the absence of compound binding. Understanding the unique conformations observed in the mutants and exploiting the ensembles of RAS and RHO in both GDP- and GTP-bound states can facilitate the identification of target sites of various mutant-specific conformers.

Methods

Protein expression and purification

Plasmid encoding human full-length RHOA in pGEX-2T vector was a gift from Jonathan Chernoff (Addgene plasmid # 12202). It was used to introduce a stop codon at residue 182 and the F25N mutation using QuickChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) according to manufacturer’s instructions, resulting in a construct encoding human RHOA residue 1-181 and contains F25N mutation (pGEX-RHOAΔC, F25N). The F25N mutation is known to stabilize the expression of RHOA in bacteria and is commonly used in RHOA constructs for structural studies. For simplicity, we refer to this construct as RHOAWT throughout the paper. Additional mutations A161P, A161V, T37A, or F30L were further introduced based on RHOAWT (RHOAA161P, RHOAA161V, RHOAT37A and RHOAF30L). Plasmid encoding human full-length RAC1 in pGEX-2T vector was from the lab archive and mutations P29S and A159V were introduced using QuickChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit. Sequences of mutagenesis primers and catalog numbers of key reagents in this study are included in Supplementary Data 1.

RHOA proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli) BL21 (DE3) strain (New England Biolabs) with an N-terminal GST tag cleavable by the thrombin protease. The cells were grown in LB media with 100 μg/mL Ampicillin and induced at A600 = 0.8 with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) overnight at 20 °C. The cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in buffer A (25 mM Hepes pH7.4, 1 M NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5 µM GDP), supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 10 mM DTT and then sonicated. The supernatant was then put through the GST-affinity column (GSTrap FF, Cytiva), washed with buffer A, followed by cleavage of the GST tag with thrombin (Sigma) on column in cleavage buffer (50 mM Hepes pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM CaCl2) for 1 hour at room temperature. Cleaved RHOA proteins were then eluted out with buffer A, followed by a final purification step using size-exclusion chromatography (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg, Cytiva) with 20 mM Tris pH7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT. The protein samples were concentrated using centrifugal filters with a molecular weight cutoff of 10 KDa, and the concentrations of the proteins were subsequently measured by Bradford assay. Purified RHOA proteins are GDP-bound, as confirmed by 31P-NMR. To obtain RHOA-GTP, RHOA bound-GDP was exchanged to non-hydrolyzable GTP analog GMPPNP (Sigma) or slow-hydrolyzable GTP analog GTPγS (Sigma) using alkaline phosphatase coupled to agarose beads (Sigma) as previously described47. Briefly, RHOA-GDP, 10-fold GTP analogs, and CIP beads were rotated in ~1 mL buffer containing 40 mM Tris pH7.5, 200 mM (NH4)2SO4, 10 μM ZnCl2, 5 mM DTT overnight at 4 °C. After exchange, the supernatant was subjected to size-exclusion chromatography. The presence of GMPPNP or GTPγS was confirmed by 31P-NMR. RAC1 proteins were purified similarly.

RHOTEKIN RBD (residues 7-89), ROCK2 RBD (residues 945-1096), p190 GAP domain (residues 1-251), and PAK1 RBD (residues 67-150) were constructed in pGEX-KG vector and were expressed as described above for RHOA. The cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in buffer B (25 mM Hepes pH7.4, 1 M NaCl), supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 10 mM DTT, and then sonicated. The supernatant was then put through a GST-affinity column (GSTrap FF, Cytiva), washed with buffer B, then eluted in 100 mM Tris-HCl pH8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM reduced glutathione. Eluted proteins were then purified by size-exclusion chromatography (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg, Cytiva) with 20 mM Tris pH7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. The protein samples were concentrated using centrifugal filters with a molecular weight cutoff of 30 KDa, and the concentrations of the proteins were subsequently measured by Bradford assay.

A modified pMAL vector (pMALc2H10T) expressing LARG DH/PH fragment (residues 766–1139) was obtained from Dr. John Tesmer (Dept. Chemistry and Biochemistry, The University of Texas at Austin). The pMALc2H10T expression vector was generated by inserting oligonucleotides encoding a decahistidine (H10) tag followed by a TEV protease recognition site between AvaI and EcoRI of the pMALc2X vector (New England Biolabs)48. Expression and purification of MBP-LARG-DH/PH were carried out as previously described49 but without the TEV cleavage step. Briefly, MBP-LARG-DH/PH protein was expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain (New England Biolabs). The cells were grown in LB media with 100 μg/mL Ampicillin and 0.2% glucose, induced at A600 = 0.8 with 0.2 mM IPTG overnight at 20 °C. After standard affinity chromatography for His-tagged proteins using Histrap FF column (Cytiva), the MBP-LARG-DH/PH fusion protein was further cleaned up using size-exclusion chromatography (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg, Cytiva) with 20 mM Tris pH7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT.

GST pull-down assays

Purified GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD, GST-ROCK2-RBD, GST-p190-GAP, and MBP-LARG-DH/PH were used to pull down RHOA proteins. For GST-fusion proteins, approximately 20 μg of each GST-fused protein was immobilized on agarose-glutathione beads (Sigma) by incubating 30 min at 4 °C, washed three times in a buffer containing 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.3% Triton-X100, 1 mM DTT, 10 μM GDP or GMPPNP, and then mixed with 5 μg of purified RHOA proteins for 1 h at 4 °C. For MBP-LARG-DH/PH, approximately 10 μg protein was immobilized on amylose resin (New England Biolabs) by incubating 30 min at 4 °C, washed three times in a buffer containing 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl, 0.3% Triton-X100, 1 mM DTT, 5 mM EDTA or 5 mM MgCl2, with or without 10 μM GDP or GMPPNP, and then mixed with 2 μg of purified RHOA proteins for 1 h at 4 °C. For both cases, after incubation, beads were washed three times with the same buffer, boiled with loading buffer at 95 °C for 5 min to elute the beads. The eluted mixtures were then subjected to 4–15% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF for Western blot analysis using anti-RHOA antibody (Cell signaling Technology). The immune complexes were visualized by using chemiluminescence reagents (Bio-RAD). The GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD, GST-ROCK2-RBD, GST-p190-GAP, or the MBP-LARG-DH/PH input were obtained by stain-free imaging of the gel before transfer. Purified GST-PAK1-RBD was used to pull down RAC1 proteins with similar protocols with 10 μg GST-PAK1-RBD immobilized and 1 μg RAC1 proteins used.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) binding assay

A NanoTemper Monolith Instrument (NT.115) was used for measuring thermophoresis. In this instrument an infra-red Laser (IR-Laser) beam couples into the path of light (i.e., fluorescence excitation and emission) with a dichroic mirror and is focused into the sample fluid through the same optical element used for fluorescence imaging. The IR laser is absorbed by the aqueous solution in the capillary and locally heats the sample with a 1/e2 diameter of 25 μm. Up to 24 mW of laser power where used to heat the sample, without damaging the bio-molecules50. RHOA proteins were labeled using Maleimide labeling kit (NanoTemper Technologies, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol Thermophoresis of the RHOA proteins in the presence of varying concentrations of RHOTEKIN-RBD was analyzed for 30 s. Measurements were performed at room temperature and standard deviation was calculated from three or more independent experiments. Data were normalized to ΔFnorm [‰] (10*(Fnorm(bound) - Fnorm (unbound))). The dissociation constant (KD) was obtained by nonlinear regression fit using a one-site specific binding model for RHOAWT, and a two-site specific binding model for RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V in Prism (version 9.3.1, GraphPad Software).

Nucleotide dissociation assays

500 nM purified GDP-bound RHOA proteins in buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA were incubated with 250 nM of BODIPY FL-GDP (Invitrogen) at 25 °C for about 2 h, then MgCl2 was added to a final concentration of 5 mM. The baseline of the fluorescence signal was then monitored for 10 mins and made sure to be constant. For exchange, 5 μM GMPPNP (10-fold) was added in the presence of 5 mM MgCl2 or 10 mM EDTA. Fluorescence intensity changes were monitored at 1-minute intervals for about 30 min using an EnVision Multimode Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer) with excitation/emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm. For data processing, the fluorescence intensities after adding free GMPPNP were subtracted by buffer baseline reading and then normalized as percentages of the baseline reading subtracted by buffer baseline reading, plotted against time. Standard deviation was calculated from four or more independent experiments. For most cases, nonlinear regression and the dissociation-one phase exponential decay model were used to obtain the pseudo first-order rate constant using Prism (GraphPad Software). The exception is for RHOAF30L, RHOAA161P, and RHOAA161V dissociation under EDTA condition, nonlinear regression and the two-phase decay model were used to obtain the pseudo first-order rate constant.

Nucleotide association assays

For association, 25 μL 0.2 μM BODIPY-FL-GDP (Invitrogen) in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 5 mM MgCl2 was monitored for 10 minutes as fluorescence baseline using an EnVision Multimode Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer) with excitation/emission wavelength of 485 and 535 nm. 25 μL 2 μM purified GDP-bound RHOA proteins in the same buffer were then added and fluorescence intensity changes were monitored at 1-minute intervals for about 30 min. For data processing, the fluorescence intensities after adding RHOA proteins were subtracted by the baseline reading and then normalized as percentages of the same baseline reading and plotted against time. The standard deviation was calculated from ten independent experiments. Curves were fitted with nonlinear regression and the one-phase association model to obtain the pseudo first-order rate constant using Prism (GraphPad Software). The resulting pseudo first-order dissociation and association rates are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The rates were compared between proteins using unpaired t-tests in Prism.

GTPase activity assay

Intrinsic and GAP-stimulated RHOA GTPase activities were measured using the GTPase-GloTM Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For intrinsic GTPase assay, 1μg of GDP-bound RHOA proteins was incubated with 5 μM GTP substrate in 10 μL GTPase/GAP reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCL, pH7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, and 5 mM MgCl2) for various time points at room temperature. Upon completion of the GTPase reaction, equal amount of GTPase-Glo reagent (10 μL) is added and incubated with shaking for 30 min at room temperature. The GTPase-Glo reagent contains an enzyme that converts the remaining GTP to ATP. Subsequently, 2-fold amount of detection reagent (20 μL) is added to detect the ATP generated. After 5–10 min at room temperature, luminescence signal is read using an EnVision Multimode Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer). For dose responding curves, reactions were assembled with various amounts of wildtype and mutant RHOA proteins in the GTPase/GAP buffer without or with the presence of 2 μM GST-p190-GAP and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Each experiment was repeated for at least four times. For hydrolysis rate experiments, the luminescence reading was normalized as percentage of buffer control and fitted with nonlinear regression and the dissociation-one phase exponential decay model with constraints K0 = 100, plateau between 0 and 10% to obtain the pseudo first order rate constants using Prism. For dose titration experiments, luminescence reading was normalized as percentage of the maximum luminescence reading of the same protein and fitted with nonlinear regression and the [inhibitor] vs. response model with constraints IC50 > 0 to obtain the EC50, which is the concentration of RHOA to produce 50% of maximum hydrolysis of 5 μM GTP substrate26. The hydrolysis rate and EC50 were compared between proteins using unpaired t-tests in Prism.

Crystallization, structure determination, and refinement

Crystals of RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V in complex with GDP were grown by hanging drop method in a 1:1 volume ratio of purified RHOA proteins and reservoir solution containing 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH8.3), 16–20% PEG 8000, and 15% (v/v) 1,4-dioxane at 277 K. Crystals were equilibrated in a cryoprotectant buffer containing reservoir buffer with 30% (v/v) PEG 8000 and were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. X-ray data were collected with a wavelength of 0.97931 Å at 100 K using Beamline 31-D-D at the Advanced Photon Source (APS). The datasets were indexed and integrated using Mosflm (version 7.2.0)51 and Scala (version 3.3.22)52. The structures of RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V in complex with GDP were determined by molecular replacement with the program Phaser53 using a searching model based on the structure of RHOA-GDP (PDB ID 1FTN)54, Refinement was performed using PHENIX (version 1.19.2)55 and models were built with COOT (version 0.8.9.2)56 in alternative rounds. 97.74% and 98.31% of residues are Ramachandran favored, and 2.26% and 1.69% of residues are Ramachandran allowed in final models of RHOAA161P-GDP and RHOAA161V-GDP, respectively. In the structures of RHOAA161P and RHOAA161V, the Switch I region is relatively better defined compared to the previous RHOAWT-GDP crystal structure. Residues in the Switch I region were rebuilt on their omit map individually. In both structures, the Switch II region is well defined. The additional GS insert in the N-terminus and two N-terminal residues of RHOA are missing in the densities implying their disorder. The final model and scaled reflection data were deposited at the PDB (rcsb.org) with code 8FPX for RHOAA161P-GDP and 8FPW for RHOAA161V-GDP. Data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Structural figures are generated with PyMOL (version 3.0, Schrödinger, LLC).

31P NMR acquisition

0.5 mL of 0.5–0.8 mM solution of RHOA proteins (GDP-, GMPPNP- or GTPγS-bound) in 20 mM Tris pH7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 10% D2O were used for the NMR data collection. The protein-free nucleotide samples containing approximately 5 mM nucleotide (GDP, GMPPNP, or GTPγS) in the above buffer were used to set up the NMR parameters and served as controls. For experiments on RHOA complexes with effector, GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD was used instead of RHOTEKIN-RBD as the latter has poor stability at higher concentrations during NMR data acquisition. We have previously shown that GST does not affect RHOA 31P NMR spectra29. During the titration experiment, GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD was mixed in, and the sample was concentrated back to 0.5 mL using centrifugal filters after 15 min incubation at room temperature.

One-dimensional 31P NMR spectra were acquired at 275 K and 298 K on a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz spectrometer with 5 mm, BBI room temperature probe or BBO Prodigy probe (Bruker Analytik, Rheinstetten, Germany) with a total spectral width of 50 ppm for GDP- and GMPPNP-bound RHOA proteins or 100 ppm for GTPγS-bound RHOA proteins. NMR spectrometer is calibrated every week using 0.1 mM TSP in 10% D2O as standard with its 1H peak set to 0.0 ppm. Each probe temperature change was monitored for 10 min minimum to ensure stability. Before data collection, the sample was inserted and its temperature was stabilized for a minimum of 10 min; the signal was locked with H2O and D2O, tuned and matched on the 31P channel, and shimmed. The data was acquired using 1D pulse program “zgpg30” from Bruker pulse library with the following parameters: time domain (TD) = 30k, DS = 8, acquisition time (AQ) = 0.6 s, relaxation delay (D1) = 3 s, and adjusted number of scans (NS) depending on the sample concentration. For our samples, NS ranges from 1k to 8k. All spectra were acquired, processed, phased, and baseline-corrected using Topspin (version 4.0.3) software (Bruker). For spectral comparison, all spectra were aligned with the inorganic phosphate Pi peak, which was likely coming from GTPγS degradation or impurities from the guanine nucleotides.

1H-15N HSQC NMR acquisition

To obtain 15N-labeled RHOA for 1H-15N HSQC NMR experiments, bacterial cells were grown in M9 minimal medium with 15NH4Cl as the sole nitrogen source. Purified RHOA proteins were GDP-bound. GMPPNP- and GTPγS-bound RHOA proteins were obtained by nucleotide exchanging using alkaline phosphatase coupled to agarose beads (Sigma)29,57. NMR samples with a final volume of 0.5 mL were made with 0.4–0.6 mM RHOA in 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 10% D2O, and 2 mM GDP, GMPPNP, or GTPγS in 5 mm Shigemi tubes. RHOTEKIN-RBD was used for the effector titration experiments, as 1H-15N HSQC spectra data collection took ~1 h compared to the less sensitive 31P NMR spectra that took 3–12 h each. The smaller molecular weight of the RHOA/RHOTEKIN-RBD complex resulted in a better signal-to-noise ratio than the RHOA/GST-RHOTEKIN-RBD complex.

All data were collected at 298 K on a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz spectrometer with a 5 mm BBO Prodigy probe. Pulse program “hsqcetfpgpsi2” from Bruker pulse library was used for 1H-15N HSQC experiments with the following parameters: spectral width (SW) = 20.0 ppm (H) and 51.4 ppm (N), acquisition time (AQ) = 42.6 ms (H) and 20.5 ms (N), relaxation delay (D1) = 2 s, number of scans (NS) = 8. 3D-NOESY data were collected with the pulse program “noesyhsqcf3gpsi3d” from Bruker pulse library with the same 15N-labeled samples. The parameters were mixing time = 100 ms, relaxation delay (D1) = 1 s, and number of scans (NS) = 8. For H(N), N, H dimensions, spectral width (SW) = 20.0, 51.4, and 14.0 ppm, acquisition time (AQ) = 42.6, 14.4, and 15.2 ms.

GDP- and GMPPNP-bound RHOAWT amide HN chemical shifts were assigned by comparison with published RHOAΛC peak assignments (BMRB ID 16668 and 16669)30 and confirmed by comparisons of sequential 15N NOESY strips. RHOAA161V assignments were made by comparing 15N NOESY strips with those of GDP- and GMPPNP-bound RHOAWT. The buffer condition of BMRB 16668 and 16669 (20 mM HEPES buffer, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM TCEP, 10% D2O, pH 7) was very close to ours. We were able to transfer assignments for 68.8%, 61.8%, 52.9%, and 52.9% of HNs in RHOAWT-GDP, RHOAWT-GMPPNP, RHOAA161V-GDP, and RHOAA161V-GMPPNP, respectively. The intensities of the non-overlapping assigned peaks for all four 1H-15N HSQC spectra were normalized to the average peak intensity for each spectrum (Fig. 5a). For the RHOTEKIN titration experiments, peak intensity changes for all non-overlapping residues of RHOA titrated with RHOTEKIN at various ratios were normalized (I/Io) by dividing the peak intensities in the corresponding RHOA samples prior to the addition of RHOTEKIN (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 19b). The CSPs for Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 17a were calculated using the formula Δδ (ppm) =0.5×ΔδH2.562+ΔδN0.54258. NMRFAM-SPARKY (version 3.115)59 was used for peak assignment and Topspin (version 4.0.3, Bruker) was used for data acquisition and processing.

MD simulation

All simulations were performed according to the protocol described in our previous studies60–62. The updated CHARMM program with the modified all-atom force field (version 36 m)63–65 was utilized in the MD simulations on RHOA-GDP systems in explicit solvent. CHARMM is a biomolecular simulation program that has gained considerable traction within the scientific community due to its ability to perform atomic-level simulations of various biomolecular systems. The small GTPase RHOA proteins have well-defined crystal structures for both the wild type and its mutants, which are currently available for analysis. Our MD simulations utilized the crystal structures of the catalytic domain of GDP-loaded RHOAWT (PDB ID 6V6U), RHOAA161P (PDB ID 8FPX), and RHOAA161V (PDB ID 8FPW). For all systems, the F25N mutation was retained for consistency with the experimental data.

The initial RHOA configurations were solvated using the TIP3P model in an isometric unit cell box of 90 × 90 × 90 Å3; 51 Na+ and 43 Cl− ions were added to the simulation systems to neutralize the system with a final concentration of 100 mM NaCl. The simulation system contains approximately 74,000 atoms, including approximately 24,000 water molecules. The solvent was included in 10,000 steps of minimization and 50,000 steps of dynamic cycles with a step size of 2 fs and most backbone atoms were constrained. Here, no constraints were applied to N25 or the surrounding residues to let it stabilize. Next, the harmonic constraints were gradually lifted with long-range electrostatic calculations using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method. The simulation was controlled at 310 K with Langevin temperature control and at 1 atm with Nosé−Hoover Langevin piston method. The production runs for the systems, each with a 1 μs duration, were completed using Nanoscale Molecular Dynamics (NAMD, version 2.13)66 parallel-computing code under the NPT ensemble (constant number of atoms, pressure, and temperature). It has been argued that in general, μs simulation time frames may not be sufficient to observe real-time protein dynamics. Here, the production runs of μs were sufficient to sample the ensembles of conformations since these RHOA systems quickly reached equilibrium within the simulation time frame. In addition to the primary set of simulations, two supplementary sets of simulations were conducted for the same systems to assess reproducibility. Our findings indicated that the results were consistent between replicates, thereby providing evidence of the reproducibility of the critical results (Supplementary Fig. 20). Thus, the statistical data presented here were derived from the primary set of simulations. The RHOA systems typically reached equilibrium after 50 ns (Supplementary Fig. 8a), and thus averages were taken afterward. All simulations were performed using the Biowulf cluster at the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD, USA). VMD (version 1.9.4)67 and Chimera (version 1.13.1)68 were used to analyze the simulation systems. RHOA coordinates of the starting points and representative relaxed conformations for RHOAWT, RHOAA161P, and RHOAA161V are included in Supplementary Data 2.

Statistics and reproducibility

All biochemical assays are carried out for at least three independent repeats. Statistical analyses were performed using unpaired t-tests in Prism (GraphPad Software). No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses.
