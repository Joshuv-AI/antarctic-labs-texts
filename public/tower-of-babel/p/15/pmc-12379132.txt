Antibiofilm from phyllosphere improves survival and gut health of juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper challenged with Vibrio harveyi
Authors: Pande Gde Sasmita Julyantoro, Diana Elizabeth Waturangi, Nurmaya Papuangan, I Gusti Ngurah Permana, Ketut Mahardika
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The widespread use of antibiotics in aquaculture has become increasingly ineffective due to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, residual contamination, the disruption of aquatic microbiota, and international trade restrictions on antibiotic-laden products. As a sustainable alternative, this study investigated the effects of antibiofilm compounds derived from phyllosphere bacteria on the culture performance of juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus) challenged with Vibrio harveyi. Juveniles (1.973 ± 0.177 g; 3.421 ± 0.177 cm) were acclimated and distributed into 12 fiber tanks (300 L) at a density of 200 fish per tank. Fish were fed commercial feed supplemented with antibiofilm at concentrations of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 mL/kg for four weeks, alongside an unsupplemented control group. Culture was maintained under a flow-through system (10 mL/s). Evaluated parameters included growth, survival, deformity rate, skin pigmentation, water quality, total bacterial and Vibrio abundance, and intestinal morphology, which was assessed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and histological analysis (goblet cell count, mucosal fold height, and muscularis thickness). A challenge test was conducted by intraperitoneal injection of V. harveyi (2.3 × 10⁸ CFU/mL; 0.1 mL/fish), followed by a seven-day observation period. The results indicate that while growth was not significantly affected (P > 0.05), fish receiving 0.01 mL/kg antibiofilm exhibited significantly higher survival (P < 0.05) during both the feeding trial and the post-challenge period. Compared to the control group, deformity rates were significantly lower (P < 0.05), and skin brightness improved in all antibiofilm treatments. SEM analysis revealed improved intestinal microvilli structure in treated groups, indicative of enhanced gut health. Furthermore, a reduced abundance of Vibrio spp. and increased blood granulocyte levels in antibiofilm-fed fish suggest enhanced immune responses. These findings support the potential of antibiofilm supplementation as a promising, eco-friendly strategy for disease prevention and health management in aquaculture.

Introduction

The pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus) has emerged as a premium aquaculture species in Asia due to its exceptional growth performance, high-quality flesh, and increasing market demand, particularly in China and Southeast Asia1,2. However, the intensification of aquaculture practices has been accompanied by a higher incidence of disease outbreaks, with Vibrio harveyi identified as one of the predominant pathogens causing vibriosis in grouper culture systems3,4. This Gram-negative, biofilm-forming marine bacterium can evade host immune defenses and establish chronic infections, often leading to elevated mortality and substantial economic losses in marine aquaculture operations5,6. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop effective and sustainable disease management strategies to support the continued viability and productivity of grouper aquaculture under intensified farming systems7.

Conventional disease management in aquaculture has predominantly relied on antibiotics. However, their extensive and often unregulated use has contributed to the emergence of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) bacteria, environmental contamination, and growing international restrictions on antibiotic residues in aquaculture products7,8. Aquatic environments are recognized as hotspots for the development and dissemination of AMR, exacerbated by horizontal gene transfer among bacterial communities9. Although alternatives such as vaccines and probiotics have been proposed, their effectiveness remains inconsistent and context-dependent, influenced by host species, environmental conditions, and pathogen load10,11. In recent years, antibiofilm agents have emerged as promising tools for disease control by targeting quorum sensing pathways and inhibiting biofilm formation, which are key mechanisms contributing to bacterial virulence and persistence, without exerting selective pressure for resistance12,13. Despite these advancements, the application of antibiofilm compounds in aquafeeds for preventive disease management remains limited and requires further exploration.

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of bioactive compounds from bacteria as antibiofilm. Phyllosphere bacteria, which are microorganisms inhabiting plant leaf surfaces, are a source of antibiofilm agents for managing aquaculture pathogens. Lukman et al.14 identified six phyllosphere bacterial strains exhibiting anti-quorum sensing and antibiofilm activities against Aeromonas hydrophila, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Vibrio harveyi. The metabolites from strains JB 3B and EJB 5 F significantly inhibit and disrupt the biofilms of A. hydrophila. Similarly, Nathalia and Waturangi15 reported that eight phyllosphere bacterial isolates possessed quorum quenching and antibiofilm activities; notably, strain JB 17B effectively inhibited biofilm formation of A. hydrophila and V. harveyi, while strain JB 3B targeted S. agalactiae. Furthermore, Raissa et al.16 demonstrated that crude extracts from Actinobacteria isolates effectively control the biofilms of various aquaculture pathogens through quorum sensing disruption. These findings underscore the potential of phyllosphere-derived metabolites as innovative, eco-friendly alternatives to traditional antibiotics in aquaculture, offering promising strategies for managing biofilm-associated infections without contributing to antimicrobial resistance.

This study investigates the culture performance of juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus) fed diets supplemented with antibiofilm agents when challenged with V. harveyi, a major biofilm-forming pathogen associated with high mortality and persistent infections in marine aquaculture17. Among the key performance indicators assessed are growth rate, survival rate, incidence of deformity, intestinal morphology, and immune responses. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of dietary antibiofilm supplementation in enhancing disease resistance and maintaining fish health under pathogenic stress. Recent studies have shown that natural antibiofilm compounds derived from phyllosphere bacteria can disrupt quorum sensing and biofilm formation, reducing bacterial pathogenicity without promoting antimicrobial resistance14,16. This approach represents a promising and environmentally sustainable alternative to conventional antibiotic use, in line with global priorities for responsible aquaculture practices18,19.

Results

This study was carried out in two phases: a feeding trial and a bacterial challenge test. The feeding trial evaluated the effects of antibiofilm-supplemented diets on the performance of juvenile pearl gentian grouper, including growth (in terms of length and weight), survival, skin brightness, deformity rate, hematological profile, bacterial abundance, water quality, and intestinal condition, as assessed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In the subsequent challenge test, fish from the feeding trial were exposed to V. harveyi to assess the protective effects of antibiofilm supplementation. Post-challenge survival, hematological response, bacterial load, and intestinal histomorphology (goblet cell count, mucosal fold height, and muscularis thickness) were measured.

Fish growth and survival

The graph in Fig. 1 compares the body length and survival rate of fish subjected to different treatments, including varying concentrations of antibiofilm solution (ABF) mixed with feed (0.01 mL/kg, 0.05 mL/kg, 0.1 mL/kg) and a control group without ABF. The body length of all groups increased over time, with the 0.1 ml/kg group reaching the highest average size of 8.54 cm by the end of the experiment. The survival rate remained high across treatments, with the 0.01 ml/kg group exhibiting the highest survival at 96%, while the control group showed a slightly lower survival rate of 88.2%. The use of ABF appeared to improve both growth and survival, with the 0.01 ml/kg dosage showing the most balanced performance, indicating it may be the optimal level for maximizing fish growth and health in aquaculture.

The brightness of fish color

The administration of antibiofilm compounds through fish feed noticeably improved the brightness and vibrancy of fish coloration. This enhancement was quantitatively measured using the Toca Color Finder, which showed increased color brightness intensity values in fish that received the antibiofilm treatment compared to the control group (Fig. 2). The brighter coloration suggests that the antibiofilm has a positive influence on fish health and pigmentation pathways, possibly by reducing microbial attachment and enhancing nutrient absorption. These findings highlight the potential for antibiofilm feed additives to manage biofilm-related issues and enhance the aesthetic and market value of cultured fish.

Fish deformity

An experiment was conducted to evaluate the effect of incorporating antibiofilm agents into fish feed on the incidence of deformities in juvenile grouper fish. The key parameters monitored for the occurrence of skeletal deformities were lordosis, scoliosis, and jaw malformations. It was hypothesized that the antibiofilm supplementation could reduce deformities by limiting the presence of harmful biofilm-forming pathogens that negatively affect fish development. The expected outcomes were a decrease in deformity rates and an improvement in fish morphology in the treatment groups. The bar chart illustrates the effects of different concentrations of antibiofilm additives in fish feed on the percentage of deformities observed in fish (Fig. 3). Four groups were compared: fish fed with antibiofilm at concentrations of 0.01 mL/kg, 0.05 mL/kg, and 0.1 mL/kg, along with a control group that received no antibiofilm treatment. The control group exhibited the highest deformity rate at approximately 8.9%, with a relatively large error margin. In contrast, the group fed 0.1 mL/kg antibiofilm showed the lowest deformity rate (around 3%), indicating a significant reduction. The 0.05 mL/kg and 0.01 mL/kg treatments yielded intermediate deformity rates of approximately 5% and 6.5%, respectively. Overall, the results suggest that increasing the concentrations of antibiofilm in fish feed leads to a progressive reduction in the incidence of deformities, with the 0.1 mL/kg dose being the most effective in minimizing skeletal abnormalities.

The histomorphology, goblet cells, mucosal folds, and muscular thickness of juvenile grouper intestinal tissue

The histomorphology images (Fig. 5) depict the intestinal structure of the juvenile Pearl Gentian Hybrid grouper after feeding with different concentrations of antibiofilm additive. The parameters observed include the number of goblet cells (GCs), mucosal fold height (MF), and muscularis thickness (MT). In the control group (D), the intestinal surface appeared to be less developed than in the treatment conditions, as there were fewer goblet cells, shorter mucosal folds, and thinner muscular tissue. This indicates suboptimal gut health and potentially lower mucosal immunity. Fish fed with 0.01 mL/kg antibiofilm (A) showed a slight increase in mucosal fold height and goblet cell presence. The muscularis layer also appeared to be slightly thicker than observed in the control condition. The 0.05 mL/kg antibiofilm group (B) exhibited numerous goblet cells, well-developed mucosal folds, and increased muscularis thickness, indicating enhanced mucosal structure and function. The most pronounced improvements were observed in the 0.1 mL/kg antibiofilm group (C), where the mucosal folds were the tallest, goblet cells were the most abundant, and the muscular layer was the thickest among all groups. This suggests that the highest concentration of antibiofilm supports the best intestinal development, likely promoting better nutrient absorption and stronger mucosal defense mechanisms. Overall, the results indicate that increasing the concentration of antibiofilm in feed has a positive influence on intestinal histomorphology in juvenile grouper, with the 0.1 mL/kg dosage producing the most beneficial effects on gut structure.

Water quality

The water quality parameters (Table 1) measured during the one-month culture period of the experiment involving the addition of antibiofilm to fish feed remained within optimal and stable ranges across all treatment groups, ensuring consistent environmental conditions for fish growth and health. Across treatments, pH values ranged from 7.64 to 8.06, indicating slightly alkaline water suitable for most aquaculture species. Dissolved oxygen (DO) levels were maintained between 4.09 and 4.93 mg/L, which is adequate to support fish metabolism and overall health. The temperature remained consistent across all groups, ranging from 28.83 °C to 30.53 °C, providing a thermally stable environment that was favorable for fish growth. Salinity was maintained at a constant level of 32–34 g/L in all treatments, indicating that the experiment was conducted in brackish or marine water conditions, with no fluctuations in salinity. Oxygen saturation levels varied slightly among treatments, ranging from 64.07 to 78.83%, all of which fall within acceptable limits to ensure proper respiration and water aeration. These consistent water quality conditions indicate that any differences observed in fish deformity rates were likely due to the effects of the antibiofilm feed treatments rather than environmental variability.

Challenge test

The graph in Fig. 7 illustrates the survival rate of juvenile Pearl Gentian Hybrid Grouper over seven days following a challenge with the pathogenic bacterium V. harveyi under different dietary treatments containing varying antibiofilm additives. Fish fed the highest concentration of antibiofilm (0.1 mL/kg) consistently displayed the highest survival rate throughout the challenge period, maintaining over 70% survival by Day 7. Fish fed at concentrations of 0.05 mL/kg and 0.01 mL/kg exhibited moderately lower survival rates, with approximately 65% and 55% survival, respectively. The control group, which received no antibiofilm additive, exhibited the most significant decline in survival, with the survival rate dropping to approximately 45% by Day 7. These results suggest a dose-dependent protective effect of the antibiofilm additive, with higher concentrations providing better resistance to V. harveyi infection. The supplementation of antibiofilm at 0.1 mL/kg appears to significantly enhance disease resilience in juvenile grouper, likely by reducing bacterial colonization or boosting immune response.

Bacterial abundance

The bar graph in Fig. 9 illustrates the logarithmic bacterial density (CFU/mL) of total bacteria and Vibrio spp. in the culture water of juvenile grouper fed with different concentrations of an antibiofilm (before the challenge test). The total bacterial count remained relatively stable across all treatment groups (0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 mL/kg), fluctuating between log 3.5 and 3.7 CFU/mL, similar to the control group, which exhibited a slightly lower but comparable level. This indicates that the antibiofilm additive had a minimal impact on the bacterial population, as antibiofilm controls the formation of biofilm and/or the establishment of an existing biofilm. However, a notable reduction in total Vibrio density was observed in all antibiofilm-treated groups compared to the control. The 0.05 mL/kg treatment displayed the lowest Vibrio density (just above log 2.0 CFU/mL), closely followed by the 0.1 mL/kg group. The 0.01 mL/kg treatment also exhibited a lower Vibrio level than the control group, but a slightly higher level than the other treated groups. In contrast, the control group, which received no antibiofilm additive, had the highest Vibrio density, at nearly log 2.7 CFU/mL. These findings suggest that while the antibiofilm additive did not significantly alter the total bacterial abundance, it effectively reduced the potentially pathogenic Vibrio spp. population, with the 0.05 mL/kg dose being the most effective in suppressing Vibrio levels in the water.

Discussion

Grouper fish (family Serranidae) are highly valued in aquaculture due to their fast growth rates, high market demand, and robust flavor. However, intensive farming in aquaculture systems faces several challenges, including the proliferation of biofilms. Biofilms, consisting of microbial communities adhering to surfaces, can negatively impact water quality and fish health, reducing growth rates and increasing mortality. Various antibiofilm strategies have been explored to mitigate these issues, including the use of antibiofilm-enriched feed. This study aimed to investigate the impact of different concentrations of such feed on the growth and survival of hybrid grouper fish, providing insights into the optimization of feed formulations to improve aquaculture outcomes.

Figure 1 illustrates the effects of dietary antibiofilm supplementation at varying concentrations (0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 mL/kg) on the body length growth of juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus) over four weeks. All experimental groups, including the control, exhibited a consistent and progressive increase in body length, indicative of normal somatic development under standardized rearing conditions. However, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) in body length were observed among the treatment groups throughout the culture period. This outcome suggests that incorporating antibiofilm agents at the tested concentrations neither impeded nor significantly promoted linear growth. These results also suggest that antibiofilm compounds confer physiological benefits, such as modulating the gut microbiota and reducing the presence of Vibrio spp. density, and improving intestinal histoarchitecture (as supported by ancillary data presented in other figures). Their influence on somatic growth, specifically body length, remains negligible when the basal dietary requirements are adequately met. This observation is congruent with earlier findings indicating that functional feed additives, including probiotics, prebiotics, and phytogenic compounds, primarily enhance immunological responses and mucosal integrity rather than directly stimulating growth parameters20–22. Similarly, Uribe et al.23 demonstrated that dietary supplementation with biofilm-inhibiting agents in aquaculture species improved survival and enhanced immune responses without significant gains in length or weight. The absence of adverse effects on growth in the present study further suggests that the antibiofilm agents did not impose any metabolic burden or induce physiological stress in the fish, as such conditions are typically associated with growth suppression and feed inefficiency23,24. Accordingly, antibiofilm feed additives can be considered a safe dietary intervention to support health maintenance without compromising growth performance in juvenile grouper.

All experimental groups, including the control group, exhibited a consistent and progressive increase in body weight from Week 0 to Week 4, indicating healthy somatic development and effective feed utilization throughout the culture period. However, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) in body weight gain were observed among the treatment groups. This suggests that the dietary inclusion of antibiofilm compounds at the tested concentrations neither markedly enhanced nor impaired growth performance in juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus). These findings suggest that antibiofilm supplementation confers health benefits, including improved intestinal morphology and reduced pathogenic bacterial loads, such as those of Vibrio spp., as demonstrated in complementary analyses. The supplementation’s impact on weight gain was minimal when basal nutritional requirements were met. This observation aligns with previous reports, such as that by Dawood and Koshio21who emphasized that various functional feed additives, including immunostimulants and antimicrobial agents, often improve host defense mechanisms without necessarily accelerating growth metrics, such as body weight. Furthermore, Newaj-Fyzul et al.20 emphasized that the growth response to such additives is multifactorial, often depending on the fish species, additive dosage, and environmental variables such as water quality and rearing density. The present study’s observation that body weight gain was not negatively impacted indicates the safety of antibiofilm compounds in aquafeeds. This is corroborated by Uribe et al.23who noted that non-pathogenic feed additives that do not trigger physiological stress or gut dysbiosis typically do not compromise growth. Consequently, while not directly acting as growth promoters, antibiofilm agents can play a significant role in maintaining gut homeostasis and disease resistance, ultimately contributing to the sustainability of aquaculture systems without negatively affecting productivity.

At the onset of the experiment, all groups exhibited 100% survival, indicating a uniform and healthy physiological status across treatments. As the trial progressed, a general decline in survival rates was observed in all groups. However, fish administered dietary antibiofilm supplementation, particularly at the 0.01 mL/kg dosage, consistently demonstrated higher survival percentages than the control, especially from Week 2 onwards. By Week 4, the control group showed the most pronounced decline in survival, with values approaching or falling below 90%. In contrast, the 0.01 mL/kg group maintained survival rates above 95% throughout the culture period. Although the 0.05 mL/kg and 0.1 mL/kg treatments resulted in intermediate survival levels, they still outperformed the control group, underscoring the potential protective effects of antibiofilm inclusion even at minimal dosages. These findings suggest that dietary antibiofilm supplementation enhances survival by mitigating subclinical infections and reinforcing mucosal defenses rather than directly stimulating growth performance. GC-MS analysis showed that antibiofilm compound JB 3B contained Oxalic acid, phenol, phenylethyl alcohol, N- Hexadecanoic Acid, and Pyrolol[1,2-a]pyrazine-1,4-dione, hexadro-3-92-methylpropyl) as major components. Additionally, LC-MS analysis revealed that the antibiofilm compound also contained rac-Etomidate, Cyclo(leucylprolyl), and Phenylhydrazine, which have been reported to have antibiofilm and antibacterial properties26. Therefore, the activities of antibiofilm compounds are confirmed through our study.

However, the rapid mortality suggests that the fish in this study did not have sufficient time to mount an immune response, making it difficult to assess the specific protective mechanism. We recommend that future studies employ pre-exposure or delayed challenge protocols to assess prophylactic efficacy more effectively. Antibiofilm agents are known to interfere with quorum sensing pathways and inhibit biofilm formation by pathogenic bacteria, particularly Vibrio spp., which are ubiquitous in marine aquaculture systems and frequently associated with chronic or opportunistic infections27–29. The improved intestinal morphology and reduction in gut-associated Vibrio spp. abundance, as evidenced in other figures, further supports the hypothesis that antibiofilm compounds enhance barrier integrity and immune modulation. This observation aligns with previous reports highlighting the immunoprophylactic potential of non-antibiotic feed additives. Dawood and Koshio21 emphasized that certain dietary supplements, such as phytogenics and probiotics, can bolster immune responses and disease resistance even without measurable improvements in growth. Similarly, Newaj-Fyzul et al.20 reported that including probiotics and biofilm-disrupting agents significantly reduced mortality during pathogen exposure trials by limiting microbial colonization and enhancing host defense mechanisms. These results underscore the utility of antibiofilm supplementation as a preventative health strategy in sustainable aquaculture. Antibiofilm agents offer a promising alternative to conventional chemotherapeutics and antibiotics by reducing pathogen-associated stress and promoting survival without compromising growth.

Methods

The antibiofilm agents from phyllosphere-associated bacteria

Following previous research, the antibiofilm agents used in this study were derived from phyllosphere-associated bacteria47. Specifically, as reported in earlier studies, Pseudomonas fluorescens JB3B was isolated from the surface of Psidium guajava leaves collected in Karanganyar, Jakarta, Indonesia. The bacterial isolates were cultured on Brain Heart Infusion Agar (BHIA) (Oxoid) and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. Following initial growth, the isolates were subcultured onto King’s B selective medium, composed of 2 g protease peptone, 1.5 mL glycerol, 0.15 g K₂HPO₄, 0.15 g MgSO₄∙7 H₂O, 20 g agar, and 1 L of distilled water, to ensure the selective enrichment and maintenance of the target strains.

Lukman et al.14 described that 100 µL of V. harveyi suspension (OD600 = 0.132) was applied onto a cover glass and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h to observe biofilm formation. Subsequently, 100 µL of the supernatant derived from soil and plant-associated bacteria was added to the same cover glass and re-incubated under identical conditions. For visualization under a light microscope, the cover glass was rinsed with distilled water (aquadest), stained with 0.4% (w/v) crystal violet for 10 min, rinsed again, and then examined under a microscope at 400× magnification (10× ocular × 40× objective).

Experimental fish

The experimental fish utilized in this study were juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus), with an average length of 3.421 ± 0.177 cm and an average weight of 1.973 ± 0.177 g. The juveniles were sourced from the BRIN hatchery at the Marine Biota Scientific Conservation Area (KKIBL), Gondol, Bali. Before the experiment, the fish underwent a two-week acclimation period in a 4,000 cm³ concrete tank. Following acclimation, the fish were subjected to a 15-minute freshwater bath containing 100 ppm formalin for prophylactic treatment. Subsequently, they were randomly distributed into 12 fiber tanks, each with a 300 L capacity, and stocked at a density of 200 individuals per tank. A continuous flow-through water system was maintained, with 100 mL delivered every 10 s. The fish were fed a commercial pellet diet (Megami GR No. 2) to satiation until they exhibited a consistent and enthusiastic feeding response.

Preparation of antibiofilm-supplemented feed

Antibiofilm compounds were incorporated into the test feed by mixing liquid-form antibiofilm with powdered juvenile-stage feed (crude protein 45%, lipid 10%, fiber 3%, ash 12%, moisture 8%). The antibiofilm was administered at three concentrations corresponding to the experimental treatments: 0.01 mL/kg, 0.05 mL/kg, and 0.1 mL/kg of feed. A control group was prepared without the addition of antibiofilm. The mixing process ensured the uniform distribution of the antibiofilm solution throughout the feed matrix. After mixing, the feed was air-dried for 24 h at room temperature to remove excess moisture and stabilize the formulation before being used in the feeding trials.

Feeding trials with antibiofilm-supplemented feed

In this study, juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus) with an average weight of 1.973 ± 0.177 g and an average length of 3.421 ± 0.177 cm were acclimated for two weeks in a 4,000 cm³ concrete tank. Before experimental stocking, the fish underwent prophylactic treatment via immersion in freshwater containing 100 ppm formalin for 15 min. Subsequently, the fish were randomly distributed into 12 fiber tanks, each with a capacity of 300 L, at a stocking density of 200 individuals per tank. A flow-through water system was maintained at a rate of 10 mL/s to ensure optimal water quality. The experimental groups received commercial feed supplemented with antibiofilm compounds at concentrations of 0.01 mL/kg, 0.05 mL/kg, and 0.1 mL/kg. The control group was fed the same diet but without antibiofilm. The feeding trial was conducted for four weeks, during which data were collected on growth performance, survival rate, skin pigmentation (brightness) using Toca Color Finder, deformity incidence, and culture water quality. Additionally, total bacterial and Vibrio abundance were assessed, and detailed intestinal condition analyses were carried out using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and histomorphology evaluations, including measurements of goblet cell count, mucosal fold height, and muscularis thickness.

Challenge test with Vibrio harveyi

The V. harveyi strain used in the challenge test (isolate ID: Km_ALk-13, Vibrio harveyi strain HH101315 16 S rRNA gene, partial sequence, database accession number KF746885.1)69 was obtained from the bacterial culture collection of the Pathology Laboratory, Marine Biota Scientific Conservation Area (KKIBL), Gondol, Bali, as previously described by Mahardika et al.70. The bacterium was cultured on Trypticase Soya Agar (TSA) and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. Bacterial colonies were harvested using a Drigalski spatula and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to achieve a density of 10⁸ CFU/mL, based on the McFarland turbidity standard (HiMedia). The final concentration of the suspension, confirmed via the Total Plate Count (TPC) method, was 10⁸ CFU/mL. This concentration causes mortality in maricultured fish, including grouper71 was used for the challenge test. For the challenge setup, 45 juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus) from each treatment group (0.01 mL/kg, 0.05 mL/kg, and 0.1 mL/kg of antibiofilm) were distributed into four 100 L plastic tubs per treatment, resulting in a total of 16 tubs. For each group, three tubs were stocked with 10 fish each for mortality monitoring, while a fourth tub held 15 fish for sampling at 6, 12, 24, and 72 h post-infection. The challenge was conducted via intraperitoneal injection of 0.1 mL of the bacterial suspension (2.3 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) per fish. Before injection, the fish were anesthetized by immersion in seawater containing clove oil at a concentration of 0.2 mL/L. Post-challenge observations were conducted over seven days to assess mortality and physiological responses.

Fish blood profile assessment

Two fish per replicate tank from each treatment group (six fish per treatment) were sampled and anesthetized using clove oil at a concentration of 0.2 mL/L in seawater. Once anesthetized to the point of loss of equilibrium, the fish were placed on a moistened white towel for handling and processing. Blood samples were collected from the caudal vein using a 1 mL tuberculin syringe with a 26.5 G needle pre-rinsed with EDTA solution to prevent coagulation. Approximately 600 µL of blood from each fish was transferred into Eppendorf tubes and subsequently analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (RT-7600 for Veterinary Use, Rayto), which has been previously applied in marine teleosts, including groupers. Hematological data were printed and recorded using Microsoft Excel for further analysis. This procedure was repeated for three fish per treatment at each of the designated time points, 6, 12, 24, and 72 h post-challenge with Vibrio harveyi, to assess temporal changes in blood parameters.

Isolation of intestinal bacteria

We perform euthanasia with clove oil (eugenol) in graduated doses as described by Mahardika et al.72. First, the fish is immersed in a solution with a dose of 1 mL eugenol/L of seawater without aeration for 2–3 min, the fish was carefully transferred into a eugenol solution with a dose of 2 mL/L of seawater without aeration for 3–5 min. Fish are ready to be dissected when their gills are no longer moving and the fish remains still when shifted on the bottom of the container. Following anesthesia and blood sampling, the identical fish specimens were dissected to isolate the foregut section of the intestine. Intestinal contents (fecal matter) were carefully extracted using sterile forceps and transferred into sterile 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. A subsample of 1 mg of intestinal content was weighed and suspended in 99 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to prepare a 10⁻² dilution. The resulting suspension was inoculated onto Trypticase Soy Agar (TSA) to quantify the total bacterial population (Total Plate Count, TPC) and onto Thiosulfate Citrate Bile-Salts Sucrose Agar (TCBSA) to enumerate Vibrio spp. cultures. All inoculated media were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, bacterial colonies were counted using a digital colony counter to determine microbial abundance within the intestinal tract.

Histology assessment

Following dissection and the removal of intestinal contents, the internal organs of each fish specimen—including the liver, spleen, kidney, and intestines—were fixed in buffered formalin solution for one week. The preserved tissues were subsequently processed using an automated tissue processor (RH-12EP, Sakura Finetechnical Co., Ltd, Japan). The process involved sequential dehydration in graded ethanol solutions (70–100%), clearing in xylene, and infiltration with paraffin over an eight-hour processing cycle. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin blocks and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm using a microtome. Histological sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), mounted with Entellan, and examined under a light microscope at magnifications ranging from 4× to 40×. Microscopic observations were documented for further analysis of tissue morphology and potential pathological alterations.

Scanning electron microscopy assessment

Foregut segments approximately 0.5 cm in length were collected from three fish per treatment group before and after the Vibrio harveyi challenge. The tissue samples were immediately fixed in a sterile Eppendorf tube containing a fixative solution composed of acetic acid, formalin, and absolute ethanol in a 1:1:1 (v/v) ratio. Following fixation, the samples were processed for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis at the Integrated Laboratory of Bioproducts (ILaB), Biomaterials Research Center, LIPI-BRIN, located in Cibinong, West Java.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data obtained in this study, including growth performance, survival rate, deformity incidence, and skin coloration metrics, were subjected to statistical analysis using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test, with significance at P < 0.05. The analysis was performed utilizing SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to determine significant differences among treatment groups. This approach enabled the assessment of the effects of dietary supplementation.

Conclusions

Dietary supplementation with antibiofilm compounds at varying concentrations significantly influenced juvenile pearl gentian hybrid grouper (Epinephelus fuscoguttatus × E. lanceolatus) in terms of growth performance, health status, and physiological parameters. The treatments notably enhanced key performance indicators such as weight gain, survival rate, and skin brightness while reducing the incidence of skeletal deformities, indicating improved overall fitness and developmental quality44,62. Furthermore, marked enhancements in intestinal histomorphology were observed, including increased goblet cell density, taller mucosal folds, and thicker muscularis layers, all of which suggest strengthened gut integrity, enhanced mucosal immunity, and improved nutrient absorption capacity40,51. Improvements in hematological profiles, particularly elevated granulocyte counts and stable erythrocyte parameters, further supported the immunostimulatory effects of antibiofilm compounds. These immune enhancements are critical for frontline defense against pathogenic threats and reflect the compound’s ability to modulate host immunity without causing systemic hematological disturbances22,23. Additionally, the treatments significantly reduced the total bacterial load and Vibrio spp. density, especially at the higher concentrations (0.05 and 0.1 mL/kg), supporting the antibiofilm agent’s targeted antimicrobial effects through quorum sensing disruption and inhibition of biofilm formation22,29. Importantly, these protective effects persisted following an experimental challenge with V. harveyi, as antibiofilm-fed fish demonstrated higher survival rates, lower pathogen loads, and faster recovery of immune cell populations compared to the control group. These outcomes demonstrate the compound’s efficacy in enhancing innate immunity and resistance to acute bacterial infection, essential traits in intensive aquaculture systems20. Notably, the compound exhibited selective antibacterial activity without significantly disrupting the microbial balance of the rearing environment, a critical factor for maintaining ecosystem stability and long-term productivity42. These results affirm the potential usefulness of antibiofilm agents as effective, non-antibiotic feed additives that contribute to fish health, environmental sustainability, and biosecurity in aquaculture. Their application aligns with the growing need for antibiotic alternatives and integrated strategies for disease prevention in modern fish farming.
