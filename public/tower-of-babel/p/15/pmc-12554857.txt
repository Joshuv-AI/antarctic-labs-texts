The relationship between community spiritual comfort services and cognitive function in the older adults: a random intercept cross-lagged panel study
Authors: Long Yan, Xiaowen Li, Benxian Yao, Yu Liu, Hao Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore whether the impact of community spiritual comfort service (CSCS) on cognitive function is mediated by social participation and moderated by genetic factors.MethodsData were derived from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey conducted in 2008, 2011, 2014, and 2018, involving 3598 participants. A Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model was utilized to determine the relationship between CSCS and cognitive function, as well as the mediating role of social participation and the moderating effect of genetics on the identified CSCS and cognitive function relationship.ResultsLower levels of CSCS at earlier assessment points were significantly associated with lower cognitive function at subsequent assessment points. Social participation partially mediated the prospective relationship between CSCS and cognitive function. Distinct genetic polymorphisms showed significant differences in effect sizes, with the Val group experiencing a significantly greater impact of CSCS on subsequent social participation, and of social participation on subsequent cognitive function, compared to the Met group.ConclusionEnhancements in CSCS can predict subsequent improvements in cognitive function. The relationship between CSCS and cognitive function can be mediated by social participation and is moderated by genetic factors. Multidisciplinary interventions aimed at improving the quality of CSCS and enhancing social participation will benefit the cognitive and psychological health of the older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10433-025-00885-7.Keywords: Community spiritual comfort service, Social participation, Cognitive function, Gene, Random intercept cross-lagged panel model, Older adults

Introduction

As a critical response to aging, community service, particularly spiritual comfort service, is increasingly recognized and rapidly developing worldwide (Zheng et al. 2020). Under the current national and local standards in China, community spiritual comfort services (CSCS) are defined as “a series of specialized services aimed at satisfying the spiritual needs of older adults by relieving emotional disorders such as loneliness, fear, depression, and anxiety through emotional soothing, empathetic communication, psychological counseling, and social relationship adjustment (Jiangxi Provincial Administration for Market Regulation 2022; Ministry of Civil Affairs of the People’s Republic of China 2020).” Spiritual comfort service not only provides emotional support to older adults but also significantly enhances their quality of life and mental health. Research indicates that older adults face numerous challenges in life, such as the loss of loved ones, health issues, and changes in social roles, which often lead to loneliness and depression. Through spiritual comfort service, older adults can receive emotional support and psychological counseling, which helps alleviate psychological stress and improve their mood states (Johnson and Barer 1997; Narayanasamy et al. 2004).

Cognitive function is a vital component of brain neural functions, encompassing attention, concentration, memory, learning, language, comprehension, reasoning, judgment, logical thinking, behavior, and executive abilities, reflecting the functional status of the brain (Fisher et al. 2017). Cognitive function directly affects the quality of life and self-care ability of older adults. With aging, the natural decline in cognitive function is a common phenomenon, and this decline is closely associated with neurodegenerative diseases such as dementia (Harada et al. 2013). Existing research has developed theoretical frameworks from psychological, social, and neurobiological perspectives to explain how CSCS influence cognitive functioning in older adults. First, from the psychological perspective, Erikson’s psychosocial development theory posits that the principal developmental task in late life is achieving ego integrity while warding off despair. During this stage, older adults have an especially strong need for affective solace and social identity. By providing emotional soothing and empathetic communication, CSCS help older adults rebuild their sense of self-worth and belonging, thereby enhancing well-being and promoting cognitive health (Sokol 2009). Second, from the social perspective, social capital theory underscores the health protective role of relational resources (Portes 2024). CSCS enlarge older adults’ social capital, alleviating the adverse effects of stress and anxiety and, in turn, supporting cognitive health. Finally, from the neurobiological perspective, the cognitive reserve hypothesis argues that accumulated participation in cognitively stimulating activities strengthens the brain’s resilience to age-related decline. The emotional soothing, entertainment, and related programs embedded in CSCS furnish such cognitive stimulation, fostering neural plasticity. Empirical evidence shows that older adults with greater cognitive reserve can maintain favorable psychological well-being even when their cognitive ability is comparatively low (Fratiglioni et al. 2004). Current research on the relationship between CSCS and cognitive function in older adults is relatively sparse, and often relies on cross-sectional data, facing challenges in distinguishing between correlations among variables and individual differences, which may lead to an unreasonable assessment of the relationships (Livingston et al. 2017). Therefore, this study aims to investigate the longitudinal connection between CSCS and cognitive function in older adults.

In recent years, there has been increasing academic interest in the impact of social participation on the health of older adults. Social participation refers to the process by which individuals integrate into society by engaging in social activities, maintaining social connections, and fulfilling social roles, and is considered a key protective mechanism for individual health. CSCS provide emotional support and spiritual comfort, helping older adults establish and maintain social connections. These services can offer a safe, friendly environment, giving seniors a sense of belonging and support. Studies have shown that older adults individuals involved in religious and spiritual activities tend to have wider social networks and higher levels of social engagement (Krause and Hayward 2014). By participating in these services, older adults can feel valued and meaningful, boosting their self-confidence and thereby more actively engaging in social activities (Chen and Koenig 2006). Furthermore, CSCS also help alleviate feelings of loneliness and depression in older adults, which are significant factors affecting their social participation. By providing emotional support and spiritual care, these services can significantly improve the psychological health of older adults, thereby enhancing their willingness and ability of social participation (Levin 2013). Social participation has a significant positive impact on cognitive function in older adults. Firstly, social participation can promote cognitive function by increasing social interactions and intellectual activities among older adults. Studies have shown that frequent social participation helps stimulate brain activity and maintain an active state of cognitive function. For example, older adults individuals who participate in social activities such as clubs, volunteer work, and community events experience a significantly slower decline in cognitive abilities compared to their peers who lack social interactions (Fratiglioni et al. 2004). Secondly, social participation has a positive impact on the psychological health of older adults, which, in turn, indirectly improves their cognitive functions. Social activities can reduce feelings of loneliness and depression among older adults, enhancing their mental health status. A good psychological state helps maintain stability and improve cognitive function. Research has found that older adults individuals actively involved in social activities show fewer symptoms of depression and better cognitive function (Zhou et al. 2020; Li and Wu 2022). Lastly, social participation can also enhance cognitive function by boosting the self-esteem and self-efficacy of older adults. Participating in social activities makes older adults feel valued and socially recognized, which strengthens their confidence and zest for life, thereby benefiting the maintenance and improvement of cognitive function (Berkman et al. 2000).

Research from behavioral genetic indicates that cognitive function has a significant genetic basis, with heritability estimates ranging from 24 to 55% (Cao and Zhang 2019). The COMT (catechol-O-methyltransferase) gene is an important candidate gene for cognitive function (Różycka et al. 2016). Located on the long arm of chromosome 22 at band 1 of sub-band 2 (22q11.2), COMT is the primary metabolic enzyme for catecholamines (including adrenaline, noradrenaline, and dopamine). This gene has at least eight single-nucleotide polymorphic variations in its coding region, with the Val158Met polymorphism (rs4680 polymorphism) being the most common functional site, and the COMT activity encoded by the Val allele is 3–4 times that of the Met allele (Lachman et al. 1996). Studies have investigated the interaction between COMT gene Val158Met polymorphism and social environment on cognitive function, but the conclusions are still inconsistent. The social salience hypothesis, which posits that oxytocin can enhance an individual's sensitivity and responsiveness to the social environment, may explain the disparities in these research findings (Bartz et al. 2011). The Shamay-Tsoory team further elaborated on the neurophysiological mechanisms by which oxytocin influences social salience, namely, the interaction between the oxytocinergic system and the dopaminergic system, which regulates individual sensitivity to the environment (Shamay-Tsoory and Abu-Akel 2016). Although the COMT gene does not directly affect oxytocin levels, it can influence the oxytocinergic system by regulating the number, organization, and function of oxytocin receptors, thereby regulating individual sensitivity to the environment. fMRI studies also show that the COMT gene can affect the functional coupling between the hypothalamus and the amygdala, regulating individual sensitivity to environmental factors, and thereby influencing social emotions and behavior (Wu et al. 2020). Thus, based on the social salience hypothesis and fMRI research evidence, this study speculates that the COMT gene exhibits either protective or risk effects depending on the individual's social environment, indicating a gene–environment interaction.

Accordingly, the present study systematically investigates the longitudinal association between CSCS and cognitive function in older adults. It further tests whether social participation partially mediates this association and whether the strength of the pathway differs by individual genotype.

Method

Data and sample

The data for this study come from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), which is the most comprehensive and extensive dataset of older adults population in China. The survey has been conducted by the Center for Healthy Aging and Development Studies and the National School of Development at Peking University since 1998. The Human Subjects Protection program of the CLHLS was approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-13074), and written informed consent was obtained from all individuals participating in the study.

The CLHLS sample spans 23 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in China, targeting respondents aged 65 years and older. In 2008, 5090 older adults completed the full questionnaire and provided genetic data, forming the study’s baseline cohort. Successful follow-up surveys were conducted in 2011 (n = 4617; 90.7% of the 2008 baseline), 2014 (n = 4104; 80.6%), and 2018, the final wave (n = 3598; 70.7%). The present analysis, therefore, relies on the complete longitudinal data of 3598 participants interviewed at baseline and in all three subsequent waves (2011, 2014, and 2018). Participants added in later waves were not included, ensuring consistency in baseline characteristics—particularly genetic data.

Beginning in 2008, peripheral blood samples were obtained for DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was isolated using the phenol–chloroform method and genotyped with the Illumina HumanOmniZhongHua-8 high-density SNP array. Rigorous quality control was imposed: Samples with a genotype call rate < 95% and close-related individuals were excluded; SNPs with a call rate < 90%, minor allele frequency < 0.01 were removed. To adjust for population structure, principal components analysis was performed on genome-wide SNP data, the leading principal components were entered as covariates, and genetic outliers were excluded. Within the quality-controlled dataset, the COMT Val158Met polymorphism (rs4680) was examined: Under a dominant model, individuals carrying ≥ 1 Met allele were classified as the Met group, whereas Val/Val homozygotes formed the Val group. This genotype classification was used in subsequent moderation analyses.

To exploit all available information and minimize bias from missing data, multiple imputation was applied to every variable with missing observations. Five imputed datasets (m = 5) were generated, each subjected to 10 iterations to ensure stable convergence.

Measurement

The CLHLS measures CSCS by asking respondents whether their community provides the following social services: spiritual comfort and chatting to relieve boredom, recreational activities, and handing neighborhood disputes. According to prior research (Dai et al. 2023), if a community provides any one of these social services, CSCS is coded as 1, and if none are provided, it is coded as 0. This study used SPSS25.0 for the internal consistency of the demand for CSCS, and the result was Cronbach’s coefficient of 0.883.

According to Zhang et al.'s study within the framework of the CLHLS, social participation is assessed across six dimensions: outdoor activities (including tai chi, square dancing, visiting and interacting with friends, and other outdoor activities), gardening, reading newspapers or books, playing cards or mahjong, watching television or listening to the radio, and engaging in social activities (Zhang et al. 2023). The response scale for these activities ranges from 1 (never), 2 (not monthly, but sometimes), 3 (not weekly, but at least once a month), 4 (not every day, but at least once a week), to 5 (almost every day). The sum of the answers to all questions represents the level of social participation of older adults, with total scores ranging from 6 to 30. Higher scores indicate more active participation in these activities. In this study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.890.

Currently, most studies utilize the internationally popular Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) to assess cognitive function in older adults. In the CLHLS, the MMSE has been appropriately modified to fit China's cultural context. It includes general cognitive abilities, responsiveness, attention and calculation skills, memory, and language comprehension coordination, comprising a total of 24 questions (Li and Cui 2017). Except for question 6, "the number of foods named in one minute," which is scored up to a maximum of 7 points, all other questions are scored 1 point each, making a total possible score of 30 points. In this study, a correct answer is awarded 1 point, and an incorrect answer or inability to answer is scored as 0 points. The sum of the scores from the 24 questions constitutes the total cognitive function score. A higher score indicates better cognitive function. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for this measure in the study was 0.914, indicating excellent reliability.

Control variables

In this study, to ensure the robustness of the findings, various variables were controlled, including demographic factors such as actual age, gender (Male = 1 and female = 0), living arrangements (living with others = 1 and otherwise = 0), place of residence (urban = 1 and rural = 0), ethnicity (minority = 1 and Han = 0), and marital status (married = 1 and other = 0). Health status variables such as the ability for self-care (Has self-care disability = 1 and otherwise = 0), the presence of chronic diseases (Has chronic diseases = 1 and otherwise = 0), and self-rated health (scored from 1 to 5, with 5 being the best) were also included. Socioeconomic status was assessed through the actual years of education received, pre-retirement occupational status (high occupational status = 1 and otherwise = 0) (Wang et al. 2018), and the logarithm of the last year’s per capita family income. Additionally, dietary structure, regular consumption of fresh vegetables and fruits (yes = 1 and no = 0), smoking status (yes = 1 and no = 0), and alcohol consumption (yes = 1 and no = 0) were considered.

Date analyses

The Cross-Lagged Panel Model (CLPM) has traditionally been used as a standard method for studying the causal influences between variables in longitudinal panel data. However, CLPM only considers the temporal stability of variables and does not adequately account for time-invariant individual differences, which can lead to biased estimates (Livingston et al. 2017). To address this issue, the Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) has been proposed (Rogosa 1980; Lucas 2023). The modeling process of RI-CLPM is described in the supplementary material's Description of RI-CLPM section (Mulder and Hamaker 2021; Mulder 2023).

Longitudinal measurement invariance is a prerequisite for modeling changes over time. Using tests for measurement invariance, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) has shown that our proposed construct of CSCS remains constant over time in terms of factor loadings and intercepts, thus meeting the requirements for strong measurement invariance. The scalar longitudinal invariance model for CSCS allows for correlations between residual variances at the same plots and imposes invariance constraints on factor loadings and intercepts at four time points. Moreover, the CFA for the cognitive function scale also shows scalar longitudinal invariance, indicating that longitudinal cross-lagged analysis between cognitive function and CSCS is feasible. Our RI-CLPM involves the comparison of a three-tier nested model (Table 1). Model 1 assesses the correlations between CSCS and cognitive function within the same time points and the autoregressive paths between time points. Model 2 adds cross-lagged paths between CSCS and cognitive function, testing their mutual relations. In Model 2, we incorporated a suite of control variables—demographic characteristics, health status indicators, educational and occupational background, and lifestyle factors. These variables entered the RI-CLPM as time-invariant covariates to account for potential confounding. Model 3 extended Model 2 by introducing both the mediator and the moderator. To evaluate mediation within the two genotype subgroups, we applied a non-parametric bootstrap procedure with 5000 resamples to obtain confidence intervals for the indirect effect. This resampling approach, free of normality assumptions, repeatedly draws (with replacement) from the original data to build an empirical sampling distribution, yielding more reliable interval estimates when sample sizes are limited or distributions are non-normal. Genotype was coded under a dominant model, classifying Val/Val homozygotes as the Val group and individuals carrying one or more Met alleles as the Met group. The moderating effect of genotype was tested by comparing the fit of the corresponding nested regression models. All these analyses were conducted in SPSS 25.0 and R version 4.3.3.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The final analytical sample comprised 3598 older participants, with 1683 assigned to the Val group (Val/Val homozygous genotype) and 1915 to the Met group (carrying at least one Met allele). From 2008 to 2018, the average scores for CSCS in the existing sample were 1.10, 1.22, 1.59, and 2.28, respectively, while the average scores for social participation were 18.34, 18.96, 20.43, and 22.14. The average scores for cognitive function were 16.44, 17.31, 19.04, and 18.86, respectively (for detailed data, see the Descriptive Statistics section in the supplementary materials).

The mediating role of social participation and the moderating role of genes

As shown in Fig. 2, Model 3 incorporates three indirect paths of social participation as potential mediators and adjusts for control variables, maintaining a good fit with the data (RMSEA = 0.044 and CFI = 0.963, as presented in Table 1). Two cross-lagged regression models were developed based on genetic grouping, specifically for the Val and Met groups.

Mediating effects of social participation on the relationship between CSCS and cognitive function within different gene groups. ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, and *p < 0.05

The results indicate that higher previous levels of CSCS predict higher subsequent social participation (higher scores) for both genetic groups (Val group: β = 0.254, 0.344, p < 0.001; Met group: β = 0.180, 0.199, p < 0.001). Additionally, higher previous social participation predicts higher subsequent cognitive function (Val group: β = 0.236, 0.385, p < 0.001; Met group: β = 0.125, 0.143, p < 0.001). This suggests that enhanced social participation, influenced by CSCS, contributes to better cognitive outcomes, with variations observed between the genetic profiles, illustrating differential effects based on genetic predisposition.

Using the Bootstrap method, mediation effects were tested separately for the Val and Met genetic groups with the following results:

The indirect effect of T1 CSCS on T3 cognitive function through T2 social participation was 0.0599 (95% CI [0.0299, 0.0683]); because zero is not included in the interval, T2 social participation acts as a partial mediator between T1 CSCS and T3 cognitive function. The pathway from T2 CSCS → T3 social participation → T4 cognitive function produced an indirect effect of 0.132 (95% CI [0.0121, 0.0598]), likewise excluding zero and indicating that T3 social participation partially mediates the relationship between T2 CSCS and T4 cognitive function.

Separately, the indirect effect along the T1 CSCS → T2 social participation → T3 cognitive function path was 0.0225 (95% CI [0.0324, 0.0558]); the confidence interval again excludes zero, confirming that T2 social participation partially mediates the link between T1 CSCS and T3 cognitive function. The pathway from T2 CSCS → T3 social participation → T4 cognitive function pathway yielded an indirect effect of 0.0284 (95% CI [0.0335, 0.0826]), with zero outside the interval, demonstrating that T3 social participation also partially mediates the association between T2 CSCS and T4 cognitive function.

Both the Val and Met groups show significant indirect effects of earlier CSCS through social participation on later cognitive function (effect size = 0.035, p < 0.05; effect size = 0.047, p < 0.05). Using a two-sided difference in proportion z-test, the impact of CSCS on subsequent social participation and the effect of social participation on subsequent cognitive function were significantly greater in the Val group compared to the Met group (Z = − 0.899, p < 0.001). This indicates a significant moderating effect of genetic on the relationships tested.

Robustness testing

In longitudinal research, high attrition rates can potentially impact study conclusions, so we employed two methods of robustness testing.

The first method tested the randomness of participant attrition, a precondition for subsequent research. The process was as follows: From the initial 5090 participants in 2008, we divided them into two groups—the retained group with 4617 participants who remained in the 2011 dataset and the attrition group with 576 participants who were lost by 2011. We conducted independent sample T-tests to compare differences between these two groups. The results showed no significant differences between the groups in variables such as CSCS, social participation, and cognitive function. We repeated this process by splitting the 2011 and 2014 participants into retained and attrition groups for differential testing. Ultimately, we found no significant differences between the retained and attrition groups, indicating that the attrition was random. Detailed results are available in the supplementary material, Robustness Test_Testing the Randomness of Attrition in Subjects.

The second method involved segmenting the study from 2008 to 2018 into multiple shorter longitudinal studies, using different sample groupings to test the robustness of this 10-year longitudinal research. The cross-lagged results from these multiple shorter studies were consistent with those from the full 2008 to 2018 longitudinal study, confirming the robustness of the research. Detailed findings can be seen in the supplementary material, Robustness Test_multiple shorter longitudinal studies.

Discussion

This study conducted a longitudinal analysis on a representative sample of older adults Chinese to explore the relationship between CSCS and cognitive function, including the mediating role of social participation and the moderating effect of genetic factors. Using the RI-CLPM, which controls for time and individual effects, our findings reveal: (1) a positive correlation between CSCS and older adults cognitive function; (2) social participation partially mediates the relationship between CSCS and older adults cognitive function; and (3) the COMT gene Val158Met polymorphism plays a moderating role.

Our research demonstrates a positive correlation between CSCS and cognitive function among older adults in China, aligning with the previous findings (Dai et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). According to Erik Erikson's psychosocial development theory, individuals face different psychosocial tasks at different life stages. For older adults, the primary task is achieving ego integrity rather than succumbing to despair. During this stage, older adults individuals seek to find meaning and satisfaction in life by reflecting on their experiences, thereby achieving psychological peace and wholeness (Sudak 1983). CSCS play a key role in this process. Studies have found that cognitive abilities in older adults improve significantly through spiritual support and community engagement. This suggests that CSCS enhance psychological health and cognitive function by meeting older adult's needs for social connections and emotional support. According to psychosocial development theory, positive social interactions and emotional support help older adults overcome feelings of loneliness and isolation, facilitating the achievement of ego integrity. This, in turn, helps them better cope with the challenges of later life and maintain psychological and cognitive health (Zhang et al. 2022; Sokol 2009). Yue et al. have confirmed such relationships across different cultural backgrounds, suggesting that these services not only provide emotional support but also help older adults find meaning and value in life through enhanced social interactions, thus achieving psychological satisfaction and a sense of identity (Yue et al. 2021; Chen and Zhang 2022).

This study innovatively explores whether social participation mediates the relationship between CSCS and cognitive function among older adults, and whether genetic plays a moderating role.

Social participation serves as a crucial mediator in this process. According to the social capital theory, social participation enhances social capital by providing emotional support and cognitive stimulation, thus improving cognitive function (Capital 1998). Specifically, by engaging in CSCS, older adults can expand their social networks and increase social interactions. These interactions not only offer emotional support but also foster cognitive activities. Studies have found that older adults individuals participating in CSCS enhance their social capital through increased social engagement, significantly improving their cognitive function (Huang et al. 2024; Giebel et al. 2022). Furthermore, Wang et al. have pointed out that CSCS significantly reduce the risk of cognitive decline by promoting social participation. Their research shows that older adults individuals who regularly participate in community activities have wider social networks, more emotional support, and better cognitive function (Lin et al. 2023), supporting the perspectives within the social capital theory. Additionally, studies have found that older adults individuals utilizing CSCS report significant improvements in self-rated health and cognitive function. These findings further validate the mediating effect of social capital theory—that increasing social engagement and interactions can enhance social capital, thereby promoting cognitive function (Yang et al. 2021). By demonstrating a partial mediating effect of social participation, this study indicates that positive social interaction is the key pathway through which CSCS yield cognitive benefits. Theoretically, the findings resonate with and extend the frameworks of active aging and cognitive reserve: Enriching social roles and interactions can be translated into gains in cognitive functioning, providing empirical support for how environmental supports are converted into cognitive health. Practically, these results offer guidance for optimizing community service provision. In delivering CSCS, communities should place greater emphasis on cultivating opportunities and an enabling climate for older adults’ participation in community activities and interpersonal engagement, thereby maximizing CSCS’s positive impact on cognitive function and offering a feasible pathway to delay cognitive decline and enhance quality of life in later life.

The COMT gene marker is associated with susceptibility to cognitive function, aligning with some previous studies (Nagel et al. 2008; Mier et al. 2010). The COMT gene Val158Met polymorphism plays a critical regulatory role in dopamine levels in the frontal regions (where dopamine transporters are less distributed). Compared to carriers of the Met allele, individuals carrying the Val allele have higher COMT enzyme activity, lower synaptic dopamine levels, and exhibit weaker activation of frontal neurons (Stein et al. 2006), as well as weaker functional connectivity between the prefrontal cortex and the amygdala, which plays a critical role in the generation, recognition, and regulation of emotions (Klucken et al. 2015). Weakening of frontal lobe functions and overactivation of the amygdala are significantly associated with cognitive function (Rive et al. 2013). Johnson et al. have suggested that genetic predispositions can influence how individuals handle stress, social interactions, and environmental changes, which are all related to the experiences provided by CSCS (Johnson and Smith 2019). CSCS offer social support, participation, and resources that are beneficial for mental health. However, the effectiveness of these services may vary according to an individual's genetic makeup, potentially affecting their responses to social and environmental stimuli (Brown et al. 2017). By incorporating genetic factors into the analytic scope of community-based psychological intervention effects, this study enriches the theoretical framework concerning determinants of cognitive health in older adults and deepens our understanding of why some individuals benefit more from interventions. The identified moderating role of genetic factors provides a rationale for differentiated cognitive intervention strategies. For example, older adults carrying risk variants may require more intensive or individualized programs of social participation and cognitive stimulation, or a combination with other interventions (e.g., cognitive training and nutritional guidance) to compensate for a potentially attenuated response to standard CSCS. In sum, the genetic moderation revealed here points to new directions for research on cognitive aging and brings a more refined, personalized perspective to intervention practice: Integrating genetic evidence into the implementation of active aging is likely to improve intervention effectiveness and, ultimately, better safeguard and promote the cognitive health of older adults.

The distinctive Chinese cultural context may shape the above findings in unique ways. On the one hand, collectivism and the ethic of filial piety are likely to amplify the cognitive gains associated with CSCS. Chinese society emphasizes the shared responsibility of the family–community collective for individual well-being; earlier research has shown that the dense social interaction environment fostered by collectivist and filial piety values can enhance social stimulation and positive affect, thereby further strengthening the protective effect of CSCS on cognitive functioning (Zhang et al. 2010). On the other hand, China’s policy “turn toward the community” in elder care provides institutional support for the dissemination and effectiveness of CSCS. As material living standards have risen, the government has made older adults’ spiritual care and community support focal points of elder-care service reform (Reynolds et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2023). For instance, in 2022, the National Health Commission launched the “Elderly Psychological Care Project (2022–2025),” mandating widespread community-level mental health and cognitive screening (National Health Commission of China 2022). Local authorities have since accelerated the construction of embedded community elder-care complexes, shifting spiritual comfort services from gap-filling remedies to routine provision.

Limitations and Future Directions of the Study: Firstly, self-reported measures were used for assessing CSCS, social participation, and cognitive function, which could lead to measurement bias. Future research could mitigate this potential bias by combining self-reported data with objective measurements (such as physiological data or neuroimaging data). Secondly, it must be acknowledged that the dataset used in this study has inherent limitations (the CLHLS provides relatively coarse items that merge multiple conceptual dimensions into a single indicator. For example, "spiritual comfort and chatting to relieve boredom" simultaneously covers the dimensions of soothing emotions and empathic companionship, and may even encompass informal psychological counseling, yet the questionnaire does not disaggregate these qualitatively different services; some conceptual dimensions are not covered at all. The more specialized and in-depth form of support—psychological counseling—is not explicitly queried, implying that even if communities provide counseling, it may be subsumed under the "spiritual comfort and chatting to relieve boredom" item or not recorded). These limitations prevent us from verifying certain aspects of the CSCS concept and weaken the alignment between the concept and the available data. Lastly, although the RI-CLPM provides an effective method for analyzing longitudinal data and exploring causal relationships between variables, caution should still be exercised in interpreting causal relationships. Even with advanced statistical models, it is challenging to completely eliminate all potential confounders or definitively prove causality. In the future research, we plan to employ rigorous experimental designs to explore causal relationships between variables more conclusively.

Conclusions

In summary, the longitudinal sample of older adults Chinese used in this study significantly aids in understanding the correlation between CSCS and cognitive function in older adults. To minimize any potential evaluation biases, we applied the RI-CLPM to control for numerous confounding factors. Furthermore, this study explored the mediating role of social participation and the moderating effects of genetics in elucidating the aforementioned relationship. These findings extend our understanding of the mechanisms through which social environments and genetic factors interact to shape cognitive health, offering fresh empirical support for the development of cognitive theory in later life. The results demonstrate that enhancing the quality of CSCS and actively fostering older adults’ social participation can effectively boost cognitive functioning—insights that are instructive for optimizing community service provision and strengthening cognitive health interventions for seniors. From a policy perspective, we recommend incorporating these findings into healthy aging strategies by institutionalizing the delivery of high-quality CSCS, expanding programs that promote social participation among older adults, and designing precision cognitive health interventions that account for individual differences, thereby facilitating the translation of theoretical advances into practical outcomes.
