Oral artificial microbio-vaccine triggers synergistic immunogenic cell death induction and tumor microenvironment remodeling for liver cancer therapy
Authors: Qian Cheng, Siyao Li, Lu Deng, Li Xiong, Huiling Hu, Jingjing Li, Lele Zhang, Boda Guo, Chuan Zheng, Jinming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial cellular systems, engineered to mimic biological functions, offer a promising platform for advanced drug delivery and immunotherapy. Translating such systems into effective oral tumor vaccines, however, remains challenging due to the harsh gastrointestinal environment, inefficient immune activation, and the toxicity of potent immunogenic agents. To overcome these barriers, we engineered an oral artificial micro bio-vaccine via a hierarchical assembly strategy. Triptolide (TP), an immunogenic cell death inducer, was co-assembled with glycyrrhizic acid (GA), an immunomodulator, into a nanodetoxifying core to facilitate efficient loading into hollow Saccharomyces cerevisiae microcapsules (YC), forming the final aggregate (TP/GA-NYPs). This rational design leverages the YC for gastric protection and gut mucosa-targeting, while the Chitosan shell ensures robust encapsulation. Crucially, the TP/GA core demonstrates a synergistic interplay, in which TP potently induces immunogenic cell death, while GA concurrently remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by suppressing metastasis-related markers and enhancing immunostimulatory cytokine profiles. This spatiotemporally coupled action within a single oral aggregate elicits robust intestinal mucosal and systemic immunity, effectively suppressing liver cancer progression and metastasis while mitigating the systemic toxicity of TP. This work highlights the therapeutic potential of functional aggregates in orchestrating synergistic anti-tumor immunity and offers a versatile platform for oral immunotherapy.

Introduction

The therapeutic challenges in liver cancer starkly highlight the limitations of current modalities. The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) of liver cancer renders it notoriously resistant to treatments1. While immune checkpoint inhibitors have marked a paradigm shift, their efficacy is limited by primary resistance in many patients and the inability to establish long-term immunological memory to prevent recurrence2, 3, 4. Therapeutic tumor vaccines eliminate tumors by activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes, generating a more specific and durable anti-tumor immunity5. For instance, BioNTech's mRNA-based vaccines, which encode tumor-associated antigens and are delivered via intravenous injection, have shown promise in early clinical trials by inducing potent T cell responses6. Furthermore, Wang et al.7 have explored nanovaccines co-delivering peptide antigens and mRNA-encoded antigens, demonstrating enhanced dendritic cells (DCs) targeting and activation in mouse models. However, most existing platforms rely on injectable administration8, 9, 10, 11, which often yields suboptimal efficacy due to poor biodistribution and fails to induce robust mucosal immunity—a critical component for preventing metastases12.

As a critical component of the mucosal immune system, the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) harbors abundant immune cells13. Notably, the gut–liver axis creates a unique functional link between the GALT and the liver. Immune cells primed and activated in the GALT can traffic directly to the liver via the lymphatic system and portal venous circulation, mediating robust local hepatic immunosurveillance. This functional crosstalk renders oral vaccine delivery an exceptionally promising strategy for liver cancer immunotherapy, as it can elicit potent mucosal immune responses that are inaccessible to conventional systemic vaccination via intramuscular or subcutaneous routes14, 15, 16. Compared to injectable vaccines, oral formulations offer non-invasiveness and reduced iatrogenic infection risk17. Yet, the oral route presents formidable biological barriers: destructive gastric conditions, the intestinal epithelial barrier, and the challenge of achieving targeted accumulation in tumor tissue18, 19, 20. Critically, potent immunogenic agents (such as immunogenic cell death inducers) often induce severe toxicity, while current vaccines predominantly focus on antigen presentation while neglecting TME reprogramming and metastasis suppression21, 22, 23, 24. Thus, developing novel oral vaccines to safely and effectively activate mucosal and systemic immune response, and remodel the immunosuppressive TME is imperative for inhibiting tumor metastasis and recurrence25,26.

Here, we have engineered an oral artificial microbio-vaccine formulation (designated NYP) based on natural, food-grade yeast cell wall (YC) microcarriers, to overcome the core bottlenecks of oral liver cancer immunotherapy. Briefly, self-assembled nanocomplexes of the potent immunogenic cell death (ICD) inducer triptolide (TP) and immunomodulatory glycyrrhizic acid (GA) were chitosan-modified to confer positive surface charge, then encapsulated into YC to fabricate NYP. The YC platform endows NYP with robust resistance to gastrointestinal degradation and specific Peyer's patches targeting via microfold cells (M cells) recognition for efficient gut-associated lymphoid tissue translocation27, 28, 29, 30. Following entry into the systemic circulation, NYP is preferentially internalized by circulating macrophages, which act as “Trojan horses” to hitchhike the formulation to the tumor site via their inherent chemotaxis toward the immunosuppressive TME. Once internalized by tumor cells, NYP is rapidly shuttled to the lysosomal compartment, and achieves pH-responsive on-demand drug release, which synergizes with GA-mediated protection to mitigate TP's dose-limiting gastrointestinal toxicity31,32.The co-delivered components elicit a multi-pronged synergistic anti-tumor cascade: TP triggers robust ICD to prime adaptive immunity; GA cooperates with YC β-glucan to promote DCs maturation, downregulate metastasis-related MMP9, and collaborate with TP to remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and activating both mucosal and systemic anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 1). Collectively, the NYP oral vaccine system achieves potent inhibition of liver tumor growth, lung metastasis, and tumor recurrence via establishing durable immunological memory. This work provides a safe, clinically translatable oral therapeutic-prophylactic vaccine modality for liver cancer, addressing critical limitations of current cancer immunotherapies.Figure 1Illustration depicting the process of preparation and mechanism of the oral artificial yeast vaccine (TP/GA-NYPs) for synergistic immunotherapy against liver cancer.

Materials and methods

GA was obtained from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology (Dalian, China). Chitosan (CS) was provided by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). FITC was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). Cyanine5.5 (Cy5.5) was obtained from MedChem Express (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). DAPI, annexin V-FITC/PI detection kit, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) detection kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) kits were purchased from BioSharp (Hefei, China). Recombinant murine Granulocyte-Macrophage Colony Stimulating Factor (GM-CSF) was purchased from Novoprotein (Suzhou, China). High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1) rabbit monoclonal antibody and Calreticulin (CRT) rabbit monoclonal antibody were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody was purchased from ZENBIO (Chengdu, China). Mouse IL-10, IL-12 p70, IL-4, and TNF-α enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were purchased from MultiSciences (Hangzhou, China). Antibodies, including PerCP anti-mouse CD11b, FITC anti-mouse F4/80, PE/Cy7 anti-mouse CD206, FITC anti-mouse CD11c, PE/Cy7 anti-mouse CD80, PE anti-mouse CD86, PE/Cy7 anti-mouse CD3, FITC anti-mouse CD45, PE anti-mouse Ly-6G/Ly-6C (Gr-1), PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-mouse CD4, PE anti-mouse CD25, PE/Cy5 anti-mouse CD8a, PE anti-mouse CD62L, FlTC anti-mouse CD44 were purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA).

Cell cultures and animals

The mouse mononuclear macrophage leukemia cells (RAW264.7), mouse hepatocarcinoma cell line (H22), and human hepatocarcinoma cell line (HepG2) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). RAW264.7 and HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, Shanghai, China), and H22 cells were cultured in a RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Shanghai, China). All of them were supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Tianhang Biotechnology, Huzhou, China) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Boster, Wuhan, China). All cells were cultivated at 37 °C within a 5% CO2 water-jacketed incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Immunocompetent male BALB/c mice weighing about 18–20 g were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animal experiments involved in this study complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. The animal experiments were conducted under the permission of the Animal Ethics Committee of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Chengdu, China) (permission number SYXK2020-124), and all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant regulations.

Preparation of NP

TP/GA-NPs (NP) were fabricated based on the solvent injection method. Briefly, TP (2 mg) and GA (4 mg) were dissolved in acetone (1 mL) together, and CS (1 mg) was dissolved in ultrapure water (10 mL). The organic solvent mixture was added dropwise into ultrapure water with CS at 45 °C with a constant rate of 1 mL/min.

Preparation of YC

YC was prepared with baker's yeast according to the previously reported method33, 34, 35. Briefly, 20 g of baker's yeast was dispersed in 200 mL of NaOH (1 mol/L), and the suspension was continuously stirred at 80 °C for 1 h. The insoluble material containing the YC was collected by centrifugation at 424×g for 10 min (TGL-25M, BIORIDGE CENTRIFUGE, Shanghai, China). Later on, the sediment was resuspended in 40 mL of pure water, which was adjusted to pH 4–5 with HCl, and incubated for 1 h at 55 °C. Subsequently, the insoluble residue was again collected by the same centrifugal conditions and then washed with 200 mL of pure water once, four times with 40 mL of isopropanol, and twice with 40 mL of acetone. The extracted material was frozen in a −80 °C freezer and then lyophilized to obtain a grey white fine powder.

Fabrication of NYP

TP/GA-NYPs (NYP) were prepared by electrostatic force-driven self-deposition36,37. First, 100 mg YC was incubated with 1 ml of pure water at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 10 mL NP was added to the solution containing YC. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, it was centrifuged at 1696×g for 10 min and resuspended it using deionized water. Subsequently, to better encapsulate the drug, the suspension underwent a lyophilization–hydration cycle, repeated twice. Before the last lyophilization, thoroughly wash with deionized water to remove unpackaged NP.

Characterization of NP

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, LC-2030C-3D, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) analysis was employed to measure the drug encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) of TP and GA within NP.

Before HPLC analysis, three times the volume of methanol was incorporated into the NP solution, followed by sonication for 15 min to disrupt the nanostructure and release drugs thoroughly. The mobile phases for HPLC analysis to determine TP and GA were acetonitrile/water (v/v, 46:54%) and methanol/0.1% phosphoric acid aqueous solution (v/v, 75:25%), respectively38. The EE and LE of the drugs were calculated according to Eqs. (1), (2):(1)EE (%) = (Amount of drug in nanoparticles)/(Amount of TP/GA added) × 100(2)LE (%) = (Amount of drug in nanoparticles)/(Total amount of drug added) × 100

The average hydrodynamic particle size (nm), polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential (mV) of NP were measured using dynamic light scattering with a Particle Analyzer Lite sizer 1090 (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria). The calculation of the average and standard deviations (SDs) of the diameter or PDI was repeated three times.

The morphology of NP was precisely viewed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM 1200X, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). A diluted NP suspension drop was delicately placed on a carbon-coated copper grid and then stained with uranyl acetate and analyzed via TEM.

Characterization of NYP

The particle sizes of YC and NYP were determined through a micron particle size analyzer (Bettersizer 2600, Bettersize Instruments, Dandong, China). The zeta potential measurement was carried out in the same way as that for NP, using the Particle Analyzer Lite sizer 1090.

Untreated yeast cells were dispersed in a minimal volume of water first and subsequently subjected to freeze–drying in conjunction with YC and NYP. Subsequently, a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Gemini 300, ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) was employed to characterize the morphological features of the resultant samples meticulously. To optimize imaging quality, the samples were sputter-coated with gold under vacuum conditions prior to imaging acquisition.

For the purpose of scrutinizing the encapsulation status of NP within YC, the aforementioned three sets of samples were further analyzed and characterized using TEM.

For the determination of EE and DL, the sample treatment and detection methods were identical to those described in 2.6. section, and the following Eqs. (3), (4) were used for calculation:(3)EE (%) = (Amount of drug in NYP)/(Amount of drug added) × 100%(4)LE (%) = (Amount of drug in NYP)/(Total amount of drug and YC added) × 100%

The fluorescent dye calcofluor white can bind to glucan in the YC. To visually confirm whether NP are loaded into the empty YC or not, we labeled the NP with FITC and then loaded them into the YC labeled with calcofluor white. Subsequently, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, TCS SP8 SR, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) was utilized to observe the co-localization of NP and YC.

Then we evaluated the stability of NYP in simulated gastric fluid (SGF) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) by referring to the methods reported previously29,39. The NYP loaded with TP was mixed with SGF (pH 2) or SIF, and then incubated in a 37 °C shaker (100 rpm) for 4 h. After freeze–drying, the morphology of NYP was observed using a scanning electron microscope. Furthermore, NP and NYP suspensions (1 mL) containing the equivalent amount of TP were placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO 3500 Da) against different buffers (15 mL, SIF, SGF) containing 1% Tween 80. They were incubated in a shaking bath (100 rpm) at 37 °C. At various time intervals, release medium (0.5 mL) was collected, and fresh medium in an equal volume was added. With the help of the aforementioned HPLC analysis technique, the TP concentration in the release medium was identified, and the cumulative drug release was determined. The release profiles of TP and GA were similarly evaluated using the same method in conventional buffer media at pH 1.2, pH 4.5, and pH 7.4, respectively.

Toxic effects of drugs on cells

The CCK-8 kit was used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of TP, NP, and NYP to H22 cells. Briefly, H22 was seeded in the 96-well plates (with 1×104 cells per well) and incubated in DMEM or RPMI 1640 medium for a certain period. The cells were cultured for 24 h with TP, NP, and NYP samples prepared in low serum medium (0.5% FBS) with equal amounts of TP (0–1000 nmol/L). Subsequently, we added the CCK-8 detection reagent as required by the instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured and documented through a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Using the same method, the cytotoxicity of TP and NYP was assessed in Caco-2 and RAW264.7 cells.

Cellular uptake in cells

To evaluate the cellular uptake efficiency of NYP, the uptake of NYP by HepG2 was assessed. We added FITC additionally during the preparation process of NP to label the NP. Subsequently, we loaded it into NYP to obtain FITC-NYP. HepG2 cells (1×105 cells per well) were seeded into 12-well plates and confocal dishes. After the cells adhered to the plates or dishes, the culture medium was replaced with the medium containing FITC-NP and FITC-NYP with the same FITC concentration. The cells were co-incubated for different durations. After being collected by trypsin digestion, the cells were resuspended in PBS, cellular uptake was visualized using CLSM, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified by flow cytometry (FCM, FACS CantoII, Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Using the same method, the uptake efficiency was further investigated in RAW264.7 cells.

Apoptosis analysis via flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis induced was detected by an Annexin V-FITC/PI detection kit40. H22 cells (1×105 cells per well) were seeded in twelve-well plates and then treated with GA, TP, NP, YC, and NYP at equivalent concentrations of 50 nmol/L TP for 24 h. The culture medium without adding drugs was used as a control. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were collected and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI according to the manufacturer's protocol. Finally, all samples were analyzed using FCM.

ICD effect of NYP in vitro

To evaluate the ICD effect, H22 tumor cells (2 × 105 per well) were seeded into 12-well plates. After treatments with the various materials (Saline, GA, TP, NP, YC, NYP) for 24 h, the cells were subjected to a routine immunofluorescence examination to assess CRT surface exposure and HMGB1 release. Upon collection, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA. Subsequently, we fixed the cells on adhesive glass slides (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), two slides per group. The two parts of the cells were respectively stained with HMGB1 rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:100) and CRT rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:100) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C. It should be emphasized that the cells intended for incubation with the HMGB1 antibody were initially subjected to permeabilization using 0.1% Triton at room temperature for 15 min before the incubation process. The next day, they were stained with FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:1000). Then, the samples were mounted with an anti-fluorescence quenching agent containing DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired via CLSM. Meanwhile, the ATP kit was used to measure the amount of ATP inside the cells. According to the manufacturer's instructions, we detected the intracellular ATP content by chemiluminescence method at a wavelength of 560 nm on a multi-functional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Using the same method, the ICD-inducing capability of NYP was compared with that of DOX in H22 cells, and HMGB1 release, CRT exposure, and ATP release were further examined in HepG2 cells.

Activation of BMDCs

Bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) were generated from bone marrow cavities of 7–8-week-old C57BL/6 mice reported previously. After the animals were euthanized, they were put in 75% ethanol for disinfection for 30 min. Then the bones were collected and placed into 75% ethanol for 2 min, and afterward washed in PBS. Both distal ends of the bones were cut off, and the bone marrow was collected by flushing with RPMI 1640 medium. Red blood cells were lysed by incubating the bone marrow preparation in ACK lysis buffer for 2 min. The remaining cells were collected by centrifugation at 300×g for 5 min, and then grown in 12-well plates (1 × 106 cells per well) in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 10 ng/mL IL-4, and 20 ng/mL GM-CSF. Fresh medium was added on Day 3. On Day 6, the nonadherent cells were aspirated and incubated in 12-well plates with fresh medium and were treated with RPMI 1640 medium, GA, TP, NP, YC, and NYP. Following 24 h of incubation, BMDCs were collected and stained with FITC anti-mouse CD11c, PE/Cy7 anti-mouse CD80, and PE anti-mouse CD86, measured by FCM.

Biodistribution studies

To establish an H22 tumor-bearing BALB/c mouse model for evaluating the tumor-targeting capability of NYP, 3 × 106 H22 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into male BALB/c mice. Following successful tumor establishment, the mice were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 3) and administered orally with free Cy5.5, Cy5.5-NP, or Cy5.5-NYP (0.6 mg/kg), respectively. In vivo imaging was performed at predetermined intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 10, and 24 h) using an IVIS Lumina imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). After 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys), Peyer's patches, mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN), and tumors were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was conducted using Living Image Software Version 4.5. Additionally, frozen sections of tumor tissues and Peyer's patches were prepared to visualize drug distribution through fluorescence microscopy.

To further investigate the biodistribution of NYP after oral administration, H22 tumor-bearing mice were orally administered with NP and NYP separately. The mice were euthanized at 0, 2, 4, 12, and 24 h post-administration, followed by collection of the ileum, colon, and tumor tissues. The harvested tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and prepared as frozen sections. The spatial distribution of TP in these tissues was subsequently analyzed using mass spectrometry imaging.

Immune activation and anti-tumor effects in vivo

A tumor xenograft model was established with H22 cells. H22 cells (3 × 106/100 μL, respectively) were inoculated in the lower right side of the back of BALB/c mice. Six days later, mice were randomized to six groups (n = 9) and received different treatments. The drugs were administered orally by gavage. Saline, TP, GA, NP, YC, and NYP (TP dose of 0.3 mg/kg) were administered four times, on Days 0, 4, 8, and 12. The first administration day was defined as Day 0. Mouse survival, tumor growth, and body weight were continuously monitored. The tumor volume was calculated according to Eq. (5):(5)Tumor volume = Length × 1/2 Width2

Tumor volume thresholds are set at 2000 mm3 as the mandatory sacrifice indicator. Of note, for the survival analysis, all mice (n = 9 per group) were included from the beginning of the experiment until their respective endpoints. For the terminal measurements (e.g., tumor weight and volume), only mice that survived until the scheduled sacrifice at the end of the study were analyzed.

Seven days after the last administration, all mice were sacrificed. The number of Peyer's patches in the small intestine was quantified. The tumors were dissected, weighed, and photographed. Each tumor specimen was divided into multiple segments for distinct analytical purposes. A part of the tumor tissues was lysed, and the supernatant was collected for quantification of IL-10, IL-4, IL-12 p70, and TNF-α cytokine levels using commercially available ELISA kits. Some of the tumor tissues were fixed with 4% (v/v) formalin neutral buffer solution to preserve them, and then the tumor tissue slices were acquired using cryoembedding. Subsequently, H&E, TUNEL, MMP9, VEGF, and CD31 staining procedures were performed to further evaluate the tumor inhibition, apoptosis, and angiogenesis suppression efficacy. To further evaluate the inhibitory potential against tumor metastasis, immunofluorescence staining of EMT-related markers (N-cadherin and E-cadherin) was performed. Additionally, double staining of cGAS and STING was carried out to investigate the molecular mechanisms of TP-induced ICD.

Lung specimens from each experimental group were collected, intratracheally perfused with India ink (Leagene Biotechnology, Beijing, China; 1:5 PBS dilution), and subsequently fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Photographic documentation of ink-stained pulmonary foci was performed and quantified by direct manual counting.

ICD and immune activation effect in vivo

To validate the impact of ICD induction in vivo, tumor tissues were used to analyze CRT and HMGB1 expression in vivo via immunofluorescence.

Tumor tissues were enzymatically digested into single-cell suspensions, followed by flow cytometric analysis of immune cell infiltration using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific for DCs (CD11c+CD80+CD86+), CD4+ T cells(CD45+CD3+CD4+), CD8+ T cells (CD45+CD3+CD8+), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (CD45+CD11b+Gr-1+), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) (CD11b+F4/80+CD86+/CD206+), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (CD45+CD3+CD4+CD24+). Single-cell suspensions derived from spleen and tumor draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) were also immunophenotyped by multicolor FCM to quantify DCs (CD11c+CD80+CD86+), CD4+ (CD45+CD3+CD4+), and CD8+ (CD45+CD3+CD8+) T cell populations.

Immunization and antitumor efficacy in the anti-recurrence model

The same modeling method was built as described previously. H22 cells (3 × 106/100 μL, respectively) were inoculated in the lower right side of the back of BALB/c mice. Six days later, the mice were randomized into six groups and received different treatments. The drugs were administered orally by gavage. Saline, TP, GA, NP, YC, and NYP (TP dose of 0.3 mg/kg) were administered four times, on Days 0, 3, 6, and 9. The first administration day was defined as Day 0. On Day 11th, tumor resection surgery was performed on tumor-bearing mice, leaving approximately 10% of the residual tumor tissue to verify the ability of NYP to inhibit tumor recurrence.

Mouse survival, tumor growth, and body weight were recorded every other day. Twelve days after the resection surgery, all mice were sacrificed (n = 5). Tumor cells, lymphocytes in tumor-draining lymph nodes, and splenocytes were collected on Day 24 and stained with anti-mouse CD3, CD4, CD8, CD44, CD62L, CD86, CD80, and CD11c antibodies. The effector memory T cells (CD3+CD4+CD44+CD62L–, CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L–) and mature DC population (CD11c+CD80+CD86+) were analyzed by FCM.

Safety evaluation of the oral vaccines

The biosafety studies were carried out on healthy BALB/c mice. mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5) and injected intravenously with Saline, TP, GA, NP, YC, and NYP (TP dose of 0.3 mg/kg) every 2 days for 4 times, respectively. Then, the body weights of the mice were monitored every 2 days.

On the 13th day, whole blood samples were collected and analyzed for white blood cells (WBCs), platelets (PLTs), lymphocytes, monocytes, hemoglobin (HGB), granulocytes, and red blood cells (RBCs) concentration using automated hematology analyzers (XN-1000, Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). Serum samples were collected for the hematological assay to detect the levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CRE), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). Besides, the major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys, were collected for H&E staining. Additionally, gastric, ileal, and colonic tissues were collected and processed into H&E staining sections to evaluate the protective effects of orally administered NYP on the gastrointestinal tract of mice.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality and homogeneity of variances were tested for all datasets. A one-way two-sided analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied assuming normality and homogeneity of variances. If these assumptions were violated, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used instead. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant difference, Dunn's post hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied for pairwise comparisons. The statistical significance of the differences among the two groups was assessed by analysis of variance followed by a test based on a value of P < 0.05 using GraphPad Prism software Vision 8). Statistical significance was set as follows: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ns denotes no significant difference.

Results

Preparation and characterization of NYP

We first fabricated TP&GA self-assemble nanoparticles (NP) using the solvent injection method. AutoDock was employed to reveal the mechanism of this assembly through performing molecular dynamics simulations on the binding capability between TP and GA. The results were typically evaluated based on binding energy to determine docking success. As shown in Fig. 2A, the two molecules formed four hydrogen bonds at the docking site, with an optimal binding energy of −4.0 kcal/mol, indicating a stable interaction between TP and GA. NP exhibited a monodisperse size profile with a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 139.25 ± 0.63 nm (PDI <0.2), as determined by dynamic light scattering (Fig. 2B). TEM image (Fig. 2B, top left corner) indicated that the NP displayed a spherical or quasi-spherical morphology. To achieve effective encapsulation within YC, surface modification of nanoparticles with chitosan resulted in a complete reversal of Zeta potential from −24.44 ± 1.19 mV to 33.76 ± 1.52 mV (Fig. 2C), achieving cationic surface charge conversion and facilitating electrostatic interaction-driven self-assembly with negatively charged YC. The encapsulation efficiency of TP and GA in NP are 99.65 ± 2.25% and 91.63 ± 4.59%, respectively, and the loading capacity of TP and GA in NP are 28.95 ± 0.19% and 53.47 ± 1.80%, respectively. Fig. 2D illustrates the fabrication process of nanoparticle-loaded yeast capsules (NYP), where yeast capsules were loaded with NP via synergistic integration of electrostatic self-assembly and aqueous hydration–lyophilization cycles. As the result showed in Fig. 2E, YC exhibited a negative surface charge with a zeta potential of −9.81 ± 0.32 mV. Upon loading with cation-modified nanoparticles, the zeta potential reversed to a positive value of 8.81 ± 1.63 mV, indicating the drug loaded nanoparticles were successful loaded into yeast capsules, while the particle size after nanoparticles loading remained basically unchanged (Fig. 2F–H). SEM and TEM micrographs demonstrated distinct morphological features (Fig. 2I): yeast cells exhibited smooth spherical surfaces, whereas yeast capsules displayed irregular porous architectures indicative of cytoplasmic component removal. NYP presented a relatively plump morphology, further demonstrating successful encapsulation of NP within the YC. To visually confirm the encapsulation of NP within YC, fluorescent staining with Calcofluor White, a β-glucan-specific dye targeting the YC, was employed for selective CLSM imaging. As shown in Fig. 2J–a characteristic core–shell architecture was observed, wherein FITC-labeled NP exhibited homogeneous distribution within the YC core, providing direct evidence of successful NP loading. After encapsulation into YC to form NYP, the encapsulation efficiency of TP was 65.26 ± 4.60%, while the drug loading capacity was 1.78 ± 7.60%, and the encapsulation efficiency of GA was 62.31 ± 5.18%, and the drug loading capacity was 2.71 ± 7.47% (Supporting Information Table S1). The pivotal challenge in oral drug delivery systems lies in ensuring that nanoparticles can maintain structural integrity while traversing the harsh gastric environment. To investigate whether YC could protect NP from the harsh gastrointestinal environment, SIF and SGF were employed to assess the stability of NYP following oral gavage. As anticipated, NYP maintained structural integrity after 4 h incubation in SGF (pH 1.2) and SIF (pH 6.8), confirmed by SEM (Fig. 2K). As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S1, the cumulative release of both TP and GA from NYP in SGF and SIF remained relatively low over 24 h, when compared with NP. These findings indicated that NYP remained stable during gastrointestinal transit, which was a critical feature for subsequent M cell-mediated transport. Furthermore, the release profiles of NYP in standard buffer solutions at pH 1.2, 4.5, and 7.4, are provided in Supporting Information Fig. S2. The results demonstrated that NYP exhibited minimal cumulative release of TP and GA at pH 1.2 and pH 7.4 over 24 h, indicating good stability in gastric and circulatory environments. At pH 4.5, NYP showed sustained and significantly higher release, with approximately 67.84% of TP and 71.14% of GA released within 24 h. Notably, compared with uncoated NP, NYP displayed a slower release profile at pH 1.2, confirming the effective protective role of YC shell in preventing premature drug leakage in the acidic gastric environment. In vitro immune activation analysis of NYP

In this study, TP was chosen as a model chemotherapeutic agent to induce ICD, which can kill tumor cells and facilitate the uptake, processing, and presentation of tumor antigens by APCs to improve T cell responses. First, we evaluated cellular uptake of FITC-labeled NYP in tumor cells and macrophages. CLSM and flow cytometric quantification (Supporting Information Figs. S3 and S4) revealed efficient uptake by both cell types after 4 h, with 80.13% of RAW264.7 and 69.23% of HepG2 cells exhibiting positive fluorescence. Then CCK-8 assay was conducted to evaluate the cytotoxicity of NYP against tumor cells, intestinal epithelial cells and immune cells in vitro. As shown in Fig. 3A, TP, NP, and NYP all decreased the cell proliferation of H22 cells; the IC50 values were determined as 44.21 ± 0.96 nmol/L (TP), 40.27 ± 0.98 nmol/L (NP), and 41.73 ± 0.98 nmol/L (NYP), respectively, after incubation for 24 h. The slightly attenuated cytotoxicity of NYP may be attributed to the encapsulating effect of YC on NP. For the potential toxicity of orally delivered TP to intestinal epithelial cells and immune cells in Peyer's patches, we evaluated the cytotoxic effects of free TP and NYP using Caco-2 and RAW264.7 as representative cell models. Recent studies have demonstrated that ICD triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including extracellular ATP, surface-exposed CRT, and secreted HMGB1 from dying tumor cells. The release of DAMPs by tumor cells activates DCs, thereby triggering T cell-mediated immune responses41,42. To validate the ICD effects of NYP, the release of HMGB1 and ATP, and the expression of CRT were examined in H22 tumor cells post-drug treatment. As depicted in Fig. 3D, relative to the saline group, CLSM images revealed a marked decrease in nuclear co-localization of blue fluorescence in TP, NP, and NYP treatment groups, indicating a notable release of HMGB1 from the nucleus into the extracellular milieu which will facilitate DCs maturation. As shown in Fig. 3E, the expression of CRT on the H22 cell surface was enhanced after TP, NP, and NYP treatment incubation for 24 h when compared to the saline group. In addition, the content of intracellular ATP levels of TP, NP, and NYP groups was significantly decreased compared to the saline group (Fig. 3F), indicating that NYP was able to induce the release of intracellular ATP to the extracellular space. To verify the universality of the ICD for TP in our NYP vaccine, we have further validated TP-induced ICD in another human liver cancer cell line, HepG2. The results consistently demonstrated that NYP effectively induce CRT exposure, HMGB-1 and ATP release in HepG2 cells (Supporting Information Fig. S6). Notably, NYP induced ATP secretion, CRT exposure, and HMGB1 release at levels comparable to those of commonly used ICD inducer DOX43 (Supporting Information Fig. S7). DCs play a vital role in generating tumor specific immune responses, thus, we investigate the maturation of BMDCs treated with different formulations via FCM for co-stimulatory molecule upregulation (CD80+/CD86+). Before FCM analysis, BMDCs were co-cultured for 24 h with the supernatant of H22 cells treated with different formulations. As shown in Fig. 3G and H, compared to TP groups, the YC group showed slightly increased maturation of DCs, while NYP group exhibited the highest proportion of CD80+CD86+ BMDCs, suggesting the DAMPs released by tumor cells treated with NYP could enhance the maturation of DCs in vitro, thus potentially boosting the progression of ICD.

The frequency of effector memory CD8+/CD4+ T cells (TEM, CD3+ CD8+/CD4+ CD62L−CD44+) was also analyzed in the tumor. The percentage of CD8+ TEM in the NYP group was 4.02- and 1.82-fold higher than those in the saline and NP groups, respectively (Fig. 8G and H). Similarly, CD4+ TEM percentages increased by 6.46- and 2.88-fold compared to saline and NP groups (Fig. 8I and J). TEM in the spleen of mice across different treatment groups was further examined. In the spleen, CD8+ TEM cells in the NYP group exhibited a proportion of 5.14%, representing a twofold increase compared to the saline group (Fig. 8K). Analogous trends were observed in TDLNs, where the NYP group exhibited significantly elevated proportions of mature DCs and CD4+/CD8+ TEM cells relative to other groups (Fig. 8L–O). Thus, all the datas revealed that the NYP tumor vaccine indeed has the potential to induce a memory effect in mice for effectively inhibiting tumor recurrence.

Biosafety assessment and protective effects against TP-related side effects

Each mouse received a total of four treatments (Fig. 9A). During the 12-day treatment, the body weight of mice was monitored, and no significant abnormalities were observed in all groups (Fig. 9B). Discussion

Liver cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, with extremely limited curative options for patients with advanced disease. Current systemic therapies, dominated by targeted agents51 and immune checkpoint inhibitors52, are plagued by a core clinical bottleneck: an objective response rate below 30%, frequent acquired drug resistance, and severe systemic toxicities. These limitations are largely rooted in the inherent “immune-cold” phenotype, characterized by insufficient effector T cell infiltration and massive enrichment of immunosuppressive cells in the TME53. Meanwhile, conventional injectable tumor vaccines face critical challenges in breaking through the intestinal barrier and effectively activating mucosal immunity, failing to elicit robust and safe anti-tumor immune responses. To address these challenges, we developed an oral in situ tumor vaccine (TP/GA-NYPs) based on natural YC, establishing a full-chain therapeutic strategy of gastrointestinal stability–intestinal barrier breakthrough–lymphatic systemic transport–active tumor targeting–cascade immune activation. After oral administration, the rigid YC shell protects the micron-sized formulation from premature degradation and drug leakage in the harsh acidic and enzymatic gastrointestinal environment.

Upon reaching the ileal Peyer's patches, β-glucan on the YC surface is specifically recognized by the Dectin-1 receptor highly expressed on intestinal M cells, mediating efficient transepithelial transport across the intestinal barrier into the subepithelial region of Peyer's patches. Subsequently, NYP is efficiently phagocytosed by resident macrophages in the gut-associated lymphoid tissue; these NYP-laden macrophages act as “Trojan horses”, migrating through lymphatic vessels to the thoracic duct and then into the systemic circulation, and actively home to the liver tumor site via their inherent chemotaxis toward the immunosuppressive TME. After internalization by tumor cells, NYP is shuttled to the acidic lysosomal compartment, where the YC shell is degraded by β-glucosidase, triggering on-demand site-specific release of the loaded active agents. In this formulation, YC also acts as natural immune adjuvant to promote antigen-presenting cell maturation. TP efficiently induces tumor ICD, triggers in situ release of endogenous tumor neoantigens and damage-associated molecular patterns, and activates the cGAS–STING pathway to initiate tumor-specific anti-tumor immunity. GA downregulates metastasis-related MMP9, blocking tumor invasion and metastasis, and collaboratively reversing the immunosuppressive TME.

This oral vaccine ultimately achieves deep TME remodeling, with significantly increased cytotoxic CD8+ T cell infiltration and reduced accumulation of regulatory T cells and M2-type tumor-associated macrophages in tumor tissues. In immunocompetent mouse models, TP/GA-NYP exerted potent tumor growth inhibition, and simultaneously markedly suppressed lung metastasis and postoperative tumor recurrence. Notably, NYP exhibits promising synergistic potential in combination with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, as its robust TME reprogramming and efficient tumor-specific T cell priming can effectively overcome the primary resistance to anti-PD-1 monotherapy in immune-cold liver cancer54, 55, 56, 57. Collectively, this study provides a novel design paradigm for oral liver cancer vaccines. Follow-up studies will complete systematic preclinical evaluation via orthotopic liver cancer models, to advance clinical translation and provide a promising new therapeutic solution for advanced liver cancer immunotherapy.
