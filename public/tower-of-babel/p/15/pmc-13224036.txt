Insights from a rare case of eosinophilic solid and cystic renal cell carcinoma: Diagnostic highlights and therapeutic directions
Authors: Jun Qiu, Luhua Ni, Liangzhi Yu, Yuanxing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEosinophilic solid and cystic renal cell carcinoma (ESC RCC) is a rare, recently recognized renal neoplasm. We present a case of a 19-year-old female with an incidentally discovered left renal mass. Computed tomography revealed a 3.2 cm well-defined enhancing lesion. The patient underwent laparoscopic partial nephrectomy. Histopathology demonstrated mixed solid and cystic growth patterns with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry showed CK7(+), CA9(−), and AMACR(+), and NGS analysis excluded VHL gene mutations, consistent with ESC RCC. The patient recovered uneventfully with favorable prognosis. This case contributes to the limited literature on ESC RCC in young female patients.Keywords: Eosinophilic solid and cystic renal cell carcinoma, ESC RCC, Renal cell carcinoma, Kidney neoplasm, Case report

Introduction

Eosinophilic solid and cystic renal cell carcinoma (ESC RCC) is a rare, recently recognized subtype of renal cell carcinoma. Since its initial description, ESC RCC has been increasingly identified as a distinct entity with unique clinicopathological features.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumours of the Urinary System and Male Genital Organs (5th edition, 2022) officially recognized ESC RCC as a standalone entity.2,3 ESC RCC predominantly affects female patients, with a median age at diagnosis of approximately 50–60 years. The tumor is typically unilateral and solitary, most commonly located in the cortex of the kidney. Most cases are discovered incidentally during imaging studies performed for unrelated reasons.4,5 Herein, we report a rare case of ESC RCC in a young female patient with comprehensive imaging, pathological, and molecular findings, and review the relevant literature.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old female was admitted to our hospital following an incidental finding of a left renal mass during a routine health examination one week prior. Physical examination revealed no significant abnormalities. Laboratory tests including complete blood count, liver and kidney function, and urinalysis were within normal limits. Serum tumor markers (AFP, CEA, CA19-9, SCC) were all negative.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen revealed a well-defined, round mass in the upper pole of the left kidney, measuring approximately 3.2 × 2.8 cm. The lesion demonstrated slightly lower density on plain scan compared to surrounding renal parenchyma, with mild heterogeneous enhancement after contrast administration (Fig. 1A and B). No lymphadenopathy or distant metastases were observed. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) showed the tumor was supplied by a branch of the left renal artery (Fig. 2).6 Chest CT and abdominal ultrasound revealed no other abnormalities.

CT angiography showing the tumor blood supply from a branch of the left renal artery (arrow).

After comprehensive preoperative evaluation and exclusion of surgical contraindications, the patient underwent successful laparoscopic partial nephrectomy under general anesthesia. The surgical procedure was uncomplicated, with an estimated blood loss of approximately 50 mL. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 with good recovery.

Macroscopically, the resected specimen measured 3.5 × 3.0 × 2.5 cm. The cut surface showed a grey-red, solid-cystic tumor with relatively clear margins. Histopathological examination revealed a tumor with mixed solid and cystic growth patterns. The solid areas displayed densely packed small nests or acinar structures, while the cystic areas were lined by single or pseudostratified epithelium. Tumor cells exhibited abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, round to oval nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli, and rare mitoses. Foamy macrophages and cholesterol clefts were observed in some areas. The tumor demonstrated clear boundaries with surrounding renal tissue. No renal pelvis invasion or vascular tumor thrombus was identified (Fig. 3A and B).1,7

(A) Hematoxylin-eosin staining (×40) showing small nests and acinar growth patterns. (B) Hematoxylin-eosin staining (×20) demonstrating cystic structures with hobnail cells.

Immunohistochemical staining revealed positive expression of CK7, EMA, AMACR, Vimentin, E-cadherin, and P504s. Carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9) and CD117 were negative. HMB45, Melan A, and TFE3 were also negative. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 2%.1,5,8

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis showed no mutations in the VHL gene, which distinguishes ESC RCC from clear cell renal cell carcinoma.9

Discussion

ESC RCC is a rare renal epithelial neoplasm first described by Trpkov et al., in 2015 and formally recognized by the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) in 2021.1 This tumor predominantly affects females, and cases in young female patients are particularly uncommon.4,7

The clinical presentation of ESC RCC is non-specific, with most cases discovered incidentally during imaging studies. Typical CT findings include a well-defined, solitary, round mass with clear margins and mild to moderate enhancement.6 However, these imaging features are not specific and may overlap with other renal tumors, making preoperative diagnosis challenging.

Histologically, ESC RCC is characterized by a combination of solid and cystic growth patterns. The tumor cells typically show abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm with fine granular inclusions, round to oval nuclei with inconspicuous or low-grade nucleoli, and rare mitoses.1,5 The immunohistochemical profile of ESC RCC shows consistent expression of CK7 and AMACR (P504s), while being negative for CA9 and CD117. This immunophenotype helps differentiate ESC RCC from clear cell RCC (CA9+), papillary RCC (CK7+, AMACR+, but without cystic component), and oncocytoma (CK7−, CD117+).8,10

The molecular pathogenesis of ESC RCC differs from that of clear cell RCC. Unlike clear cell RCC, which is characterized by VHL gene inactivation, ESC RCC typically lacks VHL gene mutations. Recent studies have identified TSC gene mutations or mTOR pathway activation in a subset of ESC RCC cases, suggesting alternative molecular mechanisms.9

ESC RCC should be differentiated from several other renal tumors. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma is characterized by clear cytoplasm rich in glycogen and lipid, positive for CA9, and commonly associated with VHL gene mutations. Papillary renal cell carcinoma shows predominant papillary architecture, positive for CK7 and AMACR, but lacks the typical cystic structures seen in ESC RCC. Renal oncocytoma is composed of oncocytic cells arranged in solid nests or tubules, positive for CD117 and negative for CK7. Chromophobe renal cell carcinoma features cells with voluminous cytoplasm and prominent cell membranes, positive for CK7 and CD117.2,3

The prognosis of ESC RCC is generally favorable. Most reported cases show no recurrence or metastasis during follow-up periods ranging from 6 months to 5 years.4,7,10 Complete surgical resection remains the treatment of choice. For localized tumors, partial nephrectomy can preserve renal function while achieving oncological control. Long-term follow-up with periodic imaging examinations is recommended due to the limited data on long-term outcomes.

Conclusions

ESC RCC is a rare and distinct renal epithelial neoplasm with unique clinicopathological features. This case contributes to the limited literature on ESC RCC in young female patients. The diagnosis relies on histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry, with the characteristic immunophenotype of CK7(+), CA9(−), and AMACR(+) being helpful for diagnosis. Complete surgical resection offers favorable outcomes. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed to better understand the biological behavior and optimal management of this rare entity.
