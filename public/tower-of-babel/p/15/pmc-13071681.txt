Central nervous system post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder relapse after pediatric liver transplantation: a case report and literature review
Authors: Cheng Wang, Yilin Wang, Yunjia Xu, Zicong Li, Jian Jiang, Lingzhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) represents a potentially life-threatening and grave complication that can arise following solid organ transplantation (SOT). Clinically, extranodal involvement in PTLD is frequent, whereas involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) is relatively rare and frequently associated with a poor prognosis. Currently, there is no standardized therapeutic approach for CNS-PTLD.Case DescriptionWe report a pediatric patient who suffered from multiple recurrences of CNS-PTLD after liver transplantation. The patient was Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-negative before transplantation but developed PTLD involving lymph node, liver, and multiple bones 2 years after liver transplantation, accompanied by elevated EBV-DNA in peripheral blood (PB). As the lesion was systemic and CD20-positive, we chose to use rituximab (RTX) monotherapy and achieved remission. However, after 3 cycles of RTX, the child developed neurological symptoms such as facial nerve palsy and mouth deviation. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a mass in the inner ear and thickening of the facial nerve, suggesting that the disease had invaded the CNS. Therefore, we upgraded the treatment plan to R-CHOP chemotherapy (rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone), and achieved a complete remission (CR). Following 2 cycles of R-CHOP, the patient experienced facial nerve palsy again, and brain MRI indicated a lesion in the left cerebellopontine angle region, suggesting disease recurrence. Re-treatment with 4 cycles of R-CHOP provided minimal symptomatic relief, and follow-up MRI demonstrated progressive multiple intracranial lesions. Brain biopsy confirmed CNS-PTLD [EBV-positive Burkitt’s lymphoma (BL)]. Due to the patient’s resistance to standard chemotherapy (R-CHOP) and the limited location of the lesion in the CNS, we switched to a more central-permeable treatment regimen. Then the patient achieved CR following treatment with high-dose methotrexate (HD-MTX) combined with intrathecal methotrexate (IT-MTX), and subsequently underwent timely chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy to consolidate the therapeutic effect and prevent recurrence. The patient currently maintains sustained CR.ConclusionsWe observe that the combination of HD-MTX and intrathecal chemotherapy exhibits remarkable efficacy in managing post-transplant CNS-PTLD that is resistant to conventional R-CHOP chemotherapy. CAR-T therapy emerges as a potential option for patients suffering from relapsed or refractory CNS-PTLD.Keywords: Central nervous system post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (CNS-PTLD), liver transplantation, children, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), case report

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study reports a pediatric case of recurrent central nervous system post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (CNS-PTLD) following liver transplantation, biopsy confirmed as Burkitt lymphoma. We successfully administered intravenous high-dose methotrexate (HD-MTX) and intrathecal MTX combined with chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, adjusting HD-MTX doses based on the response. The patient ultimately achieved complete remission and long-term survival.

What is known and what is new?

• CNS-PTLD is a rare but severe complication with poor prognosis following solid organ transplantation in children, and there is currently no standard treatment for CNS-PTLD.

• This case report highlights the significant efficacy of intravenous HD-MTX combined with intrathecal chemotherapy in treating CNS-PTLD; CAR-T therapy can be considered as a potential treatment option for relapsed/refractory CNS-PTLD, providing a new treatment direction for these critically ill patients.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• When neurological symptoms and signs occur in the children after transplantation, we should be alert to the occurrence of CNS-PTLD.

• CAR-T therapy represents a potential treatment option for refractory/relapsed CNS-PTLD cases.

Introduction

Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) is a severe complication following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) or solid organ transplantation (SOT), characterized by abnormal lymphoid cell proliferation. As the most common malignancy in pediatric transplant recipients, PTLD encompasses various pathological subtypes, among which Burkitt’s lymphoma (BL) is relatively rare but more aggressive (1,2). PTLD typically presents as a systemic disease, central nervous system (CNS) involvement is relatively rare, and primary CNS-PTLD (PCNS-PTLD) is an extremely rare and complex subtype of PTLD, accounting for 5% to 30% (3).

Pediatric patients with PTLD generally exhibit poor clinical outcomes. Studies indicate that the 5-year survival rate for children developing PTLD after liver transplantation ranges from 68% to 85%, with associated mortality rates of as high as 12–60% (4,5). The median survival of all patients with CNS-PTLD was 122 months, and the 5- and 10-year overall survival (OS) rates were 67.5% and 50.6%, respectively (6).

Children are at a significantly higher risk of developing PTLD compared to adults. Previous studies report an incidence rate of 5–20% for PTLD following pediatric liver transplantation (7). Notably, primary Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection during immunosuppression in EBV-seronegative recipients is a major risk factor for PTLD development (8). Evidence suggests that EBV-negative recipients have a 10- to 76-fold increased risk of PTLD compared to EBV-positive patients (9).

Currently, no standardized treatment protocol exists for CNS-PTLD, and patient outcomes vary considerably. Thus, developing individualized treatment strategies to optimize the balance between efficacy and safety is crucial. Although induction and consolidation therapies can achieve high response rates, some patients still progress to refractory or relapsed disease, posing substantial therapeutic challenges.

Here we report a case of pediatric liver transplantation recipient with multisystem PTLD who developed CNS relapse, aiming to provide new clinical insights for the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric CNS-PTLD. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-644/rc).

Case presentation

A male infant was diagnosed with type III biliary atresia at one month of age. At 8 years and 10 months, he underwent living-donor liver transplantation from his father due to decompensated cirrhosis accompanied by splenomegaly and esophageal varices. He was EBV-seronegative prior to transplantation. Immunosuppression following transplantation included tacrolimus, methylprednisolone (MP), and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). Steroids were successfully tapered and discontinued by 3 months post-operation. The patient maintained excellent graft function with no episodes of acute rejection throughout follow-up.

At 21 months post-transplantation, the child manifested sleep disturbances and exhibited markedly elevated EBV-DNA levels (1.469×106 copies/mL). Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed no abnormalities, but subsequent imaging uncovered widespread lymphadenopathy, hepatic nodules, and diffuse osseous metabolic activity, highly suggestive of PTLD (Figure 1A). Axillary lymph node biopsy definitively confirmed EBV-associated polymorphic PTLD (CD20+/EBER+/Bcl-2+ with 80% Ki67 proliferation index). Due to the positivity of CD20 and EBV, he was treated with ganciclovir, immunosuppression adjustment (tacrolimus + sirolimus + steroids), and three cycles of rituximab (RTX) (375 mg/m2), resulting in significant resolution of lesions on follow-up computed tomography/positron emission tomography-computed tomography (CT/PET-CT) and undetectable EBV-DNA (Figure 1B).

At 25 months post-transplantation, the child developed facial nerve palsy with mouth deviation. Laboratory tests showed elevated EBV-DNA (7.135×104 copies/mL) and cytomegalovirus (CMV) negative. MRI of the inner ear revealed an occupying lesion (longest diameter 0.88 cm) in the left internal auditory canal and signal changes in the left cochlea (Figure 1C). In light of progressive PTLD, he received 2 cycles of R-CHOP (rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) and 2 cycles of RTX alone (375 mg/m2). Subsequent PET-CT showed no obvious abnormal metabolic lesions with resolution of the previously noted subcapsular hepatic nodule (Figure 2A), and EBV-DNA became undetectable.

Three months after complete remission (CR), the child experienced recurrence of facial deviation. Brain MRI identified a lesion in the left cerebellopontine angle measuring approximately 15 mm × 12 mm × 12 mm (Figure 2B). PET-CT revealed a mildly hypermetabolic nodule in the same region, approximately 14 mm in diameter, along with multiple enlarged lymph nodes, suggestive of recurrent CNS-PTLD. The patient subsequently underwent 3 cycles of R-CHOP. Follow-up brain MRI demonstrated a reduction in the size of the space-occupying lesion in the left internal auditory canal by approximately 95% compared to prior imaging (Figure 2C), and EBV-DNA remained undetectable.

During the fourth cycle of R-CHOP, he experienced recurrence of aforementioned neurological symptoms. Brain MRI demonstrated multiple nodular lesions, the largest in the left cerebellopontine angle region (approx. 48 mm × 33 mm), showing and perilesional edema (Figure 3A). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis indicated a white blood cell count of 25.00×106/L, and cytospin smears showed a small number of atypical unclassified cells. Flow cytometry of CSF revealed approximately 16.15% of nucleated cells were CD45dimCD19+ cells expressing CD10, CD19, CD34, and CD38, suggestive of abnormal immature B lymphocytes. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) detected EBV DNA in the CSF despite undetectable serum EBV DNA. To reduce tumor burden, R-CHOP was continued, and intrathecal chemotherapy was administered [cytarabine (Ara-C) 30 mg + MTX 15 mg + dexamethasone (Dex) 4 mg]. Follow-up brain MRI showed persistent abnormal signals with the largest lesion located in the corpus callosum (37 mm × 33 mm × 30 mm), indicating a suboptimal response (Figure 3B).

A stereotactic brain biopsy was performed under general anesthesia. Pathology confirmed EBV-positive BL with the following immunohistochemical profile: CD2 (+), CD3 (+), CD20 (+), CD10 (+), CD30 (−), CD5 (+), CD19 (+), Bcl-6 (+), Bcl-2 (−), Ki-67 (~95%), p53 (wild-type expression pattern), c-myc (95%+), Epstein-Barr virus encoded RNA’s (EBER) (+) (Figure 4). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) showed BCL2 (−), MYC (+). Combined with imaging, CSF findings, and pathology, the final diagnosis was CNS-PTLD (BL, EBV+). Since the lesion was located in the CNS, we opted for a high-dose methotrexate (HD-MTX) regimen with stronger CNS penetration. The child received 4 cycles of RTX plus HD-MTX combined with intrathecal MTX injection for the treatment of CNS-PTLD. Based on the patient’s good organ function and the observed partial radiological improvement, the dose of HD-MTX was gradually increased from 3 to 5 g/m2. The child’s follow-up contrast-enhanced brain MRI showed remaining residual lesions (Figure 5). In order to consolidate the therapeutic effect and prevent recurrence, the child subsequently underwent autologous chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) cell infusion (CD19/22/20 targeting, cell dose 7.02×106/kg) with intrathecal administration of autologous CAR-T cells (7×105/kg). On day 8, an additional infusion of cryopreserved autologous CAR-T cells (2.55×106/kg) was administered, followed by “feeder cell” infusion on day 16, both without complications. The patient’s hearing partially recovered. Follow-up brain MRI showed near-complete resolution of intracranial lesions (Figure 6A), and PET-CT confirmed no abnormal metabolic activity (Figure 6B). The patient is currently under regular follow-up. So far, this patient has maintained a state of event-free survival (EFS) and no disease recurrence or long-term adverse reactions related to CAR-T therapy have been observed. Discussion

CNS involvement occurs in approximately 4–16% of pediatric PTLD cases following liver transplantation (10,11). The recurrence rate of PTLD is less than 10%, with children maintaining long-term elevated EBV loads being at increased risk of both new and recurrent PTLD (1). CNS-PTLD is positive for EBV infection in up to 70% of cases (12). A study on PCNS-PTLD reported that late recurrences of EBV-positive PTLD are common, with 40% of cases diagnosed 6 or more years after transplantation (13). Since EBV DNA may not be detectable in peripheral blood (PB) in PCNS-PTLD, CSF analysis for EBV becomes critically important and serves as a valuable predictor of CNS involvement. In our case, elevated EBV loads were detected in PB and CSF at the time of recurrence. However, PTLD is not always associated with positive CSF findings, and EBV may not be detected in either PB or CSF (10,14), which complicates early detection. These observations underscore that even in the absence of detectable EBV-DNA in PB and/or CSF, a high suspicion for CNS-PTLD should be maintained—especially when multiple risk factors for EBV reactivation are present alongside CNS-related symptoms.

Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosing CNS-PTLD, with the majority of cases originating from the B-cell lineage. In CNS-PTLD cases, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a more common pathological subtype, while BL is relatively uncommon (6,10). Consistent with this, a Taiwanese retrospective study of 16 pediatric PTLD patients after liver transplantation identified only two cases of BL, only one of which involved the CNS (4).

We conducted a review of domestic and international databases for case reports published in the past decade and identified six articles reporting CNS-PTLD in children, encompassing a total of seven cases (Table 1). Among these, three occurred after liver transplantation, four were early-onset (≤1 year post-transplant), only one was pathologically confirmed as BL, one case experienced recurrence, and the overall mortality was approximately 29%. The patient in our report developed CNS-PTLD 21 months after liver transplantation, which is consistent with late-onset disease.

Current management of PTLD includes antiviral therapy (AT), reduction or withdrawal of immunosuppression, anti-B cell monoclonal antibody therapy, EBV-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (EBV-CTL), CAR-T cell therapy, radiotherapy, and local surgical resection. Notably, calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) have been identified as exerting a protective effect against the development of CNS-PTLD (12). However, reduction or discontinuation of immunosuppression alone is often insufficient to achieve remission in CNS-PTLD, necessitating more aggressive treatment strategies. HD-MTX, capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB), represents a key therapeutic option for CNS-PTLD (15). However, due to its potential hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, close monitoring is essential—particularly in pediatric liver transplant recipients—to prevent graft injury. Albusoul et al. successfully treated an adult kidney transplant patient with CNS-PTLD using reduced-dose HD-MTX (16). Additionally, Twist and Semkiu et al. have reported favorable outcomes with intrathecal methotrexate (IT-MTX) in cases of CNS-PTLD (17,18). In our case, HD-MTX was initiated at 3 g/m2 and carefully escalated to 5 g/m2 under close safety surveillance, with good tolerance observed. These collective findings suggest that reduced-dose HD-MTX is a valuable therapeutic option for PTLD patients post-liver transplantation, though further studies are needed to establish the optimal dosing for both safety and efficacy.

Most cases of CNS-PTLD express CD20, and RTX is widely utilized in the treatment of PTLD. For the majority of CD20+/EBV + PTLD cases, RTX-based regimens, including RTX monotherapy, sequential therapy, or combination with chemotherapy, are recognized as standard treatment approaches (19). However, a major limitation of RTX is its low permeability to the BBB, which limits its drug activity in the CNS and CSF. Nevertheless, multiple studies support the efficacy of RTX in the treatment of CNS-PTLD (20,21). Additionally, several case reports have indicated that intrathecal RTX (IT-RTX) is a feasible treatment strategy for pediatric CNS-PTLD patients who experience failure of first-line therapy (22,23). Schultze-Florey et al. reported a case of pediatric CNS-PTLD in which the patient achieved CR following comprehensive treatment with intravenous and IT-RTX combined with EBV-CTLs (24). However, further clinical research is needed to confirm its safety and efficacy. In our case, the child developed CNS involvement but was not treated with IT-RTX due to CD20 negativity in CSF and predominant parenchymal (rather than leptomeningeal) disease localization.

CAR-T therapy has demonstrated significant efficacy in the treatment of resistant/refractory PTLD (25,26), but its use in CNS-PTLD is limited. Studies suggest that the combination of radiotherapy and CAR-T therapy increase the remission rate in refractory/relapsed CNS-BL patients (27). Additionally, CAR-T therapy for CNS lymphoma is safe and feasible (28,29). Our patient received adjunctive CAR-T therapy and achieved CR. However, broader clinical trials are warranted to evaluate and mitigate risks associated with CAR-T therapy in CNS-PTLD.

New treatment methods are constantly being proposed, bringing more hope to patients. Zhao et al. proposed that chemotherapy regimens such as CP (cyclophosphamide, prednisone) and/or modified EPOCH (prednisone, etoposide, doxorubicin, vincristine, cyclophosphamide plus RTX) are effective in treating aggressive mature B-cell lymphoma after liver transplantation (30). Ibrutinib is capable of penetrating the CNS, and adding BTK inhibitors to first-line treatment for CNS-PTLD is feasible (31,32).

CNS involvement is a recognized poor prognostic factor in PTLD, with PCNS-PTLD carrying an especially unfavorable outlook (33,34). Tajima et al.’s study on 45 children with PTLD after liver transplantation showed that the OS rates at 3, 5, and 10 years were 81%, 79%, and 71%, respectively (5). Separately, Taj et al. conducted a study focusing on pediatric patients with PCNS-PTLD, and their findings showed that 4-year EFS and OS rates are of 50±10% and 74±9%, respectively (11). With the advent of preemptive therapy and various therapeutic measures, the prognosis of CNS-PTLD has significantly improved, but the mortality rate remains high. Finding biomarkers that can effectively evaluate prognosis is crucial. Research has shown that soluble CD95 ligand (sCD95L) is a potential prognostic biomarker for CND-PTLD, which may provide new evidence for risk stratification and clinical management of CNS-PTLD (35).

Conclusions

In summary, CNS-PTLD represents a serious complication in pediatric patients following SOT. Early and accurate diagnosis and intervention are crucial for improving outcomes. We emphasize the importance of long-term surveillance in transplanted pediatric patients to enable timely detection of PTLD occurrence and recurrence. Notably, when conventional R-CHOP proves ineffective for CNS-PTLD, HD-MTX combined with intrathecal chemotherapy demonstrates substantial efficacy. Additionally, CAR-T therapy represents a promising modality for patients with relapsed or refractory CNS-PTLD.
