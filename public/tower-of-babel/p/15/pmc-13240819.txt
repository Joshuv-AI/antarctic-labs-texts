Modulating hawthorn pectin properties: Effects of fruit storage and post-extraction enzymatic treatment
Authors: Xueyan Wei, Mengxin Chen, Zhengtao Zhao, Kang Xu, Li Zhao, Xueping Liu, Dongxiao Sun-Waterhouse, Mengmeng Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of fruits storage time and an enzymatic treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase post pectin extraction on the structural, physicochemical, emulsifying, gelling, antioxidant, α-amylase/pancreatic lipase inhibitory, and prebiotic properties of hawthorn pectin (HP). Increasing fruit storage duration decreased molecular weight (Mw), degree of methylesterification (DM), antioxidant, α-amylase inhibitory or pancreatic lipase inhibitory activities of HP, but increased its prebiotic potential on two Lactobacillus casei strains. Enzymatic treatment post extraction decreased Mw, DM (from 68 to 74% to 39–48%) and average size of rhamnogalacturonan-I (RG-I) side chains, increased galacturonic acid (GalA), homogalacturonan and RG-I contents, antioxidant, α-amylase/pancreatic lipase inhibitory and prebiotic potential. Strong correlations exist among HP's Mw, DM, GalA content, and key functional properties. These results show the multifunctional potential of HP, importance of postharvest storage according to fruit's biochemical and physiological changes, and feasibility of modifying HP via an enzymatic approach.Keywords: Hawthorn pectin, Fruits storage time, Enzymes-assisted extraction, Methylesterification, Physiological properties, Functional properties

Introduction

Pectins are complex heteropolysaccharides extracted mainly from the cell walls and middle lamella of plants, and possess diverse processing properties (e.g., gelling, stabilizing and emulsifying functions) and health functionalities (e.g., prebiotic, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects) (Dou, Tian, Hong, et al., 2025; Niu et al., 2025; Zhang, He, et al., 2021). Usually, they are composed of an α-1,4-D-galacturonic acid backbone, and may contain as many as 17 different monosaccharides and over 20 types of interlinkages. The main building blocks of pectins include homogalacturonan (HG), rhamnogalacturonan I (RG-I), and rhamnogalacturonan II (RG-II), whilst small amounts of xylogalacturonan and apiogalacturonan segments may occur (Li et al., 2021). Based on the degree of methylesterification (DM), pectins can be divided into high methoxy pectins (HMP: DM > 50%) and low methoxy pectins (LMP: DM < 50%). DM significantly influences pectin's total distribution of charge along the backbone, magnitude of local charge, amphiphilic feature, molecular packing density, thereby affecting pectin's interactions with water, lipids, proteins, polyphenols and other carbohydrates, eventually the functional properties of pectin-containing systems. Besides the DM, variables such as monosaccharide composition, molecular weight (Mw), characteristics of co-existing proteins and phenolic compounds, proportion of HG, RG-I and RG-II domains, presence of xylogalacturonan or apiogalacturonan segment(s), degrees of acetylation, amidation, branching and polymerization, and polymeric chain arrangements, all influence the properties of pectins (Zhang, Waterhouse, et al., 2021).

Hawthorn fruit is rich in high-methoxyl pectins (10–16% w/w, dry basis), with the content of galacturonic acid (GalA) ranging from 68% to 90%. Compared with commercial apple or citrus pectin, hawthorn pectin (HP) was found to exhibit higher viscosity and gelling ability, better emulsifying and stabilizing properties probably due to its longer HG domain and shorter RG-I domain (Li et al., 2021). The amount and structure of HP are highly influenced by fruit maturity and storage time, as well as the extraction process. During storage (accompanied with post-harvest ripening), multiple endogenous enzymes like pectin methylesterase (PME), pectin acetylesterases, polygalacturonase (PG), and pectinolytic lyases (PL) are involved in the modification via reorganization, degradation, hydrolysis and β-elimination of pectins, causing the dissolution of the middle lamellae and rupture of cell walls along with a decrease in the yield of pectins and changes in their structures (Zheng et al., 2021). In addition, hawthorn fruit contains considerable starch, which could be co-extracted during the extraction. Roman et al. (2021) reported that hot-acid extraction followed by enzymatic starch removal remarkably increased the purity of hawthorn pectin, accompanied with a notable decrease in DM. This occurrence was possibly related to the prolonged aqueous thermal incubation during starch hydrolysis. The structural alteration of pectin is bound to lead to the diversity of its function, which induces that many modification methods relying on natural processes (e.g., maturity and post-harvest ripening) are being applied as greener approaches for producing high-quality pectins with specific properties.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that postharvest storage and post-extraction enzymatic purification may regulate HP properties through endogenous and exogenous enzymes. Therefore, the effects of pretreatment with α-amylase and amyloglucosidase, as well as the impact of fruit storage duration, on the physicochemical characteristics, structural features and functionalities (including antioxidant, gelling and emulsifying capacities, α-amylase and pancreatic lipase inhibitory effects, and in vivo prebiotic properties) of the pectins extracted from hawthorn were investigated. This work aims to provide insights into the structure-function relationship of HP as affected by fruit storage duration and the enzymatic pretreatment. The obtained results can guide the selection of hawthorn fruit at an appropriate stage for the enzyme-assisted extraction of pectins with specific structure and properties.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Fresh hawthorn fruits (Dajinxing cultivar, DA) were manually and randomly collected in the 20th week after full bloom in the Central Shandong Mountains, China (36°04′ N – 36°37′ N, 118°14′ E - 118°49′ E), and supplied by Yijia Food Co. (Shandong Province, China). All the collected fresh hawthorn fruits were stored in a controlled warehouse at 0-4 °C and a relative humidity of 85%, then, subsamples after being stored for different durations (at the 0 d, 30th d, 60th d and 90th d, respectively) were withdrawn for fruit physicochemical analyses and preparation of HP.

Amyloglucosidase from Aspergillus niger (activity: 3300 U/mL) was obtained from Megazyme (Megazyme Ltd. Wicklow, Ireland). Type VI-B α-amylase from porcine pancreas (activity: 10 U/mg), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid, ABTS), D-galacturonic acid (GalA), monosaccharides including L-rhamnose (Rha), L-arabinose (Ara), D-xylose (Xyl), D-mannose (Man), D-galactose (Gal) and d-glucose (Glc) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, Missouri). P-82 pullulans standards were purchased from Showa corporation (Denko KK, Tokyo, Japan). Pancreatic lipase (activity: 30 U/mg), α-amylase (activity:10 U/mg) and p-nitrophenyl butyrate (pNPB) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents were of analytical or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade.

Preparation of hawthorn fruit powders and pectin

The detailed procedure for hawthorn fruit powder preparation and pectin extraction (Fig. 1) was established following the method of Roman et al. (2021).

Flow chart of extraction and purification of hawthorn pectin.

Chemical analyses

Protein content was measured by the method of Bradford (1976). Total phenolic content was evaluated by the Folin-Ciocalteu assay (Xu et al., 2020). Proximal composition was determined and expressed as weight percentage on dry basis. Profiling of monosaccharides was performed by ionic chromatography, following the method as described by Du et al. (2024). DM was determined using the reported methods of Roman et al. (2021). Mw distribution of HP was measured following the method of Xu et al. (2020).

Optical and thermal properties analysis

HPs were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Sigma 300, ZEISS, Germany) under a magnification of 500 ×. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of dried HPs was carried out using a FTIR spectrometer (Spectrum BX II, Perkin Elmer Co., MA, USA) in a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1, with 32 scans and at a resolution of 4 cm−1 by the KBr pellet method (Dou, Tian, Wang, et al., 2025). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of HPs were recorded at 25 °C and a scanning rate of 5°/min using an X-ray Diffractometer (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany). Tube current, operating target voltage and diffraction angle (2 θ) range were 40 mA, 40 kV, and 4–60°, respectively. Thermal gravimetric (TG) analysis of dried HPs was carried out using a thermogravimetric analyzer (DTG 60 A, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Detailed procedure and parameters followed those used by Wei et al. (2024).

Emulsifying activity (EA) and emulsion stability (ES)

EA and ES were followed the methods of Roman et al. (2021). Optical microscopy examinations of HP emulsions (20 μL) were performed at 25 °C using an IX73 fluorescence inversion microscope system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Gelling properties

Preparation of pectin-rich hydrogels

Pectin-rich hydrogels (high soluble solid/sugar gel) prepared with CHP followed fully the method of Wei et al. (2024). As for the EHP, 0.05 g calcium chloride was required to be added together with sucrose to form a hydrogel (LMP gel) (Löfgren et al., 2002), with the remaining steps being the same as those for CHP gel-making.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

TPA was performed using a TA-XT2i Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Co., UK) equipped with a flat cylindrical probe (P/0.5 R). TPA parameters were as follows: compression distance 3.0 mm, trigger force 5 g, test speed 1.0 mm/s, duration of 30 s between two compression cycles (Wei et al., 2024).

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

Water migration of gels was examined at 25 °C by the transverse relaxation time (T2) assays using LF-NMR (Niumag Electronics Technology, Shanghai, China) equipped with a 60 mm probe. The relaxation curves were acquired using a Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill pulse sequence. Data from 2000 echoes were obtained by sixteen-scanned repetitions. The repetition time between two successive scans was 1 s. An aliquots (1.0 g) of each of the HP gels was placed into a 1.5 mL LF-NMR tube, which was then sealed with parafilm to avoid moisture loss during the test.

Evaluation of bioactivities

Antioxidant capacity and inhibitory activities against α-amylase or pancreatic lipase

DPPH radical scavenging activity (DPPH-RSA) and ABTS radical scavenging activity (ABTS-RSA) were determined to evaluate the antioxidant activities of HPs (Xu et al., 2020). The α-amylase inhibitory activity was determined using the method of Wei et al. (2024). Pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity was measured according to the method of Wei et al. (2024).

Prebiotic activity

Two strains of Lactobacillus casei (LC6009 and LC6117) were used to evaluate the prebiotic potential of HPs, according to the method of Dou, Tian, Zhang, et al. (2025). Carbohydrate-free base medium (0.2 g/L peptone, 0.2 g/L yeast extract, 2 g/L triammonium citrate, 5 g/L CH3COONa, 0.2 g/L MgSO4•7H2O, 0.05 g/L MnSO4•4H2O, 2 g/L K2HPO4, 1.08 g/L Tween 80, pH = 5.9) was used as the blank for lactic acid bacteria, whilst the carbohydrate-free base medium supplemented with fructooligosaccharide (FOS) was used as a positive group. Both FOS and an HP were added to the carbohydrate-free base medium to generate a final carbohydrate content of 10 g/L. Prior to each assay, the two Lactobacillus casei strains were activated in the MRS medium at 37 °C for 24 h. The seed culture with an inoculum size of 2% (v/v) was transferred into a 20 mL centrifuge tube containing 5 mL of basal medium. Then, the cultures were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 12 h. The growth of probiotic strains was assessed by measuring the bacterial populations in the medium after incubation for 6 and 12 h, which were detected by the plate counting method. The bacteria count was presented as colony forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL).

Statistical analysis

Experimental tests were independently conducted in triplicate. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Before one-way ANOVA, the normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance were checked using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Differences among the data were examined using Fisher's least significant difference (LSD) test at the level of p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Yields, chemical compositions and molecular properties of HPs

Table 1 summarizes the yields, chemical compositions and molecular properties of the pectins extracted from hawthorn fruits stored for different time durations in the absence and presence of an enzymatic treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase. For the same type of pectin (CHP or EHP), the pectin yield decreased significantly with an extension of fruit storage time, due to the continuous hydrolysis/degradation of pectins catalyzed by the endogenous enzymes of hawthorn fruits during fruit storage (Zheng et al., 2021). The protein contents in all the HPs ranged from 3.03% to 4.47%, which is in agreement with the results of our previous study on HP (Wei et al., 2024). EHP had a higher protein content than CHP, probably due to the removal of starch by amylase and amyloglucosidase and the retention of these residual exogenous enzymes added for treatment after pectin extraction in the EHP. Both CHP and EHP samples contained some phenolics, with the total phenolic contents of the EHPs being twice as high as those of CHPs subjected to the same fruit storage duration. This result is probably related to the specific method we used (the Folin-Ciocalteu assay could detect both phenolics and protein contents due to its extensive range). EHP treated by amylase and amyloglucosidase increased the measurable values. For the same type of HP samples (CHP or EHP), the total phenolic contents showed a slight downward trend as the fruit storage proceeded further, which accounted for the general decreasing trends of their antioxidant capacities (as DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging activities) (Table 2). Phenolic compounds could attach to pectic polysaccharides or protein molecules, or even form phenolic-pectin-protein complexes during extraction (Zhang et al., 2023), thus, more phenolics might be retained in the extracted EHPs. Also, the removal of starch during the treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase after pectin extraction would increase the proportion of the phenolic contents in the extracted EHPs.

Seven monosaccharides, GalA, Rha, Ara, Xyl, Man, Gal and Glc, were found in both CHP and EHP samples, which is in agreement with the previous finding related HPs. GalA as a main structural unit of the backbone of galacturonans was one of the main monosaccharides in the HPs of this study, ranging from 53.01 mol% (CHP-0) to 70.30 mol% (CHP-90), and from 85.68 mol% (EHP-0) to 86.67 mol% (EHP-90). Compared with the CHP samples, the much higher GalA contents in the EHPs also resulted largely from the removal of starch components by amylase and amyloglucosidase after pectin extraction. The remarkably lower Glc contents in EHPs (from 2.68 mol% (EHP-0) to 1.98 mol% (EHP-90), compared with those of the CHP samples (from 37.03 mol% (CHP-0) to 20.07 mol% (CHP-90), further confirmed the effective removal of carbohydrates like starch from the HPs by amylase and amyloglucosidase. In terms of other monosaccharides, Rha, Ara, Xyl, Man and Gal, their contents (< 4.3 mol%) were far lower than GalA and Glc in the CHPs, with the CHPs having slightly lower contents than the EHPs. The detected Rha content indicates the presence of rhamnogalacturonan I (RG-I) and/or RG-II fractions in pectin molecules. Ara and Gal are the main monosaccharides for the side chains of pectins, and Xyl might come from xylogalacturonans (XGs) and RG-II's side chains (Zhang et al., 2023).

As for the HG and RG-I contents, the EHPs had much higher values than the corresponding CHPs from the fruits stored for the same periods, and the EHPs had a much higher proportion of linear HG units (83–85 mol%) compared with the CHPs (51–69 mol%). Rha/GalA was the main contributor to the RG-I domain, and the molar ratio of (Ara + Gal)/Rha indicative of the degree of branching of RG-I segments (Reichembach et al., 2024). As shown in Table 1, both CHP and EHP samples had Rha/GalA molar ratios within the range of 0.02–0.03, indicating the presence of some individual RG-I domains in these pectins' structures (Du et al., 2023). The molar ratios of (Gal + Ara)/Rha reflected the average size of neutral side chains (branching degree) of the RG-I region in a pectin (Du et al., 2023). Thus, the CHPs had a longer pectin side chain in RG-I regions (from 4.01 to 4.33) than the EHPs (from 2.48 to 3.51), with both CHP and EHP samples showing a small increase in the average size of neutral side chains as the storage duration of hawthorn fruits increased.

As shown in Table 1, the DM values obtained by the titration method or through FTIR analysis revealed that the CHPs were HMP (DM: 68–74%), whilst the EHPs were LMP (DM: 39–48%), indicating that the post-extraction treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase was associated with a significant decrease in DM. This finding was consistent with our previous research (Roman et al., 2021), in which a similar decrease in DM was observed after enzymatic purification. This phenomenon could be related to the prolonged times at high temperatures occurring β-eliminative de-polymerization during starch hydrolysis (Diaz et al., 2007). Furthermore, trace enzyme activities such as PME might participate in the pectin demethylation (Frommhagen et al., 2025).

The Mw of HP samples decreased gradually with an extension of fruit storage time i.e. CHPs (from 222.9 kDa to 158.2 kDa) or EHPs (from 218.5 kDa to 142.3 kDa). The Mw values in this study were in the range of 111.7–295.2 kDa, which was the Mw range of hawthorn fruits' pectic polysaccharides. As discussed earlier, decrease of Mw over fruit storage typically occurs in most fruits due to actions of multiple endogenous pectin-degrading enzymes like PME, pectin acetylesterases, PG and PL (Zheng et al., 2021). Comparison of the Mw values between the CHP and amyloglucosidase after pectin extraction. Such small differences in Mw were associated with the reduction of DM after the enzymatic treatment, and indicate that amylase and amyloglucosidase did not cause large changes in pectins e.g. did not induce depolymerization of the main chains of the pectins (Vivar-Vera et al., 2007). The de-methoxylation effect on pectins, which occurred during the starch removal induced by enzymatic treatment might take place in parallel with acid hydrolysis (i.e., acid-initiated cleavage of RG-I and/or HG backbone), and/or β-elimination of the HG backbone, resulting in pectins with lower DM and smaller Mw (Einhorn-Stoll et al., 2019).

SEM examination of HPs

Supplementary Fig. 1 showed some morphological and microstructural features of CHPs and EHPs. The CHP-0 sample appeared like big pieces of irregular, thin and “foldable” flakes with smooth, continuous and slightly curling surfaces. As the fruit storage time extended, the degrees of fragmentation, disorganization and surface unevenness of the CHPs increased. Such changes of pectins' morphology and microstructure indicate the increase in the extent of pectin degradation with storage duration (Lin et al., 2024). In comparison, the EHPs appeared as “leaf-like” fragments with much smaller and less continuous surfaces and increasingly irregular edges, which resulted from the removal of starch from the pectin sample matrix via the enzymatic treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase. Their degrees of fragmentation, disorganization, and surface unevenness, curling and narrowness increased with an elevated storage duration. Similar phenomena were previously observed when an enzymatic treatment was used for purifying citrus pectin (Einhorn-Stoll et al., 2019).

XRD, FTIR and TG analyses

Fig. 2A and B showed the very similar XRD patterns of CHPs and EHPs, which conform to pectin's typical characteristics as semicrystalline-amorphous polymer materials that would facilitate the hydration process and stabilization of pectin's hydrogel network structure (Naqash et al., 2021). All these HPs exhibited a broad characteristic diffraction peak around 21.5° (2θ), indicating the occurrence of some crystalline domains. The CHPs did not show a significant difference in their XRD patterns indicate the minimal effect of storage time on the crystallinity of the HPs. A similar crystalline region was found for citrus pectin (Du et al., 2025), apple/grape pectins (Xu et al., 2022). While the highly similar XRD patterns of the CHP and EHP samples suggest the enzymatic treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase did not cause major changes in the nature of the pectin samples, compared with the CHPs, the characteristic diffraction peak of the EHPs did exhibit a small shift towards lower angle, indicating a small increase in interplanar spacing caused by changes in the pectin sample (e.g. change in pectin sample's composition due to starch removal by the enzymatic treatment).

XRD (A and B), FTIR (C and D) and TG (E and F) analysis results of hawthorn pectins. CHP and EHP represent hawthorn pectins extracted without and with enzymolysis, and the number following CHP or EHP indicates the storage duration (d) of hawthorn fruits.

FTIR analysis was performed to characterize the changes in functional groups of CHP and EHP samples. As shown in Fig. 2C and D, all HP samples exhibited typical pectin spectra, with characteristic bands at around 3350 cm−1 for O—H stretching, 2900 cm−1 for C—H stretching of methyl or methylene groups, 1740 cm−1 for esterified carbonyl groups, 1630 cm−1 for free carboxyl groups, 1230 cm−1 for C-O-C stretching related to esterification, 1140 cm−1 for glycosidic C-O-C vibrations, and 1105–1020 cm−1 for pyranose ring vibrations of pectin side chains (Xu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). The intensity ratio of the band at 1740 cm−1 to the combined intensities of 1740 and 1630 cm−1 can be used to estimate the DM of pectin (Xu et al., 2022). The region from 1250 to 800 cm−1 represents the carbohydrate fingerprint region, reflecting glycosidic linkages and sugar ring vibrations (Du et al., 2023). With increasing fruit storage time, the intensities of major pectin-related bands decreased, indicating pectin degradation, hydrolysis and structural reorganization during storage (Goulao & Oliveira, 2008; Zheng et al., 2021). The post-extraction enzymatic treatment also changed the FTIR profiles of HPs, especially by reducing the intensities of the bands at 3350, 2900 and 1010 cm−1, which may be related to a combination of starch removal, prolonged warm incubation, and possibly trace side activities; it is not a direct enzymatic demethylation by the two enzymes used. In addition, the altered relative intensities of the bands at 1740 and 1630 cm−1 were consistent with the observed changes in DM values.

TG analyses were used to examine the thermal stability of the HPs. Fig. 2E and F showed that in general, the CHP and EHP samples showed similar thermal-induced weight loss behaviors within the analyzed temperature range (30–600 °C), although the effect of fruit storage duration on such thermal-induced behavior on the CHP differed from that on the HMP, e.g. such an effect became much smaller on HMP than on CHP in the last stage at 350–600 °C. These findings suggest that fruit storage duration affected significantly HP's thermal behaviors, and the enzymatic treatment post pectin extraction could reduce such storage-induced differences in thermal responsive behaviors. In the first stage (30–230 °C), the sample weight loss was initially small then almost levelled off, which was mainly due to the rapid evaporation of free/adsorbed water in the voids of the pectin polysaccharide chain along with some volatiles (Hu et al., 2021). In the second stage (230–350 °C), the pectin samples underwent the fastest and greatest weight loss (more than 60% of their initial weight), possibly due to the degradation and decomposition of pectins (including decarboxylation and decomposition of their backbones) and other polymeric substances, along with gas release (Zhang et al., 2023). In the third stage (350–600 °C), weight loss continued at a much lower rate as a result of thermal decomposition of char (Hu et al., 2021). In the third stage, the reduced differences in high heat-induced weight loss among the EHPs caused by the enzymatic treatment suggest that the inconsistent high heat-induced weight loss might be associated with the starch/starch-like substances removed by the enzymatic treatment. The mass percentages of the final residues ranged from 20% to 30%.

Emulsifying properties of HPs

In general, an emulsion is considered to be kinetically stable when the Brownian motion-induced diffusion of small oil droplets is greater than the effects of creaming and gravity (which effectively hinders the sedimentation, aggregation, flocculation and coalescence of droplets in the emulsion (Zhang et al., 2021b). To explore the emulsifying properties of the HPs, their emulsifying activity (EA) and emulsion stability (ES) were determined, and the storage stability of the HP-emulsions was evaluated over 30 days. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, the CHP samples and the EHP samples had rather comparable EA and ES values (34.5–40.4% and 35.9–38.1%, respectively). The EA values of CHPs gradually decreased as the fruit storage time increased, whilst the EA values of EHPs remained relatively constant especially when less than 60 days. Similarly, much smaller changes for the EHPs than for the CHPs were also observed in ES values. These phenomena were likely associated with the removed starch/starch-like substances in the HPs through the enzymatic treatment with after pectin extraction. Overall, an HP sample might offer better emulsifying properties when the hawthorn fruit under no storage or 30-day storage before pectin extraction, and an extra enzymatic treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase post pectin extraction might provide a more consistent emulsification outcome.

Emulsifying activity (A) and emulsion stability (B) of hawthorn pectins, and microscopic images of CHP (C) and EHP (D) emulsions for a 30-day storage at 4 °C. CHP and EHP represent hawthorn pectins extracted without and with enzymolysis, and the number following CHP or EHP indicates the storage duration (d) of hawthorn fruits.

Fig. 3C and D are the microscopic images of the CHP emulsions and the EHP emulsions, respectively, with/without a 30-day storage at 4 °C. For the freshly prepared CHP emulsion (in the absence of emulsion heating and storage) (first row in Fig. 3C), the droplets in these emulsions had relatively small sizes and were relatively stable (although some large droplets appeared in the CHP-90 emulsion, which tended to become unstable), indicating that the repulsive electrostatic interactions among the droplets in the emulsion were adequate to offset the attractive interactions to prevent flocculation or aggregation (Tavasoli et al., 2022). The droplet size of these emulsions became much bigger after the emulsions were stored for 30 days at 4 °C (second row in Fig. 3C), probably due to gradual weakening of pectin's adsorption on the oil-water interface and subsequent reduction of electrostatic repulsion or steric hindrance between droplets, thereby making droplets more likely to aggregate and coalesce (Jia et al., 2024). The characteristics of the droplets in the EHP emulsions are shown in Fig. 3D. Comparisons between the corresponding CHP and EHP samples (i.e. same fruit storage duration and same emulsion storage duration) revealed that the droplets of EHP-0 and EHP-30 emulsions were generally more spherical and finer with a denser and more uniform distribution than those of the corresponding CHP-0 and CHP-30 samples. For the same EHP emulsion, the droplets generally became bigger (except for the EHP-30 sample) after the emulsions were stored for 30 days at 4 °C. These phenomena indicated the effects of fruit storage time before pectin extraction, the enzymatic treatment post pectin extraction and emulsion storage on droplet stability of an HP emulsion (Du et al., 2024).

Some characteristics of HP hydrogels

Textural characteristics

Supplementary Table 2 summarizes the textural characteristics of different high soluble solid (sugar) pectin-rich hydrogels. The CHP gels had higher firmness, gumminess and chewiness but very similar springiness and cohesiveness compared with the corresponding EHP gels (i.e. the same fruit storage duration).For the same type of pectin, the firmness, cohesiveness, gumminess and chewiness exhibited different extents of reduction whilst the springiness remained statistically unchanged, as the fruit storage time extended.). The values of springiness and cohesiveness exhibited insignificant or minimal differences among these different HP gels, suggesting that these two textural features were not influenced by fruit storage duration and the absence/presence of the additional enzymatic treatment post pectin extraction. The changing trends of gumminess and chewiness resembled that of firmness, indicating that the overall gel strength was mainly governed by the structural changes of pectin molecules and the resulting network interactions. Additionally, prolonged fruit storage led to a decrease in Mw and the proportion of RG-I region, which would weaken molecular entanglement, thus the decrease in firmness, gumminess and chewiness of both CHP and EHP gels. The alterations in gel texture could therefore be attributed to changes in the strengths of hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions and, for LMP systems, ionic cross-linking in the pectin network (Gallery et al., 2024).

It is worthy to note that the CHPs were HMPs, with DM values of 68–74% (Table 1), and could form gel structures under acidic conditions with a high soluble solid content. The formation of HMP gels mainly depends on hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between pectin molecular chains, which support the development of a three-dimensional network structure. Besides, the gelatinized starch in CHPs after thermal process provides fortified strength of gels as well. By contrast, the EHPs were LMPs, with DM values of 39–48% (Table 1). Therefore, in the absence of sufficient divalent metal cations such as calcium ions, LMPs generally exhibit weaker gel-forming capacity (Han et al., 2017). Accordingly, it is not surprising that the CHP-0 gel had higher firmness, gumminess and chewiness than the EHP-0 gel.

The fact that the other CHP gels also had higher firmness, gumminess and chewiness than their corresponding EHP gels suggests that the effect of fruit storage duration could not offset the generally higher gel strength of HMPs compared with LMPs. In addition, the co-extracted starch in the CHPs might have provided an auxiliary gel effect (Chen et al., 2023). Higher-Mw pectins are generally more effective in promoting gelation because they can more readily form network structures (Costa et al., 2022). This structural feature could increase space occupancy and shorten the distance between pectin chains, thereby promoting chain-chain interactions and inter-chain entanglements, eventually resulting in a compact and stable gel network (Zheng et al., 2020).

Taken together, the underlying mechanisms of textural properties between CHP and EHP gels were totally different. CHPs with higher Mw and DM, favored more molecular entanglement via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic association. However, purified EHPs with lower Mw and more free carboxyl groups interacted with Ca2+ through ionic cross-linking. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for applying hawthorn pectins in different food applications, especially gel-based products such as jellies, jams, yoghurts and ice creams.

Moisture distribution in pectin hydrogels

LF-NMR provides non-invasive and real-time measurements of the moisture state and distribution in a sample, with the relaxation time (T2) of protons depending on chemical environment and degree of freedom of water (i.e. a shorter relaxation time suggests tighter water-material binding, lower proton degree of freedom, thereby making discharge more difficult). As shown in Fig. 4A and B, the patterns of all the LF-NMR spectra of the CHP and EHP gels were very similar, containing two peaks: bound/immobile water (T21, 10–100 ms) and free water (T22, 100–1000 ms). Their peak areas (A21, A22) reflected the relative content of each fraction of water, thus can be used to measure indirectly the amount of water bound by the esterified pectin (Wei et al., 2024). Bound water is usually tightly bound to the hydrophilic groups (such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) on the pectin molecular chain, whilst free water normally exists in the voids/pores of the gel network thus is highly mobile (Osuch et al., 2025). In this study, T₂₁ and T₂₂ of the CHP and EHP gels shifted gradually to the left (towards the negative T2, indicating reduction of freedom of movement) as the hawthorn fruit storage duration increased. All the CHP and EHP gel samples exhibited higher peak values and shorter relaxation times, suggesting that the gel network could capture more water (which was associated with pectins' dense gel network structures) (Osuch et al., 2025). According to Fig. 4 A and 4 B, the changes of T21 and T22 in CHP gels (ΔT21 = 11.7, ΔT22 = 109.38) were greater than EHP gels (ΔT21 = 3.74, ΔT22 = 74.82), indicating that the stability of free water and bound water in CHP gels was lower than the stability of EHP gels. The variations in moisture stability depended greatly on pectin samples' compositions and pectins' functional groups e.g. the presence of starch/starch-like substances in CHPs, whilst these substances were removed via the enzymatic treatment post pectin extraction. Compared with the differences between other CHP gels and corresponding EHP gels, the T21 and T22 values of CHP-90 gel were much closer to those of EHP-90 gels likely due to the fact that only a small amount of starch (< 0.6%, Supplementary Table 1) was left after the hawthorn fruit was stored for 90 days, thereby the variation in moisture stability caused by the starch component was much smaller.

Moisture distribution of high soluble solid (sugar) pectin-rich hydrogels prepared with different hawthorn pectins. CHP and EHP represent hawthorn pectins extracted without and with enzymolysis, and the number following CHP or EHP indicates the storage duration (d) of hawthorn fruits.

An increase in the peak area of bound water (A₂₁) indicates an increased water adsorption capacity of a pectin gel, whilst a decrease in the peak area of free water (A₂₂) suggests a decrease in the free-flowing water in a gel. As shown in Fig. 4C and D, the amount of free water decreased with an increase of fruit storage time for both the CHP and EHP samples, indicating less free-moving water in the gels made with pectins extracted from fruits stored longer, likely due to more hydrophilic residues, such as hydroxyl groups, available to bind to water molecules through hydrogen bonds (Izydorczyk & Biliaderis, 1995). Overall, the CHP gels a higher amount of immobilized water than the corresponding EHP gels likely due to the presence of starch in CHPs and more stable and firmer gel structure formed (Said & Lee, 2025).

Antioxidant properties of HPs

As shown in Table 2, DPPH-RSA and ABTS-RSA gradually increased with an elevated pectin concentrations in the range of 0.5–4.0 mg/ mL (for DPPH assay) and 1.0–5.0 mg/mL (for ABTS assay), respectively. For example, DPPH-RSA of the EHP-0 was 57.46% at a pectin concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and was 76.79% at a pectin concentration of 4.0 mg/mL. At the same pectin concentration, DPPH-RSA and ABTS-RSA of any CHP or EHP decreased significantly with an increase of fruit storage time, which was consistent with the gradual decrease in total phenolic content during storage (Table 1). This suggests that phenolics were an important contributor to the higher antioxidant capacity. Other influencing factors including monosaccharide composition, turbidity, viscosity, chain characteristics, degrees of methylation, acetylation, branching and polymerization might also contribute to the differences in their antioxidant capacities (Du et al., 2023). In addition, EHPs exhibited much higher DPPH-RSA and ABTS-RSA than the corresponding CHPs (subjected to the same fruit storage duration) at the same pectin concentration. For example, at pectin concentrations of 1.0 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL, the ABTS-RSA values of the CHP-0 were only 24.12% and 63.24%, respectively, whilst the ABTS-RSA values of EHP-0 were 34.79% and 72.35%, respectively. This phenomenon was attributed to a combination of the higher phenolic content, lower DM and Mw in EHPs. These results agreed with the findings of Celus et al. (2018), who reported that low methoxyl citrus pectin exhibited higher antioxidant activities than high methoxyl pectin, likely due to the higher presence of carboxylic groups of galacturonic acid units in low methoxyl citrus pectin. The hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of pectins are electron donors can quench free radicals and/or delocalize unpaired electrons, with a high uronic acid content leading to a greater antioxidant effect (Sun-Waterhouse et al., 2008). A lower molecular weight of a pectin could greatly increase the number of reducing hydroxyl groups per specific surface area and the chance of contacting hydroxyl radicals, which would improve hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity (Xu et al., 2022). Taken together, CHPs and EHPs possess good antioxidant capacity, thus can act as antioxidants, besides emulsifiers, to prevent lipid oxidation in emulsions.

α-amylase or pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity of HPs

Polysaccharide-based α-amylase inhibitors have recently garnered significant interest due to their ability to delay postprandial rise of blood glucose.As shown in Table 2, both CHPs and EHPs had a higher inhibitory activity against α-Amylase or pancreatic lipase within the analyzed pectin concentration range. α-Amylase or pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity of either a CHP or EHP decreased with an increase of fruit storage time, likely due to decreases in Mw, DM and RG-I content as well as structural reorganization that occurs during the storage (Morales-Contreras et al., 2024). Moreover, at the same pectin concentrations, the EHPs exhibited greater inhibitory effects against α-amylase or pancreatic lipase compared with CHPs (except for the CHP-0 (52.68%) and the EHP-0 (50.79%), which exerted comparable inhibition). These results suggest that purified EHP without co-extracted starch might increase the probability of GalA exposure, which allows more active pectin domains to participate in enzyme binding (Bai et al., 2021; Espinal-Ruiz et al., 2014). It is well known that both purified pectin products and pectic polysaccharides play significant roles in suppressing the activities of amylases and pancreatic lipase. Pectins were found able to inhibit digestive enzymes through both competitive and non-competitive mechanisms. The competitive inhibition tends to take place when pectin binds to the digestive enzyme, forming a complex, whilst non-competitive inhibition is likely enhanced by pectic polysaccharides with a higher monosaccharide content, Mw and DM (Ahmadi et al., 2022). EHPs with more free carboxyl groups can compete with starch for binding to the active site of pancreatic α-amylase through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions (Espinal-Ruiz et al., 2014). From another point of view, CHPs with high-Mw may increase local viscosity and generate steric hindrance, which further restricts enzyme-substrate collision. Regarding pancreatic lipase, purified pectin may inhibit its activity by lipase binding capacity, the oil-water interfacial environmental modification, bile salt-assisted lipid emulsification interference, and lower accessibility of lipase to lipid substrates (Cervantes-Paz et al., 2017). In our previous research, a strong correlation was found between pectin's GalA content and Mw and pectin's enzyme inhibitory activity (Wei et al., 2024). In this study, the most significant correlation was among pectins' GalA concentration, Mw and the inhibitory activity against α-amylase or pancreatic lipase (Fig. 6). Therefore, enzyme inhibition was governed by the combined effects of pectin purity, accessible carboxyl groups, molecular flexibility and possible phenolic-associated components. It is worth noting that some previous studies reported that the relationship may not be universally consistent. Such discrepancies in concluding the relationship/correlation were due to the fact that there exist many influencing factors, such as the structural features of pectins, raw plant material/pectin product handling conditions, and the specific experimental conditions for pectin-enzyme interactions (Bai et al., 2021).

Prebiotic properties of HPs

The prebiotic activity of polysaccharides refers to their capacity to selectively promote the proliferation of particular probiotic strains relative to non-prebiotic substances such as glucose (Fei et al., 2024). To evaluate the prebiotic effect of the CHPs and EHPs, changes in the populations of two probiotic strains (Lactobacillus casei of LC6009 and LC6117) following 6 h or 12 h in vitro fermentation were measured. The control groups (CK) exhibited the lowest counts of L. casei strains, whilst the group containing fructooligosaccharide showed the highest counts of both Lactobacillus casei LC6009 (Fig. 5A) and LC6117 (Fig. 5B), indicating that fructooligosaccharide had the strongest prebiotic effect. The CHP and EHP samples also stimulated the growth of both probiotic strains throughout the 6-h or 12-h fermentation, even although their prebiotic potential were smaller than that of fructooligosaccharide at the same prebiotic concentration. The influences of the different pectins and fructooligosaccharide on the counts of both probiotic strains were much greater after 12-h of fermentation than after 6-h of fermentation. Furthermore, there were insignificant differences in the total plate counts between the two strains after 6 h of fermentation, but under the same conditions (including the same substrate), the total count of L. casei LC6009 was notably higher (1.10–1.47 times) than that of LC6117 after 12 h of fermentation. A noteworthy finding was that the prebiotic effect of both CHP and EHP increased with prolonged fruit storage duration, whilst storage was accompanied by a decrease in pectin Mw and, to some extent, structural degradation of the pectin fraction. This result appears paradoxical because storage-induced degradation is often considered unfavorable for maintaining pectin quality; however, in terms of probiotic growth promotion, the partial depolymerization of pectin may generate lower-Mw polysaccharide fractions that are more readily utilized by probiotic bacteria (Gamonpilas et al., 2021; Ho et al., 2017). Previous research found that the Mw of polysaccharides could be a key factor influencing their prebiotic potential, and polysaccharides with a lower Mw generally exhibited a more pronounced prebiotic effect (Fei et al., 2024). Ferreira-Lazarte et al. (2018) reported that enzymatically modified citrus pectin and artichoke pectin with a low Mw exhibited enhanced prebiotic activity, and enzymatic modification of citrus pectin promoted the growth and acid tolerance of B. bifidum and L. acidophilus by providing more readily available carbohydrate substrates. In general, at the same pectin concentration, the EHPs exhibited slightly higher prebiotic potential on the L. casei strains than the corresponding CHPs, which may be associated with the removal of starch by enzymatic treatment and changes in pectin characteristics such as Mw (Table 1). From a practical perspective, hawthorn with prolonged storage time could be regarded as a diverse source with improved prebiotic functionality, which provides a potential strategy for the differentiated utilization of stored fruits.

Component-structure-property relationships of HPs

Fig. 6 illustrates the interplays among HP components, structural characteristics and functional properties. There were no significant correlations between HP's protein content and other measured indicators, owing to only a very small amount of protein occurring in the HPs. DM and Mw exhibited a strong positive correlation with emulsifying properties such as EA and ES. Ciriminna et al. (2022) confirmed that the acetyl/methyl esterification degrees, ferulic acid residues, neutral sugar moieties and Mw of pectin influenced its emulsifying properties and exhibited significantly positive correlations. Pectin's Mw was found in close association with its conformational feature and subsequent behaviors in aqueous solutions, thereby influencing its capacity to penetrate the oil-water interfaces, form robust interfacial layers, and then stabilize emulsions through steric repulsion mechanisms (Lin et al., 2024). Therefore, the decrease in Mw during fruit storage may reduce chain entanglement and weaken the formation of interfacial protective layer, while the decrease in DM may reduce hydrophobic interactions between methyl-esterified pectin domains and oil droplets. These structural changes explained the decline in emulsion stability of HPs with increasing storage time. Similarly, the storage-induced reductions in Mw, DM and RG-I content may also weaken gel formation. Higher Mw favors chain entanglement and network continuity, whereas higher DM supports hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic association under high-soluble-solid acidic conditions. RG-I side chains may further contribute to water retention and spatial network support. Therefore, the reorganization of these structural domains during storage provides a mechanistic explanation for the decreased firmness, gumminess and chewiness of HP gels. Both GalA content and DM of HPs showed high impacts on their DPPH-RSA and ABTS-RSA values. In Fig. 6, the magnitude of the absolute values of correlations among different indicators is indicated by the thickness of lines. The thicker the line, the stronger the correlation. Accordingly, a significant positive correlation exists between HP's total phenolic content and its antioxidant capacity. The EHP group (Fig. 6B) had higher total phenolic contents and exhibited superior antioxidant capacity compared with the CHP group (Fig. 6A). The enzymatic treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase post pectin extraction resulted in a large reduction in DM and a significant enhancement of antioxidant capacity. These results were in agreement with those of Wang et al. (2025), who reported that citrus pectin with a higher total phenolic content and a lower DM exhibited the stronger antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, a strong correlation between HP's GalA content and pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity was also found. Overall, the correlation network provides an integrated relationship between structures and functionalities of HPs treated by prolonged storage time and enzyme-assisted extraction. On one hand, fruit storage mainly induced decreases in Mw, DM and RG-I-regions, thereby weakening the gelling and emulsifying properties of HPs. On the other hand, enzymatic treatment purified the pectin while caused a notable reduction in DM, contributing to the enhanced antioxidant activity, enzyme inhibitory activity and prebiotic potential. Collectively, the observed correlations among HP Mw, DM, GalA content, total phenolic content and functional properties indicate that HP functionality is jointly regulated by fruit storage and enzymatic treatment. These findings suggest the potential of HP as a multifunctional food ingredient, although its functional performance is strongly affected by raw material status, storage duration and pectin preparation method (Cui et al., 2021).

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the postharvest storage duration of hawthorn fruit and an additional enzymatic treatment with amylase and amyloglucosidase post pectin extraction from the hawthorn fruits could individually or jointly influenced the composition, physicochemical characteristics, structural features, thermal-induced behaviors, emulsifying capacity, gelling properties, antioxidant activities, α-Amylase or pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity and prebiotic potential of HP. Increasing fruit storage duration led to the increase of water-soluble pectin but decreases of total starch content, total sugar content, total pectin content and hardness of hawthorn fruit, resulting in reductions in Mw, DM, and antioxidant, α-amylase inhibitory or pancreatic lipase inhibitory activities but enhancement of prebiotic potential on two Lactobacillus casei strains. The additional enzymatic treatment post pectin extraction decreased Mw and DM (from 68 to 74% to 39–48%), increased HG and RG-I contents, and reduced the average size of neutral side chains (branching degree) of the RG-I region, which enabled the increases of antioxidant activities, α-amylase or pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity and prebiotic potential. Therefore, the fresh or short-stored hawthorn fruits are more suitable when gelling or emulsifying properties are desired, whereas longer-stored fruits may be preferred when prebiotic potential is the primary target. Correlation analyses revealed strong correlations among HP's Mw, DM, GalA content, and key functional activities (including antioxidant capacity, emulsifying properties, pancreatic lipase inhibition, and textural attributes). The results of this study indicate the potential of HP as a multifunctional ingredient in various food applications, guide the selection of hawthorn fruit at appropriate postharvest stages for pectin preparation, and offer an enzymatic approach for modifying HP after pectin extraction.
