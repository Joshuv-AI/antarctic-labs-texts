Participation, knowledge, and attitudes toward cervical cancer screening among migrant Burmese women on China-Myanmar border: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiawei Hu, Honghao Wang, Shuang Zhao, Liuye Huang, Xiaoqing Chen, Xuelian Zhao, Yong Zhang, Fanghui Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate cervical cancer screening participation, knowledge of cervical cancer and screening, attitudes toward relevant healthcare services, and associated factors among migrant Burmese women in Mangshi, a county on the China-Myanmar border in Southwest China's Yunnan province.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Mangshi Maternal and Child Health Hospital from October 2020 to December 2021. Migrant Burmese women attending routine gynecological examinations were recruited via convenient sampling. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was utilized to collect data. Logistic regressions were utilized to assess factors associated with cervical cancer screening participation and knowledge level.ResultsAmong 1504 participants, 31.3 % ever attended cervical cancer screening. In the recommended age group of 25–64 years, the participation rate was 35.2 %. Screening participation was associated with age, marital status, occupation, and history of genital tract infection. Approximately 20.9 % had heard of human papillomavirus, 23.2 % knew cervical cancer is preventable, and 41.6 % had heard of cervical cancer screening. Awareness of risk factors, screening techniques, and symptoms was limited. The median knowledge score was 2.0 out of 19.0. Knowledge level was associated with education, age at menarche, number of sexual partners, and history of genital tract infection. Most (99.7 %) participants were willing to attend cervical cancer screening, but only 84.5 % would accept therapy if necessary. The most common barrier to therapy was unaffordable cost.ConclusionsMigrant Burmese women in Mangshi had suboptimal screening participation and inadequate cervical cancer knowledge. Screening policies should prioritize socioeconomically disadvantaged women. Enhancing targeted health education is essential.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Cervical cancer screening, Immigrant, Burmese, Practice, Knowledge, Attitude

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth common cancer among women globally, with an estimated 662,301 new cases and 348,874 deaths in 2022, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries (Bray et al., 2024). China contributes to approximately one-fifth of the global burden, with 150,659 new cases and 55,694 deaths. Recent studies in China showed a decreasing age at onset (Li et al., 2017) and increasing incidence and mortality (Sun et al., 2024) for cervical cancer.

Correspondingly, the World Health Organization and China's National Health Commission have launched initiatives to eliminate cervical cancer through expanded population-based screening. According to the Guidelines for Cervical Cancer Screening in China, the recommended age group for general women is 25–64 years (Li et al., 2023). The national screening program was launched in 2009, initially targeting rural women aged 35–64 years. By 2019, the program was expanded to cover both rural and urban women within the same age range. In 2023, the Action Plan to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer (2023–2030) set targets of achieving 50 % screening coverage among eligible women by 2025 and 70 % by 2030 (Simms et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023). While most countries have initiated public screening programs, screening access remains uneven, particularly among socioeconomically marginalized populations and those in less developed regions (Bao et al., 2018; Bruni et al., 2022). Migrant women, often impacted by structural barriers to healthcare, are less likely to undergo screening than native residents, thus facing a higher risk of cervical cancer (Bucchi et al., 2019; Rosato et al., 2023). Prevailing barriers include socioeconomic disadvantages, limited awareness, unfavorable policies, and insufficient healthcare provision (Crawford et al., 2016; Marques et al., 2020).

Mangshi, a multiethnic border county in Dehong prefecture, Southwest China's Yunnan province, with a population of 464.3 thousand, hosts a large number of Burmese migrants who are at increased risk of sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Human immunodeficiency virus prevalence among migrant Burmese in cross-border marriages in Dehong prefecture and its capital Mangshi county has reached 2.0 % (Xu et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2021), substantially higher than that among general premarital screening populations (Dehong prefecture 0.45 %; Mangshi county 0.67 %), which may be attributed to limited health literacy, risky sexual behavior, drug trafficking and use in regions bordered to Southeast Asia, and poor access to HIV testing in their original living places (Gong et al., 2019; Han et al., 2016). While HPV and HIV share overlapping risk factors, little is known about the pattern of HPV infection among this population, with limited evidence on their knowledge and practices regarding cervical cancer and screening. This study investigated cervical cancer screening participation, knowledge, and attitudes among Burmese women in Mangshi, aiming to inform targeted health education and screening strategies for migrant populations in underserved border areas.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Mangshi Maternal and Child Health Hospital between October 2020 and December 2021. The hospital is the only facility that undertakes population-based cervical and breast cancer screening in the county. The Cervical and Breast Cancer Screening Clinic of this hospital offers free routine gynecological examinations to women with a history of sexual activity. All women attending routine gynecological examinations at the screening clinic were eligible if they: 1) were aged 14–70 years; 2) had initiated sexual intercourse; 3) had an intact cervix; 4) had no history of cervical treatment and pelvic radiotherapy; 5) were not currently pregnant; 6) were born in Myanmar with Burmese nationality; and 8) could understand the objectives, procedures, potential risks, and expected benefits of the study, and volunteered to attend the study.

Sample size determination and sampling

Sample size was determined using the single-population proportion formula:

Assuming a 25.7 % expected screening coverage (Zhang et al., 2020), a 2.5 % margin of error, and 95 % confidence level. Considering a 20 % non-response rate, the minimal required sample size was 1468. Given the non-probability sampling method employed, a larger number of eligible women were enrolled. Hence, a total of 1588 women were recruited via convenient sampling.

Instrument and variables

Questionnaire

The questionnaire used for interview was developed by a multidisciplinary team of gynecologists and epidemiologists and applied in several previous studies on knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding cervical cancer (He et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2018a; Zhang et al., 2018b; Zhao et al., 2022). It was adapted and pretested by local health professionals for comprehensibility and acceptability. The questionnaire covered four domains: 1) sociodemographic characteristics; 2) knowledge about cervical cancer; 3) participation in cervical cancer prevention and control programs; and 4) attitudes toward cervical cancer prevention and control. For this analysis, we used data on cervical cancer and screening.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was cervical cancer screening participation, which was defined if a participant had ever attended cervical cancer screening.

The secondary outcome was knowledge level. In this domain, single-choice questions were scored as one for “yes” and zero for “no” or “unknown”; multiple-choice questions awarded one point per correct answer, with a maximum score of 19 points. Knowledge level was dichotomized into high (>2 points) and low (≤2 points) levels based on the median knowledge score (2 points), following the method adopted in previous studies (Hu et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021).

Explanatory variables

Included variables and categorizations are as follows: age (14–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–63), marital status (single, married/cohabitating, divorced/widowed/separated), education (no formal education, primary, secondary or higher), occupational status (housewife/unemployed, farmer/laborer, public institution/company), age at menache (<14, ≥14), age at sexual debut (<20, ≥20), number of sexual partners in the last three years (≤1, >1, refuse to answer), number of deliveries (0, 1, 2, ≥3), and history of genital tract infection (GTI) (yes, no).

Other descriptive variables

In the knowledge domain, participants who had heard of cervical cancer screening were asked about the information source. In attitudes domain, participants were asked about the willingness to participate in cervical cancer screening and perceived facilitators for participation. Regarding attitudes toward therapy, participants were asked whether they would be willing to receive therapy in a hypothetical situation where treatment would be required following screening or diagnostic results indicating precancerous lesions or cervical cancer. Participants unwilling to receive therapy even if necessary were asked about perceived barriers.

Interview and data collection

All participants were interviewed face-to-face and one-on-one by trained health workers using the questionnaire in appropriate settings to ensure privacy, either at the clinic, participants' homes, or community centers in their villages, depending on participants' availability and scheduling considerations. Necessary prevention measures were implemented throughout the survey, including mask wearing, body temperature check, physical distancing, and hand disinfection, in light of the public health situation during the Covid-19 pandemic. Data were entered by trained health workers via the Goodata system, which allowed safe storage of data, data export, logic checks, and outlier and discrepancy alerts. Personal identifiers were removed from the database used for analysis to ensure participants' confidentiality. The data were stored securely in password-protected files, and access to data was limited to the core research team.

Statistical analysis

Participants' characteristics were reported using frequencies with percentages. Univariable and multivariable binary logistic regressions were applied to identify factors associated with cervical cancer screening participation and knowledge level. Variables with p-value <0.10 in univariable analyses were included in multivariable models. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratio (OR, aOR) with 95 % confidence interval (CI) were estimated, with p-value <0.05 considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using Statistical Product and Service Solutions software version 26.0.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, Beijing, China (Approval Number: 18-190/1768). All participants, as well as the parents/guardians of minors under the age of 18, were fully informed of the objectives, procedures, potential risks, and expected benefits, and voluntarily participated in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. Both written informed consent from parents/guardians and written assent from participants were obtained for minors under the age of 18. For participants who were illiteracy, fingerprint was used instead of signature for informed consent. Participants had the right to leave the study at any time. All methods were performed following relevant guidelines and regulations, and participants' privacy, confidentiality, and rights were consistently protected throughout the study.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 1504 out of 1588 Burmese women with valid responses were included in the final analysis. The age of participants ranged between 14 and 63 years. The majority (88.1 %) were married or cohabitating. Approximately 40.2 % had no formal education, and 68.3 % worked as farmers or laborers. Over half experienced menarche at age 14 or later (56.7 %) and first intercourse at age 20 or later (60.0 %). Most (90.5 %) reported no more than one sexual partner in the last three years. About half (49.9 %) gave birth to two children, and 2.1 % reported a history of genital tract infection (GTI) (Table 1).

Knowledge about cervical cancer and screening

Knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 17, with a median (interquartile range) of 2.0 (1.0–3.0). Approximately one-fifth had heard of human papillomavirus (HPV) (20.9 %) or knew cervical cancer is preventable (23.2 %). HPV infection (12.2 %) and multiple sexual partners (12.0 %) were the most frequently identified risk factors. A total of 626 (41.6 %) participants had heard of cervical cancer screening, mainly through health professionals (91.4 %) (Fig. 1). Colposcopy (6.8 %) was the most recognized screening method. While nearly all (99.8 %) agreed that women at appropriate ages should undergo regular screening, awareness of cervical cancer symptoms was very limited (Table 3).

Attitudes toward cervical cancer screening and therapy

Most (99.7 %) participants expressed the willingness to attend cervical cancer screening in the future. Health education (93.1 %) and well-organization (66.0 %) were the most frequently mentioned facilitators. Unaffordable cost (84.5 %) was the predominantly mentioned barrier among those who were unwilling to receive therapy even if necessary (Table 5).

Discussion

This study revealed low cervical cancer screening participation and limited cervical cancer knowledge among migrant Burmese women in Mangshi, China. Although most expressed willingness to attend screening, approximately 15 % were unwilling to receive therapy even if necessary, primarily due to unaffordable cost.

Approximately 35.2 % of Burmese women in recommended age group in our study had attended cervical cancer screening, notably lower than that of native Chinese in the capital city of Yunnan province (58.1 %) (Yuanyue et al., 2018). Similarly, migrant women worldwide were 46 % less likely to undergo screening than native residents (Rosato et al., 2023). A study in another Asian setting also demonstrated lower participation among migrant Yemeni women (23.1 %) compared to native Malaysian (47.3 %) (Al-Oseely et al., 2023). Socioeconomic status and healthcare access may play crucial roles in participation. Single women in our study were less likely to be screened than those married or cohabitating, partly due to lacking spousal support (Chigbu et al., 2013). Women working as farmers or laborers, who earned a salary independently, had higher participation than those who were housewife or unemployed. These results are aligned with findings from Denmark, where low screening participation among immigrants was associated with unmarried status and unemployment (Hertzum-Larsen et al., 2019). In addition, health insurance coverage and access to healthcare facilities were also identified as facilitators of the screening uptake among migrant women in other comparable Asian settings (Al-Oseely et al., 2023). These patterns emphasize that migrant women, especially those in recommended age group for screening, without spousal support, and socioeconomically disadvantaged, should be prioritized in screening policies.

Currently, however, there is no specific national cervical cancer screening policies/programs in China for migrant populations. In many regions, access to free screening services is restricted by quotas, household registration, or place of residence, which limits the inclusion of migrant women. Some border areas in Yunnan province have made specific provisions to include Burmese women in free screening programs. However, in many of these cases, eligibility is limited to those with legal residency and a formal marital relationship with Chinese citizen; Mangshi only lifted these restrictions in 2025. Similarly in Guangxi province, the border town Naliang has extended free screening services to Vietnamese women, but still restricts eligibility to those registered as local residents or permanent inhabitants. These underscore urgent need to address structural barriers in policies to improve access to cervical cancer screening among migrant women in recommended screening age group, especially those without legal citizenship and socioeconomically disadvantaged (Guerrero et al., 2016).

High knowledge level is also associated with cervical cancer screening participation among migrant women (Al-Oseely et al., 2023). However, only one-fifth of migrant Burmese women in our study had heard of HPV or recognized cervical cancer as preventable, contrasting drastically to native Chinese women in the same province (51.1 %) and native Burmese women in Myanmar (69.5 %–90.7 %) (Mon et al., 2009; Wint, 2019; Yuanyue et al., 2018). Similar patterns were also reported in high-income settings, including the United States, Greece, and the United Kingdom, where migrant women had lower awareness of HPV and cervical cancer compared to native populations. This disparity may be attributed to linguistic and cultural barriers (Bhattacharya et al., 2022; Ekechi et al., 2014; Riza et al., 2020).

Migrant Burmese women in our study demonstrated poorer knowledge of cervical cancer etiology, risk factors, and symptoms compared to both native Chinese women in the same province and native Burmese women (Mon et al., 2009; Yuanyue et al., 2018). However, other migrant populations exhibited notably higher knowledge: over two-thirds of Turkish women in Florida (Uysal Toraman and Yildirim, 2018) and 22.2 %–65.7 % of Yemeni women in Malaysia identified risk factors, while 14.6 %–55.1 % of the latter recognized symptoms (Azzani et al., 2023). The inadequate knowledge among Burmese women in our study might be partially explained by lower educational attainment, underscoring the significance of targeted health education.

Women with vulnerable characteristics should be prioritized in health education. In our study, higher education and earlier menarche were associated with better knowledge, and history of GTI was associated with both better knowledge and screening participation, consistent with previous findings (Argaw et al., 2022; Issa et al., 2021; Muluneh et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). Conversely, women refusing to report the number of sexual partners tended to have lower knowledge levels. Prior studies found this reluctance was associated with limited health knowledge and engagement in behaviors with high-risk of STIs (Peruga et al., 1993). This highlights the need to reach women with insufficient health literacy and inadequate healthcare access. Besides, fewer than 10 % of the Burmese women obtained screening information from mass media, compared to 60 % of native Chinese women (Zhang et al., 2023). This suggests health information on local media should incorporate local languages (Burmese, Tai, etc.) to address cultural and linguistic barriers and improve communication efficacy.

In the attitude domain, good organization was another critical facilitator mentioned by Burmese women. As most lacked access to workplace-based health check-ups, this strategy in urban areas is unfeasible in remote low-resource areas. Instead, community- or village-based screening programs should be considered. HPV-testing, with its lower frequency requirements and self-sampling option, may also enhance participation among hard-to-reach women (Alam et al., 2023; Arrossi et al., 2015). However, its relatively high cost may pose a barrier to widespread implementation in low-resource regions. Additionally, as Mangshi and many border areas, with large migrant populations, lies in tropical/subtropical zones with a rainy season spanning from May to October, some mountainous or remote villages may become difficult to access. These environmental constraints may hinder their participation in screening programs. Thus, careful scheduling and mobile clinics are needed in program organization to address unsuitable weather periods. Moreover, routine public health programs including cervical cancer screening and relevant research were inevitably affected during infectious disease pandemics due to mobility restrictions and medical staff reallocation. To enhance system resilience, it is vital to implement appropriate protective measures, prioritize populations with elevated risk of cancer, adopt self-sampling method, and promptly resume routine healthcare services when feasible.

Our study shows fewer women were willing to receive therapy than to undergo screening, with unaffordable cost being the most vital barrier. This phenomenon may be attributed to the limitations in financial support policies. In China, public financial assistance schemes for cervical/breast cancer therapy targeting women with low-income are subject to local household registration in most regions, including border areas like Mangshi. Even those maintaining a legal marriage with a Chinese citizen, who may have access to free screening programs, may be excluded from assistance schemes for cancer therapy. This emphasizes the need for robust follow-up and referral systems and inclusive financial support to address health inequity in low-resource border areas.

To our knowledge, this is the first study assessing cervical cancer screening participation, knowledge, and attitudes among migrant women in China. The findings could provide insights for tailoring screening strategies and health education for migrant women in remote areas with limited health resources. However, several limitations should be noted. First, it was a monocenter study without probability sampling, though a sample size larger than anticipation might partly offset selection bias. Therefore, conclusions are limited to Burmese women in Mangshi county, and generalization to other migrant populations in border areas should be made with caution. Second, the cross-sectional design made it impossible to establish causal relationships between independent factors and outcomes. Third, recall and reporting biases inevitably existed in self-reported data. Fourth, critical factors including residency status were not fully collected.

Conclusions

In summary, migrant Burmese women in China's southwest border areas had insufficient screening participation and inadequate knowledge regarding cervical cancer. Screening policies and financial support should prioritize socioeconomically marginalized migrant women in recommended age group. Structural barriers, such as limitations on household registration, residency status, and marital relationship, should be reduced to promote health equity. Targeted health education is essential, particularly for those who have low education, experience menarche at an older age, refuse to report sexual history, and have no GTI history. Linguistic and cultural barriers should be addressed. Screening programs should adapt to migrants' living conditions.
