Utilization of vector bionomics, remote sensing and larval habitat characterization data to inform larval source management decision in the high malaria burden state of Kebbi, Nigeria
Authors: Adedayo O. Oduola, Petrus U. Inyama, Lazarus M. Samdi, Muhammad A. Bunza, Abubakar Bagudu, Ifeanyi Okeke, Grace Yina, Mary Esema, Godwin Ntadom, Kelley Ambrose, Jules Mihigo, Melissa Yoshimizu, Aklilu Seyoum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a public health challenge with Nigeria having the highest burden in cases and deaths worldwide. Increased outdoor and indoor biting of Anopheles gambiae sensu lato (s.l.) and large gaps in protection have become emerging challenges threatening malaria control efforts in Kebbi State, Nigeria. This study provided data on vector behaviour, the distribution, diversity, and other characteristics of larval habitats to inform the feasibility of larval source management in the state.MethodsRoutine entomological data were comparatively analyzed across six entomological monitoring sites located in Akwa Ibom, Ebonyi, Kebbi, Oyo, Plateau and Sokoto states in Nigeria. With a focus on Kebbi State, using Geographical Information System (GIS) data integrated with satellite imagery, potential larval habitats were remotely identified, characterized and mapped in Argungu, Bunza and Kalgo local government areas (LGAs). Field data on larval habitat abundance, larval density, and proximity of larval habitats to human habitation, and breeding index were determined and analyzed.ResultsAnopheles gambiae s.l. was the predominant 13,201(100%) malaria vector species in Kebbi State, in contrast to other states where Anopheles coustani, Anopheles funestus s.l., Anopheles maculipalpis, Anopheles marshallii group, Anopheles moucheti, Anopheles pharoensis and Anopheles rufipes were also collected. The average human biting rate of An. gambiae s.l. in Kebbi was the highest at both indoors and outdoors in the dry (15.2, 8.7 b/p/n) and rainy (42.2, 14.3 b/p/n) seasons across the six routine surveillance sites. The human behavioural observation data in Kebbi showed a protection gap of 0.83 (83%) from mosquito bite exposures both indoors and outdoors. A total of 3218 and 635 potential mosquito larval habitats were sampled by the field enumerators and sample collectors respectively across Argungu, Bunza and Kalgo LGAs. Of the 635 potential larval habitats identified by sample collectors, 460 (97.9%) were positive for Anopheles larvae. Eleven different larval habitat types were identified including temporary and permanent habitats. The proportion of temporary larval habitats within 100 m proximity of inhabited houses was highest in Argungu LGA 464 (43%) Bunza 230 (18%), and Kalgo 276 (61%). The highest proportions of those habitats were rice fields in Argungu 231 (80%), Bunza 446 (96%) and Kalgo 90 (94%).ConclusionThe consistently high entomology indices, measured protection gaps, productive and abundant larval habitats situated near human habitations throughout the year indicate the need for complementary vector control interventions, such as larval source management (LSM) in Kebbi State.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05568-3.

Background

Malaria remains a major public health challenge and is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Nigeria [1]. According to the World Malaria Report, there were 249 million cases globally in 85 malaria endemic countries in 2023. Nigeria was first among the 29 countries accounting for 95% of the global cases, and first (at 31.1%) among the four countries accounting for nearly half of all malaria deaths worldwide in 2022 [2].

Nigeria, with malaria cases estimated at 68 million and deaths from malaria at 194,000 in 2022 [1], has been identified as one of 11 high burdens to high impact countries. Between 2019 and 2022, there was an increase of about 5.3 million estimated cases in the country [2]. Out of 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory, Kebbi State contributed an estimated 3.1% of the 68 million malaria cases in 2021, and children under 5 years accounted for 49% of the cases. This makes Kebbi the state with the highest malaria burden in Nigeria [3, 4].

Malaria burden is driven by multiple factors ranging from socioeconomic to ecological. Ecologically, rice fields have been reported as major contributors to the proliferation of competent malaria vector species in countries with high malaria transmission [5–7] including Nigeria. Leading rice producing areas in Nigeria are Kebbi (the major producer in northern Nigeria), Borno, Kano, and Kaduna [8, 9] states. Kebbi State has several hectares of fadama (Hausa for “irrigable lowland”) farming stretching across 15 out of the 21 local government areas (LGAs) in the state, and rice farming has become a mainstream commercial activity, reducing the need for importation [9].

Traditional rain-fed rice farming in Kebbi has been driven by rainfall and suitable ‘tableland’ topography, which encourages water retention. The access of small holder farmers to the government’s Anchors Borrowers Programme, an initiative to support the production of agricultural commodities such as rice, maize, sugarcane and wheat, has further increased farmer incomes due to multiple rice harvests in the year, made possible through irrigation [10, 11]. Rice fields now are flooded by rain during the rainy season and irrigated via other water sources such as solar/fuel-powered boreholes during the dry season [12]. These newer irrigation practices create and maintain mosquito larval habitats in drier months, when traditionally, mosquito populations and malaria transmission decreased. In Kebbi, Fadama farming along with use of dry season irrigation practices in agriculture may support the proliferation of mosquito larval habitats in drier months, bringing communities into proximity with efficient malaria vectors year-round [13]. By providing suitable and productive larval habitats [14], rice farming has been linked to increased malaria prevalence.

Vector control, a vital component of malaria control and elimination, remains one of the most effective methods to reduce malaria transmission and burden. In Nigeria, insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) are the major vector control strategy. Kebbi State conducted mass ITN distribution campaigns in 2014 and 2018, and most recently 2022, with between 3 and 3.5 million ITNs distributed in each campaign. Using insecticide resistance data to support evidence-based decision-making for ITN procurements, a shift to new types of ITNs (dual active ingredient) was made for the 2022 mass ITN distribution in Kebbi State.

Despite data from the Nigeria Malaria Indicator Survey in 2021 indicating that 73% of households in Kebbi owned an ITN, only 38.2% slept under an ITN the night before the survey [15]. This suggested there were likely gaps in protection and the need to investigate human behaviors and the feasibility of complementary vector control interventions, such as larval source management (LSM).

LSM is the targeted management of mosquito larval habitats with the aim of reducing the number of larvae and pupae and is recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a supplementary vector control tool [16]. While there exists a body of knowledge on the different types of mosquito larval breeding habitats in limited locations in Nigeria [17–19], only a few published studies have reported on the evaluation of larvicides that can be used for LSM in Nigeria [20, 21].

This study analysed human behaviour, entomological indicators and satellite imagery data to understand the distribution, diversity and productivity of larval habitats in northern Kebbi State with its high malaria burden and wide-scale rice farming, to inform the feasibility of LSM.

Methods

Study area

Kebbi State is in the northwestern part of Nigeria (12° 27′ 7.79″ N, 24° 12′ 0.60″ E) with a total landmass of about 37,699 km2, over a third of which is farmland, and with an estimated population of 4.4 million as of 2016 (National Population Commission of Nigeria, 2016). The landscape is dominated by extensive flood plains (fadama) of the inland river valley systems. The Niger River flows southwest across part of the state and the Rima River flows southerly through the center of the state to join the Niger River. Both rivers have broad flood plains. The flow of the Rima River is, however, constant and reaches its peak during the short rainy season between June and September.

The LSM habitat survey was carried out in Argungu, Bunza and Kalgo LGAs in Kebbi State between July and August 2022 (Fig. 1), which cover a land mass of about 1042 km2, 1034 km2, and 599 km2, respectively. The LGAs were selected due to high malaria incidence rates, availability of larval habitats, community acceptance and stable security. The survey area is characterized by rainy and dry seasons; the rainy season runs from June to September in the north and April to October in the south. Mean annual rainfall varies significantly from the northern part of the state (733 mm) to the southern part (1045 mm). The average number of rainy days also varies, from 50 days in the north to 80 days in the south. Monthly average rainfall ranges from 1.15 to 216.34 mm across the three LGAs, while monthly average temperatures range from 24.15 to 33.41 °C. Review of key entomological indicators of major malaria vector species and human behavior data

Vector species composition, abundance, human biting rate (HBR), indoor resting density and mean hourly indoor and outdoor HBR data from two representative communities in each of the six routine entomological surveillance sites in Mkpat Enim LGA, Akwa Ibom, Ezza North LGA, Ebonyi, Argungu LGA, Kebbi, Akinyele LGA, Oyo, Shendam LGA, Plateau and Rabah LGA, Sokoto states from October 2021 to September 2022 were extracted from the DHIS2-based VectorLink Collect database and reviewed alongside data from Kebbi. Data were also segregated into dry (October to March) and rainy (April to September) seasons to identify the peak period of mosquito abundance to determine the most appropriate location and timing of LSM as an intervention. While the focus is on Kebbi, the routine entomological data presented along other sites data to fully appreciate the vector dynamics leading to the LSM vector control decision in Argungu and not other sites where the same data was collected. Human behavior observation data was collected in Kebbi, using a well designed questionnaire pretested in a similar setting.. This was conducted due to the extremely high burden in the locality, and also to understand gaps in protection based on low ITN use. relative to high ownership. Location (indoor/outdoor) and ITN use (sleeping under/no net use) were collected on the household level through hourly direct human observations done during hourly United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) light trap collections carried out between 6:00 pm and 11:00 a.m the following day.

Data collection, grids acquisition, analysis and interpretation of satellite images for multicriteria parameters promoting mosquito larval habitats

Geographical Information System (GIS) data integrated with satellite imagery, enhanced by machine learning and the Google Earth Engine platform, were used to identify potential larval habitats for LSM. A grid system, which includes a pattern of straight lines that cross over each other, forming squares (1km × 1 km), was generated and overlayed on maps of sites to help identify the specific larval habitats. A multicriteria analysis using a pairwise comparison matrix, analytical hierarchical processing and ranking of the selected principal criteria (Normalized Differenced Vegetation Index, Normalized Differenced Moisture Index, Land Surface Temperature, and slope) was employed to generate locations suitable to mosquito larval development. Additional information on the choice of algorithm showed that ensemble learning algorithms, such as Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost and XGBoost as well as a type of Neural Network algorithm called the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model, were used. These machine learning algorithms were selected due to their robustness. Satellite imagery from the rainy season (July 2022) was the primary dataset with mosaic datasets spanning January to June 2022 used as controls.

Enumeration team composition and roles

Four cadres of personnel were engaged to support the ground-truthing enumeration of the LSM habitat survey: 30 field enumerators (14 for Argungu LGA, 10 for Bunza LGA and 6 for Kalgo LGA), 15 sample collectors (7 in Argungu LGA, 5 in Bunza LGA and 3 in Kalgo LGA), eight supervisors and an advisor. The role of the field enumerators was to identify and collect information on the mosquito larval habitats/waterbodies, while sample collectors focused mainly on the collection and rearing of larvae and the identification of emerged adults in the insectary. Identifying two separate cadres of workers allowed field enumerators to cover many locations, while sample collectors spent longer amounts of time at fewer locations to best determine the larval density and capture specimens to be taken to the laboratory for identification. Prior to data collection, personnel were trained by the US President’s Malaria Initiative VectorLink Nigeria Project in collaboration with a GIS consultancy firm on how to use the mobile devices to capture, map and characterize field data relating to mosquito larval habitats. The mobile devices were used to capture additional details on numbers and specific surface area coverage of the different larval habitats identified.

Mapping of larval habitats and data collection

Field enumerators were tasked with locating and validating locations identified from the GIS analysis and collecting the physical and entomological characteristics of each habitat. Unique codes were issued to each field enumerator to ensure they were aware of the grids assigned to them for the week; this enumerator code also aided in validating the data collected in the field. Field enumerators used mobile (Android) devices with the Qfield application to map and geo-reference all larval habitats within a grid and capture key characteristics of the larval habitats, including: status (permanent, semi-permanent or temporary), type (such as rain pools, gutters, rice fields), water origin (rain, streams, river). Larval habitats regarded as permanent are mostly from natural sources with water available year-round; semi-permanent refers to artificial water sources created for humans and availability is dependent on daily use; temporary are also artificially created, with availability dependent on sources such as overflow from natural events like flooding and rainfall. This type usually dries up until the event re-occurs.

Collection of larval samples, rearing and estimation of larval density

Sample collectors were trained and assigned the responsibility of collecting larval samples and other information on the identified habitats (larval density, pH, temperature) as well as other climatic parameters that might influence the emergence and growth of mosquito larvae. Sample collectors used mobile (Android) devices programmed with the Qfield application, along with a ruler for measuring water depth, a sample collection bucket, larval dippers, sample collection scoops, and a dual-purpose pH meter and thermometer.

The sample collectors visited 30% of the total grids earlier enumerated by field enumerators and selected a minimum of 10 locations within each grid. Global Positioning System coordinates of sampled sites were recorded using the mobile device. If a larvae could be spotted without dipping, or at least one larva could be seen in each of the dips taken, then the location was regarded as having high larval density [22]. Locations where only one or two out of the 10 dips taken contained Anopheles sp. larvae were regarded as having low density. Locations where no Anopheles sp. larvae could be found in 10 dips were regarded as negative or empty [23]. Mean larval density for each location was calculated by dividing the total number of larvae by 10 dips. All Anopheles mosquito larvae collected in the field were sorted to remove the predators. The immature larvae were reared in larval bowls covered with nets. Emerging adults were asptrated into a net cage following which were reared to adult at the Federal University Birnin Kebbi, and later identified using standard morphological keys [24, 25].

The distance between the mosquito breeding site and closest human dwellings as observed by the sample collectors and field enumerators were recorded for every larval habitats surveyed. These were categorized into two: the proportion within a distance greater than 100 m and lesser than 100 m. This was calculated to establish the proximity of the breeding site to human dwellings.

Data analysis

The mean hourly human biting rate (HBR) of each site, representing the number of bites per person per hour, was estimated and multiplied by the proportion of people at a given time and location(site, indoor/outdoor) to estimate exposure for each location and time. The protection gap was calculated by subtracting from 100% the percentage or proportion of all vector bites prevented by using an ITN, which represents the protection provided against vector bites to someone using an ITN during sleeping hours. Productivity of larval habitats in Argungu, Bunza and Kalgo LGAs were determined using the breeding index calculations. It was computed as: BI = (TLP) ÷ (ND × BP), where: TLP = total number of larvae and pupae collected, ND = total number of dips performed and BP = number of larval habitats sampled. [26]. Abundance of each larval habitat identified in the three LGAs was compared using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Chi-square tests were applied to compare the significance of the proportion of larval habitat within 100 m and ≥ 100 m from human habitations.

Results

Vector species composition and abundance

In Kebbi State, An. gambiae sensu lato (s.l.) was the predominant 13,201(100%) vector species collected and identified. In other sentinel sites, in Ebonyi, Oyo, Plateau and Sokoto states, An. gambiae s.l. was also the dominant mosquito species collected. These states also had other Anopheles species within the collections: Anopheles coustani, Anopheles funestus group, Anopheles maculipalpis, Anopheles marshallii group, Anopheles moucheti, Anopheles pharoensis, Anopheles rufipes and Anopheles squamosus (Fig. 2). Anopheles funestus s.l. was more abundant in Oyo than in the other sentinel sites, and An. marshallii group was dominant in Akwa Ibom. A total of 29,563 Anopheles mosquitoes were collected from the six sentinel sites using human-baited CDC light traps (indoors/outdoors) and pyrethrum spray catches (Fig. 2). Argungu LGA, Kebbi alone recorded the highest mosquito abundance (13,201) (p = 0.0155), across all the other sites in Shendam LGA Plateau (3655), Mkpat Enim LGA, Akwa Ibom (2033), Ezza North LGA, Ebonyi (1512) and Akinyele LGA, Oyo (1466) except in Rabah LGA, Sokoto (7716). Human behaviour observations

In Kebbi, biting occurs predominantly indoors at bedtime with a peak indoor biting rate of 3.11 bites per person per hour occurring between 1 and 2 a.m. The outdoor biting rate in Kebbi peaked at 0.89 bites per person per hour between 9 and 10 p.m. (Fig. 3a). The directly observed human behavior data indicated that most people were outdoors (not in bed) before 10 p.m., and most people were indoors not under a net between 11 p.m. and 6 a.m. At the peak indoor biting hour of 1 to 2 a.m., about 57% of the people were in bed, but not protected under nets. The proportion of bites occurring indoors for unprotected individuals is estimated as 0.70 (70%). The proportion of bites prevented by using nets indoors was estimated as 0.16 (16%). The proportion of bites occurring outdoors (Fig. 3b) for unprotected individuals was generally low and estimated as 0.14 (14%). The proportion of bites prevented from individuals using nets outdoors was 0.01 (1%). Mosquito larval habitats enumeration

Abundance of the larval habitats

During the enumeration exercise, a total of 3853 potential mosquito larval habitats were identified by field enumerators (3218) and sample collectors (635) across the three LGAs (Table 3 and Fig. 6). Field enumerators and sample collectors encountered higher proportions of potential mosquito larval habitats in Argungu (35.9% and 47.0%) and Bunza (48.4% and 35.0%) compared to Kalgo (15.7% and 18.0%). Sample collectors in these three LGAs collected and identified mosquito larvae from 298 (47%) larval habitats in Argungu, 222 (35%) in Bunza and 115 (18%) in Kalgo. Out of the 635 potential larval habitats identified by the sample collectors, 470 (74%) were positive for Anopheles and Culex mosquito larvae. Of these, the proportion that was positive for Anophelines 460 (97.9%) was significantly higher (p = 0.00002) compared to those positive for the Culicines 10 (2.1%) (Table 3). Diversity of mosquito larval habitats from selected local government areas in Kebbi State

A total of 3218 potential mosquito larval habitats were identified by field enumerators in Argungu 1158 (35.9%), Bunza 1556 (48.4%) and Kalgo 504 (15.7%) (Table 3). The larval habitats were classified into three categories: temporary (ditches, gutters, animal hoof marks, mud brick pits, rain pools and rice fields), semi-permanent (spillage from boreholes and water taps) and permanent (ponds, stream edges and swamps) (Table 4, Supplementary file). Discussion

Remotely sensed data have found useful application in disease mapping, epidemiology and control, with researchers deploying this tool to support surveillance and control efforts [27]. In this study, malaria prevalence in addition to supportive entomological and human behavior data were used to identify Kebbi State as a potential location for complementary vector control interventions.

Kebbi State has the highest HBR indoors, outdoors and indoor resting density during both the wet and dry seasons compared with all the other routine entomological monitoring sites in Nigeria except in Sokoto and Plateau State during the wet season. According to other reports, the deployment of larval control will reduce the abundance of malaria mosquito larvae and adult females by as much as > 90% [28, 29]. In Kebbi State, there was higher outdoor biting in the early part of the night, which potentially expose the residents to mosquito bites prior to bedtime. Similarly, the human behavior data also showed large protection gaps during the night. This agrees with the report of changing mosquito feeding and biting behaviour influencing malaria transmission across Africa [30]. While entomological surveillance data collections including annual insecticide susceptibility tests have been used to guide ITN selection for mass distribution and IRS implementation, there is a need to also generate more information to support LSM implementation in Africa.

Through the LSM habitat survey component, GIS data were integrated with satellite imagery and enhanced machine learning to identify and predict potential mosquito larval habitats in Argungu, Bunza and Kalgo LGAs in Kebbi State. Similar studies have reported the creation of malaria risk maps using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index classification of satellite images with medium and low spatial resolution of environmental water bodies and vegetation [31, 32]. This is the first successful attempt of using GIS and satellite imagery tools to predict larval habitats in preparation for LSM in Nigeria. This as a useful tool that could be adapted to monitor the implementation progress of the LSM activities.

Despite Bunza LGA having the highest proportion of mosquito larval habitats encountered, followed by Argungu LGA, the latter had the highest larval breeding index. Across the three LGAs, rain pools and rice paddies contributed a significant portion of the larval habitats observed in this survey. In agreement with other authors, the diversity of larval habitats as observed in this study (ditch, gutters, animal hoof marks, mud brick pits and standing pools from boreholes and broken water taps) is associated with human activities [33, 34] and as such community-oriented groups should be involved in the planning and implementation of environmental management strategies to eliminate and manage these habitats.

The different types of mosquito larval habitats identified in the three LGAs can be classified into two groups based on size, abundance, accessibility and permanence. The first group, which consists of gutters, hoof marks, mud brick pits and water spillage from boreholes and water supply taps, are smaller in size, more limited in numbers, and closer to human habitations. The potential of these group to be positive for larval of mosquito larvae is highly dependent on water use practices of members of the communities. The second group includes rice paddies, stream edges, ponds, and swamps. The origin and emergence of some of these larval habitats also are linked to human practices such as the digging of boreholes and dam development to drive irrigated rice farming. As observed in this study and in agreement with other studies in Tanzania and Kenya, land use practices including agricultural systems result in increased prescence of breeding sites, survival of the Anopheline mosquitoes and transmission of malaria [33, 34].

According to the W.H.O [35], the review of historical data in determining the feasibility of LSM must include information on the target vector populations, their ecology and the behavior of the adult mosquitoes and larvae within the specific area targeted for any LSM. In Kebbi State, there is a dearth of “up-to-date” information on malaria vector species, unlike other areas where routine data collections have taken place for nearly a decade. Longitudinal surveillance began in Kebbi State in July 2021 as part of baseline data collection activities to assess the impact of the new types of ITN (dual active ingredient) distributed statewide in November 2022 [13].

The variation in the diversity of mosquito species across Nigeria is consistent with other studies reporting the influence of different ecological zones on Anopheles species distribution [36] and further emphasizes the influence of ecological conditions as well as human activities on the abundance and distribution of mosquito species across the different geographical locations in Nigeria Irikannu et al. [37]. From entomological collections conducted in Kebbi State thus far, An. gambiae remains the predominant malaria vector species identified despite the variety of larval habitats [13].

Understanding the ecology of a vector species helps improve choice and planning of vector control interventions and enables effective surveillance and monitoring. For example, An. funestus is known to prefer breeding in shady permanent or semi-permanent water bodies, usually with floating or emergent vegetation such as that found in swamps, marshes and the edges of streams [36]. Highlighting, the role of predominant breeding habitats such as rain pools and rice paddies observed in Kebbi State points to the eco-preference of An. gambiae These larval habitats supporting high productivity of An. gambiae in Kebbi State are findable, agreeing with one of the WHO criteria for recommending LSM [35], whereas the identification of An. funestus that breeds in less accessible places would have made the choice of LSM less practical in Kebbi State.

Studies have reported that larval habitats near human habitation play a key role in the proliferation of Anopheles species [37, 38], particularly in the dry season, when supported by human activity such as irrigation farming [39]. This study also witnessed this in the communities studied. The rain pools and rice fields found within 100 m of inhabited houses constituted over 50% and 20%, respectively, of the larval habitats in Argungu LGA. Even though the rice paddies are 20% of the larval habitats, they constitute about 98% of the total surface area responsible for Anopheles larval productivity in Argungu. Despite reports of mosquitoes being able to travel more than 1.5 km from larval habitats to human dwellings [40], proximity of human habitation around irrigated crop irrigation systems in Ghana, Ethiopia, Kenya and Tanzania have been linked to an increased risk of malaria [41–43]. The significant occurrence of larval habitats even within 100 m helps explain the high exposure of residents to large populations of An. gambiae that carry malaria parasites.

This study has shown that the existence of larval habitats depends both on natural events like rainfall and flooding, and on human activities. For example, entomological data show that certain mosquito populations now stay abundant during the dry season, which in the three study LGAs is supported primarily though dry season irrigation of rice fields. Previous reports have indicated that communities near irrigated rice fields are exposed to greater mosquito abundance and greater malaria risk [14, 44]. A recent study in Cote d’Ivoire demonstrated that the combined use of ITNs with bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis var israelensis (Bti) larvicide significantly reduced the incidence of malaria and entomological inoculation rates in high transmission setting [45]. As such, in Kebbi State, where the malaria burden is very high, and more broadly throughout Nigeria, the effect of rice farming must be considered when making decisions about malaria vector control interventions like LSM. Challenges faced during this study ranged from coming up with a strong study design to support an unbiased data collection reflective of the community and changing topography. Again fluctuations in the weather elements such as rainfall or excessive could disrupt data collection and suspension of work for days until the fooding receeds. Data collection on the field is labour and time intensive requiring personnel training and constant supervision of field to collect the relevant data.

Conclusion

The entomological and human behavior data show that Kebbi State produces significantly large An. gambiae populations, with people exposed during peak biting times. The contributions of rain and agricultural irrigation, along with other human activities, on the proliferation of diverse larval habitats was observed via the LSM habitat survey conducted in the three selected LGAs in Kebbi State. LSM through source reduction and/or larviciding is required in these high malaria burden LGAs to make the breeding sites unsuitable for mosquito larval development, with particular emphasis on dry season irrigated fields. Since most areas within study areas are of low socioeconomic status,, members of the local community and rice farmer associations can be recruited, trained and supervised on monitoring entomological indicators of potential larval habitats and how to source reduce or treat with larvicides when these sites become active [46, 47]. Effective vector control requires a multisectoral and multilevel collaboration of various government agencies, including but not limited to the LGA administration, state- and federal-level ministries of Health, Environment, Agriculture, Water Resources and Town Planning and regulatory agencies for import and use of larvicides in Nigeria. The consistently high entomology indices measured protection gaps and productive and abundant larval habitats situated near human habitations year-round indicate the need for complementary vector control interventions, such as LSM, in Kebbi State.
