Adherence to the dietary inflammatory index and dietary approaches to stop hypertension and risk of gestational diabetes: a case-control study
Authors: Amirabbas Moarefian, Shervin Kazeminejad, Masumeh Gharang, Anahita Mansoori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough multiple studies have explored the connection between dietary patterns and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) risk in Iran, there are limited and inconsistent results on the DASH diet's relationship, especially with attention to diet-related inflammation. Given the high prevalence of GDM and the limited number of studies in the Middle East, this study aimed to investigate the link between the DASH diet, Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), and GDM among Iranian women.MethodsThis case-control study, conducted in healthcare facilities in Abadan, Iran, included 138 pregnant women with GDM and 136 healthy controls. GDM was diagnosed based on a 2-hour 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test conducted between 24 to 28 weeks of gestation. The FFQ with 147 items was used to assess the usual food intake of all women during the year before pregnancy and DASH/DII scores were calculated.ResultsThe mean age of the case and control group was 30.28 ± 5.01 and 29.07 ± 5.57 years respectively. In the fully adjusted model, participants in the first tertile of DII compared to the third tertile had a lower GDM risk (OR: 0.52; 95% CI: 0.27, 0.98; P = 0.04). Meanwhile, in the fully adjusted model, those in the third tertile of the DASH diet had a lower risk of GDM than those in the first tertile (OR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.19, 0.69; P = 0.002).ConclusionsHigher adherence to the DASH diet and lower intake of a pro-inflammatory diet are associated with decreased risk of GDM.Keywords: Dietary inflammatory index, Dietary approaches to stop hypertension, Gestational diabetes mellitus, Case-control study

Introduction

The term “gestational diabetes mellitus” (GDM) refers to impaired glucose tolerance that is identified or initiated during the second or third trimesters of pregnancy [1]. Compared to the global standardized prevalence of GDM, which is 14% this rate varies from 1.3 to 18.8% in Iranian pregnant women [2, 3]. GDM is a significant public health concern because it raises the risk of numerous short and long-term complications for both the mother and the child, such as impaired glucose metabolism, obesity, cardiovascular disease, and infectious problems [4–6]. Consequently, GDM prevention and therapy are receiving more attention in clinical practice. While the exact cause of GDM is still unknown, several risk factors have been identified, including age, ethnicity, overweight or obesity, family history of diabetes, and a history of GDM [7, 8]. Additionally, there is evidence that dietary patterns before and throughout pregnancy are linked to the risk of GDM [9].

There is compelling evidence that some dietary components affect both inflammatory processes [10–12] and the incidence of GDM [13, 14]. Moreover, specific dietary patterns, such as the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), may influence the risk of developing GDM. Originally designed to lower blood pressure [15], the DASH diet is a low-glycemic index (GI), an energy-dense eating plan that has also demonstrated benefits in managing type 2 diabetes [16] and metabolic syndrome [17]. Its high content of dietary fiber, phytoestrogens, and isoflavones, along with its low GI [16, 17], may help individuals with GDM improve their metabolic profile. Additionally, the DASH diet has been shown to reduce insulin resistance [18] and may modulate the risk of GDM by affecting molecular pathways involved in inflammation [19]. Shivappa et al. have developed the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) in accordance with the literature to capture the total inflammatory potential of a diet [20]. Compared to dietary consumption of individual foods or nutrients, DII has been demonstrated to be a reasonable and meaningful tool for quantifying the overall inflammatory potential of diet and an excellent method for examining the association between diet and diseases. The link between the DII and dietary patterns such as the DASH diet, as well as the risk of GDM, has been investigated in several studies, though findings have been inconsistent. The DII has been correlated with an increased risk of GDM in some studies [21–23] whereas a study conducted in Iran reported no significant relationship [24]. Regarding the DASH diet, a prospective analysis involving 1,887 women demonstrated an association between adherence to the DASH diet and a reduced risk of GDM [25]. Furthermore, a case-control study in Iran linked the DASH diet to a decreased risk of GDM among Iranian women [26]. Conversely, a cross-sectional study of 284 women in Australia found no association between GDM and the DASH diet [27]. While several studies have examined the association between dietary patterns and the risk of GDM within the Iranian population [24, 26, 28–30], there are few and inconsistent studies exploring the relationship between the DASH diet, the inflammatory potential as measured by the DII, and the risk of GDM. Given the high prevalence of GDM in the Middle East, including Iran, and the limited research investigating the potential relationship between the DASH diet, the DII, and GDM in Iran, the objective of this study was to evaluate this relationship among Iranian women.

Methods

Study design and population

This case-control study, which was carried out within healthcare facilities located in Abadan, situated in the southwestern region of Iran, involved 138 pregnant women who had recently been diagnosed with GDM alongside 136 healthy control subjects. Pregnant women with GDM who were between the ages of 18 and 40, had no communication issues, and were singleton pregnant were included. GDM screening was performed utilizing a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), which was administered between the 24th and 28th weeks of gestation in alignment with the guidelines set forth by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) [31]. The control group was comprised of healthy pregnant women who obtained negative results for GDM during the 24 to 28 weeks of pregnancy. Those with cardiovascular diseases, cancer, type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus before pregnancy, a history of GDM in a previous pregnancy, a stillbirth, or twin pregnancies were excluded. Additionally, those who reported dietary intake below 800 or above 4200 kcal per day, or who did not provide sufficient information about their dietary consumption or health status, were also excluded [26]. Written consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences (IR.AJUMS.REC.1403.532). The present study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for case-control studies.

Outcome ascertainment

The diagnosis of GDM was confirmed when any of the following glucose values were reached during the 2-hour OGTT with 75-g glucose load: fasting ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour post-OGTT ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour post-OGTT ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [31].

Assessment of dietary intake

A Persian-validated version of the semi-quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) with 147 items was used to assess the usual food intake of all women during the one-year period prior to their pregnancy, evaluated at 24 to 28 gestational weeks [32]. This FFQ is a valid and reliable tool for assessing nutritional intakes in Iranian adults, according to previous research [33]. All participants were given instructions by a certified dietitian on how to complete the FFQ, which asks about the frequency and amount of food items consumed over the preceding year. The portion sizes reported by participants were then converted into grams per day using standard household measurements. Ultimately, the Nutritionist IV software was utilized to input the gram value of every food item and derive the overall daily. FFQ-derived dietary data was used to calculate DII and DASH diet scores for all participants.

Calculation of DII

We employed the methodology proposed by Shivappa et al. to build the DII score [20]. They identified that 45 distinct nutrients and food items exert an influence on the concentrations of various inflammatory and anti-inflammatory biomarkers, including interleukin − 6, interleukin-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), C-reactive protein, interleukin − 10, and interleukin-4. The dietary intakes in our study were adjusted for energy using the residual method [34]. Total energy, carbohydrate, fat, protein, total dietary fiber, cholesterol, trans fatty acids, omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pyridoxine, folic acid, cobalamin, vitamin A, C, D, E, β-carotene, zinc, iron, selenium, magnesium, caffeine, garlic, and onion were among the 30 food items for which the DII score was determined. A percentile score was derived by converting the z-score, which was subsequently multiplied by its corresponding overall food parameter score. Finally, the DII score was calculated by summing all food parameter-specific DII scores. A heightened DII score indicated an increased inflammatory potential of the dietary pattern.

Calculation of DASH diet pattern

We used the DASH diet score based on eight dietary components, such as a high intake of fruits, vegetables, dairy products, nuts and legumes, alongside a low intake of grains, sodium, sugary drinks and sweets, and red or processed meats, to evaluate the adherence of participants to the DASH diet [35]. For the purposes of this analysis, we classified whole and refined grains as a singular category of grains. The inadequate intake of whole grains within the Iranian population (< 10 g/day) [36] led us to disregard total grain consumption as a healthy dietary option, despite the fact that whole grains were originally allocated a high score in the initial DASH diet scoring. First, we used the residual technique to calculate energy-adjusted intakes of these foods and nutrients [34]. After that, the participants were divided into deciles according to their energy-adjusted intakes. For fruits, vegetables, dairy products, legumes and nuts, individuals in the top decile scored ten, while those in the bottom decile scored one. On the other hand, those in the lowest decile scored ten, while those in the top decile scored one for total grains, red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages and sweets, and sodium. Lastly, each participant’s DASH diet score was calculated by adding up the scores for each item. As a result, participants’ scores ranged from 8 to 80.

Assessment of other variables

The individuals’ ages, medical histories, levels of education, socioeconomic status, Passive smoking, family history of GDM, and supplement use were all recorded by trained interviewers. To determine the levels of physical activity (PA), we used the globally recognized and validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [37]. At the beginning of the study, weight was measured using a digital scale, without shoes and with light clothing, to the nearest 0.5 kg. In order to measure the participants’ height to the nearest 0.5 cm, participants were instructed to stand barefoot with their shoulders touching the wall. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of the height in meters.

Statistical analyses

We first divided the DASH diet and DII scores into tertiles, then grouped the study participants by these tertiles. Second, an analysis was conducted to compare participants’ characteristics across tertiles of DASH diet and DII score. Using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and chi-squared tests, respectively, we examined the values of continuous and categorical variables across DASH diet and DII scores. The participants’ dietary intakes across DASH/DII tertiles were assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Energy intakes were adjusted for age while other dietary intakes were adjusted for age and energy intake. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to investigate the relationship between the incidence of GDM and the tertiles of DII and DASH diet. Both crude and adjusted models were employed to report the odds ratio (OR) alongside 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The following variables were considered in the multivariable-adjusted analyses across various models: age, family history of GDM, pre-pregnancy BMI, Passive smoking, socioeconomic status, utilization of multivitamins during the current pregnancy, levels of PA, and total energy intake. By treating DII and DASH tertiles as ordinal variables in the logistic regression models, the overall trend of the odds ratio across rising tertiles was determined. In all models, the first tertile of the DASH diet score (representing the lowest adherence) and the third tertile of the DII score (representing the highest inflammatory potential) were considered the reference categories. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22. P-values were considered significant if they were less than 0.05.

Multivariable-adjusted odds ratios for GDM across tertiles of dietary patterns are shown in Table 4. Both dietary pattern adherence scores were significantly and inversely related to the risk of GDM. In the crude model of the DII, participants in the first tertile, compared with those in the third tertile, had a lower risk of GDM (OR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.37, 1.18; P = 0.16). This significant relationship persisted even after full adjustment (OR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.19, 0.69; P = 0.002).

Discussion

After accounting for potential confounding variables, our results in this Iranian case-control study show that women who had lower DII scores and higher adherence to the DASH diet were at lower odds of GDM, supporting the hypothesis that eating a lower pro-inflammatory diet and following a healthy dietary pattern like DASH are associated with lower GDM risk. However, in our analysis, a significant association between the DII score and the outcome was observed in only one tertile, while the P-trend across tertiles did not reach statistical significance. This finding suggests that the relationship between DII scores and the outcome may not be linear, potentially indicating a threshold effect or a non-monotonic pattern of association. Several factors could contribute to this observation. For instance, the lack of a significant trend might reflect variability in the inflammatory potential of diets within tertiles, where only a specific range of DII scores (captured in the significant tertile) exerts a measurable impact on the outcome. Alternatively, unmeasured confounders, such as individual differences in metabolism, gut microbiota, or lifestyle factors, might modulate the effect of DII across the tertiles, obscuring a consistent trend. Additionally, the sample size or the categorization into tertiles may have limited the statistical power to detect a linear trend. These findings underscore the complexity of dietary inflammation’s role in health outcomes and suggest the need for further investigation, potentially using alternative analytical approaches such as spline regression to explore non-linear relationships or larger cohorts to enhance precision.

In line with our study, in a hospital-based case-control study of Iranian women, Shivappa et al. [22] found that people with more pro-inflammatory diets had higher odds of developing GDM than people with more anti-inflammatory diets. Another study, which involved 2639 eligible women from the Tongji Maternal and Child Health Cohort, discovered that a higher risk of GDM was linked to a more pro-inflammatory diet, especially for pregnant women who were obese or overweight before becoming pregnant [21]. Furthermore, findings of two recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrate the same associations [38, 39]. However, a prospective cohort study involving 812 pregnant Iranian women revealed no such correlations [24]. For the DASH diet consistent with our findings, a prospective study of 1887 women showed a correlation between following the DASH diet and a lower incidence of GDM [25]. Additionally, a case-control study found that Iranian women who followed a DASH diet had lower odds of GDM [26]. Also, according to a prior randomized controlled trial, women with GDM who followed the DASH diet for four weeks had better pregnancy outcomes [40]. In contrast, there was no correlation between GDM and the DASH diet in a cross-sectional study of 284 women in Australia [27]. The disparities in results between studies were probably caused by variations in sample size, adjusted for various variables, and study methodology (cross-sectional, prospective cohort, and case-control). Additionally, the components used to calculate DII and DASH diet differ in a few ways. We calculated DII in the current study using 30 dietary factors from the FFQ. Conversely, other studies may incorporate either a greater or smaller number of food parameters. It is also important to note that while certain research, such as that conducted by Sen et al. [41], determined the DII using the average dietary intakes of mothers assessed during the first and second trimesters, our study assessed dietary intake at gestational weeks 24–28, where participants were asked to report their usual dietary intake for the one-year period prior to their pregnancy. Regarding the DASH diet, we assigned a negative score to the grains category in the DASH scoring system, as a majority of Iranian households predominantly utilize refined grains [36], however, in an Australian study, the original DASH diet was employed and seen null results [27].

In the context of GDM development, metabolic inflammation may serve a pivotal role in the manifestation of insulin resistance and the impairment of glucose tolerance [42]. Several hypotheses have been proposed to elucidate how a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern may elevate the likelihood of GDM onset. Firstly, the development of chronic inflammation may be influenced by certain dietary components. For example, a low-energy diet that emphasizes frequent consumption of nuts, whole grains, and vegetable protein can offer a rich source of dietary fiber, magnesium, and unsaturated fatty acids while also having a lower inflammatory and insulinemic potential [43]. It is possible that these dietary components could help prevent GDM by attenuating insulin resistance and inflammatory markers [44]. Additionally, prior research has suggested that the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (Ahr) natural ligands present in cruciferous vegetables have anti-inflammatory characteristics [45]. Reduced inflammation results from AhR activation, which upregulates anti-inflammatory mediators and downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines [46]. An increase in inflammatory cytokine levels from a pro-inflammatory diet can result in insulin resistance, pancreatic islet β-cell dysfunction, structural damage, and other factors that can lead to the development of GDM [47, 48]. Furthermore, dietary changes that affect body weight, BMI, and fat mass might intensify the inflammatory response [49], which raises the risk of GDM via these interconnected physiological processes. Another possible mechanism is the impact of the gut microbiome. A diet rich in fruits and vegetables, which are abundant in dietary fiber and polyphenols, can enhance glucose homeostasis by modulating the composition and abundance of gut microbiota [50, 51]. For instance, Zheng et al.‘s investigation into the connection between the DII and gut microbiota revealed a clear connection between alterations in the gut microbiota’s composition and inflammation caused by diet [52]. Insulin function and other aspects of metabolism are significantly influenced by the gut microbiota. A disruption in the gut microbiota’s equilibrium can result in decreased insulin receptor sensitivity and elevated insulin resistance, both of which contribute to the development of GDM [53]. On the other hand, the DASH diet is a plant-based, healthful pattern that emphasizes the consumption of fruits, vegetables, legumes, fibers, seeds, nuts, whole grains, low-fat dairy products, fish, and poultry along with a reduction in fats, red meat, sugary drinks, sweets, which has anti-inflammatory properties [54]. More than the DII mechanisms that have been proposed, which may also apply to the DASH diet, a lower dietary glycemic index brought on by a greater intake of complex carbohydrates could represent an additional potential mechanism that mediates the association between the DASH diet and GDM [55]. Additionally, Anue et al. verified that cheese and low-fat dairy products have a preventive impact against the incidence of type 2 diabetes [56]. Dairy products appear to have a beneficial effect since they may be a source of calcium [57]. Since a study showed high dietary calcium was linked to a lower incidence of GDM [58]. Moreover, other micronutrients, like magnesium, may also play a part in enhancing insulin sensitivity and regulating glucose metabolism. A possible association between dietary magnesium and the risk of type 2 diabetes was supported by evidence from observational studies [59]. According to various studies, following the DASH diet can help improve endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation [60–62]; the three important underlying mechanisms linked to the development of insulin resistance and GDM [63].

The majority of the evidence concerning the association between dietary patterns and the risk of GDM is predominantly sourced from Western nations, while there exists limited data from countries situated in the Middle East. To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study to examine the association between GDM risk and the DII score and the DASH diet in a sample of pregnant women in southeast Iran. However, a number of limitations must be considered when interpreting our findings. Our study’s primary weakness is its case-control design, which forbids us from inferring a causal link. Therefore, additional prospective research is required to validate our results. Moreover, adherent selection and recall biases should also be considered in this case-control study, since mothers were asked to report the food intake during the year prior to pregnancy. As with all epidemiological studies, misclassification of study participants due to the use of FFQ is unavoidable. The FFQ employed in our study had been validated within a non-pregnant population due to the absence of a pregnancy-specific FFQ in Iran. However, this questionnaire has been utilized in all other studies involving pregnant women in the country, and it appears to yield valid assessments of dietary intake during pregnancy, as it predicts various pregnancy-related outcomes. Additionally, IPAQ designed for short-term physical activity assessment, was used in this study. This presents a limitation as the study focused on long-term activity patterns. Despite controlling for several confounding variables, we cannot exclude the effect of residual confounders such as prior medication history. Another limitation is that only 30 of the possible 45 food parameters were available for DII calculation. The generalizability of study findings might be weakening with this methodological choice.

Conclusion

This case-control study indicated that lower intake of a pro-inflammatory diet and closer adherence to DASH diet before pregnancy may reduce the probability of developing GDM in a small sample of Iranian mothers. This kind of research helps us better understand the relationships between diet and diseases in particular parts of the world where social factors and dietary patterns may be different. By identifying the potential influence of dietary patterns on GDM risk, policymakers, and healthcare providers may think about taking proactive steps to encourage healthier eating habits during pregnancy. This could potentially reduce the incidence of GDM and also offer a chance to reduce healthcare costs and challenges associated with the healthcare system. In the future, more studies can be conducted, including longitudinal prospective studies with large samples and more accurate dietary evaluations during multiple weeks of pregnancy to examine this association.
