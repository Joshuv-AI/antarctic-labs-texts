From symptoms to standards: Integrating guidelines, validated tools, and dietary strategies in the care of Arabic-speaking patients with IBS and pelvic floor disorders
Authors: Mohammed A. Aljebreen, Daniel Sadowski, Adriana Lazarescu
Published: 2026

Disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) and pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) share several inconvenient truths. They are highly prevalent, frequently underdiagnosed, costly to health systems, and deeply disruptive to patient’s lives, yet they remain troubled by overlapping symptoms, variable diagnostic approaches, and a chronic shortage of culturally adapted, region-specific evidence. In this issue of the Saudi Journal of Gastroenterology, three complementary contributions begin to close some of these gaps. Alzahrani and colleagues present the first Saudi consensus statements on the management of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS);[1] Ajabnoor and Tashkandi apply a structured food trigger score to Saudi adults with IBS, identifying region-specific dietary culprits;[2] and Alharbi and colleagues validate the Arabic version of the initial measurement of patient-reported pelvic floor complaints tool (IMPACT) Bowel Function Short Form for assessing PFD symptoms.[3] Read in isolation, each paper makes a useful contribution. Read together, they sketch the outline of a more coherent, patient-centered model of care for a population whose needs have been served too long by extrapolation from Western data.

A framework for diagnosis and management

The Saudi consensus statements on IBS,[1] developed by the Saudi Neurogastroenterology and Motility Group under the support of the Saudi Gastroenterology Association and in collaboration with the European Society of Neurogastroenterology and Motility, are a welcome milestone. Using a modified Delphi methodology, the panel generated 29 statements and two clinical algorithms covering definitions, Rome IV classification, diagnostic workup in both primary and secondary care, and stepwise pharmacological and non-pharmacological therapy. Notably, the document endorses a positive, symptom-based diagnosis supported by careful targeted investigations rather than a diagnosis of exclusion—an evidence-based shift that should reduce unnecessary colonoscopies and imaging in young patients without alarm features.

Equally important is what the consensus emphasizes beyond pharmacology. Dietary modification, including traditional dietary advice, soluble fiber, and a supervised low-FODMAP diet, is positioned as a cornerstone of management. Gut–brain neuromodulators and brain–gut behavioral therapies are recognized as effective second-line and adjunctive options for refractory or pain-predominant disease. This biopsychosocial framing reflects the modern understanding of IBS as a disorder of gut–brain interaction and aligns Saudi practice with the most recent international guidance. The reported regional prevalence of IBS in Saudi Arabia—ranging from roughly 15% to 45% across studies, with a recent pooled estimate of 21%[1]—makes this framework not only academic but also clinically urgent.

Translating diet from principle to practice

If the consensus document tells clinicians what to do about diet, the work by Ajabnoor and Tashkandi[2] helps explain how to do it in a Saudi context. Their cross-sectional study of 98 adults with Rome IV–defined IBS applied a 30-item food trigger score across four food groups, demonstrating excellent internal reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.935). The most frequently reported triggers—spicy foods (64.4%), legumes (62.0%), cabbage (48.2%), milk (47.7%), and fried foods (43.3%)—are deeply part of everyday Saudi and broader Middle Eastern cuisine. This is precisely the problem with importing dietary strategies wholesale: a low-FODMAP protocol designed around Western staples can feel impossible to apply when legumes are central to traditional meals and dairy is a daily fixture.

Two findings deserve special attention. First, the regression analyses linked higher trigger scores to symptom severity, older age, chronic comorbidity, and employment status, reinforcing the consensus view that dietary management must be individualized rather than prescriptive. Second, dairy and caffeinated beverages emerged as the top symptom-provoking categories, an observation that complicates the common assumption that wheat and FODMAP-rich produce dominant Arab patients’ trigger profiles. A validated, culturally tailored food trigger score has clear utility for primary care and dietetic clinics: it offers a structured, less restrictive alternative to elimination diets that risk nutritional inadequacy and disordered eating patterns, and it can help clinicians focus counseling on the foods that matter for an individual patient.

Measuring what patients experience

The third piece of the puzzle is measurement. Bowel-related symptoms span colorectal surgery, gynecology, urology, and pelvic floor rehabilitation, and historically each specialty has used its own instruments. The International PFDs Consortium developed the IMPACT to address this fragmentation.[3] Until now, however, no Arabic version of the IMPACT bowel function short form has been validated—a substantial omission for one of the world’s most widely spoken languages.

Alharbi and colleagues[3] address this gap rigorously. In 205 Arabic-speaking adults with clinically diagnosed PFD, the Arabic IMPACT bowel function short form demonstrated strong face and content validity, excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.922), and a coherent 11-factor structure accounting for 80% of the variance. The instrument also discriminated meaningfully between clinical subgroups: scores were significantly higher among patients with chronic disease, asthma, neurological disorders, and Bristol Stool Form Type 1 stools. Two items—stool type and urgency frequency—were removed after item analysis because they correlated weakly with overall symptom burden, a thoughtful refinement that improves construct specificity without compromising reliability.

A validated patient reported outcomes (PRO) measure is not a luxury. In populations where stigma and cultural reticence often inhibit disclosure of bowel and pelvic symptoms, a standardized, self-administered tool can get information that direct questioning may miss. It also enables longitudinal monitoring, supports multidisciplinary communication, and provides a common metric for clinical research conducted in the region.

An integrated approach

Taken together, these three papers illustrate how guidelines, validated assessment tools, and culturally informed dietary strategies can reinforce one another. A consensus framework is only as effective as the instruments clinicians use to apply it. A PRO tool is only as useful as the management pathway it informs; and dietary advice is only as actionable as the cultural context permits. The alignment here is striking. The consensus recommends symptom-based diagnosis supported by limited targeted testing, individualized dietary intervention, and a tiered pharmacological and behavioral strategy. The Arabic IMPACT short form provides a reliable means to capture bowel-related symptom burden in routine practice. The food trigger score offers a practical bridge between general dietary recommendations and the specific foods most likely to provoke symptoms in Saudi patients.

Several priorities for the next phase of work emerge. First, both the IMPACT short form and the food trigger score need further validation in larger, geographically diverse samples, including test–retest reliability and responsiveness to intervention. Single-center recruitment and over-representation of certain regions and of women, while consistent with the epidemiology of IBS and PFD, limit generalizability. Second, the relationship between bowel-related PFD symptoms and IBS subtypes deserves dedicated study; the overlap is clinically obvious but poorly characterized in Arab populations. Third, implementation research is needed to determine how these tools perform in primary care, where most patients with IBS and PFD are first seen, and whether structured use influences referral patterns, investigation rates, and patient outcomes. Finally, the consensus statements themselves will require periodic revision as Rome V approaches in 2026 and as new agents, including secretagogues and gut–brain neuromodulators, become more widely available in the region.

Patients with IBS and PFD have waited a long time for care pathways built on regional evidence rather than borrowed assumptions. The three studies in this issue do not solve every problem, but they shift the field meaningfully forward: a Saudi consensus that codifies a positive, biopsychosocial approach to IBS; a food trigger score that grounds dietary management in local culinary reality; and a validated Arabic PRO tool that finally allows bowel-related pelvic floor symptoms to be measured in patients’ own language. The challenge now is integration of these resources into clinical workflows, training programs, and research agendas so that the next generation of Arabic-speaking patients with bowel and pelvic floor symptoms experiences care that is at once evidence-based, standardized, and recognizably their own.
