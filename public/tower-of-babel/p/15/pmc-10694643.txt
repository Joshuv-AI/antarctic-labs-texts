Empyema necessitans (EN) as a rare complication of tuberculosis – A case report
Authors: Manahil Jamil, Muzna Mehmood, Fraz Abid Janjua, Faiza Ahmad, Arooj Fatima Atiq
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmpyema necessitans (EN) is an uncommon condition where an intrathoracic empyema extends into surrounding extra-thoracic tissues. This case report presents a rare instance of tuberculous EN in an immunocompetent individual.Presentation of caseWe present a case of a healthy young male with complaints of weight loss and a chest wall swelling, initially treated as a subcutaneous abscess. He had a history of TB contact and initial laboratory tests showed elevated CRP and ESR, with no bacterial growth on initial culture from FNAC sample. Subsequent imaging revealed the presence of pleural empyema. Following surgical intervention, a connection between fluid collection outside the thoracic wall and the pleural cavity was identified. The patient was further treated with anti-tuberculous treatment.DiscussionEN, rare extrapulmonary complication of tuberculosis, is challenging to diagnose due to nonspecific symptoms and paucibacillary nature of extrapulmonary TB. Imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosis. Accurate diagnosis requires a combination of clinical insight, imaging, and laboratory tests. Introduction

Empyema necessitans (EN) is a clinical condition where an intrathoracic empyema breaks through the parietal pleura and extends along the path of least resistance into surrounding extra-thoracic soft tissues [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The most prevalent cause of empyema is untreated parapneumonic effusion [5]. These effusions accumulate bacteria and fibrinous debris leading to the accumulation of pus in the pleural space. If not treated timely, the resulting pressure and inflammation within the pleural cavity can dissect the pleura, giving rise to a fistula. This can lead to the collection of pus beneath the skin, which manifests as swelling on the chest wall [1,6].

Actinomyces spp., Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus are the most frequent causative agents for EN, with Mycobacterium tuberculosis being the prevailing agent [7]. This condition frequently affects patients from endemic regions and can result in grave consequences for the individual in question. Treatment of this disease typically involves a combination of surgical and medical interventions [8]. Here, we report a rare case of tuberculous EN in a healthy immunocompetent 30-year-old male presenting with ambiguous history and minimal systemic symptoms, in community healthcare setting. He was treated with tube thoracostomy and anti-tuberculous medications.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old immunocompetent male with no significant past medical and surgical history, presented in the outpatient department with persistent, progressive right lateral thoracic swelling for the last 4 months which was associated with mild pain on palpation. He denied symptoms of cough, hemoptysis, shortness of breath, orthopnea, fever, recent travel, insect bite, or sick contacts within the last 6 months. However, he did report a weight loss of approximately 8 kg in the last three months, reporting a BMI of 18.7. He was otherwise, in good health working as a physical laborer at a construction site and had a history of smoking equivalent to 3 pack years. Notably, there existed a family history of tuberculosis (TB); specifically, the patient's father, who resided with the patient, had successfully recovered from pulmonary TB two years earlier. Additionally, it should be noted that he had been taking antibiotics prior to the current presentation, which had been prescribed by a private physician from his local area.

Physical examination revealed a heterogeneous, soft, fixed, and fluctuant swelling measuring 4 × 5 cm at the level of the 7th intercostal space on the right lateral chest wall (Fig. 1). The swelling exhibited mild tenderness, along with local signs of erythema and purulent discharge. Pulmonary auscultation indicated diminished breath sounds in the right lung's mid-to-base regions. However, no adenopathy was appreciated.

Fluctuant swelling (arrow) measuring 4 × 5 cm at the level of the Right 7th intercostal space.

Laboratory investigations were performed, and all parameters including Total Leukocytes count, Hemoglobin, platelets and Serum Creatinine fell within normal limits, except for erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) which were elevated at 76 mm/h and 39.5 mg/dl respectively. Subsequently, fine needle aspiration of the fluid was performed. The obtained Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology (FNAC) sample was then subjected to cytological examination and bacterial culture, and the patient was sent home with instructions to return for a follow-up visit after 7 days, with the results.

However, after an interval of 27 days, the patient reappeared at the Surgical outpatient department. During this visit, the patient presented with reports indicating no growth from the bacterial culture. Interestingly, there was a noticeable enlargement of the chest wall swelling. Furthermore, the White blood cell count showed a slight elevation, although it remained within the normal range, and no signs of leukocytosis were observed. Importantly, at this juncture, the patient's medical history remained unremarkable, and there were no respiratory symptoms indicative of pulmonary tuberculosis.

At this point, a chest X-ray (Fig. 2) was carried out, revealing pleural effusion in the right lung along with an elevated right hemidiaphragm. Consequently, a Chest CT scan (Figs. 3 & 4) was ordered which showed a fluid density collection that was appreciated in the right hemi thorax along the lateral chest wall, measuring about 6.0 cm in its maximum thickness, with passively collapsed underlying parenchyma and nodules exhibiting tree bud appearance in the bilateral upper lobes.

CT chest showing a fluid density collection in the right hemi thorax along the lateral chest wall, measuring about 6.0 cm in its maximum thickness with passively collapsed underlying parenchyma.

CT chest with dense fluid collection in right lung.

Based on the findings from the CT scan, the patient was admitted for surgical intervention. Subsequently, under sedation an incision was made over the swelling which revealed yellowish purulent material coming from the thoracic cavity through the intercoastal space revealing a connection between fluid collection outside the thoracic wall and the pleural cavity, thus diagnosing empyema necessitans (EN). The whole content was then drained and a thoracostomy tube with an underwater seal was then placed to drain the intrapleural empyema, and the wound was closed. A post-operative chest X-ray confirmed the correct placement of the chest tube. Additionally, a sample of the drained fluid was sent for culture and sensitivity testing, returning positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and indicating susceptibility to all anti-tuberculous agents. Further diagnostic measures included Ziehl-Neelsen staining, which was negative for acid-fast bacilli, and a Tuberculin skin test with an induration of >20 mm.

Post-operatively, the patient stayed hospitalized for 5 days and was subsequently discharged after consultation with pulmonologists, who initiated a 6-month anti-tuberculous regimen comprising rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. Following a 7-day follow-up visit, a radiological imaging revealed reduced pleural effusion in the right pleural cavity. Subsequently, the thoracostomy tube was removed, and the patient was instructed to schedule another follow-up in two months' time. At 2 months follow-up, the incision site had fully healed and the patient was still on anti-tuberculous treatment. Continuing as an outpatient, the patient completed his anti-tuberculous treatment. Notably, the patient remained symptom-free throughout this period, as confirmed during his last outpatient visit.

Discussion

Tuberculosis, a highly contagious infection, ranks as the 9th leading cause of death among immunocompromised patients, with over 90% of fatalities occurring in developing countries [9]. Empyema necessitans (EN), a rare and unusual complication of extrapulmonary TB, arises when a parapneumonic effusion or untreated empyema thoracis creates a fistula allowing fluid to extend from the pleural cavity to subcutaneous tissues [[1], [2], [3], [4],10]. Although Mycobacterium tuberculosis has been found as the most frequent causative agent, other bacteria and some malignancies can also result in this complication [7,9].

The most common site is the anterior chest wall between the midclavicular and anterior axillary lines and between the second and sixth intercostal spaces. Other locations include the back, diaphragm, mediastinum, esophagus, bronchus, vertebral column, breast, retroperitoneum, pericardium, flank, or groin [2,11]. In our case, the patient reported swelling in the 7th intercostal space on the right lateral chest.

The paucibacillary nature of extrapulmonary tuberculosis often leads to delayed or overlooked diagnosis due to vague clinical symptoms and late-stage consequences [6,8,12]. This was the scenario in our case, where latent tuberculous infection presented as subcutaneous swelling in a young immunocompetent male, which was initially overlooked as a subcutaneous abscess and treated as such. Literature has also found reports describing EN in patients with primary TB infection as well as relapse [[13], [14], [15]]. Additionally, we have found a case by Gomes et al. reporting EN as a very late complication of pulmonary TB [4]. Hence, proper investigations for diagnosis of extrapulmonary tuberculosis are required, which necessitates the isolation of tuberculous bacteria from an appropriate sample. However, most tests' sensitivity remains low even after successful isolation, but isolation through TB culture is considered to be the gold standard [16].

Initially, the aspirated medium from our patient yielded no growth, likely influenced by the administration of antibiotics (as prescribed by a local doctor) prior to their initial presentation, a phenomenon similar to findings discussed in a study by Kim et al., which highlighted the challenge of obtaining a definitive diagnosis due to false-negative results in acid-fast bacilli, smear culture, fine needle aspiration, and polymerase chain reaction [17]. Similarly, Afsar et al.'s research revealed that although culture served as the reference test, merely 4.05 % of total specimens in their study tested positive for TB [18]. Correspondingly, in a study involving Pakistani patients, smear-negative patients were clinically diagnosed as TB-positive and were later confirmed to have TB [19].

Owing to initial reports showing elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), no changes were made to the patient's antibiotic prescription, which had been prescribed by the local doctor. He then, later presented in the outpatient department with increasing size of chest wall swelling. The swelling was reevaluated, and a Chest x-ray followed by Chest HRCT was performed. A chest HRCT scan is pathognomonic in diagnosis of EN, as it clearly shows the connection between pleural effusion and the extra pleural chest wall swelling [20] Additionally, this scan is proficient in detecting features like pleural thickening, calcification, and rib anomalies [21,22]. While bronchopleural fistula tracts can be visualized, they pose a challenge in their detection via CT [23]. Furthermore, a CT scan also provides information on the functional state of lung parenchyma [8,12].

Although rib destruction was not evident in our patient, characteristic features like a chest wall abscess, an extra pleural soft tissue mass, and collapsed lung parenchyma were identified. These findings align with common manifestations observed in EN cases [8]. The chest wall abscess was removed by surgical intervention, and the sample collected was sent for culture, which subsequently confirmed the diagnosis of tuberculous Empyema Necessitans (EN). The confirmation of our diagnosis was reinforced by a positive Tuberculin Skin Test (PPD), a significant tool even in cases of active disease where it might yield negative results [24]. Accordingly, CT scans along with cytological examinations of tissue and fluid play a crucial role in accurate detection and diagnosis [11].

The current case involves a patient with no underlying co-morbidities and was treated by a multidisciplinary approach involving surgical intervention and medical treatment with anti-tuberculous drugs. Similar approaches have demonstrated successful recovery in many comparable cases [6,12]. However, there have been instances where outcomes differed, such as a case in 2013, that reported a patient's death due to untreated intrapleural chronic empyema while only on anti-tuberculous treatment regime [4]. Conversely, there are reports of complete recovery, like that of a 33-year-old diabetic and hypertensive female solely treated with TB medication [25]. Thus, the answer to the correct method for recovery in tuberculous EN remains ambiguous. Nonetheless, literature review suggests higher rates of recovery when combining surgical intervention with anti-tuberculous medications.

Concerning our particular case, initially considered as a subcutaneous abscess, the main challenge was identifying the causative agent behind the chest wall swelling. Eventually, it was determined to be a latent tuberculous infection stemming from a history of TB contact two years prior. Notably, we identified a case where EN emerged as a late complication of a prior TB infection in an elderly patient [4]. This case however, is the first of its kind to report latent tuberculous infection due to TB contact history presenting as EN.

In summary, EN is a quite rare and possible complication of TB-related empyema in immunocompromised and immunocompetent populations, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality. This case serves as a reminder that extrapulmonary tuberculosis can present without respiratory and constitutional symptoms. Moreover, the presence of a chest wall swelling in a TB pandemic area, recent travel or residency in a TB endemic area, or history of TB contact should raise suspicion for tuberculous EN.

Conclusion

EN is a rare extrapulmonary complication of pulmonary TB, and can also be caused by other microorganisms. The diagnosis of this case was challenging since there were minimal signs of TB infection. And, it was difficult to differentiate it from tuberculous and nontuberculous effusion. The patient's history underscores the necessity of considering TB as a potential differential diagnosis for chest wall masses, particularly in TB endemic areas. Moreover, contact history with tuberculosis-infected persons should be kept in mind while diagnosing a patient with a latent tuberculous infection presenting as EN. Accurate diagnosis relies on a solid foundation of clinical insight. The most effective treatment approach for such instances involves a combination of surgical intervention and anti-tuberculous therapy.
