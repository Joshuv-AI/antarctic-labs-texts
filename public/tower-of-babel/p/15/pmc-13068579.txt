Mesenteric gastrointestinal stromal tumor mimicking a pelvic mass - the diagnostic value of vascular pedicle tracing and multiplanar reconstructions: A case report
Authors: Rihab Amara, Adnane Boubacar, Tarik Deflaoui, Hamid Ziani, Siham Nasri, Imane Kamaoui, Imane Skiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary mesenteric gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), also referred to as extra-gastrointestinal stromal tumors (EGISTs), are rare entities and represent a diagnostic challenge due to their extraintestinal location and predominantly exophytic growth pattern. On imaging, they may mimic pelvic, gynecologic, or retroperitoneal masses, leading to diagnostic delay or inappropriate management.We report the case of a 53-year-old woman presenting with chronic pelvic discomfort, in whom contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a well-circumscribed abdominopelvic mass initially suspected to be of pelvic origin. Careful multiplanar analysis and retrospective tracing of the tumor’s vascular pedicle demonstrated arterial supply from a third-order branch of the superior mesenteric artery, suggesting a mesenteric origin. Surgical exploration confirmed a well-encapsulated tumor arising from the small-bowel mesentery, without macroscopic or histological continuity with the bowel wall. Histopathological examination confirmed a low-risk spindle-cell GIST.This case highlights common imaging pitfalls associated with mesenteric GISTs and emphasizes the diagnostic value of vascular pedicle analysis and multiplanar reconstructions in improving preoperative localization and surgical planning.Keywords: Mesenteric gastrointestinal stromal tumor, Extra-gastrointestinal stromal tumor, Computed tomography, Abdominopelvic mass, Surgical radiologic correlation

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract, most frequently arising from the stomach and small intestine. In contrast, primary mesenteric GISTs often termed extra-gastrointestinal stromal tumors (EGISTs) are rare, accounting for less than 5% of all GISTs [1].

The concept of EGIST remains controversial. Strict diagnostic criteria require the absence of direct continuity with the gastrointestinal wall on gross surgical examination and histopathological analysis, distinguishing true primary mesenteric tumors from exophytic intestinal GISTs with secondary mesenteric extension [2,3].

From an imaging perspective, mesenteric GISTs pose a particular challenge. Their extraintestinal location and exophytic growth pattern may displace adjacent bowel loops and pelvic organs, obscuring the site of origin and leading to misinterpretation as gynecologic, retroperitoneal, or other soft-tissue tumors [4]. Accurate preoperative localization is essential to guide surgical strategy and avoid inappropriate management.

We report a case of a primary mesenteric GIST mimicking a pelvic mass, with emphasis on diagnostic imaging pitfalls and the added value of vascular pedicle tracing and multiplanar reconstructions.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with chronic pelvic discomfort evolving over several months. She denied gastrointestinal bleeding, bowel obstruction, weight loss, or constitutional symptoms.

Physical examination revealed a deep pelvic mass on bimanual palpation. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and routine biochemistry, were within normal limits.

Imaging findings

Contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT was performed using a multidetector CT scanner (Fig. 1). The acquisition protocol included arterial and portal venous phases following intravenous administration of iodinated contrast material (standard dose and injection rate). Images were reconstructed with thin slice thickness, and routine multiplanar reconstructions (axial, coronal, and sagittal) were generated. Curved planar reformations were retrospectively performed to assess vascular anatomy.

(A) Coronal contrast-enhanced CT image, (B) sagittal contrast-enhanced CT image, and (C) axial contrast-enhanced CT image (portal venous phase) showing a well-circumscribed, heterogeneous abdominopelvic mass measuring 88 × 63 mm. The lesion displaces adjacent pelvic organs without evidence of direct invasion.

CT demonstrated a well-circumscribed, heterogeneous soft-tissue mass measuring 88 × 63 × 60 mm, located predominantly in the pelvis. The lesion showed heterogeneous enhancement with internal areas suggestive of necrosis. It displaced adjacent structures, including the bladder, sigmoid colon, and posterior uterine wall, without signs of direct invasion. No bowel wall thickening, mucosal abnormality, or pathological lymphadenopathy was identified.

On initial interpretation, the lesion was suspected to be of pelvic or colonic origin due to its location. However, retrospective multiplanar analysis demonstrated a vascular pedicle arising from a third-order branch of the superior mesenteric artery, directly supplying the tumor. This vascular pattern strongly suggested a mesenteric origin rather than a gynecologic or retroperitoneal mass.

18F-FDG PET-CT was not performed at initial staging. Contrast-enhanced CT showed no evidence of metastatic disease, and subsequent histopathological analysis demonstrated low-risk features. According to current clinical practice guidelines, PET-CT is not routinely indicated for the initial assessment of localized, low-risk GISTs and is mainly reserved for equivocal cases or evaluation of response to tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy.

The identification of a mesenteric arterial vascular pedicle was the key imaging feature favoring the diagnosis of a mesenteric GIST.

Management and outcome

Surgical exploration (Fig. 2) revealed a well-encapsulated tumor arising from the small-bowel mesentery, located approximately 280 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz and 40 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction. There was no macroscopic continuity with the bowel wall.

Intraoperative view showing a well-encapsulated mesenteric tumor located 280 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz.

A segmental small-bowel resection with complete tumor excision was performed (Fig. 3), achieving R0 margins. No intraoperative tumor rupture, spillage, or mesenteric lymphadenopathy was observed.

Surgical specimen demonstrating segmental small bowel resection with centrally located mesenteric tumor.

Histopathological examination demonstrated a spindle-cell GIST with low cellular atypia. Immunohistochemistry was positive for CD117 (KIT) and DOG-1. The mitotic index was ≤5 mitoses per 5 mm². No tumor necrosis was identified. The Ki-67 proliferation index was low. Histological examination confirmed the absence of mucosal or bowel wall involvement, supporting the diagnosis of a true primary mesenteric EGIST. Molecular analysis was not performed.

According to modified NIH/AFIP risk stratification, the tumor was classified as low risk. No adjuvant therapy was initiated. Follow-up CT performed 12 months postoperatively showed no evidence of local recurrence or metastatic disease.

Discussion

Diagnostic challenges and imaging pitfalls

Mesenteric GISTs frequently exhibit an exophytic growth pattern that displaces adjacent bowel loops and pelvic organs, leading to ambiguity regarding their site of origin [4,5]. The absence of mucosal involvement further contributes to diagnostic difficulty and may result in underestimation of digestive origin [6].

Vascular pedicle analysis

Tracing the tumor’s feeding vessels to branches of the superior mesenteric artery represents a crucial diagnostic clue. Multiphase contrast-enhanced CT, combined with multiplanar and curved planar reconstructions, facilitates identification of mesenteric arterial pedicles and helps differentiate mesenteric GISTs from retroperitoneal or gynecologic masses [7,8].

Role of advanced imaging and PET-CT

Multiplanar reconstructions are essential to assess bowel wall integrity and tumor boundaries. In equivocal cases, MRI particularly with diffusion-weighted imaging or enterography protocols may further clarify tumor origin and tissue characteristics [9].

The role of PET-CT in GIST imaging is well established for assessing early response to tyrosine kinase inhibitors and for problem-solving in equivocal cases. However, current NCCN and ESMO guidelines do not recommend routine PET-CT for initial staging or follow-up of localized, low-risk GISTs. In the present case, PET-CT was not performed because contrast-enhanced CT showed no metastatic disease and histopathological analysis confirmed low-risk features, making additional metabolic imaging unnecessary [10,11].

EGIST concept and pathological confirmation

The concept of EGIST remains debated. Current WHO classification emphasizes the importance of excluding any continuity with the gastrointestinal wall on both macroscopic and histological examination to establish a diagnosis of true primary mesenteric GIST [3]. In the present case, surgical and pathological findings fulfilled these criteria.

Conclusion

Primary mesenteric GISTs are rare tumors that may mimic pelvic or retroperitoneal masses on imaging. This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge posed by their exophytic growth pattern and atypical location. Careful analysis of vascular supply and systematic use of multiplanar reconstructions are key to identifying mesenteric origin preoperatively. Increased awareness of these imaging features can improve diagnostic accuracy, guide appropriate surgical management, and optimize patient outcomes.
