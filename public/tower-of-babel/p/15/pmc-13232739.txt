Decomposing cultural differences in behavior toward members of different social groups
Authors: Jiashu Wang, Adrianna C Jenkins, Colin F Camerer, Ming Hsu, Jingjing Ma, Valerio Capraro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProsocial behavior is a hallmark of human societies, yet its expression—and the benefits it confers—varies across cultures. While such variation is well documented, the underlying cognitive mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we propose a decomposition of cultural differences in prosocial behavior into two key components: (i) social perception, capturing how cultural groups view others on dimensions of warmth and competence, and (ii) social valuation, reflecting how these perceptions influence the value of social actions. Using behavioral data from economic games involving participants in the United States and China (n = 5,428), we show strong evidence of a double dissociation at the level of between- and within-country variation: whereas social perception differences dominated in accounting for between-country differences, within-country differences were more driven by variation in social valuation. These findings offer a mechanistic account of cultural influences on prosocial behavior and highlight the value of computational approaches in cross-cultural research.Keywords: prosocial behavior, cross-cultural differences, social perception, social valuation

Does our cultural background shape social behavior, and can we identify specific pathways of this influence without resorting to broad generalizations that may perpetuate biases? This study addresses this by developing a mathematical model with two pathways: social perception (how people perceive others) and social valuation (how they value others). Experiments with individuals from China and the United States revealed that this model was able to produce highly accurate predictions of behavioral differences between the two countries. In particular, we found that between countries, differences in social perception were the main pathway driving behavioral differences. In contrast, social valuation was the main pathway within the country. Together, these results provide insights into the complex ways in which culture can affect human behavior.

Introduction

Travelers encountering unfamiliar cultures are often struck by the differing ways social groups are treated. Alexis de Tocqueville (1), for instance, described early 19th-century America as a place where people from different social classes were treated as equals—yet women remained marginalized. Similarly, the Jesuit missionary Matteo Ricci (2) noted the high social regard for scholars in 17th-century China, alongside widespread distrust of foreign merchants.

Far from isolated anecdotes, such observations echo a robust body of research documenting cultural variation in attitudes toward and treatment of different social groups (3–6). Despite extensive empirical evidence, however, scientific attempts to identify mechanisms underlying these differences remain elusive and contested (7–10). Much of the prior literature has emphasized average differences between cultural groups, often without decomposing the psychological processes that drive them (7, 11, 12). Moreover, interpreting observed differences has proven difficult, in part due to challenges in separating culture-specific effects from methodological artifacts and measurement biases (13, 14).

Concerns about construct, method, and item bias are particularly salient in cross-cultural research. These concerns may be due to a lack of equivalency in the constructs under study, e.g. fairness—what cultural psychologists refer to as “construct bias” (15). Similarly, experimental procedures may be seen as more trustworthy in one culture than another, i.e. method bias. Finally, it may simply be that results are impacted by different ways of translating test items across languages, i.e. item bias (16). For these and other reasons, psychologists, anthropologists, and to a lesser extent economists, have long been wary of “sweeping generalizations in the past regarding differences in traits and abilities of cultural populations, which, upon close scrutiny, were based on flawed assumptions of questionable validity” (13).

Indeed, even the utility of the common practice of using nationality of origin or ethnicity in studying cultural differences has been criticized for the fact that these categories are themselves products of historic cultural construction (17–19). Under this view, voiced most stridently by anthropologists, research using these categories when studying cultural differences risk making the “fallacious assumption of cultural homogeneity within nations” (17, 20, 21). That is, by reducing identity to a single entity, one risks “essentializing” culture as static and deterministic categories, overlooking the complexity, diversity, and fluidity of cultural identities and practices (22, 23).

Here, we take a step toward overcoming these challenges by building on recent findings suggesting the mechanistic contribution of two sets of cognitive processes to how people behave toward others: (i) social perception, capturing how individuals perceive others, and (ii) social valuation, capturing how people value others’ outcomes, including how the value of social actions is affected by associated perceptions (24, 25). In particular, these studies provide strong evidence of the predictive and external validity of a computational approach capturing the interaction of these two components by showing highly accurate out-of-sample predictions of observations in laboratory games and the field and at both behavioral and neural levels of analyses (24, 25).

Importantly, substantial evidence suggests that both social perception and social valuation are subject to cultural influences. First, culture is known to shape the specific ways in which individuals from a particular cultural group perceive others, i.e. social perception. Although core dimensions of social perception have been found to arise consistently across cultures, such as the dimensions of warmth and competence, where different groups fall within this two-dimensional space may differ substantially (24–26). Similarly, past studies have suggested that culture can shape the value that individuals place on characteristics of others, including those on social perception dimensions such as warmth and competence. Indeed, it has been long proposed that more collectivist cultures, such as those in East Asia, may be more sensitive to the perceived warmth of others due to its association with social harmony, whereas cultures that emphasize independence, such as Western Europe and the United States, may be more sensitive to the perceived competence of others (27).

Drawing upon these suggestions, we sought to decompose cultural differences into these two components by studying how different members of different culture groups from China and the United States allocated resources to others in a set of economic games (28–31). Importantly, this approach allows us to assess cultural influences as defined not only by nationality but also by key variables that have been proposed in past studies.

Our results show that cultural differences in resource allocation at both between- and within-country levels could be captured robustly by accounting for differences in both social perception and social valuation components, even in out-of-sample scenarios that require robustness against construct and method bias. Moreover, we identify a striking double dissociation in the relative contributions of these two components in driving cultural differences, defined at between- and within-country levels. Between countries, differences in social perception dominated in terms of predicting behavioral variation toward members of other groups. Conversely, at the within-country level, differences in social valuation were more strongly predictive of behavioral variation across cultural groups as defined by age, gender, ethnicity (in the case of the United States), or rural/urban residency (in the case of China). Finally, beyond mean differences, we find that variation across cultural groups within China appeared more tightly clustered around country-level means than their US counterparts, consistent with past speculations regarding the influence of cognitive styles on within-culture variation. Together, these finding highlight the value of capturing core cognitive processes in understanding how they give rise to cultural variation, which in turn raises a host of novel questions regarding proximate and distal mechanisms that are critical to unwrapping the “packaged variable” that is human culture (13, 14).

Results

Identifying cultural differences in treatment of social groups

To capture similarities and differences in how cultures behave toward members of different social groups, we studied how participants from the United States and China, respectively, behaved in two economic decision-making tasks involving resource allocation—giving and taking dictator games (DG). First, in the giving task, participants were presented with a series of independent trials. In each trial, they were provided with a single-word descriptor (e.g. “FARMER”) representing a new, randomly assigned counterpart and were instructed to decide how much of a 20-token endowment to give to that counterpart based solely on that information. The taking task was similar, with the difference being that the 20-token endowment initially belonged to the counterpart, and participants decided how many tokens to take from them. All trials were hypothetical; however, participants were encouraged to respond honestly (see Materials and methods).

In both tasks, each participant was presented with 25 different targets (Table S1) selected based on past work and pretest data suggesting that there exists variation in perception and attitude toward these groups across the two cultures. Furthermore, to minimize purchasing power differences, we set the value of each token to 1.5 USD in the experiments in the United States, and 10 RMB in the experiments in China, approximating the nominal exchange rate between the two countries at the time of the study.

For each task in each country, we collected data in two separate waves. The two waves followed identical procedures, except that the second wave was conducted on a demographically representative sample, defined by gender, age, ethnicity, and race for the United States, and by gender, age, and household geographic location for China. This allows the second wave to serve as a holdout sample as well as be used for subsequent subgroup analyses where a demographically representative sample is particularly useful (see Materials and methods and Supplementary Information). In total, this resulted in eight samples with a combined sample size of 5,428. For convenience, we will refer to a sample by indicating its country, condition, and wave code (e.g. CN-G1 will refer to the Chinese sample administered the giving task in wave 1).

Consistent with previous studies on sharing norms (28, 32), at the aggregate level (i.e. pooling across all targets), participants from both countries in both waves exhibited similar sharing norms reflected in the peaks of the distributions at reference points including selfish (0%), 1/3, and 1/2. Participants were also slightly more generous in the take task than in the give task (33, 34), where an additional peak was also observed at the completely altruistic reference point (Fig. 1). Across different social groups, the largest relative differences in giving were observed between SCIENTIST and BANKER where Chinese participants gave comparatively more than their American counterparts, and conversely, HOMELESS and JAPANESE. In taking, Chinese participants took comparatively less from SCIENTIST and FARMER than their American counterparts, and conversely took more from JAPANESE and CIVIL SERVANT. Perhaps most importantly, cross-country differences across targets were far from uniform, such that substantial variation exists in these cross-country differences, which in turn hints at the need to capture cross-cultural differences in component processes underlying this behavior.

Explaining cultural differences in treatment of social groups

To explain and, in turn, predict disparities in how social groups are treated, we build on the social perception–weighted (SPW) social valuation model where social perception and social valuation components combine to drive behavior (24). Specifically, the approach provides an opportunity to provide out-of-sample prediction of differences “between” cultures by separately capturing the “within”-culture social perception and social valuation. Importantly, by focusing on cultural differences, we remove common components between the two countries, e.g. that “Nurses” are perceived as warm, which in turn provides a more stringent test of the accuracy of model predictions.

To this end, we divided our samples according to the two different waves into training and testing sets. Specifically, giving and taking data from wave 1 were used as training data to derive social perception and social valuation information and then applied to corresponding giving and taking data from wave 2, which served as the testing set (Fig. 2A). First, we obtained social valuation weights by calibrating a multilevel model where the proportion of tokens allocated to the recipient is regressed against warmth and competence ratings (Fig. 2B).

Two different methods of eliciting social perception were used. One set consisted of ratings from participants after they completed the economic games, while another was from an independent set of participants who only completed ratings and not the economic games. In both sets of ratings, correspondence exists across the United States and Chinese participants for certain groups, such as the HOMELESS, who are perceived as less competent across cultures, while substantial discrepancies were observed in others. For example, American participants (compared with Chinese participants) perceived NANNY, FARMER, and JAPANESE as more competent social groups, while Chinese participants rated NORTH KOREAN and LAWYER as warmer than Americans did. As both sets of ratings were extremely highly correlated (Fig. S2A), we present the below results using ratings from the former method, with those using the latter presented in supplementary materials (Fig. S2B).

Using the calibrated model, we found that, consistent with past findings (24), for the United States sample in the giving task, perceived warmth (competence) of the counterpart had a positive (negative) effect on the amount allocated. Interestingly, in the Chinese sample, although perceived warmth was significantly associated with allocation amount, perceived competence did not consistently significantly influence behavior. In taking, the two countries were qualitatively similar in terms of their sensitivity to warmth and competence, where perceived warmth (competence) was positively (negatively) associated with allocation amount (Fig. 2C, see Table S2 for detailed statistics).

Comparing the magnitude of the coefficients on warmth and competence, we found that competence had a stronger negative impact on giving behavior in the US sample compared with the Chinese sample, while the Chinese sample was more strongly impacted by warmth than the US sample (Fig. S3, see Table S3 for detailed statistics). Across the two games, we found that participants in both countries were more sensitive to both warmth and competence in taking, such that coefficient magnitude nearly doubled when compared with giving, but the Chinese participants were more sensitive to the social frame of giving versus taking (bootstrap-based interaction P < 0.001, Fig. 2C, see Table S4 for detailed statistics).

Next, out-of-sample predictions of cultural differences were derived by taking the difference in the calibrated models to arrive at predicted behavioral differences. Specifically, we calculated the predicted differences based on social perception and social valuation information generated from wave 1, and asked to what extent they corresponded to the observed treatment differences in wave 2. We found that this model was able to predict a substantial share of the variance in differences across targets (giving: r2 = 0.76, 95% CI = [0.62, 0.91]; taking: r2 = 0.77, 95% CI = [0.63, 0.92]). For example, JAPANESE and AUSTRALIAN were treated more positively (more giving and less taking) by Americans than Chinese (Fig. 2D), while SCIENTIST was treated more positively by Chinese (Fig. 2E).

Between-country differences and social perception

The fact that behavioral differences in these games could be parsimoniously captured by underlying social perception and valuation components provides an important validity check that social perception and social valuation weights from the two countries are directly comparable. In contrast, a poor fit, either due to systematic errors or highly noisy measures, could pose a significant threat to the validity of any cross-cultural comparisons (13, 14, 35). Furthermore, it offers an opportunity to decompose the relative importance of underlying components in explaining cultural differences. That is, how differently a person in China (the United States) would behave if they had the social perception of someone from the United States (China) while retaining their social valuation? Or alternatively, if they had the social valuation of someone from the other country while retaining their social perception?

To decompose the relative importance of underlying components in explaining cultural differences, we computed the predictive power of the calibrated model, switching either the social perception or valuation weights of the two countries. First, using parameters trained from one wave, we calculated out-of-sample predictive power of the calibrated model if we instead swapped the social perception ratings of the two cultures while keeping the original social valuation coefficients (Fig. 3A). This “changing content” model captures how an individual from culture A might behave if she were given the social perceptions of someone from culture B. Compared with a model using a culture's own social perception ratings and social valuation weightings, the changing content model performed significantly worse (giving: r2 = 0.55 ([0.37, 0.73]) vs. 0.16 ([−0.02, 0.33]); taking: r2 = 0.82 ([0.74, 0.91]) vs. 0.45 ([0.26, 0.65]), mean ([95% CI]) of wave 1 to wave 2 and wave 2 to wave 1, Fig. 3B).

For each country, we used their own group-level stereotypes and coefficients from the other country to predict behaviors toward 25 social groups, termed “changing sensitivity” predictions. Meanwhile, employing the stereotypes from the other country and their own coefficients yielded “changing content” predictions. Finally, we separately examined the explanatory power of these two predictions on behavior from the other wave. B) The r2 of different models’ out of sample predictions. When predicting behaviors toward 25 social groups, altering stereotype content (purple) resulted in a more pronounced deterioration in the model's predictive ability (compared with using culture's own stereotypes and sensitivities, black).

In addition, we used the same procedure and computed a “changing sensitivity” model capturing how that individual would behave if she kept her social perception but was given the social valuation weights of someone from Culture B. Importantly, because this exercise used the trained model from sample 1, no additional degree of freedom was introduced in the changing sensitivities model (Fig. 3A, see Supplementary Information for details). We found that although social valuation also played an important role, it was much less impactful than social perception (giving: r2 = 0.46 [0.27, 0.66]; taking: r2 = 0.69 [0.55, 0.83], mean [95% CI] of wave 1 to wave 2 and wave 2 to wave 1; Fig. 3B).

Thus, our findings argue against the idea that cultural differences in behavior are simply due to differences in how individuals interpret the constructs of generosity and fairness, or warmth and competence, which in turn provides evidence that these constructs are to an important extent equivalent and comparable across cultures (14, 15). It also allows us to overcome past concerns about method and item biases regarding the extent to which individual targets, e.g. CIVIL SERVANT, are interpreted identically between Chinese and US participants. That is, even though civil servants in the two countries differ in important ways both demographically and in terms of socioeconomic status, differences in how they are interpreted could be accounted for by capturing their social perception, respectively.

Within-country differences and social valuation

Our cross-cultural comparisons above make an important assumption that the country averages are representative of the respective underlying populations (21). This, however, can overlook alternative explanations where cultural differences between countries instead reflect differences across other variables such as gender, age, or ethnicity. To address this, we examined within-country variation by exploiting the demographically representative samples collected in wave 2. We used the same out-of-sample approach as previously, where the wave 2 data were randomly divided into equal halves for 10,000 times, with each time one half serving as the training set and the other as the testing set.

Specifically, we defined eight subgroups with demographic characteristics that were sampled in a representative manner—gender and age in both countries, ethnicity in the United States, and geography (urban/rural) in China. Next, we computed three models capturing different assumptions about whether social perception or social valuation weights are either shared or subgroup specific: (i) model 1 assumes that subgroups share weights but their perception is subgroup specific, (ii) model 2 is similar to model 1 except weights are subgroup specific but perception is shared, and (iii) model 3 allows for both subgroup-specific perception and weights (Fig. 4). Additionally, to demonstrate the distinct contributions of social perception and valuation at the between- versus within-country level, we combined the US and Chinese data from the giving or taking wave 2, defined subgroups based on nationality, and repeated the above analysis.

Strikingly, we found a double dissociation in processes underlying between- and within-country differences. Unlike the case where nationality was used to capture culture and social perception played a dominant role, variation in social valuation was more important when additional cultural variables were taken into consideration (Fig. 4). Specifically, comparing log-likelihood in the testing set of the models, with the model that allows for both subgroup-specific perceptions and weights as the benchmark, shared perceptions with group-specific weights reduce the model likelihood more than the reverse when nationality is used to define subgroups (i.e. explaining between-country variation). However, for within-country variation, when subgroups were defined by gender, age, and ethnicity/geography, the model with shared weights and group-specific perceptions performs better for both the United States and China (Fig. 4; also see Table S5 for detailed statistics).

Next, to gain more insight into how these weights varied across subgroups, we investigated the variation in social valuation weights in the two countries. In addition to the demographic categories that were sampled representatively, we also included, for exploratory purposes, participants’ self-reported education (coded as high or low), income (coded as high or low), and political affiliation (the United States: Democrat, Republican, Independent, nonaffiliated, or other; China: Communist Party, Non-Communist Party). Consistent with the above results suggesting that the US subgroups were more heterogeneous, we found that coefficients with the US weights were more widely distributed than compared with China. Moreover, the distance between the two countries dominated differences within each country, such that none of the subgroups overlapped across countries (Fig. 5A and C).

Within the US-G2 sample, we found significant associations with gender, such that women were more sensitive to warmth (βfemalew = 0.97, SE = 0.36, t = 2.70, P = 0.007, 95% CI = [0.26, 1.67]); age, such that older individuals were more sensitive to warmth (βAgew = 1.06, SE = 0.21, t = 5.14, P < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.66, 1.47]); and education, such that higher educated individuals were less sensitive to warmth (βEduw = −0.51, SE = 0.22, t = −2.37, P = 0.018, 95% CI = [−0.94, −0.09]). In contrast, no significant variation existed in differences in the competence coefficient on giving (Fig. 5B). In taking, age simultaneously explained individual differences in the coefficients of warmth (βAgew = 1.25, SE = 0.30, t = 4.16, P < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.66, 1.85]) and competence (βAgec = −0.55, SE = 0.19, t = −2.88, P = 0.004, 95% CI = [−0.93, −0.18]; Fig. 5D, see Table S6 for full results).

Within China, subgroup variation was observed with gender (βfemalew = −1.01, SE = 0.38, t = −2.64, P = 0.008, 95% CI = [−1.77, −0.26]), age (βAgec = −0.61, SE = 0.16, t = −3.73, P < 0.001, 95% CI = [−0.92, −0.29]), and education level (βEduc = 0.44, SE = 0.19, t = 2.32, P = 0.021, 95% CI = [0.07, 0.81]) in giving, as well as income level (βIncw = −0.82, SE = 0.38, t = −2.16, P = 0.032, 95% CI = [−1.57, −0.07]) and party affiliation (βNCCPc = −1.35, SE = 0.44, t = −3.06, P = 0.002, 95% CI = [−2.22, −0.48]) in taking (see Table S6 for full results). However, given the overall lack of difference between subgroups in China, these differences should be interpreted with caution. Indeed, compared with Americans, Chinese participants exhibit higher within-group similarity in both behavior and social perception. Specifically, the mean correlations among Chinese participants regarding allocation choices, warmth, and competence ratings for the 25 social groups were higher (Fig. S4), consistent with the idea that culture can impact not only average perceptions and behavior but also the degree of variation that exists in each.

Discussion

What are the psychological mechanisms that give rise to cultural variation in prosocial behavior? Despite much evidence documenting the existence of substantial variation in behavior across cultures, much less is known about the underlying processes. Here, we provide a mechanistic account of how culture shapes social behavior toward members of different groups integrating the Stereotype Content Model—a pancultural framework for comparing group stereotypes across societies (27, 36)—with computational models that capture the interaction between social perception and valuation processes (24, 25).

First, by providing highly precise and accurate predictions of behavioral differences between the United States and China, our findings support the cross-cultural validity of our modeling framework. Indeed, while differences in cognition and behavior have often been demonstrated between American and Chinese samples in cross-cultural studies, interpretation of these differences is often contentious due to the myriad of ways in which any two cultures differ.

More specifically, using demographically representative samples in the two countries, we identified a striking double dissociation: between-country differences were best explained by how people perceive others (social perception), whereas within-country variation was better explained by how much people value others’ outcomes (social valuation). These findings offer a direct response to long-standing critiques of cross-cultural research that caution against equating nationality with culture (21, 37). Rather than treating national categories as fixed or monolithic, our approach reveals the specific cognitive components that account for variability in behavior both between and within cultural groups.

Importantly, these findings help to address concerns about construct and method bias in cross-cultural comparisons (13, 14). The high predictive accuracy of our model—even in out-of-sample contexts—suggests that warmth and competence are meaningful and comparably interpreted across cultures. This lends support to the cross-cultural validity of these constructs and reduces the likelihood that observed behavioral differences reflect artifacts of measurement or interpretation.

Our findings connect to a large body of work using economic games to document cultural variations in social behavior (28–30). Prior research has identified systematic cross-cultural differences in cooperation (31), prosocial punishment (38), trust (29), reciprocity (39), and in-group favoritism (40). Building on this tradition, our model suggests that some of these differences may arise from underlying social-cognitive processes. Whereas earlier work has established a rich empirical foundation by documenting the scope and robustness of cross-cultural variation in economic games, the present results point toward specific psychological processes that may generate these patterns. Future research can build on this foundation by identifying additional mechanisms that shape cultural variation in social decision-making.

The present findings also raise important questions regarding cognitive mechanisms giving rise to these differences. First, they are broadly consistent with Anderson's classic information integration theory (41, 42), which describes how individuals combine multiple pieces of social information to reach a final judgment. Second, our findings provide a platform to further explore how weighting of associated attributes might shift under different social or situational conditions, for example, how category labels activate cognitive prototypes as in Wyer's social information processing perspective (43).

In particular, our framework bridges research on social norms and social cognition. The culturally shared social perceptions and valuation weights we identify can be seen as the cognitive architecture underlying culture-specific social norms. This interpretation aligns with prior findings—for example, Americans’ stronger adherence to norms favoring 50–50 fairness allocations. Within the framework we propose, not only behavior but also differential weighting of attributes such as warmth and competence may reflect deeper normative expectations within a culture (28–31).

One potential limitation is our reliance on self-reported perceptions, which may be influenced by social desirability or lack of introspective access (44, 45). However, the strong behavioral predictive power of these measures, and the robustness of our results when excluding uniform raters (∼5% in the United States, <1% in China) suggest that these concerns are unlikely to undermine our core conclusions. Moreover, we observed no systematic bias related to political orientation—where such pressures might be most pronounced—within either cultural group (Fig. 5). Nevertheless, future studies using methods that do not rely on self-report measures of perception, such as third parties or directly observing them using neural measures, would be valuable in overcoming these concerns (25, 45).

Given the contribution of social perception and valuation processes, these results raise important questions about the upstream sources of these differences (7, 8), which likely reflect significant influence of broader societal narratives, including those conveyed through media, education, and public discourse. For example, the substantial discrepancies between the United States and China in perceptions of groups such as NANNY, FARMER, LAWYER, JAPANESE, and NORTH KOREAN likely reflect, at least in part, differences in how these groups are portrayed in each country's collective narrative. Moreover, differences in the homogeneity of subgroup responses observed in China compared with the greater heterogeneity in the United States may also relate to differences in information environments, where centralization of information contribute to the degree of shared perceptions and values within a society. Investigating how exposure to different societal narratives affects social perception and valuation is therefore a promising avenue for future research.

Beyond the role of societal narratives, macro-level factors such as economic development, institutional trust, and historical trajectories may also play a role (46, 47). Prior work has linked cultural differences in social preferences to macroeconomic indicators, where aggregate market integration, for example, was found to explain 23% of variation in ultimatum game behavior (4). Our findings further point to the importance of macro-level determinants of “stereotype content” in understanding cultural differences in social behavior and the need for future studies that can speak to these important questions.

Future work could also link our findings to established models of cultural differences—such as individualism–collectivism (19), Hofstede's cultural values model (48), and Inglehart's postmaterialism dimension (49), which treat culture itself as the unit of analysis. Differences in perception or valuation may align with these dimensions or even be manipulated experimentally (50). Inclusion of additional cultural groups or experimental manipulation of these factors could further explore these connections.

Furthermore, our findings support the view that culture is not a monolithic “package” but an emergent product of interacting psychological, social, and structural influences (13, 14). By decomposing cultural differences into specific cognitive processes and linking them to behavioral outcomes, our work contributes to a growing body of research aiming to “unwrap” culture into testable components. While this approach remains provisional, given the ubiquity of cultural effects and the significant methodological and conceptual challenges involved, even the small step we take here offers a productive advance toward more scientifically robust and valid cross-cultural comparisons.

More broadly, understanding the determinants of cultural differences may have important practical implications. The ability to accurately understand how individuals from different cultural backgrounds perceive and respond to others is essential for effective communication and coordination in an increasingly interconnected world. When such understanding is incomplete or inaccurate, interactions across cultural contexts can give rise to tension, miscoordination, or conflict, both between and within organizations or societies. Our paper contributes by integrating prior theories that have focused either on abstract norms, values, or generalized preferences, or on social perception alone. By showing the importance and variation in the interplay between perception and valuation, our findings provide a more precise framework for characterizing cross-cultural differences in social behavior. To the extent that disagreements and coordination failures stem from divergent expectations about how others perceive and evaluate social groups, clarifying these underlying processes may help anticipate points of friction and identify avenues for alignment. This perspective may be relevant not only for interpersonal and intergroup interactions but also for institutions and policymakers operating in cross-cultural contexts, where communication and cooperation often depend on assumptions about shared perceptions and evaluative standards.

Materials and methods

Participants

All experimental procedures were approved by institutional review boards at Peking University (for Chinese subjects) and the University of California, Berkeley (for US subjects). Participants provided informed consent before participation.

All participants performed two economic decision-making tasks involving resource allocation: a giving variant and a taking variant of the dictator game (DG). For each task in each country, we collected data in two separate waves. The two waves followed identical procedures, except that the second wave was fielded using panel samples recruited from existing online research panels maintained by the vendors designed to approximate population benchmarks. This allows the second wave to serve as a holdout sample as well as be used for subsequent subgroup analyses where a demographically representative sample is particularly useful. In total, this resulted in eight samples with a combined sample size of 5,428. See Supplementary Information for detailed information for all samples.

Experimental paradigm

Following a pretest to identify specific target groups for subsequent behavioral assessments of social perception (Table S1 and Supplementary Information), we administered two behavioral decision-making tasks—a “giving” task and a “taking” task—to measure participants’ behavioral tendencies toward members of different social groups. In each trial of these tasks, participants were presented with a single word indicating the counterpart's social group membership (e.g. “FARMER”) and made a dictator decision. Following the decision trials, participants also provided explicit stereotype evaluations (warmth/competence ratings) of the same groups. Details about task procedures are provided in Supplementary Information. For both tasks, participants were explicitly instructed that all decisions were hypothetical. They were informed that they would receive a fixed compensation regardless of their decisions. Nonetheless, they were encouraged to respond as honestly as possible.

Behavioral analysis

Social valuation

We use mixed regression to depict the social valuation process among participants from different countries. The group-level warmth and competence ratings were used to predict allocation behavior. The fixed effects characterized the social evaluation of participants from the United States and China, while the random effects were retained for subsequent subgroup analyses. Full methodological details are provided in Supplementary Information.

Cultural differences and out-of-sample r2

To determine the extent to which social perception and social valuation can explain observed behavioral differences at the cultural aggregate level, we compared the out-of-sample predictive power of models where we swapped, respectively, the stereotype content (warmth/competence ratings) or the sensitivity (warmth/competence coefficients) between the two countries while keeping the other component fixed. See Supplementary Information for a detailed description of models.

Subgroup analyses

Using giving/taking wave 2 data, we tested whether intracultural demographic differences in social perception and valuation improve behavioral prediction. Within each cultural sample, participants were repeatedly split into training and test halves to compare the out-of-sample performance of models with subgroup-specific stereotype content, valuation coefficients, or both, and to relate individual warmth and competence valuation weights to demographic variables. See Supplementary Information for details.
