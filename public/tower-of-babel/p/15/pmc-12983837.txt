Host genome regulation of the porcine gut microbiota and its impact on feed conversion efficiency
Authors: Qitian Wu, Xiaoqing Wang, Qiming Mu, Jingjing Tian, Hailing Wang, Jiayi Yang, Zhen Peng, Lili Gao, Pengfei Gao, Fuping Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of host genetics on the porcine gut microbiota and their subsequent impact on the feed conversion ratio (FCR). While initial genome-wide association studies (GWAS) did not identify significant SNPs directly associated with FCR, we investigated the gut microbiota as a potential intermediate phenotype influencing feed efficiency. Nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis distances demonstrated a distinct separation in microbial community structure between the high-feed conversion ratio (HFCR) and low-feed conversion ratio (LFCR) groups (stress = 0.19), suggesting a link between FCR and gut microbial composition. Furthermore, a significant, albeit weak, negative correlation was observed between the genomic relatedness matrices and microbial Bray‒Curtis dissimilarity (r = −0.0143, p = 0.0031), indicating host genetic control over the microbiome. Microbiome genome-wide association study (mGWAS) identified 117 significant SNPs associated with 28 microbial taxa. Functional annotation highlighted eight candidate genes (DCY8, PATJ, PTPN2, FTO, SLC13A1, ADAM28, MGST1, and PTGS2) involved in the regulation of taxa, including Campylobacter, Faecalibacterium, Streptococcus, Succinivibrio, Treponema, Turicibacter, uncultured Erysipelotrichales, and uncultured Peptococcaceae 2. Collectively, these findings establish that the gut microbiota is a heritable trait influenced by the host genome, providing novel targets for breeding strategies designed to optimize microbial composition for improved feed efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00527-y.

Introduction

The global pork market has experienced substantial growth, with a compound annual growth rate of approximately 3.5% from 2020-2025. As of 2023, the total production of pork worldwide is estimated at approximately 120 million metric tons. However, with the rapid expansion of the pig industry, the demand for feed ingredients such as corn and soybeans continues to rise, further exacerbating the strain on the feed supply. Under the dual pressures of ensuring national food security and supplying high-quality animal protein, reducing feed consumption and improving pig feed conversion ratio (FCR) have become critical challenges for the industry.

The genetic mechanisms underlying feed conversion efficiency are complex and significantly influenced by external factors such as feed composition, the rearing environment, and management practices. Identifying additional candidate loci regulating the porcine FCR, elucidating nutrient metabolism mechanisms, and enhancing production performance remain key scientific questions. Recent studies have revealed that the gut microbiota, as the host’s “second genome,” profoundly influences energy utilization efficiency through functions such as dietary fibre degradation and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production [1]. The microbial community structure is closely associated with FCR phenotypes [2]. Emerging evidence highlights the role of the microbiota in modulating host energy metabolism and nutrient utilization. Specific microbes, such as Prevotella, increase energy harvest by fermenting indigestible fibres into SCFAs, whereas pathogenic bacteria such as Campylobacter may trigger intestinal inflammation and impair nutrient absorption [3]. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for microbiome-mediated FCR regulation, positioning gut microbes as promising targets for precision breeding. However, a systematic understanding of how host genetics shape microbial composition to indirectly influence FCR—particularly the host‒microbiome interplay under genetic regulation—remains lacking.

Materials and methods

Data source

The data used in this study were obtained from an experimental trial conducted by the University of Hohenheim, Germany (Authorization No. S411/14TZ), which strictly adhered to German animal welfare legislation. The dataset included 207 Pietrain sows, with performance testing initiated at 30 kg body weight and concluded at 105 kg, spanning approximately 100 days. The measured traits included feed intake (FI), daily gain (DG), and the calculated FCR. The calculation method for the DG is the difference between the final body weight and initial body weight divided by the number of testing days. The FCR is calculated as the FI divided by the DG, where the FI is estimated through individual feed intake records. On the basis of FCR rankings, the 22 individuals with the highest FCR values were designated the high feed conversion ratio (HFCR) group, whereas the 22 individuals with the lowest FCR values composed the low feed conversion ratio (LFCR) group. All pigs were reared, slaughtered, and sampled under standardized conditions. The detailed experimental design and protocols are described in the original publication [4].

Host genotype data and matrix construction

Genotype data were derived from Illumina PorcineSNP60 BeadChip arrays for all 207 Pietrain sows. After quality control (SNP call rate ≥ 95%, minor allele frequency ≥ 5%), 51,970 SNPs were retained for downstream analysis.

The genetic relationship matrix (GRM) for all 207 individuals was computed following VanRaden’s method [5] via the ASRgenomics package (v4.3.3). Candidate gene region analysis

In this study, two distinct thresholds were applied to identify significant SNPs within candidate gene regions. The genome-wide significant threshold was set via a strict Bonferroni correction for multiple testing, corresponding to p-value < 0.05/N, where N denotes the total number of SNPs tested. Additionally, a suggestive significant threshold of p-value < 1/N was used to identify SNPs of potential interest. SNPs associated with microbial abundance that exceeded this threshold were considered significant, and their 1 Mb flanking regions were defined as candidate intervals. Using PLINK (version 1.90b6.21) [7] software, we calculated the linkage disequilibrium (LD) level (R2) between the lead SNP (MAF > 0.05) and all SNPs within a ± 2 Mb window. Candidate gene regions were determined on the basis of R2 values and annotated via the Ensembl database (http://asia.ensembl.org/biomart/martview/) with the pig genome assembly Sus scrofa 11.1.

Genetic mapping of the feed conversion ratio

A GWAS was conducted to investigate the genetic basis of the FCR in this population. The analysis revealed that no genetic loci reached genome-wide significance, indicating that no significant markers were identified to be associated with this trait. The results are visualized in Manhattan and Q‒Q plots (Fig. 1A and B, respectively). Differential analysis of the gut microbiota

GWAS results showed that no specific SNPs were significantly associated with the FCR. Therefore, this study further explored whether the gut microbiota could serve as a phenotypic trait for FCR. We performed nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) to assess beta diversity in the gut microbiota between the HFCR and LFCR groups. The analysis was based on Bray‒Curtis distances calculated from 1870 OTUs, and the results are shown in Fig. 2. The stress value of the NMDS plot was 0.19, indicating a good model fit and a reliable representation of the underlying dissimilarity structure. No significant overlap was observed between groups, which highlights the notable influence of FCR on community structure.Fig. 2NMDS plot of the gut microbiota in the HFCR and LFCR groups on the basis of Bray‒Curtis dissimilarity

Evaluation of host genetic effects on the gut microbiota

To investigate the associations between the host genetic background and the gut microbial community structure, this study analysed the correlations between the constructed GRM (Supplementary Figure 1) and the microbial Bray‒Curtis distance matrix (Supplementary Figure 2) on the basis of genomic and microbiome data. The results (Figure 3) revealed a statistically significant but weak negative correlation between the GRM and the microbial Bray‒Curtis distance (r = −0.0143, p = 0.0031). This suggested a limited association between host genetic variation and microbial composition.

Discussion

The intricate interaction mechanisms between the gut microbiota and the host genetic background represent a central topic in microbiome research. Recent studies have demonstrated that the host genome influences microbial community structure and function by regulating the intestinal microenvironment (e.g., nutrient metabolism, barrier function, and immune responses). Through mGWAS, we identified genetic loci and candidate genes that regulate the relative abundance of gut microbes, providing critical insights into the genetic regulation of the colonic microbiota.

The extent to which host genetic variation determines microbial abundance varies dynamically across different intestinal segments. Metabolic and nutritional factors [8], selective pressures [9], coevolution [10], and microbial genetic adaptation [11] collectively contribute to the unique dependence of specific microbes on the host genome. A significant negative correlation was observed between the host kinship matrix and microbial Bray‒Curtis dissimilarity, indicating the influence of genetics on microbiome composition. A study on pig gut microbiota reported similar fingdings [12] . By analysing 12 full-sib pairs of Huaxi pigs raised in the same litter (excluding cross-litter individuals), permutation tests revealed a significant negative correlation between genomic relatedness and the microbial Bray‒Curtis distance, further supporting the universality of host genetic regulation of the microbiota.

This study identified numerous genetic loci and candidate genes modulating the relative abundance of the gut microbiota through mGWAS analysis, providing crucial insights into the genetic regulatory mechanisms of intestinal microbes. Utilizing 48 microbial taxa from 207 Pietrain pigs as phenotypes, our mGWAS detected 117 significant association signals across 28 microbial species. Subsequent LD analysis and functional annotation of candidate regions localized eight candidate genes related to intestinal health, metabolism and the microbiota: DCY8, PATJ, PTPN2, FTO, SLC13A1, ADAM28, MGST1, and PTGS2. These findings demonstrate that the host genome, alongside environmental factors, diet and disease, acts as a primary determinant of gut microbial communities. The gut microbiota serves as a critical link connecting the host genome [13]. Research on the genetic basis of gut microbes can identify loci that indirectly regulate feed conversion efficiency, expanding the findings in swine functional genomics and providing new directions and insights for improving feed conversion rates in pigs: Compared with other foodborne pathogens, Campylobacter species not only are morphologically distinct but also possess relatively small genomes, with unique cellular and molecular physiology [14] (Supplementary Fig. 8). Studies have shown that ADCY8, a key enzyme in the cyclic adenosine monophosphate signalling pathway, regulates energy metabolism and immune responses [15]. This study revealed that genetic variations near the ADCY8 locus are significantly correlated with the abundance of Campylobacter in pig intestines, suggesting that this gene may influence Campylobacter colonization by modulating the gut microenvironment. At the molecular level, Campylobacter infection can impair intestinal barrier function, while ADCY8 enhances epithelial barrier integrity by promoting the expression of tight junction proteins through the cAMP‒PKA signalling pathway [16].

Streptococcus is a group of gram-positive cocci widely found in natural environments and animal bodies, including the intestines of humans and pigs (Supplementary Figure 9). In the gut environment, the colonization and proliferation of Streptococcus may be regulated by host genetic factors, among which protein tyrosine phosphatase nonreceptor type 2 (PTPN2), a key candidate gene for inflammatory bowel disease, plays a critical role in maintaining intestinal barrier function and regulating immune responses [17]. Studies indicate that PTPN2 preserves epithelial barrier integrity by suppressing the interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) signalling pathway, preventing the overexpression of the pore-forming protein Claudin-2 and thereby limiting the disruption of the intestinal barrier by proinflammatory cytokines [18]. This study revealed a significant association between PTPN2 gene polymorphisms and the abundance of Streptococcus in pig intestines, suggesting that this gene may influence Streptococcus colonization by modulating the gut microenvironment. Mechanistically, PTPN2 expression is regulated by the transcription factor NKX2-3, and Streptococcus infection may alter this regulatory network, affecting PTPN2 expression levels. When PTPN2 function is impaired, IFN-γ-mediated inflammatory responses intensify, leading to compromised intestinal barrier function, which may create favourable conditions for Streptococcus overgrowth and invasion.

Succinivibrio, a member of the Succinivibrionaceae family, competitively inhibits hydrogen utilization pathways in methanogens while stimulating succinate synthesis pathways [19] (Supplementary Fig. 10). Research has shown that the FTO gene, an important RNA demethylase, not only regulates energy metabolism [20] but also significantly influences the composition of the gut microbiota [21]. This study revealed that FTO gene variants are significantly associated with Succinivibrio abundance, suggesting that FTO may regulate the colonization of this bacterium by altering the gut microenvironment. FTO deficiency leads to an increase in the anti-inflammatory microbiota, which may affect the survival of Succinivibrio. Additionally, succinate, a key metabolite produced by Succinivibrio, may be regulated by FTO, thereby influencing the host energy balance.

Turicibacter is a highly heritable taxon in the human fecal microbiome [22] (Supplementary Fig. 11). There is a close bidirectional regulatory relationship between Turicibacter and the host gene ADAM28. This study revealed that ADAM28 expression levels are significantly correlated with the intestinal abundance of Turicibacter, revealing a potential novel mechanism of host‒microbe interactions. At the molecular level, ADAM28, a metalloprotease with unique proteolytic activity [23], may alter the gut microenvironment by modulating key signalling pathways, such as the MEK/ERK and PI3K/mTOR pathways, directly affecting Turicibacter colonization. Notably, ADAM28 expression in colorectal cancer tissues is closely associated with patient obesity status [24], suggesting that the interaction between ADAM28 and Turicibacter may play a significant role in metabolic disorder-related intestinal diseases.

Uncultured Erysipelotrichales and the MGST1 gene: Mechanistically, MGST1, a member of the μ-class glutathione S-transferase family, plays a central role in the intestinal antioxidant defense system (Supplementary Fig. 12). By catalyzing the conjugation of glutathione with various electrophilic compounds, it effectively promotes toxin excretion and detoxification [25]. Notably, this protective function of MGST1 may be closely related to the metabolic activities of unclassified Erysipelotrichales.

Uncultured Peptococcaceae2 and the PTGS2 gene: PTGS2, a key enzyme in prostaglandin synthesis, promotes inflammatory responses and tumorigenesis by catalyzing the production of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) (Supplementary Figure 13). Recent studies have shown that selective COX-2 inhibitors can significantly reduce the number and size of intestinal polyps, providing direct evidence for the critical role of PTGS2 in intestinal tumorigenesis [26]. We speculate that uncultured Peptococcaceae2 may indirectly influence PTGS2 expression or activity by modulating the inflammatory state of the gut microenvironment.

Faecalibacterium—a strictly anaerobic, gram-positive rod-shaped genus constituting ~5% of the human gut microbiome [27]—includes prominent butyrate producers (e.g., F. prausnitzii and F. longum) that ferment dietary fibre into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). These metabolites enhance intestinal barrier integrity, immune regulation, and anti-inflammatory responses [28]. A reduced Faecalibacterium abundance is correlated with metabolic disorders (e.g., obesity and type 2 diabetes), and its restoration ameliorates dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and fatty liver [29, 30]. The candidate gene PATJ encodes a multi-PDZ domain protein with an L27 domain that interacts with PALS1 to establish epithelial apical polarity and tight junction assembly [31]. Its PDZ domains also bind ZO-3 and claudin-1, stabilizing tight junctions [32]. Butyrate from Faecalibacterium strengthens barrier function (via ZO-1/occludin), whereas PATJ may further stabilize this structure by organizing ZO-3/claudin-1 [33]. This synergy likely results in the formation of a multilayered protective network, enhancing intestinal barrier integrity. Breeding strategies targeting PATJ could modulate Faecalibacterium abundance, thereby optimizing lipid degradation, alkane metabolism, and FCR.

Treponema—a gram-negative spirochete genus that thrives in anaerobic niches (e.g., the gut, oral cavity, and rumen)—possesses unique sulfur-metabolizing capabilities [34]. The candidate gene SLC13A1 encodes the NaS1 sulfate transporter, which mediates dietary sulfate uptake at the apical membrane of the small intestinal epithelium via Na+ -coupled transport [35, 36]. Treponema utilizes host-derived sulfate for hydrogen sulfide production via dissimilatory sulfate reduction [34], a process linked to fibre and starch metabolism, which is crucial for FCR improvement. Selective breeding targeting SLC13A1 may thus increase Treponema-mediated sulfur metabolism, ultimately increasing feed efficiency.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of this study lies in the 16S rRNA sequencing data analysis, which was conducted via the traditional OTU clustering pipeline. Notably, the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) approach has become more standard in the field, as it typically offers higher resolution in distinguishing sequence variants and better reproducibility. However, owing to the unavailability of the raw sequence files and the complete sample phenotype mapping for this dataset, it was not feasible to reanalyze the data via the ASV method. Therefore, the use of the OTU-based approach represents a methodological constraint. In future studies, we will employ the ASV pipeline where possible to increase the comparability and reproducibility of the results.

Conclusion

This study revealed significant associations between porcine genomic variations and the composition of the gut microbiota. Through mGWAS, we identified eight candidate genes, including DCY8, PATJ, PTPN2, FTO, SLC13A1, ADAM28, MGST1, and PTGS2, that potentially regulate microbial abundance variations. These findings provide crucial insights into host‒microbiome interactions, microbial heritability, and functional regulatory mechanisms, advancing our understanding of microbial roles in biological processes.
