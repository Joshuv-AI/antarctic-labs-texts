Drivers of artificial insemination fertility in Murciano-Granadina goats: a ten-year multifactorial approach integrating buck–doe interactions across physiological, environmental, and management conditions
Authors: María Pía Peláez Caro, Ander Arando Arbulu, José Manuel León Jurado, Juan Vicente Delgado Bermejo, Javier Fernández Álvarez, Francisco Javier Navas González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial insemination (AI) success in Murciano-Granadina goats is influenced by a complex interaction of male, female, management, and environmental factors. This study aimed to identify the main drivers and threshold conditions affecting fertility outcomes in commercial AI programs using long-term field data.ResultsA dataset of 3,122 inseminations performed between 2010 and 2019 was analyzed using canonical discriminant analysis and CHAID decision trees. Multicollinearity screening excluded 24 redundant predictors out of an initial set of 52 variables, retaining 28 variables with the greatest explanatory power. The canonical model showed that the first six functions explained 72.4% of total variance, with barometric pressure, temperature, and rainfall at insemination as dominant environmental loadings. Fertility increased when minimum temperatures exceeded 14.7 °C but declined below 9 °C, coinciding with reduced semen quality. Wind speeds above 2.9 m/s and humid easterly winds reduced conception probability by up to 18% due to potential stress-inducing effects at semen collection. Altitudes between 451 and 720 m were associated with the highest fertility rates. Management thresholds were also important: fertility declined when inseminated group size exceeded 63 does, whereas herds larger than 50 animals showed improved synchrony and higher conception probabilities. Female age strongly influenced outcomes, with immature does (~ 2 years) more frequently associated with low fertility, while multiparous females (> 3 parturitions) achieved fertility rates up to 15% higher. Bucks aged 3–5 years produced semen with superior fertility outcomes compared with younger (1–3 years) or older (> 7 years) males. Somatic cell counts below 3,000 × 103/mL increased the probability of high fertility outcomes, whereas counts above 4,000 × 103/mL were associated with markedly reduced success. Compensatory outcomes were also observed, where acceptable fertility occurred despite low semen quality under favorable physiological and management conditions.ConclusionsAI fertility in Murciano-Granadina goats results from multifactorial interactions among environmental conditions, physiological status, and herd management. Identifying key environmental, physiological, and management thresholds may contribute to improved planning of AI programs and may help guide future strategies aimed at enhancing reproductive efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05546-0.

Introduction

Artificial insemination (AI) has revolutionized genetic improvement in livestock by enabling the widespread dissemination of superior genetics, increasing the use of selected sires, and accelerating genetic progress through controlled breeding programs, particularly in dairy [18] and meat production systems [27]. Among its many advantages, AI allows for the widespread dissemination of desirable genes linked to production traits such as milk yield and disease resistance. For instance, selection and widespread use of sires carrying genetic resistance to mastitis, as reflected by lower somatic cell counts, has been successfully implemented in dairy cattle breeding programs, leading to improved herd health and reduced incidence of infection [66]. It also enables breeders to reduce the transmission of reproductive diseases, improve herd management, and alleviate the challenges posed by seasonal reproduction, a common issue in many livestock species [13, 76]. For Mediterranean breeds like the Murciano-Granadina goat, AI has become a central tool in breeding programs, ensuring genetic progress while maintaining the breed's high standards of milk quality and production. Genetic improvement programs in dairy goats rely primarily on selection based on estimated breeding values, while AI serves as a key technological tool that facilitates the dissemination of superior genetics and helps overcome limitations associated with traditional breeding systems, such as restricted semen availability and seasonal mating constraints [10].

However, one of the greatest challenges in goat breeding is the seasonality of reproduction, which can result in significant declines in fertility rates during the non-breeding season (February to May). This seasonal infertility, marked by anestrus in females and reduced semen quality in males, severely limits reproductive success and product availability [44, 77]. Despite this challenge, the Murciano-Granadina breed is known for its adaptability to environmental conditions and its remarkable grazing capacity, which, coupled with its high-quality milk production, makes it one of the most competitive goat breeds internationally [7, 18].

To combat seasonality, breeders often employ hormonal protocols designed to synchronize estrus and optimize breeding cycles. These protocols typically involve the use of intravaginal progesterone or progestogen devices, combined with cloprostenol and gonadotropins, to control luteal function and induce estrus [71]. Estrus synchronization is not only beneficial for managing breeding schedules but also improves reproductive efficiency by shortening the parturition interval, increasing offspring survival, and enhancing overall economic profitability [14].

Despite the effectiveness of AI and hormonal synchronization, achieving successful reproductive outcomes is influenced by numerous factors, both genetic and non-genetic, that affect both males and females. In female goats (does), factors such as age, age at first kidding, kidding interval, and general health all impact reproductive performance. Additionally, infectious and non-infectious factors can lead to abortion or other reproductive issues, further complicating the management of fertility [11, 14]. For males (bucks), semen quality is crucial, and it is influenced by factors such as age, seasonality, climatic conditions, and semen handling during AI [6, 40]. Even the type of semen used—whether fresh or frozen—can affect the success of insemination [75]. Given this broad range of potential influences, a holistic approach to understanding the interaction between male and female reproductive factors is essential for improving AI success rates.

The interaction between bucks and does, specifically the relationship between semen quality and fertility outcomes, is a key area of focus. While individual factors such as semen quality or the age of the does are important, the combined effect of these variables—along with external conditions such as environmental factors and semen handling—determines the overall success of AI. Understanding these complex relationships is crucial to improving fertility rates and optimizing breeding outcomes in the Murciano-Granadina breed.

Given the multifactorial nature of fertility, it is essential to adopt robust analytical methods that can simultaneously consider various factors and their interdependencies. Discriminant canonical analysis is a particularly effective tool for this type of multifactorial evaluation, as it allows for the incorporation of both quantitative variables (e.g., semen quality, age) and qualitative variables (e.g., semen type, estrus synchronization protocols). This analytical approach can also handle covariates and interactions, making it ideal for exploring how multiple factors contribute to fertility outcomes in a complex breeding system.

In this study, discriminant analysis and CHAID (Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection) are employed to explore the buck-doe interaction and identify the most influential factors affecting the success of AI in Murciano-Granadina goats. By analyzing the interactions between semen quality, female fertility, environmental conditions, and management practices, this study aims to pinpoint the key drivers of reproductive success. The findings will provide valuable insights into how best to optimize breeding strategies and improve fertility rates within the breed’s AI program.

This approach not only emphasizes the importance of genetic and environmental factors in determining AI success but also highlights the intricate relationship between male and female reproductive health. By enhancing our understanding of these interactions, breeders can implement more effective selection and management practices, ultimately improving the efficiency and profitability of the Murciano-Granadina official national breeding program.

The aim of this study is to conduct a comprehensive multifactorial analysis of the interaction between semen quality and fertility outcomes in Murciano-Granadina goats, focusing on the buck-doe dynamics. By utilizing discriminant canonical analysis and CHAID, the study seeks to identify and quantify the key factors—both genetic and environmental—that influence the success of artificial insemination. Specifically, this research aims to uncover the interactions between semen quality, female reproductive health, and external factors such as seasonality and hormonal protocols, in order to improve fertility rates within the breed’s AI program. The findings of this study will provide actionable insights for optimizing breeding strategies, enhancing reproductive efficiency, and ultimately increasing the sustainability and profitability of the Murciano-Granadina goat breed.

Material and methods

Murciano-Granadina official national breeding program

The data used in this study originate from the official national breeding program of the Asociación Nacional de Criadores de Caprino de Raza Murciano-Granadina (National Association of Murciano-Granadina Goat Breeders), which is responsible for the genetic improvement and herdbook management of the Murciano-Granadina breed in Spain [53]. This programme operates at a national level under the supervision of the Spanish Ministry of Agriculture and is formally approved through the Resolution of the Directorate-General for Agricultural Productions and Markets, in accordance with Royal Decree 45/2019 and Regulation (EU) 2016/1012. The programme is jointly implemented by the only officially recognised breeder associations for this breed, ACRIMUR and CAPRIGRAN, functioning as a single coordinated national scheme rather than independent entities.

All animals included in this study were registered in the single official herdbook (genealogical book) of the Murciano-Granadina breed, which is uniquely established at the national level and jointly managed by the recognised breeder associations, ensuring pedigree traceability and consistency in genetic evaluations [18]. Within this framework, the official national breeding program governs the systematic collection, validation, and storage of phenotypic, genealogical, and reproductive data, which are owned and managed by the breeder associations. These records are generated through routine breeding activities, including linear appraisal, zoometric evaluations, and reproductive performance recording, and are subsequently used for genetic evaluation and the estimation of breeding values. The structure, legal basis, and operational procedures of the programme ensure data consistency and traceability, providing a reliable foundation for genetic analyses and selection decisions.

Animal sample and study conditions

This longitudinal study covered a 10-year period (2010–2019) and initially included 21,757 Murciano-Granadina does born between September 1999 and June 2018. During this period, 32,693 artificial insemination records were collected between January 2010 and December 2019. Semen was provided by 115 Murciano-Granadina bucks, all participating in the official national breeding program and selected for their proven fertility and high genetic merit (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Murciano-Granadina pregnant does on their way to pasture and lactating does herds [42]

Does selected for artificial insemination were not chosen arbitrarily but according to predefined management and reproductive criteria established within the Murciano-Granadina official national breeding programme (Fig. 1). Eligible animals were required to be in an adequate stage of lactation (at least 120–180 days in milk), maintain a minimum milk yield (typically ≥ 1 L, depending on herd conditions) to ensure productive continuity in case of unsuccessful insemination, and present a positive combined genetic value. In addition, only does with confirmed reproductive soundness were included, excluding animals with recent unsuccessful matings or those inseminated within the previous 12 months.

This selection protocol ensures that insemination is performed under optimal physiological and management conditions, improving both animal welfare and the reliability of fertility outcomes. At the same time, the criteria are flexible and herd-adapted, reflecting real farm conditions within the breeding programme rather than restrictive or elitist selection, and thereby supporting robust and applicable results across production systems.

Observational data handling and sample attrition

The initial dataset comprised 32,693 AI records, which were subsequently screened to retain only observations with complete semen quality information, as these variables were required for the calculation of the Composite Seminal Quality Index (CSQI). A total of 29,571 records were excluded due to missing semen quality measurements, resulting in a final analytical dataset of 3,122 AI records. Only observations with complete information for the semen quality traits included in the CSQI—ejaculate volume, sperm concentration, total motility, progressive motility, acrosome integrity, and plasma membrane functionality assessed by HOST—were retained. Buck location

Until 2014, bucks were housed at the Andalusian Goat Selection and Improvement Center in Albolote (68HQ + 3R Granada, Spain) and were subsequently relocated to Fuente Vaqueros (66GP + 6X Santa Fe, Granada, Spain). Both facilities are located in the Vega de Granada, a continental Mediterranean region characterized by wide diurnal temperature fluctuations (> 20 °C), rainfall concentrated between late autumn and early spring, and an average annual precipitation of approximately 419.5 mm. Frost events occurred primarily during the winter months, with a recorded frequency of 6.2–8.7 days per season, while they were rare or absent during spring, summer, and autumn. Throughout the study, bucks were maintained under standardized management and feeding conditions, receiving 0.5 kg of commercial concentrate daily, ad libitum hay and water, and mineral supplementation to ensure uniform nutritional support.

Regulatory status and official oversight of the breeding center

The breeding center involved in semen collection and processing operates as a legally registered livestock facility under the General Register of Livestock Holdings, possessing its own official identification number and complying with the regulatory framework governing animal breeding and reproductive centers in Spain. In addition to the standard requirements applicable to livestock operations, the center is subject to regular official inspections conducted twice per year by the regional Regional Agrarian Offices, typically in the early and mid part of the year. These inspections are performed to verify compliance with animal health, welfare, biosecurity, and reproductive management regulations, and to ensure that semen collection, processing, and handling procedures meet the standards required for the operation of an authorized artificial insemination and genetic improvement center.

Semen collection, preparation and analysis

Semen collection and initial screening

Ejaculates were collected using an artificial vagina and immediately transferred to a 32 °C water bath for evaluation. An initial subjective screening was performed to determine suitability for artificial insemination. Only ejaculates meeting the following criteria were processed further: volume > 0.5 mL, sperm concentration > 2000 × 10⁶ sperm/mL, and mass motility ≥ 4.

Ejaculate volume (mL) was measured directly in calibrated collection tubes. Mass motility was assessed subjectively using a phase-contrast microscope (40 × magnification) on undiluted semen using hanging-drop preparations. Motility was scored on a 0–5 scale, where 0 indicated no movement and 5 indicated vigorous swirling wave motion; scores of ≥ 4 were considered acceptable for AI processing. Mass motility scoring (≥ 4) was therefore based on subjective microscopic evaluation of undiluted semen, whereas quantitative motility parameters (total and progressive motility) were subsequently measured using computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) on diluted semen samples. Sperm concentration (× 10⁶ sperm/mL) was determined using a photometer (Accucell, IMV Technologies, L’Aigle, France). Ejaculates that passed the initial screening were subsequently analysed using CASA.

Semen preservation protocols

Two semen preservation protocols were used. For chilled semen, ejaculates were diluted with INRA 96 extender (IMV Technologies, France) to a final concentration of 200 × 10⁶ sperm per straw, stored at 5 °C, and used within 4–6 h after collection, including transport to the insemination site. For cryopreservation, diluted semen was packaged in 0.25-mL plastic straws containing 150 × 10⁶ spermatozoa per straw, frozen using a programmable cryo-freezer (Digitcool programmable cryo-freezer, IMV Technologies, France) using a controlled-rate freezing program. Frozen straws were stored in liquid nitrogen (− 196 °C) until use. Prior to insemination, semen straws were thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 30 s and used immediately for insemination (within 5 min) to minimize post-thaw sperm deterioration.

CASA Motility analysis

For CASA evaluation, semen samples were diluted in INRA 96 extender to a final concentration of 200 × 10⁶ spermatozoa/mL and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C prior to analysis to ensure optimal tracking conditions for CASA analysis. Aliquots of 5 µL were placed on pre-warmed glass slides covered with 22 × 22 mm coverslips, producing an approximate chamber depth of 10 µm. Motility analysis was performed using the ISAS v1.2 system (Integrated Semen Analysis System, Proiser R + D, Spain) equipped with a high-speed digital camera operating at up to 200 frames per second (fps). For each ejaculate, four random microscopic fields were recorded and at least 200 sperm tracks were analysed. All recordings were performed at 37 °C. The CASA-derived parameters obtained were total motility (TM, %), defined as the proportion of motile spermatozoa, and progressive motility (PM, %), defined as the proportion of spermatozoa exhibiting forward progressive movement. CASA settings for motility classification and tracking were calibrated according to the manufacturer’s recommendations for small ruminant spermatozoa to ensure consistent detection and analysis of sperm trajectories.

Plasma membrane functionality

Acrosome integrity was evaluated by preparing smears using 10 µL of semen, which were air-dried and fixed in methanol for 1–2 min. Slides were examined under oil immersion (1000 × magnification) using a phase-contrast microscope. A total of 200 spermatozoa per slide were evaluated, and the percentage of spermatozoa with intact acrosomes was recorded.

Plasma membrane functionality was assessed using the hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST) following the method of [35]. Briefly, 10 µL of semen were mixed with 100 µL of hypo-osmotic solution (100 mOsm/kg; 1.351 g fructose and 0.735 g sodium citrate dissolved in 100 mL bidistilled water) and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were subsequently fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde, and at least 200 spermatozoa were examined under 400 × magnification. Plasma membrane functionality was inferred from the presence of coiled sperm tails resulting from hypo-osmotic swelling.

All semen evaluations were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions by trained personnel to ensure methodological consistency and minimise observer-related variation.

The following semen quality traits were retained for subsequent analyses and for the calculation of the CSQI: ejaculate volume (mL), sperm concentration (× 10⁶/mL), total motility (%), progressive motility (%), acrosome integrity (% intact acrosomes), and plasma membrane functionality assessed by HOST (% endosmosis). These parameters collectively represent key functional dimensions of semen quality, including ejaculate output, sperm motility, acrosomal integrity, and membrane functionality, and were therefore integrated into the CSQI to provide an overall measure of seminal quality.

Equipment and protocol specificity

Laboratory equipment for semen evaluation

Ejaculate volume was measured using calibrated tubes, and sperm concentration was determined with a photometer (Accucell, IMV Technologies, France). Microscopic evaluations, including mass motility and acrosome integrity, were performed using a phase-contrast microscope (Nikon Eclipse E200, Nikon Instruments Inc., Japan) equipped with a heated stage at 37 °C.

Sperm motility was further assessed using a computer-assisted sperm analysis system (ISAS v1.2, Proiser R + D, Spain) with a high-speed digital camera (up to 200 fps). Samples were analysed on pre-warmed slides (37 °C), evaluating at least 200 sperm tracks across four fields per ejaculate.

Artificial insemination was performed using standard vaginal specula and catheters for small ruminants. Pregnancy was diagnosed by transabdominal ultrasonography (5-MHz linear transducer; IMV Technologies, France) at 30–40 days post-insemination.

Semen preservation and handling protocols

Ejaculates were diluted using INRA 96 extender for chilled semen or Triladyl for cryopreservation (IMV Technologies, France). Chilled semen was cooled to 5 °C and inseminated within 4–6 h of collection.

For cryopreservation, semen was packaged in 0.25-mL straws (150 × 10⁶ spermatozoa), equilibrated at 5 °C for 2 h, and frozen using a programmable cryo-freezer. Straws were stored in liquid nitrogen (− 196 °C) until use. Thawing was performed at 37 °C for 30 s, and semen was used immediately (within 5 min).

Thermo-Resistance Test (TRT)

For frozen semen doses, sperm resistance to post-thaw physiological stress was evaluated using a thermo-resistance test (TRT) (Fig. 4). In this procedure, one straw from each batch was thawed and subsequently incubated at 37 °C to reproduce the thermal environment encountered by spermatozoa within the female reproductive tract after insemination. The TRT is designed to simulate the time required for spermatozoa to migrate from the site of deposition in the cervix to the oviduct, thereby providing an indirect estimate of their functional longevity under physiological conditions.Fig. 4Schematic representation of the thermo-resistance test (TRT) applied to frozen–thawed goat semen, showing incubation at 37 °C and motility assessment at successive time points (0, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min)

Schematic representation of the thermo-resistance test (TRT) applied to frozen–thawed goat semen, showing incubation at 37 °C and motility assessment at successive time points (0, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min)

To evaluate sperm persistence under thermal stress, motility parameters were assessed at successive incubation intervals (0, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min after thawing) using CASA. The progressive decline in motility across these time points provided an indication of the ability of spermatozoa to maintain functional activity during prolonged exposure to body temperature. In addition to motility measurements, structural and functional sperm integrity were evaluated through acrosome integrity assessment and plasma membrane functionality using the HOST.

These complementary evaluations allow the identification of sperm cells capable of maintaining membrane stability, acrosomal integrity, and motility over time, which are critical prerequisites for successful fertilization. The plasma membrane represents one of the sperm structures most susceptible to temperature-induced damage, particularly during cryopreservation and thawing, when membrane permeability and ion homeostasis may be disrupted. Consequently, assessing the ability of spermatozoa to withstand thermal incubation provides valuable information about their resilience to physiological stress and their potential fertilizing capacity following artificial insemination.

For chilled semen processing, ejaculates were first tempered in a 30–32 °C water bath immediately after collection and subsequently maintained at ambient laboratory temperature (≈25 °C) during handling and dilution. Processed semen was then packaged into 0.25-mL straws, grouped into plastic tubes, and placed in a water-filled container to facilitate gradual thermal equilibration. Following preparation, the tubes were transferred to a laboratory refrigeration chamber and subsequently to portable compressor-based refrigeration units maintained at 5 °C for transport to the insemination site. The cooling process lasted approximately 1.5 h, depending on the distance to the farm, allowing semen temperature to progressively decrease from approximately 30–32 °C to 5 °C under controlled conditions. During transport, straws were stored in insulated containers equipped with programmable thermometers, enabling continuous monitoring of internal temperature in both storage and transport systems. Despite variability in travel distances and times among farms, temperature stability was consistently ensured throughout the process. Prior to insemination, temperature conditions were routinely verified to guarantee optimal preservation of semen quality.

Semen transport and temperature control

To ensure the preservation of semen quality during transport from the laboratory to the insemination sites, both chilled and frozen semen doses were carried in a portable compressor-based freezer (CoolFreeze CF-40, WAECO, Germany). This unit operates with 12/24 V DC and 100–240 V AC power sources, allowing stable temperature control during field transport. The device has a gross capacity of approximately 37 L and allows a temperature range adjustable from + 10 °C to − 18 °C, enabling the maintenance of appropriate storage conditions for both chilled and frozen semen. Cooling is provided by a Danfoss BD35F compressor with integrated control electronics, combined with polyurethane foam insulation to maintain thermal stability. The system incorporates specialized electronic controls, including automatic turbo-cooling, three-stage battery protection, reverse-polarity protection, and memory functions that retain previous temperature settings. Power consumption is approximately 45 W, with current consumption of 0.68 Ah/h at 20 °C ambient temperature and 0.83 Ah/h at 32 °C when maintaining an internal temperature of + 5 °C. The cabinet is constructed from polypropylene with a polyethylene lid, weighs approximately 16 kg, and includes a removable wire basket and detachable carrying handles. Compliance with CE, TÜV/GS, and automotive EMC standards ensures safe operation under mobile conditions. The use of this portable freezer allowed semen doses to be transported under controlled and stable temperature conditions, minimizing thermal fluctuations and thereby helping to preserve semen quality and fertilizing capacity until the moment of artificial insemination.

Estrus synchronisation protocol

During the study period, estrus synchronization was consistent across farms, following a short-term progestagen-based protocol used in the Murciano-Granadina official national breeding program [61]. This protocol used intravaginal progestagen devices combined with prostaglandin F₂α and eCG to induce luteal regression and synchronized ovulation [25, 37, 47].

Before treatment, all does were examined by transabdominal ultrasonography to exclude pregnancy or pseudopregnancy. The same hormonal products and procedures were applied across farms, and an 8-day protocol (P8) was used in all cases.

On Day 1, intravaginal sponges containing fluorogestone acetate (45 mg; Chrono-gest®, MSD Animal Health, Madrid, Spain) were inserted to act as the progestagen source and 50 μg cloprostenol (PGF₂α analogue; Estrumate®, MSD Animal Health, Madrid, Spain) was administered intramuscularly (0.2 mL per goat). On Day 6, the sponges were removed and equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG; Foligon®, MSD Animal Health) was administered intramuscularly at doses ranging from 222 to 400 IU per goat, depending on seasonal conditions and farm reproductive history.

Although equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG) doses varied within the range of 222–400 IU, this factor was not explicitly re-evaluated in the present study, as its effect had been previously assessed [61]. Earlier findings indicated that, while eCG contributes to reproductive outcomes, its individual effect is relatively limited when compared to other determinants such as insemination timing and farm-level interactions. In particular, the interaction between farm, synchronisation protocol, and eCG concentration was shown to capture most of the variability associated with hormonal treatments, supporting the notion that fertility is driven by multifactorial processes rather than by eCG dose alone. Therefore, the present study focused on those factors with greater explanatory relevance, while acknowledging that eCG dose variability reflects routine field practices within the official national breeding program.

Artificial insemination and pregnancy diagnosis

Artificial insemination (AI) was performed on 110 commercial Murciano-Granadina farms approximately 46 h after sponge removal, using cervical insemination with a speculum and integrated illumination. Pregnancy was diagnosed 42 days post-insemination by transabdominal ultrasonography (5 MHz probe), and outcomes were confirmed through kidding records. All inseminations were carried out by a single trained technician within the Murciano-Granadina official national breeding program; therefore, technician identity was not recorded and was not included as a predictor in the statistical analyses. Procedures followed highly standardized protocols aligned with ISO 9001 [33] and ISO/IEC 17025 [34] standards, ensuring consistent semen handling, preparation, and insemination. Semen production and evaluation were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, further minimizing operator-related variability. Exploratory checks comparing interfarm and intrafarm fertility showed no systematic differences, and the use of a single operator prevented technician-specific effects.

Milk yield standardization and composition analysis

The Murciano-Granadina is a polyestrous dairy breed typically presenting two kidding seasons per year and lactations ranging from 210 to 305 days [18]. In the present study, milk yield and composition were evaluated across lactation and expressed in kilograms following standardized procedures [62].

Individual milk production (RPj) was calculated following [63] using ICAR-compliant milk recording schemes (A4, AT4T, AT4M, A6, AT6M, AT6T; [32]. As recording intervals varied among farms, milk yield was standardized considering the interval from kidding to first control (d₁ = FC − KD) and between the last two controls (d₂ = LC − PC), assuming approximately monthly test-day records. On average, ~ 5 milk controls per animal were recorded per lactation.

Milk composition was determined from monthly samples collected during routine milk recording. Samples were analyzed at the Milk Quality Laboratory (Córdoba, Spain) using a MilkoScan FT1 (Foss Electric, Hillerød, Denmark) for protein, fat, total solids, and lactose determination, and a Fossomatic FC (Foss Electric, Hillerød, Denmark) for somatic cell count, following standard milk quality assessment protocols.

Weather and moon phases data

Daily meteorological data were obtained from the Spanish State Meteorological Agency (AEMET; http://www.aemet.es) using records from stations nearest to each artificial insemination (AI) center and farm (within 5–15 km). The variables analyzed included ambient temperature (°C; maximum, minimum, and mean), mean wind speed (m/s), wind gusts (km/h), photoperiod (hours of daylight per day), barometric pressure (hPa; maximum and minimum), wind direction (degrees), and mean daily rainfall (mm).

To ensure consistency, selected variables—particularly wind direction and gusts—were preprocessed by: (i) expressing gust angles relative to geographic north; (ii) assigning wind direction to the nearest cardinal point (e.g., 45° = NE, 90° = E, 135° = SE); and (iii) converting wind speed to meters per second (m/s).

Meteorological station data were used as standardized environmental inputs to characterize climatic conditions affecting reproductive performance. Linking farm data to the nearest official station is a validated and widely used approach [13, 21], enabling consistent assignment of environmental variables such as temperature, humidity, solar radiation, precipitation, and atmospheric pressure across animals [3, 48]. This approach provides a reliable approximation of environmental exposure at the population level.

Barometric pressure was treated as a time-specific variable evaluated at biologically relevant time points (semen collection and insemination), consistent with its use as a daily environmental predictor in reproductive analyses [15]. Although local variability may exist, station-based values provide a practical representation of ambient atmospheric conditions. Accordingly, pressure effects should be interpreted as location- and time-specific environmental signals acting through physiological pathways—such as hypoxia-related responses—rather than as fixed farm-level attributes [38].

Lunar phase at semen collection and insemination was obtained from the Astronomical Applications Department of the United States Naval Observatory (http://aa.usno.navy.mil) and included as an additional temporal factor.

Statistical analysis

Assumption testing

Discriminant analysis assumes linear relationships between predictors and group membership and requires prior evaluation of key statistical assumptions [28, 64]. Accordingly, multivariate normality was assessed using the Lilliefors-corrected Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Q–Q plots in XLSTAT 2014 [2], Paris, France), while homogeneity of variance–covariance matrices was evaluated using Levene’s test. Outliers were identified using the ROUT method (Q = 1%) in GraphPad Prism 9.0 [52]. Sample size adequacy was confirmed by maintaining at least ten observations per predictor variable. These diagnostics ensured the suitability of the dataset for canonical discriminant analysis and related multivariate techniques.

Canonical Discriminant Analysis (CDA)

Canonical Discriminant Analysis (CDA) was used to evaluate whether linear combinations of environmental, physiological, and management variables could explain clustering patterns in the interaction between seminal quality and fertility outcomes. The explanatory variables were grouped into thematic clusters representing biological, environmental, and management factors.

Dependent variables: composite seminal quality and fertility indices

Combined selection indices (ICOs) were developed following the linear selection index framework of [73] to jointly evaluate multiple reproductive traits measured on different scales. Two indices were constructed: the Composite Seminal Quality Index (CSQI), including six seminal traits (W₁–W₆), and the Composite Fertility Index (CFI), including three fertility traits (W₁–W₃). Both indices were calculated as weighted linear combinations of standardized variables. In the absence of reliable economic weights or prior biological evidence, all traits were assigned equal weights, ensuring balanced contribution across components. Calculations were performed in MathWorks MATLAB R2015a (Natick, MA, USA) 72.

Variable selection

Regularized forward stepwise multinomial logistic regression was used for variable selection. Group size priors were adjusted to avoid bias. Forward selection was chosen for computational efficiency.

Sample size considerations

The study exceeded recommended minimum ratios of observations to variables, ensuring robust discriminant analyses.

Multicollinearity analysis

Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity among predictors. VIF is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${VIF}_{j}=\frac{1}{1-{R}_{j}^{2}}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{j}^{2}$$\end{document} is the coefficient of determination obtained by regressing the jth predictor on all other predictors. A commonly used threshold of VIF > 5 was applied to identify and remove highly collinear variables. This threshold is recommended in the literature as a practical cutoff for detecting multicollinearity without being overly conservative [29, 58].

Canonical correlation dimensions

The number of canonical correlations equals the number of variables in the smaller set, with values ≥ 0.30 considered meaningful (~ 10% explained variance). To aid interpretation, a biplot of the first two canonical functions (F1, F2) was constructed, showing variable positions based on standardized canonical coefficients and structure loadings. Variables farther from the origin indicate stronger contributions, while vector orientation reflects the direction of association. Interpretation was supported by standardized coefficients in Supplementary Table S2 to ensure consistency between graphical and numerical results.

CDA Efficiency and model reliability

Bartlett’s test was used to assess whether the correlation matrix differed from an identity matrix (p < 0.05), while Wilks’ Lambda (Λ) evaluated the contribution of variables to discrimination, with values closer to zero indicating stronger effects. Pillai’s trace criterion was also applied to test multivariate significance, providing a robust alternative under violations of normality and homogeneity, with significance set at ≤ 0.05.

The hit ratio was calculated, and leave-one-out cross-validation was used. Data mining: CHAID decision tree

Methodology

The CHAID algorithm was applied for classification of discrete data. Nodes were split using Chi-square significance (p < 0.05) with Bonferroni adjustment. Terminal nodes represented Buck/Doe Interaction (seminal quality x fertility rate outcomes scales, Table 4). Pruning avoided overfitting. Decision Tree is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Cross-validation performance of CHAID models

Ten-fold cross-validation estimated prediction accuracy. The optimal tree minimized cross-validation error within one standard error of the minimum. Resubstitution and cross-validation errors were compared; reliable models showed similar values with a quotient close to ± 1.

Results

Assumption diagnostics

Preliminary analyses indicated deviations from normality in semen quality parameters. Although the Lilliefors-corrected Kolmogorov–Smirnov test rejected normality, this appeared driven by minor tail deviations likely due to non-significant outliers. Q–Q plots suggested approximate normality (p > 0.05). Assumptions were systematically assessed, and Levene’s test revealed heteroscedasticity (p < 0.05), prompting the use of permutation-based canonical correlation analysis, which is robust to violations of normality and variance homogeneity. Outlier detection using the ROUT method (Q = 1%) in GraphPad Prism 9.0 did not identify significant outliers.

Visual inspection was prioritized because it provides a direct assessment of distributional features such as skewness and subtle deviations that formal tests may over-detect in large datasets. Although no significant outliers were found, minor deviations or moderate outliers may still exist. The observed non-normality likely reflects subtle asymmetries, clustering, or heavy-tailed distributions rather than extreme values, highlighting the importance of visual evaluation alongside statistical tests.

Pillai’s Trace (3.12) was significant (F = 12.45; DF1 = 888; DF2 = 74,016; p < 0.0001), indicating that the predictors explain a substantial proportion of multivariate variance. Given its robustness to violations of covariance homogeneity, this result provides strong evidence of significant group differences.

Discriminant function reliability: cross-validation

The predictive accuracy of the classification model was evaluated using Press’s Q statistic. Out of 3,122 cases, 1,346 were correctly classified, corresponding to a moderate overall accuracy of 43.1%. The resulting Press’s Q value was 0.57, and the test statistic (Q = 1052.40, p < 0.001) indicated that the classification accuracy was significantly better than would be expected by chance alone. This confirms that the discriminant functions captured meaningful structure in the data, despite moderate overall accuracy.

Data mining: CHAID Decision tree

The classification tree (Supplementary Figure S2) organized 25 seminal quality × fertility categories and is interactively available via the SVG Tree Viewer v1.0 (Supplementary Material S2) 55. Supplementary Table S6 summarizes significant CHAID splits (P < 0.05) and node structure (n = 3,121).

Outcomes followed a gradient: best were Excellent × High (light pink) and Excellent × Very High (darker green), followed by Very Good × High (light blue) and Very Good × Very High (pink). Intermediate categories included Excellent × Medium (red) and Good × High (dark red), while poorest outcomes were mainly Medium (light green, teal, pink) and Discardable (blue, orange, grey).

Key drivers were doe age (> 2 years), herd size (> 50 AI), and young bucks (< 3 years), all associated with high-fertility categories. In contrast, primiparity, older bucks, and small herds favored poorer outcomes.

Environmental and physiological thresholds were critical: optimal temperatures (15–18 °C), early lactation (< 100 d), low SCC (< 3,000), and low altitude favored top categories (light pink, darker green), while cold stress (< 13 °C), late lactation (> 200 d), high SCC (> 4,000), and high altitude shifted outcomes toward Medium/Discardable (teal, grey, orange).

Milk composition and external factors further modulated results: higher fat/protein/dry matter and favorable moon phases supported better outcomes, whereas poor composition, adverse phases, and harsh weather increased lower-quality categories.

Paradoxical cases

Positive

Interestingly, the classification tree also identified conditions where the first-term categories of Discardable or Medium semen quality were nonetheless associated with High or Very High fertility levels (e.g., Discardable x High in blue, Medium x High in dark blue; Supplementary Figure S2). These “compensated” outcomes were most likely to occur under thresholds reflecting biological or management buffers. For instance, doe age below two years occasionally led to Discardable x High, suggesting that heightened receptivity in young does could offset poor ejaculate quality. Similarly, small herd sizes (< 20 inseminations) increased the probability of Medium x High, likely due to more careful handling and closer management in small-scale settings. Older bucks (> 7 years) and inseminations at cooler temperatures (< 13 °C) also generated Discardable x High nodes, indicating that female physiological conditions or precise timing may rescue fertility despite low semen scores. In addition, favorable female factors—such as low somatic cell counts or early lactation (< 100 days)—were able to elevate fertility outcomes even when semen quality remained average (Medium x High). Together, these results demonstrate that high fertility is not strictly dependent on semen quality, and that female physiology, environment, and management intensity can interact to buffer against otherwise suboptimal ejaculates.

Negative

The tree revealed paradoxical outcomes where high semen quality (Excellent/Very Good) coincided with poor fertility—specifically Excellent × Low (cyan), Excellent × Very Low (purple), Very Good × Low (orange), and Very Good × Very Low (teal). These were mainly associated with adverse conditions such as late lactation (> 200 DIM), high somatic cell counts (> 4,000 × 103/mL), high altitude (> 600 m), and stressful climates (gusts, < 13 °C), highlighting the strong influence of female health and environment. Additional factors such as primiparity and unfavorable moon phases reinforced that high seminal quality alone does not guarantee fertility.

Conversely, optimal conditions—mature does (> 2 years, multiparous), favorable temperatures (15–18 °C), low altitude, calm wind, appropriate timing, larger herd size (> 50 AI), younger bucks, and low SCC—drove the most desirable outcomes: Excellent and Very Good with high fertility (light pink, darker green, light blue, pink). Poor outcomes (Medium/Discardable; teal, grey, orange, yellow) were linked to immature age, high SCC, late lactation, harsh environments, and small-scale management.

The model also identified “compensated” cases where low-quality semen achieved high fertility (e.g., Discardable × High, Medium × High) under favorable conditions such as young does, early lactation, and low SCC. Overall, these results show that fertility depends on the interactio.

Cross-validation performance of CHAID models

The CHAID model showed a resubstitution error of 0.57 (± 0.01) and a cross-validation error of 0.61 (± 0.01), indicating that it captures real structure but with some expected overfitting. Overall cross-validation accuracy was moderate (43.1%).

Classification performance varied notably across seminal quality × fertility categories. Highest accuracies were observed for well-defined groups such as Discardable × Very High (100%), Discardable × Very Low (100%), Discardable × Low (80%), Medium × Very High (76.3%), Medium × Very Low (69.9%), and Excellent × High (59.3%). Moderate accuracies occurred in several intermediate categories (≈41–54%).

Lower accuracies were found in overlapping or less represented categories, including Good × Very High (16.1%), Very Good × High (22.6%), Very Good × Very High (25.4%), and Excellent × Very Low (29.9%).

Overall, the model performed better for clearly separated groups, while intermediate categories were more frequently misclassified, likely due to overlap and class imbalance, suggesting that the 25-category structure may be overly granular for the available predictors.

Discussion

Artificial insemination fertility in small ruminants is widely recognized as a multifactorial trait influenced by male, female, environmental, and management-related factors [61]. The present study extends this understanding by showing that, in Murciano-Granadina goats, reproductive outcomes were not driven by a single dominant variable but rather emerge from the interaction of several biological and environmental gradients operating simultaneously. Identifying and prioritizing those factors with the greatest impact is essential, since establishing an insemination program for the dissemination of genetic improvement of a native breed carries significant economic implications, such as the cost of hormonal treatments, the maintenance of bucks providing semen (in the case of Murciano-Granadina, free for breeders within the Selective Nucleus), and company staff costs (veterinarians, farm workers, among others).

By analysing the interactions among semen quality, female fertility, environmental conditions, and management practices, this study aims to identify the main determinants of reproductive success within the Murciano-Granadina goat breed. In this context, fertility must be understood not only as a biological outcome but also as a key component of production efficiency, as artificial insemination (AI) is implemented within a structured selection nucleus operating under standardized conditions. Both bucks and does are preselected according to defined physiological, productive, genetic, and management criteria, meaning that inseminated females represent a non-random, relatively homogeneous subgroup aligned with routine breeding practices designed to maximize reproductive reliability. Within this controlled framework, reproductive failure entails considerable economic consequences, including loss of productive lactations, reduced availability of offspring for replacement or commercialization, and increased costs associated with resynchronization and extended kidding intervals. Therefore, identifying the factors that drive fertility is essential not only for improving reproductive performance but also for enhancing the overall efficiency and sustainability of the Murciano-Granadina official national breeding program, which is officially approved and regulated by the Spanish Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food [49].

Between 2010 and 2019, CAPRIGRAN technician performed inseminations that made it possible to analyze critical thresholds and ranges of influence across multiple dimensions, often revealing the relevance of factors not expected a priori. As the response variable reflects the combination of male fertility on the day of insemination and semen quality, it is clear that male factors condition female reproductive outcomes. Identifying which variables provided independent and non-overlapping explanatory power was crucial, leading to the elimination of those with multicollinearity problems (VIF > 5), after 36 iterations and 24 variables removed [19]. Farm, province, and village were excluded, as their homogeneity within the Selective Nucleus (shared sanitary programs, commitment to minimum AI use, linear morphological qualification, and genetic orientation) reduced their discriminative potential. Distance between AI center and farm, previously considered relevant [11], was also discarded, as semen transport under strict temperature control (4–6 °C, never less), quality standards, and monitoring protocols [50] ensured results similar to semen kept on site. Likewise, doe and buck age in months were removed due to collinearity with age in years, the latter being more practical for technicians given its direct derivation from tattoo identification codes and its link to reproductive status (nulliparous versus lactating, buck maturity and competition avoidance).

Among management factors, the number of inseminated goats was one of the most decisive, as previously reported by [11]. An experienced technician requires about one hour to inseminate 30–40 goats, meaning that in larger groups, the delay between first and last insemination increases, creating variability relative to the LH peak and compromising fertility. In this study, fertility dropped significantly when group size exceeded 63 does, consistent with the evidence that smaller groups yield better outcomes. This timing effect is reinforced by physiological differences: multiparous goats show a shorter interval from estrus onset to ovulation than nulliparous goats [17], so synchronization within groups is not uniform.

Temporal and cyclic influences also proved relevant. The decision tree first split the data by year rather than month or season, aligning with the low reproductive seasonality of Murciano-Granadina compared to other breeds such as Blanca Andaluza [77], and with the capacity of eCG treatments to override seasonal constraints [61]. Beyond management, year effects captured broader climatic differences, distinguishing wetter or hotter years [18]. The lunar phase also played a significant role, particularly the new moon, which consistently yielded higher fertility than other phases. Prior studies suggested waning moons coincide with increased progesterone [44], and that lunar cycles modulate reproduction, menstruation, and birth rates in humans, and behavior in donkeys [56]. In mares, the full moon favors fertility [23, 39], but since their reproductive season is the inverse of goats, this supports the finding that new moons were optimal for goats. The importance of melatonin in initiating reproductive cycles in goats [22], together with evidence from rabbitfish showing higher melatonin at new moon when nights were darkest [70], further corroborates this relationship.

Environmental stressors shaped fertility in significant ways. Minimum temperatures above 14.7 °C favored fertility, even when semen quality was not optimal, reflecting goats’ strong homeothermic capacity and adaptation to heat [43]. Conversely, fertility declined sharply below 9 °C, a condition also associated with poorer semen quality. While goats mitigate heat stress via evaporative cooling and metabolic adjustments [78], in cold they increase thyroxin secretion to mobilize glucose and fatty acids, raising metabolic heat production [69]. Combined with their general sensitivity to cold [68], this added physiological burden likely compromises reproduction. Rainfall exacerbated these effects, with higher precipitation coinciding with poorer fertility outcomes. Wind speed and gusts were also influential: fertility decreased above 2.9 m/s, reflecting the stress impact of wind as described by [16] and the broader role of cortisol-mediated hormonal disruption under stress [74]. Wind direction further modulated fertility, with easterly, humid winds reducing outcomes despite high semen quality, while dry, cold westerly winds allowed acceptable fertility even with average semen [1, 12, 13, 59]. These findings align with the concept of humidity and perceived heat stress being detrimental to reproductive success.

Altitude added another layer of variability, with optimal fertility observed between 451 and 720 m. In small ruminants such as sheep, fertility has been reported to peak at intermediate altitudes (~ 500 m), whereas higher elevations are associated with reduced reproductive performance due to hypoxia-induced impairments in luteal function and steroidogenesis [60]. Although the altitudinal range evaluated in the present study is comparatively moderate, similar physiological mechanisms may still operate in a subtler form. This is particularly relevant considering that altitude is intrinsically linked to barometric pressure, which declines with elevation and directly reduces oxygen partial pressure, thereby influencing ovarian activity, corpus luteum function, and early embryonic development. The strong discriminant effect observed for barometric pressure variables in the present analysis supports its role as a key mediator of the altitude–fertility relationship.

Moreover, altitude interacts closely with thermal conditions, as higher elevations are typically associated with lower ambient temperatures. While fertility in this study benefited from higher minimum temperatures, increasing altitude may counteract this effect by reducing thermal comfort and altering metabolic and endocrine responses [54]. These environmental constraints are further reflected in productive traits, as altitude has been associated with increased milk fat, free fatty acids, and somatic cell count, indicating physiological adaptation to hypoxic and energetically demanding conditions [8]. Such metabolic adjustments may shift resource allocation toward maintenance and stress response pathways, indirectly compromising reproductive efficiency.

Taken together, the optimal fertility range identified likely reflects a balance between adequate barometric pressure (ensuring sufficient oxygen availability), favorable thermal conditions, and metabolic stability, highlighting the importance of considering interacting environmental factors rather than altitude alone when evaluating reproductive performance.

On the productive side, younger does with higher milk protein percentages but lower yields performed better in terms of fertility. This is economically relevant, since milk pricing depends heavily on protein and fat [45]. Lower prolactin levels after the first full-term pregnancy [31] may also favor conception, as prolactin, while essential for lactation, can act antagonistically to fertility [41]. This trade-off between milk production and reproduction reflects long-standing antagonistic genetic–environmental relationships [51]. Seminal quality amplified this interaction: high-quality semen further boosted fertility in young, protein-rich does, underscoring the joint importance of male and female traits. However, high somatic cell counts (SCC) in milk were negatively associated with fertility, consistent with evidence in cows that inflammatory responses compromise corpus luteum function and embryo survival [30, 36, 65]. Still, management practices reducing SCC and stress can mitigate these effects [65].

Age and parity also conditioned fertility outcomes. Goats with more than five kiddings showed higher fertility, even when inseminated with semen of lower quality. Their resilience, productive longevity, and adaptation to herd stressors allow them to compensate for adverse factors (Fernández [24]. This contrasts with younger does, particularly those with fewer than three kiddings, who exhibited lower fertility despite high semen quality, diverging from findings that 3–4 parity is most favorable [13]. Such paradoxical cases may be explained by declining milk production beyond mid-life, reduced prolactin, and greater body capacity, creating a physiological state more favorable to conception [41, 67]. These results emphasize that reproductive potential cannot be inferred from semen quality or parity alone, but rather from their interaction with physiology and longevity.

Male-related factors, particularly buck age, also proved decisive. Semen quality peaked between 3 and 5 years, consistent with reproductive system maturity and greater development of the testicular vascular plexus [4, 20]. In the dataset, most ejaculates were from younger bucks (1–3 years), which may have reduced average fertility despite larger semen availability. This reinforces the importance of aligning semen extraction schedules with optimal male age.

The present study provides a comprehensive multifactorial assessment of factors influencing artificial insemination fertility in Murciano-Granadina goats using long-term field data and multivariate analytical approaches. Rather than indicating a single dominant determinant, the results suggest that fertility outcomes arise from the interaction of environmental conditions, physiological status, and management practices operating simultaneously. The strong contribution of climatic parameters to the canonical discriminant structure suggests that environmental stability plays a key role in shaping reproductive outcomes under field conditions. Variations in temperature, barometric pressure, and wind conditions may influence both sperm functionality and female reproductive physiology, thereby indirectly affecting the probability of successful conception, highlighting the sensitivity of reproductive processes to environmental variability. These findings reinforce the concept that reproductive efficiency in small ruminants is not solely dependent on semen quality or female reproductive status but was shaped by a broader ecological and management context that modulates the probability of successful conception.

The thresholds identified by the decision-tree analysis highlight how reproductive success depends on specific biological and management conditions. For example, improved fertility among multiparous does likely reflects greater physiological maturity and more stable reproductive cycles, while thresholds related to herd size and buck age may reflect differences in management intensity, semen quality dynamics, and reproductive synchronization efficiency. Importantly, the presence of both compensatory and adverse interaction patterns indicates that reproductive outcomes cannot be predicted from isolated variables alone. Situations in which moderate semen quality still resulted in high fertility, or conversely where high-quality semen yielded poor outcomes, suggest that female physiological status and environmental conditions may either buffer or suppress the reproductive potential of the ejaculate. These observations reinforce the concept that fertility in artificial insemination systems reflects the resilience of the overall reproductive system rather than the isolated performance of individual parameters.

The robustness of the analysis was supported by the use of a large longitudinal dataset collected over a ten-year period and by the application of standardized management and evaluation procedures throughout the Murciano-Granadina official national breeding program. Semen collection, laboratory assessments, estrus synchronization protocols, and insemination procedures were conducted using consistent methodologies, and all inseminations were performed by only two trained technicians operating under the same institutional procedures. These measures contributed to minimizing operator-related variability and improving the comparability of reproductive outcomes across farms and years.

Nevertheless, certain methodological considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. The analysis relied on observational field data collected across commercial farms, which reflects real production conditions but also limits the ability to infer strict causal relationships among variables. Additionally, the reduction of the dataset from 32,693 to 3,122 insemination records due to the requirement for complete semen quality information may have reduced the total number of observations available for analysis; however, this step ensured the reliable calculation of the Composite Seminal Quality Index and maintained consistency across the multivariate models. The classification framework used in the CHAID analysis, which involved 25 combined categories of seminal quality and fertility, also increased the complexity of the predictive task and likely contributed to the moderate classification accuracy observed during cross-validation. Environmental descriptors were obtained from the closest meteorological stations rather than from on-farm sensors, which may not fully capture local microclimatic variation; however, the use of standardized meteorological datasets ensured consistent environmental characterization across the entire study period.

Overall, the findings should therefore be interpreted as identifying meaningful associations and predictive patterns rather than definitive causal mechanisms. Even so, the multivariate structure revealed by the analyses highlights consistent gradients linking environmental conditions, physiological status, and herd management with reproductive outcomes. These results provide a valuable framework for improving the interpretation of fertility performance in AI programs and may contribute to the refinement of management strategies aimed at enhancing reproductive efficiency in Murciano-Granadina goat breeding systems.

Overall, the multivariate analyses highlight fertility as the result of complex interactions. Canonical functions clustered variables into thematic axes: atmospheric and climatic stressors (barometric pressure, rainfall, wind), temporal and lunar influences, extraction-site effects, herd-scale and management factors, milk composition and health axes, event-driven disruptions like gusts, and integrative production–lactation contrasts. The CHAID decision tree further refined these relationships, identifying thresholds such as doe age, buck age, herd size, SCC, temperature, altitude, and days in milk as decisive splits. Importantly, it also revealed paradoxical cases where fertility outcomes contradicted expectations: goats or bucks thriving under seemingly adverse conditions, or failing under favorable ones. Fertility thus emerges not from single dominant drivers but from multilayered, often compensatory interactions between physiology, environment, management, and production. From a practical perspective, the thresholds and interaction patterns identified in this study may provide useful reference points for optimizing insemination scheduling and management strategies within international commercial Murciano-Granadina breeding programs.

Conclusions

Overall, the findings underscore that improving artificial insemination success in Murciano-Granadina goats requires integrated strategies combining optimal buck selection, appropriate female management, and careful consideration of environmental and climatic thresholds. The multifactorial approach adopted in this study highlights how reproductive outcomes arise from the interaction between male, female, management, and environmental factors. Recognizing and managing these interacting components can help breeding programs enhance reproductive efficiency, improve genetic dissemination, and strengthen the long-term sustainability and profitability of dairy goat production systems.
