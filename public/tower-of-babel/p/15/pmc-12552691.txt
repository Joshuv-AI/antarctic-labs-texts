Effect of intrapulpal cryoanesthesia on intraoperative pain during root canal treatment in mandibular molars: a double blinded randomized control trial
Authors: Sherwit Eldafrawi, Tarek Elsewify, Ranya Elemam, Bassem Eid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the impact of cooling the anesthetic solution prior to administering intra-pulpal anesthesia on the severity of intraoperative pain during root canal treatment in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and symptomatic apical periodontitis. Forty patients with mandibular molars diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and symptomatic apical periodontitis received root canal treatment by a single operator following a standardized technique. Patients received an intra-pulpal injection of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine and were randomly and equally assigned to two groups based on the temperature of the cartridge. The room-temperature group received a cartridge that was kept at 25–26 °C while for the cryoanesthesia group, the cartridge was cooled to a temperature of 4–5 °C. The HP-VAS scale was used to evaluate the patients’ preoperative, intraoperative pre-injection, and intraoperative post-injection pain scores. The success rate was calculated by the lack of additional anesthesia following the intra-pulpal injection. Pain scores were statistically analyzed at a significance level of 0.05. Both the cryoanesthesia and the room-temperature groups were effective in significantly reducing the severity of intraoperative pain, where p < 0.001 for both groups. The cryoanesthesia group showed a 100% success rate compared to 80% for the room-temperature group without a statistically significant difference (p = 0.106). The difference in pain score reduction in both groups, however, was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Cryoanesthesia yielded more profound pulpal anesthesia compared to the room temperature intra-pulpal injection.

Introduction

Achieving pulpal anesthesia in mandibular molars diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis is done through the inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB)1, which demonstrates a failure rate of 44–81%2–6. Certain hurdles may present to the clinician regarding the IANB, such as anesthetic failure, non-continuous anesthesia, delayed onset, and limited duration of action. In certain cases, despite the utilization of both IANB and supplemental anesthesia in the form of infiltrations, intra-ligamentary, or intra-osseous injections, complete pulpal anesthesia is yet to be achieved, and the incidence of intra-operative pain persists. This is seen in 5–10% of mandibular posterior teeth diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis7. In such cases, intra-pulpal injections may be put to use.

Intra-pulpal anesthesia refers to a technique in which the anesthetic solution is deposited directly into the pulp by wedging the needle either into an exposure in the pulp chamber or into the canal space itself. One of the major advantages of intra-pulpal anesthesia compared to other supplemental techniques is that it does not require removal of the rubber dam, thus reducing the risk of leakage throughout the procedure.

The exact mechanism by which intra-pulpal anesthesia works remains uncertain. A recent literature review conducted by Penukonda et al.8, suggests two possible mechanisms, the first of which is direct nerve inhibition. This phenomenon refers to the binding of sodium channels by the anesthetic agent, thus preventing the entry of sodium ions, which are essential for the transmission of nerve signals. The second mechanism suggested is related to the incidence of nerve fiber degeneration as a direct result of the pressure applied during the administration of intra-pulpal anesthesia. The exact role of pressure, however, is yet to be fully understood.

This technique, however, relies heavily on the presence of back pressure during injection9–11. While intra-pulpal injections are typically done using an anesthetic solution, some authors have stated that the technique is just as effective using a sterile saline solution, as long as the injection is performed under sufficient back pressure. This was explored by Birchfield et al.12, in a double-blinded procedure, where results revealed complete profound pulpal anesthesia in all injections administered under back pressure. Similar results were obtained during a later double-blinded study conducted by VanGheluwe et al.13, wherein no statistically significant difference was found between the groups. This indicates that back-pressure plays a more significant role than the contents of the cartridge in the effectiveness of intra-pulpal anesthesia. This, however, remains controversial due to the scarcity of research regarding the topic.

Cryotherapy is yet another form of pain management that has been explored, with several ways in which it can be combined with multiple anesthetic techniques in order to reduce the incidence of intraoperative pain. This concept utilizes cold temperatures, which are known to induce vasoconstriction, as well as reduce edema, which in turn, leads to a significant reduction in the sensation of pain. It was proposed earlier in endodontics as a way to decrease postoperative pain by utilising cold irrigant solutions or submucosal infiltration, after which its potential to decrease the incidence of intraoperative pain was explored14–16.

An innovative approach was investigated by Gurucharan et al.17, wherein the anesthetic solution itself was cooled in order to serve as the cryotherapy application and yielded promising results. The same concept was further studied by Aggarwal et al.18, but in regards to the effect of cooling the anesthetic solution on the success of an intra-ligamentary injection, rather than the initial IANB with no significant difference. The effect of cryoanesthetic efficiency has been become an area of interest for research, particularly in regards to permanent molars. Elheeny et al.19,20, however, posed the question of whether age plays a role on the efficiency of cryotherapy as an anesthetic adjunct. The overall success rate of the group receiving cryotherapy was 79.2%, which was significantly higher than that of the control group, which exhibited a success rate of 50.6%. The second study20 showed a significant increase in the anesthetic success rate in the cryotherapy group (59.2%), in comparison to the control group (40.8%).

There is a scarcity of research on cryoanesthesia in combination with supplementary anesthetic techniques. The aim of this study is to measure the effect of cooling the anesthetic solution prior to administering intra-pulpal anesthesia on intraoperative pain during root canal treatment of mandibular molars diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis. The null hypothesis tested is that there is no statistically significant difference in intraoperative pain reduction using cryoanesthesia and room-temperature intra-pulpal anesthsia during root canal treatment in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis.

Materials and methods

This randomised clinical trial has been written according to Preferred Reporting Items for RAndomized Trials in Endodontics (PRIRATE) 2020 guidelines as shown in Fig. 1.

Ethical approval and trial registration

This study has received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Gulf Medical University, Ajman, United Arab Emirates, under the reference number IRB-COD-STD-109-JUNE-2023. The clinical trial was registered under NCT06194721 on clinicaltrial.gov on 08/01/2024. Patients were treated according to the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki21 and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study design & randomization

This study was designed to be a double-blinded randomized controlled trial. The sample was randomized using two techniques. Initially, randomizer.org was utilized to provide two random sets of 20 numbers per set, wherein the numbers belonging to the first one were designated to the room-temperature group, and those belonging to the second were designated to the cryoanesthesia group. All 40 numbers were written down on pieces of paper, carefully folded, and placed in a box. Upon being selected for the clinical trial, each patient was instructed to draw a number, which corresponded to one of the two groups.

Two hundred and thirty-seven patients were screened, out of whom one hundred and twenty-nine met the inclusion criteria to be initially recruited for the study, all of whom were presented with the informed consent form. The recruitment process continued until the desired sample size of forty patients requiring intra-pulpal anesthesia was reached. Out of the one hundred and twenty-nine, fifty did not require supplemental anesthesia following the initial IANB, and were thus excluded from the study. Additionally, thirty-six patients reported a response to the EPT following the initial IANB, deeming it a failure, after which they were excluded from the study, and provided with an additional IANB. Three patients were excluded because they reported pain while drilling in dentin, making them illegible to receive an intra-pulpal injection. The number drawn by those who were excluded was withdrawn and returned to the box (Fig. 1).

All patients participating in this study were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Post-graduate Dental Center at Thumbay University Hospital, between the dates of June 6th 2023 to December 6th 2024, conforming to the following criteria:

Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated based on assuming a confidence level of 95% and a study power of 80%. According to a pilot study performed on 20 patients (n = 10), the intraoperative mean HP-VAS score was 47.54 ± 38.58 for the room-temperature group and 1.64 ± 6.15 for the cryoanesthesia group. Based on the comparison between independent means using the highest SD of 38.58 to ensure enough study power, a minimum sample size of 13 patients was required per group (n = 13), yielding an effect size of 1.190. This was increased to 40 patients (n = 20) to increase the power of the study. The pilot and main phases of the study were conducted under an identical, pre-specified protocol and a single ethical approval. Pilot participants were retained in the final analysis.

Patient management

Diagnosis

The chief complaint was recorded, after which and medical and dental history were obtained. Thorough extraoral and intraoral examinations were carried out in order to confirm the target diagnosis of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and symptomatic apical periodontitis, including an intraoral periapical radiograph for the tooth in question.

Pulp vitality was confirmed upon eliciting a response using an electric pulp tester, D640 Digitest II Pulp Vitality Tester (Parkell, Ohio, USA). The status of the pulp was then assessed using a cold test in the form of dousing a cotton pellet with Endo-Ice refrigerant (Cotene, Ohio, USA). Additionally, a periapical diagnosis was reached through a combination of mobility, palpation, and percussion tests, in addition to the preoperative radiograph. The patients were informed of the treatment plan and their inclusion in the study, and they signed an informed consent.

Pain assessment

All pain assessments throughout this study, pre- and postoperatively, were conducted by the second author using Heft-Parker Visual Analogue Scale (HP-VAS). It was explained to the patients that one end of the 170 mm horizontal line represents no pain (score 0), and the other represents the maximum possible level of pain (score 170). In between these two extremes, a score of 23 represents faint pain, 36 represents weak pain, and 54 represents mild pain. The next pain scores on the scale represent moderate, strong, and intense pain 85, 114, and 144, respectively.

Endodontic procedure

All the treatment was performed by the first author. An IANB using one cartridge containing 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Septodont, Paris, France), was administered using a 27-gauge, 1½-inch needle. Anesthetic success was assessed 10 min post-injection using a subjective method, lip numbness, and an objective method, cold test and an EPT were performed. To be included in this study, no response to the cold test, as well as two consecutive negative responses to the EPT’s maximum setting were required22. If not, the IANB was deemed unsuccessful, and the patient was excluded from the study.

Single-tooth isolation was achieved using a rubber dam sheet, and an access cavity was prepared. Those who reported pain associated with an exposure of the pulp chamber were presented with a second HP-VAS. Those who reported pain while drilling within dentin were excluded from the study and provided with an additional IANB.

Intra-pulpal injection

Room-temperature group

A 27-gauge needle was bent and advanced into the pulp chamber opening until resistance was felt, and injected with one cartridge of 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine kept at room temperature. In instances where the opening was too large, the needle was surrounded by a piece of sterile cotton gauze to achieve the desired resistance. The solution was then deposited under back pressure, and the process of completing the access cavity preparation was resumed.

Cryoanesthesia group

The same protocol was followed except for the cartridge, that has been cooled to a temperature of 4–5 °C17,18, where the temperature was confirmed using a built-in thermometer (Cooper Atkins, Connecticut, USA). Upon removal from the refrigerator, the temperature of the cartridge was preserved by placing it in a box containing ice, as well as a digital thermometer. Intraoperative post-injection pain score was recorded.

Failure of the intra-pulpal anesthesia was identified by the patient’s inability to tolerate the remainder of the access cavity preparation process, wherein an additional intra-ligamentary injection was administered. Anesthetic success was defined as the patient’s ability to proceed with the treatment without requiring any additional anesthesia following the intra-pulpal injection. The anesthetic success rate was calculated.

Biomechanical Preparation

Following the completion of the access cavity preparation, the pulp tissue was extirpated, a glide path was created, and coronal pre-flaring was done. The working length was determined, and instrumentation was done using the ProTaper Gold system (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The final irrigation protocol for both groups consisted of 5mL of 3% NaOCl per canal, activated using passive ultrasonic activation (PUI) for 30 s. This was washed off using 5mL of sterile saline, prior to injecting 1mL of 17% EDTA, which was left in the canal for 1 min, and activated ultrasonically for 30 s.

Obturation

A single cone obturation was performed using matching gutta-percha cones and Totalfill bioceramic sealer (FKG, Le Crêt-du-Locle, Switzerland) after confirmation of the master cone clinically and radiographically. A final composite restoration was placed for all patients immediately after obturation.

Statistical analysis

Categorical and ordinal data are presented as frequencies and percentages. Categorical data (gender, tooth type, and the need for additional anesthesia) are compared using Fisher’s exact test. Ordinal data (pain intensity) are compared using the Mann-Whitney U test for intergroup comparisons and Friedman’s test, followed by Nemenyi’s post hoc test for intragroup comparisons. Numerical data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) values. They were tested for normality by viewing the distribution and using Shapiro-Wilk’s test. Age data were normally distributed and were compared using an independent t-test. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate method. Correlation analysis was made using Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficient. The significance level was set at p < 0.05 for all tests. Statistical analysis was performed with R statistical analysis software version 4.4.2 for Windows.

All the cases in the cryoanesthesia group didn’t need additional anesthesia. In contrast, 4(20%) cases in the room temperature group needed an additional injection. However, the difference between both groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.106).

Discussion

The occurrence of intraoperative pain remains a commonly encountered challenge and inconvenience for both the practitioner as well as the patient. Incidence of intraoperative pain, as well as its association with tooth type, has been reported to range from 6.1%, as cited by Kayaoglu et al.23 to 10.3%, as per Yucel et al.24.

The intra-pulpal technique was explored in this study due to the clear scarcity of literature concerning this anesthetic method. Moreover, several clinical trials25–29 exploring the efficacy of different supplementary anesthetic techniques have reported administering intra-pulpal anesthesia in instances of failure, after which complete, profound pulpal anesthesia was obtained.

No statistically significant difference was shown between the two study groups in terms of age, gender, and preoperative pain scores. This aids in the standardization process, thus ensuring that no external factors other than the variable being tested play a role in the results obtained.

The incidence of non-profound pulpal anesthesia as a result of an IANB remains a common concern among practitioners performing root canal treatment on mandibular molars diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis. Argueta-Figueroa et al.30, reported that mandibular posterior teeth diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis demonstrated an IANB success rate of 64.2%, while those with an asymptomatic status demonstrated a higher success rate of 86.9%. This claim was further supported by Gupta et al.31, Kayaoglu et al.23, and Segura-Egea et al.32.

Aggarwal et al.33, noted a positive correlation between pre-operative and intra-operative pain scores. This was found to be in agreement with the findings of the present study, where a statistically significant, moderate positive correlation was noted.

In order to ensure accurate assessment of pulpal anesthesia following the initial IANB, a combination of subjective measures (lip numbness) as well as both a cold test and an electric pulp test were conducted34–38.

The definition of ‘anesthetic success’ varies from one author to another, with some studies citing complete absence of intraoperative pain22,39, while others consider ‘mild’ pain as negligible26,40,41. Therefore, in an attempt to overcome the drawback of the subjectivity of the sensation of pain among patients, anesthetic success, in this study, was defined as the patient’s ability to proceed with the treatment without requiring any additional anesthesia. This interpretation of successful anesthesia was also adopted by Vangheluwe et al.13. Following this definition, the cryoanesthesia group displayed a success rate of 100%, while the room temperature group displayed 80%, wherein the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.106).

In this clinical trial, all intra-pulpal injections were administered under strong back-pressure, the importance of which has been highlighted within the literature. Birchfield at el.12, found that regardless of the solution used, as long as back-pressure is present, pulpal anesthesia will be achieved. Similar results were also shown by Vangheluwe et al.13. This aligns with the results of the current study as both experimental groups experienced a significant reduction in intra-operative pain following the intra-pulpal injection (p < 0.001). The volume of anesthetic solution administered as in intra-pulpal injection in this study was 0.2mL, in accordance with the recommended volume mentioned throughout the present literature7,12,42,43.

Cryotherapy has been abundantly explored within literature, mainly in the form of a final irrigant in order to reduce the incidence of postoperative pain. This theory was corroborated by several authors, including Almasoud et al.44,, Shalabi et al.45, and Vera et al.44,46. While cryotherapy has been widely investigated regarding its potential in post-operative pain reduction, limited evidence exists to conclude its effect on intra-operative pain.

Cryoanesthesia refers to the process of reducing the temperature of peripheral nerves to diminish the sensation of pain. Therefore, significant vasoconstriction is achieved, with subsequent pain reduction47. In addition to enhanced efficiency, lower temperatures may also enhance the anesthetic’s binding to proteins of the nerve membrane, thus prolonging its duration of action48.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess cryoanesthesia in intra-pulpal injections. The concept of cryoanesthesia in the form of cooling the anesthetic solution has only been explored by two previous authors, with contradictory results. In the present study, the null hypothesis was accepted, citing no statistically significant difference in intra-pulpal anesthetic success between the cryoanesthesia and the room-temperature group. In agreement with this study, Aggarwal et al.18, stated no statistically significant difference between the cryoanesthesia and room-temperature groups in regards to a supplementary intra-ligamentary injection. It is, however, important to note that while our statistical analysis displayed no significant difference in need for additional anesthesia, a clinical difference in post-injection pain scores exists. In the cryoanesthesia group, 100% reported no incidence of any form of post-injection intra-operative pain, while this was only true for 40% of the room temperature group. Additionally, 15% of the room temperature group experienced ‘weak’ pain, while 10% and 35% reported ‘mild’ and ‘moderate’ pain, respectively. Those who requested an additional injection, thus deeming the anesthesia a failure, all reported ‘moderate’ post-injection pain.

On the other hand, Gurucharan et al.17, cited a statistically significant difference between the cryoanesthesia and room-temperature groups in regard to anesthetic success. This difference in results, however, can be attributed to the difference in the selected sample in comparison to our study. While our study was conducted on mandibular molars, the aforementioned clinical trial was carried out on maxillary molars, wherein the anesthetic solution was cooled prior to administering a buccal infiltration.

Limitations of the current study include the lack of exploration of other factors related to the incidence and severity of intraoperative pain, such as patient anxiety and apprehension, in addition to the potential influence of the placebo effect, as the patients were informed about the study. Methods of pain assessment remain reliant on the patient and are thus considered subjective.

There exists a clear lack of consensus on whether or not cooling the anesthetic solution plays a role in increasing anesthetic potency. This controversy is directly linked to the scarcity of literature in the field of cryoanesthesia. Further trials should be carried out to investigate the relationship between anesthetic success and a lower temperature of the solution used among various anesthetic techniques. Additional research should also be conducted to close the large gap in evidence between intra-pulpal anesthesia compared to other supplemental techniques.

This study found that both the cryoanesthesia and the room-temperature groups were successful in reducing the severity of intraoperative pain. The cryoanesthesia group, however, resulted in more profound pulpal anesthesia compared to the room temperature group, thus providing the patient with a procedure with the least amount of pain possible.
