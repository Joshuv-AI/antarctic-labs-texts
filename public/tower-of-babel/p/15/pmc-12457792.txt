Statistical cure fraction in patients with borderline resectable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma undergoing neoadjuvant therapy followed by radical resection: a secondary analysis of reconstructed individual patient data
Authors: Yong Hyun Jang, Ru Ri Lee, Yongkeun Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAlthough neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has been applied in patients with borderline resectable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (BR-PDAC), limited data on long-term outcomes are available to support its role as a potentially curable therapeutic option. This study assessed the probability of being statistically cured among these patients.MethodsThe literature search was conducted focusing on previous studies that investigated the long-term recurrence-free survival rates of patients with BR-PDAC after NAT followed by radical resection. The reference cohort data were extracted from studies including patients undergoing upfront radical resection for resectable PDAC. Pseudo-individual patient data were reconstructed from the original papers. A non-mixture cure model was adopted to estimate the statistical cure fraction.ResultsTwelve retrospective studies reporting long-term recurrence-free survival after NAT followed by radical resection for patients with BR-PDAC were secondarily analyzed. The probability of being statistically cured in these patients was 8.1% (95% confidence interval [CI], 5.4%–11.5%), which was lower than that of the reference cohort of 5 retrospective studies (14.7%; 95% CI, 13.2%–16.4%). There was a statistically significant difference between the 2 groups (hazard ratio, 1.110; 95% CI, 1.007–1.224).ConclusionFrom this study, we concluded that a cure can be expected in around eight percent of patients with BR-PDAC after NAT followed by radical resection, which would be helpful in counseling patients, as well as deciding whether to perform the surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic cancer ranks as the 12th most common cancer and the 6th leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [12]. Despite advances in diagnostic techniques, surgical methods, and systemic therapies, it remains one of the deadliest solid tumors. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), the most prevalent subtype of pancreatic cancer, accounts for over 90% of cases [3]. Radical resection offers the best chance for long-term survival in PDAC [4]. However, many patients present with unresectable disease at diagnosis due to late detection, rapid tumor progression, or poorly controlled comorbidities. From a clinical perspective, PDAC is categorized into a respectable, locally advanced, and metastatic stages. Approximately 80% of PDAC cases are diagnosed at either the locally advanced or metastatic stage, rendering radical resection infeasible. In the absence of reliable early detection guidelines, aggressive surgical approaches for the locally advanced stage, such as neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) and en bloc resection of tumor-invaded vessels, have been proposed to improve outcomes [3].

The term “borderline resectable PDAC” (BR-PDAC) emerged with the advent of high-resolution imaging, which enables precise evaluation of vascular involvement [5]. BR-PDAC refers to tumors with limited vascular invasion that may still be amenable to radical resection under certain conditions [6]. Currently, the recommended approach for treating BR-PDAC often involves NAT including chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy followed by reassessment to determine the potential for surgical tumor removal, which has marked a significant advancement in the treatment of BR-PDAC, improving overall survival and expanding therapeutic options [3].

NAT offers several advantages, such as facilitating tumor resectability, converting initially unresectable tumors into resectable ones, and targeting micrometastases. However, it also poses risks, including adverse effects, deterioration of the patient’s general condition, continued tumor growth despite therapy, and the potential emergence of new distant metastases due to delayed surgery [7]. Studies indicate that approximately 2/3 of patients with BR-PDAC successfully undergo radical resection following NAT, while the remaining 1/3 experience disease progression during initial treatment [89].

Demonstrating clear survival benefits and the potential for cure are essential to guide patient decisions and justify the pursuit of this aggressive treatment regimen. Given that resectable PDAC is widely regarded as the subgroup with the best prognosis and highest likelihood of cure, it is logical and clinically meaningful to use it as a reference for comparing the outcomes of surgical treatment in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer, such as those with BR-PDAC. While recent studies suggest that NAT followed by radical resection improves survival in BR-PDAC patients [4810], direct comparisons of postsurgical cure rates between patients with BR-PDAC treated with this approach and those with resectable PDAC undergoing upfront surgery are limited. Addressing this gap is essential in providing clinicians and patients with evidence-based insights into the potential benefits of this treatment approach. This study aims to estimate the statistical cure fraction in patients with BR-PDAC treated with NAT followed by radical resection and to compare this outcome with existing data on patients with resectable PDAC who underwent upfront surgery.

METHODS

Literature search

A comprehensive search was conducted to identify prospective or retrospective observational studies reporting survival outcomes in patients with BR-PDAC treated with NAT followed by radical resection. The search was performed in MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and WorldWideScience databases, covering publications from January 2001 to December 2024. This timeframe was selected because the first article to report the concept of BR-PDAC was published in 2001, using the term “marginal resectable” [11]. Titles and abstracts of retrieved articles were screened to exclude irrelevant studies, and reference lists of review articles were examined for additional relevant publications. Only studies published in English were included. Selected articles were then reviewed by all 3 investigators using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This study is based solely on previously published data and does not involve identifiable patient information. The need for ethical approval was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Gibbeum Hospital (No. 001-250804-001).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they provided recurrence-free survival data for patients with BR-PDAC who underwent NAT followed by radical resection, with Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves available for analysis. Studies that combined recurrence-free survival data for BR-PDAC with resectable or locally advanced PDAC were excluded unless they reported separate survival results for BR-PDAC, allowing data extraction. Only studies with long-term recurrence-free survival data were included, excluding those reporting outcomes of 3 years or less. In cases where multiple studies from the same institution presented overlapping data, the most recent publication was selected.

To evaluate the cure fraction in patients with BR-PDAC treated with NAT followed by radical resection, we also included recent studies reporting long-term recurrence-free survival in resectable PDAC patients treated with upfront surgery. These patients were used as a reference cohort to compare their cure fraction with that of BR-PDAC patients.

Survival data extraction and reconstruction of Kaplan-Meier curves

To analyze survival outcomes comprehensively, KM survival curves from the included original articles were digitized using DigitizeIt software (Bormisoft, Braunschweig, Germany; www.digitizeit.de). Time and survival probability coordinates were extracted, and risk tables were used when available. Event counts were obtained from the study text. Pseudo-individual patient data (IPD) were reconstructed using an advanced algorithm to inversely solve KM equations, enabling precise survival analysis [12].

Non-mixture cure model for analyzing long-term survivors

The cure model assumes that a subset of patients may never experience disease recurrence, a concept particularly relevant in oncology. This assumption allows for estimating the “cure fraction,” or the proportion of patients considered cured. Additionally, “time-to-cure” refers to the minimum survival time required to evaluate the potential for cure. In this study, a non-mixture cure model was employed to estimate the proportion of BR-PDAC patients treated with NAT followed by radical resection who could be classified as cured.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (ver. 4.3.0, The R Foundation). The “survival” and “survminer” R packages were used for survival analysis, hazard ratio (HR) calculations, and survival curve plotting. For non-mixture cure model analysis, the “flexsurvcure” package was utilized to estimate cure fractions and time-to-cure metrics.

RESULTS

Twelve studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the analysis [8910131415161718192021]. The basic demographics and treatment regimens of the patients included in these studies are outlined in Table 1. The publication years of the studies ranged from 2013 to 2024. Of the 12 studies reporting recurrence-free survival after NAT followed by radical resection for BR-PDAC, 6 were conducted in Eastern countries, and 6 were based on data from Western countries. The total patient cohort comprised 1,156 individuals, of whom 760 completed both NAT and radical resection and were included in the final analysis. For comparison, a reference cohort was established using recently published studies that reported postoperative outcomes of resectable PDAC patients undergoing upfront radical resection without NAT [2223242526]. These reference studies were single-center retrospective cohort analyses providing long-term recurrence-free survival data.

To compare survival outcomes, the HR was defined as the relative risk of recurrence or death in BR-PDAC patients compared to patients in the reference cohort. Since the HR was not directly available in the original texts, it was estimated by reconstructing KM data from published survival curves. KM curves for BR-PDAC patients and the reference cohort were digitized and reconstructed individually for all 17 included studies. Using the reconstructed KM survival data, pseudo-IPD for both groups were generated, enabling HR estimation.

The BR-PDAC group included 760 patients, while the reference cohort consisted of 3,690 patients. KM analysis revealed recurrence-free survival rates for the BR-PDAC group at 1, 3, and 5 years of 57.1%, 18.4%, and 14.9%, respectively. Corresponding rates for the reference cohort were 52.2%, 25.7%, and 20.4%. While the BR-PDAC group exhibited inferior recurrence-free survival compared to the reference cohort, the difference was not statistically significant (HR, 1.075; 95% CI, 0.959–1.204; P = 0.140) (Fig. 1).

Cure fractions

The cure fraction, as determined by the non-mixture cure model, was 14.7% (95% CI, 13.2%–16.4%) for resectable PDAC patients treated with upfront radical resection, with a time to statistical cure of 104.8 months (95% CI, 52.9–121.4 months) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the cure fraction for BR-PDAC patients treated with NAT followed by radical resection was 8.1% (95% CI, 5.4%–11.5%) (Fig. 2B). The non-mixture cure model revealed a significantly lower proportion of cured patients in the BR-PDAC group compared to the reference cohort (HR, 1.110; 95% CI, 1.007–1.224). Due to limited long-term survival data across the included studies, it was not possible to accurately estimate the time to statistical cure for the BR-PDAC patient group. The insufficient follow-up duration in the published survival curves limited the model’s ability to reliably assess when the probability of recurrence approaches zero.

DISCUSSION

A key consideration for treatment of BR-PDAC is whether the advantages of NAT followed by radical resection justify the associated risks, particularly given that some patients may not ultimately undergo surgery after completing NAT. While a definitive conclusion cannot be drawn, the notable survival benefit may serve as evidence supporting the justification of the risks associated with NAT followed by radical resection for BR-PDAC [4810]. However, these studies primarily focus on survival benefits rather than directly addressing the curability of the disease. Cure model analysis, which has been applied in various conditions, offers a method for estimating the likelihood of cure [27]. In this study, we utilized cure modeling to evaluate the probability of achieving a cure in patients with BR-PDAC treated with NAT followed by radical resection. Additionally, we compared the statistical cure fractions between this group and patients with resectable PDAC who underwent upfront surgery, using the latter as a reference. Notably, approximately 8% of patients with BR-PDAC were estimated to achieve a cure following NAT and radical resection, providing valuable information for patient counseling and decision-making regarding treatment. Although the resectable PDAC group exhibited statistically better outcomes, it is noteworthy that the BR-PDAC group also demonstrated a measurable chance of cure, highlighting the potential effectiveness of NAT followed by radical resection in achieving long-term survival. This result underscores the potential value of this intensive approach and serves as encouragement for patients to commit to the treatment. To our knowledge, this study is the first to indirectly estimate the cure fraction in BR-PDAC patients after NAT followed by radical resection, addressing a critical gap in the literature.

We selected patients with resectable PDAC who underwent upfront radical resection as the reference population in this study because they represent the current gold standard for achieving optimal survival outcomes in pancreatic cancer management. This group demonstrated the best-case scenario in terms of long-term survival and potential for cure, providing a benchmark against which the outcomes of NAT followed by radical resection for BR-PDAC can be meaningfully assessed. By comparing these 2 cohorts, we aimed to address a critical clinical question: can aggressive NAT-based treatment strategies for BR-PDAC enable patients to achieve survival outcomes comparable to those with initially resectable disease? Moreover, the resectable PDAC cohort embodies a highly relevant and aspirational treatment goal for patients and clinicians. Patients with BR-PDAC often face uncertain prognoses and challenging treatment regimens; thus, demonstrating that NAT and radical resection can bridge the survival gap between these 2 populations, offering significant justification for the burdensome and resource-intensive nature of NAT. Importantly, this comparison provides a tangible measure of success that can be used to counsel patients, optimize treatment strategies, and enhance shared decision-making. Finally, choosing the resectable PDAC cohort acknowledges the clinical heterogeneity of pancreatic cancer. Unlike general population-based survival metrics, this focused comparison ensures that both groups share a surgical intent, allowing for a fair evaluation of the effectiveness of NAT while minimizing confounding factors such as comorbidities and eligibility for curative surgery. This choice reinforces the relevance of our findings to real-world clinical practice and underscores the importance of advancing treatment paradigms for borderline resectable disease.

Over the years, chemotherapy has emerged as a cornerstone in the treatment of pancreatic cancer, complementing surgical interventions. The introduction of gemcitabine marked a significant milestone, offering a survival benefit for advanced pancreatic cancer. This progress was further amplified by the development of combination regimens such as FOLFIRINOX (folinic acid + fluorouracil + irinotecan + oxaliplatin), which demonstrated superior efficacy in improving survival outcomes. These advancements in systemic therapy have not only enhanced treatment options but also redefined the management of BR-PDAC, enabling more patients to become candidates for radical resection after NAT. Upfront surgery is no longer advised for BR-PDAC, even when technically feasible. However, there remains no consensus on the optimal neoadjuvant regimen. Recent studies highlight the efficacy of FOLFIRINOX for BR-PDAC, although earlier studies primarily relied on gemcitabine-based regimens. All studies to date, however, have been retrospective in nature [171920]. The role of concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) in BR-PDAC remains uncertain, with no agreement on its ideal regimen, dosage, or timing. While some studies have suggested that CCRT enhances the likelihood of negative surgical margins, lowers local recurrence rates, and extends survival, others have found no significant advantage over chemotherapy alone [28].

Cure model analysis is a specialized form of survival analysis that accounts for the possibility that some patients may never experience disease recurrence or death [29]. Its value lies in its ability to estimate the probability of cure, providing actionable insights for both clinical decision-making and postoperative surveillance planning. Cure model analysis can also guide treatment strategies for comorbid conditions in affected patients. There are 2 main types of cure models: mixture and non-mixture. Mixture models assume that the population consists of 2 subgroups—those cured and those still at risk—combining their survival functions into a weighted average. By contrast, non-mixture models directly estimate the population’s survival function using flexible mathematical forms, allowing for a non-zero survival probability at an infinite time. The non-mixture cure model is often better suited for long-term cancer survival analysis because it provides a more realistic and flexible representation of survival dynamics, does not assume a predefined cure fraction, and allows estimation of time to statistical cure, which is crucial in understanding treatment effectiveness. We employed a non-mixture cure model for this study, providing a robust framework for evaluating long-term outcomes in patients with BR-PDAC.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, as this was a secondary analysis, we could not extract key factors influencing tumor recurrence, such as tumor size, nodal involvement, and vascular invasion. Second, the analysis was based solely on retrospective studies, which may introduce biases and limit the strength of the evidence. Furthermore, our comparison focused exclusively on the cure fraction between patients with BR-PDAC who received NAT followed by radical resection and those with resectable PDAC who underwent upfront radical resection. However, the role of NAT in resectable PDAC remains a topic of ongoing debate and represents an important avenue for future research.

In conclusion, this study provides statistical evidence supporting the potential for cure in patients with BR-PDAC treated with NAT followed by radical resection. This approach can be considered a reasonable treatment option, offering hope for achieving a cure in this challenging patient population.
