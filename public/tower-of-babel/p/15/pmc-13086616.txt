The Effect of an Art Practice‐Based Psycho‐Training Program on Psychological Well‐Being, Care Burden, Expression of Emotions, and Coping Levels of Mothers Who Have Children With Special Needs
Authors: Havva Kaçan, Funda Gümüş, Nurhan Eren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study was conducted as a feasibility and proof‐of‐concept intervention to examine whether an art‐based psychoeducational program may be associated with changes in the psychological well‐being of mothers of children with special needs. Psychological well‐being was considered the primary outcome, while caregiver burden, emotional expression, and coping levels were examined as secondary outcomes.DesignThis single‐group, quasi‐experimental study was conducted using a pretest, posttest, and follow‐up test.MethodsThe study sample consisted of 30 mothers of children with special needs. Data were collected using an Information Form, Psychological Well‐Being Scale (PWBS), Scales, Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), Emotional Expression Scale (EES), and Stress Coping Styles Scale (SCSS). A setting was created for mothers to interact within the group, and 15 different workshops, based on training and art, were offered. At the end of the study, a technical visit was conducted to a women's cooperative to support the economic empowerment of mothers. Data were analysed using numbers, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Repeated‐measures ANOVA and complementary Bonferroni tests were used for comparisons of within‐group measurements.ResultsCaregiver burden scores were found to be 44.90 in the pre‐test, 20.67 in the post‐test, and 17.33 in the follow‐up phase; a significant and marked decrease was observed over time (F = 69.374, p < 0.001, Partial Eta Squared = 0.832). Pairwise comparisons showed statistically significant decreases between each measurement phase. In terms of coping styles with stress, the intervention increased self‐confident, optimistic, and social support‐seeking approaches in the post‐test and follow‐up phases, while significantly decreasing helpless and submissive approaches. These positive changes were observed to continue in the follow‐up measurement. Psychological well‐being scores were measured as 345.20 (±73.93) in the pre‐test, 361.13 (±73.10) in the post‐test, and 382.27 (±73.13) in the follow‐up phase; The time effect was found to be statistically significant and very strong (F = 479.324, p < 0.001, Partial Eta Squared = 0.943). The findings indicate that the intervention has a high effect size in reducing caregiver burden and increasing psychological well‐being and functional coping styles.ConclusionVarious art workshops and trainings regarding care burden, psychological well‐being and emotional expression are effective and are also recommended for mothers with children with special needs.Patient or Public ContributionMothers of children with special needs contributed to this study by participating voluntarily in the art‐based psycho‐training program. Prior to the intervention, participants shared their experiences and needs related to coping with caregiving challenges, which informed the implementation process. Following the program, mothers provided feedback on the content and activities, contributing to the interpretation of the study outcomes. Patients or the public were not involved in the study design, data analysis, or manuscript preparation.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06535100Keywords: art practices, care burden, coping, mental well‐being, mother, psycho‐training, special individual

Introduction

Individuals with special needs, unlike individuals with typical development, are defined as individuals with mental, visual, hearing, physical disabilities, language and speech disorders, attention deficit and hyperactivity problems, learning difficulties, autism or gifted individuals (Data Resource Center for Child and Adolescent Health 2021). UNICEF published a report estimating that approximately 240 million children worldwide had special needs in 2021 (UNICEF 2021). It is estimated that there are approximately 13.5 million children who have special needs in the United States, and half of the children need caregivers (Bamber et al. 2023). In Turkey, the prevalence of individuals with special needs has been reported as 12.29%, corresponding to approximately 8–8.5 million people in the total population (Varış and Hekim 2017). According to the World Bank and the Global Burden of Disease 2019 Report, approximately 49.8 million (7.5%) children under the age of 5, 241.5 million (12.6%) between the ages of 5 and 19, and 291.3 million (11.3%) under the age of 20 had mild‐to‐severe disabilities, most of whom are thought to live in Sub‐Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia (Olusanya et al. 2022). According to the data of the Turkish Statistical Institute (Turkish Statistical Instute 2022), the disability rates in children aged 4–12 in Turkey were determined as 0.8% in total for those with visual difficulties, 0.4% in total for those with hearing difficulties, 1.5% in total for those with walking difficulties, 1.5% in total for those with learning difficulties, and 1.0% in total for those with speech difficulties (Turkish Statistical Instute 2022).

Caring for children who have special needs is an emotional burden, a stressful life experience, and a situation that requires constant coping responses from all family members. Moreover, this can create depression, anxiety, burnout, fatigue, unemployment, poverty, social isolation, stigmatization, and tension in the family relationships of caregivers (Alay and Kaçan 2024; Liu et al. 2020; Vadivelan et al. 2020; Kaçan et al. 2023). Mothers mostly undertake the care of children in families. Mothers who have a heavy care burden often neglect their personal needs (Liu et al. 2020). Caregiver burden can increase parental stress, anxiety, depression, and physical symptoms (Kaji et al. 2021; Kaur 2016). Increased caregiver burden was also associated with financial distress, lower socioeconomic status, less training, young mothers, being unmarried, poor psychological well‐being, family functioning, and quality of life (Iacob et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2020; Lackner et al. 2023). Moreover, previous studies also show that the severity of a child's symptoms further affects maternal well‐being negatively and creates a greater caregiver burden (Shepherd et al. 2021; Baykal et al. 2019) and that there is a relationship between caregiver burden and the psychological resilience of caregivers. Bildirici (2014) reported a negative relationship between the family burden assessment scale scores and the psychological resilience scale scores in his study conducted with mothers of children with intellectual disabilities. In this respect, as family burden increases, psychological resilience decreases. The frequency of psychiatric problems such as stress, depression, and anxiety affecting psychological resilience increases because of behavioural problems in children and their inadequate coping skills (Bildirici 2014). To reduce the care burden of mothers who have special needs children, it was suggested that economic support must be provided and mothers must be informed and trained about the care of children with special needs (Sevgi and Ayran 2024).

Interventions for families of children who have special needs show that programs based on various approaches were implemented and effective results were achieved. These programs include parenting training based on social learning or applied behaviour analysis, emotion‐ and problem‐focused parenting coping skills training based on cognitive‐behavioural approaches, behavioural parenting training interventions aimed at teaching families the skills to interact effectively with children, interventions based on attachment theory that focuses on changing parents' sensitivity to their children who have disabilities, interventions that involve cognitive‐behavioural approaches and other support services, interventions based on the Family Systems Theory, and relaxation programs focused on reducing caregiver stress, and programs based on art therapies (Erbiçer et al. 2024). There are also other studies reporting that psychosocial studies conducted with parents of children who have special needs increase problem‐solving skills, self‐compassion, and life satisfaction in parents, reduce care burden, stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, provide anger management and affect interpersonal relationships positively (Lunsky et al. 2021; Oliva et al. 2021; Shaffer et al. 2020; Nam and Park 2017). In the literature, training activities provided to families of children who have special needs were generally conducted in group sessions with an average of 8–10 (min: 3, max: 25) participants. The session duration of the training activities varied between 1 and 4 h (average duration 2.82) and the intervention duration varied between 2 days and 14 months (average period 10.5 weeks). The implementation period varied between 1 and 5 days a week, but this frequency was stated as once a month in some studies. Training activities were provided by healthcare staff (i.e., nurses, physicians, psychologists, physiotherapists, or social workers) and experienced parents or community volunteers (He et al. 2024).

As a result of the decision to increase training for parents in the 10th (2014) and 11th (2019) Development Plans specially prepared by the Presidency of the Republic of Turkey, it is seen that there was a significant increase in the number of studies targeting parents in Turkey. It was decided to increase Psycho‐training programs for parents, to make the services provided to parents more comprehensive, and to disseminate training on family communication and interaction by using media resources in these programs. Unfortunately, it was seen that there was no systematic and nationwide Psycho‐training program for these parents (Erbiçer et al. 2024) and that families with individuals with special needs need emotional, financial, and informational support (Arslan et al. 2020). Based on this, a Psycho‐training program based on different art practices must be provided for the strengthening of mothers, as well as group training activities for the required fields because there is extensive evidence that art participation is associated with changes in psychological abilities and motivational processes ranging from behavioural development to cognitive decline in children, adolescents, adults, and older adults (Çulhacik et al. 2021; Ibrahim et al. 2024). Art‐based psycho‐educational interventions have increasingly been recognized as effective alternative approaches for caregivers of children with special needs, particularly parents who experience high levels of emotional burden, chronic stress, and limited opportunities for self‐expression. Unlike traditional psychoeducational or verbally focused interventions, art‐based practices provide a non‐verbal and experiential medium that enables parents to express complex emotions, process traumatic experiences, and enhance emotional awareness in a supportive group context. For parents of children with special needs, who often prioritize their child's care over their own psychological needs, art‐based interventions offer a flexible and accessible way to promote psychological well‐being without requiring advanced verbal or cognitive skills. Furthermore, engaging in creative activities has been shown to foster emotional regulation, reduce caregiver burden, strengthen coping resources, and support meaning‐making processes. Therefore, incorporating art‐based practices into psycho‐educational programs may offer unique advantages for parents of children with special needs by addressing emotional expression, stress coping, and psychological well‐being in a holistic and culturally adaptable manner. In light of this information, the present study was designed as a feasibility and proof‐of‐concept intervention study to examine the potential effects of an art practice–based psycho‐training program for mothers of children with special needs. The primary outcome of the study was psychological well‐being, while the secondary outcomes included caregiver burden, emotional expression, and coping with stress. The study aimed to explore whether participation in the program may be associated with changes in these outcomes.

Study Hypotheses

There is no significant difference in psychological well‐being, caregiver burden, emotional expression, and stress coping levels of mothers of children with special needs following participation in an art‐based psychoeducation program.

Participation in the art‐based psychoeducation program may be associated with increased psychological well‐being, emotional expression, and stress coping levels, and decreased caregiver burden among mothers of children with special needs at post‐test and follow‐up compared with pre‐test measurements.

Method

Study Design

This study was a quasi‐experimental design based on a single‐group, pre‐test, post‐test, and follow‐up test.

Population and Sample

The population of the study consisted of mothers (n = 100) who had children diagnosed with moderate and severe intellectual disability and registered with three special training and rehabilitation centers and the Special Individuals and Families Association located in the central district of Kastamonu. The sample size was determined at the beginning of the study by using the G*Power 3.1 software and a power analysis. In a similar study in the relevant literature, the effect size for the change in care burden was calculated as 0.641 in the study conducted by Çulhacik et al. (2021). To exceed the 95% value in determining the power of the study, 28 people need to be reached at a significance level of 5% and an effect size of 0.641 (df = 27; t = 1.703). Considering the high power of the test and the losses, the study was conducted with 34 people. The sample initially consisted of 34 participants. Four participants were excluded from the sample because they did not continue. The study continued with 30 participants (Figure 1). Consequently, the final sample consisted of 30 participants. All 30 participants completed the full intervention program. Attendance at workshops was mandatory, and in cases where a participant was unable to attend a scheduled session, a make‐up session was arranged at a suitable time under the supervision of the project coordinator. Therefore, the average number of sessions attended by the final sample was equivalent to the total number of planned sessions, with no variation in attendance among completers. The range of attendance among participants included in the analysis was complete attendance for all sessions.

Flowchart of the study participants.

Inclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were: being a mother aged between 18 and 65 years who has a child with special needs, living in the same household with the child (with or without a spouse), having no diagnosed psychiatric disorder according to DSM‐5 criteria, having no hearing or comprehension problems, being open to communication, and agreeing to participate voluntarily in the study. Mothers of children diagnosed with moderate or severe intellectual disability, as well as those whose children had accompanying conditions such as visual, hearing, and physical disabilities, language and speech disorders, attention deficit, and hyperactivity disorder, were included in the study in accordance with DSM‐5 diagnostic criteria.

Exclusion criteria: mothers with severe intellectual disabilities, other additional disabilities, communication problems in the child, mothers who did not agree to the condition of attending the workshops, and non‐volunteer mothers were excluded.

Procedure‐Implementation of the Study

The study was produced as a result of the “Strong Mother Strong Special Individual” project within the scope of TUBITAK 4008 Inclusive Community Practices for Individuals with Special Needs Program. Mothers who met the project criteria were informed about the project and it was explained that the project would be completed in 6 months and would consist of a total of 15 workshops, including training and art, followed by a technical visit to women's cooperatives. Appointment times were set with the mothers who volunteered and information was provided about the project. Voluntary permissions to participate in the project and study were obtained. The project was opened on June 10, 2024, at 14:00 with the participation of 200 people, including mothers and special children, academic staff and leaders, and employees in institutions and organizations in the society. Among mothers of children with special needs (P), does participation in an art‐based psycho‐educational program (I), compared with pre‐intervention status (C), improve psychological well‐being, emotional expression, and stress coping, and reduce caregiver burden (O)? A total of 15 workshops and technical visits were performed, the dates, times, workshop names, and contents of which are given below. The project was performed in the Faculty of Health Sciences of a state university in a province. One executive, two researchers, and four guides took part in the project, each step of which was performed according to official permission and ethical principles. One of the faculty classrooms was turned into an art workshop and the other into a training workshop. A schedule and timetable were created for each workshop. Participants were encouraged to attend the workshops. Time was made up for mothers who did not attend. Participation in the intervention was facilitated by the fact that the mothers, who were primarily responsible for household management and caregiving duties, were able to adjust their daily schedules to attend the sessions. Attendance was entirely voluntary, and session times were arranged flexibly to accommodate caregiving responsibilities, thereby supporting sustained participation throughout the program. When developing the workshop content, each workshop had its own purpose and objectives to be achieved. Therefore, the study continued for 6 months. Participation in all workshops was mandatory for all 30 mothers included in the study. For mothers who indicated they could not attend for any reason, suitable days and times were determined and the relevant workshop was repeated. This ensured that the intervention was completed with equal content and duration for all participants. Attendance was recorded in each session using attendance sheets. The intervention program was conducted within the framework of a structured training and implementation plan, standardized in terms of content and duration. All workshops were delivered using predefined durations, objectives, and materials to ensure standardization. Implementation fidelity was monitored throughout the program by the project coordinator. The intervention concluded with dissemination activities, including a panel discussion and a public exhibition of the products created during the workshops. Information Form

The form consists of descriptive questions such as age, gender, educational status, economic status, difficulties faced in caring for the child, and methods used to cope with caregiving (Alay and Kaçan 2024; Gümüş and Kaçan 2024; Çulhacik et al. 2021; Şen Ün and Akgül 2022).

Psychological Well‐Being Scales (PWBS)

We confirm that the 84‐item version of the Psychological Well‐Being Scale (PWBS‐84) developed by Ryff (1989) was used in this study (Ryff 1989). The scale consists of six sub‐dimensions (Autonomy, Environmental Mastery, Personal Growth, Positive Relations with Others, Purpose in Life, and Self‐Acceptance), each containing 14 items (total = 84 items). All items are rated on a 6‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 6 (“Strongly agree”). The total score ranges from 84 to 504, while subscale scores range from 14 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater psychological well‐being. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the total scale was 0.888, indicating good internal consistency.

Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI)

The Zarit Burden Interview is a data collection tool developed by Zarit, Reever, and Bach‐Peterson (1980) to assess and reveal the difficulties experienced by caregivers(Zarit et al. 1980). The Turkish adaptation of the scale was made by İnci and Erdem (2008). It uses a Likert‐type rating ranging from 0 to 4 on a scale of 22 items, with response options such as “never,” “rarely,” “sometimes,” “often,” and “almost always.” Scale scores range from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating a higher burden of care. The minimum score that can be obtained from the scale is “0”, and the maximum is “88”; a high scale score means high distress (Zarit and Zarit 1990). The version of the scale adapted to Turkish consists of one single dimension, and the internal consistency coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.95. In this study, Cronbach's alpha value was 0.837 for the total score.

Emotional Expression Scale(EES)

The Emotional Expression Scale was developed by King and Emmons (1990) to assess individuals' tendency to express their emotions. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Kuzucu (2011). The scale consists of 15 items that evaluate the expression of positive, negative, and intimate emotions. Items are rated on a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). The total score ranges from 15 to 105, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency to express emotions. The scale assesses emotional expression both within interpersonal relationships and independently of interpersonal contexts. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the total scale was 0.818, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Stress Coping Styles Scale (SCSS)

In this study, the 30‐item short form of the Stress Coping Styles Scale (SCSS) was used. The original scale was developed by Folkman and Lazarus, and the Turkish short form was adapted and validated by Şahin and Durak (1995). The SCSS is a self‐report instrument designed to assess individuals' coping styles in response to stressful situations. The scale consists of five subscales: self‐confident approach (7 items), optimistic approach (5 items), helpless approach (8 items), submissive approach (6 items), and social support–seeking approach (4 items). Items are rated on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale. Higher scores on the self‐confident, optimistic, and social support–seeking subscales indicate the use of effective (functional) coping strategies, whereas higher scores on the helpless and submissive subscales indicate the use of ineffective (dysfunctional) coping strategies. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the total scale was 0.894, indicating high internal consistency.

Data Collection

When the data were collected, patient files of children who had special needs were first examined in the institutions where the study would be conducted. Then, with the support and guidance of healthcare staff—psychiatrists working in the institution, mothers who had children who had severe and moderate disabilities were identified. Mothers were interviewed on the days when the children and families visited the institution and were informed about the purpose and implementation of the project. A list of names of those who met the inclusion criteria and volunteered to participate in the study was created, and they were invited to the institution where the study would be conducted for the first interview. The interviews were conducted face‐to‐face by the researcher (HK) in an empty classroom. Each interview lasted approximately 20–25 min. A WhatsApp Group was created for the mothers who were interviewed. Preliminary tests were completed on June 6–9, 2024, and the mothers were invited to the 2024 project opening on June 10. The project manager (HK) provided further information about the project. The mothers were invited to the faculty on September 1, 2024 (1 month after the completion of 15 different workshops), and follow‐up data were collected face‐to‐face 1 month later on October 1, 2024.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome of the study was psychological well‐being, while caregiver burden, emotional expression, and stress coping were considered secondary outcomes. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 for Windows, and results were reported following APA Journal Article Reporting Standards. Numerical data were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), minimum and maximum values, and categorical data as percentages (%). Normality of the variables was examined using Kurtosis and Skewness, which fell within acceptable ranges (+1.5 to −1.5 or +2.0 to −2.0), indicating a normal distribution (Tabachnick and Fidell 2013; George and Mallery 2010). Given the normal distribution, parametric tests were deemed appropriate (Field 2018; Pallant 2020). For repeated measures, a one‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA was conducted. Mauchly's test of sphericity was performed to check the assumption of sphericity; if violated, Greenhouse–Geisser or Huynh‐Feldt corrections were applied. Post hoc comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction to control for multiple outcomes. 95% confidence intervals (CI) and effect sizes (Cohen's d or partial eta2) were reported for all outcomes. Additionally, analyses were re‐evaluated using a linear mixed‐effects model (LMM), which allowed for flexible modelling of repeated measurements and accounted for potential correlations within subjects over time. Both ANOVA and LMM results are presented to provide a comprehensive understanding of the intervention effects. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the University Ethics Committee, Social and Human Sciences Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Board approval date: (2024–13/06.06.2024). Written institutional permission and support letters were obtained from the three special education and rehabilitation centers where the study was conducted, as well as from the Association for Individuals with Special Needs and Their Families. Written permission was also obtained from the university and faculty hosting the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating mothers prior to data collection. In addition, written consent was obtained for the sharing of workshop‐related activities conducted within the scope of the project on the project's official YouTube channel and website. Consent was also obtained for the participation of the children attending the workshops and for the use of visual materials on social media and video‐based platforms (e.g., YouTube and web‐based platforms). To ensure confidentiality, faces and any personally identifiable information of both participants and children were not displayed in publicly shared materials or were fully anonymized. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2008).

Results

Results Describing Caregivers

The majority of mothers (80.0%) were married, 50.0% had two children, 40.0% were high school graduates, 66.7% had incomes equal to their expenses, 76.7% lived with their spouses and children, 13.3% lived with their mothers and fathers, and 10.0% lived alone (Table 3). Among the problems faced in child care, discrimination and health problems were the most common (33.3%), while accessibility problems were 16.7%, training‐related problems were 13.3%, and employment‐related problems were 3.3%. The status of receiving training in child care was equally distributed, with 50.0% of the participants stating that they received training on this subject and 50.0% stating that they had not. The mean age of the mothers was 46.93, and the age distribution varied between 32 and 66 (SD 8.12).

The Measures of Caregiver Burden, Coping With Stress Inventory, Psychological Well‐Being, and Expression of Emotions

The study examined how the caregiving burden changes over time. The results showed that caregiving burden was measured as 44.90 in the pretest, 20.67 in the posttest, and 17.33 in the follow‐up, indicating that caregiving burden significantly decreased after the intervention. Statistical tests confirmed that these differences were significant and the effect size was high (F = 69.374, p < 0.001, Partial Eta‐Squared = 0.832). Pairwise comparisons showed that the caregiving burden decreased significantly at each step and this decrease was statistically significant. These findings show that the intervention was effective in reducing caregiving burden and the magnitude of this effect was high. The study results also showed that the intervention study was highly effective in increasing changes in the self‐confident approach, optimistic approach, and social support‐seeking approach in the posttest and follow‐up steps that it was effective in reducing the helpless approach and submissive approach, and that this decrease continued in the follow‐up step. The changes in psychological well‐being in pre‐test, post‐test, and follow‐up steps were also examined in the present study. The pre‐test mean was measured as 345.20 (±73.93), the post‐test mean was measured as 361.13 (±73.10) and the follow‐up mean was measured as 382.27 (±73.13) (F = 479.324, p < 0.001, Partial Eta Squared = 0.943). These results show that the intervention was quite effective in increasing psychological well‐being and this increase continued in the follow‐up step (Table 4).

Discussion

Art activities, one of the psychosocial‐based practices, can help families of children who have special needs accept their children's conditions, express their feelings, learn and cope with their children's disease, and protect their mental health (Erbiçer et al. 2024). In this context, the study was conducted to examine the effects of a psycho‐training program based on art practices on psychological well‐being, care burden, expression of emotions, and coping levels of mothers who have children who have special needs.

This study revealed that 40.0% of participating mothers had at least one chronic illness, and approximately one‐third of the mothers experienced stigma and discrimination against their children. Furthermore, one‐third of the mothers reported that their children had additional health problems such as epilepsy in addition to their disability; two‐thirds of the children had speech and language disorders, and half of them did not benefit from any educational services. These findings demonstrate that children with special needs and their families face multiple risk factors. The literature also reports that speech and language disorders are common in children with special needs, that perceived stigma is high within families, that mothers are the primary caregivers, and that chronic physical and mental illnesses are frequently observed in caregivers (Gümüş and Kaçan 2024; Yigitalp et al. 2017; Kaçan et al. 2023). Previous studies have shown that these families experience social exclusion, inadequate social support, and isolation (Gümüş and Kaçan 2023; Kayama et al. 2017; Vadivelan et al. 2020; He et al. 2024). In this context, it can be said that the mothers in our study experienced multifaceted challenges and had a high need for intervention.

This study determined that the perceived caregiving burden of mothers decreased significantly after the implementation of an art‐based psychoeducational program. It is known that mothers caring for children with special needs experience a high level of caregiving burden due to the child's health problems, behavioural difficulties, and social limitations (Sevgi and Ayran 2024; Bamber et al. 2023). This finding from our study is consistent with previous studies showing that psychoeducational and art‐based group interventions are effective in reducing caregiving burden (Abass Mohamed et al. 2022; Cheng and Lai 2023; Çulhacik et al. 2021). It is thought that group‐based and experiential content, in particular, reduces mothers' perception of loneliness, thereby alleviating their caregiving burden. These results support the idea that art‐based psychoeducational programs are an effective intervention approach in reducing the burden on caregivers.

In this study, it was found that after the intervention, mothers' levels of self‐confident approach, optimistic approach, and seeking social support, which are sub‐dimensions of stress coping, increased significantly in both the post‐test and follow‐up measurements. It is known that having a child with special needs is associated with moderate to high levels of stress in parents. The findings of our study are consistent with the literature showing that psychoeducational and art‐based interventions strengthen parents' coping skills (Kalalo et al. 2021; Muthukaruppan et al. 2022; Gümüs et al. 2020). In addition, it is reported that mindfulness, art, and body‐based interventions reduce negative coping strategies and support positive coping skills in the long term (Altan Sarıkaya et al. 2017; Shaffer et al. 2020; Masulani‐Mwale et al. 2018). In this context, it can be said that the multi‐component art‐based program applied in our study supports mothers in coping with stress in more functional ways.

Parents often experience moderate‐to‐severe distress because of the challenges of raising a child with special needs. It was found with the applied interventions in this study that the sub‐dimensions of coping with stress, secure approach, optimistic approach, and social support‐seeking approach increased significantly in the post‐test and follow‐up steps. Similar to our study findings, the literature contains studies that report an increase in caregiver coping skills (Muthukaruppan et al. 2022). In a study that attempted to determine the effects of Psycho‐training on the parental stress index, a decrease was detected in the parental stress index and severity of children who have autism spectrum disorder after the Psycho‐training intervention (Kalalo et al. 2021). In their study, Gümüs et al. (2020) aimed to determine the effects of Psycho‐training on the stress levels of parents of children who had attention‐deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder and reported that Psycho‐training was effective in reducing the stress levels of families of the children (Gümüs et al. 2020). A study investigating the effectiveness of a 6‐week online mindfulness‐based course in reducing the stress experienced by parents of children who had special needs showed that it could be an effective intervention to reduce the stress experienced by parents of children who had special needs (Shaffer et al. 2020). In their study, Masulani‐Mwale et al. (2018) reported that there was a relationship between being a parent of children who had mental special needs and experiencing parental stress and that the psychosocial interventions improved mental health outcomes among parents, increased their confidence in caring for their children, and reduced stress levels (Masulani‐Mwale et al. 2018). A study that analysed the effect of dance/movement therapy on stress management found that the intervention reduced negative coping skills and increased positive coping skills in the long term (Altan Sarıkaya et al. 2017).

The present study also found a significant increase in the expression of emotions. Implementations based on art therapies are important in revealing individuals' emotions. Studies in the literature concluded that mindfulness‐based interventions are effective in reducing stress and anxiety while increasing emotion regulation (Alvarado‐García et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2020; Erkan et al. 2022; Farisah et al. 2024; Crane et al. 2021; Park 2021). Arts‐based methods encompass a range of creative exercises and experiential activities that stimulate expression and communication among participants. Arts‐based methods are defined as “the use of a variety of arts, movements, drawings, paintings, sculptures, music, writing, sound, and improvisation to facilitate growth and health in a supportive environment.” In recent years, professionals have recognized the benefits of arts‐based methods and are incorporating these methods into mainstream mental healthcare services and research practices (Oystrick et al. 2023; McKay et al. 2020). In the present study, it is considered that practical communication workshops and psychodrama, especially the mud art therapy workshop, are practical in expressing emotions.

The present study also found that psychological well‐being increased significantly. One aspect of psychological well‐being is the elicitation of emotional responses, including positive and negative emotions, stress, pleasure, and mood changes, and is often referred to as experienced psychological well‐being (Stone et al. 2013). There is extensive evidence that arts participation can increase experienced well‐being. For example, a systematic review found that regular participation in community‐based music and singing programs can help maintain and increase psychological well‐being among young, middle‐aged, and older adults (Daykin et al. 2018). The frequency and variety of arts participation may be more important than the total time spent in arts activities (Kuykendall et al. 2015). Studies in the literature have found that training provided to families of individuals with special needs supports psychological well‐being (Sadoo et al. 2022; Tekola et al. 2020; Rutherford et al. 2019). Psychosocial interventions that include factors such as group harmony, universality, knowledge transfer, instilling hope, purification, sacrifice, interpersonal learning, learning through modelling, socialization, and organizing the first family experience can contribute positively to the psychological well‐being of individuals and the functioning of families (Erbiçer et al. 2024). In the present study, eight different clay workshops, conducted over 4 days, and clay expression art therapy were found to be effective in helping mothers express their positive or negative emotions.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study's limitations include the small sample size of 30 mothers and the absence of a control group. The study was conducted in a small province in Türkiye, and due to limited funding, it was not possible to include a larger number of individuals. Conducting this study with a larger sample and control group is recommended.

The strengths of the study were that it consisted of 15 different applied art and training workshops, that it was performed with expert educators in the field, that the study was supported by TUBITAK, a prestigious institution in Turkey, which had financial resources, that the transportation needs of the mothers and their children were met by institutions and organizations, that the project was performed in a comfortable environment in the summer months when the mothers came to the institution with their children, that the children were looked after by the research team while the mothers were at the activity, that the activities with adaptable children were supported by special educators in the field, that each workshop of the project was followed by the press and that press news was made, that it was transparent through the YouTube channel (https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCKa54BxrbhZeWFP7‐TwU‐Uw) and the web page (https://gucluanne.kastamonu.edu.tr), that the study was planned over a long period of time, and that cooperation was made in the study conducted with the associations of individuals with special needs.

The intervention proved to be effective in improving mothers' psychological well‐being, coping skills, and emotional expression. However, it is a resource‐intensive practice, requiring substantial time (long duration, multiple sessions) and financial resources (instructors, materials, venue, transportation, etc.). These factors represent a clear limitation in terms of feasibility for broader clinical application. Therefore, future efforts should consider developing a shorter, more cost‐effective version of the program that retains its therapeutic benefits while reducing time and financial demands. Implementing such a version could enhance accessibility and sustainability, particularly for caregivers in low‐ and middle‐income settings.

However, the results should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. First, the study employed a single‐group design without a control group, which limits the ability to draw causal conclusions and raises the possibility of history or maturation effects influencing the results. Second, all outcomes were assessed using self‐report measures, which may be subject to response biases such as social desirability or participants' expectations regarding the benefits of the program. Third, the sample consisted of volunteer mothers from a single setting, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations of caregivers. Additionally, the relatively intensive structure of the intervention may influence participation and may not be feasible in all settings. Future studies using randomized controlled designs, larger and more diverse samples, and longer follow‐up periods are recommended to further evaluate the effectiveness and sustainability of art‐based psychoeducational interventions for caregivers of children with special needs.

Implications for Practice

In the present study, the art‐based psycho‐training program applied to mothers of children with special needs was found to be effective in improving psychological well‐being, coping skills, and emotional expression through various art workshops and training activities. These results suggest that similar programs could benefit mothers of children with different types of special needs, providing opportunities to meet psychosocial needs such as establishing friendships, sharing common challenges, and socializing. The group environment, which facilitated sharing, validation, and the feeling of not being alone, may have also created a therapeutic effect that contributed to improved mental health outcomes (Erbiçer et al. 2024). Previous research supports that art‐based psycho‐training programs can enhance multiple aspects of caregiver and child functionality. However, the implementation of such interventions can be resource‐intensive, with main challenges including lack of family support for the caregiver, limited time availability, and financial constraints. Therefore, future programs should consider these obstacles in their design and structure. Delivering interventions in a group‐based format can provide additional benefits, such as social support for caregivers who often experience isolation or discrimination. Group‐based programs may also require fewer resources while still enhancing caregiver skills, suggesting that caregiver support groups are a promising strategy, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries, for sustaining interventions and supporting families over time (He et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2021).

Conclusion

The results of the study demonstrated that an art‐based psychoeducation program was highly effective in reducing caregiving burden, fostering a self‐confident approach, optimism, social support‐seeking, and psychological well‐being. While the intervention proved beneficial, it is resource‐intensive in terms of time, number of sessions, materials, and logistics. Therefore, future adaptations could focus on developing a shorter and lower‐cost version of the program to enhance feasibility and accessibility for caregivers in clinical practice, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income settings.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

The present study demonstrates that participation in art‐based psycho‐training programs can effectively enhance the psychological well‐being of mothers of children with special needs. The intervention provided opportunities for mothers to socialize, express emotions, share experiences, and gain practical art skills, which contributed to their sense of support and self‐efficacy. Activities such as sheet‐tying and jewellery workshops were designed to continue at home, ensuring sustainability of engagement. Moreover, mothers were able to convert the products created during the workshops into economic value, further supporting their autonomy and well‐being. These findings suggest that art‐based interventions can be a valuable, holistic approach in clinical practice to support caregivers, promote mental health, and enhance family functioning. Future adaptations could focus on shorter and lower‐cost versions to increase feasibility and accessibility in diverse clinical and community settings. The present study aimed to examine the potential effects of an art‐based psychoeducational program on psychological well‐being, caregiver burden, emotional expression, and coping styles among mothers of children with special needs. The findings suggest that participation in the program may be associated with improvements in psychological well‐being, emotional expression, and adaptive coping strategies, along with a reduction in caregiver burden. These results are consistent with previous studies indicating that psychosocial and psychoeducational interventions can support the psychological adjustment of caregivers of children with special needs.

From a clinical perspective, the observed changes may reflect meaningful improvements in caregivers' emotional and psychological functioning. In particular, the decrease in caregiver burden scores measured by the Zarit Burden Interview and the increase in psychological well‐being scores suggest that structured psychosocial support programs may contribute to strengthening caregivers' coping resources. Although the magnitude of change should be interpreted cautiously due to the study design, these improvements indicate that art‐based and experiential activities integrated with psychoeducation may offer supportive benefits for mothers who face ongoing caregiving demands.

The findings also highlight the potential role of creative and expressive activities in facilitating emotional expression and interpersonal communication. Art‐based interventions may provide a safe and supportive environment in which participants can express feelings, share experiences, and develop adaptive coping strategies. In the context of caregiving for children with special needs, such opportunities may help reduce emotional stress and promote psychological resilience.
