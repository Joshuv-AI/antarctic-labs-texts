Asymptomatic Charcot foot in longstanding diabetes
Authors: Omar T. Abdel-Moneim Alheti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCharcot neuropathic osteoarthropathy complicates an estimated 0.1–7.5% of diabetic patients in general clinic populations, yet misdiagnosis rates remain high — partly because the foot is often warm, swollen, and painless rather than obviously deformed. Undiagnosed cases range from 0.4% to 13% in published series.AimTo detect preclinical Charcot disease by plain X-ray in longstanding diabetic patients attending routine follow-up, before clinical deformity develops.MethodsCross-sectional prevalence/screening study, with structured short-term follow-up restricted to X-ray-positive patients, of 60 consecutive adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) of >10 years duration, seen at Al-Ramadi Teaching Hospitals between September 2023 and March 2024. All underwent bilateral foot X-ray in antero-posterior and oblique views, full clinical and neurological assessment, and laboratory investigations including HbA1c.ResultsThirty-one patients (51.7%) had X-ray changes fulfilling a pre-specified Charcot pattern — predominantly focal bone demineralization, subchondral fragmentation, and debris formation at articular margins. X-ray positivity correlated significantly with older age (p = 0.024), longer DM duration (p < 0.001), higher HbA1c (p < 0.001), and greater BMI (p < 0.001). Gender and medication type were not significant. At 6-month follow-up, six of the 31 initially positive cases (19.4%) showed radiological progression; all underwent MRI and were offloaded with total contact cast or airwalker. These findings were not adjudicated against MRI or a validated staging system in most cases and represent radiographic suspicion rather than confirmed neuroarthropathy.ConclusionOver half of longstanding diabetic patients attending routine follow-up had X-ray changes fulfilling a Charcot pattern, though most were not MRI-confirmed. These findings support further investigation, with MRI-validated confirmation in a larger sample, before systematic plain bilateral foot X-ray screening can be recommended in this population; glycemic optimization and patient education on trauma avoidance remain appropriate in the meantime.

Introduction

Charcot neuropathic osteoarthropathy — the Charcot foot — is progressive bone and joint destruction driven by peripheral neuropathy [1,2]. In diabetic patients, sensory loss allows repeated microtrauma to go unnoticed, triggering an exaggerated osteoclastic response that can dismantle the midfoot architecture within weeks [1]. The result, if unrecognised, is the irreversible rocker-bottom deformity that precedes ulceration and amputation [6,7].

The condition is clinically deceptive. The acute presentation — warm, erythematous, swollen foot — mimics cellulitis, gout, or deep vein thrombosis and is misdiagnosed in a substantial proportion of first encounters [3,5]. But before those acute signs emerge, plain X-ray changes already exist: focal demineralisation, subchondral fragmentation, debris at articular margins, capsular distention [3,7]. Detecting these pre-deformity findings matters because offloading at this stage can arrest progression; waiting for deformity to declare itself does not [6].

Population estimates of Charcot foot prevalence in diabetic patients range from 0.1% to 7.5%, a span that reflects heterogeneous definitions as much as true variation [[3], [4], [5]]. Studies using active radiological screening consistently report higher detection rates than those relying on clinical ascertainment alone. Published figures for undiagnosed Charcot disease reach 0.4% to 13% [[3], [4], [5]]. This study was designed to quantify the proportion of longstanding diabetic outpatients carrying asymptomatic X-ray evidence of Charcot changes, and to identify the clinical variables that predict radiological positivity.

Radiographic change of the kind screened for here is not the earliest stage of the disease. Eichenholtz Stage 0 Charcot neuroarthropathy is a pre-radiographic phase characterised by bone marrow oedema on MRI in the absence of cortical change, and MRI, rather than plain film, is the reference standard for detecting this stage and for excluding osteomyelitis when ulceration is present. Current international position statements, including guidance from the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF), do not at present recommend routine radiographic screening of asymptomatic diabetic patients for Charcot neuroarthropathy. Against this background, the present study was conceived as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing: our aim was to establish whether radiographic abnormality is common enough in a longstanding diabetic outpatient population to justify a future, MRI-validated screening trial, not to recommend screening on the strength of this study alone.

Charcot neuroarthropathy and diabetic foot osteomyelitis share risk factors and overlapping imaging features, and their misdifferentiation is a recognised source of both over- and under-treatment. The distinction matters clinically because established Charcot deformity, once it progresses to instability, recurrent ulceration, or infection, may ultimately require surgical reconstruction or limb salvage, which underscores why detection at an earlier, potentially reversible stage is clinically important [33,34].

Methods

design and setting

Cross-sectional prevalence/screening study, with structured short follow-up (1, 3, and 6 months) restricted to the X-ray-positive subgroup, carried out between September 2023 and March 2024 in Al-Ramady Teaching Hospitals, Al-Anbar governorate, Iraq.

Participants

Sixty adults with type 2 DM of >10 years duration attending routine diabetic follow-up were enrolled (Fig. 1). Exclusion criteria included age below 18 years; prior history of Charcot foot; peripheral neuropathy from any cause other than diabetes; active foot ulceration or prior amputation; peripheral vascular disease; absent dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial pulsations; and previous angioplasty or arterial bypass. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed; the 60 patients enrolled represented a convenience sample of consecutive eligible attendees at the diabetic foot clinic during the recruitment window. Clinical assessment

All patients underwent standardised history covering DM type and duration, glycaemic control, current medications, neuropathy symptoms (tingling, burning, numbness, autonomic features), and comorbidities. Examination included BMI, arterial assessment (skin temperature, capillary refill time, peripheral pulses), neurological assessment (monofilament testing, quantitative sensory testing, deep ankle and ankle jerk reflexes), and graded pitting oedema assessment.

Radiological protocol

Bilateral plain foot X-rays were performed using Stephani X and Apollo devices in antero-posterior (AP) and oblique (45°) views. AP films were assessed for alignment of metatarsals 1–5 and inter-tarsal joint spaces; oblique films for the cuboid, fifth metatarsal base, and tarsal sinus. Films not meeting technical adequacy criteria were repeated. All radiographs were reviewed by the radiology department for Charcot-specific findings: focal bone demineralisation, articular debris, subchondral fragmentation, capsular distention, subluxation, dislocation, and periarticular fractures (Fig. 8) [3,7,21]. Follow-up

Patients with positive X-ray findings were reviewed clinically and radiologically at 1, 3, and 6 months. Those with new changes at 6 months underwent MRI [26,27]. Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using (IBM SPSS version 21). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD with range; categorical variables as n (%). Between-group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Distributional assumptions underlying the Mann-Whitney U test were checked before use. Because age, DM duration, HbA1c, and BMI were each associated with X-ray positivity at p < 0.001 or better on univariate testing, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of radiographic positivity while accounting for likely collinearity between these variables.

Of the 31 X-ray-positive cases at presentation, MRI confirmation was obtained only in the six patients who progressed radiologically at 6-month follow-up. The remaining 25 X-ray-positive, non-progressing, non-MRI-confirmed cases cannot be distinguished with certainty from osteopenia, degenerative joint disease, or healed subclinical trauma on plain film alone, and the design of this study cannot exclude substantial overdiagnosis within this subgroup.

At 6-month radiological review of the 31 positive cases, six (19.4%) showed new X-ray changes. Clinically, four of the six had mild swelling, one had moderate swelling, and only one reported mild mid-foot discomfort. MRI was performed in all six: four were managed with total contact cast and one with an airwalker [[27], [28], [29]].

The planned multivariable logistic regression analysis has been removed from this manuscript, as it was never completed. Age, DM duration, HbA1c, and BMI were each significantly associated with X-ray positivity on univariate testing (p<0.001 or better; Table 4), but because these variables are clinically inter-related, independence among them could not be established without a formal adjusted model. These associations should accordingly be read as univariate findings only, not as independent predictors of radiographic positivity.

Discussion

The headline finding — 51.7% of routine diabetic outpatients with occult Charcot X-ray changes — is higher than most published clinical estimates, but consistent with active radiological screening studies [25]. Viswanathan et al. detected Charcot findings in 54% of cases with foot ulceration and 14% of those with diabetic neuropathy alone using standardised weight-bearing radiographs [25]. Smith et al. identified Charcot changes in only 1.4% of 428 radiographed diabetic cases in a 1997 series [8]; Fabrin et al. estimated an incidence of 0.3% per year among 3000–5000 diabetic patients [11]. The gap between these rates and our finding is unlikely to have a single explanation. It probably reflects a combination of: (i) genuine under-ascertainment in prior clinical-diagnosis-based series, which our active radiographic screening was designed to test; (ii) enrichment of our sample for very long DM duration (>10 years, mean 14.4 years) and comparatively poor glycaemic control (mean HbA1c 8.1%), both established risk factors that would be expected to raise prevalence relative to unselected diabetic populations; and (iii) diagnostic non-specificity of our radiographic criteria, which, absent MRI or a validated staging system in most cases, likely inflated the apparent prevalence to some degree. We cannot partition how much of the gap is explained by each of these three factors, which is precisely why MRI-validated confirmation in a larger sample is the necessary next step rather than treating 51.7% as an established population prevalence.

Age was a significant predictor in our data (p = 0.024), with a mean of 62.1 years in the X-ray positive group. Smith et al. reported a cohort mean of 63.7 years, though their inclusion of patients with prior amputations biases that figure upward [8]. Wanzou et al., who screened 100 Ugandan diabetic patients with a DM threshold of >7 years (versus our >10), found no significant age association, possibly because their cohort was younger (mean 51.3 years) and their disease-duration threshold was lower [9]. A significant link between Charcot foot and advancing age has been reported by several other investigators [10]. What our data suggest is that the decade between 55 and 65 years is where radiological positivity begins to cluster in this population.

Gender showed no significant association (p = 0.16), consistent with the majority of published series [[11], [12], [13]]. Salini et al., in a retrospective review of 1475 Indian T2DM patients with severe peripheral neuropathy, reported a higher prevalence of Charcot foot among males, attributing this to heavier physical activity and lower self-care engagement [14]. Higher male frequency has been documented in other cohorts as well [8,10,[15], [16], [17]]. Conversely, a female predominance has been reported elsewhere [9,18,19]. The absence of a gender effect in our series may reflect the sample size or a genuine absence in this Egyptian outpatient population; larger prospective data are needed to resolve this.

The BMI difference between groups was substantial — 31.2 versus 24.8 kg/m² (p < 0.001). Stuck et al. documented a dose-response relationship between obesity and Charcot risk in a large US administrative dataset, proposing that excess mechanical load accelerates joint damage once neuropathy removes protective pain sensation [24]. Nehring et al. reported a mean BMI of 32.8 in their Charcot cohort, close to our positive group mean of 31.2 [10]. Sebastian et al. and Thewjitcharoen et al. reported means of 29.2 and 28.2, respectively [19,20]. A significant BMI–Charcot foot association has been confirmed in multiple cohorts [10,20]., though not universally [9,13]. Notably, our negative group mean BMI of 24.8 kg/m² falls within the normal-weight range, underscoring that non-obese patients with long-duration DM and poor glycaemic control still carry meaningful radiological risk.

DM duration was the strongest categorical predictor (p < 0.001). The mean duration in the positive group was 17.6 years versus 11.7 in the negative group — a 6-year difference that tracks cumulative neuropathic damage. Most diabetic patients with Charcot foot have been reported to have had diabetes for at least ten years [3,21], and a significant relationship between longer disease duration and Charcot foot has been documented across multiple study designs [9,10,13,18]. Our data reinforce the 10-year mark as the threshold beyond which systematic radiological surveillance is justified.

The HbA1c gap was equally stark: 9.03% in the positive versus 7.23% in the negative group (p < 0.001). Hyperglycaemia is thought to impair the osteoclast–osteoblast balance, promoting bone resorption over repair [22]. Chantelau and Poll reported a mean HbA1c of 8.6% in their Charcot cohort, consistent with our findings [12]. A significant association between poor glycaemic control and Charcot foot has been reported by several groups [20,23], though not all [9]. Even the negative group mean HbA1c of 7.23% exceeds the 7.0% target, indicating that moderately controlled patients are not immune to risk, merely less vulnerable to it.

Seventy-eight percent of X-ray changes in our series were detectable on the AP view alone; the oblique view was necessary to capture the remaining 22%. This has direct protocol implications: single-view foot radiograph protocols will systematically underestimate disease burden in this population [3,7].

The 6-month progression rate of 19.4% (six of 31 positive cases) is clinically instructive for how quiet it was: five of the six had only mild or moderate swelling, and only one reported any discomfort. Without systematic follow-up imaging, all six would have continued unmanaged until deformity declared itself. Four ended up in total contact casts — the standard of care for active Charcot — after MRI confirmed the extent of joint involvement [26]. Chantelau and Richter's review of 71 acute Charcot cases managed on MRI findings reinforces this: plain films fail to detect earliest-stage disease (Eichenholtz stage 0), and MRI is required whenever clinical suspicion persists despite negative or equivocal radiographs [26,27]. MRI also defines the extent of marrow oedema, guides offloading decisions, and detects complications — particularly osteomyelitis — that X-ray cannot [[28], [29], [30]].

Management of confirmed Charcot neuroarthropathy is primarily conservative in the acute phase: strict non-weightbearing or total contact casting until oedema and temperature asymmetry resolve, followed by graduated return to protected weightbearing in accommodative or custom footwear [6,7]. Surgical intervention — exostectomy, realignment arthrodesis, or, in severe unstable or ulcerated deformity, more extensive reconstruction — is reserved for cases with instability, recurrent ulceration, or deformity that conservative offloading cannot control [6,28].

Diabetic foot osteomyelitis can mimic Charcot neuroarthropathy both clinically and radiologically, since both may present with bone destruction, periosteal reaction, and marrow signal change; differentiating the two, usually with MRI and, where relevant, nuclear medicine imaging or biopsy, is essential before surgical or antibiotic treatment is planned [29,33,34]. None of the 60 patients in the present cohort had active ulceration, an exclusion criterion, so osteomyelitis was not an operative differential at the time of imaging; we make this distinction explicit here to avoid the two entities being conflated by the reader.

Several limitations deserve acknowledgement. First, this was a single-centre study, which may limit generalisability to other populations and imaging services. Second, the sample of 60, though adequate for detecting the primary outcome, is insufficient to stratify risk by DM subtype, neuropathy pattern, or specific comorbidity, and no a priori sample-size calculation was performed. Third, MRI confirmation was obtained only in the six radiological progressors; the diagnostic status of the remaining 25 X-ray-positive cases could not be confirmed against a reference standard, and substantial overdiagnosis cannot be excluded. Fifth, patients were recruited as a convenience sample of clinic attendees, which carries potential selection bias relative to the broader population of longstanding diabetic patients. Sixth, follow-up is limited to 6 months; longer surveillance of this cohort is ongoing. Seventh, no validated Charcot staging system (Eichenholtz or Sanders-Frykberg) was applied to any case, including the six MRI-confirmed progressors. Eighth, a planned multivariable logistic regression to adjust for collinearity between age, DM duration, HbA1c, and BMI was not performed; the significant associations reported for these four variables are therefore univariate only and should not be interpreted as independent predictors of radiographic positivity.

Conclusion and recommendations

More than half of cases with longstanding type II diabetes attending routine outpatient follow-up had plain X-ray changes fulfilling a pre-specified Charcot pattern, largely without symptoms; these changes were MRI-confirmed only in the subset who progressed radiologically. The condition does not declare itself. Four variables — age, DM duration, HbA1c, and BMI — identify the subgroup at highest radiological risk. These findings support further investigation, with MRI-validated confirmation in a larger sample, before systematic bilateral foot X-ray screening in both AP and oblique views can be recommended for this population; in the interim, clinicians should not wait for the foot to become red or swollen before considering imaging when risk factors are prominent [3,6,7,31]. When X-ray changes are found, structured offloading, glycaemic tightening, and close radiological follow-up are required. When X-ray is negative but clinical suspicion persists, MRI should be obtained rather than deferring to normal plain films [26,27,32].
