Perineurioma‐like EMA‐positive calvarial neoplasms: clinicopathological study of eight cases
Authors: Raheel Rizwan, Yingting Mok, Josephine K Dermawan, Alexandra Isaacson, Veronica Ulici, Scott E Kilpatrick, John D Reith, Gabrielle Yeaney, Hakan Ilaslan, Karen J Fritchie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncidental skull lesions are increasingly detected due to the widespread use of brain imaging. We have encountered an unusual cohort of unclassifiable calvarial spindle cell lesions showing phenotypic overlap with perineurioma and meningioma, which we sought to further characterize.Material and methodsCases of unclassifiable low‐grade spindle cell lesions arising in the calvarium were collected. Clinical, radiological and pathological findings were reviewed. All cases were uniformly stained for a panel of meningothelial and perineurial immunomarkers.ResultsEight cases were identified (6 females, 2 males; 51–81 years), arising in the parietal (n = 4), frontal (n = 3), parietal‐occipital (n = 1) bones. Radiologically, the lesions appeared as lytic with non‐aggressive features, but progressive enlargement was documented in cases with serial imaging. Histologically, all cases were composed of uniform, bland spindle cells with elongated, tapered cytoplasm and inconspicuous nuclei arranged in short fascicles and whorls. Immunohistochemically, all cases were positive for EMA (8/8) and Collagen IV (3/3). The lesions showed consistent but limited staining for SSTR2a (2 cases, 6%–50%; 4 cases, 1%–5%). The lesional spindle cells were negative for PR (0/7), GLUT1 (0/6), S100 (0/6), CD34 (0/5), SOX10 (0/4) and claudin 1 (0/3). Follow‐up (available in 6 patients; range 6–61 months, median 15.5 months) showed no evidence of recurrence after curettage or excision.ConclusionPerineurioma‐like, EMA‐positive calvarial neoplasms are a rare group of spindle cell tumours for which no local recurrence or distant metastasis has been documented to date, although they do show a propensity for progressive growth. Recognition of their distinctive clinicopathological and radiological features is important for accurate diagnostic classification and appropriate patient management.

Introduction

The increasing utilization of advanced neuroimaging techniques has led to a growing frequency of incidental detection of skull lesions often identified during the evaluation of localized neurological symptoms or in the context of staging for systemic diseases. 1 Calvarial lesions encompass a broad spectrum of pathological entities ranging from reactive and benign processes to primary and secondary malignancies. Despite advances in multimodality imaging, radiological differentiation among these entities remains challenging and diagnostic uncertainty frequently necessitates histopathological assessment following biopsy or surgical excision.

In our orthopaedic practice, we have encountered several examples of a novel perineurioma‐like spindle cell neoplasm occurring in the frontal and parietal bones, often discovered incidentally. We sought to better characterize its clinicopathological features to aid in appropriate management of these lesions.

Materials and Methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained. Intraosseous skull lesions with the following diagnoses were retrieved from institutional and consultation archives: benign spindle cell neoplasm, benign spindle cell proliferation, benign fibro‐osseous spindle cell proliferation, benign fibro‐osseous spindle cell neoplasm and low‐grade spindle cell proliferation. Clinical details were recorded, and available imaging was reviewed with a musculoskeletal radiologist. Follow‐up data was collected from the patients' records and referring pathologists.

Morphological reassessment of haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)‐stained slides was performed to document the presence of the following features: pericellular hyaline nodules, perivascular hyalinization, foamy histiocytes, psammomatous calcifications, microcystic change and peripheral nerve segments. The growth pattern of the lesional cells and quality of the intralesional bony trabeculae were noted.

Previously performed immunostains were documented, and additional immunostains were performed on cases with available materials for the following antibodies: EMA (VMS/Cell Marque, E29 Mouse monoconal), SSTR2a (Abcam, UMB1 rabbit monoclonal), PR (VMS, 1E2 rabbit monoclonal), S100 (Agilent (Dako), rabbit polyclonal), GLUT1 (Bio SB, EP141 Rabbit monoclonal), CD34 (Cell Marque, QBEnd/10 mouse monoclonal), BRAF V600E (Abcam, VE1 mouse monoclonal), claudin 1 (Invitrogen, polyclonal) and collagen IV (Dako). All analyses were performed on formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissues (FFPE) employing the Leica Bond‐3 (Leica, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA) automated stainer platform. All immunohistochemical signals were then detected using the Ventana OptiView DAB Detection Kit (Ventana). Immunostains were scored as follows: 0, 0%; 1+, 1%–5%; 2+, 6%–50%; 3+, >50%. Information regarding decalcification status and method of decalcification was recorded when available.

Our control cohort consisted of calvarial fibrous dysplasia (n = 8), intraosseous meningioma (n = 3), Paget disease (n = 1) and burnt‐out Langerhans cell histiocytosis (n = 1).

Results

Clinical Findings

Eight cases were identified arising in six females and two males with an age range from 51 to 81 years (median 71 years) (Table 1). One case represented a routine internal case, while the remaining seven were external consultations. Four cases presented in the parietal bones, three in the frontal and one in the parietal‐occipital bone. The sizes ranged from 1.3 to 3.4 cm (median 2.2 cm). Of the eight cases, clinical history was available for six cases; five were incidentally identified on imaging workup for unrelated conditions, while one patient presented for suspicion of Paget disease. All cases underwent complete surgical resection, and no local recurrence or distant metastasis has been reported in patients with follow‐up (n = 6 patients; range 6–61 months, median 15.5 months).

Imaging

Imaging studies were available for five out of eight cases, consisting of MRI from two cases and CT from three cases (Figure 1). The lesions were centred within the diploic space, with variable involvement of both the inner and outer tables; one case was noted to abut the meninges (Case 3). Central lucency was observed on CT imaging in two lesions. While all cases demonstrated indolent, non‐aggressive radiological features, two lesions demonstrated slow interval enlargement on serial imaging with progressive cortical thinning and loss of the deep cortex in one case (Case 5).

Histology

Histologically, all cases showed a proliferation of bland spindle cells arranged in whorls and short loose fascicles in a variable collagenous and fibromyxoid stroma (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5). The lesional cells contained small oval nuclei and elongated tapered cytoplasmic processes. No cytological atypia or mitotic activity was observed. In the majority of the cases (7 of 8), the tumour cells showed a diffuse growth pattern with spindle cells involving host bone in a permeative manner. The remaining case appeared to have a circumscribed border (Case 6). All tumours exhibited foci of dense perivascular hyalinization (8 of 8), and in the vast majority (7 of 8), pericellular hyaline nodules were appreciated throughout the lesion (7 of 8). Microcystic change was present in 2 cases (Case 4, Case 6), while foamy histiocytes (Case 4) and psammomatous calcifications were identified in one case (Case 4). The native bone within the lesion was thinned in most cases (6 of 8), while two cases showed hyperostosis. No lesion contained portions of non‐neoplastic peripheral nerve.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed EMA expression in all cases, with most cases (7 of 8) showing staining in greater than 50% of the tumour cells (3+) (Table 1). All cases tested also showed diffuse staining for collagen IV (3/3). The lesions showed consistent but limited staining for SSTR2a (2 cases, 2+; 4 cases, 1+). PR (0/7), GLUT1 (0/6), CD34 (0/5), SOX10 (0/4) and claudin 1 (0/3) immunoreactivity was absent in all cases evaluated. The lesional cells were negative for S100 (0/6), but the admixed dendritic cells were positive for this marker. Decalcification status was known in six cases; tissue from five of those cases was decalcified with formic acid before processing.

Control cases of fibrous dysplasia (n = 8), burnt‐out Langerhans cell histiocytosis (n = 1) and Paget disease (n = 1) showed no staining for EMA, SSTR2a and PR while intraosseous meningiomas (n = 3) showed consistent expression of SSTR2a (3/3) but variable positivity for EMA (2/3) and PR (2/3).

Discussion

The most common calvarial lesions include histiocytosis, meningiomas, metastases and osteomas, while fibrous dysplasia and intraosseous hemangioma are less common occurrences. 2 In this study, we describe a series of incidental calvarial lesions with unique but consistent radiological and pathological features which we provisionally term ‘perineurioma‐like EMA‐positive calvarial neoplasm (PLECN)’. PLECN has a predilection for the frontal and parietal bones with a female predominance and a median age of presentation in the eighth decade of life. Radiological review often shows ill‐defined lytic lesions with non‐aggressive features but persistent growth and potential cortical breach, in keeping with a low‐grade, slow‐growing intraosseous neoplasm.

The lineage of PLECN remains unclear as they do not show typical features that allow classification into recognized entities. While these tumours have some features suggestive of intraosseous perineurioma, including bland elongated cytology and EMA expression, the lesional cells lack expression of other supportive perineurial markers including GLUT1, claudin 1 and CD34. Intraosseous perineuriomas have been reported in the head and neck region, but these cases have been predominantly limited to the gnathic bones. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Anatomically, the calvarial periosteum (pericranium) is innervated by peptidergic sensory fibres, 9 and neuronal tracing further shows that meningeal afferents send collaterals through cranial foramina and sutures to pericranial tissues including the periosteum. 10 Thus, perineuriomas of the calvarium could theoretically occur. However, to the best of our knowledge, intraosseous perineuriomas of the calvarium have not been previously reported. We also did not identify any peripheral nerve segments in the examined sections, further arguing against these lesions representing a true peripheral nerve sheath tumour.

The anatomical distribution of PLECN raises the possibility that these may represent unusual fibroblastic meningiomas or underrecognized meningothelial proliferations. Additionally, one case abutted the meninges. Cases in our series showed co‐expression of EMA and SSTR2a, and one case in our cohort demonstrated psammomatous calcification and foamy histiocytes, which can be seen in meningiomas. The demographics of our cohort mirror those reported by Vega et al. in their description of microcystic meningioma of the calvarium, and two cases in our series demonstrated focal microcystic architecture. 11 Primary intraosseous meningiomas are rare, accounting for less than 2% of meningiomas overall, with less than 200 cases published in the literature mostly as case reports. 12 , 13 Most intraosseous meningiomas are the meningothelial subtype with epithelioid cytology, contrasting with the histological features of our cohort. Even though SSTR2a was positive in most of our cases, this marker is not entirely specific and can be seen in phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, 14 synovial sarcoma, 15 follicular dendritic cell sarcoma 16 and solitary fibrous tumour. 17 Host bone within a meningioma often becomes hyperostotic; in our cohort, the intralesional bone was predominantly thinned. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Even though PLECN expressed SSTR2a, the degree of staining was more robust in both our control cases of meningiomas, and PLECN were consistently negative for PR. It is well recognized that acid‐based decalcification may adversely affect antigen and nucleic acid preservation, thereby contributing to variability in immunohistochemical results. Although EDTA‐based protocols provide superior antigen retention and are increasingly preferred in contemporary practice, their use remains inconsistent across institutions. These considerations are therefore important when interpreting the immunohistochemical findings in this cohort, as the majority were decalcified with formic acid (5 of 8 cases), and the status of the remainder was unknown, as these cases were received through our consultation practice with limited data.

While decalcification may lead to a false negative result, two of three control cases showed diffuse staining for PR, including cases which were also decalcified. Unfortunately, only formic acid decalcified tissue was available for this study, limiting further molecular testing.

The differential diagnosis of PLECN also includes other common calvarial spindle cell neoplasms or lesions with prominent blood vessels. Fibrous dysplasia often presents as an expansile lesion with a characteristic ‘ground‐glass’ appearance on CT and low‐signal intensity on MRI. 23 Histologically, it shows irregular, curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone within bland fibrous stroma. In PLECN, the formation of new bone accompanying a bland spindle cell proliferation may impart a superficial resemblance to fibrous dysplasia. However, the presence of pericellular hyaline nodules, perivascular hyalinization and EMA immunoreactivity helps distinguish this entity from fibrous dysplasia. Intraosseous hemangioma typically produces a sunburst or honeycomb trabecular pattern on CT due to osseous trabecular thickening and vascular channels. 23 Microscopically, these are vasoformative lesions that lack the EMA‐positive spindle cell proliferation seen in PLECN. Burnt‐out Langerhans cell histiocytosis may enter the differential when presenting as a fibrotic intraosseous skull lesion with residual spindled histiocytoid cells. Prior destructive bone change, residual CD1a or Langerin positivity in lesional cells, coupled with the lack of EMA expression, can aid in the distinction between these two entities. Although the morphological spectrum of Paget disease is broad, end‐stage Paget disease is also a diagnostic consideration. The intra‐trabecular spindle cell component of Paget disease should harbour a null immunophenotype.

Malignant spindle cell neoplasms typically demonstrate more aggressive radiologic characteristics, including cortical destruction, poorly defined margins and extraosseous soft tissue extension. Histologically, low‐grade spindle cell sarcomas exhibit infiltrative growth with at least mild cytological atypia and mitotic activity, features that help distinguish them from PLECN. While low‐grade central osteosarcoma is an exceptionally rare diagnosis in the skull, ancillary testing for MDM2 and CDK4 amplification by fluorescence in situ hybridization may be helpful. Metastatic spindle cell carcinomas may express EMA, but correlation with clinical history and immunohistochemical profiling for cytokeratins and lineage‐specific markers aids in their distinction.

A limitation of the present study is the lack of molecular characterization, and we fully acknowledge that contemporary genomic techniques, including next‐generation sequencing and DNA methylation profiling, can provide important additional insights into tumour classification. However, it is also well recognized that many established mesenchymal neoplasms were originally described and accepted based on consistent clinicopathologic features prior to the routine availability of such molecular methods (e.g., dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, synovial sarcoma and desmoid fibromatosis). Furthermore, there remain several entities in current practice that are primarily defined on morphological and immunophenotypic grounds, with molecular alterations that are either not fully characterized or not entirely specific (e.g., plexiform fibrohistiocytic tumour and cellular neurothekeoma). In this setting, the lesions described in our cohort demonstrate a consistent and reproducible constellation of clinical features, histomorphology and immunophenotype, which, in our view, supports their recognition as a distinct entity. At the same time, we agree that future studies incorporating comprehensive molecular analysis would be of value in further elucidating their pathogenesis and in refining their classification.

To date, PLECN appears to behave in an indolent fashion with no reports of local recurrence or distant disease. However, three cases in our series showed progressive growth with cortical thinning/breach on successive imaging studies. Since these lesions were completely resected, it is difficult to predict the natural progression of tumours without surgical intervention. Additionally, long‐term follow‐up is necessary to better understand the behaviour of this lesion.

In conclusion, we describe a series of novel calvarial lesions with consistent EMA expression and cytologic features of perineurioma but lacking other findings diagnostic of either intraosseous perineurioma, meningioma or other mesenchymal neoplasms. Future DNA sequencing studies to assess for NF2 mutations or DNA methylation profiling may help to yield more definitive data as to whether these represent unusual fibroblastic meningiomas. To date, there has been no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis, but PLECN do show a propensity for progressive growth.
