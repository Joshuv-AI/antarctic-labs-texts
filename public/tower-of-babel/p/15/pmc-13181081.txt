Body mass index (BMI) versus socioeconomic factors and physical activity in early childhood
Authors: Patrycja Widłak, Katarzyna Milde, Paweł Tomaszewski, Monika Lopuszanska-Dawid, Bartosz Molik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical activity is vital for a child’s development and is influenced by their living environment and parental role models. This study examined the relationship between selected socioeconomic factors, children’s physical activity levels, and Body Mass Index (BMI). Data were collected from 9,333 children aged 6.5 to 9.5 years participating in the “Physical Education (PE) with University of Physical Education (abbreviation in Polish - AWF)” Programme (2021–2024). Information included anthropometric data of children and parents, physical activity levels, children’s leisure activities, and parents’ employment status. The data was collected using a questionnaire designed for the programme. Children with normal BMI were more active and more often engaged in organized (χ2 = 33.92; p < 0.001) and unorganized (χ2 = 4.08; p = 0.04) physical activities than obese peers. They spent less time on passive activities like watching TV (χ2 = 51.57; p < 0.001) or using a computer (χ2 = 88.18; p < 0.001). Overweight children preferred sedentary and low-intensity activities. Normal BMI correlated with parents’ higher education and employment (mothers: χ2 = 115.80; fathers: χ2 = 45.30; both p < 0.001) and living in larger cities (χ2 = 40.14; p < 0.001). Parental education and employment positively influence children’s BMI and activity levels. Normal BMI is associated with greater engagement in both structured and unstructured physical activity.Keywords: Early childhood, Physical activity, BMI, Social factors, Economic factorsSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Health care, Risk factors, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

The impact of socioeconomic conditions on child development has been a subject of research for many years1–11. Studies have assessed factors such as parents’ education, income, professional status, and social standing4,7,8. Researchers have also examined access to resources, opportunities for development, and the overall health of both children and parents2,5,8,9,12. Key factors such as unemployment rates in the child’s living environment, income per household member, access to healthcare, and availability of various types of infrastructure have been analyzed3,6. Despite the complexity of these factors and their interactions, researchers agree that poorer socioeconomic conditions are generally associated with worse child development. Another crucial aspect influencing a child’s well-being is physical activity, which is fundamental to both physical and mental health. A strong correlation has been observed between a child’s physical activity levels and those of their parents5,13, as well as the equally essential role of adequate sleep in maintaining overall health. The importance of physical activity for overall health is indisputable. In adults, it contributes to general well-being and a sense of health. In children, it plays an even greater role in proper development. According to World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, children and adolescents should do at least an average of 60 min of moderate to vigorous aerobic activity per day and perform muscle-strengthening and flexibility exercises at least three times per week14. This level of physical activity supports the proper development of the musculoskeletal system, promotes correct posture, and enhances motor skills15,16. Additionally, it contributes to cardiovascular fitness, supports the nervous system, and strengthens the immune system. Regular physical activity also reduces the risk of metabolic disorders, overweight, and obesity17,18. From a psychological perspective, it improves mood, helps regulate emotions, reduces stress, and enhances concentration16,19,20. Furthermore, it fosters the development of social relationships21. Early childhood is a crucial period for establishing healthy habits. At this stage, children are mature enough to understand the link between movement and maintaining health while possessing abundant energy. This makes it an ideal time to instill a lifelong habit of regular physical activity22,23.

The ‘PE with AWF – Active Return to School after the Pandemic’ programme was launched in Poland in 2021 in response to restrictions on physical activity and participation in school and extracurricular sports activities during the COVID-19 pandemic. The programme was developed by experts from Polish physical education academies who, based on many years of research and experience, prepared a set of activities to support teachers in improving students’ physical fitness and mitigating the negative effects of isolation and limited movement. The ‘PE with AWF’ programme was open to all interested physical education teachers who, after completing dedicated training organised as part of the Programme, were prepared to conduct classes with children as part of additional, extracurricular physical activity. This form of activity was an additional sports offer aimed at children willing to participate, enabling them to develop their physical fitness and motor skills outside the standard physical education programme.

In subsequent years, the programme evolved, focusing not only on improving the overall physical fitness of children in Poland, but also on developing fundamental motor skills and promoting the concept of the ‘physical literacy’. In 2023, the programme was renamed ‘PE with AWF – Active today for health in the future’ and, in addition to supporting regular physical activity and raising awareness of the consequences of its deficiency, an assessment of pupils’ motor skills was introduced using a test of Fundamental Motor Skills in Sport (abbreviation in Polish - FUS), developed specifically for the programme, and the assessment of physical literacy. Great emphasis is also placed on the education and involvement of parents who support the development of healthy movement habits at home. The aim is to create a lasting habit of regular physical activity, which has a long-term positive impact on the health of children and society as a whole.

Over a five-year period, the programme offers additional physical activities for children and young people and includes a multifaceted study of the activity levels of participants and their parents24–26.

The novelty of this study stems from its large scale and comprehensive scope of data collection. The project is based on an exceptionally large, nationwide sample of over 9,000 children participating in the ‘PE with AWF’ programme, combining information on physical activity, motor skills and anthropometric parameters with the socio-economic characteristics of families. Such a broad and integrated approach enables a multidimensional analysis of the relationship between family environment, physical activity and child development, including BMI. Unlike most previous studies based on smaller, local samples, this project fills an important gap in the literature, offering a multifaceted insight into the phenomenon on an unprecedented scale and significantly expanding the current state of knowledge.

One of the research objectives of the “PE with AWF” program, and the main aim of this study, was to assess the relationship between selected socioeconomic factors and the body weight and physical activity levels of children from different backgrounds.

The specific objective of the study was to assess the associations between selected socioeconomic factors and children’s body weight status and physical activity levels. In particular, we examined whether parental education and occupational status differentiate children’s physical activity and weight status. Additionally, we investigated whether parental physical activity is associated with children’s activity levels, and whether children with normal BMI are more likely to engage in organised and non-organised forms of physical activity while limiting sedentary behaviours.

In our study, we assumed that higher levels of education and professional status among parents are associated with higher levels of physical activity among children and a healthy body weight. Furthermore, we assumed that children’s physical activity is correlated with their parents’ activity, and that children with a healthy BMI are more likely to participate in organised and informal forms of exercise and spend less time on sedentary activities, which is further supported by adequate sleep, considered an important factor in weight regulation.

Materials and methods

Material and study design

The study used data from 9,333 prepubertal children aged 6.5 to 9.5 years who participated in the “PE with AWF” program between 2021 and 2024. The data was collected using questionnaires completed by the children’s parents. The standard formula was used to calculate the BMI of adults:

BMI = body weight (kg) / height (m)².

For children, BMI was also calculated using this formula and then classified using the Cole index, which defines BMI thresholds for different age groups and genders27,28.

Survey data

The study was conducted using a diagnostic survey method. Data was collected using a proprietary questionnaire created for the ‘PE z AWF’ programme by a team of experts (supervisor - Prof. Monika Lopuszanska-Dawid24–26 responsible for the substantive side of the project.

It was assumed that the questionnaire did not require separate validation, as it only included factual questions that did not involve the measurement of latent constructs or complex scales requiring the assessment of validity and reliability. The tool was based on standard categories commonly used in population studies, which ensures its compliance with recognised methodological standards.

The questionnaire consisted of eight sections and was completed independently by the respondents. It included questions addressed to both parents concerning the basic anthropometric parameters of the child and parents, the child’s physical and recreational activity, the parents’ level of physical activity, as well as the place of residence based on the degree of urbanisation (rural area, urbanised village, small town < 20,000, medium-sized town 20,000–100,000, large town ≥ 100,000 inhabitants). The questionnaire was prepared in Microsoft Forms, and a link to the survey was published on the ‘PE z AWF’ programme website. Teachers implementing the programme sent parents an invitation to participate, encouraging them to complete the survey. The study was survey-based, participation was voluntary, and the sample was convenience-based, which may indicate greater involvement of parents who participated more actively in the programme. Informed consent was obtained by having parents and/or legal guardians complete the survey. The survey was conducted periodically, once in each edition of the programme between 2021 and 2024. Only fully completed questionnaires were included in the analysis. The project was commissioned and financed by the Ministry of Education and Science (agreement no. MEiN/2023/DPI/156 of 6 March 2023).

Although the method based on self-reported data has typical limitations, such as the possibility of distortion or lack of full compliance with reality, the study has significant strengths: the unique nature of the programme, the large sample size and the wide range of data obtained, which significantly increases the cognitive value of the analyses carried out.

Statistical methods

To describe the groups in terms of quantitative variables, basic statistical measures were used, including arithmetic means (x̄), standard deviations (± SD), and medians. Discrete variables were characterised using fractions. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to verify whether the distribution of study variables followed a normal distribution. The assumption of equality of variance in groups was evaluated using Levene’s test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the significance of differences in mean values of the analyzed variables. For significant ANOVA effects, detailed comparisons between pairs of means were made post hoc using the Tukey test for different sample sizes. Partial eta-squared (h2). statistic was used as a measure of effect size. The following classification was adopted: 0.01 - small effect size, 0.06 - medium effect size, 0.14 - large effect size. For count data, a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis rank ANOVA was applied. The relationship between qualitative variables was assessed using the χ² log-rank test of independence. TIBCO Statistica software (version 14.0, TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis [29]. The level of α = 0.05 was set to evaluate the significance of differences and correlations.

Results

Table 2 presents the characteristics of the parents of the studied children, considering age, education level, BMI values, and the amount of time spent on physical activity per week. The analysis found no significant differences in the ages of mothers and fathers of children across different BMI categories. The average age was 37.2 ± 5.1 years for mothers and 40.2 ± 5.4 years for fathers. However, the analysis revealed significant differences in mothers’ BMI values across all child BMI categories (F3.8948=183.0;p < 0.001;ƞ2=0.06). The highest BMI values were found in mothers of obese children, while the lowest were observed in those of underweight children. Among underweight children, no mothers were classified as obese, whereas among obese children, approximately 32% of mothers were also obese (Table 2). For fathers, significant BMI differences were observed for underweight and normal-weight with underweight and obesity of the children. The fathers of overweight and obese children had the highest BMI values, while the fathers of underweight children had the lowest BMI, as in the mothers’ group. As in the mothers’ group, none of the fathers of underweight children were overweight, and only 0.6% were categorised as obese, while among the fathers of obese children, 33% were overweight and 37% obese. A significant correlation was also found between parents’ education level and the BMI category of the child. Mothers and fathers of overweight and obese children were more likely to have vocational, primary/middle school, or incomplete primary education compared to those with secondary or tertiary education. Among normal-weight children, mothers and fathers were more likely to have a university or secondary education. Additionally, the study found that overweight and obese children were more likely to have non-working mothers (difference of ~ 8%, p < 0.001). However, no such relationship was observed among fathers. No significant differences were found between the physical activity levels of mothers and fathers across different child BMI categories.

In addition to analyzing the living environment and surroundings of the studied children, this research also assessed their physical activity levels. (Table 4) The analysis revealed significant differences between BMI categories and the amount of time children spent engaging in physical activity per day. Approximately three times as many obese children and almost twice as many overweight children did not engage in any physical activity at all compared to children in the same BMI categories who participated in some form of physical activity. An inverse relationship was observed in the group of underweight and normal-weight children. Normal-weight children were the most likely to engage in physical activity for more than three hours per day, while underweight children most commonly engaged in two to three hours per day. The smallest proportion of children was those who did not engage in any physical activity at all. Significant differences were also found between BMI categories in parents’ responses regarding their children’s attitudes toward physical activity over the last seven days, as in the case of the declared physical activity time of the surveyed children. Approximately 11% of obese children and 22% of overweight children spent all or most of their time engaged in activities requiring very little physical effort. This is about twice as high as the percentage of their peers who were often or very often physically active in their free time. Among underweight and overweight children, an inverse relationship was observed, where a higher proportion of respondents engaged in physical activity quite often, often, or very often, compared to children in the same BMI category who spent most or all of their time engaging in low-effort activities. Considering the types of sports activities in which early school-age children participated, it was found that approximately 90% of children took part in physical education lessons. The second most frequently chosen activity was unstructured exercise with classmates outside of school (more than half of the surveyed children), with normal-weight children significantly more likely to engage in this activity than obese children (χ2 = 4,08; p = 0,04; Vc = 0.012). It was also found that obese children were less likely to participate in physical activity at a neighborhood club/local children and youth center compared to normal-weight children (χ2 = 33.92; p < 0.001; Vc = 0.035), underweight children (χ2 = 26.16; p < 0.001; Vc = 0.031), and overweight children (χ2 = 20.21; p < 0.001; Vc = 0.027). Additionally, a significantly smaller proportion of obese children (approximately 24%) participated in sports club training and competitions or attended sports schools compared to the other groups (p < 0.001). The highest percentage of children involved in sports training, competitions, or sports classes were normal-weight children (approximately 37%), which was a significant difference compared to the other groups. Furthermore, a significant difference was found in the number of sports activities chosen by children in each BMI category (H(3.9333) = 59.0; p < 0.001). Obese children participated in the fewest sports activities, with an average of 1.8 activities (p < 0.001). Normal-weight children participated in significantly more sports activities than overweight children, with an average difference of 0.1 (z = 3.77; p < 0.001). Underweight and overweight children participated in a similar number of sports activities, averaging two activities.

Discussion

In line with the study objective, this study examined the associations between selected socioeconomic factors and children’s physical activity and body weight status. Beyond identifying the direction of these relationships, we sought to explore potential environmental mechanisms that may shape health behaviour patterns. The findings indicate that parental education, professional status, and physical activity level differentiate children’s lifestyle behaviours and somatic indicators to varying degrees. These results contribute to the broader body of research on the social determinants of health.

Lifestyle, including physical activity, a key component of child development, is closely linked to parents’ knowledge of the subject. This relationship has been confirmed by multiple studies. Several factors influence parents’ knowledge and engagement in developing healthy habits, including their encouragement of and participation in physical activities with their child. These factors include parents’ education level, occupational activity, and socioeconomic conditions30–35. The study concludes that parents with a university education and who were professionally active had lower body weight, suggesting that their lifestyle included healthier habits compared to those with lower education levels and those who were not professionally active. Similar observations regarding the relationship between education level and body weight were made by Benson et al.36 and other researchers, including Saurel-Cubizolles, who, with his team, assessed the French population37. The same conclusion was also reached by Kim et al., who, in a meta-analysis of 24 population-based studies, found a direct association between lower education levels and higher body weight38. In a study of a population of Danish children, the factor of parental education was even the strongest, showing a relationship of the following nature: the lower the education of the parents, the higher the tendency for both parent and child to be overweight39.

The second area assessed in this study was the relationship between the body weight of the studied children and their place of residence. A strong correlation was found between these parameters. Children living in rural or suburban areas showed a significantly higher tendency to be overweight or obese compared to those living in urban areas. Similar observations were made by Erdei et al., who assessed children aged 6 to 8 years from different regions of Hungary. Their findings indicated that rural children were significantly more likely to have excessive body weight compared to their urban peers40. Conversely, completely different results were obtained when analyzing the population of Greek children aged 7 to 9 years. In this group, a clear tendency toward excessive body weight was observed among children living in large cities, particularly in Athens, whereas children from smaller towns and rural areas had lower body weights41. A similar pattern was reported by Oktaviani et al., who analyzed a population of Indonesian children and found a higher prevalence of excess body weight among those living in urban areas42. Wolnicka et al., in their study of 9-year-old Polish girls, presented yet another perspective. Their research, which compared children from rural areas, small towns, and large cities across five provinces, found no significant differences in body weight depending on the area of residence. However, they did observe a general trend toward excessive body weight among all studied groups43.

In this study, in addition to analyzing the living environment and surroundings of the studied children, an assessment of their physical activity levels was conducted. The results indicated that normal-weight children were more physically active than their overweight peers and less likely to spend long hours passively watching television or using computers. Furthermore, children with normal body weight were more likely to participate in a variety of physical activities, both organized and unstructured. Conversely, overweight and obese children were more likely to choose recreational forms of exercise and less likely to participate in organized activities. These findings suggest a direct correlation between body weight and activity levels. However, for children aged 6 to 10 years, due to their developmental stage, it is difficult to determine whether they are more active because they are not overweight or obese or their normal weight is a result of engaging in more frequent physical activity. For overweight children, however, the conclusion appears more straightforward as excess body weight is not conducive to physical activity, causing faster fatigue and poorer sports performance compared to leaner children and discouraging them from engaging in physical exercise. Overweight and obese children tend to avoid organized physical activities, preferring unorganized ones, possibly because they have greater control over the intensity of effort in unorganized activities while their performance is not directly compared to their peers. Raistenskis et al. made similar observations, noting that overweight children engaged in moderate to vigorous physical activity for 22.4 min less per day than their normal-weight peers. Additionally, their performance on the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) was over 50 m worse than that of normal-weight children, indicating that they were both less active and less physically fit44. Conversely, Fairclough et al. found a correlation between physical activity intensity and a tendency toward higher body weight. They reported that overweight children were more likely to engage in lower-intensity activities compared to their normal-weight peers45,46. On the other hand, Oliveira et al. found that participation in organized team sports had a positive impact on reducing body fat and improving cardiorespiratory fitness in overweight and obese children46. Thus, although children with excessive body weight tend to avoid structured physical activities, such exercises are beneficial and, when properly implemented, can positively influence several health parameters47,48. Building on this idea, Puci et al. studied overweight children who participated in online workouts through the Zoom platform. Their findings indicated that this form of training had a measurable positive effect on children’s physical fitness and reduced the stress associated with attending in-person group classes. It can, therefore, serve as a starting point in increasing these children’s motivation to participate in systematic sporting activities and, as a result, improve their self-esteem and become more courageous to participate in stationary activities49.

The study also examined children’s leisure activities. Screen time (computer use for both education and entertainment), watching television, reading books, and listening to music were analysed. These data were compared with the time spent on leisurely physical activity. Although the time spent on these activities varied significantly across body weight categories, the overall trend indicated that children with excess body weight were more likely to engage in passive forms of leisure after school compared to their normal-weight peers. Several studies suggest possible reasons for this relationship. On the one hand, overweight children are less likely to participate in physical activities46,47,50; on the other hand, a strong link exists between passive leisure, especially watching television and video games, and higher calorie intake, especially carbohydrates, which contribute to excess body weight51,52. A trend was also recognised whereby excessive body weight is particularly caused by sedentary activities (watching television, playing video games), while the use of mobile devices is less likely to be a factor, although it also generates a risk of overweight or obesity52–55.

Another aspect analyzed was the relationship between children’s sleep duration, body weight, and BMI. The study found that normal-weight children slept more hours daily than their overweight peers. Similar results were reported by Ruan et al., who conducted a meta-analysis of 25 studies and found a direct relationship between sleep duration and body weight. Each additional hour of sleep per day reduced the risk of overweight or obesity by 21%, and the annual BMI increase was lower by 0.05 kg/m². Furthermore, it was found that children with the longest sleep duration of ~12.2 h per night had a 76% lower risk of excess body weight compared to those who slept around 10.0 h56. Similar findings were reported by Wu et al., who analyzed 50 cohorts from 13 studies from different parts of the world. Analysing the collected data, they confirmed a significant relationship between short sleep duration and obesity risk57. This conclusion was further supported by Sluggett et al., who also identified a direct association between insufficient sleep and obesity in children58.

Limitations of the study: The main limitation of this study was the inability to collect a fully randomized sample of participants. Participation in the “PE with AWF” program was determined by the order of application submitted by physical education teachers, and children’s participation in additional sports activities was voluntary. This may suggest that the program primarily attracted children who preferred active forms of leisure.

Conclusion

Analysis of the data collected allowed key conclusions to be drawn:

Parents with tertiary education levels were associated with lower body weight than individuals with non-tertiary education.

Based on the results of the present research, it can be concluded that higher parental education seems to result in greater knowledge of the principles of optimising the conditions for normal child development. This factor, combined with parents’ occupational activity and greater access to sports facilities, which is more common in larger cities, may contribute to healthier lifestyles, leading to higher levels of physical activity and better weight management in both parents and children. In contrast, physically active children with a normal body weight are more likely to engage in various forms of physical activity because they have better fitness levels, which in turn boosts their self-confidence and fosters a positive attitude toward movement. Therefore, further prevention and education programs on healthy lifestyles should specifically target groups at higher risk of sedentary behavior and unhealthy overweight, particularly poorly educated populations from areas with low levels of urbanization. Additionally, given the significant environmental influences on the development of overweight and obesity, educational programs should be designed to include entire families rather than focusing solely on children or adults. Implementing further public health initiatives of this kind is crucial not only to reduce the economic burden of the obesity epidemic but also to enhance the overall quality of life and promote longevity within the population.
