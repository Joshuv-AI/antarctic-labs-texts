The enteric virus associated with baby chick nephropathy in Japan was genetically identified as chicken astrovirus, not avian nephritis virus
Authors: Masaji MASE, Kanae HIRAMATSU, Satoko WATANABE, Taichiro TANIKAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Japan, avian nephritis virus (ANV) isolates associated with the 1989 outbreak of baby chick nephropathy (BCN) were previously reported as pathologically and serologically distinct from known ANV strains. However, genetic analysis conducted in this study revealed that these viruses were actually chicken astrovirus (CAstV), first described in 2004, and genetically distinct from ANV. Phylogenetic analysis based on the open reading frame 2 (ORF2) gene, which encodes the viral capsid protein, classified this virus within the subgroup Bi, along with isolates from Saga Prefecture in 2020 which were the only CAstV reported in Japan to date. These findings suggest that CAstV was already present in Japan in the 1980s, predating its first global report in 2004 and its first domestic report.

Astroviruses are small, round, non-enveloped viruses with a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genome of approximately 7.5 kb length [12]. This genome contains three overlapping open reading frames (ORFs):ORF1a, ORF1b, and ORF2. ORF1a and ORF1b encode viral protease and polymerase, while ORF2 encodes viral capsid protein [12]. The ORF2 sequence is commonly used for classification and subgrouping of astrovirus isolates [12, 20].

The Astroviridae family comprises two genera; Mamastrovirus and Avastrovirus (AAstV), which infect mammalian and avian species, respectively. Within AAstV, the Avastrovirus galli infects chickens and includes chicken astrovirus (CAstV) and avian nephritis virus (ANV) [25]. Notably, there is no serological cross-reactivity between these two viruses [2].

Among these, CAstV was first reported in 2004 in commercial 5-day-old broiler flocks exhibiting runting-stunting syndrome (RSS) and uneven growth [2]. RSS, also known as malabsorption syndrome, is an enteric disease in chickens that causes significant economic losses due to reduced weight gain, poor feed conversion, and flock nonuniformity [1]. Since then, numerous CAstV strains have been identified and characterized in chickens with RSS worldwide [4, 9, 13, 14, 21, 22]. Genetically, CAstV has been classified into two major groups, A and B, based on the ORF2 sequence [20].

On the other hand, ANV was first isolated in the 1970s from the rectal contents of a one-week-old, apparently healthy broiler chicken [24]. Based on its physicochemical properties, ANV was initially classified as a picornavirus [24]. However, analysis of its complete nucleotide sequence later revealed that ANV belongs to the genus Avastrovirus within the family Astroviridae [6]. Experimental inoculation of one-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) chicks with the ANV isolate resulted in diarrhea, weight loss, mortality, and interstitial nephritis, leading to the naming of the virus as ANV [5].

Subsequently, it was suggested that there are at least two serotypes of ANV, and that its strains differ in pathogenicity [15]. Furthermore, a serologically novel type of ANV associated with baby chick nephropathy (BCN) was reported [16]. BCN is characterized by renal damage and visceral urate deposits and occurs in very young chicks—typically under five days old, and certainly less than seven days [17].

Even in the case of ANV, genetic analysis has been conducted based on the ORF2 sequence, which encodes the capsid protein; to date, 11 genetic groups have been reported [8, 23]. However, in Japan, genetic analysis has only been performed on two ANV strains [6], and no genetic data are available for the BCN-derived ANV strains described above. Therefore, we were interested in determining which genotype the BCN-derived ANV corresponded to and planned to conduct a genetic analysis.

In this study, to contribute to the molecular epidemiology of ANV in Japan, we conducted genetic analyses of BCN-derived ANV strains that had not been previously characterized. Our results revealed that these strains were classified as CAstV, not ANV.

The previously reported WG-3, WG-4, and WG-5 strains, derived from chicks with BCN in 1989, were used in this study [16]. These strains were isolated from the kidneys of affected chicks and passaged 11 times in 6-day-old embryonated eggs, including three rounds of limiting dilution via the yolk sac route. The chicken embryos were harvested, and the homogenized material was freeze-dried and stored in our laboratory. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to the freeze-dried material, which was then used for subsequent testing. As reference ANV strains, the serotype 1 G-4260 strain [24] and the serotype 2 M-8 strain [15] were used.

Viral ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from the above materials using a commercial kit (QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit; QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A reverse transcriptase (RT) reaction was carried out with SuperScript III (Life Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) using random 9 mers.

The resulting PCR products were purified using the Montage purification system (Amicon Ultra, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified products were subsequently used as templates for sequencing using an Applied Biosystems 3130XL automated DNA sequencer, employing dye-terminator cycle sequencing chemistry (BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit, Life Technologies). Sequencing was performed in both directions.

The nucleotide sequences of the PCR products from strains WG-3, WG-4, and WG-5 were found to be completely identical. A GenBank search revealed 96% sequence identity with the FP3 strain of CAstV (GenBank accession number: JN582328), which was isolated in the United Kingdom in 1984 [20]. Consequently, the WG-3, WG-4, and WG-5 strains were genetically identified as CAstV, not ANV. Among these, WG-5 strain was used as a representative strain for the following tests. To facilitate future epidemiological studies, the WG-5 strain has been officially designated as CAstV/Chicken/JP/WG-5/89, following the naming conventions used for influenza viruses. This designation includes the host species, country of origin, specimen identifier, and year of isolation. For simplicity, however, the abbreviation “WG-5” will be used throughout this text.

Next, to further investigate the genetic characteristics of Japanese CAstV, complete genome sequence of WG-5 strain was determined. Specific primers for genome sequencing using the Sanger method were designed based on sequences deposited in GenBank and those obtained from PCR amplicons (Supplementary Table 1). Both the 3′ and 5′ termini of the genome were determined using the rapid amplification of cDNA ends technique (3’ RACE System & 5’ RACE System, Life Technologies).

The sequenced fragments were assembled using ATGC-Mac ver. 10 (Nihon Server Corp., Tokyo, Japan). ORFs were identified using Genetyx-Mac ver. 23 (Nihon Server Corp.) and confirmed by alignment with previously published CAstV genome sequences. The genome sequence of WG-5 strain was deposited in GenBank under the accession number LC918465.

The genome sequence of the WG-5 strain was 7,503 nucleotides in length, excluding the poly (A) tail, and exhibited typical astrovirus features, including a 5’untranslated region (UTR), three ORFs (ORF1a, ORF1b and ORF2), a 3’UTR, and a poly (A) tail [12]. Chicken astrovirus (CAstV) is a relatively newly identified virus, first reported in 2004, and has since been detected in poultry flocks across many countries [2, 19]. This virus is associated with a range of clinical conditions, including diarrhea, visceral gout, RSS, white chick syndrome (WCS), yellow chick, malabsorption, and stunting, all of which contribute to economic losses [4, 9, 13, 14, 21, 22, 26, 27].

In Japan, only one case of CAstV-associated nephropathy was reported in a 2022 article in broiler chicks from Saga Prefecture [10]. However, the present study reveals that the virus was already present in Japan in the 1980s, more than 30 years prior to that report. Although research on CAstV in Japan remains limited, it is hoped that future studies, including comprehensive genomic and epidemiological analyses, will help clarify the prevalence and impact of CAstV in Japanese poultry flocks.

CAstV has been classified into two major groups, A and B, based on ORF2 sequences, and further subdivided into several subgroups [20]. Among these, subgroup Biii viruses have been shown to cause high mortality and gout in experimentally infected specific pathogen-free (SPF) chicks [3], while subgroup Biv viruses are more commonly associated with RSS and WCS [7, 11, 21]. However, the molecular basis of CAstV pathogenicity remains unclear.

The WG-5 strain used in this study was classified into the subgroup Bi, along with three strains isolated from affected chicks in Saga Prefecture, Japan [10]. These strains were the only domestic CAstV isolates obtained from 4-day-old broiler chicks exhibiting high mortality and visceral gout. Also, in a previous study [16], experimental infection of SPF chicks with the WG-5 strain resulted in similar outcomes including nephropathy. Therefore, The WG-5 strain is useful as a pathogenic model and may contribute to elucidating the pathogenic mechanism of CAstV for future research.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that CAstV was already present in Japanese poultry flocks in the 1980s, predating the first reported case in Japan in 2020. The genetic information obtained here, when analyzed with previously reported CAstV sequences, is expected to contribute to a better understanding of the molecular basis of CAstV pathogenicity and support future epidemiological research.

Furthermore, this study highlights the importance of investigating animal pathogens that were genetically uncharacterized in the past and emphasizes the need for molecular characterization.
