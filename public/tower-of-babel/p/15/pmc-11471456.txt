Evaluation of the prevalence and risk factors of perinatal depression among women living with HIV in the Amhara Region, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Aynalem Belay, Asmare Talie, Fentahun Tamene, Asmamaw Getnet, Zewdu Tefera, Tesfalem Geremew
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDepressive disorder is a common mental health issue among perinatal mothers living with HIV, potentially leading to significant despair and anxiety. This condition can hinder maternal-infant bonding and undermine efforts to prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV. Despite its importance, little is known about the factors associated with depression in this population.Main objectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of perinatal depression among women living with HIV in the Amhara region, Northwest Ethiopia, in 2022.MethodsAn institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 399 HIV-positive pregnant and postpartum women, both with follow-up and newly diagnosed cases, who attended PMTCT services in the East Gojjam Zone Public Hospitals from May 28 to July 12, 2022. Participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique. Data were collected through direct interviews and document reviews. The Edinburgh Perinatal Depression Scale was used to assess perinatal depression which demonstrated high internal reliability (Cronbach's alpha of 0.93). Data were cleaned and analyzed using Epi-data version 3.1 and SPSS version 25. Binary logistic regression models were used to identify variables associated with perinatal depression, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsOut of 399 eligible women, 394 participated, yielding a response rate of 98.7 %. The prevalence of perinatal depression among these women was 34.3 % (95 % CI: 29.6–39.4). Factors significantly associated with perinatal depression included intimate partner violence (AOR = 4.24, 95 % CI: 2.507–7.168), internalized stigma (AOR = 4.93, 95 % CI: 2.912–9.560), poor social support (AOR = 4.0, 95 % CI: 1.693–9.560), and poor adherence to antiretroviral therapy (AOR = 3.65, 95 % CI: 1.774–7.519).ConclusionApproximately one-third of perinatal women living with HIV experienced depression. Key factors contributing to this included intimate partner violence, internalized stigma, low social support, and poor adherence to antiretroviral therapy. Interventions should focus on enhancing social support, empowering women, counseling on drug adherence, and screening for depression among perinatal women living with HIV.Keywords: Depression, Ethiopia, HIV/AIDS, HIV-Infected women, Pregnant mothers

Background

Pregnancy is a transformative period for women, marking the transition to motherhood and bringing both joy and significant challenges. For many women, this period is characterized by emotional and psychological adjustments [1]. Perinatal depression, which can occur during pregnancy or within the first year after delivery, is a significant concern during this time. It is characterized by symptoms such as disturbances in eating and sleeping behaviors, exhaustion, crying, anxiety, and feelings of inadequacy in caring for the infant [[2], [3], [4]]. HIV is a devastating disease that severely impacts the immune system, increasing susceptibility to infections and other health issues, particularly during pregnancy [5]. The risk of perinatal depression is further heightened in HIV-positive women, who may experience severe symptoms, including suicidal ideation and self-harm [6]. By 2030, depression and HIV/AIDS are projected to be leading causes of disability worldwide [7]. Evidence indicates that the prevalence of depression among HIV-infected individuals is significantly higher than in the general population, with rates estimated to be as high as 40–42 % [8]. Globally, depressive symptoms among pregnant and postpartum women living with HIV are alarmingly high. For instance, antenatal depression in HIV-positive women has been reported at 36 %, while postnatal depressive symptoms are found in 21 % of cases [9,10]. In Africa, the prevalence of perinatal depression among HIV-infected women is notable, with figures of 23.4 % for prenatal depression and 22.5 % for postpartum depression [11].

In Ethiopia, the prevalence of perinatal depression among HIV-positive women is reported to be 38.4 % [4]. Factors that contribute to perinatal depression among women living with HIV include age, income, level of education, number of deliveries, health problems during pregnancy, prejudice related to HIV, an absence of social assistance, and fears about revealing their infection with HIV, poor ART drug adherence, concerns about their infant's health and HIV status, alcohol use, intimate partner violence, history of previous depression, unplanned pregnancy and family history of psychiatric disorders [1,4,[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Among women living with HIV, perinatal depression (PND) can increase the risk of HIV progression, impaired immune function, and low engagement in HIV care [17,18]. It also impairs child well-being, posing concerns for the perinatal spread of HIV, impaired physical condition, and delayed intellectual, behavioral, and emotional development [4,19]. Mothers suffering from depression are less likely to follow doctors' advice on safe caregiving practices, including vaccines and check-ups for their children [20]. The National Psychological Health Strategies advocates for a distributed approach, with psychological services available at nearby hospitals, district and regional health centers, and higher facilities. It also aims to deliver therapy to the patients in the least restrictive environment [21]. Despite this effort, maternal psychological problems for women having HIV in Ethiopia are prevalent [4]. Depression among women who have HIV is a significant issue that requires attention. Women living with HIV may experience a double burden of physical and mental illness, and perinatal depression has been associated with lower adherence to antiretroviral therapy. The high prevalence of PND and its potential impact on maternal health and HIV care underestimate the need to understand the factors contributing to PND among HIV-positive women and to develop targeted interventions to improve their mental health and overall well-being. It is crucial to understand the prevalence of the condition, identify risk factors, and develop targeted interventions to improve their mental health and overall well-being. This can ultimately lead to better outcomes for both the mother and her child. However, there is insufficient data for assessing the severity and related factors related to prenatal depression. This study aims to examine prenatal depression and related variables within HIV-positive women in the Amhara region Northwest, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design, period and area

A cross-sectional study was undertaken at an institutional level between May 28 and July 12, 2022. The study was conducted at East Gojjam Zone public hospitals. East Gojjam is a zonal administrative zone within the Amhara Region. The zone includes a comprehensive specialty hospital, a general hospital, and eight primary hospitals. There are a total of 743 perinatal women living with HIV who use PMTCT services in East Gojjam zone health facilities.

Source population

The source population consisted of all HIV-positive perinatal women who were receiving Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) services at public hospitals in the East Gojjam Zone.

Study population

The study population included all HIV-positive perinatal mothers who were receiving Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) services at public hospitals in the East Gojjam Zone during the data collection period.

Criteria for inclusion and exclusion

Inclusion Criteria: All HIV-positive women who were either pregnant or postpartum, and who had attended PMTCT services at public hospitals in the East Gojjam Zone within the last 12 months, were included in the study.

Exclusion Criteria: Women who were unable to communicate effectively or who presented at the hospital solely for delivery purposes without prior PMTCT follow-up were excluded from the study.

The sample size and methods for sampling

The sample size was determined by applying the single population proportion formula. Consider the following assumptions: The proportion of women living with HIV who develop perinatal depression was 38.4 % [4], With a level of confidence of 95 %, a five percent margin of error, and a 10 % nonresponse rate, the total sample size is 399.

Methods for sampling

There are ten public hospitals in the East Gojjam Zone. The number of study participants was allocated proportionally (based on the number of HIV-positive pregnant and postpartum women in each hospital). Participants in the study were chosen at random from each institution using a simple random method of sampling. (Fig. 1).

Data collecting tools and techniques

The data collection tool was adapted by reviewing related literature and modified according to the local situation of the study area, but some of the questionnaires were adopted from previous studies, especially the questions to measure the outcome variable. The questionnaires cover sociodemographic, obstetric, HIV, and psychological aspects. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) was utilized to evaluate perinatal depression. Clinical information was gathered from the patient's chart, including viral load, ART regimen, WHO stage, and other characteristics. Data were gathered by standardized, pretested questionnaires, face-to-face interviews. The collection process involved ten trained midwives and two trained psychiatric nurses for supervision.

Instruments

Edinburgh Postnatal depression scale (EPDS)

Depression was assessed by using the EPDS, which is a standardized and structured questionnaire. The EPDS is most frequently used in the research setting and clinical practice. It is a 10-item self-report scale based on a one-week recall. Each question has a score of 0–3, giving a maximum score of 30 and a minimum score of 0. Women with scores of 12 or higher were classified as depressive, while those with scores less than 12 were classified as non-depressed. In Ethiopia, the Amharic version of the EPDS was validated through a study conducted in Addis Ababa with a sample size of 109 participants. The validation research, carried out from September 2008 to June 2009, showed that an area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve of 0.85 and It achieved a sensitivity of 78.9 %, specificity of 75.3 %, for detecting postnatal depression [22]. The internal reliability of Cronbach's alpha for the EPDS was 0.93 [16,23].

Center for Adherence Support Evaluation (CASE) Adherence Index

ART drug adherence was assessed by using the Center for Adherence Support Evaluation (CASE) Adherence Index. It comprises three self-reported measures of adherence: Women with a CASE index score over 10 were regarded to have high adherence, while those with a score below 10 were considered to have 'poor adherence' [24]. To examine perceptions of the risks and benefits of anti-retroviral therapy (ART) for PMTCT, questions were adapted from the NIAID clinical trial group. The study examined women about their confidence in the effectiveness of ART and neonatal prophylaxis for PMTCT, as well as their concerns about the potential harm to their infant [13].

Social support Assessment: Oslo-3 scale

The Oslo-3 scale (3–14) was used to assess social support qualities. The Oslo-3 social support scale assigns scores of 3–8 for poor, 9–11 for moderate, and 12–14 for good support [25].

Internalized prejudice scale

The internalized prejudice scale, consisting of 10 items graded on a 5-point scale from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree," was used to assess respondents' feelings about themselves after being diagnosed with HIV. Summing the replies to all elements yielded a total score (with a possible range of 10–50). Higher mean scores suggest internal stigmatization, while lower levels do not [26]. Alcohol use history was examined by asking if the individual had consumed alcohol since the start of pregnancy [4].

Intimate partner violence

Intimate partner violence was measured using the World Health Organization's (2005) criteria. The questionnaire included four items for psychological violence, six for physical violence, and three more for sexual violence [27].

Data quality assurance

The investigator constructed the data-collecting instrument in English, translated it into Amharic, and then returned to English to ensure consistency. The principal investigator taught data collectors and supervisors for one day about the study's purpose, tool contents, and sampling technique, and provided clear and acceptable descriptions to participants. Before data collection, the questionnaire was pretested on 5 % of the overall sample population at Finote Selam and Burie Asrade public hospitals from May 20–26/2022. The questionnaires were adjusted depending on the pretest findings and collected by skilled data collectors. The selected and trained supervisors monitored the data collectors to ensure the accuracy and uniformity of the completed questionnaires.

Data processing and analysis

The data was validated, coded, cleaned, and entered using Epidata version 3.1 before being exported to Statistical Product and Service Solutions version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were calculated. The analyzed data was presented as text, tables, and graphics. Bivariable logistic regression was used to test the relationship between independent variables and the dependent variable. Variables with a p-value of ≤0.25 were included in the final multivariable logistic regression model to identify factors associated with perinatal depression. The adjusted odds ratio and 95 % confidence interval were calculated to assess the association's strength and direction. A p-value of <0.05 indicated statistical significance. The Hosmer-Lemshow's test was used to assess the quality of the final model, which yielded a p-value of 0.55. The multicollinearity test examined the association between independent variables using the variance inflation factor. All variables had a variance inflation factor of less than 4, indicating no multicollinearity.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was conducted under the Ethiopian Health Research Ethics Guideline and the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Research Ethics Review Committee of Debre-Markos University (Ref. No: HSC/R/C/Ser/PG/Co/163/05/2022). A formal letter of administrative approval was gained from each selected hospital. Written informed consent was taken from each of the study participants after a clear explanation of the aim of the study.

The majority of participants (317, 80.5 %) were urban residents. Regarding educational status, 168 (42.6 %) of the respondents were unable to read and write. One-third of 135 (34.3 %) of the study participants had 1651-3200-birr monthly income (Table 1).

Intimate partner violence

Among the study participants, 138 (35 %) had experienced intimate partner violence; specifically, 109 (30.7 %) and 102 (25.9 %) had experienced psychological and physical violence, respectively, whereas 18 (4.6 %) had experienced sexual violence.

Magnitude of depression

The magnitude of depression during the perinatal period among women who have HIV was 34.3 % (n = 135) (95 % CI: 29.6, 39.2). (Fig. 3).

Factors associated with PND among women living with HIV

Binary logistic regression was used to assess the relationship between sociodemographic, obstetric, HIV/AIDS, and psychological factors and perinatal depression. Variables having a P-value of less than 0.25 in bivariable logistic regression analysis, including intimate partner violence, internalized AIDS stigma, low social support, parity, unplanned pregnancy, HIV drugs that can prevent HIV from spreading to the baby, alcohol use, drug adherence, ART regimen and comorbid illness, were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model and examined using the backward likelihood ratio approach. Hence, Variables having a P value < 0.05 and AOR with 95 % confidence intervals were considered statistically significant relationships. The multivariate logistic regression study found that intimate partner violence, internalized AIDS stigma, limited social support, and poor ART adherence were all significantly linked with perinatal depression. The model's goodness of fit was assessed using Hosmer and Lemeshow's test, yielding a p-value of 0.552. Multicollinearity was checked between independent variables by using the variance inflation factor, and All predictor variables had variance inflation factors <4, indicating no multicollinearity. After adjusting for confounding variables, this study found that women experiencing intimate partner violence were four times as likely to develop perinatal depression than their non-HIV counterparts. (AOR = 4.24 95 % CI: 2.507–7.168 with P value < 0.001). The likelihood of perinatal depression among women living with HIV who had internalized AIDS stigma was 5 times higher than that among women who had no internalized AIDS stigma (AOR = 4.93 95 % CI: 2.912–8.328 with p-value <0.001). In addition, Women with inadequate social support were four times more likely to experience prenatal depression compared to those with good support (AOR = 4.02, 95 % CI: 1.693–9.560, p-value = 0.002). Furthermore, Women with poor ART drug adherence had a 3.6 times higher risk of developing perinatal depression compared to those with strong adherence (AOR = 3.6 95 % CI: 1.774–7.519, p-value <0.001) (Table 5).

Discussion

The study discovered that 34.3 % of HIV-positive women develop perinatal depression, which is a significant concern. This implies that one in three HIV positive women was screened positive for depression. This is due to a double burden of high depression risk, financial difficulties, a lack of social support, and concerns for their children's health, all of which are influenced by risk factors such as HIV, pregnancy, fear of transmission, and stigma [28]. The study's results were similar to those conducted in Gondar (38. 4 %) [4] Uganda 39 % [29], Thailand (30 %) [30], and Los Angeles (30.8 %) [31]. Yet, the outcomes of the present research were lower than those of the other research conducted in South Africa (48.7 %) [16], India (52.5 %) [32], and Chicago (46 %) [33]. The possible reasons for the disparity include sociodemographic, lifestyle, study period/time, media access improvement and health care systems [13,33]. Nevertheless, the result of this research was higher than those conducted in Ma1awi (13 %) [34], Ukraine (25 %) [13], and Pennsylvania(25 %) [1]. The variance is likely related to sociodemographic or lifestyle deviations and the health care system [1,34]. The health care system of developed countries (Pennsylvania) recommends breastfeeding avoidance among women living with HIV, which may reduce depression [35]. In addition to reporting the magnitude of perinatal depression, the present study was also aimed at exploring factors associated with it. Women who had experienced intimate partner violence were more likely to develop perinatal depression among women living with HIV compared with those who had not experienced Violence against intimate partners. This has been strengthened by research done in South Africa [16], Zimbabwe [15], and Thailand [30]. This may be due to Pregnancy and childbirth being pivotal times in relationships, presenting both joy and challenges. Women experiencing intimate partner violence face extreme control, reduced access to support services, and economic difficulties. This can lead to shame, powerlessness, isolation, and depression [36,37]. The likelihood of perinatal depression among women living with HIV who had internalized AIDS stigma was higher than that among women who had no internalized AIDS stigma. Similar outcomes have been reported in Gondar [4], Kenya [38], and South Africa [16]. The possible explanation may be that HIV-positive women may experience negative self-images, leading to feelings of guilt, shame, and low self-esteem, affecting their daily lives, and social interactions, and causing emotional and behavioral consequences [38]. In addition, Individuals may fear the negative consequences of disclosure, including social withdrawal, rejection, and reduced emotional support, leading to demoralization, low self-efficacy, and distress [4]. In addition, women having minimal social support had a higher risk of developing perinatal depression compared to those with great support. This result was strengthened by investigations carried out in Kenya [38], South Africa [16], Ukraine [13], and Nordic countries, including Denmark, Finland, and Sweden [28]. This may be because Social support, including family, friends, and communities, provides psychosocial and material support during illness, pregnancy, and stressful conditions. It enhances self-esteem and promotes healthy lifestyles. Low social support can lead to isolation, loneliness, worthlessness, and economic instabilities, potentially predisposing individuals to depression during this perinatal period [13,28] Furthermore, women with insufficient compliance with ART drugs increased the risk of depression in the perinatal period than their counterparts. This association was supported by studies conducted in Gondar [4], Addis Ababa [24], South Africa [16], and Los Angeles [31]. This may be because women with Poor ART drug adherence in women can lead to advanced viral replication, increased transmission into fetus, immunosuppression, and recurrent infections, resulting in depression, loss of interest, guilt, and poor performance in daily activities [24].

Conclusion

The study's findings reveal a significant prevalence of depressive disorders during the perinatal period, with one-third of HIV-positive perinatal women experiencing depression. Factors such as intimate partner violence, internalized AIDS stigma, poor social support, and insufficient ART drug compliance were identified as contributors to perinatal depression. To address this issue, there is a need to enhance social support systems, provide counseling on ART drug compliance, and implement strategies to prevent intimate partner violence. To effectively support women suffering from perinatal depression, mental health services should be fully integrated into PMTCT care. This can be achieved by training healthcare providers in mental health screening and intervention, establishing routine screening programs for depression during ANC visits, and creating targeted interventions tailored to the needs of HIV-positive women. Additionally, ensuring that these support services are accessible, culturally sensitive, and adequately funded will be essential for their success.

Study strengths and limitations

This study assessed multiple variables associated with perinatal depression, covering a large geographical area, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. However, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causality, and there may be recall and social desirability biases from participants.
