The Bacterial Landscape of Facial Skin: From Homeostasis to Skin Conditions
Authors: Simon Ernst, Thomas Dirschka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human facial skin microbiome is a complex and dynamic ecosystem that plays a central role in maintaining skin health, immune regulation, and preventing dermatological skin conditions. Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis) are the most prominent bacterial species, with shifts in their relative abundance correlating with skin site, age, skin site, and health status. Exploring the facial microbiome offers exciting opportunities, though it requires careful methodological consideration. Sampling techniques vary in invasiveness and depth, which can influence the accuracy and reproducibility of microbiome profiles. While traditional cultivation methods provide valuable insights, they often miss nonculturable microbes, limiting the view of microbial diversity. Molecular approaches such as amplicon sequencing and metagenomics enable a more comprehensive understanding of microbial communities, even though they currently cannot distinguish between viable and nonviable microbes. Addressing these challenges will help unlock the full potential of facial microbiome research. A balanced facial skin microbiome is associated with healthy skin, whereas a dysbiosis of C. acnes and S. epidermidis is commonly observed in acne-prone skin and more pronounced clinically manifest acne. A comprehensive understanding of the diversity and distribution of C. acnes phylotypes, as well as distinct lineages of S. epidermidis associated with skin disorders, is crucial for developing targeted, microbiome-based cosmetic and medical treatments. Emerging strategies aim to restore microbial balance by leveraging the skin’s native microbiota, including probiotic approaches. These strategies represent a promising yet still emerging approach, as current clinical evidence remains limited and further well-controlled studies are required, although they may offer benefits by enhancing microbial diversity and supporting skin barrier function.

Introduction

A Planet on its Own—The Skin Ecosystem

The distribution and localization of bacteria on human skin are complex and dynamic [1]. The skin has three main layers: the epidermis, the dermis, and the hypodermis. The outer layer, the epidermis is composed of multiple layers of keratinocytes that undergo terminal differentiation, forming the stratum corneum, a barrier of cross-linked corneocytes that protects the skin [2]. The dermis is the central, denser layer primarily composed of resilient connective tissue. It houses blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles, sweat glands, and sebaceous (oil) glands. Additionally, the dermis is responsible for producing collagen and elastin, which contribute to the skin’s strength and elasticity [3]. The third layer, the hypodermis, commonly referred to as the subcutaneous layer, is the innermost layer of the skin. It is mainly made up of fat and connective tissue, which serves to secure the skin in place and insulate the body. Additionally, it contributes to energy storage and helps create a smooth appearance of the skin [4].

The skin constitutes diverse habitats and ecological niches that select for sets of particularly well-adapted microorganisms comprising bacteria, fungi, viruses, and microscopic animals such as mites. However, relative to other body sites, it exhibits a notably low biomass, attributable to its nutrient-poor, exposed, and arid environment [5, 6]. Skin habitats are typically categorized into sebaceous, moist, and dry areas, owing to regional differences in skin anatomy. The facial skin has been particularly well-studied because of its visibility and vulnerability to skin conditions. The facial microbiota primarily consists of bacteria such as Cutibacterium acnes and species of the genus Staphylococcus, mainly S. epidermidis, both preferring different ecological niches (shown in Fig. 1). Sebaceous areas of the face comprise the forehead, nose, and chin, and are characterized by an increased density of pilosebaceous units. These areas tend to harbor a high microbial density and favor the growth of lipophilic microorganisms, e.g., Cutibacterium. Moist areas such as the nostrils and the nasolabial fold instead reveal elevated levels of S. epidermidis and members of the genus Corynebacterium [1]. In contrast, drier areas, including the cheek, exhibit lower quantities of bacteria, but are characterized by a higher microbial diversity [7]. Especially cutaneous appendages and invaginations are relevant for microbial diversity. Facial skin pores act as anatomically isolated microhabitats that strongly restrict microbial migration. Their structure creates single-cell bottlenecks that limit ecological interactions within pores and thereby stabilize resident C. acnes lineages, while promoting the coexistence of multiple strains across the facial skin [8]. Additionally, the composition of the facial skin microbiome varies considerably with age. In neonates and infants, microbial communities tend to be less diverse and are mainly composed of Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species, indicating initial microbial colonization [10]. As individuals mature, the skin microbiome exhibits increasing diversity, gradually evolving to resemble adult microbial community patterns. C. acnes, Corynebacterium, and Staphylococcus characterize this maturation process [11]. During adolescence, there is a notable increase in lipophilic C. acnes, especially in sebaceous areas, which closely matches the rise in sebum production typical of this stage [12, 13]. In adulthood, the facial skin microbiome generally stabilizes but remains vulnerable to changes caused by skin health, hygiene, and environmental factors. For older individuals, microbial diversity typically increases, accompanied by shifts in community composition, most notably a decline in C. acnes and an increase in Proteobacteria populations. These changes are thought to be predominantly driven by age-related alterations in skin physiology, including sebum production, moisture content, skin pH, and immune response [14–17]. Recent research suggests even species-specific age-related colonization dynamics. C. acnes colonization rate was found to be highest during the transition to adult facial skin, exhibiting a remarkable community stability during adulthood. However, S. epidermidis lineages show high turnover throughout life, with each lineage typically lasting only about 2 years. These lineages are constantly acquired and lost, even within short time frames, while the forces that drive these dynamics remain elusive [18].

Moreover, skin issues/disorders can cause disruption of the cutaneous ecosystem through genetic predisposition, environmental insults, immune dysregulation, or antimicrobial therapy. Microbial dysbiosis in this context is typically characterized by a loss in microbial diversity, depletion of beneficial commensal taxa, an overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens, and functional alterations within the microbial community. These microbiome shifts are usually disorder specific and best described in atopic dermatitis and acne vulgaris. Atopic dermatitis flares are accompanied by a decrease in microbial diversity, an overgrowth of Staphylococcus aureus and a depletion in coagulase-negative Staphylococci (CoNS) as well as Cutibacteria [19]. Acne, however, is mainly associated with a loss of diversity within C. acnes communities. In contrast to healthy skin, acne-prone skin exhibits enrichment of C. acnes phylotypes IA1 and IA2, whereas a reduction in health-associated phylotypes II and III is commonly observed. Although other phylotypes can be detected, their relative abundance is markedly reduced [20–23].

Taken together, this highlights the dynamic and individualized nature of the microbiome across various skin regions, stages of life, host factors, skin health, and individual lifestyle.

The aim of this narrative review is to summarize current knowledge on the facial skin bacteriome, with a particular focus on the dominant bacterial species C. acnes and S. epidermidis, their distribution across facial skin sites, their relevance in healthy and acne-prone skin, and the methodological approaches used to study them. In addition, this review highlights emerging microbiome-based strategies for acne-prone skin and discusses their therapeutic potential and current limitations.

The Role of the Microbiome in Skin Health and Skin Issues

Two of the most common and arguably most important bacterial inhabitants of facial skin are C. acnes and S. epidermidis. Recent research uncovered growing evidence for their contribution to skin health, but also to their involvement in skin issues.

Originally classified as Propionibacterium acnes owing to its ability to produce propionic acid, this skin-associated bacterium was reclassified as Cutibacterium acnes in 2016 after genomic and phylogenetic analyses demonstrated its distinct lineage and specific adaptation to the human cutaneous environment [24]. Under normal conditions, C. acnes is considered crucial for maintaining skin health by supporting barrier function and microbial balance. For example, it produces short-chain fatty acids, such as propionic acid, which lower the skin’s pH and inhibit the growth of harmful pathogens [25]. Additionally, C. acnes secretes bacteriocin-like substances to suppress competing bacteria and maintain its presence in sebaceous follicles [26]. The bacterium may further contribute to skin health not only by mitigating oxidative stress through the secretion of the potent antioxidant RoxP [27], but also by synthesizing essential vitamins such as vitamin B12, riboflavin, and folate [28]. Moreover, C. acnes activates Toll-like receptors, e.g., TLR2, to stimulate the production of host antimicrobial peptides and regulate immune responses [29]. These beneficial properties, among others, contribute to a balanced and healthy skin environment.

Conversely, C. acnes is frequently recognized for its association with the inflammatory skin condition acne vulgaris. It possesses considerable virulence potential, which may, however, vary significantly among phylotypes [30–32]. Secreted lipases hydrolyze sebum triglycerides, generating free fatty acids that can contribute to inflammatory processes. Notably, an acne-associated strain belonging to phylotype IA1 has been shown to elicit a stronger inflammatory response on HaCaT keratinocytes and exhibit enhanced growth in lipid-rich environments compared with a nonacne-associated strain [33]. Additionally, hyaluronidases, glycosidases, and proteases break down a variety of human tissue components, thereby promoting inflammation and acne progression [34–36]. Christie–Atkins–Munch–Petersen (CAMP) factors are pore-forming toxins that disrupt host cell membranes, leading to tissue damage. In C. acnes, five CAMP factor genes (CAMP1-5) have been identified, which show phylotype-specific expression patterns and have been implicated in the bacterium’s virulence [37, 38]. Furthermore, porphyrins produced by C. acnes have been linked to the induction of perifollicular inflammatory responses via cytotoxic mechanisms, including K+ leakage and upregulation of proinflammatory signaling pathways. Notably, strains associated with acne vulgaris appear to produce significantly higher amounts of porphyrins compared with health-associated strains [39, 40].

S. epidermidis represents a key commensal of the skin microbiome, contributing to colonization resistance and preventing the ecosystem from invading pathogens and opportunistic microbes [15, 41–43]. Compared with C. acnes, S. epidermidis displays even greater strain-level heterogeneity, particularly regarding genes related to virulence and host interaction. Approximately 20% of its genome is variable, allowing for a remarkable genetic repertoire and adaptability [44]. The bacterium positively influences skin barrier homeostasis and integrity by modulating host immune function, accelerating wound healing and producing protective ceramides. For example, secreted factors of S. epidermidis were found to activate different innate immune response pathways and induce expression of antimicrobial peptides in human keratinocytes [45]. In mouse models, S. epidermidis-derived lipoteichoic acid and lipopeptide LP78 moreover inhibited skin inflammation and accelerated wound healing through Toll-like-receptor 3 mediated mechanisms [46]. In a recent study, it was demonstrated that S. epidermidis produces a potent sphingomyelinase, which enhances ceramide levels and effectively prevents transepidermal water loss, thereby contributing to skin hydration and barrier function in vivo [47].

Other studies revealed that S. epidermidis not only affects its host but contributes to a balanced microbiome by synthesizing antimicrobial substances and suppressing virulence of invading pathogens. Release of antimicrobial peptides, such as phenol-soluble modulins (PSMs), lantibiotics, or the recently discovered peptide polyene epifadin effectively kill and inhibit pathogens including S. aureus and group A Streptococcus [48–51]. An additional mechanism by which commensal bacteria can outcompete opportunistic pathogens is through quorum sensing interference. Staphylococcus epidermidis produces autoinducing peptide I (AIP-I), which has been shown to inhibit the agr quorum sensing system of S. aureus. This inhibition attenuates the production of virulence factors and protects the skin barrier from damage. It has been proposed that this inter-species communication could be an important mechanism that underlies the competitive advantage of S. epidermidis over S. aureus on healthy skin [52].

In addition to its beneficial role as a commensal organism, the pronounced genetic plasticity and variability of S. epidermidis enables it to act as an opportunistic pathogen under certain conditions [43]. Several skin issues have been associated with shifts in S. epidermidis abundance and strain diversity, including atopic dermatitis and recently acne vulgaris [22, 53].

Recent findings shed first light on the involvement of S. epidermidis in acne pathophysiology. Elevated levels of the bacterium have been observed in areas affected by comedones and papulo-pustular lesions compared with healthy skin [54]. Notably, the relative abundance of Staphylococcus species appears to increase with acne severity, indicating a possible contribution to disease progression. Interactions between S. epidermidis and C. acnes have been documented, with evidence suggesting that S. epidermidis may inhibit C. acnes growth through fermentation-derived short-chain fatty acids and antimicrobial peptide production [55], potentially influencing the microbial equilibrium in acneic skin.

Furthermore, phylogenetic analyses have identified four distinct lineages of S. epidermidis [56]. Among these, types 1 and 2 are relatively enriched in acne-affected skin, whereas type 3 shows reduced relative abundance, implying lineage-specific roles in skin homeostasis and inflammation [22]. These observations underscore the complexity of S. epidermidis involvement in acne and highlight the need for further mechanistic studies to elucidate its functional contributions to disease onset and progression.

Sampling Techniques for Analysis of the Facial Skin Microbiome

The human facial microbiome has garnered significant interest, yet debates continue over the methods used to study its composition and functions. These disagreements impact the reproducibility of research results and our understanding of how microbes affect skin health and disease. In general, the skin microbiome has a lower biomass compared with other body regions such as the gut due to its nutrient-poor, exposed, and dry environment [5, 6]. This creates specific challenges for microbiome studies, as most current protocols are designed for high-biomass sites, such as the gut [57]. Low-biomass samples are more susceptible to contamination during collection and processing, resulting in false positives and biases [58–60]. Consequently, developing a reliable sampling method is crucial for accurate analysis of the skin microbiome.

There is an ongoing debate on which sampling method is appropriate for facial skin microbiome analysis. Current research has demonstrated that both sampling and sequencing methods significantly impact the results of skin microbiome studies [61].

Several validated methods exist for sampling the skin microbiome, such as swabs, biopsies, surface scrapes, cup scrubs, and tape strips [11, 62–68]. Each technique differs in terms of biomass yield, the amount of human DNA involved, sampling depth, and invasiveness. The scientific question, study design, study population, and the specific sampling sites should guide the choice of method.

The most commonly used sampling technique is premoistened swabbing [69, 70]. This noninvasive method is frequently selected to minimize discomfort in study participants, particularly those with skin diseases [54]. An additional benefit of using cotton swabs is that they tend to minimize human DNA contamination, as they mainly collect microbes from the outermost skin layer (stratum corneum), which is mostly made up of dead keratinocytes lacking genomic DNA [71]. Conversely, dry swabbing [72] is less prevalent because it collects less biomass. Tape stripping yields a consistently high level of biomass [62] and allows for analysis of stratum corneum depth within a specific area [67, 73]. However, tape stripping is not appropriate for all body regions due to its size and the duration required for sample collection. Different film dressings are available, including urethane or silicone adhesives, which are especially feasible for individuals with fragile skin [74]. Scraping methods can enhance the amount of biomass gathered and may be advantageous in studies focusing on low-abundance microbes, such as fungi. Nevertheless, this method can only capture superficial bacteria and may not be representative of the whole skin microbiome [64, 65]. The cyanoacrylate glue method can be used on any body area. But harvesting from hairy regions can be painful due to hair removal. Additionally, the strong intercorneocyte cohesion on palms and soles can exceed the glue bond, making uniform collection difficult. Moreover, this method cannot be performed on oozing or eroded lesions as the adhesive does not bond effectively with such surfaces [75]. Moreover, different side effects, such as e.g. allergic reactions or skin irritations can occur [76]. Cup scrubbing, initially developed for culture-based investigations [77], is also applicable in microbiome research [62]. Skin punch biopsies are an invasive sampling approach that enables the examination of microbial DNA from the skin’s deeper layers. Besides homogenizing tissue for comprehensive DNA extraction, some biopsy samples can also be used for additional analyses, including microscopy and fluorescence in situ hybridization [65, 66, 78].

In summary, various sampling techniques offer distinct advantages and limitations, and their selection should be guided by specific research objectives or diagnostic requirements. Among these, swabbing is a widely used approach for facial microbiome sampling. It is easy to perform, minimally invasive, and less susceptible to technical failure. Importantly, swab-based sampling provides a representative profile of the facial microbiome, yielding results comparable to more invasive methods such as biopsies [70, 79].

Methods to Analyze the Facial Skin Microbiome

Cultivation-based methods have been fundamental in microbiology since the pioneering work of Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch in the late nineteenth century [80, 81]. These methods involve isolating microorganisms from skin samples on growth media, allowing researchers to identify and characterize viable microbes through morphological, biochemical, and molecular techniques. Historically, Koch established criteria for linking specific microbes to diseases, laying the foundation for diagnostic microbiology. A key advantage of cultivation-based approaches is the detection of viable cells, which confirms that microbes are alive and potentially active contributors to skin processes or pathologies. However, these methods have notable limitations: many skin microorganisms are fastidious or exist in states that prevent their growth in standard culture conditions, leading to an underestimation of the true diversity within the microbiome [63]. Furthermore, culture methods are labor-intensive, often time-consuming, and biased toward faster-growing species. Nevertheless, cultivation remains a valuable tool for studying microbial physiology, virulence, and antibiotic susceptibility profiles, providing essential functional insights that complement molecular techniques.

High-throughput sequencing technologies have revolutionized the analysis of the facial microbiome, enabling comprehensive taxonomic and functional characterization of microbial communities. Amplicon-based sequencing, typically targeting conserved marker genes such as the 16S rRNA gene for bacteria or the Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) regions for fungi, remains a widely used and cost-effective approach [82]. This method allows for profiling microbial composition at the genus or species level across large sample cohorts but is limited in its resolution, often unable to discriminate between strains owing to sequencing depths, primer biases, and reliance on reference databases [83].

In contrast, metagenomic shotgun sequencing offers an in-depth perspective by sequencing all genomic content within a sample, facilitating strain-level resolution and the assessment of functional potential such as metabolic pathways, and antimicrobial resistance genes [84]. Moreover, metagenomics and recently developed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)-based methods allow accurate definition of microbial communities and provide unmatched taxonomic resolution even in extremely low biomass samples [85]. Although more resource-intensive in terms of cost and computational requirements, and with higher requirements regarding quality of the samples, this approach captures bacteria, viruses, fungi, and their genes simultaneously, providing a holistic view of skin microbiota ecology.

For higher-resolution strain discrimination, methods including single-locus sequence typing (SLST) focus on a single informative locus, for example, the recA gene in C. acnes, allowing rapid and targeted strain analysis [86]. SLST is advantageous for its simplicity and speed but is limited to specific taxa. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), which sequences multiple housekeeping genes, offers even greater discriminatory power and has been extensively used for epidemiological study of C. acnes, revealing diversity linked to pathogenicity [20, 32]. However, MLST is more laborious and less suitable for large-scale community profiling.

Addressing fungal components, particularly yeasts such as Malassezia species, relies on sequencing the ITS region, which serves as a universal fungal barcode. ITS sequencing provides high-resolution species identification, crucial for understanding fungal roles in skin conditions such as dermatitis and dandruff, though primer biases and limited fungal reference databases sometimes hinder comprehensive analysis [87].

In summary, the choice of sequencing approach in facial microbiome studies depends on research goals, balancing depth of resolution, cost, and technical resources. While amplicon sequencing remains useful for broad community surveys, metagenomic and strain typing methods such as SLST and MLST enable more detailed insights at the strain and functional levels.

Understanding the Facial Skin Microbiome in Acne-Prone Skin

Acne vulgaris is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder that affects the pilosebaceous unit [88]. It is characterized by elevated sebum production, leading to noninflammatory comedones and inflammatory lesions, such as papules, pustules, or nodules, primarily on the face, back, and chest. In addition, early, nonpathological precursors of acne include follicular hyperkeratosis and the formation of microcomedones—accumulations of keratinocytes and sebum within the pilosebaceous unit—often accompanied by increased sebum secretion and initial shifts in the skin microbiome. In addition to acute symptoms, acne may result in persistent erythema, hyperpigmentation, and scars, commonly associated with a negative self-image [89]. As recently reviewed by Layton and colleagues [90], an increasing number of studies highlight the adverse effects of acne on quality of life, social life, and its association with psychological disorders. For example, social media and dating behavior of women suffering from acne was noticeably affected due to acne lesions and postacne marks [91].

The etiology of acne involves multiple external and internal factors, including among others, environmental variables, nutrition, smoking, stress, genetics, and hormonal state [92]. The pathophysiology is characterized by changes in sebum production, hyperkeratinization of pilosebaceous units, increased release of inflammatory mediators and disruption of the microbial balance [39, 93–95]. During puberty, hormone-induced increased sebaceous gland activity results in excess sebum production and causes hyperkeratinization, creating a microenvironment that allows overgrowth of certain C. acnes lineages [12].

The proliferation of C. acnes within the pilosebaceous unit and the resulting disruption of the skin microbiome further cause inflammatory processes by stimulating keratinocytes, sebocytes, and immune cells such as peripheral blood mononuclear cells to produce proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin(IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor(TNF)-α, and IL-8. This cytokine release promotes immune cell infiltration and sustains inflammation, leading to tissue damage and lesion development [96, 97]. Notably, high levels of porphyrin production by C. acnes have been strongly associated with increased acne severity. Porphyrins, which are photosensitizing molecules, can induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses upon exposure to light, exacerbating lesion formation [40, 98]. Conversely, reduced porphyrin levels have been observed in patients who respond well to acne treatments, correlating with clinical improvement and decreased bacterial virulence [99].

Another aspect in acne development is the bidirectional interaction between sebum composition and bacterial lipid metabolism. Lipidomic analyses of various Cutibacterium strains have revealed distinct bacterial lipid profiles, including specific phosphatidylcholines and cardiolipins, which indicate metabolic adaptation to sebum and vary across phylotypes. These strain-specific lipid signatures suggest that bacterial lipid metabolism plays a role in niche colonization and pathogenic potential [100]. Complementary metabolomic studies of sebum in patients with acne demonstrate significant dysregulation of the lipid metabolism, characterized by elevated proinflammatory free fatty acids, peroxidized squalene, and reduced linoleic acid levels. Such alterations not only create a favorable environment for C. acnes proliferation but also amplify inflammatory signaling, contributing to follicular dysbiosis and acne exacerbation [101–103].

A significant association between follicular C. acnes populations and acne development has been established by Jahns and colleagues in 2012 [104]. In particular, phylotypes IA1 and IA2 have consistently been linked to acne vulgaris [21] and overgrowth of C. acnes within comedonal contents of inflammatory acne lesions further supports its involvement in disease pathogenesis [105].

Besides C. acnes, S. epidermidis and other staphylococci play a role in acne pathology [54]. A comparative analysis of facial skin microbiomes in healthy individuals and those with moderate-to-severe acne revealed significant shifts in bacterial composition. Beyond changes in C. acnes phylotype distribution, acne was associated with increased relative abundances of Staphylococcus epidermidis, S. capitis, and S. saprophyticus, while S. saccharolyticus and S. hominis decreased. Additionally, lineage-level shifts within S. epidermidis populations were observed [22].

S. epidermidis can suppress C. acnes growth and attenuate its proinflammatory effects on the skin by producing succinic acid, a fermentation-derived fatty acid that inhibits C. acnes proliferation and reduces keratinocyte cytokine release [55]. Other coagulase-negative staphylococci, such as S. capitis contribute to the microbial equilibrium by producing phenol-soluble modulins that selectively kill C. acnes [106].

Conversely, C. acnes limits S. epidermidis development and biofilm formation by modulating follicular microenvironment, hydrolyzing sebum triglycerides, and secreting propionic acid [107]. Both species also impact monocyte survival, suggesting a potential link to postinflammatory hyperpigmentation [108].

Although the exact mechanisms of bacterial interactions in the development of blemished and acneic skin remain elusive, maintaining a balanced microbial community appears critical for skin homeostasis.

C. Acnes Phylotypes and Their Relevance in Facial Skin Health

C. acnes exhibits remarkable genetic heterogeneity, comprising multiple phylogenetic lineages identified through genomic and molecular analyses. Six major phylotypes, namely IA1, IA2, IB, IC, II, and III, display distinct genomic and phenotypic traits [32, 39, 109]. Community-level diversity can be further resolved using SLST typing, which classifies strains into ten clades (A–L) [86]. Clades A–E correspond to IA1, whereas F, G, H, K, and L represent IA2, IC, IB, II, and III, respectively. The composition of C. acnes populations is shaped by host characteristics, anatomical site, and skin condition [110]. Ecological niches determine transcriptional and metabolic profiles, thereby influencing functional roles of certain C. acnes lineages [111].

Loss of C. acnes diversity has been implicated in triggering innate immune responses. For example, a three-strain consortium (IA1, II, III) elicited weaker innate immune response in healthy skin explants compared with individual strains [112]. Differential enrichment of phylotypes on healthy versus acneic skin has been consistently reported [96, 113] and validated by amplicon-based sequencing [114] and shotgun metagenomics [115].

Among phylotypes, IA1 and IA2 are strongly associated with acne pathogenesis, exhibiting increased abundance in lesions relative to healthy skin [116, 117]. IA1 strains demonstrate high virulence potential, including enhanced biofilm formation, lipase activity, and induction of proinflammatory cytokines [39, 118]. Conversely, IB strains are predominantly isolated from healthy skin, show minimal association with inflammatory disorders and may contribute to microbial homeostasis [20, 119]. Phylotypes II and III are generally considered commensal or low-pathogenicity members of the microbiome. Type II strains are frequently recovered from deeper tissues, soft-tissue infections, and implant-associated biofilms, suggesting niche specialization beyond superficial inflammation [20, 120]. Type III strains are consistently linked to noninflammatory skin issues such as progressive macular hypomelanosis rather than acne vulgaris [121, 122].

Advanced Targeted Therapeutic Options for Acne-Prone Skin

Traditional treatments for acne and other skin conditions often indirectly or directly influence the skin microbiome. Antibiotics, benzoyl peroxide (BPO), isotretinoin, and topical retinoids are widely used; however, their mechanisms and impacts differ.

Antibiotics (e.g., tetracyclines) are effective antimicrobial agents that reduce C. acnes and other bacteria involved in acne pathogenesis. Their advantage lies in rapid inflammation reduction, but they also disrupt the broader microbial community, leading to decreased diversity, antibiotic resistance development, and potential dysbiosis [114, 123, 124]. Long-term use can impair beneficial commensals and promote resistant strains [54].

BPO exhibits bactericidal activity by generating reactive oxygen species, effectively reducing C. acnes colonization without promoting antimicrobial resistance [125]. Despite well-documented contributions to the reduction of acne symptoms, BPO treatment has potential drawbacks. BPO may compromise epidermal barrier integrity and diminish microbial diversity [126]. Furthermore, BPO can cause several adverse events, the most common being skin irritation, xerosis, dermatitis, erythema, application-site pain, and pruritus [127].

Isotretinoin (oral or topical) primarily reduces sebum production and affects C. acnes density. Its effects on the microbiome are complex; while it reduces bacterial load, it also significantly alters the skin environment, impacting microbial diversity and community structure [123]. Despite its unparalleled efficacy, documented adverse effects range from musculoskeletal complications to neuropsychiatric events such as depression [128]. However, current evidence suggests that their incidence and clinical relevance remain subject to ongoing debate and may depend on dosage and patient characteristics [129–131].

Topical retinoids (e.g., tretinoin) enhance keratinocyte turnover and promote clearance of comedones. By improving barrier integrity and reducing sebum, they indirectly influence the skin microbiome. However, topical retinoids frequently induce irritation and xerosis, which may destabilize microbial communities, reduce diversity and favor opportunistic infections [132, 133].

In contrast to the above-mentioned treatments, targeted approaches, such as strain-specific antimicrobials, bacteriophage therapy, or probiotic applications, rely on accurate identification of the virulent phylotypes responsible for disease [134].

Probiotic therapies, using beneficial microbes to restore or enhance the skin’s native microbiota, are emerging as a promising alternative to classical pharmaceutical treatments [135]. Topical or oral probiotics aim to reintroduce or stimulate beneficial strains, which can outcompete pathogenic bacteria and modulate immune responses [136–138]. This approach offers advantages such as minimal risk of resistance development, preservation of microbial diversity, and enhancement of skin barrier function.

While still in early stages, clinical studies suggest that probiotic interventions can reduce inflammation, C. acnes colonization, and acne severity, making bacteriotherapy a compelling, microbiome-friendly alternative to antibiotics and retinoids [135, 137, 138]. Emerging research indicates that topical applications of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus species can modulate immune responses, suppress pathogenic bacteria, and improve clinical outcomes in patients with acne [137–140]. In addition to topical approaches, randomized controlled trials have also investigated oral probiotic supplementation in acne management. However, findings across trials remain heterogeneous, and recent meta-analyses of randomized controlled studies indicate only modest effects with limited certainty of evidence [141].

Skin microbiome modulation involves applying probiotic solutions containing living bacteria to the skin to alter its microbial composition, although the applicable regulatory framework varies across regions and product categories, and in some settings such approaches may be positioned as cosmetic interventions [142]. This approach can temporarily modulate the skin microbiome without adverse effects, offering potential therapeutic or skin care strategies for problem-prone skin or skin diseases associated with microbiome dysbiosis, such as acne, atopic dermatitis, or psoriasis [142, 143]. The success of engraftment depends on several factors, including bacterial concentration, strain combinations, and the recipient’s existing microbiome profile (dermatotype) [137, 138]. These factors influence the stability and persistence of introduced beneficial microbes, which is crucial for effective microbiome modulation. However, the efficacy in treating acne vulgaris remains uncertain, with questions about the ability to colonize the skin, displace pathogenic microbes, and produce consistent clinical benefits [137–139, 144].

The “skin’s own” approach emphasizes leveraging and restoring the native microbiota, particularly beneficial strains including S. epidermidis and nonpathogenic C. acnes lineages, to reinforce natural defenses, reduce inflammation, and promote microbial diversity [97, 145]. This strategy aims to optimize the skin’s inherent microbial ecosystem minimizing risks of resistance or unintended dysbiosis, and aligning with a personalized, microbiome-friendly paradigm for acne management.

Limitations

This narrative review has several limitations. First, as a narrative rather than systematic review, the article may be subject to selection bias and does not provide a comprehensive assessment of all available evidence. Second, interpretation of the current literature is limited by substantial heterogeneity in study populations, sampling strategies, sequencing approaches, and data analysis methods, which reduces direct comparability across studies. Finally, evidence for emerging microbiome-based interventions remains preliminary, and their clinical translation is further complicated by region-specific regulatory differences.

Conclusions

The facial skin microbiome is a complex and dynamic ecosystem that plays a vital role in maintaining skin health, modulating immune responses, and influencing the development of dermatological conditions. Dominated by the bacterial species C. acnes and S. epidermidis, its composition is shaped by factors including age, skin region, and health status. C. acnes is an anaerobic, lipophilic bacterium that is an important factor in the development of acne in the skin, while S. epidermidis is an aerobic bacterium that is normally found on the skin and plays a role in immune regulation and colonization resistance. However, both bacteria can also exhibit pathogenic properties when they occur in certain environments or in certain individuals and can then contribute to various skin conditions such as acne, atopic dermatitis, or other dermatological diseases.

A nuanced understanding of C. acnes phylotypes, distinguishing pathogenic strains from benign ones, is crucial for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. Despite recent advances, methodological challenges in sampling and analysis persist, as traditional cultivation underestimates diversity, and molecular techniques face issues related to biases and depth. Amplicon sequencing is suitable for broad surveys, while metagenomics, SLST, and MLST provide detailed strain and functional insights.

Emerging therapies focused on harnessing the skin’s innate microbiota hold promise for restoring microbial balance in a way that is more natural, sustainable, and skin-friendly than pharmaceutical approaches. Continued research toward understanding microbial functions, interactions, and strain-specific roles will be pivotal in designing microbiome-based interventions aimed at preventing dysbiosis and promoting skin health.
