Redefinition of Stemonosudis intermedia (Ege, 1933) and description of a new species from the western Atlantic Ocean (Aulopiformes: Paralepididae)
Authors: Hsuan‐Ching Ho, Katherine E Bemis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe examined specimens of slender barracudina in the genus Stemonosudis collected from the western Atlantic Ocean. Investigation revealed that seven specimens represent the first adult records of Stemonosudis intermedia (Ege, 1933), previously known only from juveniles. We redescribed S. intermedia based on the holotype and newly recognized adult and juvenile specimens, and using this ontogenetic series, we document changes in coloration from light‐brown juveniles to black adults. We also determined that some specimens from the western Atlantic Ocean previously identified as S. intermedia represent a new species, which we describe herein based on three adults and five juvenile specimens. The new species differs from congeners by having 43–44 anal‐fin rays, 117–121 total vertebrae and a pale mouth, gill chamber and gill arches, among a combination of other characters. We also provide information on the lectotype of S. similis (Ege, 1957), which remains known only from the type series.Keywords: biodiversity, biogeography, ichthyology, nomenclature, taxonomy

INTRODUCTION

The slender barracudina genus Stemonosudis was established by Harry (1951) based on Macroparalepis intermedius Ege, 1933, for six species in the Atlantic, Pacific and Indian oceans previously recognized as ‘Group II’ by Ege (1933:229–230). Harry (1951, 1953) diagnosed the genus Stemonosudis as having a slightly compressed and very elongate body, a moderately developed ventral carina (adipose fin), head and snout long and pointed, nostrils located behind posterior tip of maxilla, tip of lower jaw not elevated or only slightly so, upper jaw (maxilla) terminating approximately one eye diameter before the anterior margin of eye and anal‐fin rays 37–50.

Later, Rofen (1966, née Harry) included 10 species in his key to Stemonosudis. Soon after, Richards (1967) described S. rothschildi from the central Pacific Ocean and Post (1970) described S. siliquiventer from the central Atlantic Ocean. After nearly five decades, Ho, Halasan and Tsai (2019) provided data and descriptions for four Stemonosudis species and Dolichosudis fuliginosa Post, 1969 collected from Taiwan. Ho, Russell, et al. (2019) reviewed the S. rothschildi species complex and described two new species, S. multifasciata and S. retrodorsalis. Ho and Yang (2025) described a new species from Somalia and provided additional records of S. siliquiventer from the Caribbean Sea. Thus, there are currently 15 valid species in the genus Stemonosudis (Table 1; Ho & Yang 2025); however, characteristics provided by Harry (1951, 1953) for Stemonosudis are not diagnostic for all current members and more study is needed.

Nominal species of slender barracudina in the genus Stemonosudis.

Many species of Stemonosudis were originally only known from larvae or juveniles and little is known about their adult morphology. Recently, Ho and Huang (2022a) described the adults of Stemonosudis elongata (Ege, 1933) for the first time and found that unlike the light‐coloured juveniles, the adults have a uniformly black body.

Ege (1957) analysed meristic values and divided Macroparalepis intermedius (= Stemonosudis intermedia) into two populations, one from the open North Atlantic Ocean with 117–121 total vertebrae (high vertebrae population) and one from the Caribbean Sea (= West‐Indian waters in Ege, 1957) with 111–113 total vertebrae (low vertebrae population). Rofen (1966: 431) suggested that these forms may represent two different species. Stemonosudis intermedia is only known from juveniles, with the largest known individual 152.5 mm standard length (SL) (Rofen, 1966: 424; CAS‐SU 56022), which has a pale body with blotches on dorsal and ventral margins. The adult coloration and morphology of S. intermedia remain unknown.

During a survey organized by the La Organización del Sector Pesquero y Acuícola del Istmo Centroamericano in 2011, seven dark‐brown to black, slender specimens of Stemonosudis were collected from the Caribbean Sea by the Spanish research vessel B/O Miguel Oliver. We also found two adult specimens of Stemonosudis with a similar dark body and additional juvenile specimens at Mueum of Comparative Zoology, Harvard University, Zoologisches Museum Hamburg, and California Academy of Sciences. Despite similar appearances, these specimens can be divided into two species based on morphology and genetic data. These two species correspond to the two populations of S. intermedia suggested by Ege (1957) and Rofen (1966). Investigation of the holotype revealed that the low vertebrae population (sensu Ege, 1957: 19–20, 72–73) should be retained for S. intermedia, whereas the high vertebrae population represents an undescribed species.

Here, we redefine Stemonosudis intermedia and describe a new species based on the adults collected on the B/O Miguel Oliver survey and additional museum specimens. We also provide information on the holotype of S. similis (Ege, 1957).

METHOD AND MATERIALS

Materials

Specimens examined are deposited at the National Museum of Natural History, Smithsonian Institution (USNM), Museum of Comparative Zoology, Harvard University (MCZ), Zoologisches Museum Hamburg (ZMH), Zoologisk Museum, Københavns Universitet (ZMUC), and California Academy of Sciences (CAS).

Measurements and counts

Methods for measurements and counts generally followed Ho, Halasan and Tsai (2019) and Ho and Lin (2023) with all measured point to point and defined as follows. Standard length (SL): tip of upper jaw to posterior margin of hypural plate. Head length (HL): tip of upper jaw to posterior margin of gill cover. Body depth: vertical depth measured at base of the pectoral fin. Pre‐dorsal, pre‐pelvic and pre‐anal lengths: tip of upper jaw to origin of respective fin. Distance between the origins of pelvic and dorsal fins (V–D) and origins of pelvic and anal fins (V–A) were calculated from pre‐pelvic, pre‐dorsal, pre‐anus and pre‐anal lengths, or measured horizontally point to point. Pre‐anus length: tip of snout to genital papilla (posterior end of anus); pre‐anus length is equal to ‘pre‐anal length’ in Ege (1930, 1953, 1957). Eye diameter: greatest horizontal distance between bony margins of eye. Interoribital width: narrowest bony width. Snout length: tip of upper jaw to anterior bony margin of orbital. Head depth: vertical depth measured through middle of eye. Head width: broadest width of head, usually at gill cover. Pre‐nostril: distance from tip of snout to middle of two nostrils. Upper jaw length: tip of the upper jaw to posterior end of maxilla. Lower jaw length: tip of the lower jaw to articulation of articular bone. Dorsal‐fin base: length of fin base. Anal‐fin base: length of fin base. Caudal‐peduncle length: distance between posterior end of anal‐fin base to middle of caudal‐fin base. Caudal‐peduncle depth: narrowest height of caudal peduncle. Pectoral‐fin length: upper fin base to end of rays. Pelvic‐fin length: outer fin base to end of rays. Caudal‐fin length: middle of fin base to end of fin rays.

Abbreviations

DFO = dorsal‐fin origin; VFO = pelvic‐fin origin; AFO = anal‐fin origin. V–D, the distance between VFO and DFO; V–A, the distance between VFO and AFO. PVLL, PDLL and PALL are the numbers of lateral‐line scales before VFO, DFO and AFO, respectively. TLL is the total lateral‐line scales, including small scales on posterior portion of lateral line. GRI, GRII and GRIII are the number of gill rakers on the epibranchial, ceratobranchial and hypobranchial, respectively. PVV, PDV and PAV are the numbers of vertebrae before VFO, DFO and AFO, respectively; PHV, CV and TV are numbers of pre‐haemal, caudal and total vertebrae, respectively.

Specimen imaging and figure preparations

Colour photographs were taken of fresh specimens in the field, of frozen and thawed specimens before fixation and of others after preservation using a Nikon D850 and AF‐S Micro NIKKOR 60 mm f/2.8G ED lens. Vertebral counts were made from radiographs taken by a DURASCAN 1417 digital x‐ray system at USNM. We prepared figures in Adobe Photoshop and Adobe Illustrator 2024.

Genetics

DNA sequencing followed methods in Robertson et al. (2017). Newly generated data for cytochrome oxidase subunit 1 (COI) barcode region for USNM specimens were compared with publicly available sequence data in the Barcode of Life Database (BOLD) and GenBank. We include in our phylogenetic analysis other USNM paralepidids we examined to confirm identifications and MCZ 164712, which we also examined and identified as the new species of Stemonosudis. Other data available in public repositories was not included because we did not examine the specimens to confirm identifications. Sequences were aligned in Geneious Prime MAFFT v7.490 (Katoh & Standley, 2013) and trimmed to 655 bp.

Phylogenetic analyses were run in IQ‐TREE version 2.3.6 for MacOS and data partitioned using MFP (Chernomor et al., 2016; Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017; Minh et al., 2020). We recovered an optimal partitioning scheme of three models for each of the three codon COI positions based on BIC: 1, TIMe + I + G4; 2, TN + F + G4; and 3, K3Pu + F + I. We performed 10 tree searches in IQ‐TREE using the optimal partitioning scheme and used 1000 bootstraps for node support values. Analyses were rooted on Lestidium atlanticum. Newly generated COI data is deposited on GenBank under accession numbers PX412669–PX412676, PX412678 and PX412679.

Ethics statement

All specimens examined were preserved specimens in ichthyology collections.

A relatively slender body, long snout and jaws; posterior nostril slightly before, above to slightly behind posterior end of maxilla (except for Stemonosudis molesta, in which the nostril is well before); pelvic fin and anus slightly to well before dorsal fin; ventral adipose fin developed on abdominal ridge and well developed on margin between anus and anal fin; anus above to well behind tip of appressed pelvic fin; and relatively high vertebral count (up to 121 total vertebrae).

Description of holotype

Body extremely slender; eye small; snout very long, about 5 times eye diameter. Posterior end of maxilla about 1 eye diameter before anterior margin of eye. Nostrils situated slightly behind a vertical through end of maxilla, anterior margin of anterior nostril about same vertical of posterior end of maxilla. Posterior nostril about 0.7 eye diameter before the eye. Gill rakers poorly developed, each with 1 short tooth. Ventral adipose fin well developed between anus and anal fin, narrowly developed on abdominal ridge between pectoral and pelvic fins.

DFO clearly behind VFO and anus, before midpoint of V–A. Anus behind posterior tip of pelvic fin and well before DFO. Number of peritoneal sections not available because colours faded from long‐term preservation. Six tooth pairs at front of palatine, followed by a single row of small teeth. Lateral line incomplete, ending above posterior third of anal‐fin base. No light organs in front of eye or below eye; no luminescent duct tract in abdominal cavity.

Other descriptions provided in Ege (1933, 1957), Harry (1951) and Rofen (1966). The original illustration of holotype is shorter in length and deeper bodied, compared to the specimen itself (Figure 1a, vs. b,c).

Identification of Stemonosudis intermedia

Ege (1957) provided morphological data on the holotype (p. 18–19) and analysis of meristic data (tables 7–9) based on additional specimens and suggested that there are two populations differentiated by the number of vertebrae. However, Ege (1957) did not specify the vertebral counts of the holotype. Because there were three specimens collected from the same station (Dana sta. 1183) and their data combined in his analysis, we are not able to determine which value belongs to the holotype from his tables.

Photographs and radiographs taken by ZMUC (P. R. Møller, pers. comm.) show that the holotype has 110 total vertebrae (vs. 111–121 in Ege, 1957). Thus, based on vertebral counts and other meristic values (Table 2), it belongs to the low vertebrae population of Ege (1957). Our examination of new specimens show that one of the two adult black forms agrees with the vertebral formula of the low vertebrae population that we recognize as the true S. intermedia. Rofen (1966) provided data for three specimens collected from Bermuda and Gulf of Mexico, two of which have 114 (CAS‐SU 48463, 118.5 mm SL) and 111 (CAS‐SU 56022, 152.5 mm SL) total vertebrae. We also recognize these specimens as true S. intermedia. The other specimens with higher vertebral counts are treated as a new species and described below.

Diagnosis

A species of Stemonosudis that can be distinguished by the following combination of characters: body, mouth, gill chamber and gill arches black in adults; body pale with blotches on dorsal and ventral margins behind dorsal fin in juveniles; nostrils usually above posterior tip of maxilla; and anus at level of tip of appressed pelvic fin. Anal‐fin rays 41–44. Lateral‐line scales: PVLL 42–46; PDLL 53–57; PALL 66–70; TLL c. 95–109. Vertebrae: PVV 42–45; PHV 47–50; PDV 53–55; PAV 66–68; CV 62–65; TV 110–115 (mean 112.1).

Description

The following meristic data are provided for the holotype, followed by those of non‐types in parentheses. The proportional values are separated into adults and followed by juveniles in parentheses to show differences.

Dorsal‐fin rays 11 (10); pectoral‐fin rays 11 (11–13, mainly 13); pelvic‐fin rays 9 (9); anal‐fin rays 44 (41–44); lateral‐line scales: PVLL 46 (42–44); PDLL 57 (53–55); PALL 70 (66–68); TLL c. 95 (c. 96–109), 86 (83–88) large scales and c. 9 (c. 11–21) small ones on posterior portion. Vertebrae: PVV 44 (42–45), PHV 48 (47–50), PDV 53 (53–55), PAV 66 (67–68), CV 62 (64–65), TV 110 (111–115, mean 112.1). Gill rakers (adults only): 61–72 in total, 15–18 on upper limb (epibranchial) and 46–54 on lower limb, including 26–27 on ceratobranchial and 20–28 on hypobranchial.

Body relatively long, strongly laterally compressed, depth at pectoral fin 19–21 (28–30 in juveniles) times in SL. Caudal peduncle moderately long, 2.1–2.4 (2.7–3.0) times eye diameter. Ventral adipose fin weakly developed along posterior half of abdominal ridge before pelvic‐fin origin, well developed on margin between anus and anal‐fin origin (both better developed in juveniles). Anus close to tip of appressed pelvic fin, at about midpoint of V–D or slightly behind, and well before midpoint of V–A.

Head long, its length 5.0–5.2 (6.1–6.4) in SL; snout slender, its length 9.0–9.7 (9.8–11.2) in SL or 1.8 (1.6–1.8) in HL. Mouth terminal, relatively large, with gape extending to about 1 eye diameter before eye; posterior portion of maxilla about 0.8 times eye diameter before eye; posterior portion of upper jaw with a skin fold ending at about a vertical through anterior margin of eye; lower jaw slightly upturned at tip, with small distal tab of fleshy tissue. Eye small, its diameter 6.9–7.9 (8.3–9.0) in HL. No light organs in front of eye or below eye. First suborbital bone slender, the fifth and sixth expanded posteriorly, and the seventh small. Interorbital space narrow, its width 12.1–13.0 (12.2–13.2) in HL; some straight bony ridges present on top of head and snout. Two nostrils located slightly before a vertical through posterior end of maxilla, about 0.9 times eye diameter in front of eye (Figure 2b). Numerous sensory canals on snout, check, operculum and jaws (Figure 2b); numerous sensory pores on dorsal surface of snout and ventral surface of lower jaw.

Gill filaments present on all four gill arches. Fourth arch mostly connected to gill chamber wall by membrane. Pseudobranchs present within deep pocket.

DFO well behind mid‐length of fish and well behind VFO, pre‐dorsal length 1.6 (1.6–1.7) in SL. Pectoral fin long, with dorsal rays distinctly longer, about twice length of inner rays (Figure 2b; often broken in preserved specimens); pectoral base behind posterior margin of gill cover, upper end of fin base at about same horizontal drawn through lower margin of eye; a small pocket behind fin base. VFO well before DFO, pre‐pelvic length 1.8–1.9 (1.9–2.0) in SL. A slender axial scale behind each pelvic‐fin base. V–A relatively long, 19.3–21.3 (18.2–21.4)% SL; V–D 41.4–49.5 (44.1–46.3)% V–A. Anus around tip of appressed pelvic fin, distance from VFO to anus 23.0–26.7 (22.0–29.5)% V–A. Adipose fin above posterior portion of anal‐fin base. Anal fin originating on posterior fourth of body, pre‐anal length 1.3–1.4 (1.3–1.5) in SL.

Two or three small fangs at tip of upper jaw, followed by single row of numerous (ca. 80–100) small, retrorse teeth, those on anterior portion of maxilla slightly larger, gradually becoming smaller posteriorly. Vomerine teeth absent. Two or three depressible fangs at front of each lower jaw, followed by a gap and then two rows of fangs arranged in 7–11 pairs; those of inner row long with knife‐like tip and depressible; those in outer row much shorter, curved back and fixed, slightly covered by tissue. Two rows of fangs on anterior portion of palatine, forming 5–7 widely‐spaced tooth pairs, those in outer row long and depressible, those in inner row small and fixed; these fangs followed by single row of small, widely‐spaced fixed teeth posteriorly. One row of 8–12 small, straight teeth present on each side of basihyal (holotype not examined but see description in Ege, 1957: 19).

Gill rakers present on all gill arches, small, shield‐shaped and close to each other, each with 2 or 3 (rarely 1 or 4) small teeth and narrow base; teeth only slightly above the tissue covering the rakers. Teeth on pharyngeal arch relatively long, forming oval patch, with about 4 rows in middle. Single row of ca. 10 stout teeth on each fifth ceratobranchial, together forming a V‐shaped pattern.

Body scaleless except a single row of lateral‐line scales originating above pectoral girdle and running about two‐thirds the length of anal‐fin base. Lateral‐line scales slightly longer than wide, gradually becoming smaller and narrower posteriorly; four variously sized pores on upper and lower anterior margin of each scale, anterior pores largest. An additional small pore between two scales at the scale midline.

Luminescent duct absent.

Colouration

When fresh, body of adults uniformly black with gill cover, lower portion of abdominal cavity and posterior portion of caudal region slightly metallic silver (Figure 2). When preserved, body of adult uniformly dark brown with ventral margin darker (Figure 3a); mouth and gill chamber black; anterior portion of basihyal pale; gill rakers black, gill filaments pale with black base; fin rays and ventral adipose fin densely covered with melanophores or entirely black; peritoneal membrane uniformly black. Bodies of juveniles sparsely and unevenly covered by melanophores, with black blotches on dorsal and ventral margin of dorsal‐fin base and behind (Figure 3b).

Size

USNM 407825 (333 mm SL) is a female with large ovaries. USNM 407545 (302 mm SL) is a female with small ovaries and small eggs inside. USNM 407444 (303 mm SL) is a male with tube‐like testis that is likely mature. With the size gap among specimens, we are not able to present complete reproductive data for this species, such as size at maturity.

Distribution

Known from the Caribbean Sea and northwestern Atlantic Ocean; one specimen from off Gabon in eastern Atlantic (Figure 6) and another one from South Atlantic Ocean (not shown). Juveniles were reported widespread in the Caribbean Sea (= West Indies in Ege, 1957), whereas adults are only found in several localities (Figure 6). Two juveniles were collected from the surface (less than 100 m), whereas the adults were collected from 724 to 1140 m depth (see materials examined). Although the holotype was collected at ca. 1250 m depth (2500 m of wire out), it is not likely the juveniles occur this deep and we suspect the specimen was collected at a shallower depth during descent or ascent of the net.

Growth changes

Several body proportions differ between juveniles and adults, reflected by the percentages of either SL or HL (Tables 2 and 3). Compared to those of the adults, juveniles have smaller head length, body depth, snout length, eye diameter, interorbital width, head depth, head width, pre‐nostril length and jaw length. In addition, the ratio of snout length/eye diameter of juveniles is 4.8–5.5, larger than 3.9–4.4 in adults, and the ratio of caudal‐peduncle length/eye diameter is 2.5–3.0, also larger than 1.7–2.4 in adults. Juveniles also have slightly fewer lateral‐line scales (c. 95–97), compared to 101–109 in adults. The holotype and one juvenile have the nostrils slightly behind the posterior end of maxilla, one juvenile has nostrils at about the vertical of the posterior end of maxilla, and adults have nostrils slightly before the vertical of posterior end of maxilla. Thus, the position of nostrils may change slightly with growth. More specimens may show clearer trends in changes in body proportions and location of nostrils.

Diet

The holotype had a half‐digested paralepidid fish in its stomach. The vertebrae of the stomach content are complete, with PVV 34, PDV 35, PAV 52 and TV 82 counted from the radiograph and it is most likely Lestidium atlanticum. This suggests that S. intermedia feed on other paralepidids, a common habit of the family based on our observations.

Remarks

Six adult specimens from the Caribbean Sea and Bahamas represent the first record of adults. All adults have a black body, mouth, gill chamber and gill arches. Previous reports based on juveniles described a pale body with blotches on dorsal and ventral margins behind dorsal fin. There is still a large gap between the sizes available (<220 and >277 mm SL) and thus at what stage the coloration changes remains unknown.

The common name Multibone Pikesmelt is listed in Page et al. (2023). Here, we recommend a new common name, Blackmouth Slender Barracudina, based on a diagnostic feature that differentiates S. intermedia from the new species of Stemonosudis.

Size

MCZ 164712 (122.5 mm SL) is an immature juvenile. The largest specimen is USNM 407509 (348 mm SL), a female with small ovaries and small eggs, probably in the early stage of a reproductive cycle. USNM 407682 (324 mm SL) has ovaries with large loose eggs and appears to be mature. The holotype, USNM 407498 (332 mm SL) is a female with large ovaries and loose eggs inside, likely in the last stage of a reproductive cycle.

Growth changes

A number of body proportions differ between juveniles and adults, either reflected by the percentages of SL or HL. Juveniles have smaller proportions of head length, head depth, head width, body depth, snout length, eye diameter, interorbital width, pre‐nostril length and jaw lengths, compared to those of adults. The pre‐dorsal, pre‐pelvic, pre‐anus and pre‐anal lengths are also relatively small in juveniles, compared to adults. In contrast, the V–D and V–A are relatively large in juveniles, compared to those of adults. The ratio of snout length/eye diameter in juveniles is 4.5–4.9, larger than 4.2–4.4 in adults and the ratio of caudal‐peduncle length/eye diameter is 2.5–2.9 in juveniles, also slightly larger than 2.3–2.7 in adults. Juveniles have relatively few lateral‐line scales (c. 94–97), compared to 101–111 in adults. More specimens are needed to provide a clearer understanding of these trends.

Diet

USNM 407682 had a large Lestidium atlanticum in its stomach. The two other adult paratypes each had a half‐digested paralepidid in their stomachs, with ca. 77 and 81 total vertebrae, likely Lestidiops sp. or Lestidium sp. These data suggest that this species also consumes their own kind regularly.

Distribution

Stemonosudis dianea sp. nov. occurs in the open western North Atlantic Ocean from ~40°N south to the Caribbean Sea off Panama (Figure 6). The three adult specimens were collected from the Caribbean Sea at depth 676–1125 m, whereas juveniles were collected from further north, including off Cuba, Bahamas, and Massachusetts, USA at depths 0–900 m (probably near the surface) (Figure 6).

Remarks

Stemonosudis dianea sp. nov. was previously recognized as S. intermedia based on juveniles and represents the high vertebrae population of Ege (1957). We were not able to examine the specimens examined by Ege (1957); however, we examined juveniles in museum collections (listed above).

A large juvenile (192 mm SL; USNM 240407) has gill rakers developed on the ceratobranchial, but not on epi‐ or hypo‐ branchial. Two other juvenile specimens (USNM 477432 and USNM 477433) have gill rakers weakly developed. The three large type specimens (USNM 407498, USNM 407682 and USNM 407509) have gill rakers present on the epi‐, cerato‐ and hypo‐ branchials, but the rakers are somewhat rudimentary compared to congeners. This species may show a trend in gill raker reduction.

Stemonosudis sp.

Three examined specimens from the South Atlantic Ocean are most similar to S. dianea and S. similis in having the anus well behind the pelvic fin, but different in several ways. We recognized them as unidentified Stemonosudis until more specimens from the South Atlantic Ocean or Pacific Ocean are available.

Materials examined: ZMH 110467, formerly ISH 1896/71 (1, 193 mm SL), FRV Walther Herwig, sta. 455‐III/71, 13°07′S, 9°02′W, northwest of St. Helena, 990–1010 m, 6 April 1971; ZMH 110470, formerly ISH 1177/74 (1, 105.9 mm SL), FFS Walther Herwig, sta. 471‐II/71, 2°29'S, 18°58'W, 304–400 m, 10 April 1971; ZMH 113056, ISH 2169/71 (1, 105.5 mm SL), FFS Walther Herwig, sta. 61‐I/74, 2°27.5′S, 34°53.5′W, 145 m, 24 July 1974.

DISCUSSION

We show that S. intermedia and S. dianea sp. nov. have black bodies as adults. Adults of at least several species of the genera Dolichosudis, Lestidiops and Macroparalepis (Post, 1973; Ho, Halasan, & Tsai, 2019; Ho & Huang, 2022a, 2022b; Ho & Lin, 2023; this study) also have black bodies. From the other black bodied paralepidid species, species of Stemonosudis can be differentiated by having nostrils near the posterior end of maxilla and presence of a ventral adipose fin. Dolichosudis fuliginosa differs from S. intermedia, S. dianea sp. nov., S. elongata and S. similis in having fewer anal‐fin rays and total vertebrae (Table 4), but further investigation is needed to establish if it is a member of Stemonosudis (Ho, pers. obser.).

Stemonosudis intermedia and S. dianea sp. nov. have 41–44 anal‐fin rays and 110–121 total vertebrae, which differentiates them from all congeners except for two species, S. elongata and S. similis (Rofen, 1966: 423, key item 3b). Table 4 compares the meristic data for the five species presumed to have black adults (confirmed for all but S. similis) based on our data as well as data from the literature. After separating S. dianea sp. nov. from S. intermedia, the diagnosis of S. intermedia is revised as above.

Stemonosudis dianea sp. nov. differs from S. intermedia in many ways, including adults with a pale mouth, gill chamber and gill arches (vs. uniformly black); a smaller head 16.5–17.1% SL (vs. 19.1–20.2% SL) in adults; longer pre‐anus length 63.1–64.8% SL (vs. 58.4–60.2% SL) in adults, reflected by anus well behind tip of appressed pelvic fin (vs. close to tip of pelvic fin); more lateral‐line scales: 50–53 PVLL (vs. 42–46), 60–64 PDLL (vs. 53–57), 75–77 PALL (vs. 66–70), 89–93 large lateral‐line scales (vs. 83–88); more vertebrae: 48–52 PVV (vs. 42–45), 56–59 PHV (vs. 47–50), 58–61 PDV (vs. 53–55), 73–75 PAV (66–68), 117–121 TV (vs. 110–115); and fewer total gill rakers (46, vs. 61–72), which are strongly reduced in adults of S. dianea sp. nov., whereas these are well‐developed in adults of S. intermedia.

Stemonosudis dianea sp. nov. is similar to S. elongata in having a slender, uniformly black body in adults and number of total vertebrae. However, S. dianea can be separated by having fewer 43–44 anal‐fin rays (vs. 49–50 in S. elongata) and a shorter anal fin base 18.1–19.4% SL (vs. 22.7–25.7% SL); fewer gill rakers (46; vs. 66–71) in adults; more lateral‐line scales: 50–53 PVLL (vs. 42–45), 60–64 PDLL (vs. 52–57) and 75–77 PALL (vs. 64–68); more vertebrae: 48–52 PVV (vs. 44–45), 56–59 PHV (vs. 48–49), 58–61 PDV (vs. 55–57), 73–75 PAV (67–69); and fewer CV 61–63 (vs. 66–73).

Stemonosudis dianea sp. nov. can be separated from S. similis in having more vertebrae: 48–52 PVV (vs. ca. 42), 58–61 PDV (vs. 47), 73–75 PAV (vs. 58) and 117–121 TV (vs. 106). Adults of S. similis are not currently available for further investigations, but it is likely the lateral‐line scale formula also differs. Among the four species treated above, the anus is well behind the tip of appressed pelvic fin (close to DFO) in S. dianea sp. nov. and S. similis, whereas the anus is right above or slightly behind the fin tip (well before DFO) in S. intermedia and S. elongata.

Ho & Huang (2022) showed that S. elongata has ontogenetic changes in body proportions, for example juveniles have a smaller head length, body depth, eye diameter, interorbital width, head depth, jaw lengths, snout length and caudal‐peduncle length. These trends are also present in S. intermedia and S. dianea sp. nov.

Genetically, S. dianea sp. nov. is similar to S. intermedia, with only 5–12 bp of the COI barcode region or 0.79–1.73% different (Figure 7). However, S. dianea is well differentiated morphologically as described above. Only four of the 15 species currently recognized as Stemonosudis have corresponding genetic data available. New genetic data from additional genes and additional species of Stemonosudis will be important for further understanding the taxonomic diversity and phylogenetic relationships among congeners.
