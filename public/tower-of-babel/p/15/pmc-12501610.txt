Regression-based proximal causal inference
Authors: Jiewen Liu, Chan Park, Kendrick Li, Eric J Tchetgen Tchetgen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNegative controls are increasingly used to evaluate the presence of potential unmeasured confounding in observational studies. Beyond the use of negative controls to detect the presence of residual confounding, proximal causal inference (PCI) was recently proposed to de-bias confounded causal effect estimates by leveraging a pair of treatment and outcome negative control or confounding proxy variables. Formal methods for statistical inference have been developed for PCI; however, these methods can be challenging to implement because they involve solving complex integral equations that are typically ill-posed. We develop a regression-based PCI approach, employing 2-stage generalized linear regression models (GLMs) to implement PCI, which obviates the need to solve difficult integral equations. The proposed approach has merit in that (1) it is applicable to continuous, count, and binary outcomes cases, making it relevant to a wide range of real-world applications; and (2) it is easy to implement using off-the-shelf software for GLMs. We establish the statistical properties of regression-based PCI and illustrate their performance in both synthetic and real-world empirical applications. This article is part of a Special Collection on Methods in Social Epidemiology.

Introduction

Identification of causal effects can be challenging in observational studies because the standard exchangeability or treatment unconfoundedness conditions upon which causal inference commonly relies are threatened by the presence of confounding by hidden factors.1 To achieve unconfoundedness in practice typically requires a complete understanding of key causes of the treatment selection mechanism that also predict the outcome in view, and that such common causes of the treatment and outcome variables can be measured with sufficient accuracy that one can fully account for them empirically. However, even in carefully designed observational studies, where all sources of confounding are known, key drivers of confounding rarely, if ever, can be measured without error. As a result, the most one can hope for in practice is that measured confounders are (error-prone) proxies of underlying confounders; thus, residual unmeasured confounding may be present even in the most well-designed observational studies. For instance, in studies of the causal impact of HIV treatment, pretreatment CD4 count measurement is commonly used to control for confounding by indication, because it is invariably used by physicians for treatment initiation decision-making.2,3 However, CD4 count measurements are notoriously error prone and, at most, are imperfect snapshots of the evolving immune system status driving disease progression. As such, in scenarios where CD4 count directly affects treatment, CD4 count measurement is a proxy of the underlying source of confounding, which remains unmeasured.4 Pre-exposure outcome measurements sometimes provide good candidate proxies of confounding. For example, asthma rates at a county level measured the week before fine particulate matter measurements are good confounding proxies for the causal effect of fine particulate matter exposure on the county’s asthma rate in the subsequent week in a typical air pollution time-series study.5

A common type of proxy variable routinely used in epidemiology studies are so-called negative control variables, which typically are used to evaluate the potential presence of unmeasured confounding.6 Specifically, an observed exposure is said to be a negative control exposure (NCE; more broadly a treatment confounding proxy) if it is a priori known not to causally affect the primary outcome of interest and is relevant (ie, associated with an unmeasured confounder). Thus, such an NCE can be used to detect residual confounding by assessing the extent to which it is empirically associated with the outcome, given measured covariates. Likewise, a negative control outcome (NCO; more broadly an outcome confounding proxy) variable must be known a priori not to be causally affected by the primary treatment and must be relevant and, therefore, associated with a hidden confounder. An extensive list of common examples of proxy variables encountered in observational studies is given in reports by Lipsitch et al.,7 Shi et al.,8 and Zafari et al.9 provides a state of use of proxy methods in pharmacoepidemiology. Although such NCE and NCO variables have been used quite widely in epidemiology to detect confounding bias,7,10,11 only recently have formal negative control and proxy theory and methods been developed to debias causal effect estimates.12 Such methods have been referred to as proximal causal inference (PCI).13,14

Specifically, Miao et al15 studied the problem of identification using proxy variables and established that given a pair of treatment and outcome confounding proxy variables (Z and W, respectively) that are sufficiently relevant for hidden confounders, it is possible to nonparametrically identify a causal effect in the presence of unmeasured confounding. Tchetgen Tchetgen et al.13 generalized PCI to complex longitudinal studies and derived the proximal g-formula to identify the joint causal effects of time-varying treatments subject to both measured and hidden time-varying confounding, thus effectively extending the work of Robins16 to the proximal setting. They also propose proximal g-computation, a proxy-based generalization of the g-computation algorithm.

In the continuous outcomes case, under linear models for NCO W and primary outcome Y, Tchetgen Tchetgen et al.13 established that the corresponding proximal g-computation algorithm can be implemented by following a 2-stage least-squares procedure. A key limitation of the proposed 2-stage least-squares approach is that it does not easily extend to binary, polytomous, or count outcomes for which linear models are generally inappropriate.

In this article, we extend proximal g-computation for a point treatment via a 2-stage regression approach that accommodates continuous, count, and binary W and Y via generalized linear models (GLMs) with canonical link function (identity, log, and logit) that appropriately account for the outcome type. The article is organized as follows. First, we briefly review causal directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) encoding key assumptions of the proximal causal learning framework and we review the 2-stage least-squares estimation given in the Tchetgen Tchetgen et al.13 publication for continuous outcome and outcome proxy variables. Next, we consider more general settings, where the primary outcome or the outcome proxy is either a count, binary, or polytomous variable, and we propose appropriate 2-stage, regression-based PCI approaches to identify and estimate the corresponding causal effect. For each combination of link functions, we provide a detailed formulation of the corresponding estimation procedure and formally establish its validity. Detailed proofs and derivations are relegated to the Appendix. We also provide, in Appendix S10, annotated R and SAS codes to execute the 2-stage regression procedure proposed in this article. Additionally, we have developed R package pci2s to conveniently estimate causal effects with consistent SE estimates and corresponding CIs. For illustration, we re-analyze data from the Study to Understand Prognoses and Preferences for Outcomes and Risks of Treatments (SUPPORT),17 an observational study that evaluates the causal effect of right-side heart catheterization (RHC) on survival, length of hospitalization, intensity of care, and costs.

Application: RHC treatment effect

To illustrate the proposed proximal 2-stage regression approach in practice, we revisit the SUPPORT study. The objective is to estimate the treatment effect of RHC performed within the first 24 hours of intensive care unit stay on 30-day survival outcome. The SUPPORT data set comprises information on 5735 patients from a large prospective cohort study conducted at 5 medical centers. Treatment A indicates receiving RHC treatment, and outcome Y is the survival time up to 30 days. The study also collected rich information encoded in 73 covariates, including basic demographics (eg, age, sex, race, education, income, and insurance status), estimated probability of survival, comorbid conditions, vital signs, physiological status, and functional status. Ten covariates measuring a patient’s overall physiological status were measured from a blood sample collected within 24 hours in the intensive care unit and tested: serum sodium, serum potassium, serum creatinine, bilirubin, and albumin levels; the ratio of partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2) to 0.01 times the fraction of inspired oxygen; partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCo2), serum pH (arterial), white blood cell count, and hematocrit. These variables may be subject to substantial measurement error and, as single snapshots of the underlying physiological state over time, can be considered potential confounding proxies.

Discussion

In this article, we have reviewed the proximal g-computation via proximal 2-stage least-squares regression approach of Tchetgen Tchetgen et al.,13 which posits linear structural models for both outcome confounding proxies W and the primary outcome Y. The approach delivers valid causal inferences in the presence of hidden confounders for which proxies are available. Extending the approach, we have established analogous 2-stage regression approaches for structural causal models with either log, and logit links apply to either W or Y. For all possible combinations of canonical link functions for the primary outcome and outcome confounding proxies, we have formulated an appropriate 2-stage regression estimator (detailed in Appendix S1), thus making it possible for epidemiologists to use the proximal causal approach by fitting familiar GLMs for the most common settings encountered in practice.

In this article, although we focus on the implementation of PCI in settings where the use of GLMs provides an adequate fit to the data at hand, PCI has been shown to apply in more general cases13, where nonparametric models may provide a better fit to the observed data. However, as mentioned in the “Introduction,” PCI, in such more general cases, typically requires solving certain linear integral equations that can be ill posed and, as a result, numerically unstable. We refer the interested reader to the works of Miao et al.,15 Tchetgen Tchetgen et al.,13 Kallus et al.,18 Cui et al.,19 Ying et al.,20 and Zhang et al.21 These studies provide in-depth discussion and an extensive treatment of the proximal nonparametric identification problem and elaborate on nonparametric and semiparametric estimation techniques.

In the application considered in the present article, we followed the ad hoc proxy allocation of the prior work by Tchetgen Tchetgen et al.13 In their article, those authors further considered the robustness of such allocation by evaluating the extent to which the estimator remains stable upon permuting the roles of outcome and treatment confounding proxies. The approach is intuitive; however, it lacks formal performance guaranties. Future work might consider more formal proxy selection algorithms with theoretical guaranties; see, for instance, the data-driven automated negative control estimation algorithm for structural linear models, which represents initial progress.22 It is worth noting that throughout this article, we have explicitly assumed no treatment by unmeasured confounder interaction on the GLM scale. Although the proposed proximal 2-stage regression approach does not easily handle such interaction, it is worth noting that more flexible proximal methods that accommodate the presence of interactions with unmeasured confounders are available, although at the cost of computational complexity (see Cui et al.19 and Ghassami et al.23 for additional details). Finally, right-censored survival data occur commonly in epidemiologic practice, and evaluating causal effects for such outcomes using PCI techniques presents special challenges worth considering in future developments.
