Management of non-lesional super-refractory status epilepticus: a rare case report
Authors: Rawan Albalawi, Raseel Aljthalin, Sonia Khan, Raghad Fouly, Mohammed Alshahrani, Hamoud Alsahli, Moustafa Alhashemi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importance:Super-refractory status epilepticus is defined as status epilepticus that continues or recurs 24 h or more after the initiation of anesthetic therapy(continuum), a serious medical emergency with a potential for significant morbidity and mortality. Cortisectomy with invasive EEG recording electrocorticography (ECoG) can be a successful treatment option for super-refractory status epilepticus in selected cases after medical management has failed.Case presentation:The authors present a case of a young lady who suffered a super-refractory status epilepticus and failed five different anti-seizure medications, coma-producing agents, IVIG. After failure of multiple medical and sedative therapy cortisectomy was done with the use of invasive EEG recording electrocorticography (ECoG) to tailor and localize the epileptogenic zone.Clinical discussion:When dealing with status epilepticus (SE) that is refractory to treatment, early surgical intervention should be taken into consideration as a viable option. Super-refractory status epilepticus (SRSE) is defined as status epilepticus (SE) that continues or recurs at 24 h or more after the initiation of anesthetic therapy1. SRSE is a serious medical emergency with a potential for significant morbidity and mortality2. Around 30–40% of SE cases are refractory to first and second-line treatment and need coma-producing therapy to control seizures3.

Immediate resective surgery is considered as a viable treatment option for certain patients4. Some of these patients do not show improvement with coma therapy and are classified as having SRSE. Managing SRSE is very difficult, and there is limited information on effective treatments2. The existing literature consists mainly of small case studies with diverse groups of SRSE patients and different neurosurgical interventions that have shown success in controlling seizures and enhancing patient outcomes1. In specialized facilities, epilepsy surgery may be an option after unsuccessful medical treatment3. Surgical options include focal cortical resections, hemispherectomies, multiple subpial transections, corpus callosotomies, and neuromodulation4. A case study detailing a patient with SRSE who underwent frontal corticectomy and experienced improved seizure control is presented in accordance with the SCARE Criteria5.

Case presentation

Patient information

The patient was a 22-year-old woman who had an intellectual disability since birth and epilepsy. She had normal developmental motor milestones and delayed mental milestones with walking and talking up to the age of three years. She started to walk and talk after the age of 3 years and remained to have an intellectual disability, with a grade of 13 in the mini-mental status examination mainly in the attention, orientation, registration and language part. She was born at full term through spontaneous vaginal delivery and had no pediatric or neonatal intensive care unit admissions.

The patient’s parents are first-degree cousins. She has five siblings, and four of them are intellectually disabled with paraplegia. The family has been screened for genetic disorders and was found to have a mutation of the DDHD2 gene encoding phospholipase hereditary spastic paraplegia type 54. Both parents are carriers of the gene and are asymptomatic. One of the affected brothers has epilepsy that is controlled with two anti-seizure medications. The patient had no other risk factors for epilepsy.

Medical and treatment history

The patient’s first seizure was at the age of 14 years after developing a skin rash and allergy with gastrointestinal symptoms, abdominal pain, and diarrhea. The semiology was focal aware seizures in the form of unilateral left-sided clinic seizures involving the upper and lower limbs, followed by bilateral tonic clinic seizures. She was given levetiracetam (750 mg twice per day) and valproic acid (750 mg twice per day) and showed good response. She was compliant with her anti-seizure medications. The last seizure had been 6 years ago and was provoked by a lack of sleep and stress. All seizures had the same semiology. The patient had remained in remission with these medications under regular follow-up in the epilepsy clinic until the current admission.

She was admitted in 21 July 2022 from the emergency department. First week started medical management. In 29 October she was transferred to the rehab program in Alsaudi. First-line agents were benzodiazepines. Then second-line management in her case was loading with Valproic acid and levetiracetam. And finally third-line agents were coma-producing agents is ketamine and midazolam infusion. Her physical examination was normal. She was maintained on lacosamide at 200 mg twice daily, perampanel at 10 mg at bedtime, levetiracetam at 1.5 g twice daily, phenobarbital at 50 mg twice daily, and clonazepam at 0.5 mg twice daily. This did not control her seizures, so a midazolam infusion at 3 mg/kg was started, followed by intravenous ketamine loaded at 1.5 mg/kg. She was then kept on maintenance infusion at 1 mg/kg and then a 5-day course of intravenous immunoglobulin at 24 g per day. Her labs were generally within normal levels except for three times where she had high WBC, lymphocytes and neutrophils count with high inflammatory markers after deciphering a HAP and UTI.

However, seizures with jerky rhythmic movement persisted in her face and left upper limb and lower limb with secondary generalization. Electroencephalogram (EEG) during SE while on midazolam infusion was done and the findings showed right hemispheric electrographic seizures and epileptic focus in the right frontal region suggesting encephalopathy. Memantine at 10 mg once daily was given, but there was no significant improvement. The patient continued to have seizures even with midazolam infusion at 1.5 mg/g/hour (60 mg/hour). Surgical intervention and treatment

After starting the third-line midazolam infusion and failure of the medical therapy, the patient was discussed in an epilepsy surgery meeting regarding options for surgical intervention. The discussion was attended by two epileptologists, an epilepsy surgeon, a neuroradiologist, and a neuropsychologist. All data were concordant with right frontal origin, and a decision was made to perform a right frontal cortisectomy under intraoperative electrocorticography. The parents were counseled about the surgery and the possible outcomes. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication and any accompanying images. After family agreement, the surgery was performed with general anesthesia and a total intravenous anesthesia protocol.

Histopathology of the resected gray cortex showed a normal appearance of the neuronal population. The white matter also appeared to be normal. There was no histological evidence of neoplastic, dysplastic, or inflammatory processes. The blood vessels were focally and slightly thickened/hyalinized. This vascular change is frequently seen in epilepsy specimens.

Follow-up after surgery

The patient was moved to the intensive care unit for observation. She developed one right focal motor seizure 6 h after the surgery but no further seizures. Follow-up EEG showed diffuse encephalopathy. At 72 h after surgery, the sedation was slowly tapered down under continuous EEG over 7 days until midazolam was stopped completely. Over the following 3 weeks, the patient gradually regained consciousness, started to communicate with verbal commands, and had flaccid quadriparesis. She was extubated and remained free of seizures, so she was discharged to the general ward on day 10. An intensive physiotherapy program was started, and there was no further seizure recurrence. The patients is discharged home currently On: Levetiracetam 1.5 bd and Lacosmide 200 bd.

She was discharged from the hospital 1 month after her operation. She was seizure-free for 4 months after the discharge, then she developed one generalized tonic clinic seizure after traveling and a history of upper respiratory tract infection, but for the last 6 months, she was seizure-free since her last electroencephalogram (Fig. 5) showed.

Discussion

We have presented a case highlighting the potential outcome of epilepsy surgery in super SRSE. Early surgical intervention is a treatment option to be considered for SRSE6. Few cases of SRSE involving epilepsy surgery with good outcomes have been published1. The SE resolved acutely in all of cases with no mortality and no substantial morbidity1. Surgical modalities are variable and should be tailored according to the pattern of the active epileptic focus7.

The potential surgical options for treating super-refractory status epilepticus (SRSE) include focal, lobar, and multi-lobar resections, hemispherectomy (functional, anatomical, and/or modified), corpus callostomy, multiple subpial transaction with or without focal resection, as well as neuromodulation techniques such as vagal nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, and deep brain stimulation. A study demonstrated that these surgical techniques led to significant improvement in seizure control for SRSE patients, with some patients achieving complete seizure control and others experiencing a reduction in seizure frequency of more than 90%7. Given the high mortality and morbidity rates associated with SRSE, it is recommended to consider appropriate neurosurgical intervention early in the course of the illness to improve outcomes. Criteria for selecting patients for epilepsy surgery should include the failure of standard medical treatments for status epilepticus, identification of epileptogenic zones through EEG, semiology, and neuroimaging studies, as well as the performance of epilepsy surgery by an experienced epilepsy surgeon8. This case illustrates the positive outcome of epilepsy surgery in cases of super-refractory status epilepticus (SRSE). Early consideration of surgical intervention is important in managing SRSE. Patient selection for epilepsy surgery should be based on criteria such as the ineffectiveness of standard medical treatments for status epilepticus.

Conclusion

We present a case highlighting the potential outcome of Epilepsy surgery in SRSE. Early surgical intervention is a treatment option to be considered in SRSE. Criteria to select a patient for epilepsy surgery should include failure of standard medical treatment protocols for SE, the definition of epileptogenic zones (EZ) by EEG, semiology, and neuroimaging studies & performance of epilepsy surgery by experienced neurosurgeons.
