Opt-in and opt-out strategies for HIV testing—author's reply
Authors: Miłosz Parczewski
Published: 2025

I am grateful to Emila Miró and collegues1 for their important insights relating to approaches to HIV testing and the choice between opt-in and opt-out strategies. Our Review2 focused largely on the differences in the consent requirements that are at the core of country policies for testing and on reduction of barriers to consent to combat late HIV diagnosis.

Opt-out testing strategies in emergency departments provide an option to diagnose a significant proportion of people who are unaware that they have HIV in the UK, with successful pilot studies implemented across several countries in Europe.3 Discussion of implementation of these strategies is pivotal owing to increasing number of new diagnoses in vulnerable and commonly stigmatised populations who have limited access to care, such as migrants, and account for the largest proportion of newly diagnosed cases (in 2023, 38.1% across the WHO European Region and 47.9% cases in the European Union/European Economic Area).4 In general, opt-out HIV testing allows broader coverage by facilitating testing for people attending medical services for various reasons, and normalises HIV testing as a medical procedure.

It is important to emphasise that testing must be voluntary in any situation and setting, and awareness of being tested and consent for this medical procedure is central to the person-oriented approach that should be at the heart of care for people at risk of or living with HIV. Therefore, I highly agree that expansion of hybrid approaches based on local prevalence would be a relevant way forward. However, I would also like to re-emphasise the need to focus on de-centralised and de-medicalised testing options and strategies, such as community and home-based testing.5 In these models, testing is largely initiated not by the provider but by the person in need, and such approaches increase testing acceptance and uptake across various populations including women, people living in rural areas, and people who fear stigma related to testing.

Being aware of the need to harmonise the standards of care related to HIV testing, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, in partnership with European AIDS Clinical Society, recently developed a module on HIV testing that defines the desired quality of intervention.6 This document provides a guidance on optimal testing policy development and implementation at national levels. The module focuses not only on the equitable access to testing across health-care settings and key populations, and promotion of community-based testing, but also, importantly, on access to universal opt-out testing, especially in settings with high HIV prevalence (eg, >1%). This is the first initiative to provide a basis for optimisation of testing policies and strategies, including consent, reduction of barriers, and increasing coverage. In Europe, where the number of people who know that they live with HIV is still under the UNAIDS 95% target, discussion of approaches to expanding access and reducing barriers to testing remain of primary importance.
