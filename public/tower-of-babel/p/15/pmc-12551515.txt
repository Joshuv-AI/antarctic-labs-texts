Predicting 30-day mortality in emergency department patients with suspected infection: external validation of the RISE UP score in a single tertiary centre
Authors: Sophie M E van Baar de Knegt, Jan Willem Uffen, Titus A P de Hond, Patricia M Stassen, Noortje Zelis, Karin A H Kaasjager
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveRapid identification of high-risk and low-risk patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) influences clinical management and can help optimise patient outcomes as well as resource allocation. This study aims to externally validate the Risk Stratification in the Emergency Department in Acutely Ill Older Patients (RISE UP) score in adult patients in the ED with suspected infection. Furthermore, generalisability was assessed by comparing the discriminatory ability of the RISE UP with the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) as well as the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) and National Early Warning Score (NEWS).DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingSingle-centre study in the ED of a tertiary, university-affiliated hospital.ParticipantsAdult patients with suspected infection presenting at the ED for internal medicine from 2016 to 2022.OutcomesThe primary outcome was all-cause 30-day mortality. Secondary outcomes were all-cause 14-day mortality, 7-day mortality and intensive care unit (ICU) admission.MethodsPrognostic performance was evaluated using discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC)) and a calibration plot.ResultsOf the included 5038 ED visits, there was a 30-day mortality of 7.1%. Discrimination of RISE UP for 30-day mortality was good (AUC 0.809; 95% CI 0.786 to 0.832) and significantly higher than that for the other risk scores: qSOFA (AUC 0.675; 95% CI 0.644 to 0.707), MEWS (AUC 0.688; 95% CI 0.658 to 0.718) and NEWS (AUC 0.725; 95% CI 0.696 to 0.754) (p<0.001). For 14-day and 7-day mortality, RISE UP had the highest AUC, but NEWS performed best for ICU admission. The RISE UP score was well calibrated and had significantly better discriminatory ability in older patients aged ≥65 years (AUC 0.772; 95% CI 0.738 to 0.806; p<0.001) and patients with sepsis (AUC 0.746; 95% CI 0.695 to 0.798; p<0.05) compared with the other scores. Low-risk patients with a RISE UP score of <5% yielded a negative predictive value of 97.7% (95% CI 97.2 to 98.1) and a sensitivity of 79.3% (95% CI 74.7 to 83.4).ConclusionsThe RISE UP score outperformed the qSOFA, MEWS and NEWS in predicting 30-day mortality. It is generalisable to an adult infection-specific cohort and may facilitate distinction between high-risk and low-risk patients in the ED, particularly to rule out poor outcomes.Keywords: Prognosis, Mortality, Emergency Departments

Introduction

Early and accurate risk stratification of patients with infection in the emergency department (ED) remains challenging. Discriminating between those at high or low risk for poor outcomes is important, as it guides clinical decision-making.1 2 The ED is frequently the first setting where patients with infections are systematically evaluated. Presentation is heterogeneous, ranging from otherwise healthy to critically ill patients with sepsis. The 30-day mortality rate ranges from 4% in patients with suspected infection to 35% in patients with septic shock.1 3 Especially given the increasing overcrowding, accurate prediction of mortality is crucial for efficient prioritisation and resource allocation in a complex and dynamic environment like the ED.4

Risk stratification in the ED is based on limited and evolving data. Prediction models should aim to complement clinical assessment.5 Models should strike a balance between being pragmatic and easily retrievable while remaining reliable with high predictive capacity.6 7 Scores currently used in EDs include the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA), the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) and the National Early Warning Score (NEWS).4 8 9 These scores use vital parameters to predict poor outcomes and have shown varying results when used to predict short-term mortality.10 11 The Risk Stratification in the Emergency Department in Acutely Ill Older Patients (RISE UP) score uses vital signs and laboratory parameters to predict 30-day mortality in patients in the ED within the first 2 hours of arrival.12 Despite its development for an older population, it has shown good discrimination in adult (COVID-19) patients, demonstrating better discrimination than other currently used clinical scores.11 13 Its added value lies in the good discrimination on both sides of the spectrum, allowing for the identification of the acutely ill at risk of poor outcome, but also contributing to the clinical judgement in identifying those who may be eligible for discharge. The RISE UP score has not yet been validated in an unselected ED cohort with patients with suspected infection.

Many tools currently used are not specifically designed for the infectious ED population and show suboptimal discriminatory power in validation studies.8 14 It remains ambiguous which screening tool for mortality after ED presentation is most accurate. Clinical use of a prediction model with good discriminatory ability can help to improve outcomes and resource management.

This large retrospective study therefore aims to (1) externally validate the discriminatory ability of the RISE UP score in predicting outcomes in patients with infection in the ED, and (2) compare its predictive performance with that of qSOFA, MEWS and NEWS for 30-day mortality.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis and artificial intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) guidelines for prognostic model validation.15 16

Study design and setting

A retrospective analysis of clinical data from the SPACE (SePsis in the ACutely ill patients in the Emergency department) cohort, between 13 September 2016 and 8 February 2022, was performed.17 The SPACE cohort included consecutive patients visiting the ED of the University Medical Centre Utrecht (UMCU). The UMCU is a 1042-bed tertiary university-affiliated hospital in the Netherlands with 20 500 annual ED visits.

Patient and public involvement

Patients with lived experience of sepsis were consulted during the design and set-up of the SPACE cohort to ensure research questions and outcomes are relevant. Input was provided by the founder of a support network for sepsis survivors (Sepsis en Daarna). The patient panel of the UMCU was also part of the advisory group. A national sepsis awareness organisation (SepsisNet) was engaged to support the dissemination of study results to participants and the wider community.

Study population

The SPACE cohort consisted of all consecutive patients meeting the following criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) presentation in the ED with suspected infection (symptoms suitable for inclusion, i.e., fever, coughing, erythema) and (3) registration in the ED for internal medicine or its subspecialties (oncology, immunology, haematology, nephrology, endocrinology, infectious diseases, rheumatology).17 Patients were retrospectively included by researchers using a predefined protocol.17 Participants were excluded if they objected to sharing medical data, if there was no outcome available or if they had repeat ED visits within 30 days to prevent multiple inclusions for the same infection episode and to attain representative data.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical parameters, including age, triage according to the Netherlands Triage Standard (NTS),18 vital signs, laboratory results, intensive care unit (ICU) admission and follow-up data on mortality, were automatically collected from electronic medical records (EMRs). Additional data, including reason for visit, comorbidities according to the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI),19 treatment and diagnosis, were manually extracted from EMRs by independent researchers using a predefined protocol with standardised definitions.17 For blood tests measured multiple times, the test conducted closest to ED arrival was selected. If blood tests were not performed in the ED, tests taken before the visit or in the ward were considered using the same cut-offs as in other studies (≤12 hours of ED arrival for blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH); ≤24 hours for albumin and bilirubin20 21). Baseline data from the original derivation cohort was also collected.12

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was all-cause mortality within 30 days after ED presentation. Secondary outcomes were all-cause 14-day mortality, all-cause 7-day mortality and ICU admission. Mortality data were extracted from EMRs, which are linked to municipal death registries.

Prediction models

The RISE UP score was calculated using the parameters age, abnormal vital signs, serum albumin, BUN, LDH and bilirubin. A formula was used to determine the probability of 30-day mortality with the outcome as a percentage.12 The variable of ≥2 abnormal vital parameters uses the following cut-offs: heart rate >90 bpm, mean arterial blood pressure <70 mmHg, respiratory rate >20/min, oxygen saturation <95% and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) <15.

The three prediction models chosen for the comparative assessment were selected based on feasibility for use in EDs. This meant that incorporated variables should be readily available, routinely measured and practically attainable in the ED setting. Furthermore, variables had to be available in our database. The commonly used qSOFA, MEWS and NEWS were selected. These are all calculated using vital parameters (online supplemental table 1).

All scores were calculated retrospectively. The first recorded vital signs in the ED were used. Where necessary, the GCS was converted to the alert, verbal, pain, unresponsive (AVPU) scale. Sepsis was defined based on clinical diagnosis (by a physician in the ED or in the hospital, or medical diagnosis code registered in the EMR).22 The qSOFA was not used to define sepsis due to its limited sensitivity and its inclusion as a risk score; using it as both a case-definition tool and a comparator would reduce the scope of the comparative analysis.

Statistical analyses

As recommended for prognostic validation studies, a minimum of 100 events for the primary outcome were included.23 Baseline characteristics were analysed using descriptive statistics. Baseline data between complete case and incomplete case was compared.

To account for missing data, imputation was used to replace missing vitals and laboratory values. Since the observed pattern of missingness was considered to be missing at random, two different imputation methods were applied. Primary analysis used stochastic regression imputation using predictive mean matching with 20 iterations. Supplementary analyses used median imputation (missing as normal) and complete case analysis. Patients in the complete case analysis had no missing data for any of the variables in the prediction models. Outcomes of risk scores were compared across the different methods.

Performance and comparison of the risk scores were evaluated using discriminatory ability and model calibration. Discriminatory ability was determined by calculating the area under the receiver operator characteristic curves (AUCs) with 95% CI. An AUC score measures a test’s ability to correctly classify positive and negative outcomes. The AUCs were considered to be poor at 0.6–0.7, adequate at 0.7–0.8, good at 0.8–0.9 and excellent at ≥0.9.24 Patients aged <65 or ≥65 years, as well as patients with or without sepsis, were compared in subanalyses. All AUCs were compared using the method of DeLong.25

Calibration of the RISE UP score was performed by categorising patients into 10 deciles of equal sample size according to predicted risk and plotting them against observed risk.26 Calibration-in-the-large and the Brier score were calculated. The calibration plot was assessed by visual inspection. To evaluate prognostic accuracy using clinical cut-offs, individuals were stratified according to predicted risk using the following cut-off points: scores of <5% and <10% defined as very low and low risk, >30% and >50% as high and very high risk. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive values (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV) were calculated.

P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistics were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, IBM Corporation, V.26.0.

Results

Study population and patient characteristics

There were a total of 6204 unique ED visits of patients with suspected infection. After exclusion, a total of 5038 ED visits were eligible for analysis (figure 1).

Flowchart of included patient emergency department (ED) visits. Stochastic regression imputation was used for the main analysis, and additional analyses used median imputation and complete case analysis.

Patient characteristics are summarised in table 1. The cohort was 53.3% male. The median age was 62 years (IQR 46–72), and 43.5% were aged ≥65 years. The CCI score was 4 (IQR 2–7), and 21.0% of patients were triaged as urgent. Two or more abnormal vital signs, as defined by the RISE UP score, were observed in 29.9% of patients. Most patients were admitted to the hospital (65.8%), and 303 patients were admitted to the ICU (6.0%). Of the total cohort, 357 patients died within 30 days of ED arrival, yielding an all-cause 30-day mortality of 7.1%. The 14-day and 7-day mortality rates were 4.2% and 2.6%, respectively.

There were 605 patients eligible for complete case analysis. Compared with the total cohort, these patients had a higher CCI score (5; IQR 3–7), were more likely to be triaged as urgent (41.4%) and had a higher 30-day mortality rate (15.7%). Albumin, bilirubin and respiratory rate were variables with the highest missing data (online supplemental table 2). A total of 401 patients had a qSOFA score of ≥2 (8.0%), and 711 patients were clinically diagnosed with sepsis (14.1%). Patients excluded due to repeat ED visits had a similar outcome incidence. Compared with the derivation cohort,12 the current cohort was younger, had more comorbidities and had a lower mortality rate (online supplemental table 2).

Validation of prediction models

The average RISE UP score was 6.8%, indicating an average predicted 30-day mortality of 6.8%. More than 75% of patients had a predicted risk of <10%, and less than 2% a risk of >50% (online supplemental figure 1).

The RISE UP score yielded the best discriminatory value for all-cause 30-day mortality, with an AUC of 0.809 (95% CI 0.786 to 0.832), as compared with qSOFA (AUC 0.675; 95% CI 0.644 to 0.707), MEWS (AUC 0.688; 95% CI 0.658 to 0.718) and NEWS (AUC 0.725; 95% CI 0.696 to 0.754) (table 2; figure 2). The AUC of the RISE UP was significantly higher than the other scores (p<0.001 for all comparisons). For all-cause 14-day and 7-day mortality, RISE UP had the best discrimination with an AUC of 0.834 (95% CI 0.805 to 0.862) and 0.863 (95% CI 0.830 to 0.897), respectively. For ICU admission, the NEWS score performed significantly better than RISE UP with an AUC of 0.835 (95% CI 0.813 to 0.858; p<0.05). All results were consistent with median imputation and complete case analysis (online supplemental figure 2).

Clinical cut-off values

Patients with a RISE UP score of <5% had a very low risk of 30-day mortality (2.3%), yielding an NPV of 97.7% (95% CI 97.2 to 98.1) and sensitivity of 79.3% (95% CI 74.7 to 83.4) (table 3). Patients with a score of >30% were at a high risk of poor outcome with a 30-day mortality of 36.7% and an ICU admission of 25.2%, with a PPV of 36.7% (95% CI 31.2 to 41.8) and a specificity of 96.1% (95% CI 95.5 to 96.7).

DISCUSSION

In this study, external validation of the RISE UP score showed good discriminatory ability for predicting all-cause 30-day mortality in adult patients with suspected infection (AUC 0.809), performing significantly better compared with qSOFA, MEWS and NEWS. Clinical cut-off analysis demonstrated high NPVs for low-risk patients.

The RISE UP score was developed to predict all-cause 30-day mortality in older medical patients in the ED.12 Within 2 hours of admission to the ED, it is possible to predict mortality probability as a percentage using an online formula (https://jscalc.io/calc/c4zL9aYdhMf7bjJb). External validation in adult COVID-19 patients demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.83).13 Another study found that the RISE UP, along with another Covid risk score, outperformed 19 other prediction models for adult patients in the ED presenting for internal medicine (AUC 0.86).11 Analysis of clinical cut-offs showed that the RISE UP discriminated well on both sides of the spectrum and accurately ruled out poor outcomes in low-risk patients (score <5%: NPV 99.1 (95% CI 98.4 to 99.5), sensitivity 95.7 (95% CI 92.2 to 97.9)).11 The results in this current cohort are similar to previous findings. Despite development for an older ED population, the RISE UP score performs well in a younger, infection-specific cohort.

The qSOFA score, developed as a sepsis screening score, predicts deterioration outside of the ICU. In EDs, the qSOFA has demonstrated low sensitivity and was found to be less accurate than early warning scores.27 The early warning scores have higher sensitivity to detect poor outcomes than qSOFA, but at the expense of specificity.27 Studies on the MEWS have shown poor to fair prediction of short-term mortality, similar to the AUC found in this study.10 The NEWS score has previously been found to be superior to other scores in predicting mortality and ICU admission,10 27 consistent with our results. These scores may be more suitable specifically for triage and prehospital care.

The good discriminatory ability of RISE UP, as compared with the other scores, can be attributed to the score reflecting the severity of illness. Albumin, bilirubin, BUN and LDH all reflect severe diseases or comorbidities, such as sepsis, liver/kidney failure or shock, and are all associated with increased risk of mortality.20 28 Scores that reflect comorbidities, such as age and laboratory parameters, outperform those that only use vitals.1 12 The RISE UP score also showed significantly better discrimination for 30-day mortality of patients with sepsis, which may be due to the inclusion of the negative acute-phase protein albumin, specifically, which is a strong predictor of mortality and infection.29 Given the contentious definition of sepsis, this result is interesting but must be interpreted critically.

In clinical practice, the RISE UP score can contribute to identifying patients on both sides of the spectrum (at low and high risk of poor outcome), in particular to help identify patients unlikely to have poor outcomes. The score may complement the clinician’s assessment of the severity of acute and underlying illness. This could contribute to guiding decisions at an early stage of the ED visit on intensifying care or deciding which patients may be safely discharged home.

Strengths and limitations

This study validated a new clinical model in a large heterogeneous ED cohort, demonstrating generalisability and contributing to the practice of validating existing prediction models.5 14 There are several limitations.

First, the analysis was performed in a single tertiary hospital treating many specialised populations, such as patients with immunodeficiencies or malignancies; this may limit generalisability. Second, missing data posed an important limitation, especially as not all laboratory values are routinely measured in our hospital. The available variables constrained the initial selection of ED models for comparison. The data that were analysed was found to be missing at random. Missing data were addressed using two methods for imputation and conducting a complete case analysis, with comparison of results, as recommended.30 A limitation of stochastic regression imputation is the underestimation of variance.31 Despite the expected differences between complete and incomplete case populations, the discrimination results from all three analyses were consistent. Given the good discrimination of RISE UP, the association of laboratory parameters with mortality, and their feasible determination within 2 hours of arrival, it is recommended that albumin and bilirubin become part of the routine lab completed in EDs. Third, 30-day mortality as an outcome measure does not capture interventions provided during that period, while hospital treatments may influence prognosis. Nevertheless, it remains a relevant measure for assessing the severity of illness in early stages and guiding further clinical decision-making. This is demonstrated by the frequent use of 30-day mortality in risk prediction.10 Additionally, low-risk patients who are identified are typically not those receiving life-saving therapies. Furthermore, the scores used for comparison with the RISE UP were originally developed to predict clinical deterioration rather than 30-day mortality, which may have influenced the observed AUC outcomes. However, prediction scores are frequently clinically used and assessed for outcomes that differ from those for which they were originally developed. Fourth, the first recorded vital signs were used, not the most abnormal values. Despite the potential for vital signs to rapidly change, choosing the first parameters reflects on the clinical practice of immediate triage into high-risk and low-risk patients on ED arrival. Fifth, there is a slight overestimation of predicted risk as shown in the calibration plot. It is known that risk models frequently do worse in external validation.32 The overestimation may be attributed to the difference in mortality with the derivation cohort, as well as the imputation used for high numbers of missing values for albumin and bilirubin.5 33 Sixth, as the RISE UP is linear in assessing risk and not binary in outcome, the interpretation of predictive values does not completely represent the accuracy of the score. Lastly, as patients with suspected infections were included in this cohort, the selection was not affected by changing sepsis guidelines.34 For the subanalysis of patients with sepsis, the clinical diagnosis was used rather than the qSOFA score, which remains representative within the new guidelines. It is not expected that treatment for patients included before and after 2021 differs much.

Conclusions

The RISE UP is applicable to an adult infection-specific ED cohort. In clinical practice, it may contribute to early-stage clinical decision-making in the ED by accurately identifying high-risk patients needing intensified care, as well as patients potentially eligible for safe discharge. It would be interesting to further explore early discharge, including the potential for home monitoring, and how this affects patient outcomes and ED efficiency.
