Atypical Presentations of Levator Ani Syndrome With Perianal Hyperhidrosis: A Case Series
Authors: Riya Gupta, Kara J. Jencks, John A. Damianos, Xiao Jing (Iris) Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLevator ani syndrome (LAS) explains up to 7.4% of anorectal pain cases. Classic symptoms include rectal pain or pressure with sitting. We present 2 patients, ages 32 and 25 years, presenting initially with “diarrhea” and perianal hyperhidrosis. History revealed rectal discomfort, tenesmus, urgency, and sensation of incomplete evacuation. Puborectalis tenderness was present on digital rectal examination. Therapies were tailored to each patient, including pelvic floor physical therapy with biofeedback, bowel regulation, rectal diazepam, and ileostomy. These presentations offer insights into potential pathophysiological mechanisms in LAS and highlight the importance of considering LAS in non-pain presentations.

INTRODUCTION

Levator ani syndrome (LAS) is an anorectal disorder involving increased left atrium muscle tension leading to pain often described as “sitting on a ball,” sharp, or “heavy pressure.”1 LAS accounts for up to 5.7% and 7.4% of anorectal pain in men and women, respectively, but may be underdiagnosed due to lack of awareness.2 Recent studies showed that bilateral lumbar and sacral plexus neuropathy may contribute to the development of LAS.3 Diagnostic criteria (Rome IV) are chronic and recurrent rectal pain with episodes lasting >30 minutes.4 Digital rectal examination (DRE) features tenderness with puborectalis (PR) traction with exclusion of other structural/functional causes of pain.1 LAS is associated with diminished quality of life and mental distress. Treatment options include pelvic floor biofeedback physical therapy (PFPT), neuromodulation (medications or stimulators), botulinum toxin injection, digital massage, and sitz baths.5

We describe 2 cases of LAS where patients presented with primary complaints unrelated to pain. The aim of our report was to raise awareness of alternate LAS presentations and associated symptoms, including altered bowel patterns and excessive perianal sweating.

Case 1

A 32-year-old man presented with 15 years of “diarrhea,” characterized by frequent bowel movements (BMs), urgency, tenesmus, rectal pain, perianal hyperhidrosis, and abdominal pain, leading to disease-associated anxiety (Table 1).

Symptoms were thought to have started after a viral infection. He had undergone extensive testing and treatments, including fecal transplants, neuromodulators, and, eventually, diverting ileostomy. Following diversion, he developed recurrent rectal and abdominal pain, spasms, and mucus discharge. Oxycodone and botulinum toxin provided partial relief but led to dependence and fecal incontinence, respectively. Owing to persistent symptoms, he was referred to our center for further evaluation.

Colonoscopy revealed diversion colitis; he then presented to the emergency department for acute right lower quadrant abdominal pain. Laboratory results were unremarkable, and imaging showed no perforation. External rectal examination was normal; however, DRE revealed tenderness suggestive of LAS. Given his symptoms, he was diagnosed with LAS and visceral hypersensitivity, exacerbated by chronic opioid use and colonoscopy. He also noted significant perianal hyperhidrosis associated with rectal spasms to the point of soaking his undergarments.

He was treated with 6 weeks of nortriptyline at increasing doses, tapering opioids, and rectal diazepam which helped with rectal discomfort and pain-related anxiety but not tenesmus.

Anorectal manometry was consistent with LAS with high resting sphincter tone. Persistent perianal hyperhidrosis prompted evaluation of autonomic dysfunction, but perianal nerves were not assessed. We recommended intensive PFPT, resulting in dramatic improvement of all symptoms except tenesmus for which he underwent sacral nerve stimulator implant. He was able to wean off opioids.

Case 2

A 25-year-old man with attention-deficit disorder presented with “diarrhea” with over 40 BMs daily (Table 1). He initially presented locally 2 years ago with diarrhea, perianal hyperhidrosis, and tenesmus after viral infection. Colonoscopy, CT enterography, and esophagogastroduodenoscopy were unremarkable. Hyoscyamine and rectal diazepam were ineffective, and dicyclomine decreased urgency.

He presented to us for a second opinion of his persistent urgency and tenesmus. He reported the sensation of needing to defecate 40-50 times daily, but due to difficulty producing stool, he was using tap water enemas multiple times per day to pass stool and relieve the sense of urgency. External rectal examination was only notable for a prominent coccyx with a second lateral protuberance. On DRE, resting sphincter tone was high, and he had tenderness to PR palpation. MR spine for nerve impingement was negative. Given concern for LAS likely exacerbated by the frequency of enemas, he was referred for biofeedback PFPT. On completion, he had reduced urgency and was having once daily cleansing BMs without enemas.

DISCUSSION

LAS can be diagnosed through history and physical examination. Missed and delayed diagnosis can lead to unnecessary testing, procedures, and increased symptom burden, causing decreased quality of life and increased morbidity.

Risk factors of LAS include prolonged sitting, herniated lumbar disk, hysterectomy, childbirth, excessive defecation, stress, and surgical procedures such as low anterior resection.6–8 Comorbid psychiatric disease was observed in both patients, suggesting brain-gut dysfunction in the pathophysiology of LAS.9 Both also had a history of prior infection. Although no literature exists on microbiota in LAS, our cases suggest immune mechanisms may play a role, similar to other disorders of gut-brain interaction. Mechanical stress from diarrhea from a preceding infection may also be an inciting factor. Both patients reported “diarrhea” and excessive attempts at defecation though with small-sized BMs without having the amount of stool typically seen with diarrhea. Perianal hyperhidrosis, observed in both cases, is a novel symptom in LAS, suggesting potential autonomic nervous system dysregulation (Figure 1).

DRE can confirm suspicion of LAS with additional evidence through anorectal manometry as tenderness of the PR during DRE is subjective and requires experience.6 Dunphy et al presented the only other published atypical presentation of LAS featuring vaginal pain. Improved understanding of variable presentations and increased comfort with the diagnostic examination can help facilitate accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment plans.10

The impact of LAS on health, mental status, and quality of life is significant, and diagnostic delays such as those presented in our cases can lead to complications such as surgery, opioid dependence, and gastrointestinal hypersensitivity, highlighting the need for prompt diagnosis and treatment to prevent personal, social, and occupational burdens.

There is no established cure for LAS. Treatment is multimodal, involving PFPT with biofeedback, behavioral changes, medications, injections, and rarely surgery. Sitz baths, digital massage, muscle relaxants, electrogalvanic stimulation, biofeedback, inhaled salbutamol, and botulinum toxin injections have demonstrated variable improvement.11,12 A trial of 7 patients found botulinum toxin ineffective, yet it remains in use due to the lack of other effective treatments.13 Randomized trials found biofeedback and injecting the maximal tender point of the arcus tendon in the left atrium with triamcinolone acetonide and lidocaine more effective than electrogalvanic stimulation and massage.14,15 In our patients, PFPT with biofeedback was safe and provided relief of symptoms. Further randomized trials are needed to determine optimal treatment and guidelines for LAS management.

LAS may present atypically and can be under-recognized. Studies are required to better understand pathophysiology, which may include immune mechanisms from prior infections, autonomic nervous system dysfunction, and gut-brain axis dysregulation. LAS is an important diagnostic consideration for anorectal concerns and altered bowel habits. Biofeedback PFPT should be the first treatment recommendation.
