Whole-genome analyses reveal the phylogenetic history of Japanese and Ryukyu wild boars
Authors: Rikako Itoi, David Gamarra, Yohey Terai, Jun Gojobori, Masaaki Taniguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ancestral population of wild boar (Sus scrofa) split into Eastern and Western Eurasian lineages, with the Eastern lineage extending its range into the Japanese archipelago. Within this archipelago, there are two subspecies: the Japanese wild boar (JWB, Sus scrofa leucomystax) and the Ryukyu wild boar (RWB, Sus scrofa riukiuanus), which exhibit differences in morphology and habitat. Until now, the whole genomes of JWB and RWB individuals with known origins had not been sequenced, leaving their genetic relationships with other populations unclear. In this study, we resequenced the whole genomes of Japanese wild boar (JWB) individuals from Honshu (Tochigi, Aichi, and Kyoto), Shikoku (Tokushima) and Kyushu (Saga) islands, with one individual sampled from each location. Additionally, we resequenced two RWB individuals from Okinawa and Ishigaki Islands.ResultsWe analyzed the genomes to clarify the genetic relationships between JWB, RWB, and other lineages of the genus Sus. Phylogenetic analyses based on whole genome sequence showed that JWB and RWB each formed distinct clades within a monophyletic JWB/RWB lineage, which was sister to Eastern Russian and Northern Chinese wild boars. Principal component analysis further supported their genetic differentiation. Together, these results indicate that JWB and RWB represent independent boar lineages that are likely to have diverged following isolation within the Japanese archipelago. In addition, gene flow between JWB and Eastern Russian/Northern Chinese boars was detected by the f4 statistics.ConclusionOverall, our results show that JWB and RWB diverged from East Asian boars through dispersal and isolation within the Japanese archipelago.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12862-026-02556-2.

Background

The wild boar (Sus scrofa) is a species found worldwide [1]. Phylogenetic studies indicate that its ancestral population originated in Southeast Asia and split into Eastern and Western Eurasian lineages approximately 200,000 years ago [2]. Wild boars have not only expanded to major continents but also to islands separated by seas, such as those in Southeast Asia [3]. They have a high potential for environmental adaptation, including high-altitude areas [4] and low-temperature regions [5]. Previous genomic studies have documented gene flow between European and Chinese domestic pig lineages [6] as well as between wild boars and domestic pigs in both Europe and China [7].

The Japanese Archipelago is home to two subspecies of wild boar: the Japanese wild boar (JWB, Sus scrofa leucomystax) and the Ryukyu wild boar (RWB, Sus scrofa riukiuanus). JWB is found on the islands of Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu, while RWB inhabits the Ryukyu Islands. The morphological differences between these two subspecies are well-documented. One significant distinction is their body size; JWB individuals are generally larger than RWB individuals, as studies on mandible and dental morphology indicate [8]. Furthermore, RWB has been reported to be the smallest wild boar in the world [9].

Previous studies have investigated the genetic relationships among JWB/RWB using mtDNA [10, 11] or microsatellite markers [12, 13], with microsatellite-based analyses revealing population differentiation in JWB. However, population-scale whole-genome analyses have not yet been conducted. To date, whole-genome sequence data of a wild boar originating from Japan have been reported for only one individual (accession number: ERR173212) [7]; however, its geographic origin in Japan was not specified in the original report. The genome of this individual has been used in studies, such as Zhang et al. [5], which demonstrated a sister relationship between this individual and the clade that includes Russian and Korean wild boars. However, it remains unclear which subspecies this individual belongs to.

In this study, we resequenced the whole genomes of Japanese wild boar (JWB) individuals from Honshu (Tochigi, Aichi, and Kyoto), Shikoku (Tokushima) and Kyushu (Saga) islands, with one individual sampled from each location (Fig. 1). Additionally, we resequenced two Ryukyu wild boar (RWB) individuals from Okinawa and Ishigaki Islands. Using our data, along with publicly available genomic sequences from wild boars (WBs) and domestic pigs (DPs), we analyzed the genomes to clarify the genetic relationships between JWB, RWB, and other lineages of the genus Sus.Fig. 1A map showing the origin of the genomic data used in this study. Methods

Samples for whole-genome sequencing

We purchased tissue samples from wild boars collected by licensed hunters in their habitat areas through local commercial sources. Samples were selected from geographically distant locations based on the available information regarding their geographic origins. Five JWB were selected for whole-genome sequencing (WGS), covering a geographical range of approximately 1,160 km across the main islands of Japan (Honshu, Kyushu, and Shikoku). Two RWB were sampled from the Ryukyu Archipelago, specifically Okinawa and Ishigaki Islands (Fig. 1). Collection date, location, estimated age, and weight of each animal are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Tissue samples were stored at −20 °C until processing. Genomic DNA extraction from the ear and tongue tissue samples was conducted using the QiAmp DNA mini kit (QIAGEN Ltd., Hilden, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA quantity and quality were assessed the Agilent 2200 using TapeStation (Agilent Technologies), which revealed high‑molecular‑weight genomic DNA ranging from 4,600 to 8,000 bp. Subsequently, DNA samples were re‑quantified and normalized to prepare whole‑genome sequencing libraries constructed with an Illumina DNA Prep kit and standard TruSeq adapters (Illumina, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The sequenced paired‑end 150 bp reads were obtained in an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus.

Extraction of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and vcf file preparation

As a reference panel, we used published sequencing data from 34 wild boars, 19 domestic pigs, and two outgroup species (Supplementary Table 2). Sequence reads were processed using AdapterRemoval v2 with parameters –trimns –trimqualities –minquality 25 –minlength 25, which removed adaptor sequences, ambiguous bases (N), and terminal bases with Phred quality scores below 25. The trimmed reads were mapped to the Boar and Pig reference genome (GCF_000003025.6_Sscrofa11.1) [14] using BWA “BWA-MEM” algorithm.

The alignment files were exported in BAM file format and sorted and indexed using SAMtools [15]. The duplicated reads in the BAM files were marked by the MarkDuplicates algorithm, implemented in GATK v4.6 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us). We performed haplotype calling on all individuals analyzed in this study using the HaplotypeCaller algorithm in GATK v4.6, and output as gvcf format (−ERC GVCF option). All gvcf files were combined into a single gvcf file by the CombineGVCFs algorithm in GATK v4.6. The combined gvcf file was jointly genotyped by the GenotypeGVCFs algorithm in GATK v4.6. Distinct filtering criteria were applied to each analysis in order to maximize the number of SNPs retained for downstream analyses [16].

Phylogenetic analysis

We removed all sites with missing data (Max-missing 1). We removed all indels, singleton, and doubleton sites to eliminate PCR and sequencing errors that may have occurred in one individual by minor allele count (mac) filtering (mac = 3). We extracted bi-allelic sites with coverage equal to or more than one in all individuals and with GQ values equal to or more than eight in all individuals, using VCFtools [16]. To obtain approximately evolutionary independent SNPs, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning. Using PLINK ver. 1.90b6.21 [17] with an option “–indep-pairwise 50 10 0.1” was used to conduct the LD pruning. The final dataset consisted of 225,140 sites.

VCF from the SNP datasets for phylogenetic analysis were converted to PHYLIP format using TASSEL 5 [18]. First 10 kb sequences from the 5’ end of the PHYLIP format file were extracted and used to select best fit a model of nucleotide changes for the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method was selected using MEGA ver. X [19].

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the ML method using PhyML ver. 3.3.20220408 [20], using the GTR model (“−m GTR”) based on the best-fit model selected in MEGA, with 100 bootstrap replicates.

Population structure and ancestry inference

PCA and ADMIXTURE were performed without the outgroup. We removed all sites with missing data (Max-missing 1). We removed all indels, singleton, and doubleton sites to eliminate PCR and sequencing errors that may have occurred in one individual by minor allele count (mac) filtering (mac = 3). We extracted bi-allelic sites with coverage equal to or more than one in all individuals and with GQ values equal to or more than eight in all individuals, using VCFtools [16]. To obtain approximately evolutionary independent SNPs, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning. Using PLINK ver. 1.90b6.21 [17] with an option “–indep-pairwise 50 10 0.1” was used to conduct the LD pruning. The final dataset consisted of 199,123 sites.

We performed principal component analysis (PCA) using PLINK ver. 1.90b6.21 [17], and individual ancestry proportions were estimated using ADMIXTURE ver. 1.3.0 [21].

Shared genetic drift and admixture signals

We performed the Outgroup-f3 statistics to measure shared genetic drift between JWB or RWB and the other Sus populations, and the f4 statistics to test the genetic affinity between JWB/RWB and WB populations from Northern China, Eastern Russia, and European DP. For obtaining outgroup-f3 and f4 statistics implemented in ADMIXTOOLS ver. 7.0.2 [22] were used to evaluate the shared genetic drift among JWB/RWB and the Sus population from the other regions.

Inference of migration events

To infer population splits and potential migration events among Sus populations, we obtained Maximum Likelihood tree inferred by TreeMix [23]. The input file was generated from the same SNP dataset used for the PCA and phylogenetic tree construction, excluding the populations of OutGroup, European DP, and Western Russian WB. We removed all sites with missing data (Max-missing 1). We also removed all indels, singleton, and doubleton sites to eliminate PCR and sequencing errors that may have occurred in one individual by minor allele count (mac) filtering (mac = 3). We extracted bi-allelic sites with coverage equal to or more than one in all individuals and with GQ values equal to or more than eight in all individuals, using VCFtools [16]. To obtain approximately independent SNPs, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning. Using PLINK ver. 1.90b6.21 [17] with an option “–indep-pairwise 50 10 0.1” to conduct the LD pruning. The final dataset consisted of 254,533 sites.

The filtered VCF file was converted into PLINK binary format using PLINK v1.9 with the –make-bed option. Population assignments for each individual were specified in a population map file and allele frequencies for each population were calculated using the –freq –within option in PLINK. The resulting.frq.strat file was compressed with gzip and converted into TreeMix input format using plink2treemix.py. TreeMix analysis was then conducted using TreeMix ver.1.13 with blocks of 500 SNPs (−k 500). An initial tree without migration events (m = 0) was generated, followed by analyses allowing 1–6 migration events (m = 1–6). Rooted trees were inferred by using the outgroup population (OutGroup/SouthernChineseWB1). Tree topology and residual fit plots were visualized using the plotting_funcs.R script provided with TreeMix.

The tree shows the first major split between European and East Asian clades, with the Northern Chinese and Eastern Russian clades diverging from the Southern Chinese WB, consistent with a previous study [5] (Fig. 2). JWB and RWB formed a monophyletic clade, within which each subspecies formed its own subclade. Notably, the JWB/RWB clade is a sister clade to the Eastern Russian/Northern Chinese WB. Within the JWB subclade, the individual from Kyushu (Saga) is basal, followed by the individual from Shikoku (Tokushima), and the three individuals from Honshu cluster (Kyoto, Aichi, and Tochigi) together. This branching pattern reflects their geographic distribution across the Japanese mainland (see Fig. 1). Note that a sample, namely ERR173212, which has been recognized as the JWB in the public database, is placed in the clade of RWB, indicating this individual originates from the RWB (Fig. 2).

Additionally, we examined the possibility of introgression between the JWB/RWB populations and the European DP (Supplementary Fig. 11), since JWB individuals carrying European mtDNA haplotypes were reported in previous studies [24], and microsatellite analysis has confirmed the presence of hybrids between European pigs and wild boars [25]. We tested gene flow between JWB/RWB and European DP using f4 statistics. The results showed a smaller positive f4 value between European DP and JWB than the one observed between JWB and the Eastern Russian/Northern Chinese WBs (Figs. 5a, 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 11). This result suggests the possibility of past hybridization between JWB and European DP, consistent with previous studies [24, 25].

Discussion

Migration of JWB/RWB to Japanese archipelago

Our phylogenetic analysis showed that a monophyletic JWB and RWB clade is placed within the East Asian clade, and that it diverged from the common ancestor with Eastern Russian/Northern Chinese WBs. This finding suggests that the ancestors of the JWB and RWB likely originated in East Asia before migrating to the Japanese archipelago, where they subsequently diverged under isolation.

Zhang et al. 2022, 2025 [2, 5] estimated that the Southern and Northern Chinese populations diverged approximately 25,000 years ago, followed by a more recent separation between the Northern Chinese and the Eastern Russian populations occurring around 10,000 years ago. We applied these divergence times to our phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2); The divergence between the Southern Chinese and Northern Chinese/Eastern Russian populations corresponds to node T1, and the split between the Northern Chinese and Eastern Russian populations corresponds to node T2. Accordingly, we suggested the ancestral population of JWB/RWB diverged from the Eastern Russian/Northern Chinese lineage approximately 25,000–10,000 years ago (Fig. 2).

Archaeological evidence from the Japanese archipelago aligns with our suggested divergence time. Since the Jomon period (16,000–3,000 years ago), numerous wild boar remains have been excavated from archaeological sites dating to this period on the Japanese mainland (Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu) [26]. Additionally, the earliest known occurrences of wild boars on Okinawa and Ishigaki Islands date back to the Late Pleistocene [27]. Archeological remains of wild boars from the Late Pleistocene have been found in the Shiraho Saonetabaru Cave in Okinawa Island, dating from around 24,000 to 16,000 years ago [28]. Similarly, WBs remains have been reported from the Sakitari Cave on Ishigaki Island, dating to approximately 20,000 years ago [29].

While our genomic research and archaeological evidence do not allow us to identify the ancestral continental populations of the JWB/RWB or the migration routes to the Japanese archipelago. In the future, given that genomic data from modern and ancient wild boars along the continental coast become available, the discovery of the origins of JWB/RWB will progress robustly.

Differentiation of JWB and RWB populations in the Japanese archipelago

Our phylogenetic analysis revealed that within the JWB clade, the Saga (Kyushu) individual is positioned as basal, followed by the Shikoku individual, and then the Honshu individuals. This tree topology corresponds with the geographical distribution of these individuals and supports previous research by microsatellite markers [12, 13]. The genetic differentiation patterns likely reflect the history of geographical isolation in the Japanese archipelago.

During the Last Glacial Maximum, Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu islands were connected by land due to lower sea levels. Sea level rose during the postglacial period, resulting in the separation of Honshu and Shikoku islands around 7,000 years ago and of Honshu and Kyushu islands around 5,000 years ago [30]. These geological events are likely responsible for the population divergence observed among the JWB. Similar patterns of population structure in the Japanese archipelago have been reported in other mammals, such as the red fox (Vulpes vulpes) [31], Japanese Macaques (Macaca fuscata) [32], and mole (Mogera imaizumii and M. wogura) [33].

In the case of RWB, some degree of genetic differentiation between individuals from Okinawa and Ishigaki was suggested by the ML tree and PCA (Figs. 2 and 3a). Notably, these two islands have never been connected by land, even during periods of low sea levels [30]. It is likely that the ancestral RWB first colonized one island before being dispersed to others, where the isolated populations subsequently differentiated.

Introgression event of the JWB

Our analysis indicates that there is gene flow between JWB and WB populations in Eastern Russia and Northern China. However, it remains unclear in which direction this gene flow occurred: whether genomic segment of eastern Russia and northern China population introgressed into JWB, or vice versa. Furthermore, the f4 values between JWB individuals and individuals from Eastern Russia or Northern China did not differ significantly, regardless of which JWB individual was used (Figs. 5a, 5b), suggesting that gene flow occurred with the ancestral population of Eastern Russia and Northern China. However, we currently lack evidence to support this hypothesis, making it difficult to identify which specific WB populations that have experienced gene flow with JWB. Collecting more modern and ancient genomic data will help clarify the details of gene flow.

Conclusion

In this study, we newly determined the whole genome sequences of five Japanese wild boars and two Ryukyu wild boars and investigated their genetic relationships with other Sus populations. Our results showed that JWB and RWB form a monophyletic clade diverging from the common ancestor with the Eastern Russian/Northern Chinese wild boar lineage, and that JWB and RWB each form distinct clades. Based on our phylogenetic analysis and referred divergence time, we propose that the ancestral population of JWB and RWB migrated to the Japanese archipelago during the Late Pleistocene. Following the end of the Ice Age, geographic isolation between Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu islands within the archipelago likely led to the differentiation of the JWB populations. Furthermore, we detected gene flow between JWB and wild boars from Eastern Russia/Northern China. This initial NGS-based genomic analysis of JWB and RWB establishes a fundamental baseline for addressing the still-uncertain origins of their ancestors, and dispersion across the islands. Further genomic data from modern and ancient WB populations will elucidate the unresolved aspects of the historical and geographical distribution of Sus scrofa in Asia.
