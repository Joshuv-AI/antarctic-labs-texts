Between a Rock and a Hard Place
Authors: Ariela R. Orkaby, Tammy T. Hshieh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Among the many hazards of hospitalization faced by older adults admitted with heart failure are the geriatric syndromes of delirium and frailty. Delirium is a syndrome of acute cognitive change in attention and awareness, often brought on by a change in medical condition, while frailty is a syndrome of decreased physiologic reserve resulting in poor response to a stressor event, such as a heart failure admission.1,2 As the population ages, cardiovascular clinicians are increasingly faced with the challenge of balancing the complexities of cardiovascular disease with concomitant geriatric syndromes of aging, particularly delirium and frailty.

As many as half of adults living with heart failure are frail,3 while delirium is increasingly common in hospitalized older adults with heart failure.4 Both delirium and frailty are associated with poor clinical outcomes, including prolonged hospital stays, functional decline, institutionalization, cognitive impairment, and mortality.2,5 How these prevalent syndromes, which have significant consequences on quality of life, interact and influence outcomes, particularly in the context of heart failure exacerbations, is not well understood.

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Irizarry-Caro et al6 have examined this question using the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Nationwide Inpatient Sample. The investigators identified 736,392 adults aged 65 years or older who were admitted to the hospital from 2016 to 2018 with acute decompensated heart failure of any type, using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis codes. Frailty was identified using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS).7 HFRS was initially developed in hospitalized adults aged 75 years or older in the United Kingdom, capturing resource use and diagnoses on admission to categorize individuals into 3 groups of frailty: low, intermediate, and high risk. As a secondary analysis, frailty was also measured using the Claims-based Frailty Index (CFI), a well-validated definition of frailty first developed by Rockwood and colleagues based on the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment which evaluates domains of health including cognition, physical function, nutrition, medical morbidities, mental health, and so on.2 Delirium was defined using a validated algorithm based on ICD codes.8 Identifying delirium using this combination of data and ICD diagnosis codes is evidence-based with high specificity and lends itself to large data research of this type.

Among 3,577,433 admissions during the study period, delirium was present in 8.5%, and frailty was present in 8.3% using HFRS, with 55.6% at moderate risk of frailty. The presence of delirium increased the risk of mortality nearly 4-fold (OR: 3.74; 95% CI: 3.70-3.78) compared to those without delirium. Similarly, those with moderate or high frailty on admission were at a significant increased risk of morality (OR: 4.02; 95% CI: 3.96-4.09 and OR: 8.63; 95% CI: 8.47-8.78, respectively). There were significant interactions between delirium and frailty. When delirium was present with moderate or high frailty risk, odds of mortality increased as well (OR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.65-1.81, OR: 2.00; 95% CI: 3.61-3.73). Interestingly, the risk of death was highest among those with delirium and low frailty (OR: 6.09; 95% CI: 5.81-6.39), although it remained significant for those with delirium and moderate or high frailty (OR: 2.86; 95% CI: 2.82-2.90 and OR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.52-1.59, respectively). In sensitivity analysis using the CFI definition of frailty, results varied, and those with frailty and delirium had a decreased odds of mortality compared to patients without frailty while pre-frail patients with delirium had an increased odds of mortality compared to patients without frailty.

As the authors note, similarly counter-intuitive findings for delirium, low frailty, and mortality have been described previously.9 Of particular relevance to older adults, those with delirium and higher frailty scores had increased odds of nonhome discharges compared with their nonfrail counterparts (OR: 3.12; 95% CI: 3.09-3.15 and OR: 1.76; 95% CI: 1.73-1.80) but lower odds than those with delirium and low frailty risk (OR: 4.50; 95% CI: 4.38-4.62). In other words, patients with delirium and higher frailty risk were more likely to be discharged home. Taken together with the prior findings, these results may reflect that when prognosis is poor, aggressive treatments and interventions are less likely to be used, and patients are more likely to be taken home, potentially with a shift to a comfort care focus. Information on discharge with hospice, however, was not available for this study. If this pattern continues to be seen in other cohorts, it may suggest a change in focus within medical care and family caregiving—a shift towards focusing on prioritizing what matters most in end-of-life care and may help prevent admissions in the first place.

Future studies of delirium and frailty should carefully consider how frailty is defined. The HFRS is not well validated in those younger than 75 years or in the United States, includes codes for acute events such as delirium, and correlates poorly with physical phenotype of frailty or a Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment–based frailty index, such as the CFI.10 Other definitions of frailty that rely on claims data, such as the CFI, do not include delirium which may entangle the exposures and makes the results somewhat difficult to tease apart. The sensitivity analyses here suggest that more work is needed to understand how delirium and frailty interact in hospitalized patients with heart failure.

The work of Irizarry-Caro et al6 is an important contribution to very scant literature on the interaction between delirium and frailty. Fewer than 5 studies have been published in the English language literature studying the interaction of delirium, frailty, and clinical outcomes,11,12 with even fewer focused on the heart failure population. There is physiologic rationale for the hypothesis that delirium and frailty interact to worsen clinical outcomes specifically in HF patients. For example, cerebral hypoprofusion prevalent in older adults with HF may be a contributory factor to delirium and functional decline of frailty. Understanding overlapping mechanisms is necessary to develop focused and effective interventions for both. However, a first start is to begin to recognize these high-risk geriatric syndromes for all older patients admitted to the hospital. By identifying high-risk older adults with delirium with or without frailty, proven effective multicomponent, nonpharmacologic patient-centered care can be provided to prevent and treat delirium, such as the Hospital Elder Life Program.1 The Hospital Elder Life Program is an evidence-based program that systemically identifies patients at higher risk of delirium and utilizes volunteers and nursing interventions that are relatively low-technology but high-touch to manage delirium.

For older adults, their loved ones, and clinical teams, navigating the 2 risks of delirium and frailty may sometimes feel like the mythical/epic “rock and a hard place”—the monsters of Greek mythology, Scylla and Charybdis faced by Homer. In this analogy, the ocean represents the heart failure admission Odyssey with its inherent, monstrous risks as well. Recognizing and understanding the additional hazards of delirium and frailty individually and then in combination will help us move towards better outcomes for all older adults admitted with heart failure.
