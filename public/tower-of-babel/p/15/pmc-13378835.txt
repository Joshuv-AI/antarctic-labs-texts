Improving students’ perspective psychology in learning outcomes through adversity quotient and independent learning
Authors: Ulfiani Rahman, Nurul Hikmah, Idham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Many students exhibit a psychological aversion to mathematics, often perceiving it as more challenging than other subjects, leading to lower learning outcomes. This study investigates the effects of the adversity quotient and independent learning on students’ mathematics achievement.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The type of research used in this study is quantitative with the causality method. Employing descriptive statistics and multiple regression analyses, the study utilized the Adversity Response Profile (ARP) and a learning independence scale.RESULTS:The regression analysis indicated a positive and significant relationship between adversity quotient (AQ) and mathematics learning outcomes (β = 0.248, P < 0.05). This suggests that students with higher AQ tend to perform better in mathematics. However, AQ alone contributed only 10% to the overall variance in mathematics learning outcomes, implying that additional factors influence student performance. The results of the study stated that the better the AQ of the students, the better the mathematics learning outcomes obtained.CONCLUSION:The analysis revealed that AQ, independent learning, and outcomes were generally in the medium category; AQ positively influenced learning outcomes; independent learning had a significant positive effect; and both factors together significantly impacted learning outcomes.

Introduction

Learning outcomes are a central indicator of students’ success in achieving educational objectives, particularly in mathematics. Mathematics is a foundational subject that plays a critical role in the development of logical reasoning, problem-solving skills, and analytical thinking, all of which are essential for academic progression and future career opportunities.[1] Consequently, low achievement in mathematics remains a persistent concern in many educational systems and demands systematic investigation.[2]

In Indonesian middle schools, including SMP Sekolah Menengah Pertama Negeri 5 Sinjai, student performance in mathematics has consistently fallen below expected standards. A substantial proportion of students fail to meet the minimum competency criteria established by educational authorities. Classroom observations and teacher reports indicate that students often exhibit low engagement, limited persistence when faced with difficult problems, and a strong reliance on peers for answers rather than attempting independent problem-solving.[3] This dependence weakens students’ mathematical reasoning and limits their ability to develop conceptual understanding. Moreover, many students demonstrate minimal initiative to explore learning resources beyond teacher-provided materials, further constraining their learning progress.[4]

Research suggests that these challenges cannot be explained solely by cognitive ability or instructional quality. Psychological factors play a significant role in shaping students’ learning behaviors and academic outcomes. Among these factors, adversity quotient (AQ) and independent learning have emerged as important non-cognitive constructs associated with academic success, particularly in subjects that require sustained effort and perseverance, such as mathematics.[5]

AQ refers to an individual’s capacity to respond effectively to difficulties, setbacks, and challenges. In an educational context, AQ reflects students’ resilience, persistence, and ability to maintain motivation when encountering academic obstacles.[6] Students with higher AQ are more likely to view challenges as opportunities for learning rather than as threats, enabling them to persist in solving complex mathematical problems. Importantly, AQ is not an innate trait but a developable capacity that can be strengthened through appropriate educational experiences and supportive learning environments.[7]

The concept of AQ is commonly described through four dimensions: control, origin and ownership, reach, and endurance. These dimensions capture how individuals perceive and respond to adversity.[8] Based on these characteristics, students are often categorized as quitters, campers, or climbers. Quitters tend to avoid challenges and withdraw when difficulties arise; campers exert limited effort and become satisfied with minimal achievement; and climbers consistently strive to overcome obstacles and pursue improvement. In mathematics learning, climber-type students are more likely to persist in problem-solving tasks, explore alternative strategies, and achieve higher learning outcomes.[9]

Independent learning is another psychological construct closely linked to academic achievement. It refers to students’ ability to take initiative, regulate their learning processes, and assume responsibility for achieving learning goals without excessive dependence on external guidance.[10] Independent learning does not imply learning in isolation; rather, it emphasizes self-regulation, motivation, discipline, and reflective thinking. Students who demonstrate strong independent learning skills tend to engage more deeply with learning materials, seek additional resources, and persist through academic challenges.[11]

In mathematics education, independent learning is particularly important because mastery of mathematical concepts requires continuous practice, exploration, and reflection. Students who are independent learners are more likely to attempt solving problems on their own before seeking assistance, monitor their understanding, and adjust learning strategies when necessary. Core indicators of independent learning include self-confidence, discipline, responsibility, initiative, and self-control. When these attributes are present, students are better equipped to manage learning demands and achieve improved academic outcomes.[12]

Despite the recognized importance of AQ and independent learning, empirical research examining their combined influence on mathematics achievement remains limited, particularly within the Indonesian educational context. Existing studies have often focused on these constructs separately, leaving a gap in understanding how resilience and learning independence interact to influence students’ mathematics performance.[13] Addressing this gap is essential, as mathematics learning involves both cognitive processing and sustained psychological engagement.

Teachers play a crucial role in fostering both AQ and independent learning by adopting a facilitative approach to instruction. Rather than functioning solely as knowledge transmitters, teachers can encourage autonomy by providing opportunities for student choice, promoting goal-setting and self-reflection, and using open-ended questions that stimulate critical thinking.[14] Constructive feedback that guides students toward discovering solutions independently can further strengthen resilience and self-regulatory skills. Such instructional practices help create learning environments that support persistence, confidence, and academic growth.

Understanding the relationship between AQ, independent learning, and mathematics achievement has important implications for educational practice and policy. By identifying the psychological factors that contribute to successful learning outcomes, educators and curriculum developers can design targeted interventions aimed at strengthening students’ resilience and independence.[15] These efforts are expected to enhance student engagement, improve mathematics achievement, and better prepare learners to face academic challenges in increasingly complex educational environments.

Accordingly, this study investigates the relationship between AQ, independent learning, and mathematics achievement among junior high school students. It is hypothesized that students with higher levels of AQ and independent learning will demonstrate better mathematics achievement, and that independent learning will play a significant role in strengthening the relationship between AQ and learning outcomes. By examining these relationships, this study seeks to contribute to the growing body of research on non-cognitive factors in education and to provide practical insights for improving mathematics learning outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a quantitative research approach using a causal-comparative design to examine the effects of AQ and independent learning on students’ mathematics achievement. The research model consisted of two independent variables—AQ (X1) and independent learning (X2)—and one dependent variable, mathematics learning outcomes (Y). The conceptual framework assumes both individual and combined effects of AQ and independent learning on mathematics achievement.

The study was conducted at SMP Negeri 5 Sinjai during the odd semester of the academic year, within a formal classroom learning context.

Participants and sampling

The population comprised all 117 eighth-grade students enrolled at SMP Negeri 5 Sinjai. From this population, a sample of 59 students was selected using simple random sampling. This technique ensured that each student had an equal probability of being included in the study, thereby minimizing selection bias and supporting the representativeness of the sample.

Random sampling was implemented using a random number generator applied to the student roster. This procedure facilitated a balanced distribution of participants across demographic characteristics such as age and gender. The selected sample size was deemed sufficient for regression analysis and aligned with the objectives of the study.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using two standardized questionnaires and official academic records.

AQ was measured using the Adversity Response Profile (ARP) questionnaire. The instrument demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, with a validity score of 4.64 and a reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.703, indicating adequate internal consistency.

Independent learning was assessed using an Independent Learning Scale developed based on established theoretical indicators, including initiative, responsibility, self-confidence, discipline, and self-control. The instrument showed validity coefficients ranging from 0.371 to 0.727 and a high reliability coefficient of 0.908.

Students’ mathematics learning outcomes were obtained from official school records, specifically scores from the odd semester mathematics examination. These scores were used as objective indicators of students’ academic achievement in mathematics.

Instrument development and validation

The questionnaires were developed through a comprehensive review of relevant literature and existing validated instruments. Content validity was evaluated by experts in mathematics education and educational research methodology. Before the main data collection, pilot testing was conducted with a small group of students to assess clarity, readability, and feasibility.

Reliability analysis was performed using Cronbach’s alpha, and construct validity was examined through exploratory factor analysis. The results confirmed that the instruments reliably measured the intended constructs and were suitable for use in the main study.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using statistical software. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were used to describe the levels of AQ, independent learning, and mathematics achievement among students.

To examine the influence of AQ and independent learning on mathematics achievement, multiple linear regression analysis was employed. The regression model included AQ and independent learning as predictor variables and mathematics learning outcomes as the criterion variable.

Before regression analysis, several assumption tests were conducted to ensure model validity. These included tests of normality, linearity, multicollinearity, homoscedasticity, and autocorrelation. The results indicated that all assumptions required for multiple linear regression were satisfied, supporting the reliability of the analytical findings.

Discussion

The study’s results demonstrate a significant positive relationship between AQ and mathematics achievement, as well as between independent learning and mathematics achievement. These findings are consistent with previous research that highlights the importance of resilience and self-directed learning in academic success.[16] The study’s results also show that independent learning has a stronger effect on mathematics achievement compared to AQ, which suggests that students’ ability to take ownership of their learning is a crucial factor in their academic performance.

The findings of this study align with previous research emphasizing the role of non-cognitive factors in academic achievement. Prior studies have shown that students’ beliefs about intelligence and motivation significantly influence their academic performance.[17] Similarly, this study underscores the importance of AQ and independent learning as key factors contributing to mathematics achievement.

However, the study’s results also suggest that the relationship between AQ and mathematics achievement is not as strong as expected. This finding is consistent with some previous research that has found mixed results regarding the relationship between resilience and academic achievement.[18] Further research is needed to explore the complex relationships between AQ, independent learning, and mathematics achievement.

The study’s findings have implications for educators and policymakers. They suggest that interventions aimed at promoting resilience and independent learning skills can have a positive impact on students’ mathematics achievement.[19] Additionally, the study highlights the importance of providing students with opportunities to take ownership of their learning and develop self-directed learning skills.

The study’s findings have important implications for educators and classroom practices. One practical application is the incorporation of resilience-building activities that encourage students to step outside their comfort zones.[20] For example, teachers can design problem-solving tasks or collaborative group projects that require effective communication and perseverance, or establish a “resilience station” where students practice self-regulation and coping strategies. In addition, the findings support the promotion of independent learning by providing students with greater autonomy and choice in their learning processes, such as allowing them to select project topics or learning methods. Technology can further support self-directed learning through the use of online resources and learning management systems.[21] Differentiated instruction is also essential to address diverse learning needs and abilities; educators can offer scaffolding and targeted support for struggling learners while providing enrichment or extension activities for advanced students. Finally, effective feedback and assessment practices play a crucial role, as regular, constructive feedback helps students reflect on their learning and set improvement goals, while formative assessments enable teachers to monitor progress and adapt instruction to enhance learning outcomes.[22]

The study’s findings also have important theoretical implications for education policies and systems. Policies that emphasize student-centered learning and learner autonomy can support the development of independent learning skills and contribute to improved academic achievement. In addition, the findings highlight the importance of teacher professional development programs that focus on resilience-building and independent learning strategies, as these can strengthen instructional practices and positively influence student outcomes.[23] From a curriculum design perspective, integrating learning activities that promote resilience and autonomy, such as project-based learning and problem-solving tasks, can better align educational content with contemporary learning theories. Furthermore, assessment systems that prioritize higher-order thinking skills, including critical thinking and problem-solving, can encourage educators to shift their instructional focus toward fostering these essential competencies in students.[24]

By considering these implications, educators and policymakers can work together to create learning environments that foster resilience, independent learning, and academic achievement.

From a practical perspective, educators can enhance students’ AQ by incorporating resilience-based learning strategies, such as structured problem-solving tasks that challenge students to persist through difficulties.[25] For example, teachers can use growth mindset interventions to encourage students to view mistakes as learning opportunities. Schools may also implement peer mentoring programs, where students with higher AQ support those struggling with mathematics, fostering a collaborative and resilient learning environment. Limitation and recommendation

This study is the result of research conducted on senior high school students. In conducting this study, several limitations were encountered, such as the need to add a larger sample in the future. The limitation of the sample taken was that the number of students in the school who were the target of the study was also limited. While the problems in the schools that were raised in this study only occurred in these schools. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct research that covers a wider area with a larger sample. In addition, this research also still needs to be expanded to control demographic aspects such as gender, age, and level of education of parents because the level of education can be an indication of how parents assist children in their studies which of course cannot be separated from the pattern of parenting at home. This can lead to the success of students in carrying out their studies at school.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationship between AQ, independent learning, and mathematics learning outcomes among eighth-grade students. The findings indicate that students’ AQ, independent learning, and mathematics achievement were predominantly at a moderate level, with proportions of 64%, 74%, and 84%, respectively. Statistical analysis demonstrated that AQ had a positive and significant effect on mathematics learning outcomes, contributing 10% to the explained variance, while independent learning showed a stronger positive effect, contributing 33.9%. Collectively, AQ and independent learning accounted for 43.9% of the variance in students’ mathematics achievement, indicating that these psychological factors play a substantial role in academic performance.

The results further confirm that independent learning is a more influential predictor of mathematics achievement than AQ, although both variables contribute meaningfully. These findings underscore the importance of fostering students’ resilience and learning autonomy as part of mathematics instruction. Students who demonstrate higher levels of perseverance and self-directed learning tend to achieve better academic outcomes in mathematics.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. The research was conducted in a single school, which limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the cross-sectional design captured students’ learning characteristics at a single point in time and did not allow for examination of developmental changes or long-term effects.

Future research is recommended to employ longitudinal designs to explore how AQ and independent learning evolve over time and how they influence sustained academic achievement. Experimental or intervention-based studies could also be conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of resilience-building programs and self-regulated learning strategies in improving mathematics performance. Expanding research across different schools, regions, and cultural contexts would further strengthen the understanding of the role of these psychological factors in mathematics education.

Overall, this study highlights the significance of non-cognitive factors in mathematics learning and supports the integration of strategies that promote resilience and independent learning within classroom practice. By addressing these factors, educators and policymakers can contribute to the development of more effective and supportive learning environments that enhance students’ academic success.
