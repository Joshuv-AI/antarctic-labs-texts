Hardware in context

Many chemical reactions exchange measurable amounts of heat with their environment, and the study of this exchange is fundamental to understanding chemical processes, giving rise to the field of thermochemistry. This field is essential in the education of chemistry, engineering, and life sciences students [1], [2], [3]. Teaching thermochemistry requires a system that allows the reaction under study to be carried out under controlled conditions and thermally isolated from the external environment. Traditionally, this is accomplished in thermal containers in which the chemical reaction (system) takes place in an aqueous environment (surroundings) whose temperature is monitored using an analog thermometer. Traditionally, this requires manual temperature recording during continuous agitation, a process that can be tedious and that is prone to measurement errors due to irregular readings and inconsistent stirring. Additionally, traditional manual thermometry suffers from methodological limitations such as limited temporal resolution, susceptibility to reading and transcription errors, and lack of automated data logging [1]. While the historical use of mercury thermometers poses well-known safety concerns [4], [5], even non-toxic alternatives such as alcohol thermometers do not overcome these fundamental limitations. Accidents from spilling reactants are also common when agitation is done manually. High-precision digital equipment exists that avoids the use of mercury thermometers, but their cost is prohibitive for educational settings, where multiple pieces of equipment are also needed to allow greater practical student participation. In response, multiple attempts have been made to build calorimeters based on thermal cups (coffee-cup type) and monitored by electronic sensors [6], [7], [8]. Some of these lack agitation, depend on relatively expensive materials or software, or employ an external aquarium pump for stirring [9]; the latter prevents the system from remaining closed and thus requires explicit consideration of mass and energy exchange with the environment in the calorimetric analysis.

From an experimental standpoint, the quality of thermometric measurements also depends on the temporal response of the measuring system, which is governed by both the temperature sensor and the stirring conditions. Adequate stirring promotes rapid homogenization of the solution and reduces local temperature gradients, while appropriate thermal coupling between the sensor and the liquid phase enables timely detection of temperature changes. Different transient behaviors can therefore be observed depending on how these factors are balanced in a given setup.

In the present work, a closed and well-insulated reaction vessel with integrated magnetic stirring was selected to address these considerations in a pragmatic manner. This design minimizes uncontrolled exchanges with the environment over the time scale of the experiment and provides a stable and reproducible thermometric response, while maintaining simplicity, low cost, and suitability for teaching and exploratory laboratory settings.

This study presents the construction and validation of a digital calorimeter obtained by repurposing a commercial thermally insulated container with integrated magnetic stirring and incorporating a digital temperature monitoring and data logging system based on an Arduino UNO programmable board. The design addresses four objectives: (i) reducing costs compared to commercial equipment, (ii) improving precision and reproducibility in educational experiments, (iii) promoting active learning through hands-on student engagement with assembly, calibration, and operation of the instrument, and (iv) enhancing accessibility for teaching laboratories. Accessibility is achieved through the use of low-cost, widely available components and open source hardware and software, making the device suitable for laboratories with limited resources.

Arduino-based calorimeters have recently been proposed as low-cost alternatives for teaching thermochemistry. For example, Prabowo et al. [2] developed the ChemDuino calorimeter to determine the enthalpy change of acid–base neutralization reactions, demonstrating that reliable values can be obtained using a simple DIY setup. However, their design relies on disposable paper cups and a tumbler, with manual mixing and exposed electronic components, limiting its robustness and reproducibility for repeated use in educational laboratories. In contrast, our device repurposes a commercial thermal container with integrated magnetic stirring, ensuring a closed system with better thermal insulation and homogeneous mixing. Furthermore, the electronics are housed in a 3D-printed enclosure, making the device more durable, while the modular architecture of our system allows the integration of additional sensors (pH, conductivity, pressure), expanding its applicability beyond simple neutralization experiments.

Several previous studies have implemented digital temperature sensors and programmable boards [2], [6], [7], demonstrating that good precision and accuracy can be achieved with these components. However, these designs require complete device construction and, in some cases, include elements such as screens that may limit their applicability. In our work, we chose to utilize a commercial thermal container that included magnetic stirring and developed open source software for data acquisition and equipment control, connecting it via USB cable to a computer with Linux or Windows operating system for data visualization and subsequent post-processing.

High-precision digital calorimetric equipment that avoids the risks associated with mercury thermometers does exist, but costs are prohibitive for teaching laboratories. Professional-grade systems such as the IKA C 1 Package (ca. US $18,000–20,000), the IKA C 6000 series (ca. US $55,000–65,000), and calorimeters from Parr Instrument Company or TA Instruments, typically ranging from US $15,000 to over US $100,000 depending on model and capabilities, offer excellent precision and automation. However, these instruments remain inaccessible to most educational institutions that require multiple units for student laboratory sessions.

At the other extreme, basic educational calorimeter kits are available in the range of US $50–500. These include simple “coffee-cup” calorimeters or student laboratory sets widely marketed for introductory chemistry education. While affordable, such kits lack digital monitoring, automated data acquisition, and precise temperature control, limiting their usefulness for modern experimental training. More recent educational developments have also explored reusable alternatives to traditional coffee-cup calorimeters, such as low-cost 3D-printed containers with improved system closure and durability, while maintaining the accessibility and pedagogical focus required for teaching laboratories [8].

This creates a significant gap between rudimentary educational tools and professional instrumentation—a gap directly addressed by our US $30 Arduino-based digital calorimeter, which combines affordability with digital data acquisition and reproducibility suitable for classroom use.

Commercial solution calorimeters, such as the Parr 6755, employ precision thermistors and achieve high temperature resolution and accuracy after calibration. Data acquisition in these instruments relies on digitally logged measurements with fixed logging intervals defined by the instrument software. Educational kits, such as the Vernier Go Direct Temperature Probe, use a thermistor-based digital probe and a foam-cup calorimeter. In this context, a complete experimental setup requires additional components, including a thermally insulated container and a stirring system, which are already integrated in the proposed device.

For the developed device, the implemented sampling rate of approximately 1 Hz is sufficient to resolve temperature transients associated with typical dissolution and neutralization reactions.

The main limitations of the present calorimeter are related to its lower temperature resolution and simpler thermal insulation when compared to commercial instruments. Consequently, the device is not intended to replace research-grade calorimeters designed for high-precision or microcalorimetric measurements. Nevertheless, the validation results demonstrate that, after calibration, the system provides reproducible and quantitatively meaningful results for isobaric processes involving moderate heat exchange, making it suitable for educational and low-cost experimental laboratory settings.

Low-cost calorimeters not only democratize access to thermal experimentation, but also serve as vehicles for teaching electronics, programming, and experimental design in an interdisciplinary context. Our open-architecture device allows combining the teaching of both the physical chemistry principles involved in thermochemical determinations and the analytical principles it requires, as well as teaching laboratory automation in the sciences. We believe this training is necessary and increasingly needs to be done earlier due to the growing automation of tasks in research laboratories [10], [11]. The combination of these qualities fosters students’ creative capacity to develop new ways of executing their experiments. This is evidenced by the growing number of studies aimed at incorporating the use of programmable boards, such as Arduino, in natural science education [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17].

Hardware description

The developed calorimeter combines low-cost commercial components with digital data acquisition technology for educational calorimetric applications. The design is based on repurposing a commercial 380 mL thermal container as an adiabatic container, taking advantage of its inherent thermal insulation properties and its commercial availability at low cost. This approach fundamentally differs from traditional teaching calorimeters that require analog thermometers and manual data recording, as well as from professional systems that are prohibitively expensive for educational institutions.

The container used for equipment construction is a commercial thermal container with lid and magnetic stirring. This allows it to remain closed while the chemical reaction occurs inside.

The main hardware customization lies in the integration of a Dallas DS18B20 digital temperature sensor with an Arduino UNO board, creating an automated monitoring system that eliminates human reading errors and allows continuous data recording. The sensor is used in its waterproof stainless-steel encapsulated version, which places the sensing element in direct thermal contact with the liquid sample while providing mechanical and electrical protection. This configuration allows the sensor to be submerged in the reaction medium and positioned inside the calorimetric container, away from the container walls, to measure the temperature of the bulk solution. The electrical connections between the DS18B20 sensor and the Arduino UNO are deliberately simple and are illustrated schematically and photographically in Fig. 1. Specifically, the sensor is connected using a standard three-wire configuration: VCC to the Arduino 5 V pin, GND to ground, and the data line to a digital input pin (D2 in our implementation), with a 4.7 kΩ pull-up resistor placed between VCC and the data line.

A notable feature of the DS18B20 is its flexible configuration capability in terms of thermal resolution and response speed. The sensor allows selection between four different resolution levels: 9, 10, 11, and 12 bits, which correspond to precisions of ±0.5 °C, ±0.25 °C, ±0.125 °C, and ±0.0625 °C respectively. This flexibility involves a trade-off between precision and response speed, as conversion times vary from 93.75 ms for 9-bit resolution to 750 ms for 12-bit resolution. This characteristic allows the sensor to be adapted according to specific experimental needs: high-speed configurations for rapid monitoring with lower precision, or high-precision configurations for critical measurements where accuracy is a priority.

For educational calorimeter applications, the DS18B20 presents significant advantages such as easy connection requiring only one digital pin from the Arduino, reading stability, configuration flexibility, low cost, and elimination of analog noise thanks to its digital interface. In our calorimeter, we use the default 12-bit configuration (±0.0625 °C, 750 ms) to obtain maximum available precision, although the conversion time is acceptable for typical thermal dynamics in educational calorimetry experiments.

The developed software that accompanies the device provides functionalities such as automatic data logging in CSV format, real time graphical visualization, and export capability for subsequent analysis.

The open source character of the design allows future adaptations according to different available containers, facilitating scalability and customization according to specific institutional resources. The modular architecture of the electronic system enables the incorporation of additional sensors (pH, conductivity, pressure) for more complex experiments, establishing an expandable platform for teaching-oriented scientific instrumentation. The integration of automatic magnetic stirring eliminates variability associated with manual stirring, improving experimental reproducibility compared to traditional methods.

Software

The calorimeter software consists of two components: first, the Arduino UNO firmware (firmware.ino) that reports temperature readings from the Dallas DS18B20 sensor via the serial port; second, the PC-side scripts (gui.py and calorimeter_support.py), both written in Python and expected to reside in the same directory on the connected computer. For Linux and macOS systems, execution of the Python scripts requires a standard Python 3 environment and installation of the required third-party libraries. The required Python dependencies and step-by-step installation instructions are explicitly documented in the repository (see README and requirements file). This approach preserves cross-platform compatibility while keeping the manuscript concise. Optionally, these scripts can be executed in a Windows environment using the calorimeter.exe file, which is available in the repository along with the necessary dependencies for proper execution.

Prior to uploading the Arduino sketch, two external libraries must be installed: OneWire and DallasTemperature. These libraries can be installed directly from the Arduino IDE by navigating to Sketch → Include Library → Manage Libraries. First, search for and install the OneWire library, followed by the DallasTemperature library, using the Install button in the Library Manager.

The calorimeter_support.py file captures the readings received from the Arduino board through the serial port; the port to which the board is connected is automatically detected. The gui.py file generates a graphical user interface that allows operation of the calorimeter, including starting and stopping data acquisition, saving data in CSV format, navigating the graph, and clearing it (Fig. 2). Temperature readings are displayed in real time. The generated CSV files can subsequently be imported into spreadsheet software for data processing. Inline comments have been added to the Arduino firmware and Python scripts to clarify their structure and main functional blocks.

Graphical user interface of the calorimeter: (1) Start/stop data acquisition, (2) Clear graph, (3) Save time and temperature data (CSV), (4) Reset zoom, (5) Current sensor temperature, (6) Detected COM port, (7) Exit interface.

The Dallas DS18B20 sensor offers adjustable configurations in both reading speed and measurement resolution. According to the manufacturer, this device can be configured with four different resolution levels: 9, 10, 11, and 12 bits, which correspond to precisions of ± 0.5 °C, ±0.25 °C, ±0.125 °C, and ± 0.0625 °C, respectively. The following conversion times correspond to the selected resolution:

9 bits: 93.75 ms.

10 bits: 187.5 ms.

11 bits: 375 ms.

12 bits: 750 ms.

In our specific case, the sensor’s default configuration was used, which corresponds to a 12-bit resolution with a precision of ±0.0625 °C and a conversion time of 750 ms. This configuration provides the maximum available precision of the sensor, although it requires longer processing time to complete each measurement; this time is acceptable for the calorimeter’s intended operation. To select a different resolution, users can modify the Arduino firmware (firmware.ino) as indicated in the source code comments.

Build instructions

Selection of the thermal container

A commercial thermally-insulated mug with a capacity of 380 mL was selected as the base container (item 1), leveraging its thermal insulation properties and low-cost commercial availability. The specific thermally insulated mug used in this work corresponds to the model available at the time of device construction. As with many low-cost consumer products, commercial availability may vary over time. Importantly, the calorimeter design is not tied to a unique container model. The adapter components are provided as open-source design files, including an editable STEP file. This allows users to readily adapt the geometry to alternative thermally insulated mugs of similar size and shape, thus ensuring long-term reproducibility of the device. The chosen model includes a built-in magnetic stirring system, which enables agitation while keeping the container physically closed and is rechargeable via a USB port. Equivalent commercially available self-stirring thermal mugs with similar capacity and insulation properties may be used.

Preparation of the container lid

The lid of the thermal container was perforated to allow insertion of the temperature sensor. The perforation was made at a position approximately 13 mm from the outer edge of the lid. This position was selected to avoid physical interference with the magnetic stirrer while ensuring that the sensing tip of the sensor remains fully immersed in the liquid during operation.

Installation and sealing of the temperature sensor

A waterproof Dallas DS18B20 temperature sensor (item 6) was inserted through the perforation in the lid. After positioning the sensor, the opening was sealed using hot-melt adhesive to mechanically secure the sensor and minimize vapor and heat exchange with the environment. The final position of the sensor and the perforation location are depicted in Fig. 1.

Electrical connections of the sensor

The DS18B20 sensor was connected to an Arduino UNO board (item 4) using a standard three-wire configuration. The sensor’s VCC terminal was connected to the Arduino 5 V pin, the GND terminal to ground, and the data line to a digital input pin (D2 in our implementation). A 4.7 kΩ pull-up resistor (item 5) was placed between the VCC and data lines. These connections were implemented on an experimental PCB using standard electrical cables (items 2 and 3), as shown schematically and photographically in Fig. 1, Fig. 3.

(a) General internal and external appearance (b, c) of the calorimeter, using an Arduino UNO programmable board.

Assembly of the electronic enclosure

The Arduino UNO board and the experimental PCB were placed inside a 3D-printed enclosure manufactured from PLA filament (items 7, 9, and 10). The enclosure consists of a top and bottom case and an adapter piece designed to attach the enclosure to the thermal container. The STL files required to print these parts are provided in the associated repository.

Attachment of the enclosure to the thermal container

The assembled enclosure was permanently attached to the external wall of the thermal container using hot-melt adhesive. Other equivalent fixing methods, such as epoxy adhesive or industrial double-sided tape, may also be used. An exploded assembly view illustrating the enclosure components and their attachment to the thermal container is provided in Fig. 4.

Connection to the computer

To connect the calorimeter to a personal computer, a USB Type B cable (item 8) was used. This type of connector provides a more stable mechanical connection when handled by students during laboratory sessions.

Such experiments were proposed for the experimental course on Physical Chemistry for Biochemistry majors at the Faculty of Science of the University of the Republic (Uruguay). Students determined the calorimeter’s CP by dissolving an accurately weighed amount of potassium chloride (KCl) in water, whose ΔH is well-known. They subsequently used this CP value to determine the enthalpies of dissolution of two substances of biochemical interest: urea and guanidinium chloride, two common protein denaturants.

The main experimental issues when carrying out calorimetric analysis are related to the adiabaticity of the physical system and the sensor response speed. The lack of adiabaticity can be inferred from deviations from ideal behavior. Under ideal conditions, the initial and final temperature regions would remain constant (zero slope). In contrast, the regions marked as “a” in Fig. 5 show a positive slope, indicating a net heat flow into the calorimetric system due to heat exchange with the environment. On the other hand, the time delay in reaching the final temperature (marked as “b” in Fig. 5) can be attributed to insufficient agitation, slow dissolution of the substance, or a poor response of the temperature sensor. Additionally, another feature in the thermograms (denoted as “c” in Fig. 5) is attributed to a local overheating or overcooling of the solution in the vicinity of the sensor.

In our design, the agitation is performed by the integrated magnetic stirrer of the commercial thermal container, which operates at a fixed speed defined by the manufacturer. The rotational speed is not specified by the manufacturer and is not user-adjustable. To provide an order-of-magnitude estimate of the stirring speed, the rotation of the magnetic impeller was evaluated by high-speed video analysis (240 fps) using a visual reference mark. Frame-by-frame analysis yielded a stirring rate on the order of 4–5 × 103 rpm under typical operating conditions. Nevertheless, this level of agitation is sufficient to ensure rapid thermal homogenization of the solution and effective dissolution of the substance. As derived from Fig. 6, no evidence of lack of adiabaticity or overcooling can be seen, and the magnetic agitation is adequate to ensure thermal equilibrium in the time lapse of the experiments. However, a time delay of ca. 30 s is evident and can be attributed to a combination of factors, such as the sensor response speed and the time required for the chemical reaction and the system to reach thermal equilibrium.

Considering the system’s total heat capacity (≈700 J °C−1 using 150 g of water), the observed baseline stability, and the digital sensor resolution (0.0625 °C), the estimated detection limit of the calorimeter is on the order of 100 J. This corresponds to a temperature change of ≈0.14 °C, which is approximately twice the sensor resolution and represents the smallest temperature variation that can be reliably distinguished in practice.

Concluding remarks

We presented a low-cost, open-hardware isobaric calorimeter based on a repurposed commercial thermal mug, an Arduino UNO board, and open source software. The device enables automated temperature acquisition, real-time visualization, and data export, providing a robust and reproducible platform for teaching thermochemistry in undergraduate laboratory courses.

Validation experiments demonstrate that the system yields dissolution and neutralization enthalpies consistent with literature values, while maintaining simplicity, low cost, and ease of replication. Although the developed calorimeter does not aim to replace research-grade commercial instruments, it effectively bridges the gap between rudimentary educational kits and expensive professional equipment. The open architecture of the hardware and software further allows customization and extension, supporting interdisciplinary training in chemistry, electronics, and laboratory automation.

Safety considerations

The device operates at low voltage (5 V) and does not involve hazardous materials or open electrical components. It poses no specific safety risks beyond standard laboratory handling.
