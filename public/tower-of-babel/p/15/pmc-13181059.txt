Design of a photonic bolometric sensor using microring resonators for high-energy radiation detection
Authors: Morteza Maleki, Giuseppe Brunetti, Caterina Ciminelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonitoring various types of space radiation is critical in space missions, as such radiation cannot be effectively detected from ground-based stations. Picosatellites and CubeSats are increasingly used for space research, including the measurement of gamma radiation from terrestrial gamma-ray flashes and gamma-ray bursts, whereas these platforms are limited in payload capacity. In this paper, we propose and theoretically demonstrate a novel integrable photonic structure for space radiation detection, working based on resonance shifts in micro-ring resonators induced by thermal energy deposition. The device is predicted to detect energy depositions exceeding ~ 50 keV, limited by predicted thermal noise equivalent power (NEPth), with a sensitivity of 1.52 fm/MeV at 100 MeV deposited energy. Using the intensity interrogation method employing a 16-bit analog-to-digital converter, a resolution of 74.7 keV could be achieved. Among various candidate materials evaluated for the absorbing layer, gold was selected due to its high efficiency and compatibility with standard photonic fabrication processes. The proposed sensor can exhibit an absorption efficiency ranging from over 1% to nearly 100% for gamma-ray photons in the 10–270 keV range. The same design can also be adapted for detecting high-energy electrons, and, with suitable modifications in the absorption mechanism, may be extended to other types of space radiation. Integrating such compact and lightweight sensors can significantly enhance the detection capabilities of smart satellites in monitoring diverse radiation sources in space, and theoretical studies of the proposed design demonstrate its potential for future implementation in practical space applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39369-8.Keywords: Radiation sensor, Gamma radiation, High-energy particles, Micro-ring resonator, Photonic sensorsSubject terms: Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Space radiation can be categorized into three main sources: particles trapped within Earth’s magnetic field, particles emitted during solar flares or solar particle events (SPEs), and galactic cosmic rays (GCRs)1. From a particle-type perspective, space radiation includes charged particles (such as high-energy protons, alpha particles, heavy ions, and electrons), neutral particles (e.g., neutrons), and photons (primarily gamma rays). A significant portion of the charged radiation is deflected or trapped by Earth’s magnetic field, either absorbed by the atmosphere, making ground-based detection ineffective. Consequently, satellite-based detection becomes essential for comprehensive radiation monitoring. However, the size, weight, and power consumption (SWaP) constraints of onboard instruments, especially on pico-satellites and CubeSats, limit the number and type of detectors that can be deployed. This limitation raises the question of whether integrated detectors can reduce SWaP and cost (SWaP-C) while maintaining adequate sensitivity and functionality.

Gamma radiation (γ-rays) is particularly challenging to detect due to its high penetration depth2, which necessitates detector materials with high atomic numbers and thickness, both of which add weight to the system3. This requirement significantly impacts the payload capabilities of CubeSats, often necessitating dedicated satellite missions for γ-ray detection4. Terrestrial gamma ray flashes (TGFs) emit gamma photons in the energy range of approximately 10 keV to 40 MeV5,6, while background space γ-radiation can extend up to 1 TeV. Nevertheless, photon flux for energies above 10 MeV is typically below 1 photon/cm²·min·sr7. This suggests that, by directing the satellite’s field of view toward a TGF or a γ-ray burst (GRB) event, background radiation can be considered negligible, provided that sufficient photons are captured. Nonetheless, the inherently low flux of TGFs and GRBs necessitates high-sensitivity detection methods.

γ-ray photons interact with matter through several mechanisms, primarily ionizing interactions such as the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and pair production3,8, which typically require high photon energies and sufficient interaction lengths within the material. At lower energies, coherent scattering may occur, while in the medium-energy range (0.1 to 10 MeV), Compton scattering becomes dominant. At energies exceeding 1.022 MeV, pair production may take place, generating electron-positron pairs that eventually annihilate. These interactions deposit electromagnetic energy in various forms, which can be exploited for detection purposes.

Traditional γ-ray detectors rely on these interactions and include gas-filled detectors, scintillators, and solid-state devices3. Gas-filled detectors require bulky chambers and high-voltage systems, while scintillators emit low-energy photons that photodetectors must read4, often needing bulky photomultiplier tubes or silicon photomultipliers3,9. Solid-state detectors are prone to degradation under high radiation doses and typically operate effectively only within low portions of the γ-ray spectrum. Furthermore, they may struggle under harsh environmental conditions.

It has been widely reported that electronic systems degrade significantly under prolonged γ-ray exposure10–13. In contrast, optical fibers and photonic devices demonstrate far greater radiation hardness2,14,15, making them strong candidates for integrated radiation sensors with long lifetimes and stable performance. Thus, there is a growing interest in developing compact, lightweight, and highly sensitive photonic sensors that align with the stringent SWaP-C requirements of modern satellite missions.

Photonic micro-ring resonators (MRRs) have shown excellent performance and radiation hardness in response to the gamma radiation2. Additionally, they are widely used for sensing purposes16, including temperature sensing17. This is owing to their resonance susceptibility to the optical properties of their surrounding environment. This unique synergy, where MRR structures themselves survive and remain functional in harsh radiation fields while simultaneously translating subtle external changes into a precisely quantifiable optical signal, allows them to potentially serve as robust radiation detectors in harsh environments, like space.

In this work, we propose a proof-of-concept photonic sensor for detecting γ-rays and potentially other types of space radiation, intended for integration into CubeSats and smart satellite platforms to monitor GRBs and TGFs (Fig. 1). Drawing inspiration from bolometric principles, this device captures thermal energy resulting from photon interactions.

By adapting the absorption mechanism to different particle types, the same structure can be reconfigured to detect various forms of high-energy radiation.

Proposed structure

The proposed structure, illustrated in Fig. 1, consists of a silicon MRR array, each with a radius of 5 μm and a thickness of 220 nm, to be fabricated on a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer and transferred to a 1 μm-thick SiO2 membrane. MRRs and waveguides are designed with a width of 0.5 μm to support a single-mode TE-polarized wave, operating at the C-band communication wavelength range (~ 1550 nm), which are passivated by a 1 μm SiO2 layer (see Fig. 1b). A high-Z metallic absorption layer is deposited on top of the MRR to absorb γ-ray photons. When interacting with incoming radiation, energy is deposited as heat and spreads over the MRR and surrounding SiO2 layers. As shown in the following sections, this energy causes a uniform temperature increase in the ring resonator within approximately 10 µs. The resulting variation in the effective refractive index leads to a shift in the resonance wavelength of the MRR.

Geometrical parameters of the proposed device are summarized in Table 1. A small ring radius ensures a high free spectral range (FSR), minimizes device footprint, and improves the device’s thermal response time. Higher spatial resolution and enhanced thermal sensitivity make smaller MRRs ideal for detecting both space-borne and terrestrial radiation events. It is well established that silicon MRRs with radii ≥ 5 μm exhibit negligible bending losses16, making the chosen radius suitable for this application. The membrane and the passivation layer thicknesses are selected to be practically feasible to implement, while keeping the mode confined. The width and height of the support pillars assure high thermal resistance, while keeping dimensions similar to conventional bolometers. The waveguide width is chosen based on standard single-mode SOI waveguides, whereas the distances between the waveguides are carefully tuned to decrease unwanted coupling between structures. Bending radius and waveguides’ distance from membrane edges were carefully tuned to decrease losses and provide the optical mode confinement. Finally, the width and length of the membrane and the input/output supports, presented in Table 1, are functions of assumed dimensions and considerations explained above.

The device array is designed such that each signal path, from inputs to the output waveguide, includes the same number of waveguide crossings. For a row of N devices, each signal path comprises N-1 crossings. Lateral couplers route the output signals into a shared output bus, while inputs are addressed via a demultiplexer. As a result, all devices experience equivalent optical losses, allowing uniform and accurate resonance shift measurements. By employing a high-frequency demultiplexer operating at frequency fs for switching the input signals and monitoring the common output waveguide, the resonance of all N devices can be dynamically scanned within a time window Ts = N/fs. Moreover, both the input and output waveguides are placed on the same side of the chip, facilitating cleaving and coupling during integration with readout systems or the packaging process. This consideration makes it possible to excite the waveguides from an external light source or laser, through edge coupling. A potential fabrication process is proposed for realizing the device in the supplementary information file.

Simulation considerations and method

In this study, the proposed structure is analyzed from multiple physical perspectives. The absorption behavior of the high-Z metallic layer, under γ-ray irradiation, was evaluated using the Geant4 simulation toolkit (version 11.2.2)18. A heat transfer model, developed using the finite element method (FEM), assessed the thermal response of the device to the thermal energy released after radiation absorption. The results were then used to estimate variations in the effective refractive index of the waveguides and were incorporated into the MRR basic equations to calculate the corresponding resonance shifts in the MRR. Finally, a structural mechanics model was employed to assess the mechanical stability of the device.

Below, the simulation assumptions are presented, followed by the results and discussions in the next sections.

For this purpose, a model using the Geant4 toolkit was implemented18. A total of 1,000,000 γ-ray (and electron) particles were simulated, interacting with the absorbing layers at normal incidence. The absorbing materials analyzed include gold, silver, tungsten, lead, copper, and aluminium. These materials were selected for their high atomic numbers and relevance in photonic integration. The total deposited energy was computed across a wide energy spectrum range to determine the spectral response.

The simulation defines a photon-sensitive volume corresponding to the metal thin film (with dimensions adapted based on Table 1). The incident particles are distributed randomly across the surface, with direction perpendicular to the layer, ensuring the shortest traversal path, representing a conservative, worst-case scenario for energy deposition.

The QBBC physics list was used for γ-ray interactions (and FTFP-BERT for electron modeling, discussed in the following) in Geant4. These physics list(s) account(s) for particle-matter interactions, complex secondary effects, and multiple scattering processes, and balance(s) the models for low to medium and high energy physical interactions. Notably, the number of interaction events can exceed the number of incident particles due to secondary emissions and interactions. However, the net deposited energy may decrease for high-energy photons, as a portion escapes or is re-emitted.

The simulation outputs include the total deposited energy vs. incident energy, the spectral absorption efficiency across materials, and the number of interaction events (primary and secondary).

The results allow material performance comparison across the relevant spectrum and assess the detector’s operational energy range and absorber design.

Thermal analysis

As with conventional bolometers, the proposed structure is assumed to be thermally anchored to a fixed-temperature substrate maintained at 25 °C, potentially stabilized using a thermoelectric cooler. This setup ensures that the temperature increase in the suspended MRRs can effectively shift the resonance before heat dissipates. The input/output ports of the MRRs are also assumed to be at 25 °C, due to thermal conduction through the connected bulk photonic waveguide bus. Time-dependent heat transfer simulations were performed, assuming that all other boundaries are non-conductive and dissipate heat only via thermal radiation. The thermal emissivity of SiO2 and gold at 300 K was set to 0.79 and ~ 0.06, respectively20,21. Although these assumptions may appear simplified, investigations showed that air convection, natural thermal conduction through air, and surface-to-surface radiation have negligible effects on the thermal dynamics of the structure. These effects mainly influence the cool-down time and have minimal impact on peak temperature rise. It is worth noting that the SiO2 supporting pillars exhibit much lower thermal conductivity compared to the metallic contacts typically used in bolometers (e.g., aluminum).

As a result, the proposed design reduces thermal noise, where vacuum packaging, common in bolometric devices, can further mitigate convective losses.

Mode analysis and MRR modelling

Following thermal simulations, the average temperature increase in the single MRR was used to evaluate changes in the effective refractive index (Δneff) of the waveguides. The model considers silicon waveguides surrounded by 1 μm of SiO2 above and below, as well as air/vacuum below the membrane and a gold absorber above. Such a thickness (~λr/nSiO2) assures a well-confined wave inside the waveguides, even where a thick metal layer does exist at the top of the cladding. Refractive indices and thermo-optic coefficients were sourced from literature for silicon and SiO222–25, while the Johnson–Christy model was used for gold26.

Mechanical analysis

Due to the high density of the absorbing metallic layer, ensuring the mechanical stability of the suspended structure is essential. If the structure’s mechanical resonance frequencies are well above the vibrational levels experienced during launch, the device will be mechanically reliable in space applications.

A structural eigenfrequency analysis was performed by using FEM mode analysis to calculate the device’s resonance modes and assess its stability under spaceborne mechanical stress.

Results

γ-ray absorption

The absorption efficiency of various materials was evaluated through simulations using the Geant4 model described earlier. Figure 2 shows the γ-ray absorption efficiency of different high-Z metallic films, each with a thickness of 10 μm, compared to a 6 μm SiO2 layer representing the maximum thickness of the passive bus supports and passivation.

γ-ray absorption efficiency of different high-Z metals with a thickness of 10 μm, and a 6 μm SiO2 layer.

The absorbing materials tested include gold, silver, tungsten, lead, copper, and aluminum, all of which are relevant for photonic integration or known for high γ-ray interaction cross-sections. Among the candidates, gold (Au) consistently demonstrated the highest absorption efficiency across a wide spectral range, outperforming even tungsten (W) at energies above 1 MeV. This makes gold an excellent choice for integration in photonic devices, not only for its performance but also for its compatibility with standard CMOS-compatible processes. Interestingly, lead (Pb), widely used in industrial γ-shielding, showed lower absorption efficiency than gold and tungsten for photon energies above ~ 1 MeV. This is primarily attributed to its lower density and less favorable electron density (Z·ρ/A) when compared on a thin-film basis. Similarly, silver (Ag) and copper (Cu) displayed an order-of-magnitude lower absorption than gold for Eγ > 0.1 MeV, while aluminum (Al) showed the poorest performance overall, with minimal interaction across the entire spectrum. Despite its low absorption, aluminum can still play a secondary shielding role, protecting the sensor from charged particles, X-rays, or micro-debris, without significantly interfering with γ-ray detection.

The observed rapid increases in absorption efficiency near specific energies are due to K-edge absorption thresholds, where inner-shell electron transitions occur. These K-edges are characteristic of each element and appear at ~ 88 keV for lead, ~ 80.7 keV for gold, ~ 69 keV for tungsten, and ~ 25.5 keV for silver. These could contribute to the selection of optimal materials for a given radiation energy range of interest. The simulations also included the SiO2 layer used in the photonic structure. The two- to three-orders of magnitude lower absorption of SiO2 compared to metallic absorbers confirms its negligible impact on γ-ray energy capture. This result supports the use of quartz glass windows in the packaging, which would allow γ-rays to pass while maintaining device isolation. Additionally, the silicon waveguides themselves are extremely thin (220 nm) and occupy a small surface area. As such, any energy deposited in the waveguides can be safely neglected for thermal sensing.

Thermal simulation results

The most critical challenge in simulating and analyzing the thermal response of the proposed device was modeling the instantaneous energy absorption in the radiation–matter interactions. To address this, a Gaussian temporal function was employed to simulate the heat release over time, enabling the control and optimization of both time variance and simulation step size. The total energy, which equals the integration of the released power in the considered time interval, is set to the photon energy of interest. Therefore, the peak power could simply be determined according to the variance of the Gaussian function.

Investigations revealed that a Gaussian variance of 1 µs is sufficient to model the heat distribution with adequate resolution. However, time steps of the time-dependent solver should be reduced near the energy peak to maintain numerical stability and fidelity. We relate the time steps inversely to the power magnitude; therefore, when approaching the peak value, the time steps reduce. Our simulations revealed that a minimum time step as small as approximately 2 ps provides both reliable and computationally cost-effective models. After the peak, time steps were progressively increased to minimize computational load. A unit cell of the proposed structure was simulated using the developed heat transfer model, where a point heat source was used to emulate the energy deposition from a single interaction.

Figure 3a shows the maximum (Tmax) and minimum (Tmin) temperature values over time for an energy deposition of 100 MeV. The temperature difference (ΔT) within the ring was also evaluated. On a logarithmic time scale, the data show that the temperature becomes uniform across the ring within ~ 10 µs, with ΔT becoming two orders of magnitude smaller than Tmax. This uniform temperature distribution validates that, following a single interaction, it is reasonable to assume the ring as a quasi-homogeneous structure. Thus, effective refractive variations over the structure could be calculated simply by solving the eigenmodes for the waveguide’s cross section (shown as the inset in Fig. 3a). Figure 3b illustrates the device’s thermal response for various absorbed energies. The cool-down time is consistently below 10 ms, ensuring that the system can fully return to baseline temperature between consecutive events, with enough time for the processing unit(s) to register and follow the events. This is particularly important for detecting TGFs, which typically last from sub-milliseconds to a few milliseconds6. Hence, the cumulative energy deposited during a burst remains detectable within the thermal integration window of the sensor. For instance, in a 10 × 10 array, read out using a 10 MHz scanning frequency, the resonance shifts of all components can be sampled 100 times within the thermal response window, allowing dynamic event tracking.

Thermal response of the MRR: (a) Tmax, Tmin, and ΔT for 100 MeV deposited point energy, representing an instant interaction. (b) Tmax for various energy deposition. The photon energy released as a Gaussian function of time is shown by Eph in these figures.

Importantly, simulations also confirmed that larger MRRs lead to slower responses and lower peak temperatures. Specifically, for RMRR = 30 μm, the cool-down time increases to ~ 50–60 ms, and Tmax is reduced by one order of magnitude compared to the 5 μm radius case. While a larger area increases the incident energy flux, this only partially compensates for the thermal inertia.

The choice of materials for the membrane and support pillars has a substantial impact on performance. Materials with higher thermal conductivity reduce cool-down times but also limit Tmax, potentially lowering sensitivity. Therefore, a design trade-off must be made based on the desired time resolution and temperature sensitivity, especially when devices with higher radii and high temporal resolution are needed. In this design, SiO2 was chosen due to its low thermal conductivity, which ensures higher temperature rises and acts as a thermal insulator, crucial for bolometric operation. Notably, for small-radius rings (RMRR ≤ 10 μm), the supporting pillars have minimal influence on heat sinking due to their small cross-section and the low conductivity of SiO₂. However, they remain essential for mechanical stability, especially in larger devices.

Finally, simulations confirmed a linear relationship between deposited energy and Tmax. This linearity is advantageous for calibration and broadens the applicability of the detector to both narrow- and wide-spectrum radiation environments.

Photo-thermal sensitivity

The increase in temperature resulting from energy deposition leads to a change in the effective refractive index (neff) of the optical modes propagating in the silicon waveguide. These changes, in turn, produce a shift in the resonant wavelength (Δλr) of the micro-ring resonator, which forms the core of the sensing mechanism.

Figure 4a illustrates the variation of Δneff as a function of temperature for both transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) modes, in a silicon waveguide with 500 nm width, embedded between 1 μm SiO2 layers above and below. The waveguide is suspended in air (n = 1) beneath the lower SiO2 membrane, and an Au layer is deposited above the top SiO2 film. These conditions are representative of the fabricated structure. Due to silicon’s higher thermo-optic coefficient compared to SiO2, and the stronger modal confinement of the TE mode within the silicon core, TE polarization exhibits a significantly higher Δneff with temperature. This makes TE modes more sensitive to thermal variations and thus preferable for sensing applications. The mode simulations confirm that the optical field remains well confined within the silicon waveguide, with negligible leakage into the Au layer or surrounding air.

(b) An illustration of resonance shift under radiation (red curve) with respect to the stable situation (blue curve). The insets in Fig. 4a show the electric field distribution for both polarizations, verifying the absence of plasmonic or lossy modes under the given geometry, while the bending affects the electric field distribution, observed clearly for the TM mode.

The relationship between Δneff and temperature is observed to be approximately linear over the relevant thermal range. For a 100 MeV energy deposition, which results in a 2.1 mK calculated temperature rise in the 5 μm-radius ring, the index variation for the TE mode is Δneff ≈ 4.134 × 10− 7 RIU, leading to a resonance redshift of ~ 152 fm. This magnitude is detectable with a high-resolution spectrometer when tracing the resonance dip (Δλr in Fig. 4b). Moreover, the intensity monitoring of the transmitted light can also be used to measure the temperature variations. For intensity interrogation, the linear region is preferred. Thus, the mean intensity is commonly used as the reference, as shown in Fig. 4b.

Interestingly, the same deposited energy flux leads to a similar Δλr even in larger rings (e.g., RMRR = 30 μm), as the thermal energy distribution remains nearly uniform despite differences in device surface area. However, as previously discussed, larger devices exhibit longer thermal response times, which may limit detection speed. For the TM mode, the corresponding resonance shift is significantly lower, approximately 103 fm, due to reduced confinement and a lower overlap with the heated silicon region. It is worth mentioning that the TM mode induces a large bending loss in a 5 μm ring resonator, making it difficult to use this mode for practical applications.

Finally, simulations confirmed a quasi-linear dependence between deposited energy and resonance shift. This predictable behavior simplifies the sensor’s calibration and broadens its applicability across a wide range of radiation intensities. For example, a 1 GeV energy deposition results in a resonance redshift close to 1.52 pm, matching well with the extrapolated linear model (simulated: 1.56 pm).

Mechanical stability

To ensure the viability of the proposed device for space applications, it is essential to verify its mechanical robustness, particularly in the presence of a dense metallic absorber. Excessive deformation or low-frequency resonance under mechanical vibrations could compromise the optical alignment and structural integrity of the sensor.

A structural mode analysis based on an FEM model was performed to estimate the mechanical resonance frequencies of the device. These simulations considered various configurations of the MRR with and without supporting pillars, and with absorber thicknesses ranging from 10 μm to 20 μm (gold).

For the smallest structure studied (RMRR = 5 μm), the first mechanical resonance frequency was found to be 344.09 kHz with supporting pillars and 339.3 kHz without supporting pillars.

Even in the worst-case configuration, no pillars and a 20 μm-thick absorber, the first mode remains above 187.97 kHz. These values are well above the mechanical vibration limits typically experienced during satellite launch, which are usually below a few kHz4. This confirms that the structure exhibits sufficient mechanical isolation and rigidity for space deployment.

Figure 5 presents the first two mechanical modes for both configurations. In all cases, the maximum displacement is concentrated at the front portion of the membrane, far from the waveguide bus and coupling supports. These results suggest that the optical interface remains relatively unaffected by mechanical deformation under resonant excitation.

Displacement fields for the structures with (a, b) and without (c, d) supporting pillar at resonance frequencies of (a) 344.09 kHz, (b) 605.52 kHz, (c) 339.3 kHz, and (d) 597.95 kHz.

Although the bus supports (input/output waveguides) are structurally more exposed to mechanical stress, their stiffness and small cross-section reduce their vulnerability to deformation. Consequently, the photonic performance of the device is expected to remain stable unless it is impacted by extremely high-momentum particles, such as heavy ions.

Larger MRR structures were also analyzed. For instance, a 30 μm-radius device with a 20 μm-thick absorber exhibited a lowest resonance mode at 18.3 kHz, still comfortably above critical launch vibration frequencies. While larger devices provide a greater absorbing area, they come at the cost of lower mechanical resonances and longer thermal response times, requiring careful design trade-offs.

Discussion

The proposed device offers a highly compact and integrable solution for radiation detection in space applications, with performance characteristics that align well with stringent SWaP-C constraints. Its bolometric sensing mechanism, based on the thermally induced resonance shift of suspended MRRs, provides several advantages over conventional γ-ray and particle detectors, while it can further be modified to extend its application. Here, we discuss the device’s application for electron detection, followed by evaluating its merits in comparison with conventional γ- and high-energy radiation sensors, as well as further challenges and considerations to overcome for space missions.

Extended application of the proposed device: electron detection

As an extension of the previous study, the absorption efficiency of the same high-Z metallic materials was also evaluated for high-energy electron radiation, using the Geant4 simulation framework. Figure 6 presents the electron absorption efficiency as a function of energy for each material, assuming a film thickness of 10 μm. The performance of a 6 μm-thick SiO2 layer is also shown for reference. It should be noted that the electrons’ energy at the Earth’s radiation belt is < 6 MeV1, and the simulated energy range was selected based on this fact.

Electron absorption efficiency of different high-Z metals with a thickness of 10 μm, and a 6 μm SiO2 layer.

Compared to γ-rays, electrons interact with matter through different mechanisms, primarily via inelastic collisions that lead to energy loss and scattering. These interactions are more localized and result in a greater probability of energy deposition within thin films, particularly at lower kinetic energies. The results show that tungsten and gold again offer the highest absorption efficiencies across the evaluated energy range, confirming their suitability as universal absorbers for both photon and electron detection. Lead also performs well, though slightly below gold and tungsten. Silver and copper display moderate absorption, while aluminum remains the least effective, especially at energies above 10 MeV. Despite its low electron interaction probability, aluminum can still serve as a passive shielding material, potentially protecting the sensing region from other spaceborne high-energy particles without significantly contributing to electron absorption.

The SiO₂ membrane again shows minimal absorption capability for electrons, which reinforces its role as an electrically and thermally insulating substrate, rather than an active part of the detection mechanism. From a design standpoint, the dual functionality of gold and tungsten, providing strong absorption for both γ-rays and electrons, makes them highly advantageous for multi-radiation sensing applications. This opens the possibility of using the same structural design, with minimal modifications, to detect different types of high-energy particles in space. However, it is important to note that electrons deposit energy more uniformly than γ-rays, and their trajectories may be deflected depending on the device geometry and electric/magnetic fields present in the environment. Therefore, careful consideration of particle incident angles, collimation, and sensor orientation may be required in future implementations.

In the case of electron irradiation, the deposited energy profile differs substantially from that of photon absorption. While photons are absorbed through multiple optical processes (as discussed in Sections "Radiation absorption analysis" and "γ-ray absorption"), distributed over the penetration depth defined by multiple interactions, high-energy electrons lose their kinetic energy through inelastic scattering and secondary electron emission, resulting in a highly localized energy deposition within the first sub-micrometer of the absorber, especially for electrons with kinetic energies in the range of tens to hundreds of keV31,32. For higher energies, the penetration depth is higher, and secondary electrons also participate in the energy deposition process. However, this localized deposition produces a steep initial temperature gradient that rapidly equilibrates through lateral heat diffusion. The simulated diffusion time across the absorber thickness is below 10 µs, significantly shorter than the device’s thermal time constant (~ 1.15 ms), indicating that the bolometer reaches a quasi-uniform thermal state during steady-state operation, as supported by the results presented in Section "Thermal simulation results". However, the stronger spatial confinement of deposited energy may momentarily affect the transient signal shape and the calibration of the responsivity if uniform temperature assumptions are used. These effects are relevant particularly when extending the detector operation to charged-particle detection and will be further investigated in future experimental work.

Strengths to leverage, barriers to overcome

The Geant4-based simulations revealed that gold and tungsten offer the best trade-off between absorption efficiency and integration compatibility, for both γ-ray photons and high-energy electrons. The selection of gold is further supported by its widespread use in photonic fabrication processes and excellent long-term chemical stability.

While the structure is optimized for γ-ray detection around MeV energy deposition, making it a strong candidate for TGF monitoring, the absorption efficiency decreases significantly for photon energies above ~ 10 MeV. At these levels, the interaction probability becomes extremely low due to the long penetration depth in thin films. As a result, the sensitivity to ultra-high-energy γ-rays (> 100 MeV) is severely limited for all types of γ-ray detectors, as shown in Table 1. However, it should be kept in mind that the photon flux is extremely low for high photon energies5,6. Particularly, the photon flux in 50–300 keV has been observed to be significantly higher than photons with higher energy, during TGFs5, whereas the detection efficiency of the scintillating crystals commonly used for γ-ray detection drops drastically for photon energies lower than ~ 100 keV4. The proposed device with only 10 μm gold thin film absorbs the low-energy γ-rays with energies < 20 keV with efficiency higher than 95%. The efficiency remains higher than 10% for photon energies lower than 130 keV, except for a small region near the material’s K-edge and is higher than 1% for photon energies lower than 270 keV. In comparison with various scintillating materials with weights ranging from 350 to 680 g and detection ranges higher than 40 keV4, the proposed structure not only presents high efficiency for the lower γ-ray energy range, but also it is beneficial from the SWaP-C point of view, especially without needing any photomultiplier tubes or multipixel photon counters. Additionally, compared to avalanche photodiodes and solid-state γ-ray detectors, which can detect the lower portion of the energy spectrum, the proposed structure promises superior absorption, with maximum radiation hardness (discussed below). However, the energy resolution may be limited in our device due to the high decay time, which could be compensated for by using a sensor array approach, with different absorbing materials. Additionally, in comparison to giant γ-radiation tracking systems, like AGATA37, the proposed device benefits from working at room temperature and needing simple readout circuits, which makes it perfect to be integrated into small satellites.

Photons that bypass the absorbing layer and reach the photonic structure may cause defect formation in the waveguides or cladding, potentially degrading optical performance over time. Nevertheless, since the interaction of photons and SiO2 is extremely low (as shown in Fig. 2 and supported by previous studies2,15, the operational lifetime remains acceptable for CubeSat-class missions, typically limited to 1–2 years4. It should be noted that the surface area of the waveguides and active regions (i.e., silicon thin film with a thickness of 220 nm) is small enough to be securely immune from the harsh space environment during such a period. Keeping in mind that the metal layer is only employed as an absorber and does not contribute to the photonic performance of the device, its degradation could have minimal impact on the mission if the device is well protected from heavy ions and protons.

The thermal simulations confirmed that the device can respond rapidly (on the order of microseconds) to energy deposition and reset within milliseconds, enabling event tracking for transient phenomena such as TGFs or GRBs. The linear relationship between deposited energy and resonance shift simplifies calibration and extends the dynamic range of the sensor.

From a mechanical standpoint, the device demonstrates excellent stability under launch and in-orbit conditions. The high mechanical resonance frequencies, well above those typically encountered during launch vibrations, ensure structural reliability even in miniaturized formats. Furthermore, the use of vacuum packaging, common in bolometric systems, can protect the structure from mechanical shocks, humidity, and convective thermal losses, enhancing overall performance and operational lifetime.

Another challenge is the need for dedicated readout and processing electronics, including high-resolution spectroscopy and thermal insulation from the substrate. While these subsystems increase the overall system complexity and mass, integrated and shared electronics across multiple detector elements could mitigate the impact. Advances in on-chip spectroscopy and photonic multiplexing could further reduce the footprint of support circuitry in future implementations, when the wavelength interrogation method is used. Furthermore, the system could derive advantages from a single-wavelength sensitivity approach39–41 after calibrating the resonance wavelength for each MRR in the array and tuning the incident excitation wavelength dynamically. In this case, the intensity variations could be monitored (as shown in Fig. 4b), and a simple photodetector can be used for measuring the shift, based on the intensity-interrogation method. In this method, the linear region is favorable; thus, the mean of the intensity (in the transmission spectrum) is usually used as the reference. Employing a 16-bit (8-bit) analog-to-digital converter (ADC), with an ER = 6 dB, each bit change represents 11.44 ppm (0.293%) intesity variation at 10 MeV accumulated energy deposition, which leads to a 74.7 keV (19.1 MeV) resolution. Finer ADC enhances the resolution significantly. However, the electronic readout, auxiliary processing, and circuitry control should be effectively shielded to maintain their performance in space missions.

When comparing our proposed device with the others (as in Table 2), it is worth noting that the minimum detectable energy (MDE) is a function of both the system-level readout circuit and the NEPth, while for other detectors, the MDE resolution is usually reported based on Full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) at the reported photon energy. However, the calculated 74.7 keV represented here is based on the system noise floor, specifically the quantization noise limit of the 16-bit ADC, accounting for the system-level detection resolution rather than the spectral peak linewidth.

The fundamental sensitivity of any bolometric detector is ultimately limited by thermal fluctuation noise, which arises from the random exchange of energy between the sensing element and its thermal reservoir42–44. We quantify this fundamental limit using the Noise Equivalent Power (NEP). For a thermally isolated structure operating at absolute temperature T, NEPth is defined by the thermal conductance G to the substrate42:

where kB is the Boltzmann constant. In our design, the thermal conductance G of the suspended SiO2 membrane was critically determined by the geometry of the support structures. Steady-state FEM simulations of the complete structure, which accounts for lateral heat spreading and complex boundary conditions (explained in Section “Thermal analysis”), confirmed an effective thermal conductance of G = 1.18 × 10− 8 W/K. This value is consistent with reported thermal isolation for similar micromachined SiO2 and SiNx membranes of comparable dimensions43–47. At room temperature (T = 25 °C), this yields a thermal noise equivalent power of NEPth = 2.406 × 10− 13 W/Hz½. This result establishes the realistic lower bound for the minimum detectable power, confirming that the proposed structure provides adequate thermal isolation for high-sensitivity detection, in line with established microbolometer performance. Furthermore, the thermal time constant of the structure, directly calculated from the transient thermal simulations, is τ = 1.15 ms. Therefore, the minimum detectable energy Emin can be estimated as Emin = NEP τ½ ≈ 8.16 × 10− 15 J, corresponding to a minimum deposited energy of approximately 51 keV, setting the fundamental limit for the minimum detectable energy in our configuration. This value is well below the energy levels investigated in the paper (≥ 74.7 keV), confirming that the proposed device operates well above the thermal noise limit.

Importantly, the proposed architecture is inherently adaptable. With suitable modification of the absorbing layer or structural tuning, the same principle can be extended to detect other types of space radiation, including high-energy electrons, beta particles, protons, alpha particles, and even low-mass ions. For such applications, the absorption mechanism should be adapted according to the particle kinetics and type, especially when the device may be prone to mechanical damage resulting from particles’ high kinetic energy. For instance, hydrogen-rich materials (like polymers, plastic, or even water) could slow down the particles, and the particle energy/type could be predicted through calibrated devices. This flexibility enables the integration of diverse sensing functionalities on a single photonic platform, significantly enhancing the scientific return of small-satellite missions.

Although the models presented here are purely theoretical and require experimental validation, the demonstrated radiation hardness of photonic structures against gamma radiation suggests the proposed method could pave the way for the practical implementation of space radiation sensors for such a harsh environment, simultaneously keeping the sensing area secured from degradation.

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a novel photonic sensing structure for the detection of high-energy radiation in space environments. The device aims to operate on the principle of thermally induced resonance shift in silicon micro-ring resonators, triggered by the absorption of radiation energy in a high-Z metallic layer.

The structure was designed to be compact, lightweight, and mechanically robust, fulfilling the requirements of CubeSats and smart satellite platforms. Finite Element Method simulations confirmed the device’s favorable thermal dynamics, enabling rapid response and reset times compatible with transient events such as GRBs and TGFs. The high thermal resistance of the suspended SiO₂ membrane allows for efficient thermal isolation, enhancing detection sensitivity.

Geant4-based radiation simulations in the 0.01–100 MeV range demonstrated the effectiveness of gold as an absorbing material, showing high efficiency in capturing γ-ray photons and energetic electrons. The structure’s mechanical resonance frequencies were well above critical launch thresholds, ensuring operational stability even with absorbing layers up to 20 μm thick.

The optical simulations showed a nearly linear dependence between the deposited energy and the resonance wavelength shift, enabling straightforward calibration and dynamic range extension. Additionally, the same sensing platform can be adapted to detect different types of radiation, including electrons and, with further optimization, low-mass ions or beta particles.

Overall, the theoretical results offer a promising solution for integrated, low-power, and multifunctional radiation detection in space missions, especially where SWaP-C constraints are critical. Future work will include experimental validation, radiation-hard packaging, and the development of on-chip spectroscopic readout circuits. With these advancements, the proposed architecture has the potential to become a key enabling technology for next-generation spaceborne radiation sensors. Future work includes prototyping the device for experimental evaluation of its performance.
