Research progress on hepatitis B surface antigen decline and clearance
Authors: Yi-Fan Guo, Meng-Qi Sun, Yi-Zhe Zhang, Dong Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) remains a major global cause of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), particularly in China. With ongoing advances in antiviral therapy, the therapeutic objective for CHB has evolved from sustained suppression of hepatitis B virus replication toward achievement of a functional cure, defined by hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss with or without anti-HBs seroconversion. Increasing evidence suggests that HBsAg clearance is strongly associated with reduced hepatic inflammation, regression of fibrosis, and a substantially lower risk of HCC, thereby serving as a critical indicator of long-term clinical prognosis in patients with CHB. Drawing upon recent domestic and international advances, this review summarizes the biological characteristics and pathogenic significance of HBsAg, its dynamic changes during antiviral therapy, and its associations with virologic responses and clinical outcomes. Particular emphasis is placed on the integrated immunologic mechanisms underlying HBsAg decline and clearance, including the interplay between innate and adaptive immune responses, cytokine networks, and immune reconstitution during functional cure. In addition, emerging predictive models and potential therapeutic strategies targeting HBsAg clearance are discussed, together with future research directions aimed at advancing standardized management and precision treatment strategies for CHB.

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) remains a major global public health challenge and continues to represent one of the leading causes of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), particularly in China. Although nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs) can achieve sustained and profound suppression of hepatitis B virus (HBV) replication, the persistent presence of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) and integrated HBV DNA (iDNA) within hepatocytes prevents complete viral eradication and maintains the long-term risk of progressive liver disease, cirrhosis, and HCC development.1, 2, 3 In recent years, advances in the understanding of chronic HBV persistence and host immune regulatory mechanisms have driven a major shift in therapeutic goals, with increasing emphasis on achieving a “functional cure”. Current domestic and international guidelines define functional cure primarily as sustained hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss, with or without anti-HBs seroconversion, accompanied by durable virologic suppression and biochemical remission.1 HBsAg clearance is regarded as a critical milestone because it reflects not only profound suppression of viral transcriptional activity, but also partial restoration of host antiviral immune control. Nevertheless, the biological mechanisms underlying HBsAg decline and clearance remain incompletely elucidated, and the overall clinical rate of HBsAg seroclearance remains low. Furthermore, currently available antiviral strategies exhibit substantial heterogeneity in their capacity to induce HBsAg loss. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the immunologic mechanisms governing HBsAg persistence and clearance, together with identification of reliable predictive factors, is essential for improving functional cure rates and advancing precision therapeutic strategies for CHB.

Core biological characteristics and pathogenic associations of HBsAg

Serum HBsAg originates from two principal sources: transcriptional products derived from intrahepatic cccDNA and HBV DNA fragments integrated into the host genome. During the hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg)-positive immune-active phase, cccDNA exhibits high transcriptional activity and serves as the predominant source of circulating HBsAg, whereas the contribution of iDNA-derived HBsAg remains relatively limited. In contrast, during the HBeAg-negative phase, progressive suppression of cccDNA transcription is accompanied by a marked increase in the relative contribution of iDNA-derived HBsAg.2 In patients receiving long-term NAs therapy or exhibiting profound suppression of viral replication, iDNA-mediated HBsAg production becomes increasingly dominant, representing one of the principal reasons why complete HBsAg elimination remains difficult to achieve under current therapeutic conditions.3 Emerging viral biomarkers, including HBV RNA and hepatitis B core-related antigen (HBcrAg), complement quantitative HBsAg (qHBsAg) assessment by enabling more precise differentiation between active cccDNA transcription and HBsAg production driven by integrated HBV DNA. HBV RNA directly reflects ongoing cccDNA transcriptional activity, whereas HBcrAg correlates closely with intrahepatic cccDNA burden. Collectively, these biomarkers improve characterization of HBsAg origin and enhance clinical stratification during antiviral monitoring.4,5

HBsAg consists of three envelope proteins, including the small (S), middle (M), and large (L) surface proteins.6 Among these, the S protein constitutes the major structural component of HBV subviral particles. Its molecular structure is highly conserved, with its principal antigenic epitopes concentrated within the “a” determinant region, which serves as the primary target for the induction of protective anti-HBs antibodies.7 Alterations involving the M protein, particularly truncations or mutations within the pre-s2 region, have been strongly associated with hepatocarcinogenesis and HCC development.8 The L protein additionally plays an essential role in viral attachment, receptor recognition, and hepatocyte entry.

Beyond its structural functions, HBsAg actively contributes to viral persistence and immune evasion during chronic infection. HBsAg participates in viral particle assembly and secretion while simultaneously promoting chronic infection through suppression of antigen presentation, impairment of effector T-cell function, and induction of immune tolerance. Although HBsAg itself does not exert direct cytotoxic effects, sustained high-level expression can perpetuate chronic immune-mediated hepatic inflammation, thereby contributing to progressive liver injury and disease progression.9,10 Increasing evidence further indicates that persistent HBsAg expression functions as a central driver of immune tolerance and represents a major obstacle to achieving functional cure. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms regulating HBsAg production, persistence, and clearance has become a major focus of current CHB research.

Dynamic changes in HBsAg during antiviral therapy and their clinical significance

Impact of different antiviral agents on HBsAg decline

Currently, the primary antiviral therapies for CHB consist of NAs and interferon-based regimens. NAs effectively inhibit HBV DNA replication and enable sustained virologic suppression in the majority of treated patients. However, their ability to reduce HBsAg levels remains limited, and HBsAg decline during long-term NA therapy is generally slow and gradual.11 In contrast, pegylated interferon (PEG-IFN) exerts both direct antiviral and immunomodulatory effects, allowing a subset of patients to achieve substantial reductions in HBsAg levels or even HBsAg seroclearance. Increasing evidence indicates that combination strategies integrating NAs with PEG-IFN can further enhance HBsAg clearance rates, thereby representing an important therapeutic approach in the pursuit of functional cure.12,13

Notably, the magnitude, kinetics, and durability of HBsAg decline differ considerably among therapeutic regimens. NAs monotherapy primarily suppresses viral replication without substantially restoring antiviral immune function, resulting in relatively limited reductions in HBsAg. By comparison, PEG-IFN-based regimens promote immune reconstitution and enhance host antiviral immune responses, thereby facilitating more rapid HBsAg decline and increasing the likelihood of seroclearance. Nevertheless, treatment responses to PEG-IFN remain highly heterogeneous, and tolerability concerns continue to limit its widespread applicability. Although combination regimens appear to provide superior efficacy, they require careful patient selection, individualized risk-benefit assessment, and close safety monitoring.14,15 Collectively, these observations underscore the central role of immune restoration in mediating HBsAg clearance and achieving functional cure in CHB.

Association between HBsAg decline and HBV DNA/HBeAg

HBsAg decline is closely associated with suppression of HBV replication; however, the kinetics of these virologic markers are not entirely synchronous. During the early phase of antiviral therapy, serum HBV DNA levels typically decline rapidly, whereas reductions in HBsAg levels occur more gradually. HBV DNA primarily reflects active viral replication, whereas HBsAg more accurately reflects the transcriptional activity of cccDNA and iDNA, as well as the degree of host immune control over HBV infection. Multiple studies have demonstrated that patients exhibiting a greater magnitude of early HBsAg decline are significantly more likely to subsequently achieve HBsAg seroclearance, highlighting the prognostic and therapeutic value of dynamic HBsAg monitoring.13,15

HBsAg seroclearance and HBeAg seroconversion represent two critical immunologic milestones during the natural history and treatment course of CHB. HBeAg seroconversion generally reflects the initial establishment of immune-mediated control over viral replication and is considered an important prerequisite for subsequent HBsAg decline. This relationship may be attributable to coordinated epigenetic regulation of cccDNA transcription and the development of a more favorable hepatic immune microenvironment following HBeAg seroconversion.3 Accordingly, HBeAg seroconversion may be regarded as an early indicator of partial immune control, whereas HBsAg seroclearance represents a more profound suppression of the intrahepatic viral reservoir and restoration of antiviral immune function. Combined longitudinal assessment of HBsAg and HBeAg may therefore improve identification of patients with favorable treatment responses and support optimization of individualized therapeutic strategies.

Relationship between HBsAg and liver disease progression and prognosis

Correlation between HBsAg levels and liver fibrosis/cirrhosis

HBsAg levels are closely associated with the severity of hepatic fibrosis and represent an important biomarker for evaluating liver disease progression in CHB. Accumulating evidence indicates that sustained reductions in HBsAg levels are strongly correlated with decreased hepatic inflammatory activity and histologic improvement in liver fibrosis. These beneficial effects appear to be particularly pronounced in patients who maintain persistently low HBsAg levels or achieve HBsAg seroclearance.16 Large cohort studies, including the REVEAL-HBV study, have demonstrated that each 1 log10 IU/mL reduction in HBsAg level is associated with a significantly increased likelihood of fibrosis regression. Furthermore, among patients with substantial baseline fibrosis (Ishak score ≥ 3), those exhibiting an HBsAg decline of > 0.5 log10 IU/mL during treatment demonstrate significantly greater rates of fibrosis improvement compared with patients without meaningful HBsAg reduction.17 Notably, rapid and sustained HBsAg decline is frequently associated with more favorable long-term histologic outcomes, whereas patients exhibiting persistently elevated, slowly declining, or fluctuating HBsAg patterns remain at comparatively greater risk of fibrosis progression.18 Consequently, longitudinal monitoring of qHBsAg kinetics may provide valuable prognostic information regarding fibrosis progression, treatment responsiveness, and long-term disease trajectory, thereby supporting individualized clinical management and therapeutic decision-making.

Impact of HBsAg clearance on HCC risk

HBsAg clearance is widely recognized as a major protective factor associated with reduced HCC risk in patients with chronic HBV infection. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that even among patients who achieve sustained HBV DNA suppression during long-term NA therapy, persistently elevated HBsAg levels remain independently associated with increased HCC risk.19 Multiple large-scale cohort studies and meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated that patients achieving HBsAg seroclearance experience a substantially lower long-term incidence of HCC compared with those with persistent HBsAg positivity. Despite these favorable outcomes, patients with established cirrhosis or advanced fibrosis continue to require long-term standardized surveillance because residual carcinogenic risk may persist even after HBsAg clearance.16,17,20 Importantly, low-level HBsAg expression or HBsAg seroclearance should not be interpreted as equivalent to complete elimination of HCC risk. Several mechanisms may contribute to continued disease progression and hepatocarcinogenesis following apparent functional cure. First, persistent virus-related oncogenic processes may remain active because of residual integrated HBV DNA fragments within hepatocytes and low-level viral reservoirs associated with occult HBV infection.21 Second, irreversible structural and molecular alterations within the cirrhotic or fibrotic liver microenvironment may sustain a pro-carcinogenic state despite viral suppression.22,23 Third, concomitant risk factors, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-related liver disease, and diabetes mellitus, may further promote hepatocarcinogenesis through additive or “second hit” mechanisms.24 Accordingly, patients with baseline cirrhosis, advanced age, or additional hepatic risk factors require individualized risk stratification, continued long-term follow-up, and regular HCC surveillance to facilitate early detection and intervention.

To improve residual HCC risk stratification following HBsAg seroclearance or in patients with persistently low HBsAg levels, qHBsAg assessment may be complemented by additional viral and fibrosis-related biomarkers. Ultrasensitive platforms such as iTACT enable detection of very low concentrations of HBsAg and HBcrAg after seroclearance, and elevated iTACT-HBcrAg levels have been associated with increased HCC risk.25 Similarly, patients exhibiting low HBsAg levels in conjunction with persistently elevated HBcrAg demonstrate higher rates of HCC development, supporting the combined use of these biomarkers for prognostic assessment. Increasing evidence also indicates that HBV DNA integration plays a major role in hepatocarcinogenesis. The frequency of HBV integration events increases progressively across CHB, cirrhosis, and HCC, promoting clonal hepatocyte expansion and aberrant expression of oncogenesis-related genes at recurrent integration sites, including telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and mixed-lineage leukemia 4 (MLL4). Together with TERT promoter mutations, these integration events represent independent molecular pathways contributing to HBV-related hepatocarcinogenesis and may provide potential targets for stratified therapeutic approaches.26 In addition, fibrosis-based markers such as the enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) score may further refine post-seroclearance risk assessment. An ELF score <10.8 has been associated with a >97% reduction in HCC risk among patients aged ≥ 50 years.27 Integration of viral transcriptional biomarkers, including HBcrAg and HBV RNA, together with fibrosis evaluation may therefore improve prognostic stratification during antiviral therapy. Looking forward, multi-omics technologies and predictive algorithms are expected to facilitate multimodal and personalized surveillance strategies.20,28 However, despite these advances, no integrated biomarker panel has yet been universally adopted in clinical guidelines, and further large-scale validation studies remain necessary.

Improvement of long-term prognosis by HBsAg seroclearance

HBsAg seroclearance is regarded as one of the most important indicators of favorable long-term prognosis in patients with CHB. Achievement of HBsAg loss is commonly associated with sustained remission of hepatic inflammation, preservation of normal liver function, stabilization or regression of fibrosis, and substantial reductions in the risks of cirrhosis-related complications and HCC development. In comparison with persistent HBsAg positivity, patients achieving seroclearance also demonstrate significantly improved long-term survival outcomes, particularly among those with preexisting fibrosis or cirrhosis.29 Beyond its effects on liver-related outcomes, HBsAg seroclearance may also confer broader clinical and psychosocial benefits, including improved quality of life, reduced psychologic burden, decreased social stigma, and lower risk of viral transmission. Therefore, HBsAg seroclearance represents not only a major therapeutic endpoint in CHB management, but also an important indicator for long-term prognostic evaluation and individualized patient care.

Integrated immunologic mechanisms of HBsAg clearance

Immune tolerance state in chronic HBV infection

CHB is characterized by a persistent state of immune tolerance that constitutes a central mechanism underlying sustained HBsAg expression and chronic viral persistence. This immune-tolerant state does not arise from dysfunction at a single immunologic checkpoint; rather, it reflects a highly complex and coordinated network of abnormalities involving both innate and adaptive immune responses. Collectively, these alterations establish an immunosuppressive hepatic microenvironment that favors viral persistence and impairs effective antiviral immunity.30

Innate immunity

At the level of innate immunity, natural killer (NK) cells exhibit impaired cytotoxic activity together with reduced secretion of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), largely owing to elevated inhibitory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) expression of inhibitory receptors within the hepatic microenvironment. In addition, HBsAg can directly interact with fragment crystallizable (Fc) receptors on NK cells, further suppressing NK-cell-mediated cytotoxicity and IFN-γ production, thereby exacerbating immune dysfunction.31 Macrophage function is also profoundly altered during chronic HBV infection. HBx-mediated polarization toward the M2 macrophage phenotype weakens phagocytic activity while promoting the production of immunosuppressive mediators that further reinforce immune tolerance. Concurrently, HBsAg suppresses macrophage Toll-like receptor (TLR) 7/8/9 signaling pathways, resulting in reduced production of IL-6 and IFN-α and impairment of early antiviral immune responses.32,33 Dendritic cell (DC) dysfunction additionally contributes to ineffective antiviral immunity in CHB. Suppression of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling impairs DC maturation and downregulates major histocompatibility complex molecules and co-stimulatory molecules, including cluster of differentiation 80 (CD80) and cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86), thereby compromising antigen presentation capacity. Simultaneously, reduced IL-12 production together with increased IL-10 secretion further promotes T-cell tolerance and immune suppression.32,34

Adaptive immunity

Within the adaptive immune system, persistently elevated HBsAg levels drive profound T-cell exhaustion and dysfunction. HBsAg interferes with T-cell receptor signaling, suppresses IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion, and activates the programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) pathway, thereby promoting T-cell hyporesponsiveness. Consequently, HBsAg-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cells exhibit an exhausted phenotype characterized by increased expression of inhibitory receptors, including PD-1 T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), and lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3), together with impaired proliferation, cytotoxicity, and cytokine production.32, 33, 34 Persistent HBsAg exposure also contributes to substantial B-cell dysfunction, impairing plasma cell differentiation and reducing anti-HBs antibody production. Even when anti-HBs antibodies are generated, they often demonstrate low affinity and limited neutralizing activity against HBV.35,36 In CHB, HBsAg-specific memory B cells predominantly exhibit an atypical CD27−CD21− phenotype associated with functional exhaustion. These cells express high levels of inhibitory receptors, including PD-1, Fc receptor-like 3/4/5 (FcRL3/4/5), and sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin 6 (Siglec-6), which impair differentiation into antibody-secreting cells and limit the production of high-affinity neutralizing anti-HBs IgG antibodies.37 In contrast, HBcAg-specific B cells are primarily composed of functional classical memory B cells (cMBCs) capable of secreting anti-HBc antibodies, which serve mainly as markers of infection rather than mediators of antiviral protection. This distinction highlights the fundamentally different functional states of B-cell populations targeting distinct HBV antigens.38

Immune reconstitution in the process of functional cure

Functional cure of CHB depends on coordinated restoration of both innate and adaptive immune responses, which collectively drive immune reconstitution and facilitate HBsAg clearance. Innate immunity

Innate immune reconstitution primarily involves NK cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs). Under PEG-IFN or other immunomodulatory therapies, NK cells upregulate activating receptors, including natural killer group 2D (NKG2D) and NKp30, activate phosphoinositide 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-AKT) signaling pathways, and recover cytotoxic function. These cells eliminate infected hepatocytes through perforin/granzyme-mediated mechanisms and secrete IFN-γ and TNF-α to suppress HBV replication, with IFN-γ levels closely correlating with HBsAg decline.39 PEG-IFN-α can reverse HBsAg-mediated inhibition of NK-cell activity, thereby restoring clearance of HBsAg-positive hepatocytes. Concurrently, macrophages undergo TLR-mediated polarization toward the M1 phenotype, enhancing HBsAg phagocytosis and degradation. Activated macrophages also present antigens to CD8+ T cells and secrete IL-12 and IL-18, promoting Th1 differentiation and adaptive immune activation. TLR7 agonists such as GS-9620 combined with small interfering RNA (siRNA) partially counteract HBsAg-mediated suppression of TLR signaling and reduce HBsAg levels, although their efficacy remains inferior to PEG-IFN-α-based combination regimens.33,40 DC maturation is further enhanced through Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling, leading to increased CD83+ CD86+ cell populations. Mature DCs present HBsAg-derived peptides and serve as critical mediators linking innate and adaptive immunity, with the degree of maturation directly associated with HBsAg clearance.

Adaptive immunity

Adaptive immune reconstitution is centered on restoration of HBsAg-specific T-cell and B-cell responses, with HBsAg-specific T cells serving as major mediators of viral clearance. PEG-IFN promotes IL-2 secretion and signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) activation in CD8+ T cells, thereby partially reversing T-cell exhaustion and downregulating PD-1 expression, resulting in recovery of proliferative and cytotoxic capacity. Activated CD8+ T cells recognize HBsAg-MHC I complexes, release perforin and granzyme, induce Fas/FasL-mediated apoptosis, and secrete IFN-γ. IFN-γ further promotes apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic polypeptide-like 3 (APOBEC3) expression and reduces cccDNA histone acetylation, thereby suppressing viral transcription and replication while facilitating cccDNA degradation.41,42 Immune restoration additionally involves re-establishment of the T helper 1/T helper 2 (Th1/Th2) balance. Th1-derived IFN-γ enhances CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity, whereas IL-2 supports T-cell proliferation and memory formation. T helper 17 (Th17) cells secrete IL-17 to recruit inflammatory cells and IL-22 to protect hepatocytes. T follicular helper (Tfh) cells produce IL-21 to promote B-cell differentiation. Following PEG-IFN-α therapy, Tfh cells upregulate CD40 ligand (CD40L) expression and activate HBsAg-specific B cells through the Tfh-CD40/CD40L-B-cell pathway. Increased frequencies of CD40L + Tfh cells positively correlate with activation markers in HBsAg-specific B cells, including activated memory B cells (actMBCs), CD38, and CXCR3, and directly influence their functional recovery. Restoration of HBsAg-specific B-cell function is observed predominantly in patients achieving functional cure and is characterized by recovery of high-affinity anti-HBs IgG production. Following treatment, HBsAg-specific B cells transition from low-affinity IgM-dominant responses toward high-affinity IgG surface expression. These anti-HBs IgG antibodies neutralize HBsAg and block viral infection, representing a hallmark of functional cure. Concurrent alterations in chemokine receptor expression, including increased CXCR3 and decreased CXCR5, promote migration of activated immune cells from lymphoid tissues to sites of hepatic inflammation, thereby enhancing clearance of HBsAg-positive hepatocytes and amplifying antiviral immunity.38

Cytokine network regulating HBsAg clearance

Balanced cytokine regulation is essential for effective HBsAg clearance and coordinated antiviral immunity. IFN-γ, produced primarily by Th1 cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells, binds to IFN-γ receptor 1/2 (IFNGR1/2) on hepatocytes and activates JAK-STAT signaling pathways, inducing histone deacetylase (HDAC)-mediated suppression of cccDNA transcription and promoting APOBEC3-dependent cccDNA degradation.43 TNF-α enhances immune clearance by upregulating MHC and co-stimulatory molecule expression and inducing CXCL9 and CXCL10 to recruit T cells into the hepatic microenvironment. TNF-α additionally exerts direct pro-apoptotic effects through histone deacetylase (HDAC)-mediated caspase activation, whereas HBV infection increases hepatocyte susceptibility to TNF-α-mediated killing through HBx-induced downregulation of X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP).44,45 Furthermore, TNF-α synergizes with IFN-γ to activate NF-κB signaling, increase IFN-γ receptor expression, and accelerate cccDNA degradation.46 Despite these antiviral effects, excessive TNF-α activity may promote hepatocyte necrosis and liver fibrosis. This balance is regulated through multiple compensatory mechanisms, including secretion of soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor 2 (TNFR2) by hepatic stellate cells, reactive oxygen species (ROS) clearance through hepatocyte autophagy, and IL-10 secretion by regulatory T (Treg) cells.47,48 IL-27, predominantly produced by DCs, further contributes to immune restoration by promoting Th1 differentiation, reversing CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, and attenuating Treg suppression.49,50 Cytokine-profiling studies have also linked IL-27-containing immune pathways with HBsAg loss during CHB treatment.51 More broadly, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-2, IL-1 2, and IL-21 promote antiviral immune activation, whereas downregulation of IL-10 and TGF-β contributes to restoration of immune balance and facilitates HBsAg clearance.

Implications for clinical therapy

Increasing understanding of the immunologic mechanisms governing HBsAg clearance has important implications for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for CHB. Current evidence suggests that effective treatment should focus on reversing immune tolerance and promoting immune reconstitution. Potential approaches include combining PEG-IFN with immunomodulatory agents to enhance innate immune activation; promoting M1 macrophage polarization and DC maturation through TLR agonists; applying immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including PD-1 inhibitors, to reverse T-cell exhaustion52; developing therapeutic vaccines targeting dominant HBsAg epitopes to strengthen virus-specific T-cell and B-cell responses; and modulating key cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-27 to restore immune network balance.53

In clinical practice, integrated assessment of T-cell exhaustion phenotypes, including PD-1 and TIM-3 expression, together with cytokine profiles and DC maturation status, may improve prediction of therapeutic responsiveness. Individualized treatment regimens should therefore be guided by baseline immune status, HBsAg levels, and patient-specific disease characteristics. For example, ICIs may be preferentially incorporated into combination regimens for patients with pronounced T-cell exhaustion, whereas TLR agonists may be beneficial in individuals with impaired DC maturation. Therapeutic vaccines combined with siRNA-based strategies may additionally benefit patients with defective B-cell responses and inadequate anti-HBs production. Agents targeting the CD40/CD40L signaling pathway may further enhance the function of CD40L + Tfh cells and promote activation of HBsAg-specific B cells.34,37,40,52,53

HBsAg-specific B cells in CHB commonly exhibit an exhausted atypical memory B-cell (atMBC) phenotype associated with impaired production of high-affinity anti-HBs IgG antibodies. This dysfunctional state appears at least partially reversible through targeted immunomodulation. In vitro studies demonstrate that PD-1 blockade combined with B-cell maturation signals, including IL-2, IL-21, and CD40 ligand stimulation, can restore differentiation and antibody-secreting capacity in these anergic B-cell populations. Furthermore, blockade or knockdown of additional inhibitory receptors, including FcRL4 and Siglec-6, further enhances HBsAg-specific humoral immunity. Sustained HBsAg seroclearance in vivo has been associated with normalization of the atMBC phenotype and restoration of durable high-affinity anti-HBs IgG production.37 Patients at elevated risk of relapse or disease progression may require prolonged consolidation therapy and intensified immune monitoring. Future therapeutic refinement may further involve modulation of CD40L + Tfh cells, potentially through targeting of the CD40/CD40L axis in combination with PEG-IFN-α, to enhance HBsAg-specific B-cell activation and antibody production. In addition, incorporation of immunologic biomarkers, including actMBC frequency, anti-HBs IgG expression, and CD40+ Tfh-cell abundance, into predictive response models may improve individualized treatment stratification, optimize HBsAg clearance rates, and facilitate advancement toward functional cure in CHB.

Prediction model and clinical application for HBsAg clearance based on longitudinal qHBsAg trajectories

High-sensitivity qHBsAg measurement is widely used in CHB management; however, single timepoint assessments have important limitations in accurately reflecting dynamic viral and immunologic changes. In contrast, predictive models based on longitudinal qHBsAg trajectories more effectively capture cccDNA activity and host immune status over time.

Fan et al. developed and validated the GOLDEN model, a prediction model named in the original study, using the longitudinal discriminant analysis (LoDA) algorithm applied to longitudinal qHBsAg datasets from patients receiving long-term NA therapy.54 This model integrates multiple kinetic characteristics of HBsAg dynamics, including decline rate, temporal fluctuations, and plateau patterns, thereby substantially improving predictive accuracy. The GOLDEN model demonstrated excellent performance for predicting HBsAg seroclearance, with area under the curve values ranging from 0.979 to 0.981. Approximately 8.5%–10.4% of treated patients were classified as having a high probability of HBsAg clearance, corresponding to a 5-year cumulative seroclearance rate of 17.0%–29.1% within this subgroup.54

As a highly accurate quantitative tool, the GOLDEN model provides important clinical value for identifying patients with a greater likelihood of achieving HBsAg clearance and for optimizing therapeutic decision-making, including initiation of combination therapy or adjustment of treatment duration.54 More broadly, the model establishes a meaningful link between mechanistic studies of HBsAg biology and precision medicine approaches for CHB, thereby supporting more individualized and evidence-based clinical management. Nevertheless, widespread clinical implementation remains limited by the requirement for long-term serial qHBsAg measurements, the computational complexity of the LoDA algorithm, and restricted accessibility in resource-limited or primary care settings. Future simplification through web-based calculators, cutoff-based algorithms, and integration into electronic medical record systems may facilitate broader clinical adoption.

Future perspectives

HBsAg seroclearance represents the central endpoint of functional cure in CHB and reflects profound suppression of viral activity together with restoration of host antiviral immune function. Achievement of HBsAg loss is strongly associated with improved long-term clinical outcomes, including reduced risks of cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Although substantial advances have been made in understanding the mechanisms and therapeutic strategies associated with functional cure, major challenges remain, including low rates of HBsAg clearance, incomplete understanding of immune regulatory pathways, and limited availability of highly effective curative therapies. Future research should further elucidate the molecular and immunologic mechanisms governing HBsAg persistence and clearance, refine predictive biomarkers and prognostic models, and optimize individualized therapeutic strategies. Continued integration of virologic, immunologic, and multi-omics approaches may facilitate development of more effective precision therapies capable of increasing functional cure rates in CHB. Ultimately, these advances may contribute substantially to reducing the global burden of chronic hepatitis B and its long-term complications.
