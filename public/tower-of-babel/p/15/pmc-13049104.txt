A simulation study of a novel spiral volumetric modulated arc therapy for enhanced dose delivery efficiency and quality in radiotherapy
Authors: Kuo Li, Wei Zhang, Xingmin Ma, Qichao Zhou, Jian Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) is limited by its longitudinal field size for large targets, often requiring multiple isocenters, while Helical Tomotherapy (HT) offers superior longitudinal conformity but suffers from prolonged treatment times due to its narrow fan beam.PurposeThis simulation study proposes a novel spiral volumetric modulated arc therapy (SVMAT) technique designed to bridge this gap by synergizing continuous couch movement with dynamic MLC modulation.MethodsThe SVMAT technique was implemented on a model of ring‐gantry linac with dual‐layer staggered MLC. Its core is a direct aperture optimization algorithm that discretizes the delivery path into finite projections, concurrently optimizing MLC aperture, monitor unit weight, gantry angle, and couch position. A comprehensive dosimetric and efficiency comparison was conducted against state‐of‐the‐art VMAT and HT plans for three clinically challenging scenarios: hippocampal‐sparing whole‐brain radiotherapy (HS‐WBRT), bilateral breast radiotherapy (BBRT), and craniospinal irradiation (CSI), including a pediatric subgroup.ResultsSVMAT demonstrated comparable or superior target coverage and conformity index to VMAT and HT across all cases. Its most significant advantage was in organ‐at‐risk (OAR) sparing. For HS‐WBRT, SVMAT significantly reduced the maximum (Dmax) and mean (Dmean) doses to the hippocampus compared to both VMAT and HT (p < 0.05). For BBRT, SVMAT notably reduced the heart Dmean (4.78 ± 0.86 Gy vs. 9.47 ± 3.44 Gy) for VMAT. In CSI, SVMAT reduced the lens Dmax by over 33% and the heart Dmean by 36.1%. Also, the pediatric CSI analysis confirmed these benefits, with SVMAT significantly reducing doses to developing organs. Regarding efficiency, SVMAT's beam on time was significantly shorter than HT's across all plans (reductions of 33.1% to 55.3%) and was comparable to the multi‐isocenter VMAT in CSI.ConclusionsThe SVMAT technique successfully integrates the dynamic delivery of VMAT with the longitudinal integration and single‐isocenter capability of HT. By offering enhanced OAR sparing and reduced treatment times, SVMAT represents a significant advancement in radiotherapy, showing immense potential for improving outcomes, especially in vulnerable populations such as pediatric patients.

INTRODUCTION

Radiotherapy is a cornerstone in the modern management of malignant tumors, with the perpetual goal of delivering a lethal dose to the target volume while maximally sparing the surrounding organs at risk (OAR). The evolution of treatment techniques from three‐dimensional conformal radiotherapy to intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and subsequently to volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) has significantly enhanced dose conformity and treatment efficiency. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 VMAT, characterized by its simultaneous modulation of gantry rotation speed, dose rate, and multi‐leaf collimator (MLC) shape, achieves highly conformal dose distributions with markedly reduced delivery time compared to static IMRT. 3 , 5

Despite its widespread adoption and efficiency, conventional VMAT, typically delivered on C‐arm gantries with limited longitudinal field sizes (e.g., up to 40 cm but effectively constrained per rotation), faces challenges in treating large or complexly shaped targets, such as those in craniospinal irradiation (CSI) or extensive nodal volumes. This often necessitates multiple isocenters and couch shifts, potentially leading to dose inhomogeneity at the match lines and increased planning complexity. 6 , 7 Although numerous studies have evaluated dose uncertainty in junction areas between fields during radiotherapy of ultra‐long targets, the assessment of dose junctions remains unpredictable for secondary positioning due to setup errors and patient motion. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

An alternative solution for large target volumes is provided by Helical Tomotherapy (HT). With its ring gantry design and continuous couch translation, HT inherently offers superior longitudinal dose conformity and seamless treatment of extensive volumes using a single isocenter. 12 However, this advantage is counterbalanced by a critical limitation: its relatively narrow fan beam width (typically 1.25–5.04 cm), 13 Zhang et al. 14 demonstrated that smaller field widths yield better dose distribution but require more treatment time. For lengthy targets, this results in prolonged treatment times, which can increase the risk of intrafraction motion and reduce clinical throughput. Moreover, achieving the optimal balance between delivery efficiency and dose quality for a broad range of clinical indications remains an active area of investigation.

Recent research has explored integrating continuous couch motion with dynamic MLC modulation on standard C‐arm platforms, such as in dynamic trajectory radiotherapy 15 and dynamic mixed‐beam arc therapy, 16 to extend longitudinal coverage and improve OAR sparing. However, these C‐arm‐based techniques remain constrained by mechanical limitations: couch travel range and speed are restricted, collision risks persist during non‐coplanar deliveries, and trajectory planning often requires predefined or geometrically optimized paths, which may not fully exploit the dosimetric potential of continuous couch‐MLC coordination.

We have developed an innovative direct aperture optimization (DAO) 17 , 18 , 19 algorithm that integrates continuous couch translation with dynamic MLC modulation. This approach combines the dynamic delivery capabilities of VMAT with a significantly increased longitudinal field, potentially bridging the gap between conventional VMAT and HT. The spiral volumetric modulated arc therapy (SVMAT) technique, implemented on such a platform, leverages this hardware advancement. This algorithm employs a discrete projection framework. The delivery path is discretized into a finite set of projections, enabling the concurrent optimization of the MLC aperture, monitor unit (MU) weight, gantry angle, and couch position. This approach promises the efficiency and versatility of VMAT, the longitudinal integration and single‐isocenter capability of HT, and potentially superior OAR sparing through more degrees of freedom in the optimization process.

While the theoretical advantages of SVMAT are evident, a comprehensive dosimetric and efficiency comparison against established standards like conventional VMAT and HT across diverse clinical scenarios is crucial to validate its clinical utility. Critical clinical cases such as hippocampal‐sparing whole‐brain radiotherapy (HS‐WBRT), which demands exquisite dose reduction to the hippocampal region, 20 , 21 bilateral breast radiotherapy (BBRT) with involved nodal areas, 22 and CSI with its challenging target geometry, 23 serve as ideal benchmarks for evaluating this novel technique.

Therefore, this study aims to quantitatively evaluate the performance of the novel SVMAT technique by comparing it against state‐of‐the‐art VMAT and HT plans in three distinct and clinically challenging scenarios: HS‐WBRT, BBRT, and CSI. The evaluation will focus on dosimetric endpoints for both target coverage and OAR sparing, as well as delivery efficiency metrics.

METHODS

SVMAT model establishment

Treatment machine model specifications

All SVMAT plans were simulated on a NeuRT Aurora linear accelerator (Neusoft IntelliRay Technology, Shenyang, China), a circular‐gantry system featuring a 6 MV flattening‐filter‐free (FFF) beam, with a maximum dose rate of 1600 MU/min and gantry rotation up to 12°/s, enabling efficient and high‐precision arc and spiral irradiation. Beam shaping is performed by a dual‐layer MLC: the distal layer has 30 leaf pairs and the proximal layer has 29, each 1.0 cm wide at isocenter. The layers are staggered by 0.5 cm perpendicular to the leaf motion direction, yielding an effective fluence resolution of 0.5 cm. The system supports a 30 × 30 cm2 field at isocenter, with MLC leaf speeds up to 5 cm/s for rapid aperture modulation. Notably, it allows a longitudinal field width of up to 30 cm, far exceeding TOMO's 5 cm limit, enabling efficient treatment of large targets with minimal couch shifts.

Single‐isocenter realization in SVMAT

The “single‐isocenter” capability in SVMAT refers to a fixed reference point within the patient coordinate system, typically placed at the center of the target volume. Although the treatment couch translates continuously along the longitudinal (z) axis, the precise synchronization of the gantry angle θ(t), couch position z(t), and leaf positions M(t) ensures that the central axis of the radiation beam is always directed toward a fixed mechanical isocenter within the patient at every instant. This coordinated motion is achieved through discrete projection optimization. At each projection i, the system defines a unique combination of gantry angle θi and couch position zi, such that the beam's eye view (BEV) effectively rotates around and extends along this single isocenter. Consequently, the entire elongated target volume is continuously irradiated without the need for re‐positioning the patient or establishing multiple mechanical isocenters during treatment.

SVMAT optimization via discrete projections

In SVMAT, the delivery trajectory is characterized by four time‐varying parameters: gantry angle θ(t), dose rate D˙(t), leaf positions M(t), and couch velocity vc(t). Because these parameters evolve continuously during treatment, the optimization problem is inherently infinite‐dimensional. To render it tractable, we discretize the helical delivery path into a finite set of N control points (or “projections”), indexed by i=1,…,N. Each projection i is uniquely defined by a static configuration: (1) Pi=θi,D˙i,Mi,zi where θi is the gantry angle, D˙i is the dose rate (in MU/s), Mi={xj,ileft,xj,iright}j=1Nleaves denotes the positions of the left and right banks of the j‐th MLC leaf pair, and zi is the longitudinal couch position at projection i. The couch position is mechanically coupled to the gantry angle: for every gantry rotation increment Δ𝜃, the couch advances by a fixed distance Δz=k·Δθ, where k (in mm/deg) is a user‐defined pitch parameter set before optimization. Thus, the couch position at control point 𝑖 is zi=z0+k·(θi−θ0), and the instantaneous couch speed is derived from the optimized gantry speed θ˙(t) as vc(t)=k·θ˙(t). Only the gantry speed θ˙(t) (or equivalently, the time interval between control points) is optimized. Couch motion is fully determined by this relationship. For constant gantry speed, the couch position follows zi=z0+vcti, where vc is determined by the gantry speed constraint. Negative couch speed (indicating reverse movement) is permitted in the overall treatment plan but not within a single beam. Each beam has a single direction (positive or negative), which is specified before optimization. The optimization uses up to 1200 control points. If the planned gantry rotation exceeds 1200 points, the gantry angle spacing is increased (default 4°). Control points are spaced equally over gantry angle. Couch acceleration phase before the first control point is handled by the accelerator control system based on the initial speed requirement, with sufficient distance reserved for acceleration.

The DAO algorithm was executed with a maximum of 100 iterations with the final dose calculated on a 2.5×5.0×2.5mm3 grid. Notably, the couch position zi is not an independently optimized variable; it is mechanically coupled to the gantry angle through a fixed pitch parameter k set prior to optimization, that is, zi=z0+k·(θi−θ0). Thus, the couch trajectory is determined implicitly by the optimized gantry motion.

In DAO, both wi and Mi (hence Ai) are optimization variables, subject to MLC physical constraints (e.g., minimum leaf gap, maximum leaf speed between adjacent projections).

Dose deposition model

The total absorbed dose D(x) at a voxel x∈R3 in the patient is modeled as the superposition of contributions from all projections: (3) Dx=∑i=1Nwi·Dix;θi,zi,Mi where Di(x;θi,zi,Mi) is the dose influence kernel—that is, the dose deposited per unit MU at voxel x for projection i with fixed MLC aperture Mi, gantry angle θi, and couch position zi. This kernel encapsulates the full physics of photon transport, including primary and scattered components, and is precomputed using a convolution dose engine on a patient‐specific computed tomography (CT) dataset.

For computational efficiency, the kernel is factorized as: (4) Dix=∫BEVAir⊥·Kx|r⊥;θi,zidr⊥ where K(x∣r⊥;θi,zi) is the point dose kernel from a pencil beam incident at r⊥ in the BEV plane. In practice, this integral is discretized over the MLC‐defined open regions, and the dose matrix is precomputed and stored as a sparse matrix D∈RNvox×N, where each column corresponds to the dose distribution from one unit MU delivered through aperture Ai.

The non‐negativity constraint (wi ≥ 0) is enforced by clamping negative values to zero after each optimization iteration. During each iteration, the segment delivery time is set by the maximum of the leaf travel time (computed from the largest leaf bank displacement between consecutive control points divided by the leaf speed), the gantry rotation time (computed from the gantry angle difference divided by the gantry rotation rate)—which also dictates the allowable couch travel time—and the beam on time (BOT, computed from the MUs divided by the dose rate); this dominant time is then used to back‐calculate the required leaf speed, gantry rotation rate, couch velocity, and dose rate, with any values exceeding their machine‐specific physical limits iteratively clamped to ensure a feasible and constraint‐compliant delivery.

Because the aperture Ai is a discontinuous function of Mi, gradient‐based optimization is challenging. Our DAO implementation directly optimizes deliverable MLC apertures without fluence smoothing. The fluence for each aperture is computed using a commissioned physical model that accounts for dosimetric leaf gap, interleaf transmission, and tongue‐and‐groove leakage effects. Leaf positions are constrained to the MLC's mechanical limits (minimum gap: 0.25 cm; max speed: 5 cm/s), and MU weights are co‐optimized to ensure clinical deliverability. This approach eliminates the need for post‐optimization leaf sequencing and preserves dose accuracy throughout the spiral delivery.

Quality analysis of the treatment planning

Patient information

This study retrospectively selected 10 cases each of patients who underwent HS‐WBRT, BBRT, and CSI with HT at our hospital from January 2022 to October 2024. In addition, we included 10 cases of pediatric CSI targets for comparison to explore the clinical application of SVMAT in pediatric tumors. This study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (approval number: SDTHEC202510035).

Target area and OARs delineation

All patients undergoing whole‐brain irradiation with hippocampal sparing received both computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) simulation scans. The hippocampus was contoured using the T1‐weighted MR imaging axial sequence. The whole‐brain volume was defined as the clinical target volume (CTV). The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by expanding the CTV outward by 3 mm.

All breast CTVs were defined as CTV‐CW, including the chest wall and axillary lymph nodes (ALN) regions, and CTV‐LN, including the supraclavicular lymph node (SCL) region. The PTVs were generated by expanding the CTV‐CW outward by 8 mm to obtain PTV‐CW and then constricting it to the skin, and by expanding the CTV‐LN outward by 5 mm to obtain PTV‐LN and then trimming it to the skin at a depth of 3 mm. Throughout the treatment, a 5‐mm equivalent bolus was used on both sides of the chest wall to ensure the cumulative dose to the skin (a high‐risk area for recurrence).

The CSI target volume was divided into two structures: CTV‐Brain, which included the entire brain, and CTV‐Spinal, which included the entire spinal canal. The PTV‐Brain and PTV‐Spinal were contoured by expanding the CTV outward by 5 mm.

VMAT plans were created using the Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS, version 16.1, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA). Plans for the Halcyon accelerator utilizing the 6‐MV FFF beam mode consisted of several coplanar VMAT fields for each isocenter, with the collimator rotated to unique angles to reduce interleaf leakage dose. The Photon Optimizer (PO, v16.1) and Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA, v16.1) were employed for optimization and dose calculation, respectively.

For VMAT plans, the Photon Optimizer (Eclipse v16.1) was used with a final dose grid of 2.5×2.5×2.5mm3, and optimization was set to terminate upon meeting the default convergence criteria. For HT plans, the TomoTherapy TPS employed a similar iterative optimization process with a fine dose grid (2.5×2.5×2.5mm3) and a pitch optimization range between 0.1 and 0.5 depending on the target length.

Evaluation metrics

The dose volume histograms (DVHs) of the target and OARs and the RT plan files were exported from the three plans of the planning systems. For the target, the irradiated doses of 2% and 98% volume (D2, D98) were analyzed. The conformity index (CI), the homogeneity index (HI) and gradient index (GI) of the target were evaluated with the following: (6) CI=TVPIV2TV×TVRX where TVPIV represents the volume of the target receiving the prescription dose, TV represents the volume of the target and TVRX represents the total volume irradiated by the prescription dose. The CI ranges from 0 to 1, with higher CI values indicating better conformity.

The HI and GI are defined as the ratio of the dose difference between 2% and 98% of the target volume to the prescription dose, and the ratio of the volume of the 50% (PTV prescription dose) isodose line to the PTV volume, respectively. They describe the uniformity of the target dose and the steepness of the dose gradient. An HI value closer to 0 indicates better target dose uniformity, and a lower GI value indicates a faster dose fall‐off.

To compare target doses, the D2%, D98%, and the mean dose (Dmean) were compared for each disease site. For organ dose assessment, depending on the location of the organs adjacent to the disease site, the dosimetric parameters of the critical OARs were also calculated. The MU and beam‐delivering time estimated by the TPS for each plan were also compared.

Statistical analysis

Any differences among the forty patients according to the three treatment planning systems were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Non‐parametric statistical analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test to assess paired differences between plan types. As there were multiple metrics for comparison, the Benjamini–Hochberg correction method was employed to control the false discovery rate across multiple comparisons, where P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For BBRT, although the SVMAT plan had a certain reduction in V5, V20 and Dmean for lungs compared with the VMAT and HT plans, these slight differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Furthermore, the doses to the liver and heart are also noteworthy. The SVMAT plan achieved a marked reduction in the mean dose compared to the VMAT plan. Specifically, the mean liver dose decreased significantly from 5.62 ± 2.68 Gy to 2.88 ± 0.59 Gy (P = 0.038), while the mean heart dose was reduced from 9.47 ± 3.44 Gy to 4.78 ± 0.86 Gy (P = 0.001).

For CSI, the SVMAT plan demonstrated superiority in terms of dose to all OARs in the head, especially for the bilateral lens, where the SVMAT plan reduced the Dmax by more than 55% and 33% compared with the VMAT and HT plans. In the chest region, the SVMAT plan also significantly reduced the dose to the heart, with a 36.1% reduction in the Dmean compared with VMAT and 47.9% compared with the HT plan. Also, the SVMAT plan may reduce complications to the liver, as evidenced by the lower Dmean. In addition, we found that the 0° and 90° collimator had almost no impact on the dose distribution of the CSI plans.

Comparison of the pediatric CSI plans

We further investigated the potential advantages of the SVMAT technique in pediatric CSI during radiotherapy delivery in patients aged 4 years and under. A subset of 10 pediatric CSI cases was analyzed separately to evaluate the performance of SVMAT in this challenging patient population with the same parameters as those for the CSI cases.

The improvements are also observed for SVMAT plans, as illustrated in Figure 3. SVMAT significantly reduced the Dmax to the lenses (3.84 ± 0.53 Gy vs. 8.36 ± 2.19 Gy for VMAT, P = 0.009; vs. 6.88 ± 1.32 Gy for HT, P = 0.014), and SVMAT showed a notable reduction in lung exposure, with significantly lower V5 and V20. In addition, SVMAT reduced the mean liver dose and the mean kidney dose for VMAT and HT. Regarding delivery efficiency, SVMAT required a substantially shorter BOT (416.08 ± 42.24 s) compared to HT (838.04 ± 56.87 s, P = 0.009).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluates a novel delivery technique, designed to combine advantageous features of VMAT and HT. The core of SVMAT is a DAO algorithm that for the first time in this context concurrently optimizes MLC apertures and monitor units over a discretized helical trajectory with a fixed, predefined pitch coupling couch motion to gantry rotation. Our findings in the specific cases of HS‐WBRT, BBRT, and CSI suggest that this method has the potential to mitigate the longitudinal field constraint of VMAT and the efficiency limitation of HT, presenting a dosimetrically and operationally competitive solution for these challenging treatments.

Guyer et al. 15 and Zhu et al. 16 demonstrated the feasibility of dynamic table translations and rotations are incorporated into a continuous trajectory. Furthermore, clinical application of spiral‑arc delivery has been reported by Xu et al. 24 in the context of whole‑brain radiotherapy with integrated boost, where a couch‑moving helical technique showed dosimetric and efficiency improvements compared to conventional HT.

Critical innovation of the DAO algorithm

The critical innovation of SVMAT lies in its discrete projection‐based DAO algorithm, which concurrently optimizes MLC aperture shapes, monitor unit weights, gantry angles, and—crucially—couch positions. Traditional VMAT optimizes within a limited longitudinal field per rotation, often necessitating multiple isocenters 25 and complex feathering match‐lines 7 for large targets. Unlike traditional two‐step methods (fluence optimization followed by leaf sequencing), we simultaneously optimize the deliverable MLC aperture shape, defined by the binary function Ai(r⊥), and its associated monitor unit weight wi, are optimized simultaneously. This direct optimization of deliverable parameters eliminates the need for a separate leaf sequencer and preserves dose accuracy throughout the optimization.

The superior performance of SVMAT stems not from couch movement or MLC modulation in isolation, but from their synergistic co‐optimization. The algorithm leverages the couch motion to provide coarse longitudinal dose painting, effectively “scanning” the dose distribution along the patient's axis. Concurrently, the dynamic MLC system performs fine‐scale dose sculpting within each projected field. This dual‐layer modulation is mathematically encapsulated in the dose deposition model, where the dose influence kernel Di is contingent upon both the MLC aperture Mi and the couch position zi.

A key hardware enabler amplifying the algorithm's capabilities is the implementation of a dual‐layer staggered MLC. 26 , 27 It provides the necessary resolution to create complex, small aperture patterns while maintaining dose calculation accuracy and delivery reliability. The ability to modulate the effective field width by controlling the extent of the active MLC bank, combined with variable couch speed, allows for a dynamic trade‐off between dose resolution and delivery efficiency.

Mechanisms for superior OAR sparing

In OAR sparing, SVMAT demonstrates the powerful capability of “dynamic conformality.” For serial organs like the hippocampus and lens, its helical irradiation trajectory provides the opportunity for “enveloping” the target from hundreds of continuous angles, allowing high‐dose isodose lines to precisely avoid central structures. Concurrently, its use of a dual‐layer MLC possesses stronger instantaneous modulation capability, enabling the generation of more complex apertures at each projection to optimize dose gradients on a micro‐scale. 28 For parallel organs like the heart, liver, and lungs, the algorithmic innovation lies in the active longitudinal avoidance mechanism. Its single‐isocenter design fundamentally eliminates dose overlap and hot spots at the junctions inherent to multi‐isocenter VMAT, 11 , 29 , 30 thereby systematically reducing the dose to organs like the heart and lungs.

Regarding target dose quality, SVMAT exhibits the characteristics of a “synthesizer.” It inherits the high CI of VMAT, benefiting from the precise sculpting ability of its high‐resolution dual‐layer MLC. Simultaneously, it approaches or even surpasses the HI of HT, which is particularly evident in CSI. The underlying mechanism is the dose superposition effect inherent to helical irradiation—each voxel receives dose from hundreds of angles, dispersing the energy deposition. 31 This effectively avoids the high hot spots at field junctions (a drawback of VMAT) or potential inhomogeneity caused by single, long beam paths (a potential issue with HT). 32

The observed improvements are clinically relevant. Reduced doses to serial organs (e.g., hippocampus, lenses) directly lower risks of specific toxicities like neurocognitive decline and cataracts. 33 , 34 For parallel organs (e.g., heart, lungs), lower mean doses correlate with decreased long‐term complication risks. 35 , 36 The significantly shorter beam‐on time compared to HT improves patient comfort, reduces intrafraction motion, and is particularly beneficial for pediatric or anxious patients.

Advantages in pediatric radiotherapy

The subgroup analysis of pediatric CSI underscores the particular promise and dual advantages of SVMAT in pediatric radiotherapy. Its ability to comprehensively reduce doses to key developing organs such as the lenses, parotids, heart, lungs, and abdominal viscera is directly linked to a lowered risk of severe long‐term sequelae like cataracts, xerostomia, cardiomyopathy, and secondary tumors, crucially impacting the quality of life for decades to come. 37 , 38 , 39 Furthermore, SVMAT combines superior dosimetry with enhanced efficiency. Its significantly shorter BOT compared to HT, for children with poor self‐control often requiring anesthesia, substantially reduces anesthesia risks, decreases the probability of intrafraction motion, increases treatment success rates, and improves departmental throughput. Although VMAT's BOT was shorter in some instances, the complexity of its multi‐isocenter design and its comprehensive disadvantages in OAR sparing make the single‐isocenter, efficient, and comprehensively OAR‐protecting SVMAT a more ideal and safer choice for pediatric radiotherapy, especially for CSI.

Strategic trade‐offs in planning parameters

The strategic selection of MLC angle and field width in SVMAT planning reflects a deliberate trade‐off between dosimetric precision and delivery efficiency. For targets with limited longitudinal extent, such as in HS‐WBRT and BBRT, a MLC angle of 90° was employed. In this configuration, the narrower field width enhances the system's ability to perform fine‐scale dose sculpting in the longitudinal direction, thereby improving dose gradient sharpness near critical structures without substantially prolonging delivery time. In contrast, for extensive targets such as CSI, a MLC angle of 0° was selected in our study. This orientation engages all available MLC leaf pairs in the optimization process, effectively maximizing the device's modulation capacity and enabling efficient longitudinal dose painting over large volumes. While a 90° collimator combined with a reduced field width might theoretically offer superior dose conformality, our investigation into balancing plan quality and treatment efficiency revealed that this advantage is marginal (Figure 3) in CSI, possibly due to the greater distance between the target and OARs. Conversely, such a configuration significantly increases the number of gantry rotations (21.52 vs. 6.48) and BOT (722.80 s vs. 373.26 s), thereby compromising treatment efficiency—a critical consideration in clinical practice, particularly for pediatric patients requiring anesthesia. Furthermore, the implementation of automated, non‐uniform gantry velocity in SVMAT is a critical feature that mitigates the influence of the preset field width on the final plan quality. The dynamic couch movement inherently compensates for different field widths by adjusting the speed of longitudinal dose painting, ensuring consistent dose conformity and OAR sparing.

Limitations and clinical considerations

A key consideration for SVMAT arises when a serial OAR is fully encompassed within the PTV, as with the brainstem and spinal cord in HS‐WBRT and CSI. Here, HT's small projected beam width at each instant allows the optimizer to employ highly irregular MLC patterns to sculpt sharp dose gradients within the target volume itself. However, the current research mainly focuses on the dosimetric comparison of plan design and algorithm performance, while physical verification is the key next step.

CONCLUSION

In summary, SVMAT achieves an overall leap in dose distribution quality through the integration of its three critical technological pillars: the multi‐angular irradiation provided by the helical trajectory, the synergistic optimization freedom enabled by the DAO algorithm, and the fine dose sculpting capability realized by the high‐resolution MLC. Particularly in pediatric radiotherapy, where extreme demands are placed on dose sensitivity and treatment efficiency, SVMAT demonstrates tremendous potential surpassing existing technologies, positioning it as a transformative clinical tool capable of providing more precise, safer, and more patient‐centric treatment options.
