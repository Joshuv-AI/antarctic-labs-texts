Critical Pragmatism as a Paradigm for Nursing Research
Authors: Renée Gordon, Marjan Kekanovich, Aleksandra Zuk, Marian Luctkar‐Flude, Danielle Macdonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis manuscript explores critical pragmatism as a philosophical paradigm for nursing research. Critical pragmatism integrates the action‐oriented focus of pragmatism with the emancipatory aims of critical paradigms, offering a perspective that is both practically grounded and oriented toward social transformation. We outline the paradigm's philosophical foundations, including its roots in pragmatism and critical paradigms. Drawing on the doctoral work of R.G. and M.K. as illustrative examples, we conceptualize critical pragmatism as a continuum that accommodates diverse perspectives with shared ontological, epistemological, and axiological commitments. We present critical pragmatism as a promising paradigm for nursing research, with the potential to strengthen the relevance, applicability, and impact of nursing scholarship in both practice and academic contexts.

Introduction

In nursing research, identifying one's research paradigm is crucial because it makes explicit the researcher's worldview and the values and assumptions that shape how a problem is defined, whose knowledge is privileged, and how solutions are pursued in response to the research question(s) being investigated (Kivunja and Kuyini 2017). As an applied and practice‐oriented discipline, nursing research is ethically grounded in an obligation to generate knowledge that contributes to improved health, well‐being, and functioning of individuals, communities, and populations (Sherwood 2024). Common paradigms in nursing research include positivism, post‐positivism, critical theory (Lincoln et al. 2023), interpretivism, pragmatism (Weaver and Olson 2006), postmodernism, and poststructuralism (Cheek and Aston 2024), each with distinct strengths and limitations for addressing complex nursing problems. Collectively, researchers working within these paradigms have advanced nursing knowledge, yet persistent tensions between generating actionable solutions and attending to issues of power, context, and equity remain. While uncommon in nursing scholarship, critical pragmatism has been applied in disciplines such as urban planning and teaching (Forester 2012; Jones and Hall 2021); in this paper, we argue that adopting a critical pragmatist paradigm in nursing research offers a promising approach to integrate practical inquiry with a commitment to social justice.

As an applied discipline, nursing is deeply rooted in both science and human experience, requiring research paradigms that embrace multiple epistemologies. Nursing problems are complex, context‐dependent, and cannot be fully understood through a single way of knowing (Weaver and Olson 2006). While research conducted within established paradigms has contributed significantly to advancing nursing knowledge, there remains a need for a philosophical perspective that links critical approaches with practical solutions. Praxis, the dynamic interplay between theory and practice facilitated through ongoing reflection, is essential to the evolution of nursing scholarship because it makes explicit the consequences of ideas in practice, enabling scholars to evaluate and refine nursing knowledge based on its practical implications (Schön 2017; Thorne and Hayes 1997). This approach enables nursing scholars to interrogate complex, real‐world problems while simultaneously developing and applying contextually relevant interventions. Within this context, we present critical pragmatism as a promising research paradigm for nursing by integrating critique, action, and reflection to support meaningful change.

Critical pragmatism is a distinct paradigm combining critical theory's focus on the critique of power structures and emancipation (Guba et al. 1994) with pragmatism's focus on real‐world transformation and problem‐solving (Kaushik and Walsh 2019). Critical pragmatism is both emancipatory and outcome‐driven, making it particularly relevant for contemporary nursing issues. While critical pragmatism appears to be unexplored within the context of nursing research to date, it offers an approach well‐suited to address complex healthcare challenges. Critical pragmatists emphasize action, reflection, and the iterative process of learning from experience (Ulrich 2007a), aligning with the applied nature of nursing.

As PhD nursing students, R.G. and M.K. are philosophically aligned with critical pragmatism. While examples of nursing research using either critical theory or pragmatism exist, we were unable to locate studies explicitly identifying critical pragmatism as a research paradigm. This gap motivated us as lead authors (R.G. and M.K.), in collaboration with interested nursing faculty (A.Z., M.L.‐F., and D.M.), to explore this paradigm further, both to deepen our understanding and to contribute to the broader discourse about research paradigms in nursing. In this manuscript, R.G. and M.K. developed the conceptual interpretation of critical pragmatism, including its framing as a continuum, and shared examples from their doctoral research, while A.Z., M.L.‐F., and D.M. provided guidance. We believe critical pragmatism offers a valuable philosophical framework for nurse researchers, one that aligns with our emancipatory and transformative approaches to nursing scholarship.

The purpose of this paper is to provide an overview of critical pragmatism and its relevance to nursing research. We begin by summarizing the historical and philosophical foundations of pragmatism and critical paradigms, then discuss how these paradigms converge within critical pragmatism. Next, we illustrate the application of critical pragmatism in nursing research using examples from our doctoral studies. Finally, we explore the alignment of this paradigm with nursing practice and research.

Pragmatism and Critical Paradigms

To ensure a shared understanding, this section outlines our interpretations of pragmatism and critical paradigms prior to describing critical pragmatism. We identify the conceptual gaps inherent in each paradigm when used in isolation, and we illustrate how critical pragmatism integrates these traditions along a continuum, showing capacity to address these gaps.

Pragmatism

Pragmatism is a family of related philosophical approaches rather than a single unified theory, with a core assertion that knowledge, ideas, and actions should be considered in terms of their practical application and impacts (McCaslin 2012). Within this tradition, there is variation across thinkers such as Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, and John Dewey (McCaslin 2012), who at times differ in their ontological, epistemological, axiological, and methodological commitments (Mayumi and Ota 2023; Pettifer and Hughes 2025). In this paper, we focus on Deweyan pragmatism, given its central role in the critical pragmatism literature (Forester 2012; Ulrich 2007a; Kadlec 2006). In the following sections, we outline the historical development of pragmatism, focusing on Dewey's contributions, and its application in nursing research.

History of Pragmatism

Pragmatism originated in the United States in the late 19th century, first conceptualized as classical American Pragmatism by Charles Sanders Peirce, who purported that the meaning of a concept lies in its observable effects (Doane and Varcoe 2005; Hartshorne and Weiss 1935). This philosophical orientation originated during a period of rapid industrialization, scientific advancement, and social transformation. Peirce introduced pragmatism as a response to abstract rationalism and fixed truths, instead grounding meaning in inquiry and the practical consequences of ideas as they are tested in experience. For example, according to Peirce's principle of pragmatism, the concept of “health” was understood in terms of its tangible outcomes, such as improved physical functioning, emotional well‐being, and the ability to perform daily activities rather than as an abstract idea.

Classical American pragmatism was later popularized by William James, a student of Pierce, who focused on how ideas work in everyday life and stressed that truth is fluid and shaped by experiences (James 1907). Extending the earlier example of “health,” James' expansion foregrounds the individual's subjective experiences, recognizing that health is reflected not only in observable outcomes but also in how those outcomes are perceived. In the early 20th century, John Dewey, a student of James, expanded classical American pragmatism in educational and political contexts, advocating for experiential learning and the application of egalitarian ideas to improve social issues (Kadlec 2006; Dewey 1929).

Deweyan Pragmatism

For the purposes of discussing critical pragmatism, we focus here on Deweyan pragmatism, which serves as the primary reference point in the existing literature on critical pragmatism (Forester 2012; Ulrich 2007a; Kadlec 2006). For Dewey, reality is emergent and experienced through interaction with the environment (Dewey 1929). Reality is not fixed or pre‐existing but is contextually situated and understood differently depending on individuals' perspectives, experiences, and circumstances (Mayumi and Ota 2023; Kadlec 2006; Dewey 1929). While reality itself is shared, individuals interpret and experience it differently, so multiple perspectives and experiences naturally coexist (Dewey 1929).

In Deweyan pragmatism, knowledge develops through engagement with practical situations and is evaluated by how successfully it resolves problematic situations and guides action, rather than by its representation of fixed or pre‐existing truths (Chiapello and Bousbaci 2022; Dewey 1939). Dewey's instrumentalist epistemology emphasizes that knowledge is active, fallible, and socially mediated, arising through purposeful interaction with the environment and others (Dewey 1939; Henne 2023). Humans learn and understand the world by acting purposefully, reflecting on outcomes, and adjusting practice accordingly (Chiapello and Bousbaci 2022; Dewey 1939). Reflection is integral to this process: for Dewey, thinking and doing are inseparable, as knowledge is generated through action (Chiapello and Bousbaci 2022). This reflective process often involves immediate, on‐the‐spot adjustments (later conceptualized as “reflection‐in‐action” by Schön [2017]), allowing knowledge to evolve in real time (Chiapello and Bousbaci 2022).

Axiologically, Dewey views values as instrumental, arising through experience, rather than as fixed or abstract standards (Dewey 1922, 1972). Ethical evaluation is therefore not based on applying pre‐given moral rules, but on reflective assessment of how different courses of action transform problematic situations (Dewey 1939, 1922). For Dewey, ethical action is inseparable from social and political engagement, meaning that claims about what should be done are grounded in context‐sensitive inquiry and ultimately evaluated by their capacity to improve conditions of freedom, democratic participation, and human flourishing (Kadlec 2006; Dewey 1947).

Pragmatic researchers may be drawn to specific lines of inquiry through personally experienced contextual and social factors, such as a nurse identifying a research problem based on their professional experiences (Morgan 2014). This experiential grounding shapes the research process, including the formulation of research questions and research design. From Dewey's instrumentalist perspective, ideas, theories, and methods function as tools, chosen for their ability to address the practical problem at hand (Dewey 1929, 1939, 1947).

In nursing research, pragmatism has developed alongside the profession's focus on practical, real‐world solutions to healthcare problems (Dolan et al. 2022). Weaver and Olson (Weaver and Olson 2006) advocate for a pragmatic approach to nursing inquiry that emphasizes the usefulness of knowledge in practice, the integration of perspectives across paradigms, and the development of theory grounded in real‐world contexts. This orientation supports inquiry that is responsive to practice needs and encourages the use of methods suited to generating meaningful and actionable knowledge (Dolan et al. 2022).

Critiques

While some have argued that Deweyan pragmatism is inherently critical (Kadlec 2006; Midtgarden 2012; Bohman 2002), it has also been criticized as complacent and utilitarian, used as a tool to support existing social and political structures, on the grounds that its focus on practical outcomes may privilege efficiency over critique of power (Midtgarden 2012; White 2004). Some critics have argued that Dewey, while oriented toward democratic problem‐solving, may lack the critical perspective needed to fully account for historically embedded power relations and structural forms of oppression (White 2004; Habermas and Fultner 2003; West 1989). Additionally, pragmatism is often broadly described in research methodology literature as a paradigm focused on problem‐solving, practical consequences, and methodological flexibility (Creswell and Poth 2018), with less emphasis on its critical and reflexive dimensions (Kadlec 2006; Dewey 1929).

Approaching research from a pragmatic paradigm without consideration of systemic factors and broader social influences can inadvertently reinforce these structures and, in the context of nursing research, place responsibility for changing one's health situation on individuals (Kadlec 2006; Midtgarden 2012). These critiques suggest that pragmatism alone may be insufficient when the research problem is deeply entangled with power, inequity, and structural injustice. It is within this space that critical theory can complement the practical orientation emphasized in pragmatism by foregrounding historical context, structural determinants, and power dynamics.

For example, a pragmatist researching the management of diabetes may focus on elements such as diet, exercise, and/or medication adherence, proposing interventions that encourage those living with the illness to modify their behaviors. While such approaches may generate actionable, contextually grounded knowledge, they risk limited impact when political and social constraints shaping those behaviors remain unexamined. In such cases, pragmatic solutions may be implemented within existing structures, without a critical perspective to ensure that conclusions and solutions have a sustainable and larger impact.

Critical Paradigms

Critical paradigms are philosophical approaches that seek to analyze and critique society, with a focus on understanding power, social structures, and inequities (Guba et al. 1994; Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022). Traditionally, critical theory is attributed to the work of early 20th‐century German philosophers (Agger 1991), though the term has expanded to include other critically oriented approaches, such as critical race, feminist, and postcolonial theories (Lincoln et al. 2023). Postmodernism and poststructuralism can also be considered critical paradigms (Lincoln et al. 2023). Although they share some historical roots with traditional critical theory, they are distinct paradigms with different philosophical underpinnings (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Agger 1991).

Consistent with existing literature, our understanding of critical theory as it relates to critical pragmatism is informed primarily by scholars from the Frankfurt School (Agger 1991). We also consider postmodern and poststructuralist paradigms in this discussion as we believe their ontological assumptions, particularly the focus on context and the situated nature of experience, strengthen the alignment of pragmatism and critical paradigms within critical pragmatism. This ontological alignment is discussed in detail in a later section.

It is important to acknowledge that although there may be areas of convergence with certain critical paradigms discussed in this paper, Indigenous methodologies are rooted in fundamentally different worldviews that should not be subsumed within Western or colonial frameworks. Indigenous methodologies are distinct, sovereign approaches grounded in and guided by their own epistemic traditions and in culturally specific, relational, and place‐based ways of knowing (Bourque Bearskin et al. 2016; Kovach 2021). In recognition of their distinctiveness and our positionality as settler scholars, we do not attempt to represent or interpret Indigenous methodologies here.

The Frankfurt School

The origin of critical theory is most frequently attributed to the work of philosophers and social theorists associated with the Institute for Social Research at Goethe University in Frankfurt, Germany, including Theodor Adorno, Max Horkheimer, Herbert Marcuse, and Jurgën Habermas, beginning in the 1920s (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Agger 1991; Bronner 2002). Often referred to collectively as the Frankfurt School, this group was established amid significant political upheaval in Europe and sought to understand German society from a Marxist perspective (Bronner 2002). As the director of the Institute, Horkheimer (1972) introduced critical theory in opposition to traditional theory (i.e., positivism), describing it as a social critique designed to affect social change and emancipation. Since its inception, critical theorists have engaged in ongoing debates about how best to define and develop the tradition of critical theory (Mill et al. 2001). Although its early foundations were rooted in Marxism, modern critical theorists have extensively critiqued Marxist philosophy, contributing to the evolution of critical theory as a distinct paradigm (Bronner 2002; Morrow 1985).

Critical theorists adopt a historical realist1 ontology, which holds that a single, objective reality exists, but that individual experiences of this reality are embedded within and influenced by social, political, cultural, and economic structures that are shaped by history (Kivunja and Kuyini 2017; Guba et al. 1994). Epistemologically, critical theorists believe that truth and knowledge are legitimized by those who hold power within a social context, and what can be known is mediated by the relationship between the inquirer and the object or group being examined (Guba et al. 1994; Budd 2008). Axiologically, critical theorists prioritize social justice, emancipation, and critical reflection, with the aim of informing efforts to challenge and transform the power structures that create and sustain injustice (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Agger 1991). Researchers adopting a critical theory paradigm are thus guided by an awareness of social inequities, and may critique history, ideology, and everyday life to identify oppressive structures and opportunities for emancipation (Budd 2008).

Researchers in a critical theory paradigm apply methodologies to uncover power relations, with the aim of contributing to emancipation and social justice (Kivunja and Kuyini 2017). In collaboration with communities, researchers using a critical theory paradigm are active participants in the research process (Mosqueda Díaz et al. 2014). Critical methodologies, such as critical ethnography (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022), critical discourse analysis (Wodak 1991), and participatory action research (Robinson 1995), strive for emancipation and empowerment (Kivunja and Kuyini 2017).

Like pragmatism, reflection is central to critical theory, encompassing both the research process and the researchers' positionality within the broader social context (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Freyenhagen 2018). Reflexivity involves critically examining one's values, assumptions, and social position, and considering how these shape research aims and interpretations (Freyenhagen 2018; Fook 1999; Thompson and Pascal 2012). Importantly, reflexivity is an ongoing practice that seeks to make assumptions visible and open to scrutiny, acknowledging that bias cannot be fully removed but must be continually examined. Critical reflexivity, or reflection‐for‐action, further interrogates the historical, social, and political structures that influence both the researcher and the research context (Patel and Metersky 2022), challenging taken‐for‐granted assumptions that uphold systems of oppression (Thompson and Pascal 2012; Brookfield 2009).

Postmodernism and Poststructuralism

Postmodernism and poststructuralism are critical paradigms that have also evolved from the work of early critical theorists, originating in France in the mid‐20th century (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Agger 1991). Postmodernism and poststructuralism are closely related paradigms with different orientations, both drawing from many of the same philosophical influences, including the work of Michel Foucault, Jacques Derrida, and Jean‐François Lyotard (Agger 1991). While postmodernists emphasize analysis of cultural and historical phenomena, poststructuralists are concerned with the analysis of language, meaning, and discourses (Cheek and Aston 2024; Agger 1991). Postmodernism has been used in nursing research in a variety of ways; for example, to critique dominant healthcare discourses such as evidence‐based practice (Griffiths 2005), and to explore how postmodern feminist perspectives challenge conventional assumptions in nursing research processes and knowledge production (Aranda 2006). Poststructural approaches have also been applied in nursing research; for example, to explore patient–nurse interactions through a feminist poststructural analysis of institutional discourses shaping care negotiations (Griscti et al. 2016), and to examine how power, discourse, and technology affect patients' and carers' experiences in palliative home care (Nagington et al. 2016). These paradigms differ ontologically and epistemologically from critical theory (Agger 1991; Heslop 1997).

Often associated with the work of French philosophers Lyotard and Baudrillard, postmodernism began as a critique of modernist thought in disciplines such as art, architecture, and media (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Agger 1991). Cheek (2000), a nursing scholar, describes postmodern thought as rejecting “grand theories,” meaning any attempts to explain histories or social structures in their entirety (p. 19). Postmodernists embrace paradigmatic pluralism and are concerned with challenging taken‐for‐granted assumptions and social structures to generate new possibilities (C. Holmes and Warelow 2000). They reject totalizing social theories such as Marxism, instead exploring experiences from multiple perspectives (Agger 1991).

Poststructuralists argue that meanings are constructed through discourses, which in turn shape social practices (Agger 1991; Heslop 1997). Influenced by philosophers such as Jacques Derrida, Michel Foucault, Gilles Deleuze, and Félix Guattari, poststructuralists critically examine how knowledge and discourses are produced and maintained (Agger 1991; D. Holmes and Gagnon 2018). They explore how power shapes what is recognized as legitimate knowledge, which then becomes embedded in social norms (Heslop 1997). By deconstructing language, poststructuralists expose the underlying assumptions and power dynamics within discourses and offer alternative ways of knowing and being (Agger 1991; Heslop 1997; D. Holmes and Gagnon 2018).

Ontologically, unlike the historical realist perspective held by critical theorists, postmodernists and poststructuralists reject the assumption of a single, objective reality and instead assert experiences and interpretations of the world are socially constructed, context‐dependent, and mediated by power relations (Agger 1991). For postmodernists and poststructuralists, any representation of reality can only ever be partially understood (Cheek 1999).

Postmodern and poststructural epistemology involves a rejection of metanarratives and grand theories; a single, ultimate truth does not exist (Agger 1991). Rather, knowledge is specific, local, and based on the knower's personal history and relationships (Agger 1991; D. Holmes and Gagnon 2018; Watson 1995). In the absence of ultimate truth, knowledge is not discovered; rather, it is uniquely perceived and created by the knowers (D. Holmes and Gagnon 2018; Watson 1995). Postmodernists and poststructuralists consider knowledge to be subjective and pluralistic and acknowledge multiple ways of knowing (Cheek 1999).

Axiologically, postmodern and poststructural researchers problematize the dominant Western worldview, also referred to as modern thought (Watson 1995). Modern thinking positions science as truth and knowledge as a product that supports dominant structures (Watson 1995). Postmodernists and poststructuralists, in contrast, attempt to expose and deconstruct the assumptions that underpin modern constructs (Cheek 2000; C. Holmes and Warelow 2000). Postmodern and poststructural perspectives allow researchers to uncover and deconstruct the assumptions and structures that perpetuate oppression, and to imagine new possibilities (D. Holmes and Gagnon 2018; Watson 1995).

Some postmodern and poststructural researchers have rejected the modern understanding of research methodology (Cheek 1999; Stewart et al. 2021). Instead of using prescriptive research methodologies to understand specific phenomena, Stewart et al. (2021) argue that postmodernists and poststructuralists use strategies to uncover context‐specific understandings. Researchers in these paradigms choose strategies that accept contradictions, deconstruct modern ideas, and generate more questions (D. Holmes and Gagnon 2018; Cheek 1999; Stewart et al. 2021).

Other Critical Perspectives

Although the predominant understanding of critical theory originated in Western Europe, other critical theories have been developed independently and have shaped the current understanding of critical theory as a research paradigm (Lincoln et al. 2023; Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022). For example, critical theory has historically ignored gender‐based oppression (Marshall 1988), and feminist scholars have argued the need for a feminist critical theory to further the emancipatory goals of critical theory (Fraser 1985). Similarly, race‐based oppression has been a focus of other scholars, leading to the conceptualization of intersectionality (Crenshaw 1991) and Critical Race Theory (Ryoo and McLaren 2010).

Critical theorists emphasize the social, cultural, political, and historical contexts that have contributed to oppression, providing nurse researchers with a lens to consider power structures that influence health and well‐being (Weaver and Olson 2006). Nurse researchers across diverse settings have drawn on these and other critical theories as research paradigms to highlight inequities, bring attention to power imbalances, and advance equity‐oriented scholarship (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Mosqueda Díaz et al. 2014).

Critiques of Critical Theory

Critical theory provides a valuable foundation for understanding and addressing oppression and social injustice; however, some have criticized its focus on power dynamics and social structures at the expense of individual perspectives (Campbell and Bunting 1991). Researchers within a critical theory paradigm often focus on societal problems defined by a collective and consider how power dynamics and social structures influence the problem (White 2004; Campbell and Bunting 1991). Consequently, researchers from a critical theory paradigm may fail to consider individual perspectives within the collective, instead viewing the group as homogenous and attributing the problem in question to all members (White 2004; Campbell and Bunting 1991).

Further, many critical theorists' ontological assumption that a single reality can be understood in the context of historical events (i.e., historical realism) has been critiqued for its colonial underpinnings (Allen 2016; Jaramillo‐Aristizabal 2022; Law 2015). Jaramillo‐Aristizabal (2022), for example, argues that critical theory remains grounded in European and Anglo‐American intellectual traditions that frequently overlook, fail to recognize, or fail to engage with non‐colonial ontologies, such as the distinct ways of knowing, being, and relating that are central to Black and Indigenous communities. Through a historical realist ontology (Law 2015), critical theorists reinforce a linear, progress‐oriented understanding of history (Allen 2016) that marginalizes plural and relational ontologies (Jaramillo‐Aristizabal 2022). This reliance on colonial assumptions raises concern that critical theorists, despite their emancipatory aims, may inadvertently reproduce or reinforce the very structures of dominance they aim to critique (Jaramillo‐Aristizabal 2022).

In addition to these ontological concerns, critical theory's relationship to academia and its potential to reproduce unequal power dynamics within research has also been critiqued. The power structures inherent in academia mean members of the community participating in the research may begin with less power than the researcher (Campbell and Bunting 1991; Ellsworth 1989), thereby favoring the researcher's perspectives and values, either implicitly or explicitly, over those of other team members or the study participants (Ellsworth 1989). This imbalance may paternalize participants and bias all aspects of the research process (Campbell and Bunting 1991; Ellsworth 1989).

Continuing with the earlier example of a nurse researching diabetes management, a critical theorist may focus on understanding and challenging the structural factors that shape health outcomes for this population. This could involve exploring how broader inequities (e.g., socioeconomic inequities, systemic racism) intersect with institutional structures (e.g., healthcare policies, practice guidelines, and funding priorities) to influence access to care and treatment decisions for people living with diabetes. Solutions proposed by a critical theorist might include advocating for policy reforms aimed at reducing systemic inequities that contribute to health inequities. However, while critical theorists focus on these broader structural factors, they may pay less attention to the lived experiences and perceptions of individuals living with diabetes, or the need for immediate, practical interventions.

Critiques of Postmodernism and Poststructuralism

Postmodernism and poststructuralism are valuable approaches for challenging dominant discourses and established norms, but some have criticized their lack of focus on actionable solutions (Cheek and Porter 1997; Pilgrim 2000), arguing that their emphasis on relativism and local knowledge often limits their capacity to promote tangible change within existing systems (Cheek and Porter 1997).

While critical theory has been critiqued for emphasizing collective experience, postmodernism and poststructuralism have drawn criticism for focusing too narrowly on individual perspectives, which some argue limits the potential for political and social action aimed at systemic change (Beyer and Listen 1992; Traynor 1997). For example, Beyer and Listen (1992) argue that the postmodern rejection of metanarratives and focus on local knowledge can hinder efforts to address structural issues like racism and classism, which require an understanding of broader social influences. Hartsock (1989) echoes this concern, questioning “why is it that just at the moment when so many of us who have been silenced begin to demand the right to name ourselves, to act as subjects rather than objects of history, that just then the concept of subjecthood becomes problematic” (p. 163). Her critique highlights the tensions between the postmodern and poststructural rejection of fixed identity categories and the need for historically oppressed groups to draw on shared identities in their efforts to challenge systemic injustice.

From a postmodern or poststructural perspective, a researcher exploring diabetes management may challenge dominant discourses, such as the authority of the medical model, that shape how diabetes care is understood and practiced. For example, this researcher may question reliance on standardized treatment protocols and explore alternative ways of understanding and supporting individuals living with diabetes. This approach offers valuable insights that can challenge taken‐for‐granted assumptions and inform innovative, individualized care. However, by emphasizing local knowledge and rejecting universal narratives, postmodern and poststructural approaches can make it more difficult to address shared experiences caused by broader structural factors (e.g., racism, poverty, and food insecurity) that influence the health and well‐being of people living with diabetes.

Final Thoughts on Pragmatism and Critical Paradigms

This discussion of pragmatism and critical paradigms highlights the need for a research paradigm in nursing that integrates both practical, experiential inquiry and attention to social, historical, and discursive influences on knowledge, power, and experiences. Pragmatism focuses on practical, solution‐oriented, and contextually grounded insights, while critical paradigms emphasize the importance of considering the influence of history, power, and social structures. In our view, relying on pragmatism or critical paradigms alone risks overlooking important aspects emphasized by the other. Therefore, we argue for a paradigm that intentionally prioritizes both. By combining their strengths, we present critical pragmatism as an alternative paradigm that can support researchers to anticipate, identify, and address the complexities encountered in nursing and healthcare research.

The Evolution of Critical Pragmatism

Critical pragmatism is a research paradigm that intentionally integrates pragmatism and critical theory, emphasizing the axiological importance of social critique in critical theory and the pragmatic interest of addressing practical problems (Ulrich 2007b). It aims to “move us from a deconstructive skepticism toward a reconstructive imagination” (Forester 2012). While these paradigms are not commonly paired, a connection between pragmatism and critical theory can be found within Deweyan pragmatism, through his attention to issues of power and the importance of reflective practice (Kadlec 2006; Bohman 2002).

Dewey's concept of reflective practice was expanded by Schön, a pragmatist, in his text The Reflective Practitioner, through the assertion that orienting theories require conscious consideration and even reframing when outcomes differ from expectations, through reflection (Forester 2012; Schön 2017). He distinguished between two forms of reflection: reflection‐in‐action, which occurs during practice and allows for immediate adjustments, and reflective practice, or reflection‐on‐action, which involves stepping back and reviewing ones' actions after an event (Schön 2017; Chiapello and Bousbaci 2022). Thompson and Pascal (2012) extend this lineage through the idea of critical reflexivity, or reflection‐for‐action, which deepens reflection by explicitly considering the influence of context, history, and power relations on actions. In this way, reflection links the two paradigms: critical pragmatism integrates reflective practice (from pragmatism) with critical reflexivity (from critical theory), making it well‐suited for applied use within what is considered a “philosophy for professionals” (Ulrich 2007a).

Connecting pragmatism and critical theory is problematic for some, given the seemingly juxtaposed epistemological variance between these two paradigms (Kadlec 2006). These critiques are centered on the assumption that the antifoundational epistemology of Dewey's pragmatism, the rejection of permanent, unchanging standards for determining truth (Lincoln et al. 2023), is incompatible with a critical paradigm (Kadlec 2006). These critiques stem from historical tensions between American pragmatists and members of the Frankfurt School, which have contributed to misunderstandings of pragmatism and obscured the critical aspects of Dewey's work (Kadlec 2006). In this section, we will discuss the commensurability between critical and pragmatic paradigms and provide an overview of our understanding of critical pragmatism.

Ontologically, critical theory, as originally conceptualized by the Frankfurt School, presupposes a historical realist orientation (Lincoln et al. 2023), in which a single reality is shaped by historical, social, political, and economic conditions. This stance differs from Deweyan pragmatism, in which reality is emergent and transactional, experienced through interaction with the environment, with multiple perspectives and experiences coexisting and interpreted in context (Dewey 1929, 1939). These differences suggest an ontological gap: whereas critical theory emphasizes uncovering and critiquing the underlying structures of a historically mediated reality (Paradis‐Gagné and Pariseau‐Legault 2022; Agger 1991), Dewey emphasizes that understanding reality is an active, experiential, and situational process, shaped through ongoing reflection and engagement (Dewey 1929; Chiapello and Bousbaci 2022).

However, not all critical theories share this ontological perspective. Many contemporary critical approaches, particularly those informed by postmodernism and poststructuralism (Lincoln et al. 2023; Watson 1995), are ontologically consistent with pragmatism, given their attention to lived experiences and contextual understandings. By attending to both power and context, postmodern and poststructuralist perspectives help bridge the ontological gap between critical paradigms and pragmatism, linking structural analyses of power with attention to lived experience.

Despite enduring differences in assumptions about the nature and limits of reality and what can be known, Lincoln et al. (2023) argue for paradigmatic commensurability, suggesting that, except for (post)positivism, meaningful dialogue and integration can occur across paradigms, so long as they are grounded in similar philosophical assumptions or their foundational commitments resonate closely with one another. This commensurability is highlighted by both Kadlec (2006), who discussed the critical orientation of Dewey's pragmatism, and White (2004), who discussed the pragmatic dimensions of critical theory.

Epistemologically, many pragmatists, including Dewey, consider knowledge to be actively generated, fallible, and co‐created through relationships and interactions that vary with time and place (McCaslin 2012; Dewey 1929, 1939). Critical theory similarly recognizes that what can be known is mediated by social and historical structures, including relations of power, and cannot be separated from context (Guba et al. 1994). Both perspectives reject the notion of objective or value‐free knowledge and emphasize the importance of context, experience, and reflexivity in meaning‐making. This alignment highlights the epistemological compatibility between pragmatism and critical theory, supporting the integration of these perspectives within a critical pragmatist paradigm.

Axiologically, both pragmatists and critical theorists value normative societal ideals, including values and ethics, and in particular, democracy (Bohman 2002). Pragmatists and critical theorists also converge with the shared view that democratic inquiry involves open communication and reflexive problem‐solving (Kadlec 2006; Bohman 2002). Critical theorists, however, prioritize social justice and emancipation (Guba et al. 1994) and stress the importance of inquiry remaining free from domination (Bohman 2002). Further, both paradigms agree on the need for responsive democratic institutions. However, critical theorists intentionally critique how existing structures dominate, whereas pragmatists focus on solutions that work within current hierarchies (Bohman 2002).

Critical Pragmatism as a Research Paradigm

As a research paradigm, critical pragmatism remains somewhat obscure, having been used primarily in the context of public policy (e.g., authors Forester [2012] and Ulrich [2007a, 2007c]) and education (e.g., authors Jones and Hall [2021] and Wark [2018]). According to Deegan (2017), critical pragmatism was first named by Jane Addams, a pioneering activist and philosopher, whose work blended reflective theory, practical action, democratic participation, and ethical considerations in the context of education and social reform. While no agreed‐upon definition of critical pragmatism exists (Vannini 2008), it is often described as a unique perspective that considers the subjective and experiential nature of reality and knowledge, the importance of conducting research that is practical and applicable, and the need to examine the unique context of inquiry from a critical perspective, considering historical and societal sources of oppression and domination (Watson 1995; Vannini 2008). Similarly, Forester (2012) describes critical pragmatism as attending to the interrelation of processes and outcomes, identifying practical solutions while simultaneously acknowledging complex power dynamics in context.

Critical pragmatists advocate for the integration of theoretical and practical reasoning as a two‐dimensional form of rationality, meaning that rational decisions must consider both facts and ethical implications (Ulrich 2006b). Drawing on the strengths of both pragmatism and critical theory, Ulrich (2007b) suggested that the philosophy of critical pragmatism is equal parts pragmatism, critical theory, and reflexivity, which interdependently bridge theory and practice to achieve praxis.

Praxis, in the critical pragmatist sense, is a continuous process in which theories are developed, tested in real‐world contexts, and revised through reflection, which together promote reflective practice (Ulrich 2007b). This interplay of reason and practice ensures that actions are grounded in both practical considerations and critical awareness of broader societal issues (Ulrich 2007b). In nursing research, this approach can bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice by fostering inquiry that is both action‐oriented and socially responsive, enabling solutions that are grounded in practical relevance while also being ethically informed.

Reflection is an integral component of critical pragmatism, which involves both reflection‐on‐action and reflection‐for‐action to examine actions and assumptions, as well as the societal structures that shape them, uniting philosophical critique with practical application (Ulrich 2007c). Ulrich (2006a) emphasizes interrogating boundary judgments: the hidden beliefs and dominant narratives that influence research, including who is considered, which values are prioritized, and which assumptions go unquestioned—a process he calls boundary critique. Unexamined boundary judgments can distort how problems are framed and which solutions are considered valid. By addressing these distortions, critical pragmatists aim not only to improve immediate outcomes but also to influence the systems in which those outcomes are embedded. Praxis, therefore, involves both reflection‐on‐action to assess and respond to current needs, and reflection‐for‐action to question underlying assumptions, enabling meaningful change at both the micro (individual) and macro (structural) levels (Thorne 2014; Zimmermann 2018).

Praxis may also be achieved by mindfully considering the link between one's research paradigm (the philosophical underpinnings that orient the research), methodology (the overall research approach), and methods (specific tools used for data collection and analysis) (Thorne 2014; Mackenzie and Knipe 2006). For critical pragmatists, this would include selecting methodologies and methods that are both pragmatic and critical (Ulrich 2007c); examples of which are provided later in this paper.

Axiologically, critical pragmatists recognize the emancipatory potential of critical theory and the transformative potential of pragmatism while balancing the need to be ethical and reflexive scholars (Ulrich 2007a) to effectively analyze contexts and affect change (Forester 2012). Ontologically, critical pragmatists view reality as context‐dependent and socially mediated, influenced by historical events and social structures (McCaslin 2012; Vannini 2008). Epistemologically, critical pragmatists focus on conceptions of truth in context, considering the influence of historical and structural factors (Ulrich 2007b).

Returning to our example of researching the management of diabetes, a critical pragmatist would blend a focus on practical interventions with a critical awareness of power structures. This researcher would consider solutions that address both individual elements and broader structural barriers to effective disease management. In this approach, the critical pragmatist would focus on the immediate needs of people living with diabetes while also reflecting on the structural influences at play. This reflective element encourages a dynamic, reciprocal relationship between theory and practice. The researcher might propose interventions that improve short‐term management for those living with diabetes, while also advocating for longer term system changes. A critical pragmatism paradigm enables the researcher to offer tangible, practical solutions that can be applied within the current system while maintaining a commitment to critiquing and transforming unjust social and political structures that create and perpetuate health inequities.

Critical Pragmatism as a Continuum

Although critical pragmatism is a distinct research paradigm, we conceptualize it as a continuum along which researchers may position themselves more toward a pragmatist or critical‐theoretical orientation (Figure 1). All researchers along this continuum share the paradigm's core ontological, epistemological, and axiological commitments, although the questions they pursue and methodologies they use may differ depending on their orientation. This flexibility supports inquiry across a wide range of topics, from everyday clinical practice to broader organizational or societal concerns relevant to nursing.

To illustrate the application of this paradigm in nursing research, R.G. and M.K. present examples drawn from their respective doctoral studies. Doctoral Research (R.G.)

I locate my research toward the pragmatic end of the critical pragmatism continuum, while maintaining a critical lens to consider structural factors and social inequities. My PhD research examines a nurse‐led low‐carbohydrate and intermittent fasting dietary intervention for people living with Type 2 diabetes (T2DM), assessing its effectiveness and participants' experiences, while also considering how broader social and organizational factors, such as stigma toward people with T2DM and the healthcare system's focus on treating illness rather than promoting health, influence outcomes (Diabetes Canada 2024; Speight et al. 2024).

I am using a mixed methods case study methodology (Chiu et al. 2021), which aligns with critical pragmatist epistemology by allowing investigation across multiple levels, from individual participants to the broader healthcare system. Data collection includes a retrospective chart review and interpretive description of participants' experiences. The chart review provides practical, outcome‐oriented data consistent with a pragmatic focus (Vassar and Matthew 2013), while interpretive description explores participants' perspectives of the intervention (Thorne 2016). This approach allows exploration of how systemic, organizational, and interpersonal factors influence their engagement with the dietary intervention and their outcomes. By combining these methods, I integrate both pragmatic and critical perspectives, evaluating an intervention that can be applied in practice while also critically examining broader social and organizational factors to generate insights for future interventions to support the health of people living with T2DM (Thorne 2016).

Doctoral Research (M.K.)

In contrast to R.G.'s pragmatic orientation, I locate myself and my research toward the critical end of the continuum, while also aiming to generate practical insights to inform policy and practice. My PhD research includes a qualitative instrumental case study (Stake 1995) exploring how women with lived or living experience of unregulated substance use experience healthcare in an emergency department and urgent care setting. I draw on multiple data sources, including interviews with women who use unregulated substances, healthcare providers, and other hospital staff; observations within the emergency department and urgent care; and a review of relevant documents (Stake 1995).

My doctoral research critically explores how systemic, organizational, and interpersonal factors influence these women's experiences of care. The study's design was informed by Hankivsky's principles of intersectionality (Hankivsky et al. 2024), which ensured that diverse social positions and intersecting identities were recognized and represented throughout the research. Data were analyzed using feminist poststructural discourse analysis (Macdonald 2025), which provides tools to examine how relations of power and discourses (i.e., socially constructed ways of thinking and speaking that shape norms, roles, and experiences) influence women's experiences of care and the organizational and social structures through which that care is provided.

By integrating insights from all data sources, this research identifies practical strategies to improve care, situates these experiences within broader social and organizational contexts, and critically examines the structural and discursive conditions that influence them. Together, these considerations support research that is both critical and practical, generating results that are directly relevant to women accessing care, healthcare providers, and health systems.

Comparing Our Approaches

While both R.G. and M.K. approach their research from a critical pragmatist paradigm, their approaches reflect different emphases along the continuum. R.G.'s research leans more toward the pragmatic end, examining a nurse‐led intervention for T2DM management, whereas M.K.'s research is positioned toward the critical end, exploring the healthcare experiences of women who use unregulated substances. Methodologically, both studies employ case study designs, but R.G. integrates mixed methods, while M.K. uses a qualitative approach, illustrating how methodological choices can be guided by research questions and one's position along the continuum. Despite these differences, both studies share a commitment to practical research that can inform clinical practice, guide policy, and promote meaningful change in healthcare. Together, these examples illustrate how positioning along the critical pragmatism continuum can guide different research questions and methods, while remaining consistent with the paradigm's core ontological, epistemological, and axiological commitments.

Positivism, Post‐Positivism, and Critical Pragmatism

Positivist and post‐positivist paradigms have been central to the development of nursing research, particularly through their emphasis on empirical rigor and generalizability (Mohammad Ali 2024; Clark 1998; Corry et al. 2019). However, their prioritization of objectivity, prediction, and control limits their capacity to engage fully with the ethical, social, and political dimensions of nursing (Clark 1998; Corry et al. 2019; Jaja et al. 2022). Even within post‐positivist traditions, where reflexivity and fallibility are acknowledged (Clark 1998; Corry et al. 2019; Jaja et al. 2022), the primary goal remains approximation of objective truth rather than explicitly engaging with the ethical and normative dimensions of research.

In contrast, critical pragmatism emphasizes values and ethics rather than treating them as sources of bias to be minimized, positioning nursing research as an ethically engaged endeavor oriented toward improving practice and addressing structural inequities (Ulrich 2007b; Corry et al. 2019). Both approaches value pluralism and aim for research that can directly influence nursing practice and policy (Ulrich 2007b; Corry et al. 2019), but where (post)positivists seek to reduce bias to approximate objective truth, critical pragmatists openly acknowledge the role of values and ethics, advocating for research that not only addresses real‐world practice problems but also contributes to meaningful structural transformation.

Interpretivism and Critical Pragmatism

Interpretivism has contributed substantially to nursing research by deepening understanding of human experiences, meanings, and social contexts (Weaver and Olson 2006). However, its focus on subjective interpretation and context‐specific insight can limit its capacity to inform broader practice change, policy development, or structural transformation (Kivunja and Kuyini 2017; Weaver and Olson 2006; Houghton et al. 2012).

While both interpretivism and critical pragmatism focus on understanding human experiences and social contexts, they differ in their ontologies and epistemologies. Interpretivism centers on the subjective meanings and lived experiences of individuals, emphasizing that reality is socially constructed and varied (Weaver and Olson 2006). Critical pragmatism retains interpretivism's attentiveness to context and meaning while extending its purpose to focus on the practical implications of these experiences and prompting attention to issues of power, equity, and social justice (Forester 2012; Ulrich 2007a). Where interpretivism offers valuable insights into lived experiences, critical pragmatism combines this understanding with guidance that can inform improvements in healthcare practice and policy.

Critical Pragmatism's Alignment With Nursing

While nursing values vary globally, the profession is consistently grounded in commitments to social justice and health equity (International Council of Nurses 2021; Shahriari et al. 2013). As argued within this paper, critical pragmatism, with roots in critical theory, directly aligns with these commitments. Internationally, nurses also have an ethical responsibility to provide safe, appropriate, and competent care (Shahriari et al. 2013), requiring research that addresses real‐world clinical issues (Thorne 2016). As structural change toward equity and social justice takes time, nurses also need pragmatic solutions to everyday practice problems (Thorne 2016) that work within existing structures. As a research paradigm, critical pragmatism supports this dual focus, fostering approaches that advance both individual and structural solutions while holding space for equity and social justice. This alignment makes it well‐suited for nursing, which requires balancing care for individuals with addressing broader structural factors.

As an applied discipline, nursing practice focuses on improving the health status of individuals, families, and communities within broad socio‐cultural contexts (International Council of Nurses 2021). Consistent with critical pragmatist epistemology, nurses must consider multiple perspectives, including those of person(s), families, and other healthcare providers, to guide their practice. Beyond direct care, nurses advocate for the health needs of individuals, families, and communities (International Council of Nurses 2021; Nsiah et al. 2019). Critical pragmatism emphasizes ethical inquiry, which can inform the profession's advocacy role and support nurses in pursuing change through practice‐based interventions, policy development, or broader social or political action (Chiu et al. 2021).

Praxis, through reflective practice, illustrates a further connection between critical pragmatism and nursing (Patel and Metersky 2022; Ulrich 2007b). Within nursing, praxis includes the same forms of reflective practice, reflection‐in‐action (i.e., reflecting while a situation is ongoing), reflection‐on‐action (i.e., reflecting after a situation has occurred), and reflection‐for‐action (i.e., reflecting on the implications of historical, social, and political factors [Patel and Metersky 2022]) as critical pragmatism, discussed in greater detail previously. This shared orientation toward praxis highlights why critical pragmatism is a meaningful and relevant paradigm for nursing, offering a reflective, action‐oriented approach that aligns with the profession's commitment to individually and socially responsive care.

As a profession grounded in both science and human experience, nursing requires flexible approaches to care (Thorne 2016). In the context of nursing research, critical pragmatism aligns with the use of different, flexible methodologies that bridge theory and practice through praxis (Ulrich 2007b). A paradigm that aligns with flexible methodology and methods can enable nurses to address complex and dynamic healthcare challenges.

As the largest healthcare profession globally (Boniol et al. 2022), nurses are in a unique position to influence and advocate for change. In their work with individuals, families, and communities, they witness firsthand how broader structures influence healthcare experiences (McMillan 2016). Critical pragmatism, with its integration of critical inquiry and practical action, aligns closely with nursing practice and can inform strategies that enable nurses to lead transformative change in healthcare, enhancing their capacity to identify areas of improvement, advocate for reform, and implement meaningful change.

Conclusion

In this paper, we explored critical pragmatism as a research paradigm uniquely suited to the demands of nursing scholarship: addressing immediate, practical challenges while critically reflecting on systemic inequities and power structures. By integrating pragmatism's action‐oriented focus with critical theory's emancipatory goals, critical pragmatism offers nurse researchers a versatile paradigm for advancing socially conscious and contextually grounded inquiry.

By incorporating reflection through reflection‐on‐action and critical reflexivity, critical pragmatism fosters a dynamic interplay between theory and practice. By offering a perspective that addresses both individual needs and structural factors, it aligns with the philosophical and practical commitments of nursing, enabling researchers to generate actionable insights that improve practice and contribute to transformative change.

Despite its promise, critical pragmatism appears to be largely absent in nursing research, presenting an opportunity for nurse scholars to pioneer its application. By adopting this paradigm, we can bridge the gap between philosophical critique and practical outcomes, creating a robust foundation for innovative research that addresses the pressing healthcare issues of our time.
