Open modified-Latarjet procedure with suture-anchor-button fixation through a full anterior approach
Authors: Giacomo Delle Rose, Riccardo Ranieri, Raffaele Garofalo, Marco Conti, Alessandro Castagna
Published: 2025

The Latarjet procedure represents a cornerstone surgical technique for the treatment of recurrent anterior shoulder instability. By transferring a segment of the coracoid process along with the attached conjoint tendon to the anterior glenoid rim, this approach effectively addresses both osseous deficiencies and capsuloligamentous laxity.

The technique has significantly evolved over the decades,12,20 with arthroscopic approaches also being developed.2,4,19

However, as with any surgical procedure, it carries inherent risks and potential complications. Most of these can be mitigated through comprehensive preoperative evaluation and meticulous surgical technique, which likely accounts for the variability in complication rates reported across different centers.8,10,13,15

Despite various technical modifications and advances in technology, most of the current complications associated with the Latarjet procedure—both open and arthroscopic—remain related to the use of screws, the traditional method of fixation.10,13,15,18 Screw migration or loosening during the healing phase may occur, sometimes necessitating revision surgery. Subsequent trauma or dislocations can result in screw breakage, potentially leading to serious consequences. Graft resorption may cause the screw head to impinge on the subscapularis tendon or the articular cartilage of the humeral head. In addition, screws that are excessively long or positioned too medially may result in persistent pain or suprascapular nerve injury. Due to these issues, reoperation for screw removal has been reported in up to 18% of cases.18

To overcome screw-related complications, Boileau first introduced the use of metallic buttons as an alternative fixation method in the context of the arthroscopic Bristow–Latarjet procedure. Recent evidence highlights the potential advantages of buttons in reducing graft resorption and hardware-related complications while maintaining functional outcomes comparable to those achieved with screw fixation.14,24

Moreover, some authors suggested that the graft may present a remodeling phenomenon due to the flexible fixation and that button fixation has certain fault tolerance and is more friendly to surgeons.26,27

Although originally described for use in arthroscopic procedures, the Endobutton fixation system can also be used in the open approach. However, this requires a concomitant posterior approach to apply the posterior metal buttons, potentially introducing technical challenges and increasing operative time.

Based on these considerations, we developed a modified open Latarjet procedure utilizing a single anterior surgical approach, with fixation of the coracoid achieved through sutures, soft anchors, and metallic buttons (Suture, Anchor, Button). This technique aims to combine the primary advantages of both the open (safety, reduced operative time, and cost-effectiveness) and arthroscopic (suture-button fixation) approaches while eliminating the need for a posterior surgical access.

Surgical technique

Except for the fixation method, the procedure follows the surgical steps described by Walch and Boileau for the open Latarjet–Bristow technique,12 with minor technical adaptations.

Anesthesia and patient positioning

The procedure is commonly performed under general anesthesia. The patient is in beach-chair position with a small pillow behind the scapular medial border to place the glenoid surface perpendicular to the operating table.

Surgical approach

Skin incision is about 3-5 cm, starting under the tip of the coracoid process and pointing toward the axilla (Fig. 1). The deltopectoral interval is opened, keeping the cephalic vein laterally. A self-retaining retractor is placed in the deltopectoral interval. A Bankart retractor is positioned on the top of the coracoid.

Three-five–cm skin incision from the coracoid tip pointing toward the axilla.

Coracoid preparation

The coracoid is released from the coracoacromial ligament, laterally, and from the pectoralis minor, medially. The base of the coracoid is exposed and another Bankart retractor in placed medially on the coracoid base. A small, angulated oscillating saw is used to osteotomized the coracoid from medial to lateral and from superior to inferior, taking care not to damage the coracoclavicular ligaments insertion. The coracoid cut is covered with a bone wax to stop bone bleeding and a lint moistened with tranexamic acid is left over the subscapularis during the next step. The osteotomized coracoid is grasped with a forceps with teeth. Careful release of the bone and the conjoint tendon from deep attachment is performed, avoiding dissection of the medial part of the conjoint tendon.

The coracoid bone is then everted, and the inferior surface is decorticated using a saw or a burr. Using a dedicated coracoid guide, 2 parallel holes are drilled into the inferior surface of the bone graft with a 2.7 mm drill bit. The coracoid guide (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) has 2 holes spaced 1 cm apart and should be centered medio-laterally on the coracoid surface (Fig. 2A).

(A) A coracoid guide is used to grasp the coracoid, and performed 2 holes spaced 1 cm apart. (B) The lateral offset of the holes relative to the coracoid margin is measured using a dedicated tool.

This allows for precise medial-lateral positioning and consistent standardization of the inter-hole distance. After drilling, the lateral offset of the holes relative to the coracoid margin is measured using a dedicated tool (Fig. 2B). This offset measurement is subsequently replicated on the glenoid using a ruler (see below), ensuring accurate alignment between coracoid and glenoid holes. Two shuttle sutures are then passed through the drilled holes, and the coracoid is temporarily positioned beneath the pectoralis major.

Glenoid exposure

The upper limb is positioned in full external rotation and approximately 20° of abduction. A horizontal split of the subscapularis is performed between the superior two-thirds and the inferior third using Mayo scissors. The division is carried carefully down to the white capsule, with the subscapularis retracted superiorly and inferiorly using 2 long Langenbeck retractors. Subsequently, a Bankart retractor is positioned medially on the subscapularis fossa, and a Homan retractor is placed inferiorly on the scapular neck to retract the inferior portion of the subscapularis muscle.

The arm is then placed in neutral rotation, and a vertical capsular split is performed at the antero-inferior level using a scalpel. A modified Trillat or Fukuda retractor is then introduced into the joint, resting on the inferior margin of the glenoid to provide lateral exposure. At this point, the superior portion of the subscapularis can be further retracted by maintaining the superior Langenbeck retractor, or alternatively, a Steinmann pin may be placed superiorly to facilitate exposure.

The medial capsular flap is excised, along with any damaged labral tissue or glenoid fracture fragments. The antero-inferior glenoid rim is then exposed using a scalpel or electrocautery, followed by decortication with an osteotome, straight saw, or burr.

Glenoid preparation

Using a ruler to determine the position of the glenoid holes (spaced 1 cm apart and offset relative to the glenoid articular margin according to the previously measured lateral coracoid offset), 2 holes are drilled into the antero-inferior glenoid neck using a 3.0 mm drill, reaching and passing through the posterior cortex (Fig. 3).

The glenoid holes are drilled using a 3.0-mm drill, after identifying the correct position with a ruler and maintaining the drill trajectory parallel to the glenoid surface.

Next, 2 Q-Fix 2.8 mm all-suture anchors (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA), each preloaded with 2 #2 MAGNUMWIRE sutures (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) made of ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene, are introduced by the dedicated inserter through the guide holes, advanced beyond the posterior cortex, and deployed by turning the dial on the inserter (Fig. 4). At this stage, it is crucial to ensure that the anchors pass completely through the posterior cortex before deployment. Once the anchors are expanded, the guide is removed and fixation is verified by firmly pulling on the sutures. This technique allows secure fixation over the posterior glenoid cortex without requiring a posterior incision. (Fig. 5). Finally, It is necessary to ensure that both sutures of the soft anchor slide freely after implantation.

Q-Fix provides consistent deployment due to its radially expanding implant design and a delivery system that functions like a knot pusher. In this technique, the inserter is advanced beyond the posterior cortex, and the anchor is deployed outside the bone. Once expanded, the soft anchor reaches diameters of 4.7 mm and 5.5 mm, ensuring secure fixation over the posterior glenoid cortex.

(A) Introduction of the posterior anchor using the dedicated inserter, which must pass through the posterior cortex before rotating the dial to deploy the soft anchor. (B) After verifying the fixation of the inferoposterior anchor by firmly pulling on the sutures, the same procedure is repeated for the superoposterior anchor.

Coracoid fixation

The coracoid is then retrieved, and the sutures from the superior and inferior glenoid anchors are passed through the coracoid drill holes using the previously placed shuttle sutures (Fig. 6).

The sutures from the glenoid anchors are passed through the coracoid holes.

At this stage, the sutures are passed through a pegged Endobutton Round (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA). Using a dedicated tool, one limb from each of the 2 different suture pairs originating from the same posterior glenoid anchor (already passed through the coracoid) is threaded through each of the 2 holes in the Endobutton. One pair of suture limbs passing through the same hole is then tied together, creating a loop-suture configuration (Fig. 7).

Passage of the glenoid sutures through the C and the pegged Endobutton at different steps for the inferior (intraoperative passage) and superior (already tied) suture pairs. Once the limbs from each of the 2 different suture pairs originating from the same posterior glenoid anchor are passed through the C and the Endobutton holes (yellow ∗), 1 pair of suture limbs is tied together (tied limbs), while the second pair is left free (free limbs). These are then used to construct a Nice knot (yellow°), which is progressively tightened to draw the C toward the glenoid. C, coracoid.

A sliding-locking knot (Nice knot) is tied and gradually tightened to draw the coracoid towards the glenoid, ensuring that the peg of the Endobutton engages properly within the coracoid hole (Fig. 7). Suture tensioning is performed using a dedicated tensioning device (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) (Fig. 8). According to the protocol described by Boileau et al,3 a tension of 100 N is applied in 3 successive steps: first to eliminate suture creep (pretensioning), then to secure the knot (tensioning), and finally to compress the bone block (overtensioning). This sequence is first carried out for the superior suture pair and then repeated for the inferior pair.

Suture tensioning is performed using a dedicated tensioning device (Smith and Nephew) as described by Boileau et al3 for the arthroscopic technique.

A square knot is then tied for each suture pair, followed by a final round of tensioning in the same order. After verifying construct stability, 3 additional square knots are tied to definitively secure the fixation (Fig. 9).

C, coracoid; CT, conjoint tendon; G, glenoid; iSS, inferior subscapularis; uSS, upper subscapularis. (B) Schematic representation of the technique.

At the conclusion of the procedure, a labral repair or lateral capsular fixation to the remnant of the coracoacromial ligament may be performed, depending on surgeon preference.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Postoperatively, patients are instructed to wear a sling in neutral rotation for 4 weeks. Pendulum exercises are initiated after 2 weeks. At 4 weeks, the sling is discontinued and a structured rehabilitation program under the supervision of a physiotherapist is commenced, focusing on the gradual recovery of both passive and active range of motion. Swimming pool therapy is encouraged.

Heavy lifting involving elbow flexion is strictly avoided during the first 12 weeks. Strengthening exercises targeting the shoulder musculature are introduced after 3 months, with the goal of achieving full functional stability of the operated shoulder by approximately 6 months postoperatively.

Discussion

This novel modification of the Latarjet procedure enables screw-free fixation of the coracoid bone block by combining high-strength sutures, all-suture anchors, and pegged metallic round buttons through a standard all-anterior mini-open approach, without the need for a posterior surgical access. This strategy offers several potential advantages.

First, it addresses and potentially eliminates many of the complications associated with screw fixation, such as screw mobilization, breakage, impingement on soft tissues or cartilage, and nerve irritation—factors that may contribute to the relatively high rate of reoperation reported after traditional Latarjet procedures. Second, the absence of rigid and permanent metallic screws on the glenoid may simplify future procedures or revisions, including repeat instability surgeries or shoulder arthroplasty. Third, the combination of the systems used for the coracoid fixation allows the procedure to be performed via a single all-anterior deltopectoral approach with subscapularis splitting, thereby avoiding the need for posterior access and simplifying the procedure.

At our institution, the open Latarjet remains the gold standard for the treatment of traumatic anterior shoulder instability. While some authors have reported favorable outcomes with the arthroscopic approach,2,5,19,24, 25, 26 it is widely acknowledged that the arthroscopic Latarjet has a steep learning curve6,7,16,19 and carries a significant increase in surgical complexity with potential higher risk of complications.8,16,18 Moreover, comparative studies have failed to demonstrate substantial clinical advantages of the arthroscopic technique over the open approach, while highlighting its higher procedural complexity and increased costs.9,16,21,23 We recognized that the button–suture fixation system, applied using a tensioning device, represents one of the main advantages of the arthroscopic technique described by Boileau.3 Building on this concept, we firstly adopt this fixation method in an open surgical setting. However, when using metallic buttons in open Latarjet procedures, a posterior approach is typically required, which may introduce technical challenges: access to the posterior scapula must be feasible, an experienced assistant is needed to manage the posterior sutures, and posteriorly applied sutures and buttons may become entangled in surrounding soft tissues.

To overcome these limitations, we developed a new technique employing soft suture anchors for posterior fixation, delivered entirely via an anterior approach. The all-suture anchor system features an automated deployment mechanism that applies a controlled traction force of 140 N, ensuring complete expansion of the anchor beyond the posterior cortex of the glenoid. When combined with the dedicated tensioning device, this system provides reliable and reproducible fixation, allowing for adequate compression of the coracoid bone block and thereby promoting graft integration and healing (Figs. 10 and 11). Importantly, the load-to-failure for the 2.8 mm Q-Fix anchor has been reported to be approximately 600 N—significantly higher than the forces generated by the conjoint tendon in simulation studies11 and exceeding the failure loads reported in biomechanical studies evaluating similar fixation systems in the context of the Latarjet procedure.17,22

Although Barrett et al1 demonstrated that a single pair of buttons is sufficient for bone healing, we recommend using 2 pairs of buttons for 3 main reasons. First, the sutures used with this system are thinner than those used with the Endobuttons described by Boileau et al,2 potentially reducing mechanical resistance. Second, the coracoid graft harvested in open procedures is typically larger than that obtained arthroscopically, warranting broader fixation. Third, utilizing 2 button pairs allows for enhanced control during fixation. By alternating the tensioning of each suture pair, surgeons can adjust the compression of the graft and correct any residual slack that may be perceived during intraoperative stability testing.

Limitations

The main limitation of this procedure lies in the application of adequate tension to properly stabilize the coracoid before final knot tying. This challenge is particularly common in the initial cases of the learning curve. However, several technical tips can help overcome this issue.

First, it is essential to fully displace the subscapularis muscle both superiorly and inferiorly to prevent any muscle fibers from being interposed between the coracoid and the glenoid. The presence of such fibers may lead to a loss of fixation stability once the muscle moves and returns to its anatomical position. Second, after slackening the tensioning device at the end of the first tensioning step, gentle manual pressure can be applied to the bone block before performing the first half-hitch knot, helping to maintain contact and alignment. Third, repeating the tensioning step after the first half-hitch helps eliminate any residual slack and ensures firm compression of the coracoid before finalizing the construct with alternating half-hitch knots.

Conclusion

This modified open Latarjet technique offers a safe, screw-free fixation method using high-strength sutures, soft anchors, and metallic buttons, all performed through a single anterior approach. It aims to combine the mechanical reliability of open surgery with the biological and technical advantages of arthroscopic suture-button fixation, potentially reducing complications and reoperations rate.
