Primer for the current and future congenital cardiac surgery fellow
Authors: David Blitzer, Nicholas Oh, Stephanie Nguyen, Eitan Keizman, Paul Devlin, Francis Fynn-Thompson, Emile Bacha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCongenital cardiac surgery is among the most technically demanding and prolonged training pathways in medicine. The route to independent practice requires extensive preparation, deliberate skill acquisition, and strategic career planning. There is little consolidated guidance outlining how trainees can successfully navigate the transition into congenital fellowship and ultimately to employment. This primer aims to provide a structured framework for current and prospective congenital cardiac surgery fellows.MethodsWe developed a comprehensive, experience-based guide synthesizing perspectives from academic congenital cardiac surgeons across multiple high-volume programs. The manuscript outlines key domains critical to success: (1) preapplication preparation, including clinical exposure, research productivity, mentorship, and networking; (2) the standardized fellowship application and match process; (3) strategies for operative, academic, and critical care development during fellowship; and (4) a structured approach to job search preparation and contract negotiation.ResultsSuccess in congenital cardiac surgery fellowship is predicated on early commitment, focused academic productivity, meaningful mentorship, and strategic networking. During fellowship, deliberate technical refinement, mastery of complex physiology, active engagement in intensive care management, and continuous self-assessment are essential. Early and organized job search preparation—beginning 12 to 18 months before graduation—combined with sustained professional visibility and mentorship support, optimizes employment opportunities in a competitive market.ConclusionsA deliberate, longitudinal approach encompassing preparation, performance, and professional development is essential for trainees pursuing congenital cardiac surgery. This primer provides a practical roadmap to help fellows navigate training and transition successfully into independent practice.Key Words: congenital, fellowship, primer

Central Message

This primer is written as a guide for those who choose to pursue congenital cardiac surgery, providing the essential structure and operational knowledge needed to approach fellowship and beyond.

Perspective

Congenital heart surgery is among the most challenging and demanding fields in medicine, requiring a unique combination of technical skill, judgment, and emotional intelligence. The road to practice is notoriously long and uncertain, characterized by high variability and a challenging job market. We seek to create a primer to guide hopeful fellows and current fellows entering this field.

Congenital heart surgery is among the most challenging and demanding fields in medicine, requiring a unique combination of technical skill, judgment, and emotional intelligence. The challenge may be the reason for some of its appeal, with 7% to 64% of cardiothoracic surgery trainees expressing interest in pursuing congenital cardiac surgery as a career.1, 2, 3, 4 The road to practice is notoriously long and costly; it typically requires a decade of postgraduate surgical training to complete, with surgeons reaching their mid- to late 30s before even beginning their practice and then doing so with a median debt burden of $179,000.5 Despite this extensive commitment, the path is also characterized by a highly variable and a challenging job market in which the number of graduates often exceeds available positions and limited opportunities for operative autonomy.5 This primer is written as a guide for those who choose to pursue this specialty regardless of these realities, providing the essential structure and operational knowledge needed to navigate the transition into this highly specialized, yet rewarding discipline.

Foundations for Success: The Preapplication Phase

The path to a congenital cardiac surgery fellowship begins well before the formal application period. Success in securing a position and thriving in this demanding specialty requires strategic planning, deep exploration, and cultivation of a robust academic and clinical profile during general cardiothoracic surgery residency. Dedicated preparation in the following areas is essential to demonstrate genuine commitment and competitive readiness for this highly specialized field.

Exploring Your Interest and Commitment

This fellowship represents a significant commitment and a profound shift in clinical focus. Applicants must move beyond a superficial interest to demonstrate a mature understanding of the field's unique challenges, including the management of complex structural heart disease across the lifespan, the high stakes of neonatal surgery, and the need for long-term patient follow-up. Dedicated time should be spent shadowing congenital surgeons, intensivists, and cardiologists to gain an authentic perspective on the daily operational realities of a high-volume service. This due diligence ensures the pursuit of this subspecialty is a fully informed decision. It is also imperative that individuals engage in an iterative and continuous process of critical self-reflection as they explore their interests and make the decision about dedicating their careers to congenital cardiac surgery. Not only is the training process long and arduous, it is also mentally, psychologically, and often times physically taxing. Individuals should take the time to self-assess their own resilience as they face such obstacles throughout their early training years and attempt to extrapolate how they might response when the challenges are magnified by the realities of the pediatric population. During these self-assessments, the hopeful applicant should also consider how they respond to feedback and criticism. Once again, the heightened pressure of the congenital operating room can lead to greater scrutiny of the trainee, and a trainee should attempt to gain an unawareness of how they can respond and rebound from such criticism.

•Home institution electives: Maximize time on the congenital cardiac service at your home institution to solidify foundational knowledge and build a strong working relationship with your program director and faculty, who will write essential letters of recommendation.

Networking and Professional Connections

Given the limited number of programs and positions, personal connections are often the most effective method for identifying opportunities and securing interviews. Given the nature of the field, personal relationships developed over time can be critical not only to securing a fellowship position but also toward securing employment after completing training. It is difficult to overemphasize how important these personal connections can be. Networking in this instance is a longitudinal process, and effective networking should be continuous and deliberate over the course of a career. The opportunities for networking are numerous:

•National meetings: Use national conferences (eg, STS, AATS, CHSS) not only for presenting research but as a primary platform for face-to-face networking. Seek out program directors and established surgeons to introduce yourself, discuss your interests, and ask for career advice. Once again, if you pursue these interactions early in your training, it offers the opportunity to develop a real relationship with such surgeons and can fortify their interest in your career success.

For anyone pursuing an unaccredited fellowship, networking is equally as important. In most instances, these positions are obtained through direct communication with program directors or faculty outside of a formal process. This being the case, getting to know faculty from across the country or having a mentor who advocates on your behalf at another program can prove instrumental in securing a position. Although unaccredited positions were once commonplace for US trainees with the expectation that a trainee would serve a period of time as an unaccredited fellow before beginning the accredited fellowship at a program, this has become less common now that all programs have moved to the 2-year fellowship paradigm.

Application Process

This section outlines the standardized application process for accredited congenital cardiac surgery fellowships, administered through SF Match. Applicants should comply with all Thoracic Surgery Directors Association (TSDA) policies, SF Match requirements, and individual program instructions. Although specific dates may vary slightly from year to year, the overall structure and documentation requirements remain consistent.

Match Structure

All Accreditation Council on Graduate Medical Education (ACGME)-accredited programs participate in the TSDA Congenital Cardiac Surgery Fellowship Match program, conducted by SF Match. The TSDA establishes dates for applications, interviews, and the universal application form, whereas SF Match processes applicant registration, rank order list submission, and publication of match results. Individual fellowship programs retain responsibility for application review, interview selection, and postmatch institutional onboarding.

At the time of publication, there are 18 ACGME-accredited programs in the United States. Each program is 24 months in duration and typically trains one ACGME fellow for 2 years; thus, programs enter the match on an alternating basis rather than annually. Match participation is therefore staggered, and not all programs are available in any given cycle; programs may also elect to enter or withdraw from the match from year to year. As a result, the total number and identity of participating programs vary by cycle and are often not definitively known until the SF Match registration opens, or through informal communication within the congenital cardiac surgery community. This is to say that an individual applicant shouldn't necessarily try to plan out or strategize the year of their application based on which programs will be in the match. Furthermore, it can be quite difficult to elucidate which programs have internal candidates, making such strategizing all the more difficult. Just as when evaluating fellowship programs, the case volumes done by each program are easily accessible on the STS congenital database. Although case volumes are not the only metric, they are an objective measure of what a fellow can expect to experience during their training and should be noted. As a last point, individual applicants would do well to get to know the other applicants during the application cycle, as these individuals will not only be future colleagues can be an important source of information as the job search progresses later on in their training.

How to Prepare

Clinical Experience

The transition to the congenital cardiac surgery service marks a significant divergence from training focused primarily on adult cardiac pathology. Unlike adult cardiac surgery, this specialty is dedicated to correcting complex structural abnormalities of the heart and great vessels, managing patients across the entire lifespan, often involving coexisting genetic syndromes, an intensive interdisciplinary approach, and the need for multiple procedures throughout their lives. Given the unique anatomical challenges, the profound differences in patient physiology, and the expansive breadth and depth of cases seen, fellows should anticipate that much of their previous clinical experience will need to be recontextualized, requiring them to reset their foundational knowledge and clinical perspective.

Preparation for congenital cardiac surgery requires a deep, simultaneous mastery of the embryological basis of heart defects, complex congenital cardiac anatomy, and nuanced perioperative management. The fellowship is a continuous process of learning (Table 2).

Advancement in the Operating Room and Simulation

Advancement in the congenital cardiac operating room requires a different focus than that of adult cardiac surgery. Given the complexity and low volume of certain rare procedures, a fellow's training will not be as hands-on as general cardiothoracic surgery. Therefore, the trainee must be proactive in focusing on and using every opportunity to learn essential nonoperative skill sets, which are critical for surgical decision-making and optimal patient outcomes.

•Mastery of assistance skills: Focus on developing exceptional assistance skills, which include anticipating the surgeon's needs, optimizing exposure, and managing the surgical field.

Mastery of assistance skills: Focus on developing exceptional assistance skills, which include anticipating the surgeon's needs, optimizing exposure, and managing the surgical field.

•Hemodynamic and imaging proficiency: Use time in the operating room to master hemodynamic assessment and develop proficiency in epicardial echocardiography skills. These diagnostic and management skills are essential for both intraoperative decision-making and postoperative management. Take the time after cases to review the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative imaging again to correlate what you saw in the field with what you see on the echocardiography.

•Operative simulation: Work to develop and refine operative skills by practicing with operative simulation that is as high-fidelity as possible. There are structured opportunities such as the Hands-on Surgical Training (HOST) course, which is organized annually by the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto, Canada and can be attended either virtually or in-person and offers high-fidelity models and a structured environment for deliberate practice of complex procedures. You should focus your simulation on developing proficiency with fine suturing in smaller or deeper operative fields. This can be as simple as using an 8-0 suture in a box that mimics the size of an infant or neonate chest. Take note of what you personally struggle with in the operating room and do what you can to recreate those conditions in simulation. But do not wait until you struggle to be proactive with practice; depending on your training program, the opportunities to actually operate during fellowship may be few and far between, and you should be prepared to shine during those chances so that more chances can arise in the future.

Job Hunting

Job hunting is a rightly stressful, but essential, part of any congenital cardiac surgery fellow's trajectory. The advice in this chapter can be applied just as readily to ACGME fellows as to those in unaccredited fellowships or international fellows. While networking is a key and continuous activity throughout one's fellowship—leveraging mentors, faculty, and recent graduates—fellows should focus the active search process primarily during their second year of fellowship, ideally starting 12 to 18 months before completion (Table 3). Preparation involves defining career goals (academic vs balanced practice), maintaining a meticulously updated CV and case logs, and ensuring a reputation for excellence. Given that job postings are often limited, personal contacts and program directors remain the most effective methods for identifying opportunities. Fellows should make a point of attending national conferences, such as those hosted by the STS or AATS, in their final year to maximize opportunities to come face-to-face with potential employers. Interviews typically occur between October and March. Given this timeline of job postings and interviews, it is likely that the annual meeting for CHSS and STS will be the most important in the second year of fellowship as these take place in October and January. Critical evaluation factors include assessing mentorship structure, the collegiality of the team, and clarifying the nature of the practice (adult vs pediatric congenital). Once an offer is secured, the majority of contracts are signed before training completion, and fellows should always seek counsel, potentially from a lawyer, to negotiate salary, benefits, and contract logistics to ensure favorable terms.

The Job-Search Process

•Identify opportunities: Job postings are often limited, so rely primarily on personal contacts. Given how small the network is in the field, it is also worth highlighting that a strong letter or phone call of support from a mentor is probably as important as a strong CV when it comes to securing job interviews or offers.•Professional organizations: Check career centers of organizations like the CTSNet Career Center, and CHSS Job Search. •Interviewing and evaluation: Interviews typically occur between October and March of the final year of fellowship.•Assess mentorship: This is critical, as early-career support has been shown to be significantly associated with retention in the field and with greater practicing case volumes6 Some have even suggested the creation of formal transitional period programs for early-career surgeons.7 It is imperative that you ask direct questions about the mentorship structure and the career outcomes of previous early-career surgeons. Ask mentors from other institutions about the reputation of the program and senior surgeon in regard to mentorship. If there are junior surgeons who recently left the program, try to talk with them and learn about their experience and reasons for leaving. Also ask references for their perception of the senior surgeon as a leader and a person. Any reports of unprofessional behavior or a history of replacing multiple junior surgeons in quick succession should be red flags and merit thorough investigation.•Evaluate the team: Pay attention to the collegiality of future partners and the multidisciplinary team environment.•Understand the job: Clarify the nature of the surgical practice (adult vs pediatric congenital), call frequency, and nonclinical time. Just as when evaluating fellowship positions, institutional case volumes are easily accessible though the STS database and can give at least an indication of how busy a surgeon can expect to be in that practice. During the interview process, you should obtain a clear understanding of the expectations for an incoming faculty member. Is the practice looking for someone who can grow into a full congenital heart surgeon or rather someone to help with adult congenital cases, transplants/procurement, or other such responsibilities? Applicants should have a clear understanding of these expectations and make sure they align with their career goals before moving any further in the interview process.
