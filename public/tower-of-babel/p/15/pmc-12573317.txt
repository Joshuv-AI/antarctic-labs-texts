Profile of paediatric mental health presentations to Australian emergency departments in 2018
Authors: Jackson Newberry-Dupe, Marietta R John-White, Franz E Babl, Meredith L Borland, Elyssia M Bourke, Sandra Brownlea, Paul Buntine, Brooke Charters, Mathew Coleman, Tahnee Dunlop, Olga Gaitsgory, Shane George, Amit Kochar, Glenn A Melvin, Doris Tham, Bruce Tonge, Viet Tran, Simon Craig, Rohan Borschmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPaediatric mental health presentations to emergency departments (EDs) are rising. Rural/Regional mental health service users face structural barriers to accessing general and specialist treatment, including physical distance and local service capacity and funding constraints. However, differences in patient characteristics and treatment pathways between major city and rural/regional EDs remain unclear. This study aimed to compare demographic and clinical profiles of paediatric mental health patients within each location.MethodsRetrospective cohort study of paediatric (aged 0–17 years) mental health presentations at 23 Australian EDs (metropolitan=18, rural/regional=five) from 1 January to 31 December 2018. At each site, up to 100 randomly selected unique patient medical records were included. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared using logistic and linear mixed effects regression models.Results2234 young people were recruited (metropolitan=1803, rural/regional=431). Mean age was 14.4 years (SD=2.6) and 59.4% were female. Frequent presentation reasons were suicidal ideation (853; 38.2%), self-harm (668; 29.9%) and behavioural disturbance (306; 13.7%). Of those with complete documentation, 703 (70.9%) had family history of mental illness and 933 (41.8%) reported recent interpersonal difficulties (eg, family/peer conflict). Three hundred and twenty-nine young people (14.7%) were admitted and 1024 (45.8%) were referred to public child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS). Patients in rural/regional settings had shorter median waiting times for ED clinicians (24 vs 42 min) and mental health assessments (1.1 vs 2.5 hours), shorter median ED length of stay (2.9 vs 4 hours) and higher likelihood of referral to CAMHS on discharge (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.22, 3.93).ConclusionsCharacteristics of children and adolescents presenting with mental health concerns are broadly similar between metropolitan and rural/regional EDs, but treatment characteristics differ. Frequency of family mental illness and interpersonal difficulties highlights the benefits of family focused interventions. Given similar patient profiles, equitable resourcing of rural/regional services is warranted.Keywords: emergency departments, mental health, remote and rural medicine, self harm, pediatrics

Introduction

In the last two decades, mental health-related emergency department (ED) presentations by children and adolescents have increased globally.1,4 As an initial point of contact with the mental health system, the ED provides a critical opportunity for brief intervention, acute risk management and connection to specialist mental health services.5

There is a well-documented disparity in access to social, educational and healthcare services between major cities and regional/rural/remote settings.6,8 Conversely, higher prevalence of health issues among rural and regional communities may drive greater per-capita healthcare needs.6 9 10 In 2016, 71% of the children and adolescents (aged 0–19 years) in Australia lived in major cities, 18% in inner regional areas and 11% in outer regional, rural and remote communities.8 Children and adolescents who present to rural/regional EDs are less well connected with preventive and primary healthcare6 8 11 and may travel further to access services.12 A child or adolescent from a rural/regional ED who requires admission to a specialist mental health inpatient unit typically requires interhospital transport,13 14 potentially over hundreds of kilometres,12 potentially isolating them from family and social supports.

The impact of remoteness on service accessibility, patient characteristics and short-term outcomes (such as admissions, referrals and re-presentations) of ED mental health presentations by children and adolescents remains unclear.10 There is a need to better understand the profile of young people attending rural/regional EDs with mental health concerns and the care they receive to determine the extent of disparities in access, clinical characteristics and specialist care. This knowledge will be beneficial for planning and prioritisation of training and resources for rural and regional EDs15 and may be generalisable to other countries with similar rural/regional population profiles.

This study aims to (1) describe the sociodemographic and clinical profiles of children and adolescents (aged <18 years) presenting with mental health concerns to EDs in Australia; (2) determine their short-term outcomes, including admission, referrals and re-presentations within 12 months and (3) compare these profiles and outcomes between metropolitan and rural/regional EDs.

Methods

Study design

A multicentre retrospective cohort study of young people aged 0–17 years, presenting to Australian EDs with mental health-related concerns between 1 January and 31 December 2018.

Setting

Data were obtained from 23 hospitals across Australia: 13 from Victoria, four from Queensland, two from Western Australia and one each from New South Wales, South Australia, Tasmania and the Northern Territory. Hospitals were recruited from the Paediatric Research in Emergency Departments International Collaborative Network,16 a collective of paediatric EDs across Australia and New Zealand.

Classification of hospital emergency departments

In Australia, remoteness of health services is classified by the Department of Health and Aged Care according to the distance people must travel to access health services located in urban centres, using the Modified Monash Model (MMM).6 The Australasian College for Emergency Medicine (ACEM) categorises hospitals as major referral, urban district or rural/regional based on remoteness, population size and the training and supervision opportunities available to emergency medicine registrars.17 18 Ten hospitals in this study were classed as major referral, eight as urban district and five as rural/regional. To isolate the experience of rural/regional EDs, major referral and urban district sites were combined into a ‘metropolitan’ category. Of the metropolitan EDs, 12 were in tertiary hospitals (five with separate paediatric EDs, seven with mixed adult/paediatric EDs) and six were in non-tertiary hospitals with mixed adult/paediatric EDs. Of the rural/regional hospitals, two were tertiary hospitals and three were non-tertiary hospitals, all with mixed adult/paediatric EDs.

The definition of metropolitan used in this study differs from the MMM category of metropolitan (MM1), which is based on geographic proximity to major cities only. Of sites classified as metropolitan in this study (n=18), 16 (89%) were in MM1 and two (11%) were in MM2 (regional centre). Of sites classified as rural/regional by ACEM, one (20%) was MM1, one (20%) MM2 and three (60%) MM3 (large rural towns).

Hospital resources and capacity

In 2018, total visits to participating EDs for any health concern by paediatric patients (aged <18 years) ranged from 10 503 to 85 347 at metropolitan sites, while paediatric visits at rural/regional EDs ranged from 5217 to 17 873. The availability of on-site resources varied between and within the metropolitan and rural/regional hospitals. Fourteen (78%) metropolitan sites and two (40%) rural/regional sites had ED short stay units capable of accommodating children and/or adolescents. Mental health clinicians were available 24 hours/day in 16 (89%) metropolitan and three (60%) rural/regional EDs, with the remainder providing a mix of on-site daytime and evening assessment, daytime only or on-call/liaison only. None of the rural/regional services had on-site child/adolescent mental health inpatient services, while seven (39%) metropolitan sites had mental health inpatient units for children and 11 (61%) for adolescents.

Participants

Each hospital site obtained a list of index (first) presentations by eligible patients to their ED in 2018. It was decided a priori through consensus of the investigators that 100 presentations from each site would be feasible and adequately representative of overall ED mental health visits. Where sites identified >100 index presentations, lists were randomised using the ‘RAND’ function in excel and data were extracted on the first 100 patients. Patients were eligible for inclusion if the primary documented reason for ED attendance was related to mental health. Mental health-related presentations were defined as meeting at least one of the following criteria: (a) the patient was referred to the mental health team from the ED and/or (b) the patient was discharged with a primary diagnosis coded using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Australian/Clinical Modification, relating to mental and behavioural disorders, disturbances of emotional state, abnormalities of appearance and behaviour or physical injury associated with an intent to self-harm. Presentations with drug and/or alcohol intoxication as the only presenting complaint were excluded. For each patient, data on medical and psychiatric history, current living circumstances, current and prior contact with health services, length of ED (and, if relevant, hospital) stay and other relevant clinical information were extracted (online supplemental file A).

Data collection

At each site, data were extracted from electronic or paper-based medical records by trained research staff, in accordance with recommended chart review methods.19 A standardised data collection instrument was used to extract de-identified information into a secure, firewall-protected and password-protected, central web-based REDCap database hosted at Monash University.20 21 All data were de-identified at the site level, prior to entry into the central database. The collection instrument included predefined multiple-choice options, with opportunities for free-text comments. Data extractors were given explanatory notes and met regularly with the study team to resolve uncertainty. When cleaning data, free-text responses were coded using directed content analysis. Coding was conducted by JN-D, with unclear data categorised through discussion with RB, SC and GM.

Urgency and discharge disposition

Urgency of ED presentations was defined using the five-level Australasian Triage Scale (ATS) (online supplemental figure S1).22 23 These five levels were collapsed to create a binary variable classifying presentations as urgent (ATS category 1 or 2) or lower urgency (ATS categories 3–5). Discharge disposition refers to the treatment and referral pathway initiated at the conclusion of the ED visit, which could include admission at the current hospital, transfer for admission at another hospital or discharge with or without follow-up.

Psychosocial factors

Each presentation was categorised using the following psychosocial domains, commonly used by Australian mental health professionals24: family social background; history of mental illness in the patient and/or their family members and past traumas and stressors, including past or present traumatic events such as sexual, physical or psychological abuse, homelessness and substance use.

Diagnostic classification

Discharge diagnoses were identified from discharge summaries and/or mental health assessment notes. A diagnosis was included if it appeared in either one or both locations. Diagnoses were grouped within the major categories of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (online supplemental table S2).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed in Stata IC (V.16.1, StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Outliers were identified, reviewed to ensure accuracy and corrected or left untouched based on site feedback, review of normative data and author consensus. For aims (1) and (2), descriptive statistics were used. For aim (3), data were compared between metropolitan and rural/regional hospitals. For categorical data, counts and proportions were reported, with comparisons made using generalised estimating equations to produce ORs and 95% CIs, accounting for clustering within hospitals. Continuous data were described using medians and IQR, with comparisons made using linear mixed models. Due to right-skewed time-based hospital data,25 log transformations were conducted for time-to-treatment and length of stay variables. Log coefficients and CIs were exponentiated to report ORs for geometric means.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Across 23 sites, 2234 unique mental health patients were included: 1803 who visited metropolitan EDs (range of eligible patients across sites=165–3186) and 431 who visited rural/regional base EDs (site totals range=61–403) (table 1). Two rural/regional EDs received fewer than 100 eligible patient visits in 2018. Overall, 1323 (59.4%) patients were female, and the mean age was 14.4 (SD=2.6) years. Two hundred and two (9.7%) patients identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait/South Sea Islander and 158 (7.6%) were born overseas. The odds of being born overseas were lower (OR 0.25, 95% CI 0.07, 0.85) among patients visiting rural/regional than in metropolitan EDs.

The proportion of missing values for ‘Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait/South Sea Islander’ and ‘born overseas’ was higher in metropolitan (8.0%) than rural/regional (1.6%) sites. On review, it was determined that most metropolitan missing values were from a single hospital network (n=3 hospitals, 81% of missing ethnicity and country of birth data). In a sensitivity analysis excluding data from these three metropolitan hospitals, the likelihood of young people who identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait/South Sea Islander (OR 1.52, 95% CI 0.53, 4.32) or who were born overseas (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.09, 0.99) in rural/regional and metropolitan EDs was similar to that reported in the primary analysis.

Service use patterns

Service use patterns were broadly similar across hospital types. Overall, 28.9% of patients were in contact with health services in the 24 hours prior to presenting and 55.9% attended ED with family or friends (table 2). Thirty-seven per cent arrived via road ambulance, while 15.4% presented with police. Fifty-one per cent presented outside business hours (between 18:00 and 07:59 hours) and 23.1% presented over the weekend. The most common reasons for ED presentations were suicide/self-harm ideation (38.2%), self-harm/suicide attempts (29.9%) and behavioural disturbance (13.7%) (online supplemental table S3). Child protection services were involved in 15.0% of cases and 18.9% presented with acute drug or alcohol intoxication. Counts of missing values for previous 24-hour health contact, child protection involvement and acute intoxication are reported in online supplemental table S4.

Of all patients, 15.2% were triaged as high urgency, of which 95.3% were category 2 (online supplemental figure S1). Performance indicators were only met for those triaged as non-urgent (ATS category 5), with 82.6% seen on time (target indicator=70%) (online supplemental table S1).22 There was a significant difference in achievement of on-time initiation of care between hospital types, with more patients seen on time in rural/regional (60.6%) than metropolitan (41.4%) EDs (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.29, 4.00). This was most evident for patients categorised as ATS categories 3 (OR 2.40, 95% CI 1.19, 4.86) and 4 (OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.07, 3.85).

Median wait times were shorter in rural/regional EDs compared with metropolitan settings for ED clinicians (18 min shorter), mental health assessments (1.4 hours shorter) and overall length of stay in ED (1.1 hours shorter) (table 3). Overall, 1498 (68.6%) patients were assessed by mental health staff and 660 (29.5%) received at least one psychiatric intervention in ED, with family systems interventions more common in rural/regional EDs (online supplemental figure S2). In rural/regional EDs, 291 (67.8%) patients received at least one specialist consultation, compared with 1411 (79.9%) in metropolitan EDs, where ED-based mental health teams were more frequently used (online supplemental figure S3). Across sites, 329 (14.7%) patients were admitted to the same hospital, while 86 (3.9%) were transferred to another hospital for admission. For those admitted, median length of inpatient stay was lower in rural/regional (2 days, IQR 0.9–4.5) than metropolitan (5.5 days, IQR 1.8–10.3) hospitals. The proportion of patients initially admitted but eventually transferred to another hospital was 13.0% in rural/regional hospitals (n=7) and 2.3% in metropolitan hospitals (n=6). Of all presenting patients, 1661 (80.2%) received referrals to other services at discharge. Eight hundred and fifty-six (38.3%) re-presented due to a mental health condition in the following 12 months (30.3% presented 1–3 times, 8.0% 4 times or more). Counts of missing values for assessment, disposition, waiting times and length of stay are reported in online supplemental table S4.

Across sites, patients were most commonly referred to child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS) (45.8%); this was more frequent in rural/regional ED settings (60.6%; OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.22, 3.93). Referrals to general practitioners were the second most documented referral (15.8%; of which 7.5% received advice to obtain/update a mental healthcare plan), followed by the National Youth Mental Health Foundation (headspace, 9.4%), private psychiatrists (5.5%) and paediatricians (2.5% public, 1.7% private) (online supplemental figure S4).

Psychosocial factors

The most commonly documented psychosocial factors associated with ED presentations were family (70.9%) or personal (66.6%) history of mental health issues/disorders and relational problems (eg, family discord, bullying or romantic relationship breakdown; 41.8%; figure 1). Twenty-seven per cent (27.4%) reported other life-stressors (eg, school, relocation, physical appearance or health-related stress and sleeping difficulties), while 22.3% reported past or present trauma, abuse and/or neglect. Fifty-one per cent (51.5%) reported living with one parent, other relatives or in blended families, while 11.1% lived in foster care, residential care, temporary/crisis accommodation or had no fixed address.

Diagnoses and diagnostic impressions

Self-harm, suicidal thoughts and/or behaviours were the most frequently documented diagnosis (38.4%), followed by trauma-related and stressor-related (25.0%), depressive (15.2%) and anxiety (14.6%) disorders (table 4). No significant differences in diagnoses between hospital types were detected.

Discussion

This large observational study profiles young people presenting to the EDs of Australian rural/regional and metropolitan hospitals with mental health concerns. The characteristics in our cohort are broadly consistent with single-centre studies in metropolitan tertiary paediatric hospitals in Victoria26 and Queensland,27 including the proportion presenting with self-harm and suicidal ideation. However, the prevalence of trauma and stressor-related disorders (25.0%) and depressive disorders (15.2%) was substantially higher than previously found (0.6% and 2.7% respectively26). This is likely due to the inclusion of secondary diagnoses in our analysis, suggesting high rates of comorbid mental disorder diagnoses in this population.

Our study identified consistency in common psychosocial factors associated with ED visits across rural/regional and metropolitan sites, including family mental health history, relationship problems; school and sleep-related issues and documented trauma, abuse and neglect. This highlights the need for trauma-informed care, and improved connectivity between EDs and community mental health providers. Given the importance of managing these situational factors to successful containment of acute paediatric mental health crises,28 these findings highlight the importance of psychosocial assessment tools for the formulation of effective management strategies and referral plans.29

Across contexts, a moderate proportion of young people had high acuity and/or clinical severity, with 15.2% triaged as needing to be seen within 10 min (ATS 1 or 2) and 18.6% requiring admission. Following the initial presentation, 38.3% returned to the same ED at least once in the following 12 months, with 8.0% presenting >4 times. In recent years, effort has been made to reduce reliance on mental health patients in the ED, particularly in metropolitan settings.30 31 Given findings of similar re-presentation rates in rural/regional sites, improvement of community-level and outpatient supports in rural/regional areas may be warranted.

Patients presenting to rural/regional EDs were more likely to be seen quickly, receive timely care according to their triage category and spend less time in the ED. These findings align with national trends in wait times across all ED presentations in 2018–19,11 highlighting the different pressures faced by metropolitan and rural/regional ED staff and patients. Previous research found that patients attending rural/regional Australian EDs may be treated more quickly, but with less specialised care and fewer options at discharge,11 13 potentially explaining shorter wait times.32 However, this was not supported by our findings. No difference was observed in specialist referrals or the total number of referrals patients received at discharge, and referrals to CAMHS were higher in rural/regional settings. Those admitted in rural/regional EDs tended to spend less time on local inpatient wards but were more likely to be transferred to another hospital after an initial inpatient admission. Notably, none of the rural/regional sites had paediatric mental health inpatient units, but the rate of admission and transfer to other hospitals between rural/regional and metropolitan EDs was similar. Previous research has found that general paediatric or adult wards in rural/regional hospitals may be used as a ‘holding space’ while awaiting transfer to specialised mental health units in larger regional centres.11 This may delay the onset of specialist treatment and require paediatricians and adult mental health staff to act outside their skillset to deliver interim care.33 When taken with the higher referrals to CAMHS in rural/regional EDs, our findings suggest a more nuanced picture—potentially indicating a preference for local CAMHS outpatient follow-up rather than transfers to geographically distant inpatient units. However, more targeted research on discharge destinations and referrals is required to confirm this and to assess the impact of transfer distance on access to family supports.12

Evidence from a recent systematic review suggests that rural/regional CAMHS are inadequately resourced.13 In this context, the finding of the current study of significant reliance on these services in the rural/regional context supports calls for increased funding.13 Further investigation of the different models of care delivered in metropolitan and rural/regional hospitals is warranted, particularly with regard to utilisation of specialist versus generalist psychiatric emergency and community services, and the role of general paediatric inpatient beds in rural/regional hospitals versus specialist metropolitan paediatric psychiatric beds in managing crisis presentations.

Innovative solutions which support patients, families and local clinicians to manage mental health are needed to ensure young people can access prompt and evidence-based care.13 Increased funding for rural/regional CAMHS services and programmes, which increase the confidence of non-mental health clinicians to manage mental health,31 could increase specialist service options in rural/regional settings.13 A focus on patient and family co-design can ensure services are tailored to local barriers and facilitators of access.

Strengths and limitations

By sampling diverse hospital sites, we believe our findings are broadly generalisable across Australia. We used a more in-depth data collection approach than larger regional9 and national11 Australian datasets, enabling more detailed profiling of presenting young people. Given the unique health needs and near-ubiquitous specialist healthcare shortages in regional and rural communities around the world,7 8 our findings support further research to understand and mitigate the gaps in mental healthcare needs of young people in regional and rural areas. Regional and rural communities are unique and require tailored solutions.8 Nonetheless, the findings of this study could be broadly informative for research and policy in countries where similar disparities occur.

The study has some limitations. First, using a fixed number of presentations per site may misrepresent the profile of children and adolescents presenting to large metropolitan hospitals servicing catchments where most young Australians reside10 and where annual visit rates are much higher. However, given the predominance of research from major referral hospitals,26 27 emphasis on rural/regional hospitals is warranted. Second, hospitals were recruited via convenience sampling and may not be representative at the rural, regional, metropolitan or national/state level. Third, patient data were de-identified by sites prior to entry into the central database, so we are unable to determine whether a specific patient presented to and was enrolled at multiple hospitals. Finally, this study used data from a single year, with limited follow-up to determine long-term patient outcomes. Data were collected before the COVID-19 pandemic, which was associated with changes in utilisation of the ED for mental healthcare.34,36 However, use of pre-COVID-19 data enables a clearer picture of presenting profiles within relatively ‘stable’ conditions. Future monitoring and longitudinal follow-up of children and adolescents using the ED for mental health reasons postpandemic is required to contextualise these findings.

Conclusion

In 2018, the profiles of young people presenting to EDs were broadly similar in rural/regional and metropolitan hospitals, but available treatment and care pathways differed. Short-term outcomes suggest considerable morbidity, including substantial rates of re-presentation in the following 12 months. Programmes aimed at improving outcomes of mental health patients in the ED through targeted family based interventions and improved linkage between EDs, primary health services and specialist providers are warranted.
