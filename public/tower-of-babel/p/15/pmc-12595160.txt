Personalized rTMS treatment recommendation with retrieval-augmented LLM reasoning
Authors: Lingling Xu, Haoran Xie, Xiaohui Tao, S Joe Qin, Fu Lee Wang, Raj Gururajan, Soman Elangovan, Yanmei Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is an effective, non-invasive neuromodulation therapy for major depressive disorder (MDD) and treatment-resistant depression (TRD). However, current clinical practice typically relies on standardized protocols that may not adequately account for individual patient variability. To address this gap, we propose a novel, interpretable framework for personalized rTMS treatment recommendations that combines a pretrained sentence embedding model with large language model (LLM)-based reasoning in a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) setting. Specifically, our approach leverages a pretrained sentence embedding model to encode structured patient profiles into a dense semantic representation, enabling the retrieval of clinically similar cases. These retrieved examples serve as few-shot prompts for in-context learning (ICL), enabling the LLM to reason over these examples and synthesize customized rTMS treatment parameters. Unlike previous approaches that focus solely on individual aspects of personalization, our framework integrates all key parameters (frequency, intensity, and stimulation mode) into a comprehensive recommendation. We systematically evaluated various sentence embedding models and LLMs. Among them, Bge-large-en-v1.5 for few-shot retrieval and GPT-4o-mini for reasoning achieved the highest rTMS protocol matching accuracy of 78.18% using 15 few-shot examples. Our approach is fine-tuning-free, interpretable, and adaptable to real-world, resource-poor clinical settings, providing a promising step forward in data-driven personalized neurostimulation therapy.Keywords: Semantic retrieval, Sentence embedding, LLM reasoning, Personalized rTMS recommendation

Introduction

Depression is a serious mental illness affecting millions of people worldwide and has consequently attracted extensive research attention in recent years [1, 2]. rTMS has emerged as an effective non-invasive neuromodulation therapy for the treatment of a variety of depressive disorders, including MDD and TRD [3, 4]. rTMS works by delivering pulsed magnetic fields to specific areas of the cerebral cortex, typically targeting areas associated with mood regulation, such as the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) [5]. Clinical rTMS protocols typically involve the specification of several key stimulation parameters: frequency (e.g., 1 Hz and 10 Hz), intensity (usually defined as a percentage of an individual’s resting motor threshold), target location (e.g., left/right DLPFC), and session duration or number of sessions. These parameters significantly influence therapeutic outcomes, with studies indicating that both under-stimulation and over-stimulation may limit efficacy or induce adverse effects [6, 7]. However, current clinical practices for selecting these parameters often rely on standardized protocols or physicians’ empirical knowledge, which may not fully capture the differences of patient heterogeneity. This highlights the urgent need for personalized rTMS treatment recommendation systems that are data-driven, interpretable, and clinically applicable.

Many previous studies have explored personalized rTMS protocol design. Garnaat et al. [8] emphasized the importance of patient-specific factors such as clinical characteristics in guiding rTMS treatment decisions. Chou et al. [9] reviewed the efficacy of various rTMS protocols in patients with MDD, recommending personalized stimulation modes such as high-frequency, low-frequency, or intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS). Wang et al. [10] showed that personalizing rTMS targeting based on multiple resting-state fMRI scans significantly improves the reliability and consistency of stimulation compared to single-scan methods, enhancing targeting precision. In addition, Klooster et al. [11] proposed a multimodal neuroimaging-guided approach that tailors rTMS protocols to individual brain structure, connectivity, and symptom profiles. While the approach is logically sound, it remains speculative and requires validation through randomized clinical trials (RCTs). Similarly, Fang et al. [12] theoretically demonstrated the feasibility of optimizing rTMS targets and parameters using brain controllability and optimal control analysis. Despite these advances, most existing approaches focus on only a single aspect of personalization, such as stimulation mode, target localization, or theoretically reasoned strategies, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive framework that integrates all key components of personalized rTMS protocols.

Recent advances in LLMs and pretrained sentence embedding techniques have opened up new opportunities for knowledge retrieval and decision support in the healthcare domain. Among these developments, ICL, an emergent capability of LLMs, has introduced a novel paradigm that eliminates the need for explicit fine-tuning [13, 14]. By providing a few input–output examples as demonstrations, LLMs can perform reasoning and generalization by analogy, much like humans, leveraging their powerful contextual understanding without modifying model parameters. On the other hand, pretrained sentence embedding models have demonstrated a strong capacity to capture semantic features of sentences, which is critical for downstream tasks such as semantic search and text retrieval [15]. Several recent studies have explored the integration of text retrieval and LLMs for medical applications. For instance, few-shot examples retrieved using embedding-based search have been utilized to improve performance in Chinese medical question answering [16] and medical question generation [17]. However, the majority of these approaches have primarily focused on general clinical QA or text generation tasks. To date, little attention has been given to applying LLMs in conjunction with retrieval-based methods for personalized rTMS treatment.

To support personalized rTMS treatment recommendations in low-resource settings, we employ a fine-tuning-free approach using an off-the-shelf, pretrained sentence embedding model. Specifically, we first employ a pretrained embedding model to encode rich demographic and clinical information from structured patient profile data into dense semantic representations. These embeddings enable efficient and accurate retrieval of relevant treatment cases, which serve as few-shot examples to enhance LLM reasoning in RAG settings. Our method builds upon the assumption that new patients are more likely to benefit from rTMS treatment protocols that have proven effective for patients with similar profiles. Rather than simply averaging the retrieved rTMS treatment solutions, we leverage LLMs to reason over the retrieved examples, synthesize a personalized rTMS protocol, and provide an explanation for the recommended treatment. This design enables more flexible and interpretable decision-making, especially in complex scenarios where multiple clinical and demographic factors influence treatment choices. Experimental results demonstrate that providing more few-shot examples typically leads to recommended rTMS protocols that are closer to the ground-truth rTMS treatments. Notably, the combination of Bge-large-en-v1.5 as the embedding model and GPT-4o-mini as the reasoning engine achieves the highest recommendation accuracy of 78.18% when using the top-15 retrieved patient profiles and corresponding rTMS protocols as few-shot examples. Related work

rTMS treatment for depression

rTMS has emerged as a promising non-invasive brain stimulation technique, with growing evidence supporting its efficacy in treating MDD, particularly in patients who do not respond adequately to antidepressant medications. Several RCTs have demonstrated the antidepressant efficacy of rTMS. For instance, it is found that rTMS significantly improved depressive symptoms compared to sham treatment, with sustained benefits over a six-week treatment period [18]. Likewise, a meta-analysis by Berlim et al. [19] reported that low-frequency rTMS over the right DLPFC is an effective and well-tolerated treatment for MDD, showing significantly higher response and remission rates than sham stimulation. In addition, Voineskos et al. [20] suggested that rTMS in TRD reduces neurophysiological markers of cortical inhibition and excitability, highlighting its impact on brain function beyond symptom improvement. Supporting its broader clinical utility, O’Reardon et al. [21] found that rTMS demonstrates comparable efficacy to psychotherapy and SSRIs, with fewer side effects, lower risk of serious adverse events, comparable compliance, faster symptom relief, and cost-effectiveness. Lacroix et al. [22] showed that clinical factors, such as shorter episode duration, higher agitation of HDRS (or lower sleepiness), and more previous rTMS sessions, can help predict the effectiveness of rTMS treatment for drug-resistant depression. Tuinstra et al. [23] demonstrated that left DLPFC rTMS not only improves depressive symptoms but may also reduce anxiety and suicide risk in TRD patients. Squires et al. [24] introduced DECGAN, a generative model designed to oversample underrepresented fMRI connectivity patterns, thereby improving prediction accuracy of difficult-to-classify cases in rTMS treatment.

Moreover, increasing efforts have been made to personalize rTMS treatments in order to optimize therapeutic outcomes for depression. Garnaat et al. [8] discovered patient-specific factors, including clinical characteristics, genetic predispositions, and biomarkers derived from neuroimaging data, can guide personalized rTMS treatment decisions. Chou et al. [9] examined how various rTMS and theta burst stimulation (TBS) protocols can be tailored for MDD patients with psychiatric comorbidities such as generalized anxiety disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder, emphasizing the importance of individualized approaches. While Gogulski et al. [25] discussed how personalization of rTMS parameters, such as stimulation target, intensity, pulse waveform, coil type, and total number of sessions, can significantly influence treatment efficacy technically. Klooster et al. [11] employed neuroimaging to determine optimal coil placement based on individual brain characteristics and supplemented this with EEG data to inform stimulation frequency and duration, but it remains speculative and requires further validation through RCT. Fang et al. [12] proposes a personalized rTMS protocol for depression using brain network controllability and optimal control analysis, demonstrating its feasibility in more efficiently guiding the depressed brain toward a healthier state compared to standard rTMS. Hsu et al. [26] highlighted that specific stimulation parameters, particularly higher frequency, higher intensity, and sufficient pulse count, significantly enhance treatment efficacy for TRD. Cao et al. [27] proposed a feasible approach for identifying stable, personalized TMS targets based on scalp measurements, offering a more accurate method for TMS targeting in clinical settings. Che et al. [28] suggested that microstate C is associated with anhedonia and may serve as a reliable biomarker for personalized rTMS treatment. Despite these advancements in personalizing rTMS protocols, few studies have investigated automated recommendation systems for personalized rTMS protocols based on structured patient profiles, a gap this work aims to address.

LLMs for Healthcare treatment personalization

Recent studies have demonstrated the growing potential of LLMs in supporting personalized treatment across various healthcare domains. Benary et al. [29] explored the use of LLMs in precision oncology, showing that while these models do not yet match expert clinicians in accuracy, they were able to suggest clinically useful treatment options and assist in evidence screening and hypothesis generation for individualized care decisions. Similarly, Galitsky et al. [30] employed a meta-learning and abductive reasoning approach, enabling LLMs to generate user-specific prompts from deidentified health records. This study demonstrated how LLMs can be customized through personalization profiles to support individualized healthcare decision-making. In the context of rare diseases, Berger et al. [31] utilized LLMs for data augmentation combined with sentence embeddings to enable effective patient-to-patient matching based on symptom similarity. Shi et al. [32] proposed a Personalized Medical Language Model (PMLM), which integrates recommendation systems with reinforcement learning to generate and refine hard prompts tailored to individual patient needs for personalized responses. Lu et al. [33] further advanced this direction by combining a novel cancer risk prediction model with an LLM-powered healthcare recommender system to bridge the gap between risk assessment and actionable preventive care.

The MEREDITH system introduced by Lammert et al. [34] highlights how domain-specific data and expert feedback can significantly enhance LLM performance in generating clinically relevant, personalized cancer treatment recommendations. Similarly, Kruckel et al. [35] found that GPT-3.5 was capable of providing personalized recommendations for gynecologic malignancies based on patient-specific symptoms, such as pleural or ascites drainage, and interdisciplinary care considerations. Balakrishna et al. [36] demonstrated the integration of LLMs like BERT and GPT into smart drug administration systems, which provide personalized treatment recommendations based on real-time patient data, thereby improving individualized care effectiveness. Cardenas et al. [37] presented AutoHealth, a system that combines LLM-powered chatbots with wearable biosensors to deliver real-time monitoring and personalized treatment guidance for Parkinson’s disease, providing accessible, at-home care tailored to each patient’s needs. Cherukuri et al. [38] emphasized the value of integrating diverse and unstructured data sources, such as social media, wearable devices, patient narratives, and clinical records. While Chen et al. [39] introduced the ACMA system, which enhances LLMs with oncology-specific data and context-aware prompts to deliver highly personalized cancer treatment recommendations based on comprehensive patient profiles. Although LLMs have been applied across various medical domains, their use in developing personalized rTMS protocols for depression treatment remains largely unexplored.

Methodology

In this section, we begin by presenting three LLMs for sentence embedding models and then present the similarity-based semantic retrieval for few-shot example selection. Subsequently, four generative LLMs are employed for LLM reasoning to obtain the final rTMS treatment protocols. Finally, we illustrate that not only are the final rTMS parameters provided, but also, by leveraging semantic retrieval and LLM reasoning, the relevant few-shot examples and corresponding explanations are also provided. LLMs for sentence embedding

To effectively encode patient profile texts into meaningful embeddings, we explore several sentence embedding models as text encoders. Recent studies have shown that LLMs can also achieve competitive performance in sentence-level representation tasks [40, 41]. Motivated by this, we incorporate three LLMs in our experiments: Bge-large-en-v1.5,1 LLaMA-3.2-3B,2 and Mistral-7B-v0.3.3 Among them, Bge-large-en-v1.5 is explicitly designed for sentence embedding tasks such as semantic similarity and dense retrieval. In contrast, LLaMA-3.2-3B and Mistral-7B-v0.3 are primarily trained for autoregressive text generation.

Bge-large-en-v1.5 [42] is an English sentence embedding model developed by Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence (BAAI). It is specifically optimized for tasks such as semantic textual similarity and information retrieval. Built upon a transformer architecture, BGE encodes input sentences into dense vector representations that capture rich semantic meaning. The model is trained using contrastive learning on large-scale sentence pair datasets, encouraging semantically similar sentences to be close in embedding space while pushing dissimilar ones apart. In our work, we use Bge-large-en-v1.5 as a sentence encoder to obtain high-quality embeddings from structured textual patient profiles.

LLaMA-3.2-3B is a 3-billion-parameter decoder-only transformer from the Meta AI’s LLaMA-3 family. Although originally trained for autoregressive text generation, it can be adapted for sentence embedding by extracting hidden states from the final transformer layer. Specifically, given a tokenized input sequence x, we perform a forward pass (with gradients disabled) to obtain the final hidden state H∈RT×d. We then apply mean pooling over the sequence length T to compute the sentence embedding: h=1T∑t=1THt. This approach allows us to employ LLaMA-3.2-3B as a general-purpose encoder for structured clinical text.

Mistral-7B-v0.3 is a 7-billion-parameter decoder-only LLM that extends the Mistral-7B architecture with a longer context window and refined pretraining objectives. Similar to the LLaMA-3.2-3B setup, we freeze the model weights and treat it as a feature extractor. The input sequence is passed to the model and the final hidden states are mean-pooled to produce sentence-level embeddings. Despite being trained with a generative objective, prior studies have shown that such pooled representations carry rich semantic information [43]. Accordingly, we include Mistral-7B-v0.3 in our benchmark to evaluate its ability to capture meaningful representations from complex clinical narratives.

Few-shot examples selection

For personalized rTMS treatment recommendations, we construct few-shot examples in the form of input–output sentence pairs to guide LLMs via ICL. Each input consists of a textual patient profile that includes both demographic and clinical information, while the corresponding output specifies structured rTMS treatment parameters, such as treatment duration, stimulation intensity, and stimulation mode.

Our approach is grounded in the hypothesis that patients with similar profiles are more likely to benefit from similar rTMS protocols. Consequently, retrieving past cases that are semantically similar to a given (query) patient becomes a crucial step for building effective few-shot examples. To this end, we adopt a semantic retrieval strategy based on sentence embeddings. Both the query patient profile and candidate historical cases are encoded into dense vector representations using the embedding models described in Section 3.1. Semantic similarity between the query and each candidate profile is measured using cosine similarity.

Prompt-based LLM reasoning and explanation

We design tailored prompts and instructions to guide large language models (LLMs) in reasoning over retrieved patient cases and synthesizing context-aware, personalized rTMS treatment protocols. The prompt is structured to position the LLM as a domain-specific medical assistant, beginning with the instruction: “You are a medical AI assistant specializing in rTMS treatment recommendations.” To encourage in-context reasoning, we further include a directive such as: “Here are some SIMILAR patient cases and their recommended rTMS treatments that may be relevant to our new patient:”, followed by a list of semantically retrieved few-shot examples.

For the LLM-based reasoning component, we explore four powerful generative LLMs, GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-R1, and LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct.

GPT-3.5-turbo is an efficient and cost-effective variant of OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 series, widely used for interactive reasoning and instruction-following tasks due to its fast inference and balanced performance.

To enhance the interpretability and transparency of rTMS treatment recommendations generated by the LLM, we employed a prompt design strategy that both elicits structured outputs and derives explicit intermediate reasoning steps. We first enforce a clearly defined output format via explicit constraints, which ensures consistency and allows downstream evaluation and comparison. More importantly, our carefully designed prompts aim to transform the LLM from a black-box generator into a transparent reasoning agent. Before generating a final recommendation, the LLM must clearly articulate its decision-making process. This includes identifying clinically relevant features (e.g., age, diagnosis, symptom severity), referencing retrieved patient cases, and justifying the selection of key parameters (e.g., stimulation target, frequency, intensity).

Based on this intermediate reasoning process, the LLM selects the most relevant retrieved cases as reference and synthesizes a context-aware, personalized rTMS treatment plan for the new patient. This step-by-step, interpretability-oriented prompting strategy not only grounds the final recommendations in clinically similar cases but also provides traceable evidence, thereby enhancing transparency and user trust. This design makes the entire system more interpretable and supports clinical validation of the recommendations. Experiments

In this section, we first introduce the experimental dataset and evaluation metrics, followed by a description of the baseline model used for comparison with our proposed approach. We then conduct a series of experiments to evaluate the performance of different sentence embedding models and LLMs for reasoning, under varying numbers of few-shot examples.

Dataset

We collected data from 100 patients diagnosed with depression, including demographic information, clinical assessments, and corresponding rTMS treatment protocols. Demographic variables, such as gender, age, education level, and marital status, offer insight into patients’ socioeconomic backgrounds. Clinical data include age of depression onset, duration of depressive episodes, antidepressant and psychotropic medication usage, and scores from standardized psychological rating scales (SDS, SAS, HAMD, and HAMA), recorded both before and after rTMS treatment. Importantly, the answers to these scales are organized into categories, and any missing answers were filled in with the most common response. Experimental details

Experimental settings. Our dataset of 100 patient samples is randomly partitioned into a training set (80 samples) and a test set (20 samples). To address the limited sample size and enhance the robustness of model evaluation, we further apply 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, resulting in five distinct training-test splits. This strategy ensures that each sample is used exactly once in the test set, enabling a comprehensive and unbiased assessment of model performance. The evaluation metric is recommendation accuracy, which measures how closely the LLM-generated rTMS treatment protocols align with the actual protocols prescribed by psychiatrists. Baselines. Due to few study in this domain, we consider the following variant as baselines: (1) Semantic Embedding Model: To evaluate the sentence-level embedding capabilities of LLMs in our task, we compare them against several strong baseline models. Specifically, we include ClinicalBERT,5 a domain-specific model pretrained on clinical notes from the MIMIC-III dataset, which is well-suited for capturing the nuances of medical text [44]. We also incorporate SimCSE-BERT6 and SimCSE-RoBERTa,7 which are pretrained using contrastive learning and are known for their strong performance in semantic similarity tasks [45]. (2) Manual Aggregation: A rule-based treatment recommendations strategy, where the stimulation intensity and duration are computed as the arithmetic mean of the top-k retrieved cases, while the stimulation mode is directly adopted from the most similar (Top-1) case. This approach serves as a non-generative baseline, allowing us to isolate the impact of embedding quality on recommendation performance. (3) Random Selection: To better compare the semantic similarity based few-shot examples selection, random selection is introduced, where few-shot examples are selected randomly.

SimCSE-RoBERTa surpasses ClinicalBERT and SimCSE-BERT, benefiting from a larger backbone model and the use of supervised contrastive learning. Among the evaluated LLMs, Bge-large-en-v1.5, LLaMA-3.2-3B, and Mistral-7B-v0.3 demonstrate stronger embedding performance than both ClinicalBERT and SimCSE-BERT. Notably, SimCSE-RoBERTa achieves the highest Top-k recommendation accuracy when used as the embedding model, underscoring its robust sentence-level representation capabilities. Moreover, the embedding performance of the LLMs, Bge-large-en-v1.5, LLaMA-3.2-3B, and Mistral-7B-v0.3, varies significantly depending on the reasoning LLM used and the choice of top-k. Among them, Bge-large-en-v1.5 generally yields the best Top-10/15/20 accuracy across different reasoning LLMs, achieving the highest accuracy in particular when paired with GPT-4o-mini.

LLM Reasoning Vs Manual Aggregation. Manual Aggregation strategy generally outperforms GPT-3.5-turbo when more than one few-shot example is provided. Specifically, when the number of few-shot examples is limited to 1 or 2, manual aggregation consistently achieves better performance than GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-R1, and LLaMA-3.3-70B-instruct under most embedding models. However, as the number of few-shot examples increases, LLM-based reasoning begins to outperform the manual aggregation approach, likely because semantically similar few-shot examples provide richer contextual knowledge, enabling LLMs to reason more effectively.

LLMs Reasoning Capability of Different LLMs. When the number of few-shot examples is limited to one, GPT-3.5-turbo outperforms GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-R1, and LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct across various embedding models. However, when more than one few-shot example is provided, performance varies depending on the embedding model. Overall, the ranking of model performance can be broadly summarized as: GPT-4o-mini >LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct >DeepSeek-R1 >GPT-3.5-turbo. Additionally, GPT-4o-mini, when paired with Bge-large-en-v1.5, achieves the highest accuracy with 10, 15, and 20 few-shot examples.

Few-shot Examples Selection Strategies. Recommendation accuracy of random selection strategy improves when few-shot examples are selected at random as the number of examples increases, with Top-20 accuracy generally performing best, likely due to the limited size of the overall dataset, which allows more relevant instances to be included in the demonstration set. However, similarity-based semantic retrieval consistently outperforms random selection across different embedding models and reasoning LLMs. This highlights the effectiveness of using semantic similarity to retrieve informative and relevant few-shot examples, reinforcing its value as a strategy for enhancing LLM-based recommendation performance. Moreover, it is discovered that the performance of manual aggregation and LLM-based reasoning strategies improves with the number of few-shot examples under various sentence embedding models.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed data from 100 patients diagnosed with depression who received rTMS treatment. Our proposed framework explores various LLMs for both sentence embeddings and reasoning, aiming to retrieve past similar cases and recommend personalized rTMS protocols. The approach first uses sentence embedding models to identify the most relevant examples, which are then incorporated into structured prompts for LLM-based reasoning.

Experimental results show that when provided with 15 few-shot examples, using Bge-large-en-v1.5 for sentence embedding and GPT-4o-mini for reasoning achieves the highest accuracy (78.18%) in matching real-world rTMS protocols. Our results also demonstrate that contrastive learning is more effective than domain-specific pretraining alone in improving word embedding quality. For instance, SimCSE-RoBERTa achieves superior performance in certain Top-k scenarios. While LLMs like Bge-large-en-v1.5 also outperform ClinicalBERT and SimCSE-BERT, highlighting the importance of training objectives and model size.

Furthermore, the reasoning performance of LLMs is highly context-dependent. Simpler models like GPT-3.5-turbo perform relatively well with small amounts of few-shot context, while more advanced models like GPT-4o-mini consistently outperform other models as the number of few-shot examples increases. Importantly, few-shot semantic retrieval significantly outperforms random selection, especially when leveraging a combination of strong embedding and reasoning models (e.g., Bge-large-en-v1.5 + GPT-4o-mini). Finally, while manual aggregation is more effective than LLM-based reasoning in low-context scenarios (e.g., 1–2 examples), LLM-based reasoning outperforms manual strategies as the number of examples increases, demonstrating its ability to synthesize complex clinical patterns from richer contextual information.

In addition, our proposed framework also supports providing detailed explanations when providing treatment recommendations. By constructing a structured prompt to guide LLMs based on the patient’s patient profile, the model is guided to reason about intermediate clinical steps. By doing so, the decision-making process not only enhances people’s trust in the model output but also facilitates clinical validation by allowing clinicians and researchers to trace the source of each rTMS recommendation.

Despite the promising alignment between the recommended rTMS protocols and those proposed by experienced psychiatrists, as well as the improved interpretability of the recommendations, our study has several limitations. First, our patient profiles are limited to structured clinical notes. The absence of neuroimaging data (e.g., EEG, fMRI) may constrain the model’s ability to capture individualized treatment-relevant information. Second, with only 100 patients, our findings may not generalize to broader populations, including those with comorbid conditions or different demographic profiles.

To address these limitations, future work will focus on incorporating neuroimaging data, such as EEG and fMRI, to construct multimodal patient profiles. In addition, we plan to expand the dataset by recruiting a larger and more diverse cohort of patients undergoing rTMS treatment. Most importantly, we aim to conduct RCTs to rigorously evaluate the clinical effectiveness of LLM-driven personalized rTMS treatment protocols.

Conclusions

This paper proposes a personalized rTMS treatment recommendation framework that does not require fine-tuning, combining a pretrained sentence embedding model with LLM-based reasoning in a RAG setting. By leveraging the semantic representations of structured patient records, our method retrieves clinically similar cases as few-shot examples for ICL. Subsequently, the LLM reasons over these examples to generate interpretable and patient-specific rTMS treatment protocols. Experimental results show that the performance of manual aggregation and LLM-based reasoning strategies tends to improve with the number of few-shot examples used. Bge-large-en-v1.5 for semantic retrieval and GPT-4o-mini for reasoning achieve the highest accuracy (78.18%) in matching ground-truth rTMS protocols when using 15 few-shot examples. Furthermore, we validate the effectiveness of powerful embedding models, semantic retrieval for few-shot example selection, and LLM reasoning strategy for rTMS treatment recommendation. This study highlights the potential of combining retrieval-based methods with LLMs for clinically relevant decision support in neurostimulation therapy. Furthermore, our approach is fully interpretable, adaptable to resource-constrained environments, and achieves generalization without additional model training. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, our patient archives were limited to structured clinical notes and did not include neuroimaging data (e.g., EEG and fMRI), which may limit the model’s ability to capture individualized treatment-relevant features. Second, the dataset included only 100 patients, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader or more diverse clinical populations. Future work will address these limitations by incorporating multimodal patient data and expanding the dataset to support more robust clinical validation.
