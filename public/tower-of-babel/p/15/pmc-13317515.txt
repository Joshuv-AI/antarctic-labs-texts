Spinal cord stimulation for idiopathic spinal myoclonus: illustrative case
Authors: Salaar Ahmed, Vafi Salmasi, Vivek P. Buch, Mensure Polat, Jehan Zahid Bahrainwala, Jocelyn M. Jiao, Laurice Yang, Ashwin G. Ramayya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSpinal myoclonus is a rare movement disorder characterized by sudden, involuntary jerking movements arising from segmental spinal circuitry. Treatment is primarily directed toward underlying etiologies or symptomatic control with antiepileptic medications, botulinum toxin injections, or intrathecal therapies. Evidence supporting spinal cord stimulation (SCS) remains limited to isolated reports involving secondary etiologies such as cervical myelopathy or complex regional pain syndrome. Myoclonus is a phenomenological term for movements characterized frequently as sudden, nonrhythmic, involuntary jerks. These movements can involve one muscle or a group of muscles; they can be positive, resulting from sudden muscle contraction, or negative, caused by a brief interruption or relaxation of ongoing muscle activity.1

Myoclonus can be classified in several ways, including by its anatomical origin. Cortical myoclonus arises from the cerebral cortex, reticular myoclonus originates in the brainstem, and spinal myoclonus begins in the spinal cord, often activating skeletal muscles supplied by the affected spinal segment.1

A wide range of etiologies have been associated with spinal myoclonus, and in many cases, the precise underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.2 This can include vascular abnormalities, both disc prolapse and intra- and extramedullary lesions causing spinal compression, spinal cord trauma, infection, neurodegenerative processes, and iatrogenic injuries, among others.3,4

Treatment is primarily directed at addressing the underlying etiology, which may help remove the driving factor for the myoclonus. Symptomatic management has also been described, most commonly with antiepileptic medications such as levetiracetam, clonazepam, and valproic acid.2 Other therapeutic options include botulinum toxin injections and continuous intrathecal morphine in selected cases.2

Evidence supporting the use of spinal cord stimulation (SCS) remains limited. To date, there are no clinical trials or observational studies evaluating its efficacy in this setting. However, 2 case reports have described the use of SCS in a total of 3 patients with spinal myoclonus, secondary to type 1 complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) and cervical myelopathy, with reported symptomatic improvement.5,6

Here, we describe the case of a patient with idiopathic segmental spinal myoclonus who experienced significant symptomatic relief following SCS, which has not been reported before.

Illustrative Case

We report the case of an 83-year-old female who was referred to our neurosurgery clinic by the movement disorders clinic. Her past medical history was notable for resistant hypertension with lability, coronary artery disease with two prior myocardial infarctions, cerebrovascular accident, right lung adenocarcinoma, and ductal carcinoma in situ, both in remission.

She presented to us with chronic, medically refractory spinal myoclonus, manifesting as involuntary, rhythmic spasms and jerking movements involving the right midthoracic paraspinal musculature, shoulder girdle, and upper back. Symptoms began 4 years earlier following an episode of severe right-sided back pain requiring hospitalization. They progressively evolved into persistent, stimulus-insensitive myoclonic contractions that greatly interfered with mobility, posture, and activities of daily living, including her ability to monitor her blood pressure and take medications, ultimately impacting her overall health. The jerks were nonepileptic in nature, without alteration of awareness, and were frequently painful due to sustained paraspinal contraction. Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies of the right upper limb, performed in February 2022, demonstrated findings consistent with spinal segmental myoclonus spanning the rostral C2 level (spinal accessory nerve) to at least the C8 nerve roots. Needle EMG of selected right-sided muscles confirmed myoclonic discharges involving the neck, right upper limb, and midthoracic paraspinal musculature, supporting a multisegmental spinal generator. Given her prior cancer history, a paraneoplastic etiology was considered. Although serum glycine receptor was abnormally positive (ultimately deemed to be a false-positive result, given the lack of clinical correlation), CSF cell count/protein/glucose, CSF paranoplastic autoantibody evaluation, CT of the chest/abdomen/pelvis, MRI of the neuraxis, and whole-body PET/CT were unrevealing (Fig. 1). Imaging did not demonstrate any structural and/or focal lesion. Electroencephalography (EEG) showed no electrographic changes that would correlate with the myoclonic jerks. The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary movement disorder conference and was felt to be most consistent with segmental spinal myoclonus. Alternative cortical etiologies, including faciobrachial dystonic seizures and epilepsia partialis continua, were also considered; however, the absence of facial involvement, lack of cortical motor findings on MRI, and negative EEG made these diagnoses less likely. Over the subsequent 2 years, she underwent multiple medication trials, including clonazepam, levetiracetam (titrated to 1500 mg twice daily), valproic acid (later discontinued due to hyperammonemia), zonisamide, and tizanidine, as well as intermittent courses of high-dose corticosteroids, which produced temporary but incomplete improvement. Botulinum toxin injections, first given in 2023 and then multiple times subsequently, resulted in only temporary (if any) benefit. After approximately 1 year of medication trials, the patient was evaluated for worsening back pain and immobility that had been present since October 2023. MRI of the lumbar spine demonstrated L4 nerve root compression with L4–5 radiculopathy; however, this finding did not adequately account for the persistent segmental myoclonus, as the myoclonus had begun more than 1 year before the onset of radicular pain. The patient subsequently underwent lumbar decompression surgery in March 2024 for treatment of radiculopathy, which resulted in improvement of radicular symptoms but no change in the segmental myoclonus, and only limited improvement in axial back pain. These findings further supported the interpretation that the myoclonus was unlikely to be attributable to the lumbar compressive pathology. Despite exhaustive medical and interventional management, her spinal myoclonus remained refractory with recurrent exacerbations, prompting consideration of interventional treatments.

The patient was referred to our functional neurosurgery service for consideration of spinal cord stimulator placement, given prior case reports suggesting potential benefit in similar cases. The patient was counseled that SCS is not an FDA-approved treatment for spinal myoclonus; however, given the refractory nature of the disease to medical therapy, as well as patient preference, an off-label trial could be attempted. She was also informed that SCS could potentially provide additional benefit for her back pain. The patient decided to undergo a trial of SCS via a percutaneous, minimally invasive route to assess whether long-term SCS might be beneficial.

She therefore underwent a trial of thoracic spinal cord stimulator percutaneous implantation under fluoroscopic guidance. She reported improvement in spasms very early postoperatively. A follow-up appointment to remove the spinal cord stimulator was scheduled on postoperative day 7. During this visit, the patient reported that “99% of spasms were gone” and she was satisfied with the outcome of the SCS period. She requested to proceed with the implantation of a permanent spinal cord stimulator. The leads were removed without any complications. Considering her significant clinical improvement and after detailed counseling on operative as well as postoperative risks and care of a permanent spinal cord stimulator, a mutual decision was made to proceed with the implantation of a permanent spinal cord stimulator.

Just like the trial procedure, after induction of general anesthesia and endotracheal intubation, somatosensory evoked potential electrodes were placed, and the patient was positioned prone. Fluoroscopy localized the T11–12 interlaminar space. Given the epidural septations encountered at T12–L1 during the trial, epidural access was obtained one level higher. Following infiltration with 1.5% lidocaine with epinephrine (1:200,000), a 5- to 6-cm midline incision was made over T12–L2, and dissection was carried down to the spinous processes. Two 8-contact cylindrical epidural SCS leads (BIOTRONIK, 55 cm length) were advanced under fluoroscopic guidance and positioned spanning T5–7, with one lead placed midline and the second slightly right of midline (Fig. 2). Positioning was confirmed with fluoroscopy and impedance testing, and the leads were secured to the fascia using BIOTRONIK anchors and 2-0 silk sutures. A subcutaneous pocket was created in the right buttock for the implantable pulse generator (IPG). Multiphase stimulation was programmed using contacts 1, 3, and 5 of the left epidural lead, with parameters set at an amplitude of 1.0 mA, pulse width of 300 µsec, and frequency of 300 Hz. Postoperatively, the patient did well, and the neurological examination remained grossly intact. As before, the patient reported significant improvement in myoclonus, and at the 1-month follow-up, she reported as much as “98% improvement in the spinal myoclonus.” She reported experiencing only three myoclonic jerks in 2 weeks, having previously experienced multiple jerks daily, sometimes as often as every 2–5 minutes. In light of this robust symptomatic response, levetiracetam was titrated down from 1500 mg in a stepwise fashion to 500 mg twice daily. At her most recent follow-up, 30 weeks postprocedure, the patient continued to demonstrate complete resolution of myoclonic jerks and reported no episodes over the preceding several months. No long-term neurological complications or device-related adverse effects were noted, aside from IPG site pain and discomfort, which has been managed conservatively.

Discussion

Observations

There is very limited literature on the use of SCS for spinal myoclonus. To date, no observational studies have been published, and the available evidence remains limited to a small number of case reports. Notably, Bose and Banerjee reported the use of SCS in a patient with CRPS type 1 accompanied by myoclonus.5 It is important to recognize that SCS is already an established and widely utilized therapy for CRPS, with substantial supporting evidence for pain management in this population.7 Tapia Perez et al. also reported 2 cases of spinal myoclonus caused by degenerative compression myelopathy that responded to SCS.6 To date, these 3 cases constitute the entirety of the published literature describing the use of SCS specifically for spinal myoclonus.6

An important distinction between the previously reported cases and ours is the underlying etiology of the myoclonus. In all 3 prior cases, the myoclonus was secondary to an identifiable structural cause, including myelopathy from disc compression or CRPS following traumatic spinal cord injury. In contrast, despite extensive laboratory evaluation and imaging, no anatomical or metabolic cause was identified in our patient. The myoclonus was therefore classified as idiopathic, or primary, segmental spinal myoclonus.

Spinal myoclonus is thought to result from disordered communication between afferent inputs, originating in the dorsal root ganglion and synapsing within the spinal gray matter, and efferent outputs from anterior horn cells (α-motor neurons). These connections can be either monosynaptic or polysynaptic, which means there are interneurons in between, often receiving signals from the brain through corticospinal and rubrospinal tracts.8

Pathological myoclonus can arise from disruption at multiple levels within this circuitry. In segmental spinal myoclonus, as observed in our patient, the prevailing hypothesis involves loss of inhibitory control over spinal interneurons.9 This disinhibition leads to heightened excitability of anterior horn cells, ultimately producing involuntary, rhythmic muscle contractions.2 SCS primarily works by modulating dorsal column activity, and, at lower frequencies, it can modulate monosynaptic reflexes. It can also influence both segmental interneuronal circuits and descending inhibitory pathways through transsynaptic effect and can therefore restore a degree of physiological inhibition, thereby reducing motor neuron hyperexcitability and attenuating myoclonic activity.10–13

Since SCS for spinal myoclonus remains investigational, it is important to explore which stimulation parameters are most effective and whether settings traditionally developed for pain are effective for myoclonus or whether a distinct approach is required. In our case, we utilized a BIOTRONIK SCS system using RESONANCE, a multiphase stimulation paradigm. To our knowledge, the use of multiphase stimulation has not previously been reported in the treatment of spinal myoclonus. Unlike conventional single-phase stimulation, this approach delivers multiphase pulses that sweep therapeutic micropulses across multiple electrodes.14 Prior studies have reported the use of Nevro high-frequency stimulation, a 10-kHz high-frequency monophasic stimulation, which may also be effective for myoclonus.5, 6 Nevertheless, further investigation is warranted to evaluate long-term safety, durability of benefit, and broader applicability of SCS in this patient population.

This report is inherently limited by its single-patient design and lack of a control or sham stimulation condition, which makes it difficult to exclude placebo effects or expectation-related responses that are well described in neuromodulation studies. Outcome assessment relied primarily on clinical observation and patient-reported improvement rather than objective electrophysiological quantification of myoclonus frequency over time.

Lessons

Given the paucity of published data, currently limited to isolated case reports, our findings add to the emerging evidence that SCS may represent a promising neuromodulatory approach for refractory spinal myoclonus. Further studies are needed to evaluate long-term safety, durability of response, optimal stimulation paradigms, and patient selection criteria.
