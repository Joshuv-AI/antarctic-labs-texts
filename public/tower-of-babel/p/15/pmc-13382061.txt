Differences in parental risk perception of vaccine-preventable diseases based on disease name and clinical description: Results from the United States
Authors: Matthew R. Boyce, Timothy Callaghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives: To investigate the perceived risk of six vaccine-preventable diseases—diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, measles, mumps, and polio—based on disease name and clinical description, and compare differences in risk perception.Methods: We developed and administered an online survey to parents and legal guardians of children under 5 years of age in the United States from August 20–September 9, 2025. Study participants used Likert scales to characterize the risks posed by the diseases to unvaccinated children in the United States when presented with the name of the disease, and a clinical description of the disease that omitted the name. Wilcoxon matched-pair tests and multiple regression models comprised data analyses.Results: A total of 1033 participants were included in the analysis. Participants most often assessed all of the vaccine-preventable diseases as posing a high risk to unvaccinated children, based on both disease name and clinical description. Still, participants consistently rated disease risk as being lower when presented with only the name of the disease.Conclusions: Findings suggest that many survey respondents still perceive vaccine-preventable diseases to pose high risks to unvaccinated children in the United States, but that many do not fully appreciate the clinical significance of these diseases.

Introduction

Vaccination is among the most important and cost-effective public health interventions (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 1999). Globally, vaccination is responsible for saving millions of lives each year, with vaccines preventing both acute and prolonged illness (Nandi and Shet, 2020). The success of vaccines, however, has had an unexpected impact on public health. Diseases that once evoked feelings of fear and concern are now relatively rare, resulting in a misplaced and dangerous sense of complacency among some (MacDonald and SAGE Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy, 2015; Orenstein and Ahmed, 2017). Vaccination complacency exists when the perceived risks of vaccine-preventable diseases are sufficiently low that vaccination is no longer deemed a necessary precaution. This limited perceived risk of infectious diseases is a core component of theoretical frameworks that seek to explain vaccine hesitancy, defined as the delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccines despite the availability of vaccination services (MacDonald and SAGE Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy, 2015).

This study seeks to better understand the perceived risk of vaccine-preventable diseases by examining public attitudes toward diseases based on disease name and clinical description, and comparing the differences in risk perception. We hypothesize that the perceived risk of vaccine-preventable diseases will be higher when individuals are presented with clinical descriptions, when compared to the perceived risk when presented with the disease name. Secondary objectives of this research include examining associations between sociodemographic characteristics and the perception that vaccine-preventable diseases present low risk to unvaccinated individuals, as well as examining associations between sociodemographic characteristics and perceiving a disease to present lower risk based on the disease name when compared to perceived risk based on the clinical description of a disease.

Methods

Study design and population

We developed a survey questionnaire to characterize the perceived risk posed by vaccine-preventable diseases based on the name of the disease as compared to the clinical description of the disease. We chose to focus on six vaccine-preventable diseases—diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, measles, mumps, and polio—that were chosen because of their prominence in public discourse.

Participants were first presented with the names of the six diseases and asked to characterize the risk that each disease posed to an unvaccinated child in the United States using a five-point Likert scale (i.e., (1) Very low risk, (2) Low risk, (3) Neither high nor low risk, (4) High risk, and (5) Very high risk). Participants were then presented with clinical descriptions of six diseases and asked to characterize the risk that each disease posed to an unvaccinated child in the United States using the same five-point Likert scale. The clinical descriptions specified that the diseases were vaccine-preventable, but did not specify if the disease described was one of the previous diseases queried on. Clinical descriptions were developed using materials produced by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, primarily relying on vaccine information statements available for each vaccine (U.S. CDC, 2025a; U.S. CDC, 2025b; U.S. CDC, 2025c; U.S. CDC, 2026a; U.S. CDC, 2026b). All clinical descriptions were four or five sentences in length, less than 100 words long, and began with the same statement clarifying that the disease being described was vaccine-preventable.

Cint was used to recruit study participants. Cint sources participants using a double-opt-in procedure whereby individuals must first opt into the platform and then into specific surveys. Cint incentivizes participation in online surveys using a points-based system that awards points to survey participants. Eligibility criteria for this study included being 18 years of age or older, residing in the United States, and being the parent or legal guardian of at least one child five years of age or younger. Surveys were administered in English using Qualtrics, and data were collected from August 20–September 9, 2025.

Measures

Our primary outcome measure was the perceived risk of each vaccine-preventable disease. We also collected sociodemographic data on participant sex, age, race, ethnicity, educational attainment, annual household income, vaccine hesitancy, partisan identity, and state of residence. A validated survey tool was used to quantify vaccine hesitancy (Larson et al., 2015). The tool included a series of 10 statements about vaccines, and participants were asked to quantify their agreement using a five-point Likert scale, where higher scores corresponded to higher levels of vaccine hesitancy. Survey questions used in this research, including clinical descriptions, are available in a supplemental file (Appendix A).

Statistical analysis

To aid in data analysis, several sociodemographic variables were collapsed, including race, partisan identity, and vaccine hesitancy. Race was collapsed by combining American Indian or Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and other into a single category, resulting in four racial categories: Asian, Black or African American, White, and other. Partisan identity was collapsed by combining strong Democrat and Democrat into a single category, strong Republican and Republican into a single category, and Independent (lean Democrat), Independent, and Independent (lean Republican) into a single category, resulting in three partisan identity categories: Democrat, Independent, and Republican. Vaccine hesitancy was dichotomized by calculating the mean score across the 10 vaccine-hesitancy questions before categorized participants as being vaccine-hesitant (VHIS >3.0) or not vaccine-hesitant (VHIS ≤3.0). The vaccine hesitancy scale was determined to be highly reliable (Cronbach's α = 0.86). Standard U.S. Census Bureau definitions were used to condense residence into geographic regions.

Descriptive statistics were calculated to describe the study population and the perceived risk posed by vaccine-preventable diseases. Non-parametric Wilcoxon matched-pair tests were used to compare differences in risk perception based on disease name and clinical description. Multiple ordered logistic regression models were used in exploratory analyses that examined associations between parental sociodemographic characteristics and risk perceptions based on disease name and clinical descriptions. A multiple logistic regression model was used to examine associations between parental sociodemographic characteristics and lower risk perception based on name, when compared to clinical description. Multiple regression models simultaneously adjusted for participant sex, age, race, ethnicity, education, income, vaccine hesitancy, partisan identity, and geography. Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed prior to constructing multiple regression models.

Sensitivity analyses included using multiple logistic regression models to examine associations between sociodemographic characteristics and low risk perception based on disease name and clinical description. Participants who completed the survey in less than 240 s, those who failed attention check questions included in the survey, and those who provided incomplete data were excluded from data analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using StataSE/v19.

Ethical review

Prior to initiating recruitment and data collection, all study materials were submitted to the Texas A&M University Institutional Review Board, which declared the study exempt (STUDY2025–0819).

Results

Regression analysis

Several sociodemographic variables—sex, age, race, education, income, vaccine hesitancy, and partisan identity—were associated with risk perception based on disease name (Table 4). Compared to men, women had significantly higher odds of perceiving diphtheria and pertussis to pose a higher risk based on name. Compared to participants who were 18–24 years of age, those who were 25–34 years had significantly higher odds of perceiving mumps and polio to pose a higher risk based on name. Compared to White participants, participants of other races had significantly higher odds of perceiving pertussis to pose a higher risk based on name. Compared to those with a high school diploma or less, participants with a technical certification or associate's degree, or a graduate degree had significantly lower odds of perceiving polio to pose a higher risk based on name. Compared to participants with an annual household income of less than $50,000, those with an income of $200,000 or greater had significantly higher odds of perceiving measles and polio to pose a higher risk based on name. Those who were vaccine-hesitant had significantly lower odds of perceiving all included diseases to pose a higher risk based on disease name, when compared to those who were not vaccine-hesitant. Compared to Democrats, Independents had significantly lower odds of perceiving measles and mumps to pose a higher risk based on disease name; similarly, Republicans were found to have significantly lower odds of perceiving tetanus and mumps to pose a higher risk based on disease name. We also sought to understand the predictors of holding a lower perceived disease risk based on disease name as compared to disease description. In that analysis, sex, age, race, education, and partisan identity were all significantly associated with rating the risk lower when presented with a disease name (Table 6). Compared to men, women had significantly lower odds of rating the risk lower for pertussis. Compared to those 24 years of age or younger, participants who were 25–44 years and 55–64 years had significantly lower odds of rating the risk lower for mumps. Discussion

This study sought to describe the perceived risk of vaccine-preventable diseases, compare differences in risk perception based on disease name and clinical description, and examine associations between sociodemographic characteristics and low risk perception, as well as rating risk lower when presented with the name of a disease. Results demonstrated that participants most often assessed all diseases as posing a high risk based on disease name and clinical description, that several sociodemographic characteristics were associated with characterizing vaccine-preventable diseases as low-risk based on name and clinical description, that participants consistently rated the risk posed by a disease as lower when presented with only the name of the disease, and that sex, age, race, education, and partisan identity were all significantly associated with rating risk lower based on the name of a vaccine-preventable disease.

This research has important implications for the study of vaccine hesitancy in the United States. First, our findings demonstrate that despite recent declines in childhood immunization rates and the spillover of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy toward general vaccine hesitancy (Lunz Trujillo et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2025), most parents in our sample still considered vaccine-preventable diseases to pose a high risk to unvaccinated children in the United States. This suggests that a majority of parents continue to recognize the risks posed by vaccine-preventable diseases and the importance of vaccination. Still, our results also suggest that despite this risk assessment, many survey participants do not fully appreciate the clinical significance and risks posed by vaccine-preventable diseases.

Interestingly, while our results generally suggest that clinical descriptions lead to higher perceptions of risk than disease names, measles serves as a notable exception. Participants perceived a higher risk from the name ‘measles’ than from the clinical description provided, which highlighted that the disease causes fever, cough, runny nose, and a rash that covers the whole body, among other more severe symptoms that occur in a subset of cases. We suspect that this unique pattern of results could be driven by the measles outbreak that occurred in parts of the United States in 2025, prompting considerable media attention and raising the salience of measles as a disease. Alternatively, this result could reflect less public concern about the symptoms of measles. Investigating the reason for this distinct pattern of results represents an important direction for future research.

Our findings have important implications for public health practice and research, more broadly. Regarding public health practice, while the causes of vaccine hesitancy are complex, context-specific, and dynamic (Tuckerman et al., 2022), it appears that providing further education on the clinical realities of these diseases could improve accurate risk perception for many parents. Interventions based on “knowledge-deficit” approaches may be unlikely to address hesitancy in isolation, but at a minimum, could lead to improved vaccination coverage (Sadaf et al., 2013; Dubé et al., 2015; Kaufman et al., 2018). Further, these interventions may be relatively more successful in addressing vaccine hesitancy if the materials and communications are evidence-based, appropriately tailored to specific contexts, and effectively engage community leaders (Tuckerman et al., 2022).

It is important to recognize that this study is subject to several limitations, many of which are common to online surveys. These include our use of self-reported data, which may introduce social desirability bias, as well as our use of double opt-in procedures, which can introduce non-response bias. Additionally, the lack of robust comparative sociodemographic benchmarks for parents with children under five years of age in the United States limits opportunities for weighting or quota sampling. These data and results are also cross-sectional in nature. Accordingly, they should not be interpreted as causal and maintain limited temporal validity, as attitudes, risk perceptions, and vaccine propensity are dynamic and subject to change (Justwan et al., 2019; Baumgaertner et al., 2020).

Notably, study participants were first shown disease names and then clinical descriptions of the disease without rotation. This fixed ordering could subject the analysis to confounding due to order, priming, and learning. Future research should assess whether these results remain robust to alternative specifications that randomize the order in which information is presented. In addition, it is important to acknowledge that when rating risk in our survey, participants could interpret this in different ways, including the probability of infection, the probability of severe disease, or risk to the broader public. Future research would benefit from additional analyses that disentangle risk conceptualizations, with particular emphasis on analyses differentiating perceived susceptibility from severity.

Despite these limitations, our analysis still represents an important step forward in our understanding of vaccine-preventable disease risk perception. Future research should move beyond the exploration of risk perception to examine whether presenting individuals with clinical information about vaccine-preventable diseases increases vaccine intention and uptake. In addition, future research should explore whether our findings are robust to other types of vaccines, for example, those given to adolescents (e.g., HPV) or in adulthood (e.g., shingles). Finally, additional research may also analyze whether moving beyond clinical descriptions to other depictions of disease, such as images or videos of those afflicted by vaccine-preventable diseases, has different impacts on perceptions of risk.

Conclusions

Parents remain concerned about the risk posed by various diseases for children who are unvaccinated, but that risk is perceived to be higher when presented with clinical descriptions of diseases instead of disease names. Improving the understanding of parents in the United States, and the public more broadly, about the potential consequences of vaccine-preventable diseases could represent a practical method for improving immunization coverage and addressing vaccine hesitancy.
