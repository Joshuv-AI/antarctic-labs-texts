Transformation of a Medical Information Department to Meet the New Healthcare Realities of the Post-Pandemic Era
Authors: Almudena Rovirosa, Sara Jericó, Ana Muñiz, Ana Pérez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The role of Medical Information in the pharmaceutical industry is undergoing a profound transformation, driven by evolving stakeholder expectations, digital innovation, and the increasing complexity of therapeutic landscapes. Traditionally perceived as a reactive function focused on providing accurate and timely responses to healthcare professionals (HCPs), AstraZeneca’s Medical Information department has now evolved into a proactive, strategic partner within Medical Affairs (MA). This shift has been accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, which amplified the demand for rapid, accessible, and reliable medical information among HCPs, regulatory agencies, patients, and caregivers. This article describes the transformation of AstraZeneca’s Medical Information department, highlighting the integration of advanced digital platforms, artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled tools, and self-service channels that enhance the efficiency, consistency, and accessibility of responses. Initiatives, such as the medical information leaders in Europe centralized hub and the Medical Information website, exemplify how technology enables the real-time, multi-channel delivery of evidence-based content. In parallel, Medical Information processes have been standardized to ensure regulatory compliance, uphold ethical standards, and maintain the quality and personalization of interactions. Beyond information provision, Medical Information now functions as an analytical hub, systematically capturing, synthesizing, and interpreting insights from medical inquiries and other MA touchpoints. These insights inform the refinement of core medical messaging, support evidence generation priorities, and identify knowledge gaps or misconceptions within the healthcare community. The department’s cross-functional collaboration with Regulatory Affairs, Pharmacovigilance, Market Access, and Commercial ensures that global scientific content is adapted to local healthcare realities, strengthening both internal alignment and external engagement. Key performance indicators (KPIs) have been implemented to measure Medical Information’s impact, including enquiry volume trends (related to the number of requests), digital adoption rates (such as the volume of interactions per digital channel), stakeholder satisfaction (measured through qualitative feedback collected in surveys), and insight utilization (i.e., number and impact of generated insights). Such metrics enable continuous performance monitoring and facilitate data-driven decision making. Looking ahead, the Medical Information function is poised to further integrate real-world evidence, expand multi-channel communication capabilities, and deepen its role as a “listening post” for unmet needs and patient-centric innovation. By combining operational agility, technological innovation, and scientific expertise, Medical Information can continue to enhance informed decision making, improve patient outcomes, and deliver sustained value to the healthcare ecosystem.

Introduction

Medical Information departments play a pivotal role in ensuring that healthcare professionals (HCPs) and other stakeholders have access to accurate, timely, and evidence-based information about medicines [1]. This function serves as a bridge between the pharmaceutical industry and the clinical community, supporting the safe and effective use of medicines through the dissemination of non-promotional, scientific responses [2]. They also provide an additional entry point for the detection of adverse events or product quality complaints reported or detected in the medical information request (MIR). Medical Information departments played a key role during the COVID-19 global health crisis, since all healthcare stakeholders turned to them for fast delivery of accurate and updated information, both at the beginning and as the situation evolved [3]. In Europe, pharmaceutical companies have a legal requirement to provide non-promotional Medical Information services relating to their medicines and devices, adhering to a robust regulatory and ethical framework [4], such as EU Directive 2001/83/EC [5], Good Pharmacovigilance Practices [6], and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) standards [7].

Recent analyses highlight a shift in expectations not only from HCPs, but also from patients and caregivers, who are increasingly seeking reliable, understandable, and timely medical information [8]. This expansion of audience calls for new approaches in how information is delivered, regulated, and integrated into healthcare systems. Accordingly, the role of Medical Information within the pharmaceutical industry is undergoing a profound transformation [9]. Beyond regulatory compliance, the ethical obligation of Medical Information is to support informed clinical decision making and improve health outcomes through access to accurate and up-to-date medical information. When responding to patients and caregivers, this typically means providing approved patient-oriented product information (e.g., the patient information leaflet) in a clear, accessible manner, while ensuring that all content remains informative, non-promotional, scientifically balanced, and aligned with the latest regulatory expectations [8]. At the same time, aggregated insights from medical inquiries can help inform broader disease awareness or educational initiatives led by other functions, without expanding Medical Information beyond its compliant remit.

The evolution of Medical Information aligns with broader changes in Medical Affairs (MA) functions, where companies are placing increasing emphasis on high-quality, patient-centric services that extend beyond traditional information delivery to encompass digital access, multi-channel engagement, and customized content for diverse stakeholders [10, 11]. In a previous publication, we described the transformation of AstraZeneca’s (AZ) MA department to align with the MA 2030 vision [12]. Historically, MA impact was often communicated mainly through activity outputs, with limited systematic capture and use of field insights beyond documenting stakeholder feedback. In contrast, the MA 2030 vision reframes MA as an impact-oriented function, using end-to-end data/analytics and omnichannel engagement to generate structured, actionable insights from multiple touchpoints and feed them back into medical strategy and evidence planning—thereby enabling measurable improvements in engagement quality, decision-making support, and patient-relevant outcomes [13, 14].

The objective of this article is to describe the ongoing transformation of the Medical Information department in alignment with the broader strategic evolution of MA, as envisioned in the MA 2030 framework. Drawing on the experience of AZ Spain and contextualized within the European regulatory and healthcare landscape, this article will explore how Medical Information is adapting to emerging challenges and opportunities, from digital innovation and automation to new stakeholder needs and ethical imperatives. In doing so, it aims to provide a forward-looking analysis of the future role of Medical Information and its critical contribution to the pharmaceutical value chain.

The Transformation of the Medical Information Department at AZ Spain

The Medical Information function in the pharmaceutical industry is undergoing a profound transformation originally triggered by the COVID-19 pandemic, but also driven by other factors, such as changes in the way HCPs consume medical content, the increasing complexity of therapies, and the diversification of stakeholders. To meet these new trends, our Medical Information department has evolved into a strategic partner (Fig. 1), contributing to knowledge dissemination, scientific engagement, and healthcare decision making. To illustrate this evolution, during the first 3 months of the COVID-19 vaccination period, our Medical Information team handled ~ 500 vaccine-related inquiries, many addressing unmet needs such as special populations, stability, and temperature excursions. Strategic Role of Medical Information Within the Company

Medical Information interacts with virtually all departments of the company (including Compliance, Legal, Pharmacovigilance, Quality and Commercial) to address internal information requests, but the structure of the Medical Information department is now more closely aligned with other MA and operative functions, such as Evidence Generation, Transforming Care & Medical Excellence, Regulatory Affairs, Medical Ethics, and Corporate Affairs & Market Access. Medical Information was integrated into cross-functional teams within each therapeutic area (TA), facilitating a more cohesive, efficient, and targeted approach to information delivery and content development, while ensuring consistency across channels and alignment with broader scientific and strategic objectives [15].

Medical Information professionals are increasingly participating in Brand Teams and Launch Teams, contributing critical insights on data gaps, customer questions, and information needs early in the development cycle [16]. This early alignment ensures that product communication strategies are grounded in real-world evidence (RWE) and scientific integrity. The new roles of Medical Information within MA have also enhanced the strategic value of field-based interactions. Through strategic platforms, Medical Information content is now accessible in real-time during medical visits, allowing Strategic Scientific Advisors (SSAs) to provide data-driven and on-demand answers tailored to specific HCP needs. This supports the positioning of SSAs as scientific partners, improving the credibility and value of their interactions [17].

Beyond timely responses, Medical Information in AZ has evolved into a strategic analytical function, systematically capturing and interpreting insights from MIRs and triangulating them with feedback from other MA activities, such as scientific engagements, advisory boards, and educational events. These insights reveal unmet needs, data gaps, and misconceptions among HCPs, guiding the refinement of core messaging, targeted educational resources, and evidence generation priorities. Crucially, Medical Information’s strength lies in understanding the drivers behind the questions, enabling adaptation of global content to local realities and fostering strategic alignment. By integrating and systematizing this intelligence, Medical Information informs company-wide scientific strategy, identifying persistent knowledge gaps, emerging therapeutic trends, and evolving stakeholder concerns. Close collaboration with Compliance, Medical Ethics, Regulatory Affairs, Commercial, and external healthcare partners strengthens scientific credibility, supports transparent communication, and reinforces the commitment to patient safety and informed decision making throughout the product lifecycle.

Novel Tools for Responding to Inquiries and Beyond

Embracing new technologies and digital tools has been key for the transformation of the Medical Information department, driven by the implementation of standardized processes and the adoption of innovative platforms. Central to this shift was the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and advanced digital tools, such as omnichannel platforms, self-service portals, and AI-powered chatbots. These technologies improve accessibility and user experience for HCPs, while enhancing efficiency and traceability. The management of unsolicited MIRs has become more agile, accurate, and traceable thanks to the use of these tools, supporting content retrieval, creation, and delivery.

Our Medical Information website (https://medicalinformation.astrazeneca.com/) offers a self-service channel where HCPs can access scientific and product-related information 24/7, providing them with accurate, unbiased, AZ scientific medical information when, where, and how they need it. It integrates search functionalities, scientific response documents (SRDs), prescribing information pages (additional to the link provided on the home page to Local Prescribing Information), and channels for submitting medical inquiries, ensuring compliance with regulatory and privacy standards. The platform also offers optional features such as disease education pages, field medical contact requests, or feedback surveys, tailored to market needs.

In addition to this website, the Medical Information Leaders in Europe (MILE) centralized hub (migateway) facilitates seamless access to Medical Information across European markets. MILE promotes harmonization while allowing for market-level adaptation, aligned with the broader governance model for Medical Information services in Europe [18]. Another key tool for Medical Information is our Global Medical Information Platform (GMIP), which functions as a centralized system for the documentation and management of MIRs originating from both internal and external requesters. It also streamlines the referral process for adverse event reports and product quality complaints by routing them to the appropriate departments. Furthermore, GMIP integrates with Veeva Vault MedComms, the medical content repository for SRDs, medical letters, scientific papers, congress materials and frequently asked questions (FAQ), to link to and retrieve the materials used to respond to MIRs [19]. This integration enables efficient retrieval and use of approved and copyrighted content to support responses delivered via multiple channels (email and interactive email, fax, mail and telephone).

One of the major advances in content management has been the integration of AI and advanced response systems. The adoption of digital interactive and user-friendly formats for standard responses has enhanced the experience for HCPs. Instead of lengthy PDF documents or generic e-mails, responses are now provided in digital formats that are easier to navigate, visualize, and search, reducing the time required to locate specific information and improving overall satisfaction. Content is stored in centralized repositories such as GMIP and Veeva Vault MedComms, from which it can be distributed to downstream channels using chatbots and digital platforms.

Artificial Intelligence in Medical Information (AIMI) is a chatbot that enables the fast and scalable delivery of real-time answers to oncology-related inquiries via AI-driven conversations, increasing reach, reducing response times, and enhancing user experience. Currently, its expansion to additional markets and TAs is underway. Complementing these capabilities, platforms such as OPTIC and Docuvera support both content retrieval at the point of need and consistent, compliant content generation. OPTIC enables field medical representatives to access and deliver real-time medical information to healthcare professionals during in-person or virtual visits, enhancing the value of interactions and ensuring consistency of messaging. Docuvera is an AI-powered structured content platform that improves efficiency, content reuse, and compliance across labelling, clinical, medical, pharmacovigilance, and quality documentation. Within Medical Information, it serves as a structured source document by assembling pre-approved content blocks into an approved base response, from which interactive and channel-adapted digital materials can then be generated. This approach supports scientific accuracy, version control, and consistency across channels and TAs.

Overall, the new set of platforms and processes illustrates a shift from a traditional inquiry model to an integrated and strategic Medical Information function, aligned with the MA 2030 vision. By leveraging technology and intelligent content management, our Medical Information department is better equipped to deliver timely, personalized, and high-quality scientific information that supports healthcare professionals in making informed clinical decisions.

Added Value to the Healthcare Ecosystem

The Medical Information department plays an increasingly strategic role in bridging internal knowledge and external healthcare needs. Although Medical Information remains a primarily reactive function externally—responding to unsolicited inquiries from HCPs and, where applicable, patients and caregivers—the transformation described in this manuscript has materially changed how Medical Information delivers value. Specifically, new integrated systems and digital channels have improved the quality, consistency, and timeliness of responses and enabled more immediate, scalable access to validated medical content through self-service and point-of-care tools (e.g., OPTIC for field interactions and the Medical Information website). As the pharmaceutical landscape evolves toward a more patient-centered, evidence-driven paradigm, Medical Information functions as both a knowledge hub and a key interface between the company and the healthcare ecosystem. The contribution of Medical Information at AZ therefore extends beyond providing accurate and timely information, it supports clinicians, pharmacists, nurses, and other HCPs with robust, current, and relevant data to guide therapeutic decision making, while also translating recurring external questions into actionable internal insights to improve educational materials and scientific planning.

To improve health outcomes, it is essential to establish trusted communication with external stakeholders, including national competent authorities, EMA and other regulatory agencies, clinical research organizations, HCPs, and increasingly patients and caregivers. Direct Medical Information engagement with regulators is typically national and may intensify in exceptional circumstances; for example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, health authorities requested vaccine manufacturers to provide dedicated Medical Information channels and digital resources to support HCPs and the public and to help manage the surge in information needs. In addition, contract research organizations and clinical trial sites may contact Medical Information for product information queries relevant to study conduct, and Medical Information channels may also capture safety information (e.g., adverse event reporting) that is routed through established pharmacovigilance processes. Overall, these interactions support the safe and effective use of medicines, enabling the company to adapt to local clinical realities and respond rapidly to unmet needs.

The department’s embeddedness in cross-functional teams also allows it to inform the company’s positioning on emerging healthcare trends. Whether responding to increasing demand for RWE, supporting the implementation of digital health solutions, or anticipating regulatory changes, Medical Information is uniquely placed to contribute both data and contextual knowledge. In practice, this contribution can be evidenced through tracked indicators such as the volume and thematic distribution of trend-related inquiries (e.g., RWE-focused questions), the proportion of responses incorporating RWE sources, and the number of evidence gaps or recurring themes escalated to MA or other departments’ action (Table 1). These insight-to-action outputs have supported positioning deliverables such as updated scientific response content and FAQ, refinements of scientific narratives and field materials, and faster internal dissemination of regulatory updates that require aligned external communication. This evolving role underscores the importance of embedding Medical Information as a strategic pillar within MA and within the broader mission of delivering value to healthcare systems, ultimately benefiting patients.

Integration of Quantitative/Qualitative Metrics

The transformation of our Medical Information department was supported by a data-driven approach that integrated both quantitative and qualitative metrics to assess performance, guide strategic decisions, and ultimately enhance the value delivered to HCPs and the broader healthcare ecosystem. This integration is enabled through the systematic collection and analysis of data from multiple platforms, such as the Medical Information website, GMIP, the OPTIC platform, and other digital tools, which not only facilitate access to reliable and timely medical information but also capture relevant metadata on inquiry volume, response time, digital engagement, and user satisfaction.

Key performance indicators (KPIs) are regularly monitored and categorized by TA, type of stakeholder, and channel of interaction. Metrics include the total number of MIRs, asset usage (such as pre-approved SRDs), escalated inquiries, and response time distribution (Table 1). Interactive dashboards, such as the Medical Information Inquiry Dashboard developed in Power BI, are essential to share metrics and allow real-time visualization and drill-down capability. This enables Medical Information professionals and cross-functional stakeholders to explore data at various levels of granularity (by product, TA, inquiry type, or customer segment), offering transparency and actionable insights. Our functionality dashboard allows comparisons across current and previous quarters, as well as year-on-year analysis, facilitating trend monitoring and performance benchmarking.

Qualitative insights are also integrated into this framework, particularly those derived from escalated inquiries, complex information requests, or patterns in repeated questions. These insights are processed and synthesized by the Medical Information team in collaboration with MA, Regulatory Affairs, and other departments, informing strategic initiatives such as content optimization, educational program design, and the identification of unmet information needs, as described in the previous section. Importantly, these KPIs are not used only for operational oversight, they are shared with MA central teams and SSAs to identify recurring questions and unmet needs, which can then inform prioritization of educational materials and scientific engagement plans. For example, repeated inquiries indicating difficulty using an inhaler or potential misuse can be escalated as an actionable insight to relevant internal stakeholders (including Regulatory Affairs), enabling development of approved instructional materials (e.g., technique videos) to support correct use and improve patient outcomes.

Similarly, frequent requests on off-label use in special populations, potential drug–drug interactions, or practical dose de-escalation approaches can trigger rapid updates to scientific response content, targeted educational initiatives, and, where appropriate, prioritization of evidence-generation activities to address persistent data gaps. Although the examples described here reflect implementation in AZ Spain, the overall approach aligns with broader organizational processes and digital platforms, while acknowledging that harmonization across countries still requires local adaptation to different regulatory and clinical contexts. In this way, KPI monitoring supports both performance management and insight-to-action cycles across the organization.

By transforming metrics into intelligence, the Medical Information department not only improves internal efficiency but also contributes to a favorable external impact. Observed trends in information demand can signal emerging clinical or scientific concerns or recurring practical challenges for HCPs, which can be translated into targeted, accessible resources. For example, repeated requests about the administration of a new medicine with a complex dosing regimen led to the rapid development of a concise visual infographic summarizing the administration schedule. This resource was then made available through OPTIC and the Medical Information website and was also shared with SSA teams to support consistent, high-quality scientific interactions with HCPs. Moreover, user feedback and satisfaction scores guide improvements in the digital platforms and services provided. Overall, this metrics integration positions the Medical Information department as a strategic function capable of generating value beyond compliance, supporting continuous improvement, facilitating personalized and responsive information delivery, and aligning the company’s scientific communication with evolving stakeholder needs.

Discussion

The transformation of Medical Information services in the pharmaceutical industry has been accelerated by the evolving healthcare landscape, particularly after the COVID-19 pandemic [20]. With growing regulatory demands and reduced in-person interactions, accurate, accessible, and timely medical information became essential for HCPs, regulatory agencies, patients, and caregivers [3]. MA departments rapidly adopted digital platforms, self-service tools, and remote communication to ensure continuity of information [21]. The crisis triggered a lasting digital transformation, with inquiry volumes and complexity surging, especially on COVID-19 treatment and prevention, vaccine safety, and clinical guidance. Medical Information teams had to respond in near-real time, adapting to evolving data and regulations. The pandemic revealed the limits of manual, non-integrated systems, highlighting the need for automation, interoperability, and agility. It also reinforced MI’s role in cross-functional collaboration, as Regulatory Affairs, Pharmacovigilance, and MA increasingly relied on Medical Information insights to address stakeholder concerns, detect safety signals, and strengthen communication strategies when necessary.

Despite the advancements described in this paper on the transformation of the Medical Information function in AZ Spain, we continue to face challenges that are common across multinational organizations operating within global frameworks. A primary concern is the harmonization of medical information processes across countries and TAs, especially in international companies like AZ operating within a global framework. Variability in regulatory requirements, language nuances, and local HCP preferences complicate the implementation of standardized practices. While international platforms such as GMIP streamline content management and version control, aligning medical content across markets still requires meticulous oversight and cross-functional alignment. To address this, global medical information teams increasingly develop core, largely label-agnostic SRDs focused on disease background, mechanism of action, and key efficacy and safety data, which local affiliates then adapt to their national labelling and regulatory environment, clearly distinguishing any off-label information when necessary. This model, supported by global reference content packages, promotes consistency and efficiency while providing enough flexibility to accommodate country-specific requirements and practice patterns.

Another critical challenge is striking a balance between regulatory compliance and the implementation of emerging technologies such as AI. While AI holds significant promise in improving the speed, consistency, and scalability of Medical Information services through tools like chatbots, automated response generation, and insight mining, it must be deployed with caution and within the stringent boundaries of current regulatory frameworks. For instance, the EU Directive 2001/83/EC mandates that all medicinal product information must be non-promotional, scientifically accurate, and tailored to the stakeholder's query [5]. AI-driven systems must therefore be trained and validated carefully to ensure compliance, and their outputs must always be subject to human verification by trained Medical Information professionals before being shared externally. This demands close collaboration between MI, IT, Regulatory Affairs, and Legal to ensure proper training, validation, and oversight. As digital tools continue to expand, transparency, data privacy, and quality assurance become critical. Automation should never compromise quality or personalization, as HCPs value expert interpretation of complex data tailored to patient contexts. Maintaining this balance is vital to preserving trust, neutrality, and patient benefit.

The future of Medical Information will be defined by its evolution into a dynamic, multichannel communication hub capable of addressing increasingly complex therapies and diverse stakeholder needs. Incorporating RWE and advanced analytics into Medical Information systems will enable the extraction of actionable insights, supporting evidence generation, market shaping, and policy engagement. Acting as a “listening post,” Medical Information can identify recurring MIRs, unmet needs, and emerging concerns to guide medical education, clinical development, and research priorities. Greater integration with MA and other strategic departments, through early involvement in product development, cross-functional collaboration during launches, and structured feedback loops, will position Medical Information as a proactive contributor to innovation and value creation across the healthcare ecosystem, rather than solely as a reactive information provider.

Conclusion

The post-COVID evolution of Medical Information represents both a challenge and an opportunity. Departments that embrace digitalization, foster cross-functional collaboration, and maintain a relentless focus on quality, compliance, and patient-centricity will be best positioned to deliver scientific value in an increasingly complex and competitive landscape. Importantly, by converting inquiry patterns into actionable insights shared with Medical Affairs teams, the transformed Medical Information model supports earlier prioritization of educational content and more consistent scientific engagement, strengthening the organization’s ability to respond to stakeholder needs with greater agility. As demonstrated in this manuscript, the transformation of Medical Information within AZ illustrates the path forward: one that balances technological innovation with human expertise, while staying rooted in the company’s broader MA 2030 vision.
