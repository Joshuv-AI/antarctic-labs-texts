Magnetic resonance imaging in the diagnosis of periodontal and periapical disease
Authors: Katrine Mølgaard Johannsen, João Marcus de Carvalho E Silva Fuglsig, Louise Hauge Matzen, Jennifer Christensen, Rubens Spin-Neto
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Early pre-clinical inflammatory changes in periodontal and/or periapical lesions, which typically precede bone loss, are challenging to diagnose using ionizing-radiation-based imaging modalities. MRI provides relevant additional diagnostic information of inflammatory processes in soft and hard tissues. The aim of the present study is to undertake a systematic review of the literature on MRI in the diagnosis of periodontal and/or periapical disease.Methods and Materials:The PubMed/MEDLINE and Scopus bibliographic databases were searched (2000–2021) using the search string: (“MRI” or “magnetic resonance imaging”) and (“periodontitis” or “periodontal” or “apical pathology” or “endodontic pathology” or “periapical” or “furcation” or “intrabony”). The search was limited to studies published in English. The studies were assessed independently by three reviewers, focusing on the MRI sequences, imaging modalities (radiographs, cone beam CT (CBCT), and MRI), disease definition, assessed parameters, and outcome measurements.Results:The search strategy yielded 34 studies, from which 13 were included. Overall, the findings of MRI were in agreement with CBCT. The studies showed that MRI provided diagnostic information of the hard and soft tissue components affected by periodontal and/or periapical disease with a fairly high sensitivity and specificity. However, the assessed parameters (e.g. MRI acquisition protocols, and disease definition) differed substantially.Conclusions:The included studies indicate that the use of MRI in the diagnosis of periodontal and/or periapical disease is feasible and promising. More studies are needed to define the accuracy of this non-ionizing-radiation-based diagnostic modality, in the assessment of periodontal and/or periapical lesions.

Introduction

The accurate diagnosis of periodontal and periapical disease depends not only on the systematic clinical evaluation of the patient but also is frequently supplemented by radiographic findings. 1 The relevance of the additional radiographic information relies mostly on the anatomic complexity of the regions being examined and the natural progression of the disease. 2

The diagnosis of periodontal disease depends mainly on clinical signs and symptoms. 3 However, radiographic images are often included in the diagnostic workflow to provide the clinician with additional information. In the dental practice, two-dimensional (2D) periapical and panoramic radiographs are used routinely for assessing the bone levels around the roots and the overall status of the periodontal support tissues. 4,5 Yet, multirooted teeth with significant bone loss in the furcation region (namely furcation involvement, “FI”) represent a major challenge for clinicians. 6 This is related to the fact that standard periodontal examination does not always allow for an accurate diagnosis of FI, as the clinical assessment using a curved periodontal probe is prone to measurement errors due to varying root morphology and/or limited accessibility. 7–10 Furthermore, the selection and prognosis of an appropriate treatment depend largely on the accurate definition (i.e. measurement) of the periodontal interradicular horizontal and vertical bone loss extension. 6,11–13 In these cases, three-dimensional (3D) images (most often cone beam CT (CBCT)) might be needed to assist with diagnosis and treatment plan. 4

The inflammatory processes of apical periodontitis (AP) take place in the root canal system and the periapical tissues, and in many cases proceed without clinical symptoms. However, the inflammatory process can also evolve into an acute condition, in which the patient is in pain and therefore enters the dental clinic, possibly with a periapical abscess. In addition to that, clinicians must often rely on imperfect, indirect tests and the interpretation of 2D periapical radiographic images in the diagnosis of AP. 14

However, the radiographic diagnosis of periodontal and periapical disease is also affected by modality-/observer-related challenges. Conventional radiographs do not allow to adequately depict the complex 3D morphology of the periodontal anatomy. 7–9 Furthermore, the radiographic bone level definition is often affected by overlapping of the surrounding anatomic structures. 4 Ionizing-radiation-based imaging modalities cannot delineate early pre-clinical inflammatory changes in periodontal or periapical disease. These changes typically precede bone loss and are restricted to the soft tissue components of bone and considered reversible. 15,16 The size of periapical lesions is often underestimated in radiographs, 17–19 since substantial bone degeneration (i.e. 30–50% mineral loss) is necessary to identify lesions radiographically. 20,21 The consequence is that the agreement between the radiographic (i.e. based on 2D images) and the histopathological diagnosis of AP ranges from 36% to almost 90%. 22 Even although the latest 3D imaging modality used in dentistry (CBCT) provides higher diagnostic accuracy than 2D images for periodontal and periapical diagnosis. 4,22 it provides a larger radiation dose to the patient, and hence increases the risk of stochastic cancer development in the population. 23 Thus, this limits the application of CBCT for longitudinal monitoring. 16,24

There are other imaging modalities that might offer a non-invasive, ionizing-radiation free alternative when it comes to diagnostic tasks, that also depend on the accurate depiction of the soft tissues. Among those, ultrasound 25–27 and MRI demand special attention, for their potential to provide additional information to the conventional imaging modalities.

MRI generates high tissue contrast and provides very detailed images of soft tissues including the dental pulp, nerves, and gingiva. 28 MRI provides relevant additional diagnostic information of inflammatory processes in soft and hard tissues. However, until now, few reports have been published on the application of MRI in dentistry. This is mainly due to the limited accessibility of MRI for dentists as well as the occurrence of artefacts caused by metallic dental materials (e.g. dental fillings, crowns, wires etc.), which limit the use of MRI in dentistry. However, the introduction of imaging techniques that are faster, less sensitive to metallic dental materials, and optimized regarding resolution, enables the dentist to use MRI to assess, characterize and diagnose periodontal and periapical lesions in a non-invasive, non-ionizing manner. This renders MRI increasingly attractive and a modality that should be reconsidered within dentistry. 16 The aim of the present study was to undertake a systematic review of the literature on MRI in the diagnosis of periodontal and/or periapical disease.

Methods and materials

The PubMed/MEDLINE and Scopus bibliographic databases were searched from January 2000 to December 2021 for studies evaluating the diagnosis of periodontal and/or periapical disease in MRI. The search strategy was limited to studies published in English using the following search string: (MRI or magnetic resonance imaging) and (periodontitis or periodontal or apical pathology or endodontic pathology or periapical or furcation or intrabony). The results of the search were then electronically screened using the online software COVIDENCE and processed by the PRISMA guidelines. This systematic review was not registered to the PROSPERO database. However, the database was searched for other systematic reviews about MRI in the diagnosis of periodontal and periapical disease, and no reviews on the subject were identified.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be included, studies were to be MRI-based with outlined aims of evaluating periodontal and/or periapical lesions. Reviews, systematic reviews, case reports, and solely in vitro studies were excluded during the search.

After removing duplicates, the full extent of the search was screened independently by three observers (KMJ, JMCSF, and RS-N) for final inclusion. The reviewers used a dedicated software (COVIDENCE, app.covidence.org, Australia) in this process. In case of disagreement, the reviewers discussed the study and reached consensus. Additionally, a hand-search was performed based on the reference list of the selected studies to include any that had been missed by the main search.

The included publications were assessed by one reviewer (KMJ) for data extraction (MRI sequences, imaging modalities used, disease definition, assessed parameters, and outcome measurements). In case of uncertainty, the authors (KMJ, and RS-N) discussed the study and reached consensus regarding data extraction.

Finally, a risk of bias assessment was conducted on the included studies to assess the quality and applicability of the studies. This was done by using the QUADAS-2 tool. 29 The risk of bias assessment was performed independently by two reviewers (KMJ, JMCSF). In case of disagreement, three reviewers (KMJ, JMCSF and RS-N) discussed the assessment results and reached consensus.

Results

The search strategy yielded 716 titles in total (194 on PubMed and 522 on Scopus). After the removal of duplicates, the search was reduced to 545 titles. The screening of titles and abstracts yielded 34 potential studies to be included. 13 studies were excluded as they did not have outlined aims of evaluating periodontal and/or periapical lesions. 30–42 Seven case report studies 43–49 and one animal study were excluded. 50

Eventually, 13 studies were included in this review. No studies assessed MRI of periodontal and periapical disease together. Therefore, for the data extraction, the studies were differentiated into studies assessing either periodontal or periapical disease using MRI (seven periodontal 6,10,15,51–54 and six periapical 16,55–59 ). The results indicate that the periodontal findings on MRI were consistent and in agreement with those on CBCT. 6,10 Furthermore, MRI provided diagnostic information of the hard and soft tissue components affected by periapical disease with a high sensitivity and specificity. 6,10,56 In general, the included studies suggest that the use of MRI in the diagnosis of periodontal and/or periapical disease is feasible and promising.

Diagnosis of periodontal disease

Two studies 6,10 found that MRI measurements provide a strong agreement with CBCT in the diagnosis of periodontal disease with and without administration of contrast agents. In the study 10 with administration of contrast agent (Gadoterate meglumine contrast), the authors found no significant difference in the absolute measurement values among different degrees of FI in MRI. They found an MRI FI detection sensitivity of 98% and specificity of 99%. Furthermore, they found a FI classification sensitivity of 89%/84%/100% for FI degree I/II/III respectively. The authors 10 concluded that these findings indicate that the use of MRI is very promising for an accurate, non-invasive assessment and evaluation of maxillary molar FI degree. Thus, MRI may help to improve the diagnosis and treatment of patients with periodontal disease. However, in the study 6 without administration of contrast agents, the authors found a slight underestimation of the absolute horizontal and vertical measurements when MRI was compared to CBCT.

One study 15 found that MRI findings correlated with standard clinical findings. MRI was able to depict intraosseous changes before any osseous defect had occurred. Furthermore, another study 53 found that MRI revealed bone marrow abnormalities in a high percentage (78.5–93.1%) of mandibular regions with periodontal disease. Based on this, the authors concluded that bone marrow edema can be caused by periodontal disease in the mandible. Additionally, one study 52 found a strong agreement between MRI and periapical radiographs, leading the authors to conclude that MRI proved to be a comparable method to periapical radiographs for evaluating the proportion of residual periodontal bone support.

Finally, one study 51 was divided into two parts: in vitro and in vivo. The in vivo outcome measurements demonstrated that the reduction of inflammation and probing depth in gingival tissues after non-surgical periodontal therapy correlates with a decrease of RSI (signal intensity ratios between pre- and post-contrast administration) in T 1 weighted MRI. Additionally, the in vivo results also showed that two distinct types of inflammation occurred in the examined groups of patients. Thus, the authors suggest that MRI provides a new possibility to characterize the type and healing process of periodontal inflammation.

Diagnosis of periapical disease

The studies addressing periapical disease indicate that MRI contributed with additional information of the soft tissue components compared to radiographic methods. 58

Two studies 56,59 evaluated the periapical healing, tissue quantities and the diagnosis of periapical disease in 3D MRI sequences. In the assessment of tissue regeneration, 59 the authors found no statistically significant difference between SI measurements of the normal contralateral teeth and that of the regenerated tissues in the endodontically treated teeth. According to this study, 59 MRI could successfully and non-invasively assess the tissue quantities.

One study 56 found that the MRI-based diagnosis of periapical granulomas and cysts was consistent with that of histopathology for 24 out of 34 cases. The authors 56 found that the reliability between the two radiologist observers was strong for the MRI assessment (κ 0.85), and the same as for the two pathologists in the assessment of the specimens (κ 0.88). However, in 10 cases, there was a disagreement between the opinions of the radiologists and pathologists. The MRI assessments by the two radiologists were compared to the consensus of the histological assessment, considered as a gold-standard for that study. The accuracy of MRI was high with a sensitivity of 94% for both radiologists, and a specificity of 50%, and 63% for the two radiologists respectively. However, the authors observed highly mineralized structures to appear as signal void on MRI and the cortical bone and teeth were only identified based on the surrounding soft tissue, whereas root filling material and the apical foramen could not be identified clearly.

Three additional studies, 16,57,58 compared MRI directly to histopathology and found that the MRI-derived characterizations were consistent with the histological findings. Results from one study 57 indicate that MRI enables a reliable differentiation between periapical cysts and granulomas in vivo. The authors found that lesion identification and consecutive image analysis were possible on MRI, using T 2 weighted fat-saturated and contrast-enhanced fat-saturated T 1 weighted sequences. A high reproducibility was shown for MRI measurements of the lesions.

Additionally, two studies 16,58 compared MRI to CBCT, directly and non-blinded, in the assessment of the visibility of the periapical lesions. According to the authors, 58 CBCT did not allow further characterization of the periapical lesions, whereas MRI could characterize radicular cysts and granulomas in consistency with the histological findings. Furthermore, the authors 16 observed no difference in lesion visibility between MRI and CBCT. Though, they did find that lesion dimensions were slightly overestimated in MRI compared to CBCT. Mineralized tissue components such as teeth and bone were mainly visible with CBCT, and MRI complimented with information of the soft tissue components. According to Geibel et al, 16 MRI provided sufficient diagnostic detail for the assessment of the main structures of interest.

Risk of bias assessment

In summary, the risk of bias assessment resulted in five studies 15,16,54,58,59 with a low risk of introducing bias, seven studies 6,10,51,52,55–57 having a potential risk of bias and one study 53 having a potential high risk of bias during patient selection. Aside from this, there are only small concerns regarding the included patients and settings that do not match the review question in terms of studies with different severity of the target condition and, differential diagnosis (e.g. in the studies addressing periapical disease, where larger lesions are more easily seen than smaller lesions on images).

The conduct or interpretation of the index test in the included studies shows a low risk of bias in six studies, 6,10,15,52,56,57 four studies 16,51,58,59 show a high risk of bias and three studies 53–55 show an unclear risk of bias. Due to variation in test technology/interpretation, which may affect estimates of the diagnostic accuracy of a test, for instance magnetic field strength and sequence parameters, the assessment resulted in minor concerns that the index test in the included studies, its conduct, or interpretation could differ from the review question.

The third domain of the risk of bias assessment, specifically the reference standard, its conduct, or its interpretation, shows a potential high risk of introducing bias in three studies. 16,52,55 Additionally, three studies 51,58,59 show an unclear risk of introducing bias, and seven studies 6,10,15,53,54,56,57 show a low risk. There are no/minor concerns about the applicability of the reference standard, since some studies were using MRI or CBCT as the reference standard and one cannot be sure that they achieved the same outcome due to minor difference in the target condition specified. 29

Finally, the patient flow and timing could potentially have introduced bias in nine of the included studies, 6,10,15,16,52–56 whereas there was a low risk of bias in four of the included studies. 51,57–59

Discussion

In general, the included studies indicate that the use of MRI in the diagnosis of periodontal and/or periapical disease is feasible and promising. The included studies imply that MRI and CBCT are mostly consistent and in agreement regarding the diagnostic information of the hard and soft tissue components affected by periodontal and/or periapical disease. 6,10,17,56,58 However, the studies included have examined a vast array of imaging modalities, and combinations of MRI field strength, Radiofrequency (RF) pulse sequences, not to mention coil designs. Moreover, the studies were not consistently aligned as for the presence of a gold-standard for periodontal and periapical disease definition, and they differed substantially in the assessed outcome parameters. Some authors 6,10 calculated sensitivity and specificity scores, where it is a mere agreement, since they did not use histopathology in their study, and CBCT cannot be claimed as the gold-standard. Finally, the number of observers in the studies differed substantially. This is a relevant factor defining the strength of the results (e.g. results that do not take into consideration interobserver variation are more prone to bias).

Field strength

The field strength correlates with signal-to-noise (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratios (CNR), both relevant parameters defining image quality. The stronger the field the stronger the signal and contrast. 66 Yet, for some diagnostic tasks in medicine, it has been suggested that the advantage in contrast-to-noise ratio for 1.5T over 0.5T is less than 20%. However, this also depends on tissue types and pulse sequences. 67 The included studies in this systematic review tested field strengths varying from 1.0 to 3.0T, and more than 20 different pulse sequences, mostly T 1 weighted, were tested. One could speculate on how the magnetic field has affected the results in the included studies. When keeping in mind that the studies were not consistently aligned (e.g. in relation to disease definition or RF pulse sequences), it is interesting to consider that when comparing two studies 56,57 that both assessed periapical pathology (i.e. granulomas and cysts) but used different magnetic field strengths of 1.5T and 3.0T respectively, both of the studies concluded that MRI could be used reliably in the differentiation between periapical cysts and granulomas in vivo. Furthermore, the authors of one of the studies 56 concluded that the reliability of MRI is comparable to the reliability of histopathology, when comparing the MRI observations of two radiologists to the consensus of the histological assessment.

Pulse sequences

Accurate presentation of highly mineralized structures (e.g. enamel, dentine, and bone) is crucial in diagnostic imaging in dentistry. 37,43 Currently, no dedicated solution exists within MRI to provide images of all significant orofacial tissues. In recent years, alternative MRI protocols have been developed and shown to be able, at least to some extent, to present calcified tissues such as bone, joints, and even teeth. 37 Nevertheless, it is also recognized that the accuracy of MRI is highly dependent on the standard of the equipment and the experience of the observer. 68 Furthermore, as one study indicates, 6 the sensitivity of MRI may be reduced for exceptionally narrow furcations due to the lower spatial resolution of MRI compared to CBCT.

To improve the diagnostic process in relation to MRI, some studies adjusted the sequences by using fat saturation or fat suppression. 6,10,52,54,56,57 According to the literature, fat saturation/fat suppression facilitates the differentiation between fat in bone marrow and a lesion. Conversely, sometimes fatty tissues can be confused with the high signal of contrast agents or water concentrate on fat saturation-sequences and impair the diagnostic yield. 37 In relation to that, some authors 56 suggest that the application of multicontrast MRI may lead to better characterization of apical lesions, thus enabling an improved patient specific selection of the optimal treatment option. Multicontrast MRI provides great contrast information, though at the expense of longer acquisition time, which limits the application of this method in the dental clinic. 69 Another alternative is the Sweep imaging Fourier transformation (SWIFT). When used in association with an intraoral coil, SWIFT has shown to be a promising technique for dental-related MRI. 37,61

Furthermore, several of the included studies use the administration of intravenous contrast agents 10,51,52,57 in the attempt to improve the contrast information. However, one can question whether this is feasible in dentistry and routine clinical use.

Coils

A high SNR is preferable in MRI. However, SNR is not only dependent on the magnetic field strength but several additional parameters such as slice thickness, field of view, scan parameters (e.g. repetition time, echo time, flip angle) and selection of coil and coil elements. 70 The included studies in this systematic review used different coil designs, and one can only wonder how this could influence the results and quality of MRI.

In general, a coil should be as close as possible to the tissue/anatomy being imaged. The distance between the coil and the tissue under examination affects the resulting signal sensitivity. Furthermore, the intense signals produced by the cheek and the buccal fat potentially dominate the MR image when using an extraoral coil. 71,72 To achieve higher resolution with reasonable scan times, smaller and more localized RF coils have been designed. However, these are often not compatible with the dental anatomy, 71 as they are mainly extraoral and/or coil arrays. 31,33,73 To address this limitation, and additionally increase SNR and image resolution, several designs of intraoral coils (e.g. loop coils) have been proposed. 37,72,74 Some limitations of these designs were, however, reduced patient comfort (no tongue movement), reduced MR signal from apices of the anterior teeth due to other anatomical limitations, reduced operator ease (e.g. adjusting the coil for each patient is challenging as it cannot be modified), 37,72,74 decreased penetration depth and a heterogenic sensitivity over the teeth and surrounding tissues. 71

One study 37 concluded, based on a preliminary experience, that the MRI quality in vivo is not limited by SNR. Instead, according to the authors, it depends highly on coil configuration, patient comfort, and motion control, parameters also independent of field strength. Furthermore, the authors believed this aspect to be well addressed with future research at lower field strengths. 37,75

Artefacts

MRI can be affected by the presence of metal and other materials, which may cause inhomogeneity in the magnetic field and interfere with the image quality and eventually the diagnostic yield. 65,76 One study 15 found that MRI findings correlated with standard clinical findings. Yet, the authors also reported poor image quality, mostly due to susceptibility or motion artefacts caused by dental restorations and patient movement. Ultimately 178 out of 1179 teeth had to be excluded due to susceptibility artefacts, a total percentage of 15% of the sample. The authors state that although individual teeth may not have been possible to evaluate in MRI, entire patients very rarely turn out to be unsuitable for MRI. According to the literature, susceptibility artefacts in MRI are less severe in units using a lower field strength (i.e. 1T and lower) compared to units using a higher field strength. One interesting and relevant aspect of MRI to investigate further is how artefacts behave in MRI units using lower field strength, and furthermore, what the diagnostic relevance is for these artefacts in relation to applications. 77 Being able to identify the artefacts and knowing how they can be minimized or avoided will improve the diagnostic value of MRI.

Limitations of MRI

Although MRI does not emit the ionizing radiation that is found in X-ray and CBCT imaging, it does employ a strong magnetic field. The magnetic field extends beyond the machine and exerts very powerful forces on objects of iron, some steel alloys, and other magnetizable objects. 66 It is strong enough to fling a wheelchair across the room. 66

A relevant aspect of MRI is that the patients should notify their physicians of any form of medical or other implant prior to an MR scan. When identifying patients who are not eligible for MRI, some factors must be taken into consideration. 37,66 For example, patients with implants, particularly those containing iron, must be carefully evaluated. Furthermore, patients with claustrophobia may find it difficult to tolerate longer scanning times inside the MRI unit. Another limitation of MRI is the loud noise commonly referred to as clicking and beeping. 37,66 A sound intensity up to 120 decibels in certain MR scanners may require special ear protection.

Conclusion

The included studies indicate that the use of MRI in the diagnosis of periodontal and/or periapical disease is feasible and promising. However, more studies are needed to define the diagnostic accuracy of this imaging modality in the assessment of periodontal and periapical lesions. Although MRI is evolving in the diagnosis of odontogenic diseases such as periodontal and periapical disease, there is no consensus that MRI alone can fit the dental clinical demand. Paradoxically, in dentistry, diagnostic imaging is dependent of a precise depiction of highly mineralized tissues (i.e. enamel, dentine, and bone). However, the resolution of MRI for those structures is still limited. Future studies should address what the applications for MRI in dentistry are and create a consensus in relation to what can be seen on MRI and what should be seen. In relation to that, an important aspect to discuss is whether MRI should be considered an additional diagnostic modality, or if MRI should replace other ionizing-radiation based modalities in dentistry.
