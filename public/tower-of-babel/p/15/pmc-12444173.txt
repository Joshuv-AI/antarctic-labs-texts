Complementary approaches to improve cognitive health through prevention in Brazil
Authors: Claudia K. Suemoto, Cleusa P. Ferri, Wyllians V. Borelli, Raphael M. Castilhos
Published: 2025

Dementia is one of the most pressing public health challenges of the 21st century, and the projected growth of cases in low- and middle-income countries is expected to outpace that in high-income countries.1 While global estimates, notably those from the Lancet Commission, have helped shape the prevention agenda, country-specific data are essential to guide national priorities.2 Two new studies published in The Lancet Regional Health–Americas make important contributions by estimating the proportion of dementia and cognitive impairment cases in Brazil that could be prevented through modification of risk factors.3,4 Although they share similar aims, the studies differ in datasets, outcomes, design, and analytical approach, providing complementary insights into Brazil's cognitive health landscape.

The first study used data from the Brazilian Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSI-Brazil), a nationally representative survey of adults aged 50 years and older.3 The prevalence and communalities for 14 risk factors were estimated for the Brazilian population, while the relative risks (RR) were retrieved from the 2024 Lancet Commission report, as the cross-sectional nature of ELSI-Brazil did not allow direct RR estimations.5 Up to 60% of dementia cases in Brazil were attributed to 14 risk factors, with low educational attainment, untreated visual loss, and midlife depression emerging as the leading contributors.3

The second study utilized data from the Longitudinal Study of Adult Health (ELSA-Brasil), a cohort comprising over 15,000 public servants aged 35–74 years, recruited from six sites.4 Although ELSA-Brasil is not nationally representative, its prospective design with a median follow-up of eight years allowed the authors to calculate RR.6 Focusing on 8 modifiable risk factors, they estimated that 35% of cognitive impairment cases could be attributed to these exposures, with low education, hearing loss, and hypertension as the main contributors.4

These differences in the population attributable fraction (PAF) estimates likely reflect variations in the nature of the samples, the different outcomes, and the contrasting methodologies. ELSI-Brazil, being a nationally representative sample, captures the diversity of the Brazilian population, including socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, in which risk factors such as low education and poor access to healthcare are more prevalent.7 By necessity, this study relied on RR derived from global meta-analyses, which primarily used data from high-income countries.7 In contrast, the sample of civil servants used in ELSA-Brasil was healthier and more advantaged, and benefited from deriving RR for cognitive impairment from longitudinal follow-up. Its lower PAF may reflect both a younger population with fewer exposures to risk, the use of different outcome measures, the distinct methodology for PAF calculation, and the observed associations rather than the pooled international estimates.4 Therefore, the two studies offer complementary perspectives. ELSI-Brazil offers nationally relevant estimates that can guide public health priorities at the population level, while ELSA-Brasil provides high internal validity and insights into the strength of calculated RR in a well-characterized Brazilian cohort. Together, they create a more complete picture of cognitive morbidity and dementia prevention in Brazil, underscoring the magnitude of the opportunity for intervention.

The implications for clinicians and policymakers are substantial. Both studies confirm that dementia prevention must be a life-course effort, starting in early life.2 Less education was identified as the leading risk factor for prevention, which underscores the importance of investments in early-life education, requiring collaboration between health and education sectors.3,4 Sensory impairments also emerge as important targets, with vision loss being a leading risk factor in the ELSI-Brazil study and hearing loss in ELSA-Brasil.3,4 These results highlight the need to integrate affordable vision and hearing screening and treatment into primary care. Cardiovascular risk factors and depression are further critical modifiable targets, with benefits that extend beyond brain health.2,8

From a health policy perspective, these findings challenge Brazil's current approach to dementia, which focuses primarily on diagnosis, treatment, and caregiver support. Prevention should be explicitly incorporated into national dementia plans, with measurable targets for reducing exposure to key risk factors. The Unified Health System (SUS), with its strong primary care infrastructure, is well-positioned to deliver interventions if prevention is embedded into existing programs, such as routine health check-ups and chronic disease management.9

The studies also raise important unanswered questions, such as how combinations of risk factors interact over the life course in diverse Brazilian populations, and which interventions are most effective and culturally acceptable across regions. From a policy perspective, the challenge is to ensure equitable access to prevention strategies in a country marked by deep social and regional inequalities.7 Addressing structural determinants, such as poverty, limited education, and poor access to healthcare, will be essential for achieving the full potential of dementia prevention. While challenges remain in translating these insights into large-scale action, the evidence now supports prevention as a realistic and worthwhile component of national dementia strategies. These studies highlight where efforts should be focused, offering a foundation for gradual but meaningful progress in reducing the future dementia burden in Brazil.
