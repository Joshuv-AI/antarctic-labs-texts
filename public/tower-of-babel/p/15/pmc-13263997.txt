Convergent re-evolution of CNS structures: focus on the fovea
Authors: Susana da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The fovea is a specialized retinal region that supports high-acuity vision through extreme photoreceptor and ganglion cell densities and near one-to-one circuit connectivity. Although present only in a subset of vertebrates, foveae share core functional principles while differing widely in morphology, cellular composition, number, and retinal location, indicating multiple independent evolutionary origins. Here, I review comparative, developmental, and genomic evidence that the fovea represents a case of convergent re-evolution built on conserved central nervous system patterning mechanisms. I highlight retinoic acid signaling as the earliest known molecular pathway demarcating foveal progenitors across distantly related species and discuss how evolutionary modulation of this positional system may repeatedly generate high-acuity retinal specializations.

Visual ecology and the evolution of foveal specialization

Adaptation to distinct ecological niches has driven the evolution of sensory systems that are finely tuned to extract behaviorally relevant information from the environment. In species that rely heavily on vision for survival, particularly for prey capture and navigation in complex visually guided behaviors, natural selection has favored the formation of retinal specializations that support exceptionally high spatial resolution [1]. In these species, visual acuity is not uniformly distributed across the retina but is concentrated within a discrete region — the fovea — whose functional properties arise from coordinated anatomical, cellular, and circuit-level adaptations. Importantly, the fovea is not a universal feature of vertebrate vision, highlighting that high-acuity retinal specializations represent lineage-specific evolutionary solutions to ecological demands rather than a conserved feature of all visual systems [2].

Specializations underlying foveal high-acuity vision

In humans, the fovea occupies less than 1% of the retinal surface [3,4], yet approximately 50% of primary visual cortex (V1) is devoted to processing information originating from this region [5], underscoring its central role for human vision. The term fovea derives from the Latin for ‘pit’ or ‘depression’, reflecting the characteristic invagination of the retinal surface produced by lateral displacement of the inner retinal layers, a feature thought to reduce light scattering, among other potential optical functions. Although the pit is a prominent morphological hallmark, foveal identity extends beyond gross anatomy and is defined by a suite of structural, cellular, and circuit-level specializations. At the cellular level, the foveae of diurnal species are cone-only, forming a rod-free zone (RFZ) [6]. Foveal cones differ from their peripheral counterparts both morphologically and physiologically: they are slender, tightly packed, and form an exceptionally high-density photoreceptor mosaic. Compared with peripheral cones, foveal cones exhibit weaker and slower luminance adaptation, a property that may help preserve sensitivity during sustained visual stimulation [7]. The fovea also contains the highest density of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) [8], alongside interneurons. This extreme cellular packing supports a specialized circuitry characterized by reduced convergence and near one-to-one connectivity between cones and RGCs. In primates, this circuitry is called the midget pathway [9,10], which minimizes spatial summation and underlies the exceptional spatial resolution of the fovea. In species with vascularized retinas, the fovea is avascular [4]. Collectively, these features converge to maximize photon capture, preserve signal fidelity, and support high-acuity vision.

Comparative diversity and convergent evolution of the vertebrate fovea

Foveae are present in select vertebrate taxa, including primates, many birds, some reptiles (notably lizards), and a limited number of fishes, but are absent in most mammals, including rodents [1,2]. Despite sharing a common functional goal — maximizing spatial resolution — foveae exhibit substantial interspecies diversity in morphology, cellular composition, and location. Morphologically, foveae can adopt either a convexiclivate or concaviclivate configuration. Convexiclivate foveae are characterized by steep, V-shaped walls that can generate optical magnification through light refraction at the vitreoretina interface [11]. They have also been proposed to enhance light focusing at the foveal center and contribute to motion detection [12,13], and are common in non-mammalian taxa such as birds, reptiles, and fishes [1]. In contrast, concaviclivate foveae are U-shaped and are typical of primates [1], though they are also observed in the tuatara [3], indicating that this morphology is not primate-specific. Pit depth likewise varies widely, ranging from complete displacement of the ganglion cell layer (GCL) and inner nuclear layer (INL) to more partial displacement.

Photoreceptor composition also differs across species. Diurnal foveae are rod-free and cone-rich, whereas nocturnal species often possess rod-dominated or even pure-rod fovea, as seen in certain deep-sea fishes, proposed to be optimized for detecting dim point light sources [14]. A notable exception is the nocturnal primate tarsier, which retains a cone-rich fovea despite its nocturnal lifestyle, likely reflecting recent descent from a diurnal ancestor. Additional species-specific variation exists in cone subtype composition. In humans, the foveola lacks short-wavelength-sensitive cones (S) cones, a feature thought to minimize chromatic aberration at the point of highest acuity. In the non-human primates, Scones are present but sparse within the fovea. In contrast, avian raptors retain the full complement of cone subtypes found in birds, including ultraviolet- and violet-sensitive cones, while double cones are absent from the foveal region in some raptor species [15].

Species also differ in foveal number and retinal position. Primates and fishes invariably possess a single fovea, whereas many birds and some reptiles are bifoveate. Interestingly, the retinal position of the fovea is variable but constrained to the equatorial region. In primates, the fovea lies temporal to the optic nerve head (ONH), whereas in most single-foveated birds it is positioned nasal to the ONH. Owls represent a notable exception, as their forward-facing eyes and binocular vision are associated with a temporally positioned fovea [16]. In bifoveated species, the deeper central fovea is usually nasal to the ONH, while the second, shallower fovea lies in the temporal periphery, although rare exceptions have been reported, such as in seabird gulls, in which both foveae area positioned nasally [17]. In fishes and single-foveated lizards, the fovea is generally located temporal to the ONH, a location thought to optimize stereopsis and depth perception during prey capture [14]. Despite this striking diversity in morphology, cellular composition, and retinal location, all foveae seem to share a common circuit strategy characterized by extremely high photoreceptor and RGC densities, reflecting the near one-to-one cone-to-RGC connectivity. The repeated emergence of the foveae in phylogenetically distant taxa supports multiple independent origins and thus represents a case of convergent evolutionary solutions to the challenge of high-spatial resolution vision (Figure 1). A central unresolved question is whether these diverse foveae arise from conserved genetic and developmental programs and, if so, how such programs achieve sufficient plasticity to generate the wide range of variation observed across species.

Current understanding of foveal development

Our current understanding of foveal development derives mainly from classical histological studies of primate retinas [18–25], and more recently from work in bifoveate anoles [26], and has focused largely on the morphogenetic events underlying pit formation. These studies show that foveal development is a highly protracted process that begins early in retinogenesis: in humans, the presumptive fovea is identifiable by fetal week (Fwk) 8, although the pit becomes morphologically apparent only around Fwk 25 [23]. Despite this detailed anatomical description, the molecular mechanisms that establish foveal identity, including the specification of foveal progenitors, photoreceptor fate bias, increased cell densities, specialized circuitry, and their spatial restriction to a small central domain, remain largely unknown.

Developmental patterning of the retina and the emergence of the fovea

The vertebrate retina is composed of five major neuronal classes: photoreceptors (rods and cones), bipolar cells, horizontal cells, amacrine cells, RGCs, and one resident glial class, Müller glia. These cells are organized into three conserved nuclear layers: the outer nuclear layer containing photoreceptors, the INL containing the somata of interneurons and Müller glia, and the GCL, which consists primarily of RGCs. This basic cellular composition and laminar architecture are highly conserved across vertebrates [27,28], as also demonstrated by recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses spanning 17 species, from lamprey to mammals, whose lineages diverged more than 500 million years ago [29].

Developmentally, the retina is part of the central nervous system (CNS) and arises from bilateral evaginations of the diencephalon that form the optic vesicles, which subsequently invaginate to generate the optic cups (OC). The OC is populated by highly proliferative retinal progenitor cells (RPCs) that give rise to all six main retinal cell classes. Classical lineage tracing experiments and modern single-cell genomic analyses have shown that RPCs are multipotent, heterogeneous, and progress through temporally regulated competence states, generating each cell class within defined but overlapping developmental windows [30]. Like other regions of the CNS [31], the OC is patterned along the dorso-ventral and naso-temporal axes by morphogen gradients and regionally restricted expression of transcription factors (TF), which confer positional identity to RPCs and regulate their proliferation, differentiation, and topographic connectivity [32–36]. As development proceeds, RPCs enter lineage-restricted trajectories governed primarily by intrinsic TF programs, modulated by short-range extrinsic signaling, and implemented through gene regulatory networks (GRNs) that bias progenitors toward specific fates. These GRN-based programs have been extensively characterized by recent single-cell transcriptomic and epigenomic studies and constitute the core molecular logic of retinal cell fate specification (reviewed here [37,38]).

Spatially resolved single-cell transcriptomic studies comparing foveal and peripheral regions of the primate retina have revealed that the vast majority of retinal cell classes are shared between these domains [39,40]. The principal differences lie in relative cell class abundance and gene expression profiles rather than in the presence of unique neuronal classes. Thus, the fovea is constructed from the same fundamental pool of RPCs and cellular repertoire as the rest of the retina, and its exceptional functional properties arise from molecular and circuit-level specializations of conserved cell types rather than from the generation of novel ones. From this perspective, foveal specification can be viewed as a regional patterning process in which RPCs at the presumptive foveal position interpret local morphogen gradients and regionally restricted TF cues that bias them toward a specialized developmental trajectory. To date, retinoic acid (RA) signaling remains the only molecular pathway shown to pattern the early retina in a manner that uniquely demarcates foveal progenitors [41].

Retinoic acid signaling as a conserved foveal patterning mechanism

During early retinogenesis, RA signaling patterns the retina in a strikingly non-uniform manner, with a focal absence of RA activity coinciding with the presumptive fovea. This localized RA depletion is mediated by spatially restricted expression of RA-degrading enzymes of the CYP26 family within foveal RPCs. Functional studies in the chick retina, which possesses a high-acuity area (HAA) sharing key organizational features with the primate fovea [41,42], demonstrated that RA depletion within the presumptive HAA is required for the formation of all hallmark features of this region, including an RFZ and high RGC density. These findings establish RA signaling as a necessary early component of the molecular cascade driving foveal specialization.

Remarkably, this RA-based patterning mechanism is conserved across birds and primates [41,43,44], lineages that diverged more than 300 million years ago. Whereas RA-synthetic enzymes (ALDH1A1 and ALDH1A3) show broadly conserved dorso-ventral expression across vertebrates, RA-degrading enzymes (CYP26A1 and CYP26C1) display highly restricted, species-specific spot- or stripe-like patterns. In non-foveate species such as the mouse, CYP26s expression forms elongated stripes along the naso-temporal equator axis [45], whereas in foveated species a discrete equatorial ‘spot’, with or without an accompanying stripe, consistently marks the presumptive high-acuity domain [41]. In primates, CYP26A1 is uniquely expressed in RPCs of the presumptive fovea [43,44], representing the earliest known molecular marker of foveal RPCs. These observations suggest that evolutionary modulation of RA degradation, rather than RA synthesis, underlies foveal patterning, potentially through changes in cis-regulatory elements controlling CYP26s expression.

The downstream transcriptional networks through which RA controls foveal lineage decisions remain largely unresolved. In chick, FGF8 acts downstream of RA [41] and may participate in feedback regulation of CYP26 expression; however, this interaction is not conserved in primates, indicating divergence of downstream gene networks despite preservation of the upstream RA-based positional framework. In light of single-cell transcriptomic evidence showing that foveal specialization does not involve the emergence of unique cell classes, but rather quantitative and molecular shifts in the production and organization of conserved cell types, RA-dependent patterning likely acts by conferring positional identity to RPCs and biasing them toward generating high cone densities, elevated RGC output, and near one-to-one cone-to-RGC connectivity, while non-foveal RPCs are biased toward rod production and lower RGC density. This role is consistent with the well-established function of RA as a morphogen in multiple embryonic contexts [46,47], including CNS arealization and neural circuit formation in the developing cerebral cortex [48,49].

From an evolutionary developmental perspective, these findings support a model in which deeply conserved patterning systems, such as RA signaling, can repeatedly re-evolve through subtle regulatory modifications to generate complex structural and functional specializations. In this case, evolutionary changes in the spatial control of RA degradation, mediated by cis-regulatory modulation of CYP26 expression, appear sufficient to redeploy an ancestral RA-based positional framework toward the specification of a high-acuity domain in the retina. Such tuning of RA signaling may thus represent a general mechanism by which foveae have evolved convergently across diverse vertebrate lineages (Figure 2). Importantly, the fact that FGF8 acts downstream of RA in chick but does not appear to play a conserved role in primates underscores that conservation of an upstream patterning signal does not necessarily entail conservation of the downstream effector machinery. Rather, distinct genes and regulatory pathways appear to have evolved independently downstream of RA to drive foveal specification in different vertebrate lineages. Convergent evolution of the fovea, therefore, likely reflects repeated evolutionary coupling of this ancestral RA-based positional cue to lineage-specific downstream program, rather than strict conservation of a single foveal developmental module. This framework also helps explain the plasticity of the system in accommodating the diversity of foveal morphologies and specializations observed across vertebrates.

Single-cell genomics and human genetic approaches to identify foveal specification programs

Recent transcriptomic studies comparing macular and foveal regions with peripheral retina in primates have provided an in-depth characterization of mature retinal cell classes, subclasses, and gene expression profiles. However, because most of these analyses were performed in adult tissue [39,40,50], they primarily inform on genetic programs that maintain foveal identity rather than on the early developmental programs that initiate it.

Efforts to capture developmental stages of foveal formation have included bulk RNA sequencing [51] and, more recently, single-cell and multi-omic profiling of human fetal and non-human primate neonatal retinas [52–55]. These studies have begun to identify genes enriched in central versus peripheral regions and to delineate transcriptional programs associated with foveal development. In the neonatal marmoset, physical microdissection of the foveal and peripheral retina was possible as the pit was morphologically apparent, enabling identification of gene expression differences associated with late stages of foveal morphogenesis, particularly those involved in cone packing and circuit maturation. Accordingly, these datasets are likely to capture downstream or maintenance programs rather than the early regulatory events that specify foveal progenitors. In contrast, human fetal datasets encompass earlier stages of retinal development, including the window of foveal specification (Fwk8), but lack spatial dissection because no morphological landmarks are present at these stages. As a result, foveal RPCs are highly diluted within whole-retina samples. The use of CYP26A1 as an early molecular marker of foveal progenitors has partially overcome this limitation, enabling the identification of foveal-enriched transcriptional signatures and independently validating CYP26A1 as a conserved marker of the presumptive foveal domain. Among the genes consistently enriched in the developing fovea across at least two independent datasets, and thus strong candidates for functional relevance, are DIO2, a thyroid hormone-activating enzyme implicated in cone subtype specification in human retinal organoids [56]; CDKN1A, a regulator of cell-cycle exit; SOX6, enriched in both fetal human and marmoset fovea; FRZB, a modulator of Wnt signaling; as well as ANXA2 and AHR, suggesting potential roles for thyroid hormone signaling, cell-cycle control, and Wnt/aryl hydrocarbon receptor pathways in early foveal development.

Beyond transcriptomics, human genetics provides complementary insight into foveal development. Recent genome-wide association studies correlating optical coherence tomography-based measures of foveal hypoplasia with genetic variation [57] have identified both established regulators of eye and retinal development (e.g. TYR, OCA2, PAX6, KIF11, AHR) and components of the RA pathway, including CYP26A1, thereby independently implicating RA signaling in foveal formation. Importantly, these studies have also uncovered novel candidate genes, such as TUBB4B, involved in microtubule organization, and TFs including HES1 and HMX1, pointing to additional pathways that may contribute to foveal morphogenesis and cellular specialization.

Together, these approaches have generated an expanding list of candidate genes and regulatory networks associated with foveal development. Nonetheless, identifying the early fovea-specific molecular programs that control progenitor competence, lineage bias, and spatial restriction remains a major challenge. Functional validation in appropriate experimental systems, including foveated species, species with equivalent HAAs, and emerging foveated retinal organoid models, will be essential to infer causal mechanisms and to elucidate how conserved patterning cues are translated into the unique cellular and circuit architecture of the fovea.

Outstanding questions and future directions

A fundamental unresolved question concerns the downstream mechanisms of RA signaling and how RA depletion is interpreted at the transcriptional level to generate the full complement of foveal specializations. What effector cascades are activated downstream of RA to drive these processes? Do distinct regulatory programs govern individual features, such as cone specification and expansion of RGC numbers, and to what extent are these programs functionally coordinated or interdependent? Moreover, are these downstream pathways conserved across independently evolved foveated lineages, or are they largely lineage-specific, reflecting evolutionary divergence in how RA signaling is integrated with other developmental cues, particularly fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling, to regulate progenitor competence, proliferation, and fate bias? Closely related is the challenge of identifying the early GRNs that specify foveal RPCs, a task complicated by the difficulty of isolating this rare population before morphological landmarks emerge. What molecular mechanisms establish the highly localized, species-specific patterns of CYP26 expression that create an RA-depleted foveal domain? Do evolutionary changes in cis-regulatory enhancers underlie the transition from stripe-like to spot-like RA-degradation patterns in foveated species? Comparative histological and functional studies across foveated and non-foveated species will be essential to address these questions. Finally, it is unclear whether these developmental pathways are strictly confined to early developmental windows or can be re-activated at later stages and harnessed therapeutically to restore fovea structure and function in macular diseases. Pioneering studies in human-induced pluripotent stem cells-derived retinal organoids provide a promising platform to address this question, raising the prospect that a mechanistic understanding of foveal development could inform regenerative strategies and targeted interventions for disorders that selectively compromise high acuity vision.
