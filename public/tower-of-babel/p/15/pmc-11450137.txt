Extrapolation of the Efficacy and Pharmacokinetics of Belimumab to Support its Use in Children with Lupus Nephritis
Authors: Richard Dimelow, Lia Liefaard, Yulia Green, Ryan Tomlinson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveLupus nephritis (LN), a severe manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus, has greater severity in children versus adults. Belimumab is approved for systemic lupus erythematosus treatment in patients aged ≥ 5 years, and for active LN in adults in the European Union, China, Japan and Latin America, and patients aged ≥ 5 years in the USA. Low prevalence of paediatric active LN makes conducting a clinical study within a reasonable period unfeasible. We describe a model-based extrapolation of belimumab efficacy and pharmacokinetics from adults to children with LN to support US Food and Drug Administration approval of intravenous belimumab 10 mg/kg (administered every 4 weeks after the loading dose) in children (aged 5–17 years) with active LN.MethodsThis concept assumed that disease progression, response to belimumab, exposure–response, and the target belimumab exposure for efficacy are similar across adult and paediatric systemic lupus erythematosus and LN, evaluated against the published literature for paediatric LN and belimumab systemic lupus erythematosus and LN clinical trial data in adults and children. A two-compartmental population pharmacokinetic model, previously developed for adults with LN, was used to extrapolate belimumab pharmacokinetics to children with LN.ResultsThe model captured the dependence of time-varying proteinuria on belimumab clearance, and therefore exposure. Sufficient target exposures for efficacy were achieved in children with active LN. A small proportion of children aged 5–11 years are predicted to have exposures below adult levels but no impact to efficacy is expected.ConclusionsOur model demonstrated that intravenous belimumab 10 mg/kg every 4 weeks is appropriate for children aged 5–17 years with active LN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-024-01422-y.

Key Points

We used an innovative accelerated approach to drug development to demonstrate that the extrapolation of belimumab efficacy from adults with lupus nephritis (LN) to children with LN is valid through the similarities in disease progression, response to belimumab, the exposure–response relationship, and target exposure for efficacy between adults and children with systemic lupus erythematosus and adults with LN.The target exposure for efficacy of belimumab 10 mg/kg administered intravenously every 4 weeks was achieved in almost all children with LN aged 12–17 years and most children with LN aged 5–11 years; however, there was a slightly higher likelihood of exposures being below the adult target range in the 5–11-year age group, due to a weight-proportional 10-mg/kg intravenous dose with generally lower body weights in this younger age group. However, evidence from adult studies suggest that even these lower exposure levels would be sufficient for efficacy.By considering the exposure–response relationship for efficacy, our analysis demonstrated that intravenous belimumab 10 mg/kg every 4 weeks, which is approved in adults with systemic lupus erythematosus and LN, and children with systemic lupus erythematosus, would also be appropriate for all children aged 5–17 years with active LN, thereby helping to address a high unmet need for novel LN treatments in this population.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disorder with diverse clinical manifestations that presents across various age groups (15–20% < 18 years of age) [1, 2]. Approximately 40% of adults with SLE develop lupus nephritis (LN), a severe renal manifestation that often leads to end-stage kidney disease and increased mortality [3]. While, overall, adult and paediatric LN have similar pathophysiology and presentation, a higher proportion of children with SLE develop LN when compared with adults (80% vs 40%) [3, 4]. Lupus nephritis in children may also present more severely and lead to higher mortality than in adults [2–4]; however, the data summarised below indicate the response to standard therapy (ST) is similar in adults and children with LN.

Treatment options for paediatric LN are broadly based on adult LN ST and rely on the use of corticosteroids in conjunction with an intense period of immunosuppression to halt kidney inflammation, followed by maintenance therapy to prevent further flares and manage LN in the long term [5, 6]. However, LN management and outcomes in children remain suboptimal and there is a critical unmet need [5]. Corticosteroid use is associated with significant toxicities in paediatric LN, including osteonecrosis and cataracts, with more than 40% of children with biopsy-proven LN showing evidence of corticosteroid toxicity [5, 7]. Belimumab is an approved biologic agent for the treatment of active SLE in patients aged ≥ 5 years in many countries, and of active LN in adults in the European Union, China, Japan and Latin America, and patients aged ≥ 5 years in the USA [8–11]. The mechanism of action of belimumab involves binding and inhibiting the biological activity of B-lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) in the blood, a key protein involved in the selection and survival of B cells [9, 12]. Elevated levels of BLyS are found in patients with SLE and LN [13].

Thus, SLE and LN share a common underlying pathophysiology that is blocked by the mechanism of action of belimumab, which has been demonstrated by the clinical efficacy of belimumab in both adults with SLE with or without LN at the same intravenous (IV) 10-mg/kg dose, administered on days 0, 14, and 28 and then every 4 weeks [14–16].

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of IV belimumab 10 mg/kg has been characterised in adults [17] and children with SLE [18] using a linear two-compartment model with first-order absorption, distribution and elimination. Using data from randomised clinical trials, a population PK (popPK) analysis found that the kinetics of belimumab in paediatric patients with SLE are consistent with adults with SLE; average concentrations at steady state (Cavg) of 111 µg/mL and 100 µg/mL were reported, respectively [18]. At these exposure levels, there is an excess of belimumab compared with BLyS in circulation, resulting in saturation of the binding of belimumab to its target [18]. Allometric effects of body size and weight-proportional dosing resulted in a slightly lower Cavg of 92 µg/mL for children with SLE aged 5–11 years, but these lower exposures did not result in diminished efficacy [18]. The PK of IV belimumab 10 mg/kg has also been characterised in adults with LN [19]. Increased proteinuria during the first few weeks of treatment was found to correlate with lower belimumab exposure, likely due to the loss of belimumab through the kidneys. The average (geometric mean) concentration of belimumab over the first 12 weeks was 91 µg/mL.

As up to 80% of patients with childhood-onset SLE develop LN, there is a high unmet need to identify the most appropriate treatments in this population [4]. However, the low prevalence of LN among children [20] and low rates of renal flares and active disease in paediatric LN, which would be required for treatment evaluation [21], mean that it is not feasible to conduct even a small study within a reasonable time frame to evaluate the PK and efficacy of belimumab in paediatric active LN. As an alternative approach, and consistent with US Food and Drug Administration guidance [22] and recent evaluation of exposure and response in SLE [23], here we describe a modelling study to extrapolate the efficacy and PK of IV belimumab 10 mg/kg in active LN from adults to children aged 5–17 years (the target population).

Methods

Objectives

This analysis aimed to extrapolate both efficacy and PK from adults with active LN to children with active LN through a model-based approach, and to assess whether IV belimumab 10 mg/kg, administered on days 0, 14, and 28 and then every 4 weeks, is an appropriate dose to treat children aged 5–17 years with active LN (the target population).

Analysis Endpoints

For the efficacy endpoints, Safety of Estrogens in Lupus Erythematosus National Assessment-SLE Disease Activity Index (SELENA-SLEDAI) was used for SLE, and the dichotomous primary efficacy renal response and complete renal response with associated changes on proteinuria and albumin levels were used for LN. A primary efficacy renal response was defined as a urine protein/creatinine ratio of ≤ 0.7; an estimated glomerular filtration rate no worse than 20% below the pre-flare value, or ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 of body-surface area; and no use of rescue therapy. A complete renal response was defined as a urine protein/creatinine ratio < 0.5; an estimated glomerular filtration rate that was no worse than 10% below the pre-flare value, or ≥ 90 mL per minute per 1.73 m2; and no use of rescue therapy [16]. As the efficacy endpoints for SLE and LN were different, a direct comparison of efficacy between SLE and LN could not be made.

A belimumab phase II dose-ranging study in patients with SLE was not sufficiently powered on efficacy to fully characterise the exposure–response relationship, and a dose-ranging study in patients with LN has never been carried out. Therefore, a minimum concentration (Cmin) above which efficacy is maintained is unknown and the average concentration has been used to characterise belimumab exposure for efficacy. For SLE, the belimumab pharmacokinetic endpoint was Cavg at steady state. For LN, the belimumab pharmacokinetic endpoint was the average concentration over the first 4, 12 and 24 weeks of treatment; Cavg(Wk 0–4), Cavg(Wk 0–12) and Cavg(Wk 0–24), respectively, during which proteinuria, and therefore renal elimination of belimumab, is reduced in response to treatment.

Extrapolation Concept

Overview

In brief, and as discussed above, SLE and LN are driven by the same underlying pathophysiology targeted by belimumab (binding to and neutralisation of soluble BLyS), and a similar belimumab exposure for efficacy has been established in adults and children with SLE, and adults with LN [17–19]. Extrapolation of efficacy assumes the same target concentration would also apply to children with LN. Thus, selecting the dose in children that achieves this exposure is expected to deliver the same efficacy as observed in adults with LN. The advantage of adopting such an efficacy-extrapolation approach is that a large and time-consuming efficacy study in children with LN is not feasible. A small pharmacokinetic study to characterise belimumab PK in children with LN to calculate the dose required to achieve the target exposure may also not be feasible within a reasonable time frame, given the rarity of LN in children. However, in this case, as belimumab PK is well established in adults and children with SLE, and adults with LN, the PK was instead extrapolated to children with LN. These extrapolated pharmacokinetic data were then used to inform the most appropriate therapeutic dosing regime for children with LN.

Extrapolation of Belimumab Efficacy to Paediatric LN

Our extrapolation concept (Fig. 1) was based on the assumption that disease progression, response to belimumab treatment, the exposure–response relationship, and the target belimumab exposure for efficacy are similar across adult and paediatric SLE and LN populations. Specifically, in terms of disease progression, extrapolation of efficacy relies on disease aetiology and pathophysiology, manifestation and progression being similar across populations. Evidence supporting the assumptions between adults with SLE and children with SLE would demonstrate that efficacy can be extrapolated by age. Although efficacy in SLE and LN is defined by different endpoints, evidence supporting the assumptions between adults with SLE and adults with LN with respect to the pharmacological response to belimumab treatment would support the premise that the efficacy of belimumab in SLE extrapolates to LN. Therefore, the combination of both components would indicate that efficacy can be extrapolated across both age and disease to children with LN, and so a dose that achieves similar exposures as observed in adults with LN (Cavg(Wk 0-12) = 91 µg/mL) would achieve a similar renal response in children.Fig. 1Extrapolation approach and relevant assumption. Extrapolation of Belimumab PK to Paediatric LN

A popPK model, previously developed for adults with LN [19], was used to extrapolate belimumab PK to children with LN, because this model captures the dependence of time-varying proteinuria on belimumab clearance, and therefore exposure. The model was fitted to pharmacokinetic data from 224 adults with LN who received IV belimumab 10 mg/kg plus ST in BLISS-LN.

The pharmacokinetic model is two compartmental with first-order absorption, distribution and elimination, and took into account the allometric effects of body size and the impact of renal impairment in LN, with variability in belimumab PK described by the following patient-level covariates: fat-free mass (FFM) at baseline and time-dependent proteinuria and serum albumin concentrations. Fat-free mass, derived from body weight, body mass index (BMI) and sex [25], was found to be the most appropriate descriptor of body size to describe the allometric effects on PK in adults with LN [19], which was consistent with the allometric component of the paediatric SLE population model for IV dosing [18].

To accurately characterise FFM in children with SLE or LN, body weight and BMI must be sampled from a representative population across the paediatric age range, including the 5–11-year age group; however, the PLUTO study only enrolled 13 patients who were aged 5–11 years. To address this, body weight and BMI were sampled from the healthy volunteer Centers for Disease Control National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, restricted to age 5–17 years and biased 90% towards female individuals [18, 24], who are nine times more likely to develop SLE than male individuals [26]. Sampled volunteer body weights and BMI values were compared with data from PLUTO to ensure the distributions were consistent, and FFM for children with LN was imputed from the sampled body weight, BMI and sex [25].

Proteinuria and albumin at baseline and during response to ST were compared between adults and children with LN using data from the literature (Table 2); if their distributions were similar at baseline and in response to ST, it was assumed their profiles in response to belimumab treatment would also be the same in both adults and children with LN.

Validation that the popPK model developed for adults with LN extrapolates to children is challenging because belimumab has not been administered to children with LN. However, extrapolation to children was part-validated by using the model to predict belimumab exposures in children with SLE and comparing the predictions with the observed data from PLUTO.

The popPK model was used to simulate the exposure distribution over time in adults (N = 10,000) and children with LN (N = 10,000) for IV belimumab 10 mg/kg administered on days 0, 14, and 28 and then every 4 weeks. Unexplained between-patient variability of the popPK model was combined with the covariate model inputs to predict the model parameters for each sampled individual.

Statistical Analysis

All data manipulation, model development and analysis were performed on the GSK Modelling and Analysis Platform, a validated and version-controlled programming environment incorporating NONMEM version 7.3, PsN version 4.6.0 and R version 3.2.5. The minimum, average and maximum belimumab concentration over each dosing period were summarised by the median, interquartile range and the 95% prediction interval.

Results

Evidence Supporting Extrapolation of Belimumab Efficacy in Adults with LN to Children with LN (the Target Population)

Disease Progression

Adults and children with SLE share a similar pathophysiology and disease course; almost all serologic features present in adult-onset SLE are also present in childhood-onset SLE with similar or increased frequency [27, 28]. In SLE clinical trials, adults and children had similar biomarker types and levels at baseline, and a similar biomarker response to ST (see Sections S1 and S2 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] for details).

A quantitative comparison between SLE and LN is limited because of the acute nature of a nephritis flare. Increased proteinuria and the requirement for intense immunosuppressant therapy in LN limits the comparison in the pharmacology between SLE and LN. Nevertheless, high levels of anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies and low levels of complement component C3 and C4 are associated with disease activity in SLE and LN, and adults with SLE and adults with LN had similar baseline levels of anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies (median: BLISS-76 171.5 IU/mL; BLISS-52 173.5 IU/mL; BLISS-LN 133.0 IU/mL), C3 (median: BLISS-76 97.0 mg/dL; BLISS-52 90.0 mg/dL; BLISS-LN 82.0 mg/dL) and C4 (median: BLISS-76 15.0 mg/dL; BLISS-52 14.0 mg/dL; BLISS-LN 14.0 mg/dL) in clinical trials (see Section S1 of the ESM). However, those with LN had lower baseline BLyS (median: BLISS-76 1.38 ng/mL; BLISS-52 1.38 ng/mL; BLISS-LN 0.55 ng/mL) and immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels (median: BLISS-76 14.60 g/L; BLISS-52 16.20 g/L; BLISS-LN 9.06 g/L) compared with those with SLE, perhaps reflecting increased loss through the kidneys of these markers due to glomerular injury.

Lupus nephritis pathogenesis in both adults and children involves the formation and deposition of immune complexes in the kidney, and local inflammation resulting in kidney damage [6, 29]. Distribution of LN histological classes among adults and children with LN is similar: diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis (class IV) is the most frequent abnormality that is present in 40–75% of cases and is a predictor of poor prognosis [3, 29–35], and the distribution of LN histological classes at presentation is similar between adults and children [36]. Furthermore, proteinuria is the most common manifestation of LN in both adults and children [37]. Similar treatment regimens are used in adults and children with active LN to achieve the same treatment targets. Lupus nephritis disease progression in response to ST is similar between adults and children; the proportion of patients with LN achieving a complete renal response or a partial renal response to ST at 6 and 12 months is similar for both populations (Table 1).

Response to Belimumab Treatment

A similar pharmacological response to belimumab versus placebo was observed for adults and children with SLE (see Section S2 of the ESM), resulting in similar efficacy [24]. With belimumab, adults with LN experienced greater improvements versus adults with SLE in anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies, B-cell subsets and C3/C4. These differences between LN and SLE were also observed with placebo and likely reflect the background therapy used, thereby indicating the response to belimumab in SLE and LN is similar (see Section S2 of the ESM). Of note, increases in IgG over 52 weeks were observed for adults with LN, versus reductions in IgG for adults with SLE (see Section S2 of the ESM); this difference is likely related to the impact of proteinuria response in the LN population (IgG levels increase as proteinuria decreases in response to treatment). Overall, however, the IgG response to belimumab can be considered similar between adults with SLE or LN as the treatment difference relative to placebo was similar in both populations.

Exposure–Response Relationship of Belimumab

The exposure–response relationship of belimumab was established in clinical trials of adults with SLE (BLISS-52 and BLISS-76), where IV 1-mg/kg and IV 10-mg/kg doses were evaluated, and a further pharmacokinetic analysis of these trials, which also combined pharmacokinetic data from other belimumab trials, was carried out [14, 15, 17]. The primary response rate in adults with SLE increases with exposure after 1-mg/kg dosing, up to Cavg of 20–30 μg/mL (see Section S3 of the ESM). Target Exposure for Belimumab Efficacy

In adults with SLE, significant differences for efficacy were observed with belimumab versus placebo at the IV 10-mg/kg dose level, but not the IV 1-mg/kg dose level [14, 15]. Efficacy in children with SLE and adults with LN was also established at the IV 10-mg/kg dose level [16, 24]. Population pharmacokinetic analyses at IV 10 mg/kg estimated a belimumab Cavg of >50 μg/mL in adults and children with SLE, and >40 μg/mL in adults with LN (Table 3). Specifically, a Cavg > 41 μg/mL in adults with LN was derived from pharmacokinetic model simulations. As the IV 10-mg/kg dose level has efficacy in adults with LN, a minimum target exposure for children with LN can be defined as approximately > 40 μg/mL over the first 12 weeks. Together, evidence suggests that our knowledge of belimumab exposure and efficacy is sufficient to extrapolate efficacy to a paediatric LN population.

Extrapolation of Belimumab PK to Children with LN

Covariate Distributions in Paediatric LN

The sampled paediatric population of healthy volunteers (aged 5–17 years, 90% female) had a higher distribution of body weight, BMI and FFM than children with SLE enrolled in PLUTO (Fig. 2). Sampled body weights and BMI were corrected to 86% and 94%, respectively, to be representative of a paediatric SLE population; these values minimised the difference in median values summed over all ages. Sampled FFM was imputed from the corrected body weight and BMI, and closely aligned with the paediatric SLE population from PLUTO. Initial proteinuria distribution in adults with LN receiving ST in BLISS-LN is similar to published proteinuria data from children with LN, albeit with more variability within the latter (Table 2). Adults and children with LN also have similar proteinuria recovery patterns in response to ST (Table 2). Distribution of serum albumin at baseline and improvements in response to ST are also similar between adults and children with LN (Table 2). These data show that baseline proteinuria and albumin distributions and subsequent responses to ST are similar in adults and children with LN. The proteinuria and albumin response to belimumab in children has not been studied and is unknown, but the similarities between adults and children receiving ST suggest that similar treatment responses between adults and children with LN would also be expected for belimumab plus ST. The adult LN popPK model was used to simulate the belimumab concentration–time profiles for children with SLE who received IV belimumab 10 mg/kg in a visual predictive check (Fig. 3). Discussion

Belimumab efficacy and safety have been studied in adults with LN [16], as well as in adults and children with SLE [14, 15, 24]; however, the low prevalence of LN in children [20] makes it challenging to conduct a clinical trial in this population. One solution is to extrapolate efficacy from adult to paediatric patients, as has previously been demonstrated for SLE [23]. Likewise, in this study, a model-based extrapolation approach was therefore used to extrapolate the efficacy of IV belimumab 10 mg/kg to children with LN aged 5–17 years.

The validity of this extrapolation approach was established through the similarities between adults and children with SLE and adults with LN in disease progression, response to belimumab, the exposure–response relationship and target exposure for efficacy. This approach, called extrapolation of efficacy, would typically be supported by a small pharmacokinetic study to understand the PK in the target population and to select the dose that would achieve therapeutic exposure levels. However, here we proposed that knowledge of belimumab PK has been so well established in related/similar patient populations that it could also be extrapolated to children with LN without the need for collection of pharmacokinetic data through a clinical study.

The target exposure for efficacy was achieved in almost all children aged 12–17 years and most children aged 5–11 years; the likelihood of exposure below the adult 2.5th percentile was > 10% only for children weighing below 30 kg. However, there was a slightly higher likelihood of exposures being below the adult target range in the 5–11-year age group, owing to allometric effects of body size on clearance not fully compensating for the weight-proportional IV dose (IV 10 mg/kg) with generally lower body weights in this younger age group. Average exposure over the first 12 weeks of treatment modestly increased with body weight, as expected for the IV 10-mg/kg dose, meaning that most children > 30 kg would have exposure that is consistent with data from adults (> 41 µg/mL). In our model, there was a small but increased likelihood that some children < 30 kg would have exposures below the adult distribution (< 41 ug/mL). However, data from adults with SLE suggested that exposure as low as 20–30 µg/mL may still deliver maximum efficacy. Therefore, even though a small number of children with LN < 30 kg may have exposures below adult exposures for the IV 10-mg/kg dose, adequate exposure for efficacy is still anticipated in this low body weight subgroup. Finally, we note that given the low prevalence of LN in children [21, 31], this theoretical possibility is unlikely to be observed in practice.

This work has highlighted potential lower exposure compared with adults in paediatric patients with LN < 30 kg on the basis that proteinuria presents similarly in children as established in adults and is therefore independent of body weight. Adopting this assumption for the pharmacokinetic simulations means that children < 30 kg have similar proteinuria as children ≥ 30 kg, and although this study has identified children < 30 kg to have a higher likelihood of lower exposure under this assumption, this likelihood could increase should proteinuria be higher in younger or lower body weight children. Paediatric LN is rare and where data are available they do not tend to stratify between children versus adolescents; however, the comparison of adult and paediatric data does not support an association between proteinuria and age, which suggests proteinuria is independent of body weight.

For LN, the target exposure has been established in adults as Cavg over the first 12 weeks above 41 µg/mL (2.5th percentile). The pharmacokinetic simulations suggested that Cmin at week 12 was not as suitable to compare adult and paediatric exposures. First, Cmin in adults ranged from 4 µg/mL (2.5th percentile), which is close to zero and likely within a sub-therapeutic range. Therefore, consideration of the Cmin distribution alone, without consideration of the overall concentration profile over the first 12 weeks, is less informative when assessing the potential for efficacy. Second, Cavg was more conservative when assessing the likelihood for low exposure: 15.3% of 5–11-year-olds were predicted to have Cavg below the adult Cavg threshold of 41 µg/mL, whereas only 5.8% of 5–11-year-olds have Cmin below the adult Cmin threshold of 4 µg/mL (Table 4).

The safety profile of IV belimumab has been established through a large clinical development programme and supported by more than 10 years of post-marketing experience. Important identified risks include infections and psychiatric events such as depression and suicidality. No new safety signals have been identified in children. In a study of adults with active LN, the safety profile for belimumab plus standard of care therapy was similar to ST alone [16]. Based on the totality of the data, it is therefore expected that the safety profile for belimumab in paediatric LN would be consistent with the positive safety profiles established in adult and paediatric SLE and adult LN populations.

This model-based study has some limitations. To establish the extrapolation concept, comparisons between disease progression and belimumab responses for adults and children with SLE and/or LN were restricted by the data available for each population, and required inference across several different studies. In particular, the sample size of the paediatric SLE study (PLUTO) was relatively small (belimumab N = 53; placebo N = 40). Furthermore, extrapolating across SLE and LN is challenging because of different efficacy endpoints, different background therapies and the effect of increased proteinuria in LN on the biomarker endpoints between the two diseases. Given the low prevalence of LN in children, there are limited data on proteinuria and serum albumin levels in this population, and collection of pharmacokinetic data in a small paediatric LN pharmacokinetic study would be required to validate the assumption that the proteinuria/albumin component of the popPK model extrapolates from adults to children with LN. However, the limited amount of paediatric proteinuria/albumin data available were broadly consistent with those of adults with LN.

Conclusions

Our model, which extrapolated belimumab efficacy and PK from adults with LN to children with LN, demonstrated that the dose level of IV belimumab 10 mg/kg, which is approved in adults with SLE and LN, and children with SLE, would also be appropriate for children (aged 5–17 years) with active LN. This analysis was the basis for the US Food and Drug Administration approval of belimumab in paediatric patients with LN aged 5–17 years [8], and represents an innovative approach to drug development to further the availability of novel treatments for children with serious conditions and a high unmet need.
