Biofloc system with synbiotic supplementation improves Penaeus vannamei immunity against AHPND
Authors: Magdalena Lenny Situmorang, Anggie Nur Fadillah, Atian Azizy, Gede Suantika
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractMass mortality due to disease emergence has resulted in enormous losses in shrimp production. One of the deadliest shrimp pathogens is the disease-causing Vibrio parahaemolyticus (VPAHPND), which induces acute hepatopancreatic necrosis and has recently become resistant to various classes of antibiotics. Biofloc systems and synbiotic feed supplementation are potential alternative strategies for overcoming this problem. In this study, a biofloc system was generated by inoculating the probiotic Halomonas alkaliphila, a consortium of nitrifying bacteria, and the microalgae Chaetoceros calcitrans with molasses at a C/N ratio of 15. The synbiotic-supplemented feed contained 0.375% (w/w) of prebiotic red seaweed Kappaphycus alvarezii powder, 0.125% (w/w) Spirulina sp., and freeze-dried H. alkaliphila powder at 109 CFU/kg feed. Over a 84-day culture period, the biofloc system with or without synbiotic supplementation resulted in more stable water quality and significantly increased shrimp total biomass and survival. After a V. parahaemolyticus challenge, the highest survival was found in the biofloc system with or without a synbiotic (70 ± 0%), similar to the semibatch system supplemented with antibiotic enrofloxacin orally at 60 mg/kg body weight after pathogen injection (73 ± 6%). Gene expression analysis revealed enhanced humoral immune responses, with upregulation of antimicrobial peptides (ALF-a, lyz) and melanization (proPO). These findings suggest that the application of a biofloc system with or without synbiotic feed supplementation could be a potential alternative to antibiotics, increasing disease resistance and modulating the immune response against potential AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus infection in P. vannamei growout culture.Key points• Bioflocs and synbiotics provide additional nutrients, promoting shrimp productivity• Bioflocs, with or without synbiotics, improve shrimp survival and humoral immunity• Bioflocs, with or without synbiotics, offer similar survival compared to enrofloxacinSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13587-y.Keywords: Biofloc, Synbiotic, Immune response, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Penaeus vannamei

Introduction

Global production in the aquaculture sector increased significantly to 122.6 million tons in 2020, with a total value of USD 281.5 billion. The Pacific whiteleg shrimp Penaeus vannamei is one of the main species used in aquaculture, accounting for 51.7% of global crustacean production, and its production volume reached 5.8 million tons in 2020. Currently, Indonesia is one of the largest suppliers of cultivated shrimp in the world (FAO 2022). However, shrimp farmers still face many problems, including low water quality and disease-related mortality. Acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (AHPND) is a common disease found in shrimp due to an increase in the prevalence of the pathogenic bacteria Vibrio parahaemolyticus, which contains binary toxin genes (pirA and pirB), a primary virulence factor (Dhar et al. 2019; Kumar et al. 2021; Mai et al. 2021; Tran et al. 2020), and is the most serious threat to shrimp farmers, particularly during the early growout phase, causing up to 100% mortality (Kumar et al. 2021). This problem occurs because the production systems used are still dominated by conventional systems, with poor water quality management, poor disease control, and biosecurity problems that cause unpredictable shrimp productivity (Suantika et al. 2018b, c).

Several strategies have been implemented to overcome this problem, including the use of antibiotics to control disease. However, the application of antibiotics in shrimp farming poses potential risks to aquatic ecosystems and human health due to their persistence, toxicity, and role in promoting antimicrobial resistance (Holmström et al. 2003). Several antibiotics, such as enrofloxacin, oxytetracycline, ciprofloxacin, erythromycin, and streptomycin, are commonly used in shrimp farming to control bacterial diseases (Leaño And Weimin 2020). Quinolone group are the antibiotics most frequently detected in aquatic products, particularly in intestinal samples, and enrofloxacin accounts for approximately 70% or more of these antibiotics (Leaño And Weimin 2020; Suseno et al. 2016). In Asia, 87% of shrimp production operations pose significant risks of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) development (Thornber et al. 2020). The greater the number of antibiotic farmers use, the less effective the antibiotics are at controlling disease because of the development of AMR, whereas the control of infectious diseases is a key factor for productivity and efficiency in aquaculture (Leaño And Weimin 2020). Therefore, an alternative is needed to replace the use of antibiotics to minimize the risk of AMR.

Potential strategies to solve this problem include the use of biofloc systems and feed supplemented with synbiotics. A biofloc is an aggregate of microorganisms (bacteria, microalgae, and protozoa), particles, colloids, and organic matter with high porosity (De Schryver et al. 2008). In biofloc systems, microorganisms are utilized to recycle nutrient waste, especially nitrogen, which contributes to the natural stabilization of microbial communities in water (Bossier And Ekasari 2017; Emerenciano et al. 2017, 2021). The benefits of biofloc systems include improving water quality, increasing shrimp growth, protecting against pathogenic Vibrio, serving as additional feed, providing an economical alternative to water exchange, and reducing water treatment expenses by 30% (Crab et al. 2010a, b, 2012; Kasan et al. 2021; Uawisetwathana et al. 2021).

Currently, formulating shrimp feed supplements with synbiotics to increase shrimp growth, survival, and disease resistance is the most popular approach in research (Chin et al. 2025; Khanjani et al. 2024; Phan et al. 2025; Situmorang et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2021). Synbiotics are combinations of probiotics and prebiotics that work synergistically, with the prebiotics selectively supporting probiotic organisms (Okey et al. 2018). Probiotics are live microorganisms that provide beneficial effects, including growth stimulation, modification of the intestinal microbiota, and increased immune response (Khanjani et al. 2024). Prebiotics are compounds that cannot be digested but can stimulate growth and activate the metabolism of beneficial bacteria in the gut, thereby improving the balance of the gut and host immune system (Khanjani et al. 2024; Okey et al. 2018).

Synbiotic supplementation with the probiotic Halomonas alkaliphila, the red seaweed Kappaphycus alvarezii as a prebiotic, and the microalgae Spirulina sp. can increase growth, survival, and resistance to Vibrio infection and enrich beneficial microbes in the intestine of shrimp (Situmorang et al. 2021). H. alkaliphila can hydrolyze carbohydrates that cannot be digested by K. alvarezii into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which stimulate the immune system. K. alvarezii contains bioactive ingredients, such as kappa-carrageenan, which functions as an immunostimulant for disease prevention and serves as an important protein building block that promotes growth (Mariot et al. 2021; Situmorang et al. 2021; Suantika et al. 2017, 2018a). Spirulina sp. are rich in proteins (50–70%), vitamins, minerals, essential fatty acids, and pigment antioxidants, which are essential for aquatic animals (Khalila et al. 2018). Our study aimed to determine the effects of the application of a biofloc system and supplementation with synbiotic feed consisting of the prebiotic red seaweed K. alvarezii, the microalgae Spirulina sp., and the probiotic H. alkaliphila on the growth, immune response, and survival of P. vannamei shrimp following V. parahaemolyticus infection.

Materials and methods

Microbial components of biofloc systems and synbiotic supplements

The experiment was conducted at the Laboratory of Aquatic Ecology, School of Life Sciences and Technology, Institut Teknologi Bandung, Indonesia. For the biofloc system, Halomonas alkaliphila strain HAL (A11D79), which was isolated from shrimp and deposited in the culture collection of the Institute of Technology Bandung Culture Collection (ITBCC), Indonesia, was acclimatized to 10 g/L salinity and then grown in Luria–Bertani medium (Oxoid, Indonesia) at 25 °C for 12 h (Situmorang et al. 2021). A nitrifying bacterial consortium (BioPure® Commercial Product, PT. Gapura Akua Kultiva, Indonesia) was acclimatized to 12 g/L salinity and then cultured in Winogradsky media (5 g/L) for 7 days (Suantika et al. 2018b). The diatom Chaetoceros calcitrans, obtained from the working collection of The Indonesian Institute of Sciences, Jakarta (WDCM collection no. 769), was cultured at 30 ppt to maintain optimum viable cells via F/2 Guillard media for 7 days (Suantika et al. 2018a). The same H. alkaliphila strain was also used for probiotic production as a synbiotic component. Commercial dried microalgae Spirulina sp. powder (CERES®, Indonesia) and prebiotic K. alvarezii powder (Helos®, PT. Gapura Akua Kultiva, Indonesia) were used directly without subculturing.

System preparation and operation

The experiment was conducted at the Laboratory of Aquatic Ecology, School of Life Sciences and Technology, Institut Teknologi Bandung, Indonesia. The study included four experimental groups: the biofloc system group (Group B), semibatch system group (Group C), and groups in which both the biofloc system and semibatch system were supplemented with commercial feed (Group K) and synbiotic feed (Group S). Each group was tested in three replicate tanks, and each tank was equipped with aeration lines and water heaters filled with 100 L of chlorinated diluted seawater (10 ppt). The seawater from Indramayu, West Java, Indonesia, was sterilized with sodium hypochlorite (Bayclin®, Indonesia) and then neutralized using sodium thiosulfate. In the semibatch systems (CK and CS), no microbial components or molasses were added to the culture water, and water renewal was performed daily to reach the optimal water quality parameters. The biofloc systems (BS and BK) were conditioned by adding 2% (v/v) nitrifying bacterial consortia of ammonium-oxidizing bacteria (AOB) and nitrifying-oxidizing bacteria (NOB) at a density of 106 CFU/mL, 0.1% (v/v) microalgae C. calcitrans at a density of 106 cells/mL, and 0.1% (v/v) H. alkaliphila at a density of 107 CFU/mL. In the biofloc system, molasses was added at a C/N ratio of 15:1, and the system was ready to use once the color of the culture water turned brown (Situmorang et al. 2021; Suantika et al. 2015, 2018b).

Synbiotic feed production and formulation

Synbiotic feed was produced through the addition of synbiotic products into the commercial feed (Feng Li®, Indonesia) containing 41% protein, 7% lipids, 3% fiber, 13% ash, and 10% water. In this study, synbiotic feed was prepared by adding 0.375% (w/w) of the prebiotic K. alvarezii (Helos®, PT. Gapura Akua Kultiva, Indonesia) and 0.125% (w/w) of Spirulina sp. (CERES®, Indonesia) to the commercial feed. Freeze-dried H. alkaliphila powder at 109 CFU/kg feed was then applied using a spray method with a sterile 0.85% (w/v) NaCl physiological solution as the solvent (10% w/v), ensuring uniform probiotic coating on the feed surface. This feed was supplied to the synbiotic feed treatment groups (CS and BS), while the commercial feed treatment groups (CK and BK) were fed 100% commercial feed without added synbiotics. The feed was stored in a dry place at room temperature (Situmorang et al. 2021).

Experimental setup and feeding test

For this experiment, specific pathogen free (SPF) shrimp with an average mean body weight of 0.13–0.20 g were obtained from PT. Suri Tani Pemuka Indramayu, West Java. The shrimp were acclimatized in a 100-L tank for 7 days from 25- to 10-ppt salinity at the Laboratory of Aquatic Ecology, School of Life Sciences and Technology, Institut Teknologi Bandung, Indonesia. The shrimp were stocked at a density of 500 PL/m3, which is referred to as a superintensive stocking density. The shrimp were fed five times a day at a rate of 10% body weight, and adjustments were made weekly on the basis of an estimation of the mean body weight, survival, and feeding rate over an 84-day growout period (Suantika et al. 2015, 2018b, c). During the experimental period, daily water renewal of 25–30% (v/v) was performed in the CK and CS groups to maintain optimal water quality parameters. New chlorinated diluted seawater (10 ppt) was added to the BK and BS culture tanks to compensate for water loss due to siphoning and evaporation. The addition of microbes and molasses to the biofloc system was performed every week to maintain water quality stability. Water physicochemical parameters, including pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO), total ammonium nitrogen (TAN), nitrite-N (NO2−-N), and nitrate–N (NO3−-N) levels, were monitored daily.

Proximate analysis

Analyses were performed at Saraswanti Indo Genetech (Bogor, Indonesia) to determine the ash content, calories from fat, total fat, moisture content, total calories, carbohydrate, and protein contents in the feed (synbiotic and commercial feed) and floc (BK and BS) samples. The protein content was measured using the Kjeldahl method, and the lipid content was measured using the Weibull method (Situmorang et al. 2020, 2021). The carbohydrate content was calculated by subtracting other elements from the total weight, and the ash content was measured by heating the samples to 600 °C and weighing until a constant weight was obtained (Situmorang et al. 2020).

Vibrio parahaemolyticus production and challenge test

The potential AHPND-causing Vibrio parahaemolyticus strain VPA was deposited in the culture collection of the Institute of Technology Bandung Culture Collection, Indonesia (ITBCC; A11D82). This V. parahaemolyticus strain VPA isolate was validated as a potential acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (AHPND)-causing strain in a study by Situmorang et al. (2022b) on the basis of a positive amplicon result using the AP3 method (Sirikharin et al. 2015). Sequence analysis of the PCR-amplified pirA (AP3) gene from the isolate revealed 99.7% identity match to the reference strain Vibrio parahaemolyticus TX-327 pirA gene, with complete CDS at GenBank accession no. MH410659.1 (Situmorang et al. 2022b).

The V. parahaemolyticus isolate was cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) medium (Oxoid, Indonesia) (salinity 10 ppt, 37 °C, 150 rpm agitation) for 12 h (activation 1). Then, 10% v/v of the culture was inoculated into the new TSB medium for 12 h (activation 2), and the working medium was prepared by inoculating 10% v/v of activation-2 culture into the new TSB medium for 1.5 h until it reached the log phase. After incubation, the density of the bacterial cultures was determined using a spectrophotometer (Genesys 20, Thermo Scientific™) at a wavelength of 501 nm. Precultured V. parahaemolyticus was harvested via centrifugation at 7200 × g for 20 min at room temperature. The bacterial pellet was subsequently resuspended in 10 mL of sterile 0.85% NaCl physiological solution. V. parahaemolyticus suspension was obtained at a final density of 5.8 × 105 CFU/ml (Situmorang et al. 2022b).

After 84 days of rearing, the shrimp were placed in a tank containing 10 L of culture water from each treatment at a density of ten shrimp/tank. Each tank was continuously aerated by using an aeration stone connected to an air pump. The shrimp challenge test was performed by injecting 20 μL of V. parahaemolyticus into the ventral sinus of the cephalothorax at a concentration of 1.16 × 104 CFU/shrimp, while the nonchallenge group was not injected. Shrimp were fed their respective feed for 27 h of incubation following bacterial injection. An additional group was established in which the antibiotic enrofloxacin was added to commercial feed at a dose of 60 mg/kg body weight (CA-chal), and the results from this group were compared with those of the other challenge groups in a semibatch system fed a commercial feed (CK-chal), a semibatch system fed synbiotic-supplemented feed (CS-chal), a biofloc system fed a commercial feed (BK-chal), and synbiotic-supplemented feed (BS-chal).

Dead shrimp were removed from the tanks and safely disposed of during the incubation period. Shrimp survival was measured as the percentage of shrimp alive on the last day relative to the total initial number of shrimp. After 27 h, two live shrimp from each replicate tank were collected, dissected to aseptically collect the shrimp gut, and rinsed with sterile 0.85% (w/v) NaCl physiological solution. Gut samples from two shrimp in the same tank were collected in a single microtube containing RNAlater Stabilization Solution (Invitrogen™). The pooled guts were then stored in a − 20 °C freezer prior to immune-related gene expression analysis. Each group consisted of three replicates for gene expression analysis (Situmorang et al. 2022b).

Shrimp immune‑related gene expression analysis

The expression of immune-related genes was analyzed in challenged and nonchallenged shrimp gut samples. First, RNA was extracted from 60–100-mg shrimp gut samples using the RiboZol™ RNA Extraction Reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA samples were then treated with 0.5 units/μg RQ1 RNase-free DNase (Promega) for 30 min at 37 °C to remove contaminating genomic DNA. The total RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop ND8000 spectrophotometer, and total RNA integrity was assessed using 1.2% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis with UV visualization (Supplementary File). Next, the total RNA was converted into cDNA using the GoScript™ RT System (A5003, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA samples obtained were then used as templates for qPCR using GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix. The specific genes related to targeted shrimp immunity include antilipopolysaccharide factor-a (ALF-a), prophenoloxidase (proPO), lysozyme (lyz), and crustin (crus), and the oligonucleotide primers used are shown in Table 1. Each sample was run in duplicate for each gene, and a negative control reaction was included for each primer set by omitting the template cDNA. The real-time qPCR protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing/extension at 60 °C for 60 s, and a melting cycle at 95 °C for 0.2 °C/s. The actin gene was used as an internal control and reference gene for RT‒qPCR data normalization (Silveira et al. 2018). The quantification cycle (Cq) was analyzed using Bio-Rad CFX Manager 2.1 software, and the relative fold change was calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCq) method (Livak And Schmittgen 2001). Genes with a fold change in gene expression of ≥ 2 and adjusted p < 0.05 were used to identify significant biological functions (Rosati et al. 2024).

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS® version 28.0. Following the 84-day culture period, data on growth (MBW, ADG, FCR, and total biomass) and survival (normalized with arcsin transformation) for all treatments were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test with 95% confidence intervals. To determine the significance of each main factor (biofloc system and synbiotic feed) and the possible interactive effects between the two factors on shrimp survival and total biomass, two-way ANOVA was performed. Proximate analysis of the feed and biofloc was performed using an independent sample t-test with 95% confidence intervals. Following the V. parahaemolyticus challenge, data on shrimp survival (normalized with arcsin transformation) and relative fold change of gene expression were analyzed via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test with 95% confidence intervals. Furthermore, to determine the significance of each main factor (biofloc system and synbiotic feed) and the possible interactive effect between the two factors on shrimp survival and relative fold change of gene expression after the challenge, two-way ANOVA was performed. Different letters for all statistics in this study represent significant differences between groups (p < 0.05).

Results

Water quality parameters

The physicochemical parameters measured over the 84-day culture period are presented in Table 2. The temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO), and pH in all treatments were within the optimal ranges for P. vannamei shrimp growth. DO, temperature, and pH stability were maintained with aeration, a water heater, and CaCO3 during the culture. The TAN and NO3-N contents in all treatments were below the tolerance limits but tended to be greater in the semibatch system. The NO2-N concentrations were below the tolerance limits and were more stable during cultivation in the BK and BS groups (Table 2). However, in the CK and CS groups, NO2-N content fluctuated, exceeding the tolerance range for P. vannamei growth, and the total water change was greater than that in the BK and BS groups.

Shrimp biological performance in the growout culture

After 84 days of culture, significant differences in the mean body weight (MBW), average daily growth (ADG), total biomass, and survival were found between the treatment groups (p < 0.05; Table 3). In contrast, there was no significant difference in the FCR among the groups (p > 0.05). The ADG and MBW in the CK group were significantly greater than those in the CS group (p < 0.05) but were not significantly different from those in the BK and BS groups (p > 0.05). However, shrimp survival in the CK group (48.5 ± 7.6%) was significantly lower than that in the BK (83.6 ± 7.9%), BS (83.0 ± 13.9%), and CS (77.6 ± 2.0%) groups (p < 0.05). The total biomass in the BK (560.51 ± 64.73 g) and BS (601.64 ± 106.77 g) groups was significantly greater than that in the CK group (333.56 ± 27.26 g) (p < 0.05) but not significantly different from that in the CS group (487.88 ± 20.28 g) (p > 0.05; Table 3). A significant main effect of the biofloc system on shrimp survival and total biomass (p < 0.001) was observed, with a synergistic interaction between the biofloc system and synbiotic feed (p < 0.05; Table 4).

Proximate compositions of the feed and biofloc samples

Based on the results of proximate analyses, it was found that the feed used in this study was within the optimal range for the growth of P. vannamei (Table 5). The total energy and protein content parameters were significantly greater in the commercial feed than in the synbiotic-supplemented feed; however, the water content parameter was significantly greater in the synbiotic-supplemented feed than in the commercial feed (p < 0.05). Compared with those in the BK group, the total energy and carbohydrate contents in the formed bioflocs in the BS group were significantly greater (p < 0.05; Table 5).

Shrimp survival performance in the challenge test with Vibrio parahaemolyticus

In all non-challenged groups, no shrimp mortality was observed. At 27 h post-injection, shrimp survival in the B groups (70 ± 0% for both BK-chal and BS-chal) was significantly greater than that in the CK-chal group (50 ± 10%). The application of synbiotic in semi batch system (CS-chal) resulted in similar survival (53 ± 12%) compared to commercial feed (CK-chal). Two-way ANOVA revealed that only the application of the biofloc system had a significant effect on shrimp survival after the V. parahaemolyticus challenge, with no synergistic interaction between the biofloc system and synbiotic feed (p < 0.01; Table 4). Interestingly, similar shrimp survival (73 ± 6%) was observed in the group provided with antibiotic-supplemented feed (CA-chal) compared with the biofloc system with or without synbiotic supplementation (BK-chal and BS-chal) (p > 0.05).

Shrimp immune‑related gene expression

Four immune-related genes, namely, antilipopolysaccharide factor-a (ALF-a), prophenoloxidase (proPO), lysozyme (lyz), and crustin (crus), were examined for gene expression responses in shrimp among different treatments in both the challenged and non-challenged groups. These genes were selected as candidate genes to determine the effects of the biofloc system and feed supplemented with synbiotic because they are molecular markers of humoral immune responses in shrimp (Situmorang et al. 2022b). In all non-challenged groups, there were no significant changes in the gene expression of ALF-a and lyz (p > 0.05), whereas the proPO and crus genes were significantly downregulated (p < 0.05, Fig. 1(A)). However, in the challenged group, there were no significant changes in the expression of ALF-a, proPO, and lyz (p > 0.05; Fig. 1(B)) when the semibatch system was fed a synbiotic-supplemented feed (CS-chal) compared with a commercial feed (CK-chal). The addition of antibiotics to the feed in the semibatch system (CA-chal) did not significantly (p > 0.05) increase the fold change in the expression of the ALF-a, proPO, and lyz genes (Fig. 1(B)). The application of the biofloc system with commercial feed (BK-chal) increased the expression of ALF-a (3.81-fold), pro-PO (3.87-fold), and lyz (2.87-fold) genes, although not significantly (p > 0.05) compared with commercial feed (CK-chal) (Fig. 1(B)). Synbiotic feed supplementation in the biofloc system (BS-chal) significantly increased gene expression levels (p < 0.05) of ALF-a (4.47-fold), proPO (6.60-fold), and lyz (6.02-fold) (Fig. 1(B)). In both challenged and non-challenged groups, the crustin gene was significantly downregulated in all treatment groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 1(A, B)). Two-way ANOVA revealed that the application of the biofloc system significantly affected the expression of the ALF-a and proPO genes (p < 0.01; Table 4), and the effect on the lyz and crus genes was even more significant (p < 0.001; Table 4). Discussion

The application of the biofloc system resulted in stability and improved water quality in this study, as indicated by the stable concentrations of total ammonium nitrogen (TAN), nitrite, and nitrate at the optimum range during shrimp cultivation with limited water exchange (Crab et al. 2010a; De Schryver et al. 2008; Kasan et al. 2021; Rajkumar et al. 2016). The total water exchange volume in the biofloc system was much lower than that in the semibatch system indicating higher water-use efficiency (Table 2). The decrease observed in nitrogen content might have been facilitated by the microorganisms added to the biofloc system, including the consortium of nitrifying bacteria, the heterotrophic bacteria H. alkaliphila, and the microalgae C. calcitrans. These microbes play important roles in nitrogen and organic matter reutilization (Emerenciano et al. 2017). Heterotrophic bacteria can take up nitrogen from accumulated organic matter and metabolize it into biomass, nitrifying bacteria can convert ammonia into nitrite and then nitrate, whereas microalgae can take up nitrate, increase the dissolved oxygen (DO) level, and act as a source of live feed for shrimp culture (Crab et al. 2012; Suantika et al. 2017, 2018b; Uawisetwathana et al. 2021). Thus, due to optimal water quality during cultivation, the survival, growth, and productivity performance of P. vannamei shrimp increased. This was also observed in the biofloc system (BK and BS), which presented significantly greater total biomass and survival rates than did the semibatch system (CK) after 84 days of culture (Table 3). Other studies have also shown that the application of biofloc systems can increase the growth and productivity of P. vannamei (Kumar et al. 2018, 2020; Lee et al. 2017).

The increased productivity performance in the biofloc system may also be related to the consumption of the biofloc by shrimp. As shown in this study, shrimp grow better in the presence of artificial feed and biofloc than in the presence of artificial feed alone (Khanjani et al. 2016, 2020; Kim et al. 2014; Uawisetwathana et al. 2021). The biofloc microbial community can act as a nutrient recycler and provide additional nutrients that promote growth (Hussain et al. 2021). Through the conversion of nutrients by microbes in the bioflocs, the flocs can provide a nutritious food source for shrimp, with additional proteins, lipids, minerals, vitamins, and micronutrients (Bossier And Ekasari 2017; Hussain et al. 2021; Khanjani et al. 2023). Several studies have shown that bioflocs are rich in bioactive compounds, such as carotenoids, chlorophylls, phytosterols, bromophenols, and amino sugars, which act as growth promoters (Khanjani et al. 2023; Kumar et al. 2018). In this study, the biofloc formed in the BK and BS groups contained protein, lipid, and carbohydrate at levels of 4.72–4.75 g, 0.21–0.22 g, and 1.96–2.65 g per 100 g of biofloc (wet weight), respectively.

Interestingly, two-way ANOVA suggested that the application of the biofloc system worked synergistically with synbiotic-supplemented feed to increase shrimp survival and productivity. The total energy and protein contents of the synbiotic-supplemented feed were lower than those of the commercial feed. However, synbiotic-supplemented feed in the semibatch system (CS) significantly increased shrimp survival after 84 days of culture. The high nutrient content of Spirulina sp. and K. alvarezii, as well as the modulation of the shrimp gut microbiota by H. alkaliphila, can increase digestive enzyme activity and nutrient absorption, which may positively impact shrimp growth and survival (Situmorang et al. 2021; 2022a). The higher content of total energy and carbohydrates in the biofloc formed by synbiotic-supplemented feed could also play a role in increasing shrimp growth. In both the C and B groups, the shrimp total biomass was greater with the use of synbiotic-supplemented feed than with the use of commercial feed, even though no significant differences were detected. The unfavorable water conditions in the semibatch system may have caused the shrimp to be unable to digest or utilize nutrients effectively. In the biofloc system, a positive effect of the synbiotic was not detected on total shrimp biomass, which was also indicated by the results of the two-way ANOVA, where only the biofloc system had a main effect on total shrimp biomass. As suggested earlier, an important factor contributing to the very high growth rates of shrimp within these biofloc systems was likely the endogenous production and availability of microbial food organisms (“floc”) for the shrimp, as a previous study further demonstrated that integrating biofloc technology with synbiotic-enriched diets stabilizes and diversifies the microbial community; modulates shrimp gut microbial activity; suppresses Vibrio growth in both culture water and the shrimp gut; and enhances survival, growth, and total shrimp biomass (Situmorang et al. 2022a).

Other studies have reported that synbiotic supplementation in shrimp improves host immunity and reduces disease-related mortality (Hussain et al. 2021; Khanjani et al. 2024; Okey et al. 2018). Disease is a major challenge in shrimp farming, one of which is AHPND caused by V. parahaemolyticus, a Gram-negative bacterium that causes acute infection and high mortality in shrimp (Kumar et al. 2021; Tran et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021). Vibrio spp. exist in the natural microbiota of the gastrointestinal tract of shrimp and can cause opportunistic infections under adverse culture conditions (Soonthornchai et al. 2015). V. parahaemolyticus may increase in number and virulence due to poor water quality and environmental suppression, and shrimp can easily become infected during physiological imbalance and nutritional deficiency (Hong et al. 2016; Situmorang et al. 2021). AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus is more likely to be found as a pirAB-positive isolate in shrimp than in water because the bacteria prefer to colonize the shrimp digestive tract and release binary toxins that induce cell necrosis and severe sloughing in the hepatopancreas tissue (Dhar And Mai 2021; Rortana et al. 2018). In non-challenged groups, no mortality was observed; therefore, the dominant factor affecting shrimp mortality in the challenge groups was assumed to be the potential AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus used in this study. At the end of the Vibrio challenge, the greatest percentage of surviving shrimp was found in the biofloc system with or without synbiotic.

There are several suggested mechanisms by which the biofloc system reduces mortality and improves performance against V. parahaemolyticus infection: (i) bioflocs can nutritionally modulate the health status of shrimp, as the bioflocs contain microbially bioactive components (MAMPs) that can enhance cellular defense mechanisms (Anand et al. 2017; Kumar et al. 2020); (ii) the surface of bioflocs can act as adsorption sites for the toxin produced by Vibrio (Kurniawan et al. 2020; Situmorang et al. 2022b); (iii) the biofloc system allows for decreased virulence in V. parahaemolyticus through a phenotypic switching mechanism (Kumar et al. 2020); (iv) the biofloc system enhances humoral components of the innate immune system by acting as an immunostimulant (Ekasari et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2014; Pilotto et al. 2020; Khanjani et al. 2023; Situmorang et al. 2022b); and (v) bioflocs can inhibit the quorum-sensing mechanism of Vibrio to express virulence genes (Crab et al. 2010b; De Schryver et al. 2008; Kumar et al. 2020). Quorum sensing is a cell-to-cell communication process involving signaling molecules between bacteria that regulate the expression of genes encoding toxins. Inhibiting this process requires interrupting communication through quorum quenching mechanisms that deactivate signaling molecules (De Schryver et al. 2008; Khanjani et al. 2023). The heterotrophic bacteria H. alkaliphila added to the biofloc system can produce lactonase, which hydrolyzes the N-acylhomoserin lactone (AHL) signaling molecule to block Vibrio quorum-sensing mechanisms (Romano et al. 2006; Situmorang et al. 2021, 2022a).

In this study, the use of synbiotic-supplemented feed did not have a significant effect on shrimp survival after the V. parahaemolyticus challenge test compared with the commercial feed, whether it was in a semibatch system (CS-chal compared with CK-chal) or a biofloc system (BS-chal compared with CK-chal). This observation is also supported by the results of two-way ANOVA, which suggested that only the biofloc system had a significant effect on shrimp survival after V. parahaemolyticus challenge. This result was not in accordance with a previous study, which reported that the addition of a synbiotic with the same composition increased shrimp survival at the nursery stage under V. harveyii challenge (Situmorang et al. 2021). The difference in performance might be due to the different stages of shrimp development, cultivation conditions, and production systems. Some studies have reported that synbiotics can produce supplementary nutrients and bioactive compounds such as bacteriocins, quorum quenching molecules, and peptides to support shrimp survival (Butt et al. 2021; Goh et al. 2022). Additionally, synbiotic supplementation promotes microbial cell proliferation, helping to maintain or restore the balance of the gastrointestinal microbiota against dysbiosis during V. parahaemolyticus infection, which may alleviate the impact of pathogenic infections (Chin et al. 2025). Interestingly, similar survival performance was obtained between antibiotic-supplemented (CA-chal) (73 ± 6%) and biofloc systems with or without synbiotic-supplemented feed (BK-chal and BS-chal) (70 ± 0%), indicating that the biofloc system can serve as an alternative to antibiotics in disease control and minimize the risk of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in shrimp farming. AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus can develop resistance to multiple drugs, highlighting the need for AMR monitoring and alternative treatments to minimize antibiotic dependence (Rortana et al. 2018).

The gut is an important site for the expression of immunity-related genes in shrimp, as it is a route for entry, colonization, and transmission of disease-causing pathogens (Silveira et al. 2018). Shrimp depend on the innate immune response, which consists of cellular and humoral components, and lack an adaptive immune system against disease. Humoral components include prophenoloxidase-activation systems, lysozyme, and antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) (Kulkarni et al. 2021; Tassanakajon et al. 2013; 2018). Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), including penaeidins, crustins, antilipopolysaccharide factors (ALFs), stylicins, and defensins, serve as the first line of defense against invading pathogens and play a role in regulating the host microbiota (Saucedo-Vázquez et al. 2022; Tassanakajon et al. 2013; 2018). ALF and lysozyme in P. vannamei have broad antimicrobial activity against Gram-negative bacteria, especially Vibrio spp. The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, which contains lipopolysaccharide (LPS), is targeted by ALF through the binding and neutralization of LPS, while lysozyme works by cleaving the β−1,4-glycosidic bonds in peptidoglycan, which causes bacterial cell lysis (Tassanakajon et al. 2013; 2018). The proPO-activating system is initiated by the recognition and binding of the PRPs to microbial cell wall components, then triggers an activation cascade that converts proPO into PO, leading to a process of melanization that can inhibit the growth of pathogens (Amparyup et al. 2013; Kulkarni et al. 2021).

Although the innate immune system is constitutively active in maintaining microbiota homeostasis and host defense, its effector components and immune signaling pathways can be further upregulated following pathogen infection (Lv et al. 2023; Paria et al. 2022; Tassanakajon et al. 2013). In this study, the increased expression of several genes in the V. parahaemolyticus challenged groups may play an important role in protecting the shrimp during infection. However, in the non-challenge groups, there was no significant upregulation in the expression of ALF-a and lyz genes, whereas proPO and crustin genes were significantly downregulated. This may be associated with the absence of pathogenic stimuli. Furthermore, the gene silencing of some of these genes, such as ALFs and crustin, will result in animals’ death without other stimuli because of dysbiosis (Li et al. 2019; Lv et al. 2023; Ponprateep et al. 2012), highlighting their essential role in microbiota regulation and immune homeostasis.

Several studies have reported that microbes associated with bioflocs can increase the expression of certain immune-related genes and reduce susceptibility to bacterial infections (Anand et al. 2017; Gustilatov et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2017; Situmorang et al. 2022b). Although synbiotic supplementation in the semibatch system (CS-chal) and the biofloc system without synbiotics (BK-chal) in the Vibrio-challenged group led to an increase in the expression of immune-related genes (ALF-a, lysozyme, and proPO), the differences were not significant. Notably, the significant upregulation of immune-related genes (ALF-a, lysozyme, and proPO) was observed in the biofloc system with the synbiotic-supplemented feed (BS-chal) group, suggesting an enhanced host immune status of shrimp to respond to Vibrio infection. The highest fold change was observed in ProPO gene (6.60-fold; Fig. 1(B)). The prophenoloxidase (ProPO) system plays an important role in pathogen defense through melanization, antimicrobial activity, and clearance of bacteria from the circulatory system after infection (Amparyup et al. 2013; Tassanakajon et al. 2013;2018). Recent findings by Sukonthamarn et al. (2024) further confirm the critical role of the proPO system in mediating hemocyte adhesion and encapsulation while limiting Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei infection, as its suppression leads to a reduced immune response and a significant increase in pathogen load. The elevated carbohydrate and energy content of bioflocs in the BS group may have influenced shrimp immunity independently of the synbiotic feed itself, potentially contributing to the upregulated expression of immune genes. Several studies have suggested that synbiotics can stimulate the immune system of shrimp (Hussain et al. 2021; Khanjani et al. 2024; Li et al. 2018; Okey et al. 2018; Situmorang et al. 2021).

The prebiotic K. alvararezii used in this study is an indigestible carbohydrate that can be selectively fermented by the added probiotic H. alkaliphila. These bacteria produce α-glucosidase enzymes that hydrolyze prebiotics into simple compounds that can be utilized by beneficial microbes in the intestinal tract to stimulate growth and activate metabolites that improve host health. These metabolites include short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which can stimulate immune system activation (Morrison & Preston 2016; Situmorang et al. 2021). The robust growth of beneficial bacteria in the digestive tract positively affects host health and negatively affects the colonization and/or replication of V. parahaemolyticus through direct bacterial antagonism (Caro et al. 2020). Several studies have also reported that kappa-carrageenan in the red seaweed K. alvararezii can stimulate the immune system and prevent Vibrio infection in shrimp (Mariot et al. 2021; Situmorang et al. 2021; Suantika et al. 2017, 2018a). This finding correlates with the observation that survival in the BK and BS groups after V. parahaemolyticus challenge was significantly higher than that in the control groups, which indicates that an increase in immune-related gene expression might play a crucial role in protecting against V. parahaemolyticus infection.

The downregulation of crustin gene expression was observed across all treatments during V. parahaemolyticus infection, which may be attributed to several mechanisms: (i) Crustin molecules are produced and stored in hemocytes (Saucedo-Vázquez et al. 2022) and may be released during infection, with regulation occurring through post-translational (maturation or trafficking) rather than transcriptional activation (Barreto et al. 2018). (ii) Crustin expression is time-dependent, reaching its highest peak at approximately 6 h and then decreasing after infection with V. parahaemolyticus (Xu et al. 2023). (iii) The infection route may also play a role, as Soonthornchai et al. (2010) reported increased crustin expression in shrimp infected via the natural route (immersion). In contrast, the use of direct hemocoel injection in our study may have altered tissue-specific immune responses, contributing to the lack of crustin expression (Barreto et al. 2018). These findings align with previous studies showing that crustin gene expression is also downregulated following infection with Vibrio alginolyticus (Vargas-Albores et al. 2004) and V. harveyi (Supungul et al. 2004; Barreto et al. 2018). The observed fold change in crustin expression was below 2 in BK-chal and BS-chal, which suggests limited biological relevance compared to other immune-related genes.

Interestingly, there was no increase in the expression of the ALF-a, proPO, lyz, or crus genes with antibiotic supplementation in the CA-chal group, even though shrimp survival significantly increased under V. parahaemolyticus infection. These findings suggest that enrofloxacin directly inhibits V. parahaemolyticus without inducing host immunity in shrimp via antimicrobial peptides and prophenoloxidase. By inhibiting DNA gyrase, enrofloxacin exhibits broad-spectrum activity against Gram-negative bacteria, which prevents DNA synthesis and replication (Flores-Miranda et al. 2012; Grabowski et al. 2022). Enrofloxacin can be used in shrimp farming because of its effectiveness against vibriosis, and it is usually mixed with feed pellets (Flores-Miranda et al. 2012).

In conclusion, this study showed that the application of a biofloc system with or without synbiotic-supplemented feed improved water quality, provided additional nutrients, and promoted shrimp culture performance (total biomass and survival). In addition, it stimulated shrimp immune response through humoral immunity, such as antimicrobial peptides (ALF-a and lyz) and the process of melanization (proPO), and increased the survival of shrimp after infection with potentially AHPND-causing V. parahaemolyticus. Overall, the application of the biofloc system had a more significant effect than did synbiotic feed supplementation on the growth performance, survival, and immune responses of the shrimp. Importantly, the biofloc system and synbiotic supplementation are alternative strategies that can reduce reliance on antibiotics for controlling infectious diseases, including AHPND caused by V. parahaemolyticus, leading to a more sustainable shrimp farming process. Further analyses via histopathology, metabolomics, and metagenomics are needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms of action of the biofloc system and synbiotic supplementation in increasing the growth and immune response of shrimp. This study was conducted at a small-scale under laboratory conditions. In the future, industrial-scale trials are needed to evaluate the biological performance, technical viability, and economic feasibility of biofloc systems and synbiotic supplementation in shrimp growout farming.
