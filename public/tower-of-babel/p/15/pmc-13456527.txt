Tough adhesive bilayer hydrogels
Authors: Elise Ansart, Nicolas Hesse, Junsoo Kim, David J Mooney, Benjamin R Freedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAdhesive hydrogels have the potential to address unmet clinical needs in the fields of wound closure, hemostasis, and soft-tissue reinforcement. However, commercial adhesives are limited by weak adhesion to internal organs and poor mechanical performance, especially in the fully swollen state. This study reports a bilayer hydrogel that combines an alginate-acrylamide double-network gel on one side and a highly entangled polyacrylamide gel on the other side to synergistically achieve robust mechanical and tissue-adhesive properties in the swollen state. Cohesion between both layers was achieved by controlling the diffusion of acrylamide between them during polymerization. The bilayer construct achieved stronger mechanical properties under tensile stress and higher performance in burst pressure tests than the double-network gel alone. The improved mechanical performance in the fully swollen state of bilayer hydrogels may expand the range of biomedical applications.

INTRODUCTION

Adhesive hydrogels are an active focus of biomedical research due to their potential to transform the standard of care for wound closure, tissue sealing, and drug delivery.1 Hydrogels can be designed with tunable shapes, application times, and in vivo degradability, making them suitable for minimally invasive procedures and viable candidates to augment or replace sutures and staples.2,3 Fibrin-based sealants are currently the only adhesives with FDA approval as a sealant, adhesive, and hemostat, but they suffer from poor mechanical properties and adhesion.4,5 Synthetic adhesives for wound closure, such as cyanoacrylates, demonstrate sufficient strength, along with rapid and strong adhesion to tissues, but suffer from low compatibility with wet environments.1 Other hydrogel formulations show significant limitations preventing their clinical translation; for example, mussel-inspired and nanoparticle-based adhesives have poor adhesion energy (<100 J.m−2), dropping even further upon swelling in physiological conditions.6,7 Cohesive failure of the adhesive leading to debonding under mechanical load is another limitation, with most hydrogels failing to achieve high toughness (<100 J.m−2).8 For comparison, cartilage tissues have a toughness close to 1,000 J.m−2 and an adhesion energy around 2,000 J.m−2.9,10

In contrast to adhesion strategies of commercial products, adhesion based on double-network (DN) hydrogels combined with stitching solutions has proved to be a promising candidate combining both high adhesion energies (>1,000 J.m−2) and high toughness (fracture energy > 8,000 J.m−2).11,12 In these studies, tough hydrogels are coated with a layer of polymer stitching solution, which diffuses into the adjacent tissue surface and into the gel to form strong adhesion through physical entanglements and electrostatic interactions.12 A tough adhesive formulation based on an acrylamide (Aam)-alginate (Alg) DN hydrogel combined with a chitosan stitching solution was successfully used for various biomedical purposes in preclinical studies, including tendon healing and drug delivery.13,14 However, their reduced modulus and strength in the fully swollen state limit the potential uses of DN gels as sealants.

Hydrogels achieving both high adhesion to tissues and strong mechanical performance in wet conditions would be appealing candidates for several clinical needs, such as achieving fast and robust hemostasis in the context of vascular and gastrointestinal wound healing. An ideal product should be able to withstand the pressure exerted by an excess of blood, strongly adhere to the targeted zone while promoting its healing, and prevent the formation of tissue adhesions. This latter requirement can be achieved if the material possesses an ultra-low-friction surface, to hinder the formation of fibrin fibers between the damaged area and surrounding tissues.

In this study, we investigate the design of a bilayer hydrogel (termed “BiGel”) with strong bulk properties in the fully swollen state and different surface properties on each side: strong adhesion to native tissues on one side and ultra-low friction for gliding on the opposite side. We propose a formulation based on the DN hydrogel for the first layer, owing to its excellent adhesion energy and toughness. As the second layer should be mechanically compatible with the DN gel to prevent delamination, we hypothesized that combining two tough matrices with similar chemistries and close swelling ratios would generate stable bilayer constructs with strong bulk properties but distinct surface chemistry and topology. To accomplish this, we synthesized BiGels with a DN gel as the adhesive layer and an Aam highly entangled gel (HE gel) for enhanced performance in the fully swollen state (Figure 1). The HE hydrogel demonstrated high toughness, low friction (friction coefficient of 0.0067, about six times lower than that of Teflon), and stronger mechanical properties than the DN gel in the fully swollen state, especially in terms of strength and stiffness.15 BiGels demonstrated higher strength, stiffness, and work of fracture than DN gels, all while maintaining stretchability and high adhesion energy on their DN side (>2000 J.m−2). BiGels also surpassed DN hydrogels in burst pressure tests, suggesting potential applications as surgical sealants.

Overview of the design for the multifunctional performance of BiGels

BiGels were proposed to demonstrate strong bulk mechanical properties originating from the high performance of each layer taken individually and the high interfacial cohesion between layers. The toughness of the HE hydrogel stems from the numerous entanglements between long, sparsely crosslinked Aam chains.15 The toughness of the DN hydrogel results from the synergistic actions of the Aam and Alg networks maintaining the network integrity and enabling energy dissipation during loading, respectively.11 BiGels were expected to maintain strong properties in the fully swollen state and combine high adhesion to native tissues (DN layer) and ultra-low friction (HE layer).

RESULTS

BiGels made from DN and HE hydrogels remain stable upon swelling

BiGels were synthesized by polymerizing an HE hydrogel on top of a DN gel, allowing for simultaneous crosslinking and diffusion of Aam across the interface (Figure 2A). The DN layer accounts for 66% of the final bilayer construct volume. DN gels exhibited a swelling ratio of 3.2, similar to that of HE gels (4.4; Figure 2B). No delamination was observed after the immersion of BiGels in cell culture medium for 24 h, and their swelling ratio was not significantly different than that of HE gels.

Cohesion of layers within BiGels is achieved by a bridging Aam network

In the synthesis of BiGels, Aam monomers were allowed to diffuse freely from the HE precursor solution into the DN layer before final UV crosslinking of the bilayer construct. We hypothesized that cohesion between the layers resulted from the formation of an Aam gradient at the interface between DN and HE hydrogels. After crosslinking, no demarcation line was grossly visible between the layers, as expected for a gradient-like structure. Confocal microscopy on transversal cryosections of BiGels (Figure 2C) confirmed that a bilayer structure was maintained, with clearly defined layers on each side. Both layers were distinguished, with the DN layer (in green) accounting for approximately 63% of the whole cryosection, consistent with the theoretical relative thickness of both layers (66% DN) (Figure 2D). The length of the Aam gradient zone at the interface (where red and green overlap) was approximately 17% of the cryosection’s width, corresponding to a characteristic Aam penetration length L of ~400 μm. This suggests that the characteristic diffusion time of Aam monomers and growing polymer chains during UV crosslinking was about 5 min, prior to final gelation of the HE layer.

The presence of an interfacial Aam gradient was further confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) elemental mapping. The oxygen map (Figure S1) showed increased oxygen on the DN side, indicating the presence of Alg. Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) analysis (Figure S2; Table S1) confirmed that BiGels presented different surface properties on each side, with some Alg characteristic peaks only observed on the DN side. In the nitrogen and carbon XPS maps, clear positive gradients were observed from the DN to the HE side (Figures 2E and S1), indicating increasing amounts of Aam and thus a gradient-like structure across the BiGel. By averaging the signal along the y axis on the extreme left and right of the map (at x0 = 0 mm and xmax = 5 mm), a nitrogen ratio of 3:1 was evaluated, representing the decrease in nitrogen and thus Aam from the HE to the DN side of the BiGel.

BiGels demonstrate robust mechanical properties under tensile stress

To determine performance under uniaxial tension, stress-strain curves were obtained for DN gels, HE gels, and BiGels in both the as-prepared (Figure S4) and fully swollen (Figure 3) states. Overall, the general tensile profile of BiGels was intermediate between those of HE and DN gels (Figure 3A), and no delamination was observed during testing. The strength of swollen BiGels was about three times higher than that of DN gels (60 ± 9 versus 21 ± 2 kPa; Figure 3B), and their stretch at failure was significantly higher than that of HE gels (10 ± 1 versus 2 ± 0.2; Figure 3C), similar to that of DN gels (11 ± 2 kPa). The average Young’s modulus of swollen BiGels was 17 ± 1 kPa, compared to 5 ± 1 kPa for the DN hydrogel (Figure 3D). Finally, BiGels demonstrated a higher work of fracture than HE and DN gels alone (346 versus 147 and 159 J.m−3, respectively; Figure 3E). The modulus and measured fracture properties of DN gels, HE gels, and BiGels significantly decreased upon swelling. However, the relative changes in performance ((propertyas-prepared – propertyswollen)/propertyas-prepared*100) were smaller for BiGels regarding maximum stress, stretch, and work of fracture (Figure S5).

BiGels maintain high adhesion to tissues on their DN side

Achieving strong adhesion to wet surfaces is a great challenge in the field of adhesive hydrogels. DN gels were selected as a layer for BiGels, as they can generate adhesion energies higher than 1000 J.m−2 with tissues when combined with a chitosan stitching solution.16 The stitching solution was spread on as-prepared gels before maintaining a gentle compression (<5 kPa) against the tissue for 10 min. Peeling tests on pig skin (Figure 4A) revealed extremely low adhesion with HE gels and the HE side of BiGels (Figure 4C). In contrast, the DN side of BiGels demonstrated high adhesion energy to pig skin even after swelling, comparable to that of the DN gel alone (>2000 J.m−2; Figure 4E). Both DN gels and the DN side of BiGels demonstrated cohesive failure during peeling, highlighting the strong adhesion generated by the stitching solution at the interface with the tissue. Additionally, no delamination of BiGels was observed, a sign of the strong interfacial cohesion between the DN and HE sides.

BiGels achieve high burst pressures that surpass DN hydrogels

As BiGels demonstrated more robust mechanical properties than DN hydrogels, along with strong adhesion to tissues, it was hypothesized that they would perform better in burst pressure tests. All gels reached burst pressures higher than 150 mmHg immediately after stitching, including HE gels and the HE side of BiGels (Figure 4D). Interfacial fracture was observed in 100% of cases for HE gels and BiGels, while DN gels exclusively showed cohesive fracture (Figure S6). After swelling, the burst pressure fell to almost 0 mmHg for the HE gel (Figure 4F), while it was maintained at high levels for the DN gels and BiGels. Swollen BiGels achieved higher burst pressures on their DN side than DN gels (233 ± 27 versus 134 ± 30 mmHg, p < 0.001). Burst occurred through interfacial fracture after 35 s on average for BiGels, as opposed to cohesive fracture after only 7 s for DN gels (Figure S6).

BiGels are cytocompatible

Unreacted Aam monomers are cytotoxic and could remain trapped in BiGels’ network after polymerization, thereby posing a toxicity risk. As a first demonstration of biocompatibility, conditioned medium was collected from BiGels washed immediately after synthesis and immersed in cell culture medium. The conditioned medium was then used to incubate fibroblast cells before carrying out a WST assay to assess cell viability after 24 h (Figure 5). All conditions showed negligible differences from the negative control (incubation in unconditioned medium).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the design of adhesive BiGels to achieve enhanced adhesion and physical properties. Two tough polymer matrices were combined by synthesizing stable bilayer constructs with high interfacial cohesion. BiGels demonstrated strong mechanical properties and high adhesion energies in the fully swollen state. They also surpassed DN hydrogels in peak burst pressures, making them potential candidates as surgical sealants.

We first hypothesized that strong cohesion between layers of BiGels could result from the formation of a bridging Aam network between them. The characteristic diffusion length of Aam monomers through the DN layer estimated from confocal images was in agreement with predictive calculations based on a simple diffusion model in water. Several chemical analyses confirmed that Aam monomers formed a bridging network across about a quarter of the DN gel thickness. The thickness of the gradient zone can likely be tuned, if desired, by controlling polymerization kinetics via manipulation of the UV power.

Of particular importance for the performance of hydrogels in physiologic conditions, BiGels achieved higher strength, stiffness, and work of fracture than DN hydrogels in the fully swollen state, all while maintaining high stretchability. We speculate that these properties stem from physical entanglements between the layers, with the Aam gradient ensuring BiGel’s interfacial cohesion and improved bulk mechanics. The Young’s modulus of swollen BiGels (17 kPa) was lower than that predicted following the rule of mixture (89 kPa). As stiffness decreases with monomer concentration,15 this could be due to dilution of the HE layer resulting from diffusion of monomers and polymers during the synthesis of BiGels. Finally, the higher work of fracture of BiGels likely resulted from a trade-off between strength and maximum stretch compared to HE and DN hydrogels. The mechanical performance of BiGels was also higher compared to previously developed bilayer formulations.17–21 Indeed, most bilayer constructs have been designed as scaffolds for tissue engineering, especially collagen-based materials that mimic the multi-layered structure of the skin. Bilayer dressings for skin wound healing have also been designed with an upper layer to prevent dehydration and infection (anti-bacterial properties) and a lower layer to promote cell proliferation and angiogenesis.22 Among these hydrogels, the few that document adhesive properties suffer from low adhesion energy (<100 J.m−2) and toughness (<100 J.m−2), making them susceptible to debonding, especially in wet conditions.17–21 In this study, BiGels combined both strong mechanical properties and high adhesion energy in the fully swollen state, making them promising candidates for applications in fluid environments.

Not only did BiGels surpass DN hydrogels in burst pressure, but they also reached values exceeding normal arterial blood pressure values in humans (80–120 mmHg). BiGels also performed better than some commercially available surgical sealants and other hydrogels developed for gastrointestinal applications.17,23,24 Overall, burst pressure values in intestines in this study are slightly lower than previously reported results for DN gels in the pericardium.12 This is attributed to differences in the surface chemistry and hydration level of tissues.25 Interestingly, the HE gel and the HE side of BiGels did not fail immediately upon starting the burst pressure test. Their higher stiffness, combined with the absorption of water from the wet tissue into the as-prepared gel, could have helped to prevent cohesive and interfacial fractures. These results differ from those of the peel tests, but some adhesives present high burst strength and low adhesion toughness.26 Upon swelling, the burst pressure of HE gels fell to zero. This result could be due to the huge drop in adhesion energy caused by dangling chains at the surface of the HE gel, which retain water molecules upon swelling and can hinder the diffusion of chitosan chains through the gel.27 Surprisingly, the HE side of BiGels still reached high burst pressure values. This may be linked to the dilution of the HE layer within the bilayer structure, easing the diffusion of chitosan chains and thus generating stronger adhesion.27

Consistent with previous works, the gel formulation used in this study demonstrated excellent biocompatibility based on in vitro tests. Furthermore, Alg, chitosan, and Aam are already approved by the FDA for both internal and external uses. Chitosan and Alg have been used in wound dressings (ChitoFlex [Tricol Biomedicak] and Algicell [Derma Sciences]), and Aam is used as a filler material in urinary incontinence (Bulkamid). Tunable degradability can also be achieved for BiGels by selecting degradable crosslinkers for the Aam networks and changing the oxidation state of the Alg.28

In summary, we developed a straightforward fabrication protocol for bilayer structures, which can be adapted to a variety of hydrogel precursor solutions with different viscosities that form networks through radical-based polymerization. Interfacial cohesion between both layers can be controlled by manipulating monomer diffusion between them, resulting in the formation of a bridging polymer network at the interface. BiGels could thus be designed for a range of biomedical applications using this technique.

METHODS

DN hydrogel synthesis

DN hydrogels were synthesized by mixing a syringe containing a 14 mL solution of 2 wt % Alg (FMC BioPolymer, LF10/60) and 12 wt % Aam (Sigma, A8887) in HBSS (14175–095, Gibco), 50.4 μL of 2 wt % N,Nʹ-methylenebis(acrylamide) (Sigma, M7279), and 11.2 μL of tetramethylethylenediamine (Sigma, T7024), with a second syringe containing 316.4 μL of 6.6 wt % ammonium persulfate (Sigma, A9164) and 267.4 μL of 0.75M calcium sulfate dihydrate (Sigma, 31221). The gel was injected into a laser-cut acrylic spacer (1 Kern 150 W Laser Cutter) placed between two borosilicate glass sheets (110 × 15 × 1.5 mm3) and left to crosslink in a sealed plastic bag overnight.

HE hydrogel synthesis

The HE precursor solution was prepared following a previously described procedure15 by mixing Aam (66 wt % in MilliQ water) with MBAA (2 wt % in PBS 1X) and Irgacure 2959 (0.1 M in 100% ethanol, Sigma, 410896). The water-to-monomer and crosslinker-to-monomer molar ratios W and C were set at 2 and 3.10−5, respectively, with the initiator-to- crosslinker ratio I/C set at 0.4. The precursor solution was prepared at 80°C to prevent Aam crystallization, vortexed, and sonicated for 2 min. It was poured onto a glass sheet, within a rubber spacer with adequate dimensions. Another glass sheet was used as a cover, and the mold was sealed with binder clips. UV light (CL-3000L, Analytik Jena) was irradiated for 60 min at an intensity of 6 mW.cm−2, as measured by a UV light meter (UV513AB, General).

BiGel synthesis

BiGels were prepared using a three-step protocol. First, the DN layer was prepared within an acrylic spacer (75 × 40 × 1.5 mm3) following the above protocol. Secondly, the HE precursor solution was prepared and poured within a rubber spacer with dimensions matching those of the DN gel (72 × 37 × 0.8 mm3). Finally, the mold containing the DN gel was used as a cover to seal the HE solution before UV irradiation for 90 min at 10 mW.cm−2. In practice, UV power was controlled by adjusting the distance between the mold and the light bulbs.

Hydrogel storage and swelling

As-prepared hydrogels were stored in sealed plastic bags at 4°C. Fully swollen samples were obtained after immersion in DMEM (10569–010, Gibco) for 24 h at 37°C. The swelling ratio of a gel R was defined as its weight in the fully swollen state divided by its weight in the as-prepared state. The thickness of a fully swollen sample was calculated by multiplying R1/3 by the as-prepared thickness.

Confocal imaging of hydrogels’ cryosections

Fluorescent BiGels were obtained by adding 6 wt % of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated Alg to DN gels and preparing an HE precursor solution with 30 μM of Nile Blue Aam (25395, Polysciences). Punched samples (8 mm diameter) were embedded in OCT compound (4583, Tissue-Tek), frozen in liquid nitrogen, and cut into 60-μm-thick transversal cryosections (cryotome CM1950, Leica). Sections were washed and mounted before imaging using a confocal microscope (LSM710, Zeiss). Laser excitation was set at 488 nm for FITC and 633 nm for Nile Blue Aam.

XPS elemental mapping of hydrogels’ cryosections

XPS elemental analysis was performed on transversal BiGels cryosections (K-Alpha+, Thermo Fisher Scientific). They were scanned across their width for nitrogen, carbon, and oxygen.

Tensile testing

Rectangular hydrogel samples (gel thickness × 20 × 5 mm3) were stretched to failure at 100 mm.min−1 using a 3342 Instron with a 50 N load cell, recording force and extension. Maximum stretch, maximum stress, stiffness (extracted from a second-order polynomial fit of the data), and work of fracture (defined as the area under the curve) were extracted from the stress-strain curves.

Uniaxial compressive testing

Uniaxial compressive testing was performed on hydrogels using a Bose ElectroForce 3200 (50 g load cell). Hydrogel disks (8 mm diameter) coated with mineral oil to prevent dehydration were preloaded (1 g) before applying a 10% compressive strain at a rate of 5% strain per second. Load was recorded as a function of time (Wintest v.7), while strain was held constant for 300 s. The amplitude of stress relaxation was defined as the relative difference in stress between the peak force (immediately after reaching 10% strain) and the equilibrium force (after 300 s). The characteristic half-time of stress relaxation t1/2 was defined as the time needed to reach a force equal to half the difference between the peak force and the equilibrium force.

Adhesion energy measurement

Adhesion energy was measured using T-peeling tests under uniaxial tension at a loading rate of 100 mm.min−1 on a 3342 Instron with a 50 N load cell. Pig skin was purchased from Sierra for Medical Science and stored at −20°C. Strips were cut (75 × 11 mm2) using a surgical blade and kept hydrated with HBSS. To generate adhesion, an ultrapure chitosan solution (2 wt % in MilliQ water, 54046, HMC, pH 5.5) was applied to the surface of a gel (50 μL.cm−2) and compressed to tissue surfaces at 10% strain for 10 min16 The back of each gel was bonded to a rigid polyethylene terephthalate film with cyanoacrylate (Krazy Glue) to prevent plastic deformation. This ensures that all the work done by the machine is equal to the energy dissipated at the crack tip. The adhesion energy of HE gels was derived from the average force recorded during the stationary peeling regime. Peeling profiles of DN gels showed a succession of peaks marking cohesive rupture of the gel. Peak forces were averaged, and the adhesion energy was calculated as twice this average force divided by the width of a sample.

Burst pressure tests

Pig intestines (0.5–1 mm thickness) were purchased from Sierra for Medical Science and cut into circles with a 2-mm-diameter hole punched in the center. A circular hydrogel (17 mm diameter, 2.3 mm thickness) was adhered to the mucosal side of the tissue using the same chitosan solution as in peeling tests. PBS 1× was pumped from the bottom of the specimen at a rate of 10 mL/min (Harvard Apparatus PHD 2000 Dual Syringe Pump). During the test, pressure was recorded using a monitor. The maximum pressure before cohesive or interfacial fracture of the gel was identified as the burst pressure.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The WST assay is a cytotoxicity assay comparable to the MTT assay. Conditioned media were collected by incubating washed hydrogel stamps (6 mm diameter) in 200 μL complete culture medium for 24 h at 37°C while shaking. The WST assay (Millipore) was performed per the manufacturer’s instructions after seeding 5,000 3T3 cells/well in 100 μL complete culture medium in 96-well plates for 24 h (37°C, 5% CO2), before changing to 100 μL conditioned medium for 24 h (37°C, 5% CO2) prior to the assay. 10 μL Cell Proliferation Reagent WST-1 was then added, and the plate was incubated for 4 h (37°C, 5% CO2). The plates were shaken thoroughly before measuring the absorbance at 480 nm using a BioTek plate reader.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (SPSS). Significant differences among hydrogels across all properties assessed were evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test for both as-prepared and fully swollen samples. Significant factors were evaluated using post hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered significant for p ≤ 0.05. Statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends.
