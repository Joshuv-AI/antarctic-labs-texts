Engaging end-users to develop a novel algorithm to process electronic medication adherence monitoring device data
Authors: Meghan E McGrady, Kevin A Hommel, Constance A Mara, Gabriella Breen, Michal Kouril
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo engage end-users to develop and evaluate an algorithm to convert electronic adherence monitoring device (EAMD) output into the adherence data required for analyses.Materials and MethodsThis study included 4 phases. First, process mapping interviews and focus groups were conducted to identify rules for EAMD data processing and user needs. Second, algorithm parameters required to compute daily adherence values were defined and coded in an R package (OncMAP). Third, algorithm-produced data were compared to manually recoded data to evaluate the algorithm’s sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. Finally, pilot testing was conducted to obtain feedback on the perceived value/benefit of the algorithm and features that should be considered during software development.ResultsEAMD data processing rules were identified and coded in an R application. The algorithm correctly classified all complete observations with 100% sensitivity and specificity. The receiver operating characteristic curve analysis yielded an area under the curve of 1.00. All pilot testing participants expressed interest in using the algorithm (Net Promoter Score = 71%) but identified several features essential for inclusion in the software package to ensure widespread adoption.DiscussionThe decision rules implemented to process EAMD actuation data can be parameterized to develop an algorithm to automate this process. The algorithm demonstrated high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. End-users were enthusiastic about the product and provided insights to inform the development of a software package including the algorithm.ConclusionA rule-based algorithm can accurately process EAMD actuation data and has the potential to improve the rigor and pace of adherence science.Keywords: algorithms, medication adherence, patient compliance, electronic adherence monitoring device

Background and significance

Millions of people worldwide rely on prescription medication to manage a health condition, and in the United States, over 20% of children and 60% of adults are taking a prescription medication.1,2 Medication adherence, defined as medication-taking behavior that aligns with the agreed-upon treatment protocol,3 is critical for realizing the benefits of prescription medications. Over 15% of prescriptions, however, are never filled4–6 and an estimated 30%-50% of individuals do not take their medication as prescribed.7 This widespread non-adherence contributes to poor health outcomes and medication non-adherence is a primary cause of treatment failure, accounting for thousands of deaths and billions of dollars in avoidable healthcare costs in the United States each year.8,9

Medication adherence can be improved with behavioral intervention,10 and the World Health Organization has posited that targeting non-adherence may have a greater impact on health outcomes than any future improvement in medical treatment.11 A critical component of supporting adherence is accurate and efficient measurement. Medication adherence can be assessed using electronic adherence monitoring devices (EAMDs),12 pill bottles or boxes that contain a computer chip that records the date and time of each opening (or “actuation”). EAMDs are more accurate than self-report measures which overestimate adherence10 and track day-to-day medication-taking not captured by other measures (eg, pill count).13 As a result, EAMDs are a preferred measure of medication adherence13–16 and recommended for use in clinical trials.16–19

Despite their strengths, EAMDs produce actuations, or a series of date- and time-stamps, that are not inherently meaningful. Before researchers can use EAMD data, they must apply a series of decision rules to transform actuation data into adherence data.20 A few proprietary software packages can execute some of these decision rules,21 but their algorithms operate according to several assumptions regarding the medication regimen (eg, medication was never held, medication regimen never changed), patient’s schedule (eg, patient’s 24-hour day starts at midnight and ends at 11:59:59 pm), and EAMD use (eg, patient was never hospitalized). To our knowledge, current proprietary software packages do not account for violations in these assumptions. For example, some packages cannot account for medication holds or changes and others do not allow the 24-hour day to start at a time other than midnight (eg, to account for shift workers with different daily schedules). Any time these assumptions are not true, the computed adherence data are inaccurate. Historically, the only strategy to account for violations in these assumptions has been to manually re-code actuation data into adherence data. This process, however, is unstandardized, prone to human error, and can take several hours per patient. To use EAMDs, thus, researchers must choose between inaccurate software or burdensome and error-prone manual re-coding for data transformation.

Objective

An essential next step is to create an algorithm that automates the transformation of EAMD actuations into a research-ready dataset of daily adherence values. For adherence scientists, a novel algorithm could support accurate and efficient EAMD data processing, advancing the rigor and pace of both observational research investigating the drivers of non-adherence and clinical trials evaluating the effectiveness and efficacy of adherence-promotion interventions. For clinical trialists, a novel algorithm could reduce the specialized expertise needed for EAMD use, making it easier to integrate EAMDs and obtain the nuanced data critical to understand why a novel agent is “failing” or producing side effects. This manuscript reports on the multi-phase development and validation of a novel algorithm to meet these needs, and describes the definition, coding, validation, and pilot testing of the algorithm.

Materials and methods

This study included 4 phases. Phase I included individual process mapping interviews and focus groups with likely end-users to identify rules for EAMD processing and user needs. In Phase II, findings from Phase I were used to define algorithm decision rules and develop code for executing these rules. In Phase III, the accuracy of the algorithm was evaluated. Finally, in Phase IV, new likely end-users piloted the algorithm and provided feedback on modifications. Study procedures were approved by the Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center Institutional Review Board.

Participants

Individuals were eligible to participate in Phase I or IV if they had first-hand experience with EAMD data and had been employed as a Principal Investigator, Clinical Research Coordinator, or Biostatistician. Potentially eligible participants were identified from an NIH RePORTER search and a Society of Pediatric Psychology Adherence Special Interest Group inquiry and provided a study flyer via email. In Phase I, 5 eligible individuals expressed interest and provided consent (enrollment rate = 100%). In Phase IV, 7 eligible individuals expressed interest and provided consent (enrollment rate = 100%). Consistent with the principle of saturation in qualitative research,22 recruitment was ended at n = 5 for Phase I as no new decision rules were identified by the fifth participant and at n = 7 for Phase IV as no new software requirements were identified by the seventh participant.

Procedures and analyses

Phase I: Process mapping and focus groups

Phase I began with a 1:1 process mapping23 visit in which each participant walked through the steps they use to process EAMD data. Following process mapping visits, an initial list of decision rules representing the steps involved in EAMD processing was created. Then, Phase I participants engaged in 5 focus groups. During the first focus group, each decision rule was presented and refined with feedback until consensus was achieved. Algorithm coding began following the first focus group and the current algorithm was presented at subsequent focus groups along with clarifying questions (eg, “Are these the values that you want to use to calculate adherence?”). The average study visit lasted 39 minutes (SD = 7; Range = 28-45) for process mapping visits and 46 minutes (SD = 6; Range = 39-54) for focus groups. Procedures were conducted via Zoom. Interviews and focus groups were recorded and transcribed and data were analyzed in NVivo24 using thematic analysis.25

Phase II: Algorithm development

Algorithm design requirements were identified from Phase I feedback and used to develop decision rules. Next, an R package (OncMAP = Oncology Medication Adherence Processor) was developed including 3 primary components. The first component included code to input EAMD actuation data from commonly used devices identified in Phase I. It was possible to design the algorithm to accommodate multiple EAMDs as all export a data table that includes one actuation data point (with a date- and time-stamp of the dose opening) per row.21 The second component included parameters defining the medication regimen (ie, number of periods per day, prescribed doses per period, and days per week with doses prescribed), patient information (ie, 24-hour day start, time zone), and periods of EAMD non-use (eg, medication holds, hospitalizations). Including this component enables the definition of new regimens to be independent of the processing code itself, allowing the algorithm to be compatible with any medication regimen. The final component included code to synthesize input EAMD actuation data and medication regimen parameters to compute daily adherence values. The OncMAP R package uses a limited set of R libraries (readr, dplyr, lubridate, zoo, readxl) and was verified to run on multiple platforms including Windows, Mac, and Linux. R development utilized industry standard processes including version tracking, unit testing, and coverage testing. R development followed an iterative process including walkthrough sessions with the interdisciplinary team. A complementary R Shiny application was also developed to facilitate testing of individual OncMAP R package functions and obtain feedback to inform feature selection for a future comprehensive software package (Supplemental File III).

Phase III: Algorithm validation

The algorithm was validated against 8986 daily EAMD datapoints from 38 patients with cancer prescribed 1 of 10 different medication regimens. Details regarding data collection methods and participants have been previously published.26 To prepare the data validation database, 2 research coordinators independently and manually re-coded EAMD actuation data into 2 daily adherence values (per Table 1) according to the decision rules specified by the algorithm. A third member of the study team compared results. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion with the Principal Investigator and consultation with the original data source and decision rules.

Two sets of analyses were conducted to evaluate the accuracy of the algorithm. The first included receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses to assess the performance of the algorithm in classifying each of the 2 daily adherence values. For these analyses, the reference variable in the gold standard database indicated the true state of each daily value (0 = nonadherent, 1 = adherent). During non-monitored periods, or periods during which the EAMD was not used for medication administration (eg, medication holds, hospitalizations), daily values were “missing.” Because ROC analyses evaluate classifier performance across all possible thresholds based on observed (non-missing) data, these analyses were conducted on the subset of 8519 non-missing daily values. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated as a summary measure of classification performance alongside sensitivity and specificity for each of the 2 daily adherence values. The second set of analyses involved cross-tabulations on the entire dataset (n = 8986 daily values), including records marked as “missing” due to nonmonitored periods, to obtain a more detailed understanding of algorithm performance. These cross-tabulations identified exact counts and proportions of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, and discrepancies in missing data handling. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.

Phase IV: Algorithm pilot testing

In Phase IV, 7 individuals who did not participate in Phase I completed one study visit via Zoom in which they provided feedback on the algorithm and user interface. During the visit, participants were provided with a sample EAMD data file and a link to the user interface and asked to share their screen as they uploaded and processed the sample file using the algorithm. Following an active listening think-aloud protocol,27 participants were asked to “think aloud” and verbalize their thoughts while completing data processing. Study staff provided verbal affirmations and feedback tokens throughout.28

After completing the think-aloud task, participants completed a demographics form and self-report measures. To assess user satisfaction, participants rated 5 investigator-created items regarding the algorithm’s effectiveness and potential to save time, reduce resources, produce accurate data, and produce reproducible data on a scale of 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”) (Table 3). Using this same scale, participants were asked to rate the degree to which they would be interested in using this software in their lab (Table 3). The number and percentage of participants endorsing each response option for each item were calculated. Because research tools like the algorithm are often disseminated by colleagues sharing positive word-of-mouth, a Net Promoter Score was calculated to evaluate self-reported likelihood-to-recommend to others.29,30 Consistent with published guidelines, participants rated their “likelihood to recommend” the algorithm on a 0 (“not likely at all”) to 10 (“extremely likely”) scale.29 Based on their responses, participants were classified as “detractors” (rating = 0-6), “passives” (rating = 7-8), or “promoters” (rating = 9-10) and a Net Promoter Score (range = -100 to 100) was calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promoters.29

The R Shiny application was developed to facilitate testing of individual OncMAP R package functions and serve as a prototype to solicit feedback to inform future software development efforts. Frequencies were used to summarize item-level data for other measures. Phase I results identified design requirements related to the EAMD data inputs, participant data inputs, desired outputs, and decision rules. Participants reported using a variety of EAMDs and indicated a desire for the algorithm to be flexible enough to accept data from multiple EAMD types. Consistent with guidelines for EAMD data processing,34 all participants compute daily adherence values which they then synthesize across study-specific time-periods to compute overall adherence values. As a result, all participants defined daily adherence variables as the desired outputs of the algorithm.

We go through and manually calculate adherence…based on the full day’s dosing.

The computation of daily adherence values begins by creating a dataset that includes one row for each day. This decision rule and all subsequent rules are also detailed in Supplemental File I alongside a worked example.

We would start with days…we would have that column. We would have a date column.

Then, a decision is made regarding the patient’s 24-hour day start time. EAMDs default to starting each day at midnight. While this may be appropriate for many patients, it can be problematic for those on shifted sleep schedules (eg, young adults, shift workers) who take their “evening” or “bedtime” dose after midnight. To ensure these patients are not counted as non-adherent for days in which their dose occurs shortly after midnight, some research teams shifted the 24-hour day start time.

For our teenagers because [EAMD] likes to cut off at 12 or something…if they took a medication like at…2 in the morning…you’d have to make sure that like that 2:00 AM dose didn’t apply to the day that it was actually taken that it was actually the night before.

We set the start time…like 3-4 in the morning…to…capture the…late to bed folks…so that we don’t get like something weird at midnight.

Next, EAMD actuation data are manually added to a new column and used to compute the number of openings per day.

We would export that raw data um and it would just list out um…the time and date stamp for each opening, and so what we would do is having that template of…these…columns we would have to painstakingly kind of manually kind of alter those times and make sure…it occurred on the same line.

Then, columns for patient data are added. The patient’s medication prescription is indicated with a daily indicator of doses prescribed. This value may differ day-to-day as some patients are prescribed medications that are only taken on certain days of the week (eg, on Saturdays and Sundays only).

The medication, the times per day.

Because patients may not use the EAMD for medication administration on all days of the study, a dichotomous indicator of whether the period was considered “non-monitored” is also included. Research teams defined non-monitored periods differently and may use this indication if a medication was held, the patient was hospitalized, and/or the patient was not using the EAMD.

I had access to all of the oncology notes…so I would just do like a quick peruse…of their recent notes and say like “Okay. So, it seems like it was paused for this week because they were taking Vincristine, let’s say. Um so, in that regard, I just mark in that larger Excel spreadsheet like on hold like medication on hold.”

We’ll have somebody say, “Oh! I was traveling to [country] and I did not wanna take my whole bottle with me.”…We can code like when the bottle was not-well like…collecting data.

Finally, these variables are used to compute 2 adherence values per period (default = 24-hour day). As defined in Table 1, the first value is a dichotomous indicator of whether the number of openings equals or exceeds the number of prescribed doses on monitored days (“dose taken”). The second value is a dichotomous indicator of whether the number of openings equals the number of prescribed doses on monitored days (“correct dose taken”). To reflect periods during which participants are not using EAMDs for medication administration (eg, hospitalizations, medication holds), daily adherence values are “missing” during non-monitored periods. While daily variables were identified as preferred outputs, end-users are also provided with options to compute common adherence summary statistics as detailed in Supplemental File II.

Phase II: Algorithm development

The algorithm includes 3 stages of processing as illustrated in Figure 1 and Supplemental File II. Users begin by uploading a raw .csv file containing EAMD actuations (Input I). As Phase I participants indicated the most commonly used EAMDs were eCap Smart Bottle Caps (Information Mediary Corp), MEMS® Cap Smart Pill Bottles (AARDEX Group), SimpleMed+ pill boxes (Vaica Medical Ltd), and Aidia Smart Bottles (AdhereTech), the algorithm accepts .csv files from each of these devices. In processing Stage I, the input file is evaluated against these available EAMD formats to find a match. Then, actuations are processed into a standardized format (Output I).

Algorithm processing workflow.

Next, user-supplied parameters regarding the patient’s information, medication regimen, and non-monitored periods are entered (Input II). Default parameters were selected based on Phase I feedback and are as follows: patient information default = 24-hour day begins at midnight; medication regimen default = one dose per day, 7 days a week; and non-monitored period default = no non-monitored periods. Additional information about parameter definitions is included in Supplemental File II.

Finally, in Stage III, the pre-processed EAMD data and user-supplied parameters are used to compute the daily adherence values defined in Table 1. In our example, the output is saved into a variable report which then contains the 2 daily adherence values (eg, report$adh$dosecorr and report$adh$dosetkn) (Output II). Adherence summary statistics are also available as defined in Supplemental File II.

Algorithm availability

The OncMAP R package is available in the CRAN repository https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/oncmap/index.html. Using default settings, an EAMD actuation file can be transformed by calling a function called “process_eamd” of the R package OncMAP: report <- oncmap::process_eamd(input_file)as detailed in Supplemental File II. Access to the source code for an R Shiny application, a simple graphical interface to demonstrate the features of the OncMAP R library, is available from the authors upon request.

Phase III: Algorithm validation

The ROC analysis for both daily adherence values demonstrated perfect performance by the algorithm across the 8519 non-missing daily data points. The ROC curve analysis for both classifications yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.00, further confirming the algorithm’s perfect classification ability. The algorithm achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity for both adherence classifications. Because classification was perfect, the ROC analysis was functionally redundant with the cross-tabulations presented below but is included for completeness in validation reporting.

In the cross-tabulation of the gold standard adherence versus the algorithm-predicted adherence variable, all complete (non-missing) daily observations were correctly classified, with 100% sensitivity and specificity, indicating no false positives or false negatives. The only errors identified in the algorithm-computed adherence database resulted from the algorithm incorrectly identifying missing values. There were 467 instances for each daily value in which either the algorithm or the gold standard database classified the data as missing. In 460 of the 467 instances (for each daily variable), the algorithm correctly classified the data as missing. In 7 instances (1% of “missing” values), the algorithm incorrectly classified 6 “missing” values as “0 = non-adherent” and 1 “missing” value as “1 = adherent.” The sources of these errors were identified and used to inform algorithm refinements.

Phase IV: Algorithm pilot testing

Demographic characteristics of the 7 end-users who participated in pilot testing are included in Table 2. All participants agreed or strongly agreed that the algorithm would allow their team to more effectively process EAMD data, produce more accurate data, and produce more reproducible data (Table 3). In addition, all participants expressed interest in using the algorithm in their lab. When asked whether they would recommend the algorithm to a friend or colleague, 71% (n = 5) of participants were classified as promoters, 2 (29%) as passives, and none (0%) as detractors, resulting in a Net Promoter Score of 71%.

The mean System Usability Scale score was “acceptable”31 (M = 85.36, SD = 10.94) and on the System Usability Adjective Rating, participants rated the overall user-friendliness of the product as excellent (n = 5, 71%) or good (n = 2, 29%). While participants were enthusiastic about the product, on the MoSCoW rating, the majority reported it was “absolutely necessary” or “really important” that a future software package could include training videos and/or databases (n = 7, 100%), track changes made during data cleaning (n = 6, 86%), account for changes in the patient’s medication regimen (n = 6, 86%), directly import non-monitored periods (n = 5, 71%), integrate self-report data (n = 5, 71%), and create visual depictions of the patient’s adherence data (n = 5, 71%). In addition, responses to open-ended questions indicated improvements to the user interface, batch processing abilities, and conceptualization of non-monitored periods that would be necessary prior to widespread use. Discussion

EAMDs are a preferred medication adherence measurement strategy, and this manuscript reports on a novel algorithm to transform the data captured by these devices into daily adherence data. Engaging principal investigators who make the decisions regarding EAMD data processing, clinical research coordinators who complete EAMD data processing, and biostatisticians who analyze the resulting data allowed our team to identify and define the steps involved in EAMD data processing. These steps are consistent with those in previously published EAMD data processing guidelines,34 and a novel algorithm executing these steps was coded in an R package (OncMAP).

The algorithm demonstrated 100% accuracy in classifying all complete (non-missing) daily adherence values (ie, during periods when the EAMD was used for medication administration). Errors were limited to the algorithm incorrectly labeling 7 missing (non-monitored) daily values (< .001% of data) as “adherent” or “non-adherent.” The algorithm has been refined to address these errors and presents a new and rigorous strategy for EAMD data processing.

In pilot testing, end-users were highly enthusiastic about the potential of the algorithm, with the majority agreeing that this innovation has the potential to save their teams time and resources while producing more accurate and reproducible data. Despite the promise of the algorithm, large-scale adoption is likely to require the development of a feature-complete and user-friendly software package. When viewing a user interface prototype, participants identified significant essential changes to the interface, features, and software suite components. An important next step for this program of research is to build a software suite that integrates the OncMAP algorithm and addresses identified end-user needs.

While this research represents an important step in advancing the pace and rigor of adherence science, findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the EAMD market is rapidly changing, and the algorithm will need to be validated on data from new EAMD models. Second, this study included a small number of participants and future software development efforts will need to include a larger and more representative sample (eg, additional biostatisticians, individuals new to EAMDs). Third, this work focused on the development of a product for use in adherence research and there are likely to be additional modifications and refinements (eg, integration within the electronic medical record) required before this tool could be implemented within clinical care.

Conclusion

EAMDs are a valuable measurement strategy, but the data they produce must be manipulated via a series of decision rules prior to analysis. Proprietary software can execute some of these decision rules, but their algorithms do not account for the complexities of medical treatment and patient-level variations, leaving many research teams to manually process data via a time-consuming, burdensome, and error-prone approach. To our knowledge, this is the first publicly available algorithm that executes the decision rules required to process EAMD data. Initial validation efforts suggest the algorithm is accurate and an important next step is to develop a software program that incorporates this algorithm and meets end-user needs.
