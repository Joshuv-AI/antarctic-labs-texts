Colorless and transparent polyimide nanocomposite films containing organically modified fillers comprising an organoclay/functionalized-graphene complex
Authors: Changyub Na, Ae Ran Lim, Jin-Hae Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAromatic colorless and transparent polyimides (CPIs) are super engineering polymers with excellent mechanical performance and thermal stability, making them particularly suitable for optical displays and electronic materials. In this study, a complex filler was dispersed into poly(amic acid) (PAA) synthesized from dianhydride 5,5’-sulfonylbis(isobenzo-furan-1,3-dione) and diamine bis[4-(3-aminophenoxy) phenyl]sulfone monomers using a solution intercalation method, resulting in the formation of a PAA hybrid. Solutions of this PAA hybrid were thermally imidized through various heat treatments to synthesize CPI hybrid films. A new complex filler containing both clay and graphene was synthesized through organic surface modification of the clay and graphene, and the synergistic effects of the two fillers were investigated. A C16-complex synthesized from an organoclay (hexadecyl-montmorillonite, C16-MMT) and functionalized graphene sheets (hexadecyl-graphene sheet, C16-GS) was used as the complex filler in this study. CPI hybrid films were fabricated by dispersing various loading (0–2.0 wt%) of the C16-complex filler into a CPI matrix, and their thermomechanical properties, filler dispersion morphology, and optical transparency were analyzed based on their filler content. In the CPI hybrid films, the C16-complex exhibited excellent nanoscale dispersion at a filler content of 1.0 wt%, and the corresponding systems showed excellent thermomechanical properties; however, when the filler content exceeded the critical content, filler aggregation occurred, adversely affecting the properties of the hybrid films. In contrast, the optical properties of the hybrid films gradually deteriorated with increasing filler content.Subject terms: Nanoscale materials, Polymer chemistry, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Aromatic polyimides (PIs), super-engineered polymers with excellent properties, are widely used in aerospace and military applications. Currently, they are widely used in electronic materials, including substrates and devices for semiconductors and encapsulants, which require precise characteristics. Recently, the application of PIs in electronic materials has been extensively investigated. The application of PIs in various fields is steadily increasing owing to their excellent thermal stability and mechanical properties at high temperatures; however, their insoluble and infusible nature renders their processing difficult1–3.

Although PIs exhibit excellent thermal and mechanical properties, their unique deep-brown color results in unfavorable optical properties. The deep-brown color of PIs can be attributed to the charge-transfer complex (CT-complex) effect arising from intermolecular or intramolecular interactions within PI polymer chains4. A curved structure can be introduced into the main chain to disrupt molecular stacking, thereby reducing the intermolecular CT-complex effect. Alternatively, monomers with highly electronegative groups, such as trifluoromethyl (–CF3) or sulfone (–SO2–), can be used to inhibit electron transfer and reduce the CT-complex effect, enabling the production of colorless and transparent polyimides (CPIs)5,6. Notably, despite exhibiting better optical properties than traditional PIs, CPIs exhibit poor thermal and mechanical properties because the curved structure or highly electronegative groups introduced into the main chain for optical property enhancement degrade the thermomechanical properties of the system. To address this limitation, studies have focused on developing new CPIs with good thermomechanical properties that show some degree of transparency. This includes the development of new monomers or the partial use of rigid monomers in the main chain of a polymer to improve the thermomechanical properties of the system, even at the cost of a slight reduction in optical transparency7–9.

Nanocomposites are composite materials in which ultrafine nanoscale fillers are uniformly dispersed throughout a polymer matrix. Nanocomposites with nanoscale layered-structure fillers, such as clay or carbon-based materials such as carbon nanotubes or graphene, exfoliated into the matrix polymer exhibit a significantly higher interfacial adhesion than conventional blended composites10,11. These materials overcome several limitations of conventional composite materials and exhibit multifunctional and high-performance synergistic effects, thereby outperforming traditional composites12,13.

Most clays commonly used as fillers show a layered phyllosilicate structure, whereas smectite-type clays with a layered structure contain a 2:1 multilayer arrangement of silica tetrahedra and alumina octahedra14. Commonly used smectite-type clays include montmorillonite (MMT), saponite, hectorite, bentonite, and mica15–17. Clay is inexpensive, environmentally friendly, and can be easily obtained from nature. The nanoscale dispersion of clay with a thickness of 1 nm and surface area of 760 m2/g enhances the gas barrier and thermomechanical properties of composite materials18–20. Surface organic modification using alkyl groups or other organic agents is used to improve the dispersion of pristine inorganic clays, which typically show poor compatibility with organic polymers. Besides being well dispersed in common solvents such as ethanol and chloroform, such organically modified clays exhibit excellent swelling properties and effective nanoscale dispersion in organic polymers21–23.

The thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, and mechanical properties of graphene are similar to those of carbon nanotubes. A single layer of graphene exhibits an extensive surface area of 2630 m2/g, an outstanding thermal conductivity of 5000 W/mK, and an electrical conductivity of 6000 S/cm. Additionally, nanoscale-exfoliated graphene is a highly transparent material with 98% light transmittance that exhibits excellent optical properties. Owing to these properties, graphene shows significant potential for application in several areas of active research, including gas-barrier materials, transparent electrodes, and solar cells24–27. However, similar to clay, graphene exhibits poor polymer dispersion and compatibility, necessitating chemical modification for effective incorporation into polymer composites28.

CPIs are widely studied for flexible electronics and optoelectronic applications due to their excellent thermal stability and optical transparency. To further enhance their thermal and mechanical properties without compromising transparency, small amounts of organically modified fillers are often incorporated. These fillers restrict polymer chain mobility, leading to improved thermal stability and mechanical property. Moreover, their organic modification promotes uniform dispersion and compatibility with the CPI matrix, minimizing light scattering and preserving optical transparency. Thus, CPI-based nanocomposites with well-dispersed fillers offer a balanced combination of performance and transparency for advanced device applications.

Herein, a new CPI was derived from 5,5’-sulfonylbis(isobenzo-furan-1,3-dione) (DSDA) and bis[4-(3-aminophenoxy)phenyl]sulfone (m-BAPS). To ensure the fabrication of a colorless and transparent product, both the monomers constituting the CPI were designed to include highly electronegative –SO2– groups. Besides exhibiting strong electron-withdrawing properties, which help suppress the CT-complex effect, –SO2– groups rigidify the overall polymer structure and hinder the free rotation of the main chain, effectively enhancing the thermomechanical properties of the system. Additionally, both monomers were substituted with meta (m-) groups to maintain a curved polymer chain structure and suppress the CT-complex effect further.

Hybrid films containing the synthesized CPI and an appropriate amount of filler (≤ 2 wt%) were fabricated, and the properties of the hybrid films were investigated as a function of their filler content. In this study, the filler dispersion state, thermomechanical properties, and optical transparency of CPI nanocomposite films containing a C16-MMT/C16-GS complex (C16-complex), synthesized by chemically modifying the surfaces of MMT and graphene with hexadecylamine (C16-) to produce hexadecylamine-MMT (C16-MMT) and hexadecylamine-graphene sheets (C16-GS), were investigated. In addition, films with optimal filler content were identified by analyzing changes in the properties of the hybrid films on varying the complex filler content.

Deviating from the traditional use of clay and graphene as individual fillers in polymer hybrids, this study introduces a new field of polymer hybrids comprising a complex filler that contains both clay and graphene. In this study, a new complex filler consisting of clay and graphene was synthesized, and the properties of newly synthesized CPI hybrids containing this filler were characterized based on their complex filler content.

This work aimed to acquire experimental data on the intermolecular interactions between the organic components of nanofillers and the CPI matrix polymer in hybrid films and evaluate the effects of varying the filler content of CPI hybrid films on their properties.

Experimental

Materials

All reagents for synthesizing CPI, including DSDA and m-BAPS, were purchased from TCI (Tokyo, Japan) and Aldrich Chemical Co. (Yongin, Korea). Molecular sieves (4 Å) were used to remove moisture from the solvent DMAc. Graphene oxide (GO) was purchased from Standard Graphene Co. (Ulsan, Korea), and Na+-MMT with a cation exchange capacity of 119 meq/100 g was purchased from Aldrich (Yongin, Korea).

Preparation of C16-MMT

Under a nitrogen atmosphere, hexadecylamine (11.47 g, HAD, C16-) was dissolved in an HCl solution (4.8 mL of HCl diluted in 100 mL of H2O) and stirred at 80 °C for 1 h. In a separate beaker, Na+-MMT (20 g) was mixed with H2O (400 mL) and dispersed at 80 °C for 1 h. The two solutions were combined and stirred vigorously at 80 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the white powder obtained by vacuum filtration was washed with a 1:1 mixture of distilled water and ethanol (v:v) and vacuum freeze-dried at room temperature for 24 h29. The yield of the resultant white powder was ~ 95%.

Synthesis of the C16-MMT/C16-GS complex (C16-complex)

GO (1.0 g) was added to distilled water (1 L), stirred vigorously for 12 h, and dispersed by sonication for 2 h. In another vessel, phosphotungstic acid (0.5 g, PTA, H3PW12O40) was dissolved in distilled water (30 mL), allowed to disperse for 1 h at room temperature, mixed with the previously prepared GO solution, and stirred vigorously for 1 h at room temperature. Acidic clusters generated by PTA contain three protons in its unit structure, which dissociate in water as oxonium ions, such as H3O+ or H5O2+. Here, polyanionic PTA clusters were incorporated into hybrid matrices, where they exhibited strong interactions with the inorganic silica framework, remaining undissociated from the hybrid matrix; therefore, PTA functioned as a binder between MMT and GS30.

In a separate beaker, C16-MMT (1.0 g) was mixed with ethanol (30 mL), sonicated for 1 h, added dropwise to the GO solution over 1–2 min, and stirred for 30 min. Next, HAD (4.0 g) was dissolved in ethanol (30 mL), added dropwise over 1–2 min into the previously prepared mixture, and stirred for 1 h. Subsequently, the resultant solution was vigorously stirred at room temperature for 12 h. After stirring, the resultant solid was filtered, washed thrice with ethanol (300 mL), and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 24 h to complete the reaction.

Intercalation method of CPI hybrid films containing the C16-complex

CPI hybrid films were fabricated using the solution intercalation method. A similar process was used to fabricate all CPI hybrid films in this study; therefore, only the synthesis of CPI hybrid films containing 1.0 wt% of the C16-complex is described as a representative example. The diamine monomer m-BAPS (4.32 g, 1 × 10−2 mol) was added to DMAc (40 mL) and stirred until complete dissolution. Subsequently, the anhydride monomer DSDA (3.58 g, 1 × 10−2 mol) was added to this solution and stirred under nitrogen at room temperature for 16 h, resulting in the formation of a poly(amic acid) (PAA) solution. The monomer structure and detailed synthesis of CPI hybrid films are shown in Scheme 1. The C16-complex (1.0 wt%, 7.6 × 10−2 g) was dispersed in DMAc (50 mL), and the resulting dispersion was mixed with the PAA solution, stirred under nitrogen at room temperature for 16 h, and stirred and sonicated for 30 min. This process was repeated three times. Subsequently, the resultant solution was cast onto a glass plate and thermally treated in a vacuum oven at various temperatures for different durations, as listed in Table 1.

Characterization

The properties of the newly synthesized CPI films were analyzed using various techniques. To confirm the structure of the synthesized CPI, the functional groups in the CPI structure were identified using a Fourier-transform infrared (FT–IR) spectrometer (Perkin Elmer L-300; London, UK). FT–IR spectra were recorded within 4000–1000 cm−1 at a resolution and scan speed of 32 cm−1 and 0.2 cm/s, respectively. Solid-state 13C cross-polarization/magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (13C CP/MAS NMR) spectroscopy with a Bruker 400 DSX (Berlin, Germany) spectrometer with a Larmor frequency of 100.61 MHz was used to confirm the chemical structure of the synthesized CPI.

Wide-angle X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a D/Max-IIIB X-ray diffractometer (Tokyo, Japan) with a Ni-filtered Cu-Kα target was used to investigate the interlayer spacing of the fillers dispersed in the CPI hybrid films. XRD spectra were recorded at a scan speed of 2°/min within a 2θ range of 2–15°. The fracture surface morphology of the extruded samples was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi S-2400, Tokyo, Japan). Gold sputtering (with an SPI sputter coater) on the fracture surface was used to enhance the sample conductivity. The morphology of the nanofillers dispersed in the CPI matrix was observed using a high resolution-transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM) (JEOL JEM-2100, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. Samples for HR-TEM were prepared by curing the thin films in epoxy resin and then cutting them to a thickness of 90 nm using a microtome under vacuum. The filler morphologies were observed at various magnifications at the same location. Images were recorded on an atomic force microscope (AFM; Multimode, NanoScope III, Digital Instruments Inc., California, USA) and an AutoProbe CP/MT scanning probe microscope. Prior to analysis, the C16-GS sample was dispersed in toluene, sonicated for 3 h, and spin-coated onto a silicon wafer at 5000 rpm.

The thermal properties of the CPI films were measured using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Netzsch F3200; Munich, Germany) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (TA Q-500 TGA; New Castle, USA), respectively, at a heating rate of 20 °C/min under nitrogen. Thermal property tests were conducted under a nitrogen flow rate of 30 mL/min. The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the films was measured by thermomechanical analysis (TMA) (Seiko TMA/SS100; Tokyo, Japan) with a heat treatment rate of 5 °C/min under a load of 0.1 N. For reliable results, the CTE was determined after primary heating (from 50 to 200 °C) followed by secondary heating under the same temperature conditions.

The mechanical tensile properties of the system were measured using a universal testing machine (UTM) (Shimadzu JP/AG-50KNX; Tokyo, Japan) with a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min; a minimum of 10 measurements were recorded for each sample. The recorded values were averaged after excluding outliers (data points outside the error range). Experimental errors for the maximum tensile strength and initial elastic modulus were maintained within ± 1 MPa and ± 0.05 GPa, respectively.

Shimadzu UV-3600 UV–vis spectrophotometers (Tokyo, Japan) and Konica Minolta CM-3600d spectrophotometers (Tokyo, Japan) were used to measure the optical transmittance of the films.

Results and discussion

Morphology of nanofillers

Pristine clay and graphene exhibit a single-layer plate-like structure with a thickness of 1 nm and large surface area (760 and 2630 m2/g, respectively). When these fillers are dispersed at the nanoscale within a polymer matrix, the resulting nanocomposites exhibit excellent thermal- and mechanical-properties19,31.

Here, the morphology of pristine clay and graphene sheet samples was analyzed using SEM (Fig. 1). Most pure clays and graphene sheets exhibit very low polymer dispersibility or compatibility, resulting in composite materials with poor physical properties. To overcome these shortcomings, organically modified clay or functionalized graphene synthesized through organic reactions on the surface of clay or graphene are used as fillers. These modified fillers can be dispersed in polymer matrices at the nanoscale without aggregation or phase separation, improving the properties of the composite material significantly23,28.

SEM micrographs of (a) pristine clay and (b) GS obtained at different magnifications.

Despite being a planar carbon allotrope with a thickness of ~ 1 nm, graphene often aggregates to form multilayered clusters. Therefore, typically, the theoretical and actual thickness of graphene differ significantly, making precise measurements of the actual thickness of graphene vital. Here, AFM provided critical information regarding certain characteristics of the graphene sheets, such as defects, thickness, bending, and surface morphology. The lateral size and thickness of single-layer graphene and functionalized-GS (F-GS) were measured using AFM. Figure 2 shows an AFM image of C16-GS, recorded after depositing the relevant dispersion on a mica substrate. The thickness of the graphene layer of C16-GS treated with HDA (C16-) was 3.08 nm or 1.54 nm. The thickness of unmodified graphene is ~ 1 nm; therefore, the thickness values of the sample confirm the chemical modification of the pure GS surface with HDA.

AFM images obtained to investigate the thickness of synthesized functionalized graphene sheets: (a) AFM 3D image of C16-GS, (b) AFM image of C16-GS, and (c) height profile along the white line in image (b).

XRD and TEM of the C16-complex

The crystal structure of the synthesized C16-complex was investigated using XRD. The details of all the XRD peaks observed within 2θ values of 2–15°, including the peak positions and d-values of each peak, are summarized in Fig. 3. Starting with an initial peak at 2θ = 2.78° (d = 31.74, Peak A), several peaks were observed within the 2θ range of 2–15°. Notably, six peaks were observed from 2θ = 4.04° (Peak C) to 13.84° (Peak H) at almost consistent 2θ (o) intervals and a gradual reduction in intensity. The consistent 2θ interval between peaks indicates that the nanofiller composed of clay and graphene synthesized in this study shows a regular crystal structure32.

XRD pattern of the C16-complex observed in the 2θ range of 2–15°. Peaks A to H appeared at very regular intervals with increasing 2θ.

In addition to the crystal structure based on d-values determined by XRD, the actual morphology of the clay and graphene sheets in the C16-complex was observed using TEM. Figure 4 shows schematics of a C16-complex in which organoclay (C16-MMT) and functionalized graphene (C16-GS) are connected by PTA (which functions as a binder) along with the morphology of the C16-complex, as observed by TEM. The TEM results shown in Fig. 4 confirm the presence of (1) clay and (2) graphene sheets in the synthesized system33. The TEM images of MMT and graphene show distinct differences in their structural characteristics: MMT typically exhibits a clear layered structure, which appears as a series of dark, repeating lines in the TEM images. Due to its multilayered configuration, it often forms stacked structures with low electron transparency, resulting in relatively dark contrast. In contrast, graphene appears as a transparent and thin sheet composed of one or a few atomic layers. As a result, it is generally observed in gray tones in TEM images. Additionally, single- or few-layer graphene may display a characteristic hexagonal pattern34,35.

A scheme illustration of the C16-complex, in which organically modified clay and functionalized graphene are interconnected through a PTA binder, is presented along with a TEM micrograph of the C16-complex composed of clay and graphene. The overlapped and darker regions correspond to (1) clay, while the transparent, gray-toned sheet structures represent (2) graphene sheets.

FT–IR and 13C-NMR analyses of CPI

Figure 5 shows the functional groups in the PAA and CPI structures identified by FT–IR spectroscopy. The pattern of PAA contains a very broad –OH band between 2800 and 3200 cm−1, C=O stretching vibration bands at 1721 (acid) and 1639 cm−1 (amide), and a C=C stretching vibration band at 1398 cm−1. In the FT–IR spectrum of CPI, the –OH peak observed in the spectrum of PAA disappears and C=O stretching vibration bands are observed at 1780 and 1710 cm−1 along with a characteristic peak corresponding to the stretching vibration of the C–N–C bond, representing the imide functional group, at 1366 cm−1. The presence of the imide functional group confirms the formation of CPI by the reaction between the carboxyl and amide groups of PAA36.

The synthesis process of CPI through thermal treatment of PAA was confirmed by analyzing the functional groups observed in the FT-IR spectrum.

To validate the results of FT–IR spectroscopy, solid-state 13C-NMR spectroscopy was also used to investigate the structure of the solid organic compounds in this study by measuring the chemical shifts of carbon. In this characterization, the chemical shifts of the 13C nuclei were calibrated to 0 ppm using the standard peak of tetramethylsilane at 25 °C. In Fig. 6, NMR peaks corresponding to phenyl carbons are observed at 123.05 ppm (a and b, respectively), peaks corresponding to the imide and phenyl carbons are observed at 133.39, 146.95, and 154.86 ppm (c, d, and e, respectively), and the peak corresponding to the imide C=O group is observed at 165.26 ppm (f). Rotational sidebands are indicated by open circles and asterisks. The experimentally determined structure of the CPI synthesized in this study was consistent with its expected structure based on FT–IR and 13C-NMR analyses37.

Chemical shifts obtained from the 13C-NMR spectrum were used to confirm the synthesis of CPI. The chemical shifts corresponding to each peak were assigned to the respective positions in the chemical structure.

XRD patterns of CPI hybrid films

The XRD patterns of the filler, pure CPI, and CPI hybrid films containing various amounts of filler, measured in the range of 2θ = 2–15°, are shown in Fig. 7. Pristine MMT and graphene oxide (GO) exhibited strong peaks at 2θ = 8.56° (d = 11.99 Å) and 2θ = 9.62° (d = 9.19 Å), respectively. MMT is a clay mineral with a layered structure, and it exhibits a strong peak corresponding to the (001) plane at 2θ = 8.56°. GO shows a peak at 2θ = 9.62°, which also corresponds to the (001) plane and is the most characteristic peak of GO (see Fig. 7). This peak results from the leftward shift of the (002) peak of graphite due to oxidation, which increases the interlayer spacing as oxygen-containing functional groups and water molecules are inserted between the layers. However, the peak of C16-MMT, obtained through organic modification of the filler surface using hexadecylamine, showed strong peaks at 2θ = 3.38° (d = 26.11 Å) and 5.01° (d = 17.62 Å), while C16-GS exhibited distinct peaks at 2θ = 3.49° (d = 25.29 Å) and 6.53° (d = 13.52 Å). The increased d-spacing values of the organically modified fillers are attributed to the insertion of C16 chains between the filler layers, which leads to an expansion of the interlayer distance.

XRD patterns of fillers before and after organic modification, and CPI hybrid films with various C16-complex contents.

The XRD patterns of CPI hybrids containing the C16-complex are shown in Fig. 7. The spectrum of CPI hybrids with a C16-complex content of up to 0.5 wt% does not contain the characteristic peaks of the C16-complex filler. However, the spectrum of CPI hybrids with a complex filler content of 1 wt% contain a very weak peak at 2θ = 6.77° (d = 13.04 Å). On increasing the content of the complex filler further (from 1.5 to 2.0 wt%), a new peak is observed in the spectra of the corresponding CPI hybrids at 2θ = 5.58° (d = 15.82 Å). Thus, when the filler content of the CPI matrix exceeds a certain critical level, the filler is not uniformly distributed within the system and undergoes agglomeration38,39. The emergence of a new diffraction peak at 2θ = 5.58° with increasing filler content is attributed to the interlayer stacking of filler platelets, which becomes more pronounced upon agglomeration. In a uniformly dispersed system, such regular stacking is suppressed. Therefore, the appearance of this peak at higher filler loadings indicates a phase-separated domain or agglomeration.

Although XRD can accurately measure the interlayer distances between the solid filler layers dispersed in a matrix and determine their morphological structure, it cannot clearly define the orientation of the filler layers or their dispersed form in space. Consequently, it is difficult to assess the dispersion of the filler accurately using only the intensity of XRD peaks. Owing to the inadequacy of XRD in elucidating the formation of nanoscopically dispersed hybrids in this study, electron microscopy was used to investigate the behavior of nanometer-scale dispersed fillers in the CPI matrix further40,41.

TEM photographs of CPI hybrid films

XRD provides information about the crystalline structure and interlayer spacing of the nanofillers, while TEM enables the direct visualization of individual filler particles in the hybrid films with a significantly high resolution. Additionally, TEM can be used to quantitatively describe the interlayer structure of dispersed clay and graphene as well as their nanoscale dispersion within the CPI matrix40–43.

Figure 8 shows TEM images of hybrid films containing 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 wt% of the C16-complex. Each image contains hair-like black lines representing filler layers with a thickness of 1 nm; the spaces between these lines indicate interlayer gaps of the filler. The areas indicated by arrows are magnified according to the scale mentioned in the figure to observe the dispersion in greater detail.

TEM photographs of CPI hybrids with various C16-complex contents of (a) 0.5 wt%, (b) 1.0 wt%, and (c) 1.5 wt%. The arrowed regions in all images on the left were magnified and shown on the right.

In hybrid films with a C16-complex content of 0.5 and 1.0 wt% (Fig. 8a,b), filler particles are well-dispersed in the matrix over a thickness of < 20 nm. However, increasing the C16-complex content to 1.5 wt% (Fig. 8c) results in significant filler aggregation, leading to an overall filler-layer thickness of 20–50 nm. These results are consistent with the XRD results shown in Fig. 7 and the previously proposed premise, which proposes that fillers undergo aggregation, owing to an inability to disperse, when the filler content of the composite exceeds a certain critical threshold value. At filler contents exceeding 1.5 wt%, TEM images (Fig. 8c) reveal regions of aggregated fillers forming larger clusters, indicative of non-uniform dispersion. These aggregates correspond to the emergence of a new diffraction peak at 2θ = 5.58° (d = 15.82 Å) in the XRD patterns. The consistency between these observations suggests that, beyond a critical filler loading, the initially homogeneous dispersion of fillers deteriorates, leading to agglomeration and phase separation within the matrix.

However, based on the XRD and TEM results, no significant overall difference was observed even when the filler concentration increased up to 1.0 wt%. However, an excessive filler content adversely affects the thermomechanical properties and optical transparency of the hybrid films synthesized in this study; details of this phenomenon are discussed later.

Thermal property

The DSC results of CPI hybrid films containing different amounts of the C16-complex are summarized in Table 2, and DSC thermograms showing their glass transition temperature (Tg) behavior are shown in Fig. 9. In this study, Tg values were determined from the results of the second-heating DSC curve. The Tg value of pure CPI films (216 °C) increases with nanofiller addition up to a certain critical threshold; on excess nanofiller addition, the Tg value decreases. For example, the Tg value of the hybrid films increases from 216 to 239 °C on increasing their C16-complex content from 0 to 1.0 wt%. This Tg increment can be attributed to two factors. First, the small number of rigid and planar filler layers dispersed in the CPI matrix affect the free volume of the polymer chains. Second, the movement of the polymer chains inserted between the rigid clay and graphene layers is hindered, resulting in an increase in Tg10,28,44,45. However, when the C16-complex content increases to 1.5 wt%, the Tg of the corresponding system decreases to 224 °C; this trend continues up to a filler content of 2.0 wt%, at which stage, the Tg decreases to 221 °C. This trend can be attributed to the agglomeration of excessively dispersed fillers46,47 (beyond the critical content), consistent with the previously mentioned XRD and TEM results (Figs. 7 and 8).

TGA thermograms of CPI hybrid films with various nanofiller concentrations are shown in Fig. 10. The behavior of the initial decomposition temperature (TDi) differs distinctly from that of Tg. On increasing the C16-complex content from 0 to 2.0 wt%, the TDi of the hybrid films steadily decreases from 435 to 376 °C (see Table 2). This reduction in TDi can be attributed to the poor thermal stability of long alkyl chains in the C16-complex, which is uniformly dispersed in the CPI matrix and undergoes facile decomposition under high-temperature conditions48. When the C-16 complex content was dispersed at 0.5 wt%, the residual weight at 600 °C (wtR600) increased from 59 to 65%. This result is due to the excellent thermal stability of the fillers dispersed in the hybrid film, although in a small amount. However, even when the C-16 complex content increased to 2.0 wt%, these values of all hybrid films remained almost constant at 600 °C (65–67%), possibly because most organic components with poor thermal resistance in the C16-complex undergo decomposition at such high temperatures (600 °C), and only inorganic silicates are retained in the system.

TGA thermograms of CPI hybrid films containing various C16-complex contents. As the filler content increased up to 2.0 wt%, the TDi value gradually decreased.

In general, when a polymer hybrid film is heated, the polymer chains relax perpendicular to the heating direction. However, because the thermally stable and rigid planar clay and graphene dispersed in the matrix are difficult to deform, they effectively block heat transfer, suppressing the thermal expansion of the CPI matrix in hybrid films49,50. Therefore, to manufacture hybrid films with low thermal expansion, thermally stable nanofillers, such as clay and graphene, must be effectively dispersed into the matrix polymer. Table 2 summarizes the CTE values of CPI hybrid films with various C16-complex concentrations after secondary heating within 50–200 °C; the corresponding TMA thermograms are shown in Fig. 11. Up to the critical filler content, the CTE of the hybrid films decreases with increasing filler content; on increasing the filler content further, the CTE increases. For example, when the C16-complex content increases from 0 to 1.0 wt%, the CTE value decreases from 59.4 ppm/°C to a minimum of 51.0 ppm/°C. However, when the C16-complex content is increased to 2.0 wt%, the CTE of the hybrid increases to 53.5 ppm/°C. This trend occurs because the excessive filler dispersed in the matrix fails to effectively block heat owing to agglomeration; thus, increasing the filler concentration beyond the critical filler content reduces the performance of the hybrid films51. Therefore, the critical filler content and filler type are vital considerations while designing high-performance hybrid systems. The observed trend in CTE values is consistent with the trend of Tg values observed for the hybrid films.

TMA thermograms of CPI hybrid films containing various C16-complex contents. As the filler content increased up to 1.0 wt%, the CTE value decreased, but beyond the critical content, the CTE value increased.

According to thermal property measurements, when C16-complex filler layers composed of thermally stable and highly insulating clay and graphene are uniformly dispersed at low concentrations within the CPI matrix, they effectively hinder heat transfer, resulting in systems with excellent thermal properties (Tg and CTE). However, when the C16-complex content is high and exceeds a certain critical value, the fillers are not well dispersed in the matrix and tend to aggregate, exhibiting low polymer dispersibility and compatibility, thereby leading to a diminished thermal shielding effect and the degradation of thermal properties.

From these results, it can be concluded that the thermal properties of the nanocomposites were enhanced due to the excellent insulating effect of the highly thermally stable clay and graphene dispersed in the CPI matrix, leading to improvements in overall thermal characteristics (Tg, TDi, and CTE). However, when the content of the organically modified fillers exceeded a critical content, the thermal properties deteriorated. This can be explained by the fact that the fillers were not well dispersed in the matrix and formed agglomerates, resulting in reduced compatibility and a diminished heat shielding effect.

The organically modified fillers act as reinforcing agents within the CPI matrix, promoting an increase in both the crystallinity and mechanical properties of the polymer. These fillers facilitate the alignment of polymer chains, thereby enhancing crystallinity, and simultaneously improve mechanical performance by effectively distributing external stress through strong interfacial interactions. These changes in crystallinity contribute to improved modulus and tensile strength due to enhanced interfacial load transfer. However, at 2.0 wt%, crystallinity slightly decreases, likely due to filler agglomeration disrupting the polymer chain pacing.

Optical transparency

The optical transmittance of the hybrid films could be correlated with the content and degree of dispersion of the filler. When the filler content dispersed in the matrix exceeded a critical threshold, the plate-like structures of clay and graphene tended to agglomerate, hindering the facile passage of light through the hybrid film and resulting in a reduction in optical transmittance. In contrast, when the filler was uniformly dispersed on the nanometer scale (exfoliation), the optical transparency of the hybrid film increased. Therefore, the dispersion state of the filler in the hybrid material is directly related to its optical transparency. The optical transparency of the hybrid films was evaluated from the initial transmittance wavelength (λ0), transmittance at 500 nm in the visible-light range (500 nmtrans), and the yellowness index (YI).

The UV–vis spectra of the hybrid films are shown in Fig. 13, and the corresponding results are summarized in Table 4. As the content of organically modified fillers increases, the optical transparency of the CPI composite films decreases due to various factors. Specifically, the filler particles scatter light, reducing transparency, or form agglomerates by clumping together. In addition, poor compatibility between the fillers and the polymer matrix leads to interfacial inhomogeneity. Due to these combined effects, the transparency of the films decreases as the filler content increases.

Conclusion

Previous studies have fabricated composites using either clay or graphene as individual fillers to investigate the differences in their properties. In contrast, the present work synthesized a novel complex filler containing both clay and graphene simultaneously, and fabricated CPI nanocomposite films to examine the synergistic effects of the complex filler on the composite properties.

In this paper, the thermomechanical properties, filler dispersion, and optical transparency of CPI hybrid films with varying concentrations of the C16-complex, a newly synthesized composite filler, synthesized through the solution intercalation method were investigated. Hybrid films with a composite filler content of up to 1.0 wt% showed good thermomechanical properties; however, increasing the filler content further resulted in a deterioration of optical properties.

By uniformly dispersing the filler at the nanoscale within the polymer matrix, hybrid films with excellent physical properties, which cannot be fabricated by conventional processes, can be produced by maintaining high interfacial adhesion between the matrix and the nanofiller. Furthermore, dispersing a small amount of the appropriate filler at the critical content level within the CPI at the nanoscale improves the physical properties of hybrid films significantly. Owing to these enhanced properties, CPI hybrids exhibit high potential as film materials for electronic and optical applications.

Although traditional PIs with excellent thermomechanical properties are widely used in applications requiring high-performance films, their dark color limits their use in display applications for electronic materials. This study shows that CPI hybrids synthesized using appropriately designed monomers can overcome the disadvantages of conventional PIs. CPI materials synthesized through the proposed process can function as an alternative to glass, exhibiting high potential for application in a wide variety of fields.
