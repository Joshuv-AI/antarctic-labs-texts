A unified Haar wavelet collocation framework for fractional volterra integro-differential equations with application to tumor-immune dynamics modeling
Authors: Maha M. Hamood, Abdulrahman A. Sharif, Kirtiwant P. Ghadle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper develops a novel Haar wavelet collocation technique (HWCT) for solving a general class of fractional Volterra integro-differential equations (FVIDEs). The method employs operational matrices of integration and fractional integration to systematically handle both the fractional derivative and Volterra integral terms within a unified framework. By approximating the highest-order derivative using a truncated Haar series and recovering the solution through successive integration, the complex FVIDE is transformed into an easily solvable linear algebraic system. A rigorous convergence analysis establishes that the method achieves second-order accuracy under appropriate regularity conditions. The efficacy of the proposed approach is demonstrated through several numerical examples, showing excellent agreement with exact solutions. Furthermore, we present a significant application to fractional-order tumor-immune dynamics, modeling the complex interactions between tumor growth, immune response, and immunotherapy. The model captures memory effects and distributed time delays inherent in biological systems, providing insights for optimizing cancer treatment protocols. Numerical simulations confirm the method’s computational efficiency and accuracy, making it a valuable tool for solving challenging problems in mathematical biology and engineering.

Introduction

Fractional calculus, the branch of mathematics dealing with derivatives and integrals of arbitrary (non-integer) order, has transcended its theoretical origins to become an indispensable tool for modeling complex phenomena in physics, engineering, biology, and finance1,2. Unlike their integer-order counterparts, fractional differential equations (FDEs) possess an inherent ability to capture memory and hereditary properties of various materials and processes6. A particularly powerful and challenging subclass of these equations is the fractional Volterra integro-differential equation (FVIDE), which combines a fractional derivative with an integral term having a variable upper limit of integration. FVIDEs arise naturally in numerous applications: for example, in viscoelasticity to model materials with memory-dependent stress-strain relationships10; in heat conduction through media with thermal memory11; and in population dynamics and systems with after-effects, such as ecological interactions with delayed feedback24.

Despite their robust descriptive power, the analytical solutions for FVIDEs are often intractable, existing only for a limited number of linear cases with simple coefficients1,6. This intractability has spurred the development of efficient and accurate numerical methods. Over the past decades, a wide array of computational techniques has been proposed to solve fractional integro-differential equations. These include finite difference/spectral methods8, collocation methods employing various polynomials like Bernoulli and Chebyshev10,25, tau methods7, operational matrix methods9, and even artificial neural networks13. Each method offers distinct advantages, yet the quest for methods that are simultaneously simple, computationally efficient, and highly accurate remains a central focus in numerical analysis.

Among the various numerical approaches, the Haar wavelet method has emerged as a particularly attractive tool for solving differential and integral equations. Introduced by Alfred Haar in 1910, Haar wavelets are the simplest possible orthonormal wavelet family. Their distinctive features include a compact support, which leads to sparse operational matrices, simplicity of implementation, and computational efficiency16,17. The method’s core idea involves converting the governing differential or integral equation into a system of algebraic equations by expanding the unknown function as a linear combination of Haar wavelets. The success of this method has been demonstrated in solving standard integral equations4,18, delay differential equations3, and even certain classes of fractional differential equations20,21.

The existing literature reveals a rich tapestry of research on both Haar wavelets and fractional equations. Seminal works by4,17 established the Haar wavelet method for integral and differential equations, while researchers like3,18 extended its application to more complex problems involving delays and nonlinearities. Concurrently, the numerical solution of fractional equations has been advanced by scholars using various bases, such as Chebyshev wavelets5,25 and hybrid functions14. Recent studies have begun to bridge these two areas, applying Haar wavelets to specific types of fractional problems, including distributed order equations19,20 and nonlinear fractional integro-differential equations22,23.

However, a focused and comprehensive study on the application of the Haar wavelet method specifically to Fractional Volterra Integro-Differential Equations is still an area with significant potential for exploration. While some studies touch on related problems22,24, a systematic formulation, analysis, and validation of the Haar wavelet scheme for the broad class of FVIDEs, particularly those with weakly singular kernels that are characteristic of fractional integrals, is not fully developed. This gap presents an opportunity to leverage the inherent simplicity and sparsity of Haar wavelets to develop a robust numerical solver for these challenging equations. Finally, several numerical examples will be presented and compared with existing results in the literature5,10,15,25 to demonstrate the superiority, accuracy, and computational efficiency of the proposed Haar wavelet method.

Recent years have witnessed growing interest in fractional-order modeling of biological systems, particularly in cancer dynamics. Traditional integer-order models often fail to capture the memory effects and history-dependent behavior characteristic of biological processes. Fractional calculus provides a powerful framework for modeling such phenomena, as demonstrated in various biological applications12,26–28. For instance, tumor growth exhibits subdiffusive behavior due to cellular memory and microenvironmental constraints, while immune response involves distributed time delays in cell recruitment and activation. The combination of fractional derivatives with Volterra integral terms, as explored in this work, offers a mathematically rigorous approach to capture these complex dynamics. However, the numerical solution of such FVIDEs remains challenging, necessitating efficient computational methods that can handle both the non-local fractional derivative and the historical dependence of the integral term simultaneously.

Concurrently, advances in computational methods for fractional and integro-differential systems have underscored the importance of efficient numerical frameworks. For instance, wavelet-based approaches have been successfully applied in signal processing and fault prediction, as demonstrated in wavelet-guided networks for dynamic frequency decomposition29, and in variable filtered-waveform variational mode decomposition for bearing fault detection30. In the realm of fractional adaptive systems, time-varying fuzzy parameter neural networks for chaotic synchronization have been proposed31. Parallelly, fractional calculus has been extensively used to model biological and physical systems with memory effects. For example, a multi-term fractional transport model for groundwater contamination has been solved32, and HIV dynamics have been analyzed using fractional calculus33. Dengue transmission with memory effects has been explored via non-integer derivatives34, and non-integer derivatives have been applied to financial systems35. These studies highlight the growing intersection of wavelet methods, fractional modeling, and real-world applications—a gap this paper aims to bridge by introducing a unified Haar wavelet framework for fractional Volterra integro-differential equations with applications in tumor-immune dynamics.

Furthermore, the application of fractional calculus and machine learning to disease modeling has expanded significantly in recent years, especially within oncology and virology. For instance, fractal-fractional frameworks have been employed to model the dynamics of chronic myeloid leukemia with T-cell interactions36, while neural network-driven fractional analysis has been used to predict the spread of conjunctivitis viral infections37. Deep neural networks are increasingly being integrated into the computational analysis of coupled fractional integro-differential systems, offering enhanced predictive accuracy and scalability38. Similarly, artificial deep neural networks have been applied to study fractional-order epidemic compartmental models, improving the forecasting of disease progression and intervention outcomes39. Beyond physiological diseases, fractional models combined with artificial intelligence tools are also being developed to understand and simulate psychological disorders, reflecting the growing interdisciplinary convergence of mathematical biology, fractional calculus, and computational intelligence40.

Recent advances in fractional calculus and wavelet methods have expanded their applicability across diverse scientific domains, particularly in disease modeling and computational physics. For instance, piecewise fractional operators have been used to model rotavirus dynamics with modified Atangana–Baleanu–Caputo (ABC) derivatives41, and a modified-ABC fractional framework has been analytically applied to breast cancer progression42. Virus mutation dynamics have also been captured using piecewise fractional derivatives43. Complementing these developments, wavelet-based numerical techniques—such as the Fibonacci wavelet method—have proven effective for computational analysis of physical systems44. These studies underscore the growing synergy between fractional modeling tools and wavelet-based solvers in addressing complex, memory-dependent phenomena in biology and physics.

Motivated by the relative underexploration of a unified Haar wavelet framework for Fractional Volterra Integro-Differential Equations (FVIDEs)–particularly those incorporating both fractional and integer-order derivatives alongside a Volterra integral term–this paper aims to develop a computationally efficient and accurate numerical scheme based on Haar wavelet approximation. Discussion on method selection

The comparative analysis demonstrates that the proposed Haar wavelet method offers a favorable balance of accuracy, simplicity, and speed for solving FVIDEs with smooth solutions. Its piecewise-constant basis is particularly efficient for problems where second-order convergence is sufficient. However, for solutions requiring very high accuracy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$> 10^{-10}$$\end{document}) or possessing high-frequency components, higher-order methods like spectral tau or Chebyshev wavelets may eventually be more efficient despite their higher per-step cost, as they can achieve the desired error with a smaller M. The choice of method should therefore consider the solution’s smoothness, the required accuracy, and the computational budget.

Biological justification and parameter selection

The selection of fractional order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w = 0.7$$\end{document} in our tumor-immune model is biologically motivated by experimental observations of subdiffusive tumor growth dynamics. Studies by26–28 have shown that cancer cell migration in dense microenvironments exhibits memory effects characterized by fractional orders between 0.6–0.8. This value captures the anomalous diffusion behavior observed in tumor proliferation, where cell movement depends on past environmental conditions rather than instantaneous states alone. The fractional derivative thus provides a more accurate representation of the history-dependent nature of tumor progression compared to classical integer-order models.

Model validation

To validate our fractional tumor-immune model, we compared numerical predictions with experimental data from murine melanoma studies (Clinical Cancer Research, 2020). The simulated tumor regression pattern (73.2% reduction over 100 days) closely matched observed responses to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in B16-F10 melanoma models (68–75% reduction). Additionally, the predicted immune cell dynamics aligned with flow cytometry measurements of CD8+ T-cell infiltration, with correlation coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.89$$\end{document}. This strong agreement with experimental data confirms the model’s biological relevance and predictive capability for optimizing treatment protocols.

Conclusion and future work

This study has introduced a unified Haar wavelet collocation method for solving fractional Volterra integro-differential equations (FVIDEs), offering a systematic numerical framework that simultaneously handles fractional derivatives, integer-order derivatives, and Volterra integral terms. By constructing operational matrices of integration and fractional integration, the method transforms complex FVIDEs into solvable linear algebraic systems. Rigorous convergence analysis established second-order accuracy under appropriate regularity conditions, which was confirmed through extensive numerical experiments.

The application of this method to fractional-order tumor–immune dynamics demonstrates its practical utility in mathematical biology. The developed model captures essential biological features such as memory effects in tumor growth and distributed delays in immune response, providing a more realistic representation than classical integer-order approaches. Numerical simulations of a melanoma case study illustrated the model’s ability to simulate treatment responses and evaluate therapeutic efficacy, offering insights for optimizing immunotherapy protocols.

Limitations

While the method performs well for smooth solutions, its accuracy is limited for highly oscillatory or discontinuous functions due to the piecewise-constant nature of Haar wavelets. Computational cost also increases for very high resolutions, and the current formulation assumes continuous kernels. These limitations suggest areas for further refinement.
