Modeling the transmission dynamics of liver flukes (Clonorchis sinensis and Opisthorchis spp.): a systematic review and future perspectives
Authors: Xiao-Ping Han, Yu-Ying Zhu, Men-Bao Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfections with liver flukes (Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis viverrini, and O. felineus) cause high burden. Mechanistic models have been employed to disentangle their transmission dynamics and guide the design of control strategies. However, no comprehensive review of these mechanistic models has yet been undertaken.MethodsIn this systematic review, we searched six major databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Korea Med, Cochrane, China National Knowledge Infrastructure [CNKI], and Wanfang Data) for studies published up to 14 May 2025, to identify and evaluate mechanistic models of liver fluke infections. We included all mechanistic transmission models for human liver flukes regardless of language or setting, while excluded non-mechanistic models, reviews, and empirical studies.ResultsOf the 533 records identified, 18 studies were eligible for analysis. Most studies focused on C. sinensis in China and O. viverrini in Lao People’s Democratic Republic, primarily employing population-based model with ordinary differential equations. Findings consistently identified humans as the central reservoir sustaining transmission, while the role of animal reservoir hosts (e.g. cats and dogs) in transmission was less explored (in 6 out of 18 studies) and divergent in different models. Models incorporating host heterogeneity demonstrated the superiority of integrated control strategies-combining mass drug administration, health education, and environmental improvements over single intervention. High frequency, coverage and adherence of measures were shown to be critical for achieving control or even elimination targets.ConclusionResults from mechanistic models support the implementation of One Health strategies to improve liver fluke control. This review identifies the need for integrative, data-driven One Health modeling frameworks that incorporate human, animal, and environmental transmission parameters, and address key sources of heterogeneity in host behavior, exposure, and transmission dynamics to support control targets.

Introduction

Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis viverrini, and O. felineus are major liver flukes infecting approximately 27.21 million people globally [1], largely concentrated in East Asia (C. sinensis) [[2], [3], [4]], the Mekong River Basin (O. viverrini) [5], and Eastern Europe (O. felineus) [1]. Freshwater snails and freshwater fish act as intermediate hosts [6]. Humans become infected through consumption of raw or inadequately cooked freshwater fish containing metacercariae [[7], [8], [9]]. Adult worms can survive for several decades in the human bile ducts [1,10], and chronic or heavy infections may lead to serious hepatobiliary diseases [6], among which cholangiocarcinoma is fatal [11].

Control currently relies on preventive chemotherapy with praziquantel widely employed in mass drug administration (MDA) programs owing to its high efficacy [12]. Because reinfection commonly occurs, effective control strategies typically integrate preventive chemotherapy with health education and environmental management measures [13]. Evidence demonstrates that reducing raw-fish consumption and improving sanitation (e.g., constructing sanitary latrines) can disrupt transmission [14]. However, persistent dietary practices and poor treatment adherence are important challenges in specific populations [15]. Additionally, fish-eating animals such as dogs and cats act as reservoir hosts, shedding parasite eggs into the environment through open defecation, thus maintaining the transmission cycle in areas with inadequate sanitation [16]. These factors make it challenging for single intervention alone to achieve sustainable control and elimination of liver fluke infections, necessitating an integrated “One Health” approach involving coordinated interventions targeting humans, animals, and the environment [17].

Mathematical transmission models represent a powerful analytical approach to elucidating the complex multi-host transmission dynamics of infections. By translating biological and behavioral processes into mathematical frameworks, models can quantify the contributions of various factors to transmission and simulate the long-term impact of diverse intervention strategies [18].

Despite the development of numerous transmission models for liver fluke infections, a systematic synthesis of these models’ structures, assumptions, and outcomes has not yet been conducted. To fill this gap, we conduct a systematic review of mechanistic transmission models for liver fluke infections. The primary objectives of this systematic review are to: (1) summarize the types, structures, and key assumptions of existing mechanistic transmission models for liver fluke infections; (2) systematically identify and summarize the main parameters employed within these models; and (3) comparatively evaluate predicted outcomes of various control measures (e.g., preventive chemotherapy, health education, and environmental improvements) in models. Based on the findings, we further discuss limitations of existing models, such as insufficient consideration of spatial heterogeneity and oversimplified modeling of animal reservoirs, and propose future research priorities, including integrating climate-driven factors and developing agent-based model (ABM) incorporating individual-level behavioral interactions.

Methods

Database and search strategy

A systematic review of transmission dynamics models was conducted to identify the key factors influencing the transmission of liver flukes and to evaluate the impact of various control strategies on their transmission, which was registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD420251022556). The electronic literature search was performed across six databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Korea Med, Cochrane, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data. The search covered the period from the establishment of the databases up to 14 May 2025, with no language restrictions. Both English and Chinese search terms were employed, including keywords such as “Clonorchis∗,” “Opisthorchis∗,” “liver fluke∗,” “model∗,” “simulate∗,” “dynamics∗,” and “transmission∗” (search terms in detail were provided in Supplementary Material 1). The reference lists of studies meeting the eligibility criteria were manually screened to identify additional relevant references. This systematic review was conducted and reported following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [19].

Model selection: inclusion and exclusion criteria

The search results retrieved from electronic databases were screened according to the following procedure: (1) titles and abstracts were reviewed to exclude studies focusing on parasites or diseases other than liver flukes (C. sinensis, O. viverrini, and O. felineus); (2) retrieving and reviewing the full texts of all articles whose abstracts met the inclusion criteria; (3) excluding studies that either employed methodologies other than mechanistic transmission models, or focused exclusively on non-human hosts (namely only animals).

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction was performed by two independent reviewers (X.P. H. and Y.Y. Z. or M.B. Q.), with disagreements resolved by discussion. First, general study characteristics were collected, including parasite species, hosts, publication year, region, model type, the representation of population dynamics, and how host heterogeneity was addressed. Model types include population-based model (PBM), ABM, ordinary differential equation models (ODE), partial differential equation (PDE), and delay differential equation models (DDE). Second, details on the modeled interventions were extracted, including the consideration of intervention heterogeneity and primary reported outcomes. Qualitative information, such as the methods used to represent population dynamics and the reported impacts of interventions was narratively synthesized to identify common patterns and key differences among the studies. Third, the fundamental parameters in each model were extracted. Given the considerable variation in parameters across studies, we synthesized this information by grouping them into shared categories that captured core features of the modeling framework, namely host-specific parameters, parasite-specific parameters, transmission parameters, and others. Finally, research transparency was assessed by recording indicators such as data and code sharing, model verification, and the inclusion of uncertainty analysis. Counts and proportions for all categorical variables (e.g., parasite species, model type, transparency indicators) were calculated.

Modeling methodology

Methodologically, ODE models predominated (17/18, 94.4%). One study adopted DDE to incorporate biological time delays linked to egg developmental stage [27]. One study (1/18, 5.56%) additionally incorporated PDE model that modeled human age-specific worm burden under the assumption of a continuous age structure [35]. Regarding population representation, most studies (17/18, 94.4%) employed PBM assuming homogeneous mixing, although several introduced host stratification in some degree. Only one study (1/18, 5.56%) used an ABM to capture individual-level heterogeneity and stochastic interactions [36]. While ABM can better represent clustered exposure and systematic non-adherence [16], the broader adoption is constrained by the need for high-resolution individual and environmental data (e.g., demographic attributes, dietary behaviors, adherence histories, and spatiotemporal distributions of intermediate hosts), as well as parameterization and computation [38]. In short, ODE-based PBM dominate the literature, with ABM approaches rarely applied.

Host structure and heterogeneity in transmission

All analyzed models explicitly incorporated the cycle of liver flukes and focused on the “human host–freshwater snail (first intermediate host)–freshwater fish (second intermediate host)” transmission triad. A subset of studies (6/18, 33.3%) also assessed the role of reservoir hosts (dogs and cats) in transmission. For O. viverrini, Bürli et al. [32] performed a host-specific basic reproduction number (R0) and reported host-specific R0 values of 0.2548 in dogs and 0.3640 in cats. Correspondingly, host-excluded R0 values was 1.1067 when dogs were excluded and 1.0981 when cats excluded, which indicated limited transmission contributions from these animal-reservoir hosts. In contrast, the host-specific R0 for humans was 1.0935, with a host-excluded R0 of 0.4016 when humans were removed from the transmission network. These findings demonstrated that humans function as the maintaining definitive host in the modeled system.

Although PBM commonly assume homogeneous susceptibility and behavior, several studies incorporated heterogeneity through stratification. Examples included: (1) exposure-based stratification, where Huang et al. [26] grouped humans by raw-fish consumption frequency (never, fewer than 5 times per year, 5 to 10 times per year and more than 10 times per year) to compare intervention strategies and showed that combined interventions outperformed any single approach; (2) intermediate-host stratification, where Wang et al. [30] categorized fish into juvenile and adult groups based on weight, thereby simulating human consumption of fish with varying sizes and identified reduction in fish-to-human transmission effectively curbed the spread of C. sinensis; and (3) age-structure, where Bürli et al. [35] stratified the population into five age groups (under 6, 6–16, 17–36, 37–50, and over 50 years), and found that targeting ages 17–50 lead to the largest reduction in eggs per gram of feces with a population-adjusted overall coverage of 70%, whereas treatment for children under 6 years provided negligible benefits.

To capture the influence of individual characteristics on the transmission, Kamber et al. [36] developed an ABM that incorporates individual attributes including gender, age, and worm burden. ABM outputs broadly aligned with PBM predictions for worm burden. The explicit inclusion of systematic adherence helped explain persistent high burdens observed in field surveys. The study also found that long timelines are required to reach low prevalence under common intervention scenarios. In summary, all models are based on the human-snail-fish cycle, while heterogeneity is usually handled by stratification and rarely by the introduction of ABM.

Dynamical impacts of latency period and developmental delays

Liver flukes must complete multiple developmental stages before it can establish a pathogenic infection in humans. Within snail hosts, eggs require approximately six weeks to three months to develop into cercariae; within fish hosts, cercariae require about six weeks to develop into mature metacercariae; and within humans, metacercariae mature into adult flukes in about one month [1]. Analysis of surveillance data indicated that longer developmental periods were associated with reduced transmission efficiency of C. sinensis [27]. These findings suggest that environmental factors such as temperature and precipitation may influence the transmission of the parasite, as they directly affect the developmental rate of the parasite in snail and fish hosts.

The effect of fish market and mobility

Trevisin et al. [37] developed a novel spatially explicit transmission model for O. viverrini that incorporates both hydrological connectivity and human-mediated fish trade. Building on a classical compartmental framework, they represent each subcatchment as a node with its local human, snail, and fish populations, and then link nodes via (1) upstream and downstream fish mobility along river corridors and (2) fish-market exchanges. Through simulations on three synthetic river networks, they demonstrate that the spatial connectivity parameters—fish-trade distance, mobility rate, and population distribution—profoundly influence human mean worm burden, snail infection prevalence, and fish metacercariae load. Longer trade distances increase worm burden and homogenize infection across regions, whereas fish mobility disperses the parasite and alleviates local burdens. Under a random population distribution, more nodes sustain endemic transmission; by contrast, under a downstream-accumulation distribution many nodes cannot maintain local transmission and, if isolated, the disease would gradually die out, and large downstream communities can promote spread via a scale effect. These results emphasize that neglecting spatial re-seeding pathways may lead to overly optimistic assessments of single-site interventions and that reducing the scale of fish trade can effectively curb cross-community transmission.

Parameter estimation and basic reproduction number

In the studies reviewed, various parameters (e.g., egg production rate and mortality rate) differed according to each study's specific focus (Supplementary Material 2), directly influencing the R0. Sensitivity analyses of different parameters identified the primary factors influencing R0 as follows: (1) mortality and recruitment rates of host populations; (2) clearance and production rates of cercariae, metacercariae, and eggs in the external environment; (3) transmission rates among hosts; and (4) inclusion of reservoir hosts (e.g., dogs and cats) in the model. Taking the studies by Zhang et al. [27] and Wang et al. [30] as examples, both observed high sensitivity of R0 to the mortality rate of freshwater fish. However, their different handling methods for parameters such as latent period duration and the probability of cercariae release led to differences in their respective R0 estimates. At present, only one study on C. sinensis considered the potential impact of reservoir hosts (such as dogs and cats), and the consistency of the impact of reservoir hosts on R0 still needs further study.

Intervention

Among the 18 reviewed studies (Table 2), 10 (55.6%) simulated the effects of various interventions. Among these 10 studies, seven simulated pharmacological (drug-based) interventions, while nine simulated non-pharmacological interventions (with some studies modeling both intervention types). Among these nine studies, six addressed health education, five incorporated environmental management, and two considered intermediate host control. Pharmacological intervention, particularly drug treatment, currently remains the primary means of controlling liver fluke infections. Huang et al. [25] maintained a fixed treatment coverage of 72% and compared the effects of pulse or continuous dosing on host infection prevalence under different dosing frequencies. The results indicated that multi-round drug administration—whether through pulse dosing or continuous dosing produced a more pronounced suppression of infection. Notably, under pulse dosing, the infection rate exhibited a wave like decline, whereas continuous dosing under ideal conditions resulted in a steady decrease. Furthermore, Huang et al. [26] found that only under high-intensity intervention conditions, for instance, 72% coverage with treatment administered twice per year over a period of 25 years, is it possible to maintain a stable population infection rate over the long term without rebound. Bürli et al. [32], in studying O. viverrini, proposed a treatment coverage threshold. He found that a minimum treatment coverage of 7% was necessary to reduce the R0 to below 1, provided that the education campaign and improved sanitation were maintained at 25.4%. Moreover, increasing treatment coverage to 44% with one intervention per year could decrease the average worm burden in humans to less than 1 within 20 years, thereby achieving the goal of elimination. In addition, Huang et al. [26] compared and evaluated the effects of two strategies of chemotherapy, targeting the whole population and key population with the practice of ingesting raw freshwater fish. The results showed that the whole population chemotherapy strategy was superior to the chemotherapy strategy only for high-risk groups under the same intervention frequency, population coverage, and intervention duration. Further analysis shows that with the increase of treatment frequency, the expansion of population coverage and the extension of intervention duration, the intervention effects of the two strategies will be correspondingly enhanced. However, even in the ideal case of reaching 100% coverage, if the treatment is carried out twice a year and stopped after 20 years of continuous intervention, the prevalence of the disease will still rebound. He et al. [28] extended the transmission modeling framework developed by Huang et al. [26] to perform a cost-effectiveness evaluation. Their analysis showed that targeting high-risk populations or infected individuals was more cost-effective than treating the whole population, and that albendazole was generally more cost-effective than praziquantel. Bürli et al. [35] considered the dependence of worm burden on human age and the population size of different age groups and proposed a targeted treatment strategy. Prioritizing treatment for individuals aged 17–50 (coverage 88.67%) significantly reduced worm burden, demonstrating greater efficacy than a whole population (coverage 70.00%) treatment strategy. This approach even suggests that treatment for children under 6 years old may be unnecessary. Field survey data showed that although MDA has been carried out for many years, moderate and severe worm burdens still persist. This phenomenon may be attributable to poor treatment adherence among some patients. Kamber et al.’s [36] modeling results indicated that if patient adherence is improved, the effectiveness of chemotherapy in controlling worm burden can be further enhanced. In a simulation study of fish-borne trematodes, Boerlage et al. [20] demonstrated that neither sustained chemotherapy targeting humans alone nor targeting animal reservoir hosts alone was sufficient to achieve disease control. Non-pharmacological interventions include various strategies, such as health education, environmental management, and intermediate host control. Health education aims to reduce the consumption of raw or undercooked freshwater fish by promoting hygienic dietary practices, thereby providing long-term preventive potential. However, its actual effectiveness is hampered by residents' reluctance and the inherent difficulty of altering long-established raw fish consumption habits. Gao et al. [24] found that even if the effect of health education was doubled, it was not enough to control clonorchiasis. This finding reinforces the prevailing view that health education must be integrated with other interventions to enhance its overall impact. Environmental management, for example constructing sanitary toilets and removing pollution sources around fish ponds, controls transmission by preventing eggs from entering aquatic environments. Notably, Bürli et al.’s [33] study indicated that if environmental management coverage reached a minimum of 70%, O. viverrini infection could potentially be eliminated within 35 years. In contrast, Huang et al.’s [26] model showed that although a single environmental intervention exhibits a gradual initial effect, it can sustainably reduce infection rates for as long as 50 years. Through sensitivity analysis, Gao et al. [24] identified the eradication of freshwater snails as one of the most effective non-pharmacological interventions. Innovative strategies, such as fish vaccination, have gradually attracted attention in recent years. This approach aims to reduce the risk of human infection by avoiding fish infection with cercariae. Modeling simulations conducted by Haque et al. [29] have shown that the impact of vaccination is limited when coverage is low, but its potential for long-term disease control improves substantially as coverage increases. In parallel, delayed stocking of fish has been identified as a viable complementary strategy. A modeling study by Boerlage et al. [20] indicates that fish should reach a weight of over 25 g at the time of stocking to bring the R0 below 1, requiring at least 54 days of rearing in parasite-free environments. When combined with 100% chemotherapy coverage in humans, the necessary stocking weight to achieve R0 < 1 can be reduced to as low as 14 g.

Transparency indicators

Table 3 shows the overall situation of transparency indicators based on the classification of liver flukes. Discussion

This review synthesizes mechanistic transmission models for human liver flukes, and has the following core findings. First, modeling remains heavily concentrated in East and Southeast Asia and is dominated by population-based ODE frameworks, which are valuable for policy exploration but often simplify heterogeneity and spatial structure. Second, across model settings and structures, the adoption of integrated interventions (mass drug administration, health education, and environmental improvements) is more effective than single measure. Third, the model credibility is weakened by limited uncertainty analysis, scarce longitudinal validation, and minimal code sharing. Fourth, such researches are insufficient as the impact of reservoir host, fish mobility and trade, climate, seasonality, and socioeconomic aspects on disease transmission.

Dominance of ODE models and need for ABM

ODE-based PBM dominated the literature, largely because they are computationally efficient and require relatively less data, but their homogeneous-mixing assumption could underestimate or overestimate transmission risk and intervention effects. Some PBM partially addressed heterogeneity through stratification (e.g., by raw-fish consumption [26] or fish growth stage [30]), yet still cannot represent individual contact networks. A few studies used more complex frameworks, DDE to capture developmental delays (longer latency reducing R0) [27] and PDE to incorporate age-structured worm burden and age-dependent intervention impacts [35]. Although ABM can capture behavior heterogeneity more realistically, the application remains limited due to the fine-grained data required for parameterization [38]. Given that C. sinensis and O. viverrini are self-fertilizing hermaphrodites, intensity-based modeling using egg counts may be especially appropriate; however, many C. sinensis models rely on prevalence alone, potentially underestimating transmission. As uncertainty is better quantified and epidemiological data increase especially on life-stage processes and reservoir hosts, models should yield more reliable R0 estimates and stronger evidence for control planning.

Intervention strategies and effectiveness

Across models, MDA remains the dominant intervention lever, and the conclusion is consistent: sustained and repeated treatment generally outperforms single-round therapy, particularly for durable suppression and progress toward elimination targets. Models also converge on the importance of high coverage and adherence, but systematic non-adherence can maintain transmission even when nominal coverage appears adequate. Non-pharmaceutical interventions such as health education and environmental improvement have long term advantages, although they are slow to take effect. Overall, integrated strategies combining chemotherapy with behavior change and environmental improvement tend to be more effective than single component.

The need for animal-targeted measures depends on local reservoir contribution. While humans are consistently central, conclusions about cats and dogs diverge. One study suggested reservoir contributions are small and human-focused interventions alone could achieve elimination [32], whereas another indicated cats and dogs need to be treated at the same time [20]. This contrast implies that reservoir management should be assessed contextually, using models to quantify local animal contributions and guide setting-specific strategies.

Models also indicated that fish mobility and trade can drive spatial re-seeding, potentially undermining control strategies even with high local coverage. Trevisin et al. [37] suggests expanding trade networks can increase burden and homogenize worm loads across regions, pointing to the need in multiregional cooperation. Meanwhile, some measures targeting intermediate hosts remain operationally constrained. Snail control is difficult given ecological diversity [39,40] and potential ecological costs of molluscicides [1], and fish vaccination remains experimental without field-validated efficacy [41].

Although integrated intervention generally outperforms single measure, strategies that achieve elimination in one setting may not be transferable to other areas with different baseline burdens (e.g., prevalence) [42]. This context sensitivity has not yet been adequately characterized. In practice, feasible interventions should be prioritized according to the local epidemiological context, with cost-effectiveness evidence used to guide the allocation of limited resources. Overall, modeling supports integrated, cost-effective strategies tailored to local conditions.

Data limitations and transparency

Model development is constrained by the limitation of fine-grained data [43]. As neglected tropical diseases, clonorchiasis and opisthorchiasis lack comprehensive surveillance in many regions [44], so key parameters (e.g., reservoir infection rates, snail dynamics, and human behavioral patterns) are often assumed or extrapolated from other parasites, increasing uncertainty. Reporting practices also limit confidence and reproducibility: only 66.7% of studies conducted uncertainty analyses, 27.8% validated models against longitudinal field data, and only one (5.6%) study shared code. These gaps hinder assessment of predictive accuracy and restrict the ability to replicate and build on existing models.

Future perspectives

First, quantify reservoir host contributions in explicit ecological contexts. Although humans are consistently central definitive hosts, model conclusions diverge on whether cats and dogs are negligible or essential for elimination. Future work should identify the conditions under which reservoir transmission meaningfully sustains R0 (e.g., local host prevalence, densities and sanitation) and estimate the animal intervention coverage required.

Second, address heterogeneity to better approximate real-world transmission. Key challenges include behavioral and adherence heterogeneity, household clustering, and spatial heterogeneity and fish mobility. To more accurately characterize the observed propagation patterns and intervention responses, ABM, hybrid models, and machine learning can be further developed [45].

Third, integrate climate and socioeconomic drivers, while accounting for economic trade-offs. Climate shapes aquatic habitats and intermediate-host ecology, while socioeconomic conditions influence sanitation, dietary practices, aquaculture systems, and MDA coverage. Fish trade adds an economic dimension: connectivity may drive reinfection, yet trade restrictions may be infeasible given economic value. Future models should therefore incorporate climate and socioeconomic covariates and evaluate realistic control levers along key points in the trade and supply chain (e.g., targeted inspection, risk-based controls, and market-oriented interventions) rather than assuming broad trade suppression.

Fourth, modeling should become more data-driven and reproducible. The separation between field epidemiologists and model developers can lead to “model-driven data” practices. Model developers should actively participate in designing field studies that address key uncertainties (e.g., metacercariae density in fish, snail populations, reservoir-host infection intensity, and contact pathways) and standardizing transparency practices (uncertainty analysis, longitudinal validation, and code sharing) to enhance reproducibility. When possible, research endpoints should also include downstream health outcomes (e.g., long-term cancer burden) to support integrated benefit assessments.

Conclusion

This review comprehensively evaluates the application of transmission dynamics in liver fluke. The research shows that current transmission dynamics models of liver fluke are mainly PBM and typically rely on ODE to represent host lifecycle transitions and transmission dynamics. The PBM with appropriate stratification is the most practical in the current research situation, but it still faces challenges such as insufficient data fineness and incomplete consideration of propagation heterogeneity. The evaluation of interventions shows that continuous and high coverage of drug administration is effective, and improving compliance is critical to achieving long term control effects. Non-pharmaceutical interventions such as health education and environmental improvement have long term advantages, although they are slow to take effect. Combined intervention has more advantages than single intervention strategy. Future research needs to further refine the model structure, enhance the quality of data collection, and emphasize the transparency of model methods to improve the credibility and practicability of research results. This review highlights the urgent need for One Health modeling approaches that integrate human, animal, and environmental data to support evidence-based control strategies for liver fluke infections.
