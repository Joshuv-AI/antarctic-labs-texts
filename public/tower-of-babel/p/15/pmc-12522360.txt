Thyroid pathology image classification via multi-scale feature fusion and multi-instance learning
Authors: Xiangzhi Li, Guanxin Liu, Mengmeng Sun, Lu Wang, Bingdou He, Kefen Zhang, Shimei Zhao, Kaisheng Xie, Yuwei Jiang, Yajun Ying, Ning Liao, Xiaobo Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global incidence of thyroid cancer has significantly increased, while traditional pathological diagnosis remains time-consuming and expert-dependent. This study develops an auxiliary diagnostic tool designed to reduce the workload of pathologists and improve diagnostic accuracy.MethodsOur study utilized 543 WSIs from Liuzhou Cancer Hospital for model development, employing a novel multi-feature fusion architecture that combines RetCCL, iBOT, and DINO embeddings. We systematically evaluated stain normalization and multi-scale analysis across four multiple-instance learning (MIL) frameworks: CLAM-SB (single-branch), CLAM-MB (multi-branch), DTFD (double-tier), and LA-MIL (location-aware). The method was rigorously validated on an independent set of 128 WSIs from Taizhou Cancer Hospital.ResultsThe results show that stain normalization, multi-scale fusion, and multi-feature fusion significantly improve classification performance. In 10-fold cross-validation on the internal dataset, the system demonstrated significant improvements over the baseline RetCCL model: AUC (0.9900 vs. 0.9629), accuracy (0.9594 vs. 0.8951), with relative improvements of 2.8% in AUC and 7.2% in accuracy. Precision increased by 11.5% (0.9434 vs. 0.8461) and F1-score by 9.8% (0.9511 vs. 0.8665). On the external validation dataset, the model maintained robust performance with an AUC of 0.9584, accuracy of 0.9070, precision of 0.9247, and F1-score of 0.9348, confirming its reliability and applicability.ConclusionsWe propose a weakly supervised MIL framework integrating multi-scale analysis and cross-model feature fusion for thyroid cancer diagnosis. Our method showed promising and consistent results across internal and external datasets. While further clinical validation and workflow integration are needed, the results suggest the potential of this approach to assist pathologists in diagnostic workflows, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

Background

Automated diagnosis of thyroid pathology images has become a key research focus in medical image analysis, with significant recent advancements. Early methods relied on traditional image processing techniques, such as edge detection, thresholding, and morphological operations, to extract features like nuclei and follicles [1, 2]. However, these approaches had limited generalization and diagnostic accuracy when applied to complex pathological images [3]. The introduction of machine learning algorithms, such as support vector machines and random forests, marked a notable advancement, enabling classification and detection based on handcrafted features like texture, shape, and color [4, 5]. Despite improvements, reliance on manually designed features limited the adaptability of these methods to diverse pathological images [6].

In recent years, deep learning techniques, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have significantly advanced thyroid pathology image analysis. End-to-end diagnostic systems based on deep learning can learn features directly from raw images, improving both diagnostic accuracy and robustness [7, 8]. The integration of multi-instance learning (MIL) and weakly supervised learning (WSL) has further enhanced system performance, enabling precise classification and segmentation with image-level labels or sparse point annotations [9, 10]. For example, Campanella et al. [10] introduced a weakly supervised deep learning framework for whole-slide image analysis, achieving clinical-grade computational pathology performance. Moreover, the incorporation of attention mechanisms and contrastive learning has further optimized model performance [11, 12].

Despite the promise of deep learning in thyroid pathology diagnosis, the scarcity of high-quality annotated data limits model training and generalization [13]. To address these challenges, the proposed research seeks to enhance the diagnostic process by incorporating multi-scale image feature fusion, utilizing multiple feature extractors, and employing multi-instance learning techniques, thereby advancing the automation of thyroid pathology image analysis.

Materials and methods

Database

The dataset consists of 672 original pathological slides from the Pathology Departments of Liuzhou Cancer Hospital and Taizhou Cancer Hospital (Table 1), all stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and containing thyroid tumor tissues with confirmed diagnoses. These slides were digitized using a high-resolution digital slide scanner at 20x magnification, resulting in high-pixel digital images for analysis. Diagnostic conclusions were independently verified by two pathologists with over ten years of experience.

Image preprocessing

Staining normalization

Variations in histopathological slide preparation introduce differences in the appearance of H&E-stained tissue samples. In digitized pathology images, staining inconsistencies can adversely affect the accuracy and robustness of classification models. This study employs the automated stain vector estimation algorithm proposed by Macenko et al. [14], combined with color deconvolution, for image preprocessing. As shown in Fig. 1A, the source image, target image, and normalized results demonstrate that this method effectively reduces color variability during standardization while preserving critical image details.

Region segmentation

A tissue region segmentation model based on AFFormer [15] was trained using 100 thyroid pathology images and corresponding tissue masks from the Liuzhou set. Compared to the Otsu’s Method (OTSU) algorithm, which misclassifies contaminated and blank regions as tissue, the AFFormer-based method achieves higher accuracy, effectively delineating tissue regions and enhancing segmentation reliability (Fig. 1B).

Data augmentation

This experiment employs five data augmentation techniques: image compression, random rotation, gamma correction, color adjustment, and gradient-based brightness variation. These methods serve to reduce noise, enhance data diversity, improve contrast and detail, and increase robustness to variations in color and lighting. Each technique is applied randomly with equal probability to image patches, thereby enhancing model performance and generalization capabilities.

The data preprocessing workflow involves WSI segmentation using the optimized AFFormer model, generating PNG mask images. A non-overlapping sliding window then crops these images into 256 × 256 patches, with window coordinates (x1, y1, x2, y2) stored in an NPY file (Fig. 1C). The pipeline also addresses staining variability, tissue segmentation, and image augmentation to enhance model performance and generalizability.

Baseline experiment

RetCCL [16] is a clustering-guided contrastive learning framework specifically designed for extracting image features at the whole-slide image level, offering superior histopathological feature representation compared to ImageNet-pretrained models.

For classification, three MIL-based methods were employed: Clustering-constrained-attention Multiple-instance Learning (CLAM) [17], a weakly supervised attention-based MIL model that enhances representation aggregation without requiring annotated WSIs, with two variants: single attention branch (CLAM-SB) and multiple attention branches(CLAM-MB); Double-Tier Feature Distillation (DTFD) [18], a two-tier MIL framework utilizing “pseudo-bags” to enhance feature extraction; and Local Attention graph-based Transformer (LA-MIL) [19], a graph-based Transformer model integrating Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with local attention mechanisms to model spatial dependencies while reducing computational complexity. These methods were applied to classify thyroid pathological features extracted via RetCCL.

Multi-scale experiment

As shown in Fig. 1D, the process begins by segmenting the preprocessed 20× magnified image into 256 × 256 patches. These patches are then expanded by a 1:1 ratio around their centers to generate 512 × 512 patches at 10× magnification, which are subsequently resized back to 256 × 256. The RetCCL method is then used to extract features from both the 10× and 20× magnified images, followed by feature fusion to integrate information from different magnification levels, providing a more comprehensive representation of the image’s features. Finally, the fused features are input into a multi-instance classifier to yield the final classification results.

Feature fusion experiment

The overall framework of the multi-feature fusion classification model closely follows the structure of the dual-scale classification network CLAM-SB, consisting of three primary components: image preprocessing, feature extraction, and multi-instance learning-based classification. However, the key distinction lies in the incorporation of additional feature extractors. In addition to the original RetCCL feature extractor, the model integrates self-supervised learning-based feature extractors, specifically the Image BERT Pre-training with Online Tokenizer (iBOT) [20, 21] and Self-Distillation with No Labels (DINO) [22, 23] feature extractors.

This study adopts a parallel fusion approach. First, feature extractors iBOT and DINO generate feature vectors for each image patch. A zero-padding method expands the 1536-dimensional iBOT and 768-dimensional DINO vectors to 4096 dimensions, matching the feature length of the 4096-dimensional RetCCL vectors. The three sets of feature vectors are then concatenated into a single vector (Fig. 1E). Importantly, no information in the original vectors is modified; they are simply juxtaposed. Finally, the fused feature vector is input into the CLAM-SB multiple instance learning classifier for final classification.

Staining normalization and non-staining normalization experiments

To mitigate potential variations in image quality and ensure the accuracy of experimental results, stain normalization was applied. The findings indicate that, overall, the model’s AUC and ACC values improved, demonstrating that stain normalization effectively enhances the model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative samples while also increasing classification accuracy across all categories (Fig. 3A). Therefore, stain normalization can be considered a valuable preprocessing step.

Single-scale and multi-scale experiments

Following stain normalization, we further examined the impact of multi-scale analysis on classification performance. Features fused from both 10× and 20× magnification scales were input into various classifiers and compared with the previous single-scale 20× approach. The results revealed that, with the exception of a slight decrease in the F1-score for the LA-MIL classifier, the dual-scale fused feature model significantly outperformed the single-scale model. Notably, the CLAM-SB model achieved the highest AUC and ACC values among all classifiers (Fig. 3B and C).

Different feature extractors for feature extraction experiments

Based on the findings from the preceding analysis, this study selected the CLAM-SB multiple instance classification algorithm as the final classification method, and the pathological images underwent stain normalization and multi-scale feature fusion processing. To minimize experimental variability, a ten-fold cross-validation approach was employed. The Liuzhou dataset was randomly divided into ten subsets, with nine subsets used for training and one subset reserved for testing in each iteration. Corresponding classification results were obtained from each experiment.

The results indicate that all four evaluation metrics for each individual feature extractor exceeded 0.84, with the DINO classifier demonstrating the best overall performance. Notably, when the fused features derived from three feature extractors were used as input, the performance of the classification model was further improved, achieving an average AUC of 0.9900 (95% CI: 0.9804–0.9997), an average accuracy of 0.9594 (95% CI: 0.9439–0.9749), an average precision of 0.9434 (95% CI: 0.9082–0.9786), and an average F1-score of 0.9510 (95% CI: 0.9350–0.9674). These results surpass those obtained using individual feature extractors (Figs. 4 and 5).

External validation experiments

In the previous sections, we detailed the design, training process, and performance of the model on the Liuzhou set. Through a series of experiments, the proposed fusion model demonstrated excellent classification performance on this dataset, indicating its feasibility for thyroid pathology image analysis. To further evaluate its generalizability, we selected the Taizhou set as an external validation set.

The results show that on the Taizhou set, the fusion model achieved an AUC of 0.9584 (95% CI: 0.9188–0.9840), Accuracy of 0.9070 (95% CI: 0.8527–0.9535), Precision of 0.9247 (95% CI: 0.8685–0.9691), and F1-score of 0.9348 (95% CI: 0.8973–0.9672) (Fig. 6). While these results are slightly lower compared to those on the Liuzhou set, the overall performance remains strong, with high consistency across metrics. This suggests that the model excels in overall performance, positive class prediction, and sample differentiation, demonstrating its ability to adapt well to the diverse scenarios likely encountered in real-world clinical applications.

Feature visualization

To assist clinicians in more accurately focusing on tumor regions, the study further constructs heatmaps based on the attention mechanism of the optimal model (CLAM). Specifically, attention scores, which are not converted into probability distributions via the softmax function, are transformed into percentages and mapped to RGB colors. These colors are then displayed on the whole-slide pathology image according to their original spatial positions, creating the heatmap. In this heatmap, red represents high-attention regions, corresponding to patches that provide strong evidence and high contributions to the model’s prediction, while blue indicates low-attention regions, corresponding to patches with relatively lower contributions. This visualization helps illustrate the relative importance of different regions within the WSI for the final WSI-level prediction.

The goal of constructing the heatmap is not to obtain pixel-level annotations of the WSI, but rather to serve as a visual tool to assist clinicians in making judgments. Therefore, it only highlights approximate regions. A comparison with pathologist-annotated tumor locations revealed that this method effectively identifies the key regions of interest (Fig. 7).

Disscusion

This study presents a deep learning-based automatic classification model for thyroid tumor pathological images, specifically designed to assist physicians in diagnosing tumor malignancy more efficiently. Deep learning has been shown to effectively support histopathological image analysis, particularly in handling large-scale whole-slide images [24]. By leveraging the AFFormer segmentation model, retrained with domain-specific data and medical image augmentation techniques, we effectively addressed the challenge of whole-slide image size and achieved accurate segmentation of regions of interest. To overcome the difficulty of pathological image annotation, a multi-instance learning approach was introduced, complemented by Macenko staining normalization and diverse data augmentation.

A key contribution of this study is the integration of multi-scale feature fusion. By incorporating image features from both 10× and 20× magnifications and comparing multiple classification models, we ultimately selected CLAM-SB as the optimal classifier. Furthermore, a novel feature fusion strategy combining representations from three different feature extractors (RetCCL, iBOT and DINO) demonstrated superior classification performance compared to models utilizing a single extractor. External validation on the Taizhou dataset further confirmed the robustness and generalization capability of our approach, with AUC, accuracy, precision, and F1-score all exceeding 0.9. Additionally, the attention-based heatmaps generated by the model can highlight diagnostically relevant key regions to a certain extent, assisting clinicians in more quickly locating and identifying tumor areas that may have diagnostic significance, thereby enhancing the model’s interpretability and clinical utility.

Nonetheless, the study has limitations. First, it focused solely on papillary thyroid carcinoma, the most common subtype, due to limited availability of images for rarer variants such as follicular and medullary carcinoma. Future work should expand the dataset to cover a broader spectrum of thyroid tumors for more comprehensive classification. Second, essential clinical variables, such as patient age, gender, medical and family history, were not incorporated. Integrating clinical data with WSI features in future research could improve diagnostic accuracy and enable more holistic assessments, advancing personalized medicine and precision oncology.

Moreover, the 10× magnification images were simulated by extracting larger regions from 20× WSIs and resizing, rather than obtained from independent 10× scans. While this enabled multi-scale context integration, it may not fully capture the optical resolution and biological fidelity of authentic 10× images. Future studies will aim to acquire genuine 10× WSIs to support more rigorous multi-scale analyses.

In terms of feature extraction, this study adopted a sequential processing strategy using three encoders: RetCCL, iBOT, and DINO. On an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, processing a single WSI took approximately 34 min, with peak memory usage around 3 GB. The model comprises roughly 134 million parameters, with weights occupying about 513 MB of GPU memory. While sequential processing increases inference time, it was deliberately selected to significantly reduce hardware demands, making the approach particularly suitable for resource-constrained clinical environments. This strategy also improves system stability, simplifies debugging and maintenance, and allows each feature extractor to fully utilize its complementary strengths. Future work will explore parallelization and model compression techniques to enhance efficiency and support broader clinical deployment.

Conclusions

To address the key challenges in pathological image analysis, including the large size of whole-slide images, annotation difficulties, and the limitations of single-scale information, this study proposes an efficient and accurate automated classification model for thyroid pathological images by integrating multi-scale features, multiple feature extractors, and a multi-instance learning framework. The model demonstrates strong classification performance on both internal and external datasets. Furthermore, the attention-based heatmaps generated by the model visually highlight diagnostically relevant key regions. Serving as a coarse-grained interpretative tool, these heatmaps facilitate clinicians in quickly locating and identifying tumor areas of diagnostic significance within the whole-slide images. In conclusion, this study provides a novel approach to the automated classification of digital pathology images and offers a clinically applicable tool with the potential to reduce pathologists’ workload, improve diagnostic accuracy and efficiency, and support the early detection and treatment of thyroid tumors.
