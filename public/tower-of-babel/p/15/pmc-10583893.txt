Incidence and cost of clinically recognized vertebral fracture in urban China is dramatically increasing
Authors: Zhenlin Zhang
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

Osteoporotic is an age-related disease, and the world is experiencing or about to face the impact of population ageing, which means an increase in fragility fractures, especially in China. But, the harm of osteoporosis has not been given the attention it deserves. Unlike diseases of hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, cerebral stroke, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, there is no proper chronic disease management system established for osteoporosis in China.

The lack of deserved attention requires clinicians to provide disease-related evidence to attract more attention and change public perception. Song and colleagues reported meaningful findings on the incidence of vertebral fractures1 and hip fracture2 in urban China. Based on the national medical insurance database, these two studies are the largest epidemiological surveys of fragility fractures to date.

In their studies, although the total absolute number of cases is gradually increasing, the incidence of hip fractures in urban China was found to be stable, which is similar to the pattern of previously reported for most high-income nations2. The pharmaceutical management department of the Chinese government, doctors, and pharmaceutical enterprises have made tremendous contributions to this end. However, the total absolute number and the incidence of clinically recognized vertebral fractures both have continued to rise1.

The high mortality of hip fractures and associated complications (such as pneumonia, infection, thrombosis, etc.) has been given a lot of attention. But the harms of vertebral fractures were still underestimated. In fact, the increased all-cause mortality rate of vertebral fractures after 1 year was 3.1–10.04%3,4; worse, after 3 years, the mortality rate rose to 46%5. It is worth mentioning that vertebral fractures were also recognized as sentinel events for injuries associated with higher morbidity and mortality. The falls account for the majority of hip fractures but are only responsible for a quarter of vertebral fractures6, which also explains why the effect of fall prevention guidelines on the prevention of hip fracture is obvious, but on the vertebral fractures is limited. In a sense, vertebral fractures more directly reflect the severity of osteoporosis.

Due to the silent nature of vertebral fractures and the confusion caused by old vertebral fractures, the incidence rate of vertebral fracture is hard to obtain for a long time, and the data obtained from scanning surveys or cross-sectional studies are often related to the prevalence rate. Using definitions of clinically recognized vertebral fractures and the combination of data from claims-related medical insurance databases is a subtle method to present the real-world epidemiology data.

Another interesting result is that, for people over 50 years old, the incidence of vertebral fracture in females was nearly three times higher than that in males, but the prevalence of vertebral fracture was similar among men and women in the previous cross-sectional studies7,8. A possible explanation is that men are more likely to have vertebral fractures due to accidents at a young age, while women are more likely to have vertebral fractures because of postmenopausal osteoporosis9.

The results about personal health expenses and the medical burden on society were also presented. The total costs for hospitalization for vertebral fractures have increased by approximately six times from 2013 to 2017. The enormous cost of treatment forces us to seek alternative and effective prevention strategies, including osteoporosis-specific chronic disease management patterns. Besides, by relying on medical history inquiries and medical records of height reduction, patients with high risks of osteoporotic fractures can be easily, quickly, and cost-effectively detected.

Due to the availability of data, asymptomatic cases, untreated cases, and those living in rural areas were not included in their studies. But the concerns about those patients are also expressed, especially in China with a large population and unbalanced development. In the future, for those over 60 years of age, annual anteroposterior and lateral chest radiography are recommended for early detection of vertebral fractures, especially in asymptomatic patients. Once there are radiological signs of vertebral fractures, standard antiosteoporosis therapy is initiated to reduce the risk of future fractures. What is more, the allocation of medical resources should be optimized, public health efforts, and personalized treatment should be devoted equally in urban and rural areas.
