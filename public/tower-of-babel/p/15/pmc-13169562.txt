Gender dynamics affecting women’s uptake of maternal health service in agrarian and pastoral settings in Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Agumasie Semahegn, Gizachew Tadele Tiruneh, Alemnesh Hailemariam Mirkuzie, Mesele Damte Argaw, Nebreed Fesseha, Mikiyas Teferi, Hillina Tadesse, Addis Girma, Mebrie Belete, Chala Tesfaye, Muluken Dessalegn Muluneh, Sintayehu Abebe Woldie, Meskerem Abebaw, Rediet Daniel, Zemzem Mohammed, Yibeltal Kiflie Alemayehu, Wubegzier Mekonnen, Salsawit Shifarraw, Lidiya Tefera, Frank DelPizzo, Addis Tamire, Temesgen Ayehu, Dessalew Emaway, Misrak Makonnen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLack of access to essential maternal health service remains a significant public health challenge in low-income countries. Gender dynamics play a critical role in determining women’s access to and use of maternal health services. We assessed the influence of gender dynamics on women’s use of maternal health services in Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted using a mixed method in 10 selected districts from the agrarian and pastoral contexts of Ethiopia. Systematic sampling was used to recruit 3,097 women to collect quantitative data using a web-based platform. Multi-level mixed-effects logistic regression analysis was carried out to identify predictors of maternal health services utilization using adjusted odds ratio (AOR) at 95% Confidence Interval (CI). Purposively selected 148 participants were interviewed, audio recorded, transcribed, and coded using ATLAS.ti software and thematically analyzed.ResultsThe proportion of pregnant women who had antenatal care was 65.5% (95% CI: 63.8–71.0%). Most pregnant women gave birth at home (68.4%; 95% CI:66.8–70.0%). About one-third (95%CI:30.4–33.6%) attended immediate postnatal care. Women who had the autonomy to seek maternal health services, attended at least primary school, women and partner engaged in paid job, partner accompanied, strong social support, and accepting attitude towards equitable gender-norms were the factors associated with use of maternal health services. Nevertheless, women who lived in a far distance from the health facilities, had large family sizes, and live in pastoralist contexts negatively impacted maternal health services use. The old-fashioned gender-norms, lack of access to finance, fear for their safety, and a lack locally-tailored culturally-sensitive care were the barriers to maternal health service use.ConclusionsMaternal health services uptake in Ethiopia is limited and marked by huge disparity between agrarian and pastoral contexts. Geographic inaccessibility, and gender dynamics including limited autonomy, lack of culturally- and women-friendly care, inequitable gender-norms, low men’s accompaniment, and large family size affected maternal health services use. Hence, re-designing maternal health service delivery platforms to make culturally-acceptable and accessible care, transform gender barrier through enhancing women’s autonomy, encouraging male’s accompaniment, and strengthening social support are crucial in the primary setting.

Background

Gender dynamics is an embedded relationships and practical interactions among people based on the socially constructed concept of gender that creates power relationship [1]. Gender dynamics influenced maternal health service use globally while maternal health has remained as one of the health program priorities to end preventable mortality and address inequities [2]. A s a result maternal mortality has shown a substantial decline by 34% over the last couple of decades between 2000 and 2020 [3]. The highest maternal deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3], where access to quality care has seriously influenced by gender-related barriers [4], mainly in sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries, that characterized with low skilled birth attendance ranging from 24% in Chad to 96% in South Africa [5]. The observed extremely high maternal death rates reflect the inequalities in ensuring access to quality health services between countries [6–9]. Lack of equitable access to quality maternal health service remains a public health challenge in LMICs, resulting in severe maternal morbidities and mortality [10]. Maternal ill-health is not only a biomedical interplay [2, 11, 12]; it is overwhelmingly affected by social determinants ranging from the individual to the system level [2]. Women’s autonomy in health decisionmaking, access to media, partners’ support in seeking healthcare and household economic conditions have a significant impact on the access to and use of maternal health services [13]. Gender-role disparities, caused inequities in access to maternal health services that seriously affect maternal health and well-being during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period [2, 5–8, 14]. Lack of access due to geographical distance, poor infrastructure including, roads, worry for safety, family dynamics, and culture are the barriers to women’s maternal health use in LMICs [5, 10]. Financial constraints, cost of transportation and logistics appeared as major barriers to women’s maternal health services utilization [5, 10, 15, 16]. Furthermore, the presence of gender norms, including limited male engagement and support, poor access to resources, low women’s decision-making autonomy or lack of voice in the decision-making process [17–19], living in a remote rural village, and women’s social networks [17, 18], played a significant role in influencing pregnant women’s access to maternal health services. Older women in the family has a paramount influence on skilled birth attendance [18, 19]. In addition, men are heads of household, occupying major decision-making roles and having control over women’s reproductive issues that affect access to maternal health services [20, 21]. Inequitable social norms and expectations [16, 19, 21], along with women having high workloads and a lack of fair division of labor with their partners [16, 19], affect the use of maternal health services. In Ethiopia, the maternal health service has shown substantial progress for the last two decades which resulting in a reduction of the maternal mortality ratio from 871 to 412 per 100,000 live births [22, 23]. However, more than half of women have still given birth at home [23]. Gender-related barriers, such as women’s decision-making power, and men’s engagement in accompanying women during antenatal care, institutional delivery, and postnatal care [24, 25] and women’s preference for female healthcare providers [26], are critical factors to improve maternal health in pastoral communities in Ethiopia. There is a paucity of research evidence on gender dynamics and its impact on maternal health service use in the agrarian and pastoral settings of Ethiopia. Hence, this study assessed the influence of gender dynamics on women’s maternal health service use in the agrarian and pastoral settings of Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting

Ethiopia is the second most populous country after Nigeria in Africa, with an estimated population of more than 120 million, and is categorized under a low-income country located in the Horn of Africa [27]. Although Ethiopia strives to provide access to maternal health through primary healthcare [28, 29], the uptake of maternal health is still low with a huge disparity among agrarian and pastoral communities. Hence, this cross-sectional study is a part of the community-based quasi-experimental implementation research that is being conducted in 10 selected districts from five selected regional states, namely Chifra and Telalek districts from Afar; Seka Chekorsa and Shebe Sambo districts from Oromia; Bensa and Bona Zuria districts from Sidama; Degahbur, Gursum and Gunagado districts from Somali; and Shay Bench district from South Ethiopia. The study sites were selected purposely because they have high home birth rates both in the agrarian and pastoral contexts. This study was conducted from August to October 2023.

Study design

Study designA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted using sequential explanatory mixed methods (quantitative survey followed by qualitative study) among women (15–49 years) who gave birth in the previous 12 months (July 2022-August 2023). The quantitative component of the study used a cross-sectional household survey through stratified groups in the agrarian and pastoral settings. The sub-districts (called Kebeles in Ethiopia) that are far from the district capital and with high home birth rates were selected from the agrarian and pastoral contexts. This study was a baseline assessment for the two evidence-based intervention delivery approaches; namely, community-based lifesaving maternal and newborn health intervention packages [30] to prevent anemia, postpartum hemorrhage [31], and sepsis [32–34], and Participatory Learning and Action (PLA) approach [35, 36] to address gender-related barriers and strengthen women’s agency, to improve access to maternal health services and uptake, and guided by a blend of RE-AIM [37] and CIFR [38] frameworks.

Sampling methods

The sample size for the household-based cross-sectional baseline assessment was calculated using StatCalc [39], considering the double population formula for the quasi-experimental study design. The assumptions made to calculate the sample size were considering a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), the margin of error (d = 5%), design effect (e = 1.5), power of 80% and two-sided alpha error set at 0.05 to detect 12% points change in the uptake of institutional delivery and women’s decision-making autonomy on maternal health services at the end of the project. The study setting was stratified into agrarian and pastoral settings. Accordingly, 3,097 mothers (1,242 from agrarian and 1,855 from pastoral contexts) who gave birth during the previous 12 months at the data collection time were included in the study. A two-stage cluster sampling technique was used to select the households for in-person interviews. In the first stage, Primary Health Care Units (PHCUs) having a catchment population with a high rate of home birth were purposefully selected. There were 54 Kebeles(sub-districts) in the catchments of the selected PHCUs, which were the primary sampling units. Kebele (sub-district) is the smallest functional administrative structural unit in Ethiopia. In each sub-district, samples were allocated proportionally to the estimated population size. The research team identified the list of households with mothers of infants 0–11 months of age with their unique household identifiers [40] in consultation with Health Extension Workers’ family folders from health posts, and kebele leaders. This list was used as a sampling frame by the data collectors. Then, a systematic sampling technique (with Kth values ranging from 4 to 16 according to the eligible household density in the health post catchment) was used to identify targeted households from the sampling frame for women’s interviews (Fig. 1). Fig. 1Schematic presentation of the sampling methods Maximum variation sampling schemes [41] were used to recruit qualitative study participants to explore the unique perspectives of women and close family or relatives, perceptions, and experiences of community leaders, mothers, program managers, and healthcare providers. Data were collected through In-Depth Interviews (IDIs), key informant interviews (KIIs), and focus group discussions (FGDs) to gain a full understanding of the individual and group/community perceptions and experiences regarding maternal health services and vulnerability factors. Then the theoretical sampling principles were used to collect information until the level of data saturation reached [42]. The level of saturation was attained after six interviews as per the recommendation of existing evidence [43].

Data collection and quality assurance methods

Data were collected using mixed methods (quantitative and qualitative). The quantitative data were collected using a structured household survey tool customized from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS). The tool was translated into local dialects (Amharic, Affaan Oromo, and Af-Somali). To assure the quality of data, forward and back translation of the tool was carried out from English to dialect to strictly check consistency of the language in accordance with the WHO recommendation [44]. Interviews were conducted using local languages (Amharic, Afarigna, Somali, Affan Oromo and Sidamagna). Experienced data collectors were recruited and trained for two days, including practical demonstration to calibrate their capability to conduct interviews. Supportive supervision was provided for both quantitative and qualitative teams, and collected data were checked for completeness and consistency. The women’s interviews yielded data on sociodemographic and previous obstetric history, including women’s autonomy to seek maternal and newborn health (MNH) services across the continuum of care, men’s engagement, and access to and control over financial resources for MNH care. A web-based digital platform (i.e., SurveyCTO) was used to collect the quantitative data among women (15–49 years) who gave birth during the previous 12 months. The qualitative data were collected through 34 IDIs; 35 KIIs with community leaders, program managers, community health workers, and healthcare providers; and 79 discussants in nine FGDs with women who gave birth at a health facility, had a home birth, and were youth women. Interviews and FGDs were conducted in local dialects and audio-recorded with the informants’ permission. The key informant and in-depth interviews took 45 to 60 min, and FGD sessions took from 60 to 90 min.

Measurements of the variables

The antenatal care follow-up, place delivery, and postnatal care were the three continuums of care considered in this article. Place of delivery was the outcome variable and was assessed based on women’s self-report binary responses: home delivery and health facility delivery by skilled birth attendance. The antenatal care service was assessed using the WHO recommendation of positive pregnancy experience, and categorized as ANC1, ANC4 and ANC8 [45]. The postnatal care services use was also assessed as per the recommendation of the Ethiopian Ministry of Health [46]. The exposure variables were gender-related barriers, such as gender norms, values, beliefs, women’s autonomy to seek MNH services, men’s engagement, gender equitable attitude, and access to and control over financial resources. The gender-related barriers were grouped into women’s autonomy in decision-making [5, 6, 9–11, 14, 32, 33] women’s access to and control over resources [15, 16, 19–21, 24, 47, 48]; division of labor and men’s engagement [17, 19–21, 24]; accepting attitude towards old-fashioned societal gender norms [15, 16, 19–21, 24, 47, 48]; policy [47]; respectful health service during ANC [15, 20]; and financial challenges for transportation and delivery [15, 17]. In addition, the social capital study [21] was indirectly assessed through the social support to the women for maternal health service uptake. So, social support was measured using the Oslo-3 scale [49]. Accepting attitude towards equitable gender-norm was measured using a six-item tool rated using a Likert scale ranging from 1(“agree”), 2(“somewhat agree”), and 3(“disagree”). The mean values of the responses from the six-item were used as cut-off points for equitable gender-norms, ‘less than or equal to the mean value for ‘equitable gender’ and above the mean for ‘inequitable gender’ norms. Women’s experience of disrespectful or abuse by healthcare providers during maternal health service use was measured using qualitatively if a woman had experienced some disrespectful treatments.

Data analysis and management

Quantitative data were analyzed using Stata v.18 [50]. The different characteristics of the women that may determine the uptake of MNH outcomes were examined using Pearson’s chi-square statistics adjusted for cluster survey design effects. Descriptive statistics were carried out to compute the proportion of independent and dependent variables cross-tabulated with clusters. The community-level (cluster-level) variations were computed to assess the random-effect measure of variation. Inter-cluster correlation (ICC) and Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) were estimated to assess the relationship within the cluster and variations, and final model fit statistics. Multi-level mixed-effects logistic regression analysis [51] was computed to determine the relationship between gender dynamic variables with antenatal care use, home delivery and postnatal stratified by agrarian and pastoral contexts. The qualitative data were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated from the local dialect to English. The qualitative data were coded using ATLAS. ti software [52], and a thematic analysis approach was carried out with deductive and inductive coding approaches, as appropriate. The categories and the concepts that emerged from IDIs and FGDs were verified by consistently linking the emerging categories with the data received from the other groups of informants to improve the trustworthiness of the qualitative data analysis. These categories were linked to quotes from the research key informants to ensure the reliability of the study. The qualitative findings were presented through triangulation with the quantitative findings accordingly.

Approximately three-fourths (73.4%) of the women were Muslim. Of these, 99.9% of women in the pastoral context were Muslim. Maternal health service status

Antenatal care utilization

Among 3,097 women who had given birth during the previous 12 months, two-thirds (65.5%, 95%CI: 63.8–77.1%) of them had at least one antenatal care follow-up visit at nearby health facilities during their pregnancy period. Of these, antenatal care follow-ups were found to be low in pastoral contexts, which stood at 50.1% as compared with 88% of pregnant women in agrarian contexts who had at least one ANC follow-up during their most recent pregnancy. Of these, one in five women had four or more ANC follow-ups during their pregnancy period (Fig. 2).

Home delivery

More than two-thirds (68.4%, 95%CI: 66.8–70.0%) of women gave birth at their home and were attended by their relatives and traditional birth attendants. The majority of the women (87.2%) in the pastoral contexts gave birth at their home (Fig. 3).

Of these, more than two-thirds (69.5%, n = 1,346) of women gave birth at home assisted by traditional birth attendants and 29.7% (630) of them were surrounded by relatives or friends. 3% (n = 63) of women gave birth at their home without any assistance. Furthermore, women reported the reasons for giving birth at home, such as far distance or lack of transportation (44%), due to the facility not being open (26.8%), and service not customary (22.7%) (Table 3).

Postnatal care utilization

Of the 3097 women who gave birth during the previous 12 months, only one-third (32.0%, 95% CI: 30.4–33.6%) of them had used postnatal care services within 42 days. (Fig. 4). Generally, women had a huge discrepancy between the first ANC (88% in agrarian and 50% in pastoral) and skilled birth attendance (59.6% in agrarian and 12.8% in pastoral contexts of Ethiopia (Fig. 5).

Women’s autonomy and men engagement

The partner’s/husband’s engagement in accompanying the women during antenatal care services and labor and delivery time, women’s autonomy, decision on ANC follow-up, decision on place of delivery, family planning use, seeking healthcare for their children and themselves, women’s access to and control over resources, women’s attitude toward gender equitable norms were considered to assess the overall influence of gender dynamics on maternal health service uptake. Less than half (40.9%) of women were accompanied by their husbands during their antenatal care follow-up. The majority (82.4%) of the women were accompanied by their partners/husbands during delivery at health facilities. Furthermore, social support was crucial for women’s maternal health service uptake. However, approximately one in five women had poor social support during their pregnancy, labor, and delivery period. The gender dynamic variables were significantly different in the agrarian and pastoral contexts of Ethiopia (Table 4). Financial autonomy for pregnant women is crucial to access and control money and use it for transportation and any other related costs. Gender norms and beliefs

Slightly higher than one-third (35.4%) of women had gender-equitable attitudes. Nearly half of the women in the agrarian districts had a favorable gender equitable attitude while less than one-third of the women in the pastoral districts shared these attitudes (Table 3). The presence of deep-rooted gender norms poses a challenge to MNH service utilization. Husbands’ influence prevents women from accessing health facilities. Some families prohibit women from accessing health services, further hindering their ability to seek care.“…As nomadic people gender has affected some men. They do not allow wives to speak to a man they do not know privately without their permission alone.” IDI: HEW: pastoral district.

“…As nomadic people gender has affected some men. They do not allow wives to speak to a man they do not know privately without their permission alone.” IDI: HEW: pastoral district.

Cultural and belief factors

A complex interplay of social and cultural factors contributes to women opting for home delivery. Some specific factors disproportionately affect disadvantaged women, including unmarried women, lack of financial and social support, disability, and religion.“Women who have workload, lack supporters at home, who get pregnant without marriage, the poor or hand-to-mouth lifestyle – these groups, in the first place, do not give priority to it.” IDI: a health worker: agrarian district.

“Women who have workload, lack supporters at home, who get pregnant without marriage, the poor or hand-to-mouth lifestyle – these groups, in the first place, do not give priority to it.” IDI: a health worker: agrarian district.

Lack of access to maternal health service

Women who were involved in the study reported that lack of money for transportations, and worry for their safety were the barriers to accessing maternal health services. More than half (54.8%) of women who lived in the pastoral contexts had experienced money, transportation, safety, time, and permission challenges in accessing maternal health services. Only one in ten women in the pastoral contexts had no such barriers during pregnancy, labor, and delivery (Fig. 6). The overall uptake of MNH services is hindered by a multitude of barriers at various levels, encompassing individual, community, health system, and societal factors. Lack of transportation

More than two-thirds (69%, n = 2136) of women had challenges related to transportation. Transportation challenges are more common in pastoral communities (75.5%) due to the population living at dispersed distances, lack of transportation, and poor road conditions. Women residing in remote areas or inaccessible areas where transportation to health facilities is lacking face difficulties accessing health facilities, that contributes to home deliveries.

“The topography and the climate pose challenges, especially during the rainy season when rivers may flood, making it difficult for mothers to reach the health center.” IDI: a midwife: agrarian district.

The available local roads and bridges are damaged by heavy rain which affects women’s access to maternal health services. One of the in-depth interview respondents remarked that;“…in remote rural villages, there are rivers that make it difficult to provide maternity services over there, so they give birth at home. Moreover, there was one mother who even recently died of pregnancy-related complications, while being carried to a health facility. They were carrying her on a stretcher on their way to a health facility and she passed away before reaching there.” IDI: male community leader (p2): agrarian district.

Pastoral communities move from place to place to search for pasture land and water, especially in times of drought. Lack of transport is worse in pastoral contexts pregnant women are expected to travel around 40–59 km from home to health facilities. Lack of transport or roads and the places where some women live make it so they cannot be reach the health facility. The lack of ambulances makes the problem more serious and results in extra costs for private transport including “Bajaj” and motorcycles. The cost of transportation is also increasing due to fuel costs.

Financial constraints as well as opportunity costs related to seeking care are identified as major barriers associated with institutional delivery. Lack of financial support for women is a major barrier to MNH service uptake.

To be honest, we have reached the point where we are better off spending on Bajaj than on ambulances.” IDI: a community leader (p7) and FG01: women (p1): pastoral district.

Poor quality of service

Both the existing and perceived quality of services provided at facilities, lack of respectful and culturally sensitive maternal care, lack of trust in the competence of providers, and poor facility infrastructure and supplies impacted the uptake of maternal health services.

Lack of respectful maternal health service provision

Overall, one-in-ten (11.8%) women experienced disrespectful maternity care or abuse during labor and delivery (Fig. 7). Negative experiences, including disrespectful care, negligence, and lack of empathy, discourage women from seeking facility-based childbirth. Some women dropped out of services because of health professionals’ disrespectful behavior.

Discussion

The study determined the level of women’s maternal health services use and identified significantly associated gender-related barriers that hinder women’s utilization of maternal health services in the agrarian and pastoral contexts of Ethiopia. The study findings highlighted almost two-thirds of pregnant women attended at least one ANC follow-up, and a large majority of the women gave birth at their home without the presence of skilled personal and unclean environment, with a huge-disparities in the agrarian and pastoral contexts of Ethiopia. Gender dynamics including women who had low autonomy, less accompaniment, limited access to and control over resources manifested by financial constraints and lack of money for transportation, low social support, accepting attitude toward old-fashioned gender norms, women had fear for their safety due to far distance, and lack of context-tailored women-friendly, and culturally-sensitive care in the health facilities had a significant influence on women’s maternal health service utilization in Ethiopia.

In the present study, pregnant women’s attendance of at least one ANC follow-up status is consistent with the studies’ findings in the SSA countries [53, 54]. However, some SSA countries have much lower level of ANC service utilization [51, 52, 55]. It highlights home-birth has remained persistently high in Ethiopia which might be this study included women from both agrarian and pastoralist settings where women in the pastoral setting preferred and gave birth at home which make this study finding on home birth rate higher than other studies conducted Ethiopia [22, 23, 56, 56–59]. In the meantime, postnatal care utilization among women who gave birth was low, with notable variations observed within women’s uptake of the continuum of care, which is in line with a study conducted in SSA countries [60, 61].

In this study, women’s maternal health service use influenced through sociodemographic factors, including age, educational status, occupation, and marital status had significant associations with women’s maternal health service uptake. Women and husband who attended at least attended primary and above significantly improved maternal health service uptakes compared to women who did not attend formal education [53–55, 58, 59, 62–65, 69–72]. Uneducated women have negatively influenced and less likely use maternal health services in Ethiopia [73, 74]. The education might be linked with the behavioral change and its contribution to income level, in which wealth index, particularly women from middle categories were significantly associated with good uptake of maternal health services [54, 55, 58, 63, 65, 69–71]. In addition, women and husband engaged in paid job and have joint decisionmaking power has significantly improved use of maternal health services [71, 72, 75, 76]. This may address that transportation challenge due to lack of money for transportation [74]. Women being on the young age group (< 30 years old), older women [59, 64–66], women who have engaged in paid jobs [66], and women who have less than family members (< 5) [54, 55, 58, 63, 65] were more likely to use maternal health services at health facilities. Marital status has also influence on women’s use of maternal health services that shown single and/or divorced or widowed women were less likely to use maternal health services and mostly give birth at home. This finding is consistent with a study and other SSA countries [53, 55, 62].

In the present study, low social support, less accompaniment, and inequitable gender norms hindered women to receive maternal health services. Hence transforming old-fashioned norm and engaging male are crucial to curb the barriers and improve uptake of maternal health services [67, 68]. Additionally, fear for safety, and lack of respectful and culturally-sensitive maternal care services provision were some of the barriers. The lack of respectful and culturally- and women-friendly service in the primary health system hinders women’s use of service [67, 77]. In addition, women’s access to and utilization of maternal health services are affected by geographic inaccessibility factors. This finding is consistent with available evidence in SSA countries [53, 55, 57, 57, 58, 78], which has shown that distance with limited socioeconomic circumstances influenced women’s use of ANC and institutional delivery. In the present study, a lack of respectful and culturally-sensitive maternal health services is significant factor limits women’s prenatal and PNC us which contributed to home delivery. This is consistent with available evidence in SSA, disrespect during maternal health service provision [64, 79], lack of culturally-sensitive or religion-friendly maternal health service [61, 62, 64, 80] and mainly for Muslim women [64], which influenced ANC utilization, and seriously affect institution-based delivery.

This study findings identified that women’s autonomy in maternal health decisionmaking, and control over resources and education have a significant impact on women’s uptake of maternal health services. This aligns with existing evidence in Ethiopia [77] that highlights gender disparity in decisionmaking, access to resource and control over, and division of labor, has a serious negative impact on maternal service use. It is consistent with a study conducted in SSA that revealed that women’s empowerment in maternal health utilization in SSA [61, 62, 81, 82], women with more autonomy and decision-making have a positive influence on maternal health service utilization [58, 61, 64, 74, 76, 83, 84]. Women who had ANC follow-up were more likely to give birth at the health facility [66]. Similarly, financial hardship, lack of male engagement, and entrenched traditional inequitable gender norms and roles contributed to home delivery and negatively affected ANC and PNC service utilization [61, 62, 80]. Having an unfavorable attitude towards institutional delivery has contributed to high home delivery in Ethiopia [66, 78]. Context matters, women live in pastoralist context which predominantly Muslim, had low access to and use of maternal health services [69]. Pastoralist women suffered multifaceted barriers including physical distance, harsh climate, and feeling of insecurity during transportation [69, 73, 75, 76]. Muslim women were less likely to use maternal health services [69] that might link with lack of context-tailored and women-friendly services in the health facilities. On the other hand, women belong to protestant Christians more likely attend maternal health services [69].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study used a representative sample size and considered the diverse contexts of the agrarian and pastoral communities in Ethiopia. The data were collected using a web-based digital platform (SurveyCTO) with close supportive supervision by trained field and database staff to minimize missing data. A convergent mixed method data collection approach was applied for the survey to capture stakeholder insight related to gender dynamics on maternal health service uptake. The variation across clusters and different levels of barriers are handled through the multilevel mixed-effect logistic regression. Nevertheless, the temporality of the causation is affected due to the cross-sectional nature of data capturing. Women’s experience of disrespect care might be affected by both social desirability and recall biases.

Conclusions

Despite the investments in ensuring access to maternal health services, women’s maternal health service uptake was found to be extremely low in the study area, with a huge disparity between agrarian and pastoral contexts of Ethiopia. The majority of pregnant women give birth at home which is unsafe and life-threatening. Geographic inaccessibility, financial constraints, lack of culturally-sensitive care, women’s preference for traditional birth attendance, presence of inequitable gender norms, having a larger family size that might be linked with women’s workload and responsibility, and cultural and religious beliefs were the major barriers to maternal health service uptake. On the other hand, women who have the autonomy to make decisions in seeking healthcare, have husbands who accompany them during antenatal care, attend at least primary school, and have social support are enabling conditions for maternal health service uptake. Designing context-tailored culturally- sensitive and acceptable maternal health program considering the impact of gender dynamics, enhancing women autonomy, men accompanied, gender-norms transformation platforms, and alternative home-based delivery of lifesaving kits to ensure access to essential maternal health interventions are critical to saving the lives of women and newborns.
