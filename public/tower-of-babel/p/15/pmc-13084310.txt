Steering clear of incorrect laboratory reference ranges in Leydig cell testicular tumor–induced peripheral precocious puberty: A case report
Authors: Noor Alhuda Sawalha, Heba Hrizat, Fatima Thawabteh, Hind Mohtasib, Mohammed Aqel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leydig cell tumors are rare pediatric testicular neoplasms. We report a case of an 8-year-old boy who presented with early pubertal progression, including pubic hair and voice deepening, yet with biochemical results suggesting a prepubertal state. Normal testosterone levels and a flat Gonadotropin Releasing Hormone (GnRH) stimulation test response, bone age was advanced. Careful examination revealed asymmetric testicular size, and ultrasound identified right intratesticular lesion. Histopathology following unilateral orchiectomy confirmed a LCT. This case illustrates the diagnostic trap of relying solely on available laboratory reference ranges. It highlights the critical role of multidisciplinary approach when laboratory data appear inconsistent.

Introduction

Leydig cell tumors (LCTs) (ICD-O-8650/1) albeit rare, are the most common testicular sex cord stromal tumors and account for 1-2% of all testicular tumors.1, 2, 3 These tumors can present at any age; in the pediatric age group, LCTs most frequently develop between the ages of 5 and 10 years old. In such androgen-secreting tumors, boys usually present with symptoms of isosexual precocious pseudopuberty, including growth of the penis, development of pubic hair, and adult body odor, and/or painless testicular enlargement.2, 3, 4 Radical orchiectomy is the standard treatment, although testis-sparing surgeries are being increasingly utilized with successful outcomes.5,6

Here, we report a case of a young boy presenting with clinical features of precocious pseudopuberty despite a biochemically prepubertal profile. The eventual diagnosis of a Leydig cell tumor highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by misleading laboratory reference ranges in pediatric cohort and emphasizes the importance of a thorough clinical evaluation.

Case presentation

An 8-year and 9-month-old male child was referred to our pediatric endocrinology clinic due to concerns regarding early puberty. The child had presented with symptoms including voice deepening and the development of pubic hair growth, which had begun approximately 6 months prior to referral. There were no associated symptoms of axillary hair development, adult body odor or acne. No growth spurt was noted.

He had no symptoms of hypothyroidism, nor of adrenal insufficiency, no galactorrhea, no polydipsia or, polyuria or nocturia, no headache or dizziness or change of vision or morning nausea or vomiting. This child was born full-term with an appropriate birth weight for gestational age, and he had a free past medical and surgical history. Family history was negative for precious puberty or any inherited tumors.

Physical examination revealed stable vital signs and acceptable growth parameters for sex and age; a weight of 39 kg (95th percentile), height of 139 cm (87th percentile) and Body Mass Index (BMI) of 20.2 (93.6th percentile) using World Health Organization (WHO) growth charts. He was not dysmorphic, and no café au lait macules, gynecomastia or acanthosis nigricans were observed. His visual fields was intact, with no evidence of papilledema or other signs of increased intracranial pressure. Tanner staging indicated pubic hair development consistent with Tanner stage 3, and testicles were asymmetric, with right testes measuring 3 ml and the left testes 2 ml, using Prader orchidometer. Both testes were smooth with no palpable nodules. Otherwise, physical examination was unremarkable.

Initial blood workup was normal for pituitary screen, including electrolytes, IGF1, serum cortisol, prolactin, thyroid function test and blood sugar. Nevertheless, his gonadotropins were suppressed with normal testosterone, 17hydroxyprogesterone (17OHP) and androstenedione levels, along with low dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS). These interpretations were made based on the available reference ranges of our hospital's laboratory unit. When we used CALIPER (Canadian Laboratory Initiative on Pediatric Reference Intervals) reference ranges, interpretations were quite different, as it showed normal gonadotropins, elevated testosterone and elevated 17OHP and androstenedione with normal DHEAS (Table 1). His bone age was found to be advanced by three years. Accordingly, a Gonadotropin Releasing Hormone (GnRH) stimulation test was done and showed a flat response (Table 1). His LH peaked at 1.43 mIU/ml with a peak stimulated LH:FSH ratio of 0.19 (<0.66). A multidisciplinary team meeting was held, including our pediatric endocrinologist, urologist and oncologist, along with the patient and his family, and a mutual decision was made to complete a unilateral radical orchiectomy, based on a likely diagnosis of benign testicular Leydig cell tumor. Orchiectomy was done, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a Leydig cell tumor (Fig. 2A-C). The tumor measured 1.2 cm in diameter and showed no significant cellular atypia. There was no evidence of mitotic activity or necrosis, and the tumor was confined to the testis and exhibited a non-infiltrative growth pattern. Further examination revealed no involvement of the tunica albuginea, tunica vaginalis, epididymis, or spermatic cord. These findings have been consistent with a benign Leydig cell tumor. Discussion

Leydig cell tumors (LCTs) are rare testicular neoplasms arising from the interstitial Leydig cells, accounting for 1–2% of all testicular tumors in adults and for 1-4% in children, with an incidence of 0.5 to 2 per 100,000 children.2,7,8 LCTs are predominantly unilateral and benign, although bilateral involvement and malignant transformation have been reported.3,4,9,10

Precious puberty in males is defined as development of secondary sexual characteristics before the age of nine years old. This can result from peripheral etiology, such as testicular tumors. LCTs are typically hormonally active testicular tumors, secreting testosterone that induces peripheral precocious puberty, as observed in our patient. Consistent with previous reports, pubarche, voice deepening, and advanced bone age reflected androgen excess. Our patient demonstrated a benign clinical course, with no radiologic or histopathologic evidence of malignancy.3,4,7,9,11

Our patient's presentation was typical in terms of clinical features but notable for the diagnostic process, in which the application of an external reference range was crucial for accurately characterizing his biochemical pubertal profile. Initial interpretation of his hormonal data suggested normal testosterone levels with low gonadotropins, which could have led to a misdiagnosis of premature adrenarche. However, re-evaluation using the Canadian Laboratory Initiative on Pediatric Reference Intervals (CALIPER) database revealed elevated testosterone, 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17OHP), and androstenedione levels, consistent with peripheral precocious puberty.12 This revealed a critical gap in pediatric endocrinology in our region, where locally validated, age- and sex-specific laboratory reference intervals are often unavailable, and reliance on inappropriate reference ranges may result in diagnostic inaccuracies, delayed interventions, or unnecessary investigations, which remains an important consideration globally.13

Management of Leydig cell tumors generally involves radical orchidectomy, which remains the gold standard to ensure complete tumor excision and prevent recurrence. Testis-sparing surgery has been advocated by some authors in cases with small, well-circumscribed lesions and negative tumor markers.4, 5, 6,14 However, given the difficulty in reliably distinguishing benign from malignant lesions preoperatively, particularly in resource-limited settings such as ours, radical orchidectomy remains the preferred approach, as was undertaken in our patient.

Notably, our patient did not develop central precocious puberty (CPP) after his orchidectomy, unlike some other reported cases.15, 16, 17 This likely reflects that the tumor was diagnosed and removed early, limiting the duration of testosterone exposure and thereby reducing the risk of hypothalamic pituitary gonadal axis activation.

Conclusion

Our case contributes to the existing literature by emphasizing the importance of thorough clinical evaluation, access to reliable pediatric reference intervals, and vigilant postoperative monitoring. Long-term follow-up is essential to detect recurrence and potential progression to gonadotropin-dependent puberty, as recommended by prior studies. The findings also support the use of validated external reference databases, such as CALIPER, as an interim tool in resource-limited settings until local pediatric norms are established.

This case also highlights a rare but significant cause of male peripheral precocious puberty and provides insights into the diagnostic and therapeutic considerations for Leydig cell tumors in pediatric patients. It underscores the need for a multidisciplinary approach, involving pediatric endocrinologists, urologists, and oncologists, to ensure comprehensive care and optimize long-term outcomes.
