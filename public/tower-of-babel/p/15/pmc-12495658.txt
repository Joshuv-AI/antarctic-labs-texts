Causal relationship between gut microbiota and pneumonia: a Mendelian randomization and retrospective case–control study
Authors: Pengfei Huang, Yanqi Liu, Nana Li, Qianqian Zhang, Yinghao Luo, Yuxin Zhang, Yuxin Zhou, Wenjing Mu, Mengyao Yuan, Yuhan Liu, Yu Xin, Hongxu Li, Yahui Peng, Xibo Wang, Mingyan Zhao, Kaijiang Yu, Changsong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between microbiota and the gut-lung axis has been extensively studied in both experimental and epidemiological contexts. However, it is still unclear whether the gut microbiome plays a causal role in the development of pneumonia.MethodsOur study initially identified the genetic instruments in the gut microbiota GWAS across phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels. Pneumonia data were sourced from the open GWAS project of the Integrated Epidemiology Group (IEU). Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis employed several methods such as inverse variance weighting (IVW), weighted median, and MR-Egger, with Cochran's Q were calculated to assess heterogeneity via IVW and MR-Egger. Additionally, MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger intercepts were utilized to mitigate horizontal pleiotropy. A retrospective case–control study collected anal swab samples from severe pneumonia patients on the 1st and 3rd days after ICU admission. Samples were analyzed using 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (16S rRNA) and PERMANOVA analysis.ResultsEleven potential causal relationships between the gut microbiome and pneumonia (critical care), as well as nine potential causal relationships between the gut microbiome and pneumonia (28-day death in critical care) were identified. By integrating the results of PERMANOVA analysis with Mendelian randomization analysis, we were able to determine a negative correlation between genus Akkermansia and lactate levels, as well as length of ICU days in patients with septic acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Moreover, we found a potential negative causal relationship between the genus Akkermansia and pneumonia (28-day death in critical care) (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.22–0.79, P = 0.007).ConclusionsOur Mendelian randomization analysis has provided evidence for a potential causal relationship between gut microbiota and pneumonia. Furthermore, we observed that the genus Akkermansia may decrease the risk of pneumonia (28-day death in critical care), as observed in septic ARDS patients which Akkermansia could reduce ICU days and lactate levels. These findings provide valuable insights into the gut-lung axis and have the latent to inform future research in this field.Trial registrationThe study was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/index.html, ChiCTR2300075450).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03899-0.

Background

Pneumonia is a highly prevalent infectious disease globally and is associated with both short- and long-term mortality [1, 2], posing a significant disease burden and a threat to human health [3]. The link between the gut microbiome and lung diseases has get considerable attention, as evidenced by recent studies [4, 5], which have highlighted the prospective of the gut-lung axis [6]. However, the causal link between severe pneumonia and gut microbiota remained unclear and need to be elucidated.

The concept of the gut-lung axis was proposed in 1991 by Pugin, J. et al., which highlighting the role of gut microbiota in driving acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and sepsis [7]. SixteenS ribosomal ribonucleic acid (16S rRNA) amplicon sequencing approach is a sensitive tool used to analyze environmental microbiomes [8] and detect gut microbiota in patients with pneumonia [9]. Multiple studies have found that intestinal probiotics improved the incidence and outcomes of respiratory infections [10–14]. However, these studies have several limitations [15, 16], including small population sizes, confounding factors such as dietary habits, social culture, pre-existing autoimmune diseases, and chronic digestive disorders.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis is an epidemiological approach that enhances causal inference via utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variable (IV) [17, 18]. MR analysis has been utilized to establish causal relationships between gut microbiota and several risk factors [19–21]. However, the causal relationship between gut microbiota and pneumonia was not investigated [22]. In this study, we investigated the impact of gut microbiota on severe pneumonia based on public available GWAS data from a large population and verified the causal relationship between gut microbiota and severe pneumonia through a retrospective clinical study.

We used human genetic data to reveal the impact of gut microbiota on severe pneumonia. The Mendelian randomization analysis study employed severe pneumonia and its 28-day mortality as the primary endpoints. Anal swab samples were obtained from patients with clinically severe pneumonia and analyzed by 16S rRNA and PERMANOVA techniques. Our study preliminarily observed a negative correlation between the genus akkermansia and the severity of septic ARDS, especially in the lactate levels and intensive care unit length of stay. The existing investigations primary focused on gut microbiota dysregulation in ARDS pathogenesis [23, 24], we firstly recognized akkermansia as a potential protective microbiota in septic ARDS via randomization mendelian analysis and clinical observation. These findings offer novel perspective and valuable insights for future investigations into the gut-lung axis.

Methods

Overview of the study design

We performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis study to explore the causal associations between gut microbiota and two outcomes: pneumonia (critical care), pneumonia (28-day death in critical care). Gut microbiota data were derived from a large genome-wide association study (GWAS), while pneumonia (critical care) and pneumonia (28-day death in critical care) data were obtained from the UK Biobank (UKB). The study strictly adhered to the three core assumptions of Mendelian randomization [25]: (1) the genetic variants eventually incorporated for using are associated with gut microbiota taxa, (2) the instrumental variables are not associated with confounders (affecting GM taxa and pneumonia), and (3) the genetic variants influence pneumonia only through gut microbiota taxa. Eventually, we conducted a retrospective clinical study in patients with septic ARDS via 16S rRNA sequencing to further illustrate the clinical significance of the gut-lung axis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Design of the study. Data sources

Gut microbiota

The data for the gut microbiota study were obtained from the latest genome-wide association study (GWAS) by the Mibiogen consortium [26], which included 16S rRNA gene sequencing profiles and genotyping data. The microbial composition was analyzed by targeting three different variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene: V1-V2 (3716 samples, 5 cohorts), V3-V4 (4211 samples, 6 cohorts), and V4 (10,413 samples, 13 cohorts), and evaluated 211 taxa at five different levels (131 genera, 35 families, 20 orders, 16 phyla, and 9 classes). Ultimately, the study obtained 122,110 variant loci from 211 taxa.

Pneumonia

Data for the genome-wide association study of pneumonia (critical care) and pneumonia (critical care 28-day death) were obtained from the UKB [27]. UKB provided a total of 431,365 samples of patients with pneumonia (critical care) and 313,365 samples of patients with pneumonia (critical care 28-day death), with adjustments made for potential covariates such as sex, age, genotyping array and other factors [28]. The former included 2758 patients with pneumonia (critical care) and 428,697 control subjects, and the latter included 803 patients with pneumonia (28-day death in critical care) and 30,562 control subjects, with all patient and control populations being of European descent.

Clinical data

Forty-eight participants were enrolled in this trial at the Department of Critical Care Medicine, First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, Heilongjiang Province, China, between July 2021 and July 2022. Patients were selected based on the third international consensus definition of sepsis [29] and had to meet the diagnostic criteria for ARDS according to the Berlin definition [30] to be included in the study.

Analysis of intestinal microbial diversity

Fecal specimen collection

For patients who met the inclusion criteria, specimens were collected on the first and third day of diagnosis of septic ARDS using RNase- and DNase-free cotton swabs, which were then placed in a protective tube made of polystyrene. The patient was kept in the left lateral position, RNase- and DNase-free cotton swabs were inserted 2–3 cm into the rectal sphincter, rotated 360°, and left in place for approximately 20 s. The swabs were removed and checked for successful sampling. After a successful collection, the swabs were placed in a protective tube made of polystyrene. The collected samples were immediately snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in a −80 °C refrigerator.

16S rRNA sequencing

Bacterial composition was assessed through 16S rRNA sequencing, and microbiota analysis was conducted accordingly. Initially, DNA extraction and PCR amplification were performed using a manufacturer-recommended kit. The PCR reactions targeted the V3-V4 region of the bacterial small ribosome using specific primers. Subsequently, amplicons were extracted from 2% agarose gels and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction kit from Axygen Biosciences (Union City, CA, USA) following the provided instructions. The quantification of DNA was conducted using Qubit 2.0 from Invitrogen (USA). After library preparation, the sequencing tags were processed on the MiSeq/HiSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., CA, USA) [31] with 300/250 bp paired-end reads, and the overlapping 3'ends of the reads were concatenated to restore the original longer tags.

The sequencing data underwent further processing steps. The trimmed barcodes and primers were carefully inspected to ensure their remaining length and average quality. The 16S markers were constrained to a length range of 220 bp to 500 bp, with an average Phred score of at least 20 (Q20) and no more than three ambiguous bases (N). Redundant markers were removed by enumerating the copy number of each marker. For taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering, a similarity threshold of 97% was applied using the UPARSE software (http://drive5.com/UPARSE/), and chimeric sequences were identified and eliminated using USEARCH (version 7.0). The RDPClassifier tool (http://RDP.cme.msu.edu/), based on the RDP database (http://RDP.cme.msu.edu/), assigned taxonomic classifications to representative markers using a confidence threshold of 0.8. OTU profiling tables and analyses of α/β diversity were conducted using Python scripts from QIIME.

PERMANOVA (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance)

PERMANOVA is a statistical analysis method used to compare differences among multiple sets of microbial community samples. In this approach, we collect 16S rRNA sequencing data, create a data matrix, and calculate microbial community similarity between samples using distance metrics. Subsequently, we perform PERMANOVA analysis using statistical software, with the distance matrix and group factors as inputs. This analysis helps determine whether there are statistically significant differences in microbial community composition among different groups.

Correlation heat map analysis

Correlation heatmap analysis was performed using the OTU abundance table and environmental factor data. R software was utilized for clustering and analyzing the correlation between the cluster structure and various environmental factors. Subsequently, a heatmap was generated to visualize the results.

Screening of instrumental variables

To ensure data robustness and accuracy of our findings, we implemented a rigorous screening process of publicly available genome-wide association study data obtained from the Mibiogen consortium. We followed the steps as reported (Fig. 2): (1) Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to each gut microbiome taxa were selected as instrumental variables (IVs); Two thresholds were used to select the tool variables. However, in order to include more genetic instruments to explore potential associations and conduct sensitivity analysis, we selected the locus-wide signifcance level (1 × 10–5) to screen instrumental variables [26, 32, 33]. (2) We extracted SNPs which match with exposure from pneumonia GWAS. (3) We removed SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 0.01. (4) We harmonized alleles of SNPs and effect size on the gut microbiota and pneumonia. Then we remove imcompatible alleles and palindromic sequences [34]. (5) If SNPs that could be used for the outcome were not available in the GWAS data, we searched for eligible proxy SNPs in R through the LDlink online platform. (6) We conducted a linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis based on the European-based 1,000 Genome Projects [35], setting the clumping distance to 10,000 kb and excluding SNPs that did not meet the requirements (R2 < 0.001). Subsequently, we formed a new merge result without palindromic sequence. (7) We assessed the strength of the relationship between IVs and exposure using the F statistic. A weak instrumental variable was assumed if F < 10. The F value was calculated as F = [R2*(N-K-1)]/(1-R2),R2 = 2*eaf*(1-eaf), where R2 represents the proportion of variance explained by the genetic instrument, eaf represents the effect allele frequency, N represents sample size and K represents the number of instruments. (8) We examined and excluded SNP significantly associated with confounders through the PhenoScanner GWAS database (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/).Fig. 2Mendelian randomization analysis design and workflow

Mendelian randomization analysis design and workflow

Colocalization of microbiota and pneumonia

We conducted colocalization analysis to test whether the gut microbiota shared a causual variant with pneumonia (critical care) or pneumonia (28-day death in critical care). To test colocalization between exposure and outcome,the genetic variants within ± 250 kb around top snp were employed [36].

Statistical analysis

After selecting the instrumental variables as described above, we employed Inverse variance weighted (IVW), Weighted median, and MR Egger as the main analysis methods for MR. The odd ratio (OR) values for effects were used to measure the results of the three methods, and P values < 0.05 were considered significant. The IVW method is an effective analysis under the basic premise that all genetic variations are effective instrumental variables, with strong causality detection [37]; The Weighted median can also correctly estimate causality if up to 50% of instrumental variables are invalid [38]; MR-Egger regression allows all genetic variants to have pleiotropic effects, but requires that pleiotropic effects are independent of variance exposure associations [39]. If genetic variants are less than three, the wald ratio would be adopted for analysis.

To assess the robustness of the MR results, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses. First, we used MR Egger and IVW to assess the heterogeneity of the results and calculated Cochran's Q and I2 [40] to evaluate the magnitude of heterogeneity. Second, for pleiotropy, we used two methods, one was to test horizontal pleiotropy by the MR Egger intercept method and the other was to assess the horizontal pleiotropy of instrumental variables by MR-PRESSO global test [41]. P > 0.05 for both methods indicated no horizontal pleiotropy. We also evaluated heterogeneity by MR-PRESSO Outlier-corrected [42] to further screen out the SNPs that led to the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. Third, we determined whether a particular SNP caused a significant change in the results by eliminating SNPs one by one, and assessed the reliability of the results using the IVW method. In addition, we applied a more stringent Bonferroni correction for stratification, p < 0.05/n was used for correction, Strict calibration guarantees accuracy, but at the expense of statistical power, so when the P value was at 0.05/n − 0.05, we considered that there might be a suggestive association as the previous research [33].

Finally, we visualized and analyzed the MR-specific results by scatter plots and forest plots. The usage and interpretation of our MR study adhere to the STROBE-MR checklist (Table S1) [43]. All the above data analyses were performed in RStudio version 4.2.2. For the Mendelian randomization analysis, we harnessed a set of R packages including TwoSampleMR, MR Instruments, MRPRESSO, and coloc [44]. Additionally, in our 16S rRNA analysis, we made use of R packages like ggtree vegan and pegas to facilitate this specific aspect of the research [45].

Results

Instrumental variable screening

Initially, 14,587 genetic instruments for SNPS (p < 1 × 10 − 5) associated with 211 gut microbiota traits at the phylum,order,class,family and genus five levels were identified from large-scale gwas generated by the MiBioGen consortium. By searching for proxies,removing incompatible alleles(like A-C/C-G), removing palindromic sequences and Linkage disequilibrium, removing SNPS that may have Horizontal pleiotropy, we finally identified 2318 SNPS as instrumental variables of gut microbiota. All SNPs had F values greater than 10, indicating the absence of weak instrumental variables. All information about the process of filtering instrumental variables was provided (Table S2-S6).

MR analysis

We primarily utilized the IVW method to determine the existence of a causal relationship between exposure and outcome, while MR-Egger and WM methods were used to avoid bias, and scatter plots are presented (Figure S1-S2). The Mendelian randomization results for 211 taxa and pneumonia (critical care) or pneumonia (28-day death in critical care) were depicted (Figs. 3 and 4). Following stringent bonferroni correction for positive findings, we identified a negative causal effect between phylum Cyanobacteria and pneumonia (28-day death in critical care) (0.460 (0.272, 0.779), P = 0.004, PFDR < 0.005) (Figs. 5 and 6). Our analysis revealed that eleven taxa had a suggestive causal effect on pneumonia, including six taxa with potential positive causal effects: family Erysipelotrichaceae (1.56 (1.15, 2.11), P = 0.005), class Erysipelotrichia(1.56 (1.15, 2.11), P = 0.005), order Erysipelotrichales (1.56 (1.15, 2.11), P = 0.005), genus Gordonibacter (1.24 (1.03, 1.50), P = 0.022), family Peptostreptococcaceae (1.32 (1.04, 1.68), P = 0.025), and genus Marvinbryantia (1.44 (1.01, 2.05), P = 0.046) (Fig. 5). Furthermore, five taxa exhibited potential negative effects: genus Dorea (0.67 (0.48, 0.95), P = 0.023), family BacteroidalesS24.7 group (0.75 (0.58–0.97), P = 0.027), class Betaproteobacteria (0.72 (0.53, 0.98), P = 0.035), genus Fusicatenibacter (0.73 (0.54, 0.98), P = 0.037), and phylum Proteobacteria (0.73 (0.53, 1.00), P = 0.049) (Fig. 5). Additionally, we identified eight taxa with a potential causal effect on pneumonia (28-day death in critical care) (Fig. 6). Among these, genus RuminococcaceaeUCG003, exhibited a potential positive causal relationship (2.15 (1.16, 4.01), P = 0.016), while 7 taxa displayed negatively potential causal effects: class Verrucomicrobiae (0.42 (0.22, 0.79), P = 0.007), order Verrucomicrobiales (0.42 (0.22, 0.79), P = 0.007), family Verrucomicrobiaceae (0.42 (0.22, 0.79), P = 0.007), genus Akkermansia (0.42 (0.22, 0.79), P = 0.007), genus Parasutterella (0.50 (0.29, 0.86), P = 0.012), genus Ruminococcusgauvreauiigroup (0.48 (0.26, 0.88), P = 0.018), and genus LachnospiraceaeFCS020group (0.55 (0.31, 0.97), P = 0.037) (Fig. 6). Details of all the Mendelian randomization results were available (Table S7). MR-Egger intercept analysis and MR-PRESSO global test did not reveal potential horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05) (Table S9). MR-PRESSO Outlier-corrected test did not find the presence of outlier points. Discussion

This study performed a Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using gut microbiota data from European populations and pneumonia data (including pneumonia (critical care) and pneumonia (28-day death in critical care)) from the UK Biobank (UKB). We explored potential causal links between microbial taxa and both pneumonia (critical care) and pneumonia (28-day death in critical care). Furtherly, the MR findings were validated in the clinical observation which was analyzed via 16S rRNA sequencing method and PERMANOVA analysis method from patients with septic ARDS. In our MR analysis study, we found a potential association between gut microbiota and pneumonia, of which 11 gut microbiota taxa had a potential association with pneumonia (critical care), and 9 gut microbiota taxa had a potential association with pneumonia (28-day death in critical care), and through Cochran Q and I2 test, we considered the results across the microbiota cohort to be robust. Notably, the results demonstrated a negative association between the genus Akkermansia and lactate values as well as the length of ICU days in patients with septic ARDS, consistent with the findings of the MR analysis showing a negative association between the genus Akkermansia and 28-day mortality in severe pneumonia. These results highlight the existence of the gut-lung axis.

Several retrospective analyses and clinical trial studies have associated gut microbiota with respiratory disease [46–48], however, the causal relationship between gut flora and severe pneumonia has not been established. It has been suggested that the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) and the respiratory tract, although separate organs are part of a shared mucosal immune system called the gut-lung axis, and that the microbiota is involved in the gut-lung axis, influencing local and distant immune responses [6]. Gut microbes and their metabolites may achieve regulation of systemic immune responses by affecting circulating lymphocytes [49]. Local dysbiosis of bacterial ecology disrupts microbial homeostasis in vivo and is associated with inflammatory and remodeling features of macrophages in the lung [50]. Previous studies have illustrated the association between gut microbes and lung disease, but have not been able to exclude potential confounding effects such as population and diet, challenging the nature of the"gut-lung axis". Herein, we used MR analysis, as SNPs were randomly assigned at the time of conception, thus effectively avoiding confounding bias. This was followed by 16srRNA analysis and PERMANOVA analysis based on anal swab specimens from clinical patients to verify the causal relationship between gut microbes and severe pneumonia.

Many studies have suggested a possible link between our selected gut microbiota and severe pneumonia. Dickson, Robert P et al. found that the progression of disease in ARDS patients was associated with the drive of gut-associated bacteria (e.g., Lachnospiraceae and Enterobacteriaceae) [51]. Experimental and clinical observational studies have reported that gut microbes are associated with the pathogenesis of severe pneumonia (e.g., ARDS) [46, 52, 53]. Recent studies have also found that antimicrobial therapy and critical care disrupt the microbiota, impairing the function of the flora colonizing the respiratory tract and leading to pulmonary infections, including pneumonia [54]. In a study by Wang et al., increased numbers of Enterobacteriaceae and decreased numbers of Lactobacillus/Lactococcus in the intestinal microbiota of mice with respiratory infections led to intestinal immune damage and inflammation [55]. At the level of causality, our findings revealed a positive causal relationship between six different taxonomic sets of bacteria and severe pneumonia, as well as a negative causal relationship between six different taxonomic sets of bacteria and severe pneumonia.

The core finding of this study suggests a potential negative causal association between Akkermansia and severe pneumonia with 28-day mortality. Based on the currently findings, its abundance alteration may be correlated with lactate levels and ICU length of stay in patients with septic ARDS. Akkermansia muciniphila, a commensal bacterium of the mucus layer, was first isolated in 2004 [56]. In clinical patients, the total organic acids, acetate, and butyrate, from gut microbiota are significantly lower compared to healthy individuals, especially in critically ill patients, where butyrate is almost depleted [57]. Interestingly, A. muciniphila induces the production of acetate and propionate [56, 57]. A study showed that Akkermansia promotes mucosal wound repair through FPR1-dependent and oxygen reduction-mediated epithelial cell proliferation [58], which may provide a mechanism for this bacterium to alleviate intestinal dysfunction. Akkermansia has also been found to regulate peripheral mononuclear cells (PBMCs) [59, 60], and its supernatant stimulates high levels of IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNF-α. Furthermore, a study found that live A. muciniphila administration achieved the reversal of metabolic disorders, including increased adiposity, metabolic endotoxemia, adipose tissue inflammation, and insulin resistance, by stimulating the release of intestinal peptides [59]. Therefore, A. muciniphila is considered a probiotic with a bright future due to its protective function of the intestinal barrier and its ability to limit inflammation [61]. The clinical regression of septic ARDS patients may be associated with Akkermansia exerting its influence through the aforementioned mechanisms, suggesting that Akkermansia could be a potential target for early therapeutic intervention in related diseases.

In recent years, researchers have focused on oral dietary products or probiotics aimed at early intervention in disease [62, 63]. The microbiome represents a key research focus for the treatment of sepsis-related organ injury and serves as a breakthrough point for related basic research [64–66]. Studies in mice have shown that dietary fiber and some of its fermentation products, namely short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), can prevent the development of allergic airway inflammation [67]. Intranasal administration of probiotics reduced symptoms of viral infections [68, 69]. However, these studies have only been validated in mice, and their specific effects in humans need further evaluation.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the genetic tools mainly from European GWAS may lead to population stratification bias, limiting the generalization of causal relationships to non-European populations. Different races have distinct effect allele frequency (eaf) and linkage disequilibrium patterns [70], which decreased the robustness of causality results. Secondly, consistent with other gut microbiota researches about mendelian randomization analysis, the distribution of p-value for screening instrumental variables was set at 1e-05 [26, 32, 33], but a series of sensitivity analyses such as heterogeneity, pleiotropy, colocalization and phenoscanner have been adopted to reduce residual pleiotropy. Thirdly, the small sample size and single-center study may limit external validation [71]. However, we first observed the genus akkermansia was negatively associated with the severity of patients with septic ARDS. Finally, although our MR analysis suggests potential causal relationships, the mechanisms of the gut microbiota impact on severe pneumonia require further experimental validation in mice. This study provided a new platform for researchers to explore the relationship between gut microbes and severe pneumonia. Future studies could explore specific mechanisms between gut flora and lung disease to enable early identification or early intervention in patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our MR analysis revealed a negative causal direction between genus.Akkermansia and pneumonia (28-day death in critical care). Additionally, we found a negative correlation between Akkermansia and lactate values and length of ICU days in septic ARDS patients through clinical analysis. Our findings provide novel insights into the gut-lung axis. It is important to mention that this study is exploratory, and further studies are necessary to fully understand the association between gut microbiota and pneumonia.
