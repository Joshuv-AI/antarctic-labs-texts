Seroprevalence of Syphilis Among Pregnant Women on Antenatal Care Service at Wereda 04 Health Center in Kolfe Keraniyo Subcity, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Almaz Dejen, Mihiretu Kumie Takele, Dagim Jirata Birri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimSyphilis, including congenital syphilis, is a rising global public health problem. Congenital syphilis is of particular public health importance due to its adverse birth outcomes. Limited studies have been conducted among pregnant women in health centers that provide antenatal care for large number of women. This study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of syphilis among pregnant women who attended antenatal care service at Wereda 04 Health Center, Kolfe Keraniyo subcity, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted among pregnant women on antenatal care at Wereda 04 Health Center during February to May 2024. Primary data were collected from 485 pregnant women using a pretested, structured questionnaire that was selected by systematic sampling. Syphilis was detected using serological test. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.ResultsMost (64.6%, 303/469) were multigravida and 77.3% (266/344) had one or two children. Slightly more than half (51.7%, 178/344) were multiparous. Nearly 35.9% (28/78) of the women had sexual intercourse with two or more persons in their lifetime. About 7.8% (36/462) had practiced extramarital sex. The seroprevalence of syphilis was found to be 0.2% (1/483).ConclusionThe prevalence of syphilis was low, suggesting that strong public health measures were implemented by the health center. We recommend the sustainability of such measures for further reduction of syphilis and their adoption by other health institutions in the study area.

Introduction

Syphilis is a sexually and vertically transmitted bacterial infectious disease caused by the gram‐negative spirochete Treponema pallidum subspecies pallidum. Its transmission occurs through contact with infectious lesions during vaginal, anal, or oral sexual intercourse. It can also be transmitted from mother to fetus via the placenta during pregnancy and through microabrasions in the skin [1]. The risk of sexual transmission reaches its highest level during primary and secondary stages [1, 2]. If syphilis is not treated, it can lead to a number of complications, including permanent vision loss (ocular syphilis) and hearing loss (otosyphilis), cardiovascular problems, and neurological problems (neurosyphilis) [3]. Syphilis can also cause stillbirth, preterm delivery, low birth weight, and congenital syphilis in newborns, many of which are life‐threatening or result in long‐term disability [4]. In pregnant women with syphilis, up to 40% of fetuses are stillborn or die from their infection during infancy [5].

Syphilis burden has increased globally in the past decade, especially in North America, Europe, Australia, and Asia [6, 7, 8, 9]. World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that 8 million adults aged from 15 to 49 developed syphilis and the number of congenital syphilis case rate per 100,000 live births was 523 in 2022 globally [10]. The prevalence of syphilis, including congenital syphilis, is high in low‐ and middle‐income countries like Ethiopia [11]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis showed that the global pooled prevalence of syphilis in pregnant women was 0.8% globally, 3.3% in low‐income countries, and 1.5% in lower‐middle‐income countries [12], indicating that the burden of syphilis is more pronounced in pregnant women of low‐income countries.

Regarding the situation in sub‐Saharan Africa, a very recent (2024) systematic review and meta‐analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of syphilis among people living with HIV was 7.3% in sub‐Saharan Africa, 10.5% in Eastern Africa (higher than that of other African regions) and 71.% in the antenatal care setting [13]. Syphilis prevalence among sub‐Saharan Africa pregnant women varies from 3% to over 9% [14]. A systematic study published in 2019 showed that syphilis prevalence is 2.9% among sub‐Saharan African pregnant women [15]. Congenital syphilis is a major cause of adverse birth outcomes, such as stillbirth and neonatal death, in sub‐Saharan Africa [14].

In Ethiopia, syphilis prevalence among pregnant women is 2.3% although it varies from one region to another [16]. It is 1.8% in pregnant women in Addis Ababa [17]. Conducting prevalence study at health institutions may trigger such institutions to implement targeted interventions to prevent and control the transmission of syphilis. The aim of this study was thus to determine the seroprevalence of syphilis among pregnant women who attended antenatal care service at Wereda 04 Health Center, Kolfe Keraniyo subcity, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods and Materials

Study Area

The study was conducted among pregnant women attending routine antenatal care at Wereda 04 Health Center in Kolfe Keraniyo subcity, which is located in southwestern part of Addis Ababa. The health center provides a wide range of primary health care services aimed at prevention, treatment, and health promotion. These include diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic health conditions, maternal and child (MCH) services, health education and promotion, and disease control and prevention. The annual average number of all patients and pregnant women that receive service at the health center is about 210,000 and 9000, respectively. This health center was selected since it ranks first, due to its location, among health centers in the subcity in the number of pregnant women it provides antenatal care service to.

Study Design and Period

Institutional based cross‐sectional study was conducted at Wereda 04 Health Center among pregnant women seeking antenatal care from February to May 2024.

Source Population

The source population included all pregnant women who visited Wereda 04 Health Center for antenatal care service during the study period.

Study Population

The study population entailed all pregnant women attending antenatal care in the health center during the study period who met the inclusion criteria.

Eligibility Criteria

All pregnant women attending antenatal care in the health center were included. Pregnant women who were sick and who were under antibiotic treatment during the 7 days before the enrollment were excluded.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Technique

Sample size was calculated to be 499 using single population proportion formula, with an expected outcome of 50% and 30% nonresponse rate. n=z2p(1−p)/w2 where, p = proportion (50%); n = sample size; z = confidence interval (with 95% level of certainty); w = margin of error (5%).

Systematic sampling was used to select 499 study participants from all pregnant women who visited the health center to get antenatal care service during the study period. Briefly, survey data were collected over a period of 4 months. Based on antenatal care clinic records, we estimated 3000 pregnant women to visit the clinic during this period. We selected every sixth woman for the study since the sampling interval (K) was calculated to be about 6 (3000/499). The first participant was selected from the first six women at random by drawing lots.

Study Variables

The dependent variable was the prevalence of syphilis. The independent variables were sociodemographic characteristics (age, level of education, marital status, economic status), obstetric characteristics (gravidity, parity), sexual behaviors (age at first sex, use of a condom at first sex, number of lifetime sexual partners, extramarital sex), and clinical factors.

Data Collection Method

A structured questionnaire that was pretested was used to collect data from the study participants. Moreover, blood sample was collected from each participant for the detection of antibodies against syphilis. Syphilis Rapid Test Cassette (ACON Laboratories, USA) was used to detect IgM and IgG against T. pallidum in the blood sample of pregnant women, following manufacturer's protocol.

Data Quality Assurance

The questionnaire was pretested on 20 pregnant women before it was administered to the actual participants. Training was given to data collectors, and data collection was carried out under the close supervision of academic advisor. Moreover, data were checked for errors after entry into Excel.

Data Analysis

Data were entered into Excel before export to SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) 20 version statistical software for analysis. Descriptive statistics were applied to determine the distribution of the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, such as sexually transmitted infections (STIs).

Ethical Considerations

The study was ethically approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Review Committee of Ayer Tena Health Science and Business College. The aim and other details of the study were fully explained to all study participants before obtaining their written informed consent for their participation in the study. Confidentiality was maintained at all levels of the study by not recording the names of the study participants. Participants' involvement in the study was entirely voluntary. Participants who were unwilling to participate in the study and those who wished to quit their participation at any stage were allowed to do so with freedom and without fear of bearing harmful consequences.

Discussion

The present study attempted to assess the seroprevalence of syphilis among pregnant women who sought antenatal care service at a health center in Addis Ababa, and the seroprevalence of syphilis was found to be very low (0.2%), suggesting that there exists strong antenatal screening program and public health control measures. This figure is much less than the ones reported by similar, recent studies conducted in various parts of Ethiopia, including Addis Ababa [17], Gondar [18], Bahirdar [19, 20], Yirgalem [21], Gambella [22], Buno Bedele [23], and Jinka [24]. The current low syphilis prevalence is also lower than the ones reported from Kampala, Uganda [25], Tanzania [26], Eastern Cape, South Africa [27], Meghalaya, India [28], and Guangxi Zhuang, China (0.85%) [29]. These differences in the prevalence of syphilis among the pregnant women of different parts within Ethiopia and of different countries could be attributed to differences in various factors such as sexual behavior, awareness about STIs level of reproductive health education, cultural practices, sensitivity of diagnostic tests, and others.

The present study showed that about 35.9% of the pregnant women had a history of multiple sexual partners. This is much higher than that of a similar study conducted in Sede Muja district of South Gondar, Ethiopia, where 6.2% of the pregnant women had multiple sexual partners in 1 year [30]. This difference could be attributed to the length of time during which multiple sexual activity occurred; in our study, it was lifetime (not 1 year) multiple sexual partners. But it is lower than that of pregnant women in Jinka town (43.8%), Ethiopia [24]. This difference may be explained by difference in alcohol usage, which is higher among Jinka town women, Southern Ethiopia (56.2%) [24] compared to our participants (20%). Studies have shown that alcohol consumption leads to having multiple sexual partners [31, 32].

Our finding that 7.8% the study participants committed extramarital sexual intercourse is consistent with Ugandan women, 8.2% of whom practiced extramarital sex [33]. But the rate of extramarital sex in our study is lower than that of Chinese women (32.1%). This disparity could be expanded by cultural and economic differences. In Ethiopia, religion, family, and society strongly condemn extramarital sex while this may be weaker in China. In addition, Ethiopian women are economically more dependent on husbands than Chinese women, who may feel confident to practice extramarital sex.

This study has the following limitations and findings must be interpreted with caution. It was conducted at a single health institution, which limits generalizability to other health institutions. Additional studies covering multiple health institutions in Addis Ababa may result in generalizable findings. Further, since syphilis was found to be rare, it was not possible to determine factors associated with its occurrence.

Conclusion

The prevalence of syphilis among pregnant women on antenatal care at Wereda 04 Health center was very low compared to other studies conducted in Addis Ababa and the rest of Ethiopia. We recommend other health institutions in Ethiopia and developing countries to implement public health measures to reduce the prevalence of syphilis among pregnant women.
