Cocaine-Induced Dermatomucositis: Clarifying Diagnostic Controversies and Introducing IgG4 Positivity as a New Histopathological Feature
Authors: Francisco Reyes Albaladejo, Miguel Mansilla-Polo, Rafael Botella-Estrada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cocaine-induced dermatomucositis is a recently described clinical entity with significant controversy regarding its diagnostic criteria and pathophysiology. While previous reports have identified a distinct pattern of chronic inflammatory mucocutaneous lesions in people who use cocaine, the absence of standardized diagnostic markers has led to inconsistencies in classification. This case series aims to clarify and unify the defining characteristics of cocaine-induced dermatomucositis by analyzing its clinical presentations, histopathologic findings, and immunologic features. Notably, we introduce immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) positivity as a newly observed characteristic, challenging previous assumptions that excluded IgG4-related immune responses from its pathogenesis. By consolidating clinical and histologic patterns, this study contributes to the ongoing effort to establish cocaine-induced dermatomucositis as a distinct and recognizable disorder, paving the way for improved diagnosis and management.

Key Summary Points

Cocaine-induced dermatomucositis (CID) is a condition that is becoming increasingly recognized in clinical practice, characterized by inflammatory skin and mucosal lesions in people who use cocaine, which often present in the supralabial and nasal regions and is associated with plasma cell-rich infiltrates.While CID shares features with other cocaine/levamisole-related conditions, such as cocaine-induced mucocutaneous lupus (CIMDL) and levamisole-induced vasculitis (LIV), it can be distinguished by its predominant mucocutaneous involvement and absence of destructive midline lesions.Recent reports have revealed a consistent histopathological pattern involving polyclonal plasma cell infiltrates, which are occasionally IgG4-positive, suggesting an overlap with, or expansion of, the spectrum of IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD).CID is thought to involve cocaine- and levamisole-mediated vascular injury, immune dysregulation via neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and cytokine-mediated inflammation, which contribute to tissue damage and possible autoimmunity.Diagnosis is currently based on clinical presentation, lesion morphology, a history of cocaine use, and histological features, as no formal diagnostic criteria have yet been established.The mainstay of treatment is cessation of cocaine use combined with corticosteroid therapy; however, further studies are needed to refine diagnostic tools and clarify the role of IgG4 in this condition.

Introduction

Cocaine is a tropane alkaloid and one of 14 alkaloids derived from the leaves of two coca plant species: Erythroxylum coca and Erythroxylum novogranatense [1]. The Incas chewed coca leaves mixed with ashes or lime to reduce hunger, thirst, and fatigue, as well as to cope with cold temperatures and high altitudes. These leaves contain low concentrations of cocaine and are not addictive. Over time, more potent and highly addictive derivatives have been developed at different stages of processing, including cocaine paste (PBC), cocaine hydrochloride, and freebase cocaine, also known as “crack”. Cocaine hydrochloride is usually snorted or injected when mixed with water, while PBC and freebase cocaine are typically smoked [2].

Its effects induce euphoria and heightened activity by blocking the reuptake of catecholamines. Street cocaine, commonly used by addicts, is often adulterated with various substances (“cutting agents”), which may include amphetamines, antihistamines, benzocaine, inositol, lactose, lidocaine, mannitol, opioids, phencyclidine, procaine, sugars, tetracaine, and occasionally arsenic, caffeine, quinidine, or even flour and talc [1]. Cocaine is the second most used illicit drug in Europe as of 2024 with approximately 2.5 million 15- to 34-year-olds using it last year [3].

The use of the terms dermatitis and mucositis is extremely common to refer to conditions in relation to inflammation of the skin and mucosa, respectively. However, the term dermatomucositis as an entity is rarely used in scientific literature and it has been applied for several reactions related to inflammation of skin and mucosa due to different causes such as corticosteroids [4], Lortat–Jacob bullous muco-synechial and atrophic dermatitis [5], fatal dermatomucositis due to an adenoidal-pharyngeal-conjunctival virus [6], and recently by Fernández-Flores et al. who defended the term in the full sense in relation to the use of cocaine [7].

Dermatological findings related to cocaine use have been extensively reported in the literature as different entities. They appear in approximately 0.5–3% of people who use cocaine and they might be present as early as 24 h after use. Lesions occur more frequently in women with an average age of 42.7 years [8].

One of the skin manifestations that has been associated with cocaine use is the drug-induced variant of erythema multiforme major known as Stevens–Johnson syndrome, an entity that tends to lack targetoid-like skin lesions but more mucosal involvement than erythema multiforme [9]. The effects of cocaine on the skin extend beyond Stevens–Johnson syndrome; cocaine has been assumed to be the culprit of other manifestations such as drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic response (DRESS) syndrome [10], eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis [11], pseudovasculitis [12], Raynaud’s phenomenon and ischemic finger necrosis [13], urticarial vasculitis [14], thromboangiitis obliterans [8], acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis [15], IgA vasculitis [16], fixed drug eruption, retiform purpura, bullous diseases (pemphigus vegetans and pemphigus vulgaris), anetoderma [17], perniosis, pulp artophy [18], and systemic scleroderma [2].

Snorting cocaine can lead to various nasal complications specifically, including intranasal viral warts (“snorters’ warts”), crusting inside and around the nose, nosebleeds, and asymmetry of the nostrils. On the other hand, high-temperature smoke when smoking cocaine contains toxic and irritating substances that can burn the lips and facial skin, lead to eyelash loss, and accelerate skin aging [2].

Sometimes a variety of cocaine-induced effects on nasal structures lead to the formation a particular entity known as cocaine-induced midline destructive lesions (CIMDL), a condition that resembles systemic diseases associated with positive anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA). It is estimated to affect approximately 4.8% of cocaine users. Symptoms include hyposmia, facial pain, crusting, ulcers, perforation of the nasal septum and palate, sinus wall destruction, orbital damage, and erosion of the anterior skull base. A key characteristic of CIMDL is the presence of ANCAs targeting human neutrophil elastase (HNE) and other neutrophil antigens, such as lactoferrin and cathepsin G [2, 19].

Levamisole, a synthetic anthelmintic once used for its immune-modulating effects in cancer and autoimmune treatments, was discontinued in human medicine because of serious side effects, including vasculitis (levamisole-induced vasculitis, LIV), agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and arthritis. Notably, levamisole is a common adulterant in cocaine, particularly in Europe, where it is used to increase product volume and dealer profits. It is believed that levamisole enhances cocaine’s effects by inhibiting monoamine oxidase and catechol-O-methyl transferase, prolonging catecholamine activity in synapses [20].

LIV has been associated with the appearance of purpuric lesions in different parts of the body. Additionally, LIV has been reported in association with ocular cicatricial pemphigoid and its positivity to ANCA, making the differential diagnosis with granulomatosis with polyangiitis challenging [20, 21]. It must be noted, however, that Kunzler et al. described 10 cases in previous literature with ANCA-negative LIV [22].

In our practice, we received a case of a woman with cocaine-induced dermatomucositis and an anomalous high IgG4 expression, a manifestation not previously reported in the literature to be associated with cocaine-induced dermatomucositis. In fact, it had even been used to rule out this disease. We decided to review the scarce literature on cocaine- and/or levamisole-induced lesions as well as IgG4-related disease in the hope of better understanding the pathophysiology of these diseases, provide a comprehensive differential diagnosis for these entities, and ultimately to incorporate this new finding in our case to cocaine-induced dermatomucositis differential diagnosis and expected histological manifestations.

Macroscopic and Microscopic Descriptions of Cocaine-Induced Dermatomucositis in Literature

This publication aimed to clarify the diagnostic approach to cocaine-induced dermatomucositis, which started to be described in 2023 with several case studies from Udondo González del Tánago et al. [23], Fernandez-Flores et al. [7], and Viedma-Martínez et al. [24], and to expand the spectrum of manifestations that this disease may entail, since there are currently no defined diagnostic criteria or terminology for this manifestation.

In most of these cases, the initial description of the lesion is a painless thickening and/or ulcerated lesion expanding between the upper lip and the nostrils. Papillomatous, cobblestone, granulomatous, and nodular changes have been used to describe the appearance of these lesions, which ultimately describe a similar manifestation despite descriptive differences as we have seen in the images of the reported cases as well as some cases seen in our office. For instance, in the case of a woman who admitted to cocaine use and presented with the lesion shown in Fig. 1, we could see an exudative erythematous plaque in the supralabial area and nostrils. These lesions may evolve slowly over weeks to months and are often misdiagnosed or treated ineffectively until cocaine use is disclosed. In terms of clinical progression, the lesions may enlarge, ulcerate, and become secondarily infected. Our patient was initially treated with a high-potency topical corticosteroid (clobetasol) and a short course of orally administered prednisolone (30 mg per day, tapered over 4 weeks). Clinical improvement was observed within 2 weeks, with the lesion having completely resolved after 6 weeks. During follow-up, the patient reported cessation of cocaine use, which was confirmed by serial urine toxicology screens. No recurrence was observed during the 6-month follow-up period and no systemic immunosuppressants were required.

Cocaine/Levamisole-Induced Lesions Pathophysiology

Cocaine stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, increasing heart rate and causing blood vessel constriction. It also affects the vascular system by promoting the release of endothelin-1, a powerful vasoconstrictor, while inhibiting nitric oxide production, the body’s main vasodilator. Additionally, it directly damages the endothelium, encouraging platelet aggregation and leading to a pro-thrombotic state. These effects reduce blood flow, causing both systemic and localized ischemia [2].

Toxicological studies conducted both in vivo and in vitro have shown that cocaine exerts toxic effects by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), which cause oxidative damage to cells. Additionally, cocaine (itself, not its metabolites) triggers cell death through apoptosis and, in certain cell models, can also lead to necrosis. It further disrupts mitochondrial function by depolarizing the mitochondrial membrane. DNA damage and the other effects mentioned above may contribute to the observed cell death [1]. Note that despite direct cytotoxicity, vascular injury and vasoconstriction play a larger role in tissue ischemia and ultimately skin manifestations [27].

Locally, cocaine use can lead to necrosis of the nasal mucosa and even damage deeper bone and cartilage structures, causing CIMDL. While reduced blood flow is a key factor in CIMDL, other possible mechanisms include high-speed cocaine crystal trauma to the nasal mucosa, reduced ciliary beat frequency of the nasal epithelial cells, altered nasal epithelial cell gene expression, cocaine-induced vasculitis, or inflammation triggered by Staphylococcus aureus superantigens. However, none of these have been conclusively proven [2, 28].

The discussion around cocaine-induced dermatomucositis extends beyond the mechanisms of cocaine itself, as it is often used alongside levamisole. Lood and Hughes suggest that cocaine and its common adulterant levamisole may disrupt immune tolerance by inducing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) enriched with neutrophil elastase (NE), a known target of ANCAs in people who use cocaine. These NETs may be highly immunogenic because of their inflammatory mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content. The release of NETs, NE, and B cell activating factor (BAFF) could contribute to the persistence of ANCAs and the survival of autoreactive B cells, potentially leading to long-term immune dysregulation [29]. The cocaine–levamisole-induced neutrophil chemotaxis is the hypothesized pathophysiology behind some neutrophilic dermatoses described in the literature like pyoderma gangrenosum [30–32] and Sweet syndrome [33]; ultimately it might be one of the reasons why five of the cases reported in the literature specifically describe neutrophil infiltration.

While chronic drug exposure may gradually erode immune tolerance, additional factors, such as acute drug use, seem necessary to trigger clinical disease. Cocaine/levamisole-associated autoimmunity-IgG, an antibody linked to cocaine use, may amplify NET formation, potentially contributing to vasculitic or thrombotic complications. Given the possible widespread impact and underestimation of cocaine–levamisole-associated autoimmune syndrome (CLAAS)/LIV prevalence among people who use cocaine, further research is needed to better understand its role in drug-induced autoimmunity and related immune disorders [29].

Additional techniques should be explored to further solidify the efficacy and accuracy of diagnosis in this new entity. The use of the argyrophilic nucleolar organizer region (AgNOR) technique has already been described in comparing the oral mucosa of cocaine and crack users with healthy volunteers, rendering a significant difference between these groups, suggesting an altered response to damage and protein synthesis in users. The technique consists of using a silver dye which stains the argyrophilic regions of some nucleolar proteins, the differential expression of these proteins is relevant in the diagnosis and prognosis of some diseases, predominantly neoplastic ones [34].

Differential and Definitive Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of cocaine-induced dermatomucositis is similar to other entities produced by cocaine–levamisole in the same area, such as CIMDL and LIV. We should consider other midline-destructive conditions, such as vasculitis, sarcoidosis, mucocutaneous leishmaniasis, tuberculosis, tertiary syphilis, lepromatous leprosy, rhinoscleroma, invasive fungal infections (mucormycosis, paracoccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis), and NK/T cell lymphoma [2]. Most of these differential diagnoses were promptly excluded by all three published case reports/series on cocaine-induced dermatomucositis.

Another condition to consider is plasmacytoid cheilitis, which involves the infiltration of the lip mucosa by a diffuse polyclonal population of plasma cells. The exact cause of this condition remains unclear. However, it commonly appears on the lower lip as erosive patches or plaques. While it can sometimes extend beyond the vermilion border, it generally spreads towards the chin [7].

Melkersson–Rosenthal syndrome granulomatous cheilitis and rhinoscleroma are two conditions that may be considered in the differential diagnosis. The former typically presents as recurrent swelling of the upper lip and is characterized by a granulomatous infiltrate, though plasma cell predominance can occur in some cases. While it may involve other mucocutaneous areas, the lips are often the primary site [7].

Rhinoscleroma, an infectious disease caused by Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis, primarily affects the nasal cavity and can lead to chronic rhinitis, nasal obstruction, and deformity. Though it usually extends upward to the orbit, skull base, or brain, rather than the upper lip, rare cases of cutaneous involvement have been reported. Histologically, plasma cells are often abundant in the inflammatory infiltrate, alongside eosinophils and characteristic Mikulicz cells—vacuolated histiocytes containing bacteria [7].

When ANCA is present, the main differential diagnosis is localized granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener’s granulomatosis). GPA more commonly presents with systemic symptoms like fever, fatigue, arthritis, and elevated acute-phase reactants, whereas CIMDL always involves the destruction of contiguous structures, particularly the anterior nose as a result of direct contact with the substance. In GPA, nasal destruction is typically limited to the septum [35], though a GPA case with palate, nose, and sinuses destruction has been reported [36]. Histologically, CIMDL is marked by massive apoptosis, while GPA features granulomas, microabscesses, and deep necrosis, which are absent in CIMDL [35].

Although both CID and CIMDL may share overlapping features—including ANCA positivity and a history of cocaine use—they are distinguished by their clinical and pathological profiles. CIMDL is characterized by osteocartilaginous destruction of midline structures such as the nasal septum, palate, or orbital walls, often associated with high-titer ANCA, especially anti-HNE. In contrast, CID presents with inflammatory mucocutaneous plaques lacking destructive or invasive features. Therefore, even in the presence of ANCA, the diagnosis of CID remains plausible if there is no radiologic or histologic evidence of tissue destruction. In our case, ANCA was negative, but if positive, careful correlation with imaging and clinical findings would have been required to definitively rule out CIMDL [35, 36].

Serologically, GPA is characterized by c-ANCA anti-PR3, whereas CIMDL more frequently shows p-ANCA anti-MPO at lower levels, often with additional ANCA (anti-HNE, lactoferrin, azurocidin, catalase K [24] and cathepsin G). Notably, anti-HNE ANCA is highly specific for cocaine/levamisole use. Other findings may include antiphospholipid antibodies, ANA, anti-dsDNA, and complement level reductions. Occasionally, an atypical ANCA pattern (cytoplasmic anti-MPO or perinuclear anti-PR3) may appear, which is unusual and warrants attention [37].

Similarly, LIV must be differentiated from primary small-vessel ANCA-associated vasculitis, particularly when overlapping with ear, nose, and throat symptoms, common in people who use cocaine. Retiform purpura in typical LIV locations strongly suggests cocaine/levamisole exposure [38]. LIV shares a serologic profile with CIMDL but generally presents with higher ANCA titers than primary ANCA vasculitis. Histologically, extravascular changes like granulomas are not expected [2].

Cocaine-induced dermatomucositis is controversial in terms of diagnostic criteria and hence why understanding the differential diagnoses and their pathophysiology is beneficial to exclude alternatives. As of now, we have seen that the diagnosis of this entity is mostly of exclusion of other causes and the presence of cocaine use in clinical history. Histopathology varies slightly but also helps in the diagnosis with the presence of plasma cell infiltrates. However, unlike previous reported cases, in the case we previously mentioned we have found significant changes in immunohistochemistry that may challenge the differential diagnosis between IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) and cocaine-induced dermatomucositis.

IgG4-Related Disease

IgG4-related disease may be triggered by autoimmune mechanisms or infectious agents. The condition is driven by an immune response dominated by type 2 helper T (Th2) cells, followed by regulatory T (Treg) cell activation. This leads to the overexpression of interleukins-4, 5, 10, and 13, as well as transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ), contributing to eosinophilia, elevated IgG4 and IgE levels, and progressive fibrosis. The massive infiltration of inflammatory cells causes organ damage, tumefactive enlargement, and dysfunction of affected tissues. Additionally, epithelial damage may result from inflammation and immune-complex deposition [39].

The most recent diagnostic criteria for IgG4-related disease is the 2020 Revised Comprehensive Diagnostic criteria for IgG4-RD by the Japanese group [40], which we selected as the more fitting criteria compared to the American College Rheumatology (ACR) and European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) formulated the 2019 ACR/EULAR IgG4-RD Classification Criteria for international use, as these criteria are the most commonly used when diagnosing IgG4-related disease [41]. We decided to use the Japanese criteria because of the lack of inclusion of the skin as a selected involved organ for diagnosis in the American and European criteria.

Unlike previous literature, which specifically excluded IgG4+ cell predominance or high IgG4+/IgG+ ratio to rule out IgG4-RD, in our case we found positivity to IgG4, opening a discussion on whether this excludes cocaine-induced dermatomucositis or it expands the potential manifestations of this entity. Although still controversial, since the clinical history was relevant for cocaine use, the macroscopic manifestations were similar to those described in previous literature, the differential diagnoses were excluded and the histology also showed neutrophil and plasma cell infiltrates (Fig. 2); we ultimately decided to include rich IgG4+ plasma cell infiltrate (Fig. 3) as a potential manifestation of cocaine-induced dermatomucositis rather than an exclusion criterion as previous cases seemed to consider. The prognosis appears favorable in most reported cases following the cessation of cocaine use and the administration of appropriate immunosuppressive treatment, although recurrences may occur if use resumes. Recognizing the issue early and educating patients about substance use are critical to achieving sustained remission and preventing long-term complications.

Further research is required on this matter; the pathophysiology described in this review and the novel findings in our case rather than contradict each other may be useful to further characterize this entity.

Conclusion

Cocaine-induced dermatomucositis is an underrecognized but distinct clinical entity that should be considered in patients presenting with chronic inflammatory skin and mucosal lesions, particularly in people known or suspected of using cocaine. This study contributes to a growing body of literature that seeks to establish diagnostic criteria by highlighting consistent clinical, histopathological, and immunological patterns. The presence of IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltrates in some cases raises questions regarding a potential overlap with IgG4-related disease, warranting further investigation. Effective management requires cocaine cessation and corticosteroid therapy, with varying outcomes depending on disease severity and adherence to treatment. Future research should focus on refining diagnostic markers and exploring targeted therapeutic approaches to better address this emerging condition.
