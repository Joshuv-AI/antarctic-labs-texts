Association between serum prolactin and anterior pituitary dysfunction in pituitary stalk interruption syndrome: a retrospective study of 155 patients from a large tertiary medical center in China
Authors: Lei Fu, Yan Xiao, Deyue Jiang, Lingtong Ye, Yiming Mu, Zhaohui Lyu, Qinghua Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPituitary stalk interruption syndrome (PSIS) is a rare hypothalamic-pituitary disorder causing progressive anterior pituitary deficiency. Subtle early symptoms and unreliable sex hormone levels in prepubertal patients complicate diagnosis of anterior pituitary deficiency. This study aimed to investigate the association between serum prolactin (PRL) levels and anterior pituitary deficiency in PSIS patients, as well as its potential as a biomarker for complete anterior pituitary deficiency (CAPD).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 155 patients with PSIS diagnosed and treated at the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital. We assessed the association between PRL levels and the severity of pituitary function deficits as well as testicular volume, and further evaluated its diagnostic value for CAPD.ResultsAmong 155 patients with pituitary stalk interruption syndrome (89.03% male; mean age 21.76 ± 6.20 years), 88 cases (56.77%) had complete anterior pituitary dysfunction(CAPD), while 67 cases(43.23%) had partial anterior pituitary dysfunction(non-CAPD). Patients with CAPD exhibited higher serum prolactin (PRL) levels (median 13.37 µg/L [IQR 8.93–24.01] vs. 11.44 µg/L [IQR 7.08–15.32], p = 0.006), and PRL levels increased with the number of impaired pituitary axes (p = 0.002). For every 1 µg/L increase in PRL, the risk of CAPD increased by 9% (95% CI: 1.04–1.14, p < 0.001). When combined with age, the area under the curve (AUC) for PRL in predicting CAPD was 0.73 (95% CI: 0.65–0.81) in the overall patient group, and as high as 0.84 (95% CI: 0.71–0.97) in patients under 18 years old, indicating better diagnostic performance in younger patients. Serum PRL levels were negatively correlated with testicular volume (a key predictor of fertility function) (r = -0.33, p < 0.001).ConclusionsElevated PRL levels correlate with the severity of anterior pituitary deficiency in PSIS, with higher diagnostic value for CAPD in patients under 18 years. Higher PRL levels correlates with reduced testicular volume.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Pituitary stalk interruption syndrome (PSIS) is a rare congenital disorder of hypothalamic-pituitary development, defined by imaging findings including an absent or thin pituitary stalk, ectopic neurohypophysis, and anterior pituitary hypoplasia [1]. Its etiology remains elusive, potentially involving genetic mutations, embryonic developmental defects, or perinatal injuries, with proposed mechanisms including perinatal trauma, congenital genetic defects, or a combination of both [2]. Clinically, PSIS presents with marked heterogeneity, affecting one or more anterior pituitary hormone axes and commonly manifesting as growth retardation, gonadal dysgenesis, hypothyroidism, or adrenal insufficiency, while posterior pituitary function is usually minimally affected [3].

In PSIS patients, deficiencies in anterior pituitary hormones are often progressive, with the number of affected hormonal axes typically increasing from one or two at initial presentation to three or four over time. For instance, Fernandez-Rodriguez et al. reported initial deficiency rates for growth hormone (GH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), luteinizing hormone/follicle-stimulating hormone (LH/FSH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and multiple hormone deficiencies of 92.3%, 26.9%, 11.5%, 7.6%, and 26.9%, respectively; follow-up assessments several years later revealed elevated rates of 100%, 76.9%, 76.9%, 53.8%, and 96.0%, respectively [4]. These observations align with our prior study on PSIS patients [5]. These data indicate that anterior pituitary dysfunction in PSIS progresses over time before stabilization. In prepubertal patients, the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis remains incompletely activated, with sex hormone levels physiologically low, making it challenging to accurately assess gonadotropin deficiency through gonadotropin or sex hormone measurements alone [6, 7]. As a result, PSIS patients at this stage cannot be reliably evaluated for gonadotropin deficiency solely based on these hormone levels. The severity of gonadotropin deficiency significantly affects long-term reproductive and sexual development outcomes and necessitates tailored treatment strategies [8].Therefore, reliable clinical indicators are urgently needed for the early detection of anterior pituitary dysfunction, which would allow for timely intervention and preservation of reproductive potential.

The primary anatomical cause of anterior pituitary deficiency in PSIS is pituitary stalk damage, which disrupts hypothalamic-pituitary axis signaling [2]. PSIS is characterized by deficiencies in multiple anterior pituitary hormones, and complete anterior pituitary deficiency (CAPD) represents a particularly severe phenotype, involving near-total loss of anterior pituitary hormone production. In clinical practice, unlike patients with isolated growth hormone (GH) deficiency, those with multiple pituitary hormone deficiency often present with non-suppressed PRL levels, frequently within or above the normal range [9]. It is reported that the prevalence of hyperprolactinemia in PSIS patients ranges from 25.8% to 36.4% [5, 10]. Under physiological conditions, PRL secretion is tightly regulated by a balance of inhibitory factors, primarily dopamine, and stimulatory factors, including thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), estrogen, and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) [11]. Dopamine, the dominant regulator, is delivered via the hypothalamic-pituitary portal system through the pituitary stalk to the anterior pituitary, where it binds to D2 receptors on lactotroph cells, suppressing PRL synthesis, secretion, and lactotroph proliferation [12]. In PSIS patient, pituitary stalk damage impairs dopamine-mediated inhibition, leading to dysregulated PRL secretion. This observation led to the consideration of whether there is a relationship between serum PRL levels and the severity of anterior pituitary deficiency in PSIS, and whether PRL can serve as a supportive biomarker to assess its extent. This question is regarded as particularly significant for patients under 18 years of age, in whom pituitary function is not yet fully developed.

To address this, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 155 patients with PSIS, integrating clinical hormone profiles and imaging characteristics to examine the relationship between serum PRL levels and the extent of anterior pituitary hormone deficiency, with a particular focus on CAPD. We further assessed the diagnostic value of PRL for anterior pituitary dysfunction across different age groups and investigated its potential association with reproductive parameters, including testicular volume.

Methods

Study population

This study systematically evaluated PSIS patients who were hospitalized in the Endocrinology Department of the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital and underwent MRI examination between January 2010 and July 2024. A total of 155 PSIS patients were included. PSIS was diagnosed based on MRI findings, clinical manifestations, and hormone tests. Exclusion criteria included: (1)use of medications known to significantly influence prolactin levels, such as dopamine agonists or antipsychotics (e.g. risperidone); (2)A current or prior diagnosis of prolactinoma, or other neoplastic conditions that may interfere with hypothalamic–pituitary regulation and result in elevated prolactin levels; (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) poor MRI image quality or missing key sequences; and (5) unclear clinical history or incomplete medical records. General patient information was obtained upon admission.

Magnetic resonance imaging

The specific MRI diagnostic criteria included: interruption or thinning of the pituitary stalk, disappearance or ectopic high signal of the posterior pituitary, and developmental disorders of the anterior pituitary. All patients underwent pituitary MRI using a 3.0 T system (GE Signa HDxt, USA), with standard T1- and T2-weighted sequences in coronal, sagittal, and axial planes. Head fixation was used to reduce motion artifacts.

Pituitary MRI scans were independently evaluated by two senior neuroradiologists (each with > 10 years of experience), who were blinded to all clinical and biochemical data to minimize observer bias. Any discrepancies in the assessment of pituitary stalk morphology were resolved through joint discussion and consensus.

Assessment of pituitary function

Baseline serum samples used for hormonal analyses were obtained under fasting conditions, prior to any stimulation tests, and generally in the morning (8:00 a.m.), after at least 30 min of rest to minimize stress-related interference with hormone levels. Hormone replacement therapy history was defined as stable treatment for at least 3 months.

Diagnostic Criteria were as follows:1) GH deficiency: GH deficiency were defined as peak GH values less than 5 ng/ml during a standardized insulin tolerance test. 2) Hypogonadism: Gonadal dysfunction was defined as low testosterone concentrations in men with decreased or inappropriately normal luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels. 3) Prolactin abnormality: PRL deficiency was defined as basal PRL below the lower limit of the gender-specific normal reference range (pregnant and lactating females were excluded from the study). Hyperprolactinemia was defined as basal PRL above the upper limit of the respective gender-specific normal reference range (> 17.7 µg/L in males and > 29.2 µg/L in females). 4) HPA axis dysfunction: The diagnosis of ACTH insufficiency is established if the 8:00 a.m. cortisol measures below 3 µg/dL. For patients with basal cortisol levels between 3 µg/dL and 15 µg/dL, the diagnosis was established if the peak serum cortisol level during an insulin tolerance test was < 18.1 µg/dL. 5) Hypothyroidism: a basal free thyroxin (FT4) level of no more than normal reference range 10.4 pmol/L, together with thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels that may be lowered, fall within the normal range, or show mild elevation. Complete anterior pituitary hormone deficiency (CAPD) was defined as the dysfunction of four major anterior pituitary axes: GH, gonadal, thyroid, and adrenal. Prolactin deficiency was not included in this definition.

All tests were performed in the Endocrinology Laboratory of the Chinese PLA General Hospital, using laboratory reference values as standards.

Testicular ultrasound

Testicular ultrasound was performed in male patients to assess testicular volume and structure. All examinations were conducted using a high-frequency linear-array transducer. Patients were examined in the supine position. Longitudinal and transverse diameters of each testis were measured, and testicular volume was calculated using the Lambert formula:

Volume (mL) = length × width × height × 0.71 [13, 14].

Ultrasound evaluations were performed by experienced sonographers.

Data analysis

Continuous data were classified according to normality. Normally distributed data were expressed as means and standard deviations and compared using independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were reported as medians and interquartile ranges and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The trend test for non-parametric variables uses the Jonckheere–Terpstra method. Categorical data were presented as n (%) and analyzed using chi-square tests. variables associated with CAPD were examined using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. To avoid structural multicollinearity between continuous PRL levels and its derived categorical variable (hyperprolactinemia), only the continuous variable was included in the multivariate model.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated with GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

This study included 155 patients with pituitary stalk interruption syndrome, comprising 138 males (89.03%) and 17 females (10.97%). The mean age at diagnosis was 21.76 ± 6.20 years. Of these, 88 patients (56.77%) had complete anterior pituitary deficiency (CAPD), while 67 (43.23%) had partial anterior pituitary hormone deficiency (non-CAPD). Compared to the non-CAPD group, the CAPD group were older at diagnosis. No significant differences were observed between groups in BMI and the rates of breech delivery or asphyxia.

Regarding pituitary function, levels of growth hormone (GH), luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and cortisol were significantly lower in the CAPD group compared to the non-CAPD group (p < 0.05). In the non-CAPD group, the most common hormone deficiency was GH (100%), followed by gonadal axis deficiency (74.63%), adrenal axis deficiency (46.27%), and thyroid axis deficiency (37.31%).

Among all anterior pituitary hormones, prolactin (PRL) levels exhibited a distinct distribution. Hyperprolactinemia was more prevalent in the CAPD group (35.23% vs. 19.40% in non-CAPD group, p = 0.03). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings showed that anterior pituitary height was 3.06 ± 0.75 mm in the non-CAPD group while 2.89 ± 0.77 mm in the CAPD group, with no significant difference (Table 1).

Relationship between PRL and the severity of anterior pituitary hormone deficiency

PSIS typically affects multiple anterior pituitary hormone axes, including growth hormone, gonadal, thyroid, and adrenal axes. In our cohort, all patients exhibited GH deficiency. Based on the number of deficient axes, hormone deficiency was classified as mild (1–2 axes, n = 24), moderate (3 axes, n = 43), or severe (4 axes, n = 88). PRL levels increased with the number of deficient pituitary axes, with median values of 8.61 µg/L, 12.06 µg/L, and 13.37 µg/L, respectively, showing a significant trend (p for trend = 0.002) (Table 2).

Correlation analysis of PRL and clinical parameters

Spearman correlation analysis revealed that PRL levels were significantly negatively correlated with age (r = − 0.23, p = 0.004), positively correlated with TSH levels (r = 0.23, p = 0.003), and negatively correlated with FT4 (r = − 0.16, p = 0.041) and cortisol levels (r = − 0.25, p = 0.002). Additionally, PRL levels were significantly positively correlated with pituitary stalk interruption status on imaging (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Although no significant correlation was observed between PRL levels and testosterone or estradiol levels, in a subgroup of male patients who underwent testicular ultrasound, PRL levels were significantly negatively correlated with testicular volume (r = − 0.33, p<0.001), suggesting a potential association with gonadal development status (Table 3).

Risk factors for CAPD in PSIS patients

A total of nine potential variables associated with CAPD were included in the univariate logistic regression analysis, including age, PRL level, hyperprolactinemia, sex, BMI, breech delivery, history of asphyxia, pituitary stalk status, and anterior pituitary height. Univariate logistic regression analysis identified age, PRL levels, and hyperprolactinemia as significant risk factors for CAPD (p < 0.05). In the multivariable model including age and PRL, PRL remained significantly associated with CAPD (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04–1.14, p < 0.001), indicating that each 1 µg/L increase in PRL level was associated with an approximately 9% increased risk of CAPD (Table 4).

Diagnostic performance of PRL for CAPD in PSIS patients

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of PRL alone and in combination with age for identifying CAPD in patients with PSIS (Fig. 1). In the overall cohort, PRL yielded an AUC of 0.63 (95% CI, 0.54–0.72) with a Youden index of 0.244. When stratified by age (< 18 years), the AUC increased to 0.73 (95% CI, 0.56–0.90) with a Youden index of 0.426. Furthermore, incorporating PRL and age together enhanced the discriminative ability, achieving an AUC of 0.73 (95% CI, 0.65–0.81) and a Youden index of 0.357 in the entire cohort. The enhancement was more pronounced in patients younger than 18 years, where the combined model reached an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI, 0.71–0.97) (Table 5).

Discussion

Pituitary stalk interruption syndrome (PSIS) is a rare congenital disorder characterized by hypothalamic-pituitary structural anomalies, resulting in anterior pituitary dysfunction. Hallmark features include pituitary stalk interruption, ectopic neurohypophysis, and anterior pituitary hypoplasia, with an estimated incidence of 0.5 per 100,000 and a male predominance [15, 16]. In our cohort of 155 PSIS patients, all had growth hormone (GH) deficiency, with gonadotropin, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) deficiencies occurring in 89.03%, 72.90%, and 76.77% of cases, respectively, likely reflecting the progressive nature of anterior pituitary dysfunction in PSIS [4]. Clinical manifestations of PSIS are often subtle at birth, with patients typically presenting with growth retardation, short stature, or absent secondary sexual characteristics during puberty, leading to delayed diagnosis [17]. In prepubertal patients, assessing gonadotropin deficiency via sex hormone levels is unreliable, posing challenges for timely diagnosis and treatment, which can adversely affect prognosis.While studies support gonadal evaluation during minipuberty, this assessment is clinically challenging and unfeasible in our study—mainly because patients’ age at consultation, constrained by healthcare resources, typically falls outside this critical time window [18].

In our study, 56.77% of patients had deficiencies in all four anterior pituitary hormones, a severe condition known as complete anterior pituitary deficiency (CAPD). CAPD necessitates lifelong glucocorticoid and thyroid hormone replacement, alongside challenges with growth, development, and reproduction. Notably, prolactin (PRL) levels in CAPD patients were often not reduced. Hyperprolactinemia was observed in 28.39% of the entire cohort and 35.23% of CAPD patients, significantly higher than in non-CAPD patients (19.40%).

We explored the clinical role of PRL in PSIS, finding a significant association with the severity of anterior pituitary dysfunction. PRL levels were higher in CAPD patients compared to those with partial deficiencies, increasing monotonically with the number of deficient axes: 8.61 µg/L (mild, 1–2 axes), 12.06 µg/L (moderate, 3 axes), and 13.37 µg/L (severe, 4 axes) (p for trend = 0.002). This dose-response pattern suggests PRL levels reflect the extent of pituitary impairment. Our analysis further supports PRL as a supportive indicator for assessing pituitary dysfunction in PSIS, with superior discriminative accuracy for CAPD in patients under 18 years (AUC = 0.73).

Elevated PRL in PSIS is commonly linked to disrupted dopamine signaling, as pituitary stalk abnormalities impair hypothalamic dopamine’s inhibitory effect on PRL secretion through the pituitary portal system [12]. Our finding of a significant positive correlation between PRL levels and the pituitary stalk interruption on MRI (p < 0.001) corroborates this mechanism. Maghnie et al. reported that the anterior pituitary in PSIS patients depends on an abnormal arterial blood supply, with significantly prolonged MRI contrast enhancement, indicating that vascular abnormalities may contribute to elevated PRL levels, providing a physiological basis for this observation [19].

While PRL levels showed no significant correlation with testosterone or estradiol level, in male patients who underwent testicular ultrasound, PRL was negatively correlated with testicular volume(r = − 0.33, p<0.001). Testicular volume, a stable indicator of long-term gonadal function compared to sex hormone levels [20, 21], is a critical predictor of fertility outcomes in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH), with smaller testicular volumes linked to reduced fertility potential post-treatment [22, 23]. In PSIS, the negative correlation between PRL and testicular volume may stem from PRL suppressing gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulsatility, possibly via inhibition of hypothalamic kisspeptin neurons, thus impairing gonadal development [24]. This suggests elevated PRL may signal gonadotropin deficiency in PSIS. Although elevated PRL is not a specific marker of gonadotropin deficiency, when combined with testicular volume, it may provide early clues to gonadal developmental abnormalities and guide treatment decisions in some PSIS patients, though its clinical value requires further investigation.

Our study found that PRL’s diagnostic performance for CAPD was significantly higher in patients under 18 years compared to all patients. Possible reasons include: first, during adolescence, the hypothalamic-pituitary-target gland axes are still developing, and structural abnormalities such as pituitary stalk defects may exert a more direct and pronounced impact on hormone axis function, making PRL levels a more sensitive indicator of pituitary status [25–27]; Second, in patients under 18 years, serum prolactin (PRL) levels remain relatively stable, primarily regulated by sustained hypothalamic dopamine inhibition, with minimal influence from TRH-mediated stimulation or sex hormones during this developmental stage, making pathological elevations easier to detect [28–32]. Despite structural defects of the pituitary stalk in some PSIS patients, serum prolactin concentrations remain within the normal range, suggesting the presence of compensatory endocrine mechanisms. Studies in animal models with reduced dopamine signaling have shown that the anterior pituitary may upregulate dopamine D2 receptor expression to enhance sensitivity to inhibitory signals [33, 34]. Although such mechanisms remain unclear in human PSIS, it is plausible that adult patients may develop some degree of endocrine compensation under long-term structural abnormalities, reducing PRL’s sensitivity in functional assessments. This hypothesis requires further mechanistic studies for validation.

Prolactin measurement is cost-effective, technically straightforward, and already incorporated into routine endocrine assessment. In the present study, serum PRL levels correlated significantly with the severity of anterior-pituitary dysfunction and exhibited higher discriminative accuracy for combined anterior-pituitary hormone deficiency in patients under 18 years with pituitary stalk interruption syndrome. Owing to its accessibility, PRL may serve as a supportive biomarker for early detection and risk stratification of PSIS.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its single-center, cross-sectional design, multicenter longitudinal studies are needed to further elucidate the relationship between changes in PRL levels and disease progression. Second, the analysis of PRL’s association with gonadal function lacked comprehensive spermatogenesis markers, limiting the depth of the gonadal development assessment. Third, routine screening for macroprolactin was not performed. Additionally, CAPD was defined as anterior pituitary hypofunction in regulating downstream target glands (including TSH, ACTH, FSH/LH, and GH deficiencies), excluding PRL. This aligns with current consensus: as PRL is independently regulated and its deficiency does not directly cause target gland dysfunction, it is not considered a core diagnostic indicator for CAPD [35]. Ultimately, future multicenter, prospective studies incorporating dynamic follow-up data and broader functional indicators are warranted to validate PRL’s diagnostic value and explore its specific applications in the clinical management of PSIS.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that serum PRL levels are significantly elevated in patients with PSIS and show a dose–response relationship with the severity of anterior pituitary hormone deficiency. Notably, when combined with age, PRL exhibited higher diagnostic performance for CAPD, especially in patients under 18 years of age. Moreover, PRL levels were negatively correlated with testicular volume, which is closely associated with future fertility potential. These findings suggest that evaluated serum PRL levels may indicate a higher likelihood of complete anterior pituitary dysfunction and compromised reproductive capacity.
