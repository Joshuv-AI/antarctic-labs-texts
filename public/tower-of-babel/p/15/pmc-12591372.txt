Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity Caused by Non-Anesthesiologists: A Narrative Review of Case Reports
Authors: Soo Hee Lee, Ho Kyung Yu, Ju-Tae Sohn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLocal anesthetics are widely utilized by anesthesiologists and physicians to manage pain effectively. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) is an infrequent occurrence that can lead to fatal outcomes. Although local anesthetics are commonly employed by non-anesthesiologists, a comprehensive analysis of case reports detailing LAST incidents caused by physicians other than anesthesiologists remains unknown. This review was performed to analyze case reports of LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists.MethodsCase reports regarding LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists were retrieved from PubMed using the following keywords: “bupivacaine toxicity,” “levobupivacaine toxicity,” “lidocaine toxicity,” “ropivacaine toxicity,” “tetracaine toxicity,” and “prilocaine toxicity” until December 31, 2023. A total of 53 case reports involving 59 patients were identified.ResultsThe distribution of local anesthetic use linked to LAST was as follows: lidocaine alone (59.33%), lidocaine plus prilocaine (11.86%), and bupivacaine or ropivacaine alone (10.17%). The predominant presumed cause of LAST was an overdose of local anesthetics (67.80% of all patients; lidocaine: 75% of local anesthetic overdoses), with or without accompanying patient’s risk factors. The primary routes of administration resulting in LAST were subcutaneous infiltration (38.98%) and topical application (35.59%). Common symptoms associated with LAST included central nervous system symptoms alone (50.85%) such as seizures, and combined central nervous and cardiovascular system symptoms (27.12%). Local anesthetics implicated in LAST were predominantly used for pain management during various procedures (71.19%) and for post-operative analgesia (11.86%). Of the patients experiencing LAST, 28.81% received lipid emulsion treatment in addition to supportive measures, achieving a full recovery rate of 100%. Conversely, 71.19% of the patients with LAST received only supportive treatment, leading to a full recovery rate of 73.81%.ConclusionThese findings suggest that subcutaneous infiltration or topical administration of lidocaine overdoses by non-anesthesiologists, particularly in patients with or without risk factors for LAST, significantly contributes to LAST. Preventive strategies, including education on maximum recommended doses of local anesthetics, identification of patient risk factors for LAST, utilization of ultrasound-guided nerve blocks, and ensuring the availability of lipid emulsion treatment, should be emphasized. Lipid emulsions ought to be readily accessible for the management of LAST in all locations where local anesthetics are used.

INTRODUCTION

Local anesthetics, easily administered by both anesthesiologists and non-anesthesiologists, are widely employed for providing analgesia during the post-operative period and in various procedures. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), primarily resulting from an overdose of local anesthetics or inadvertent intravascular absorption, is rare but can lead to severe complications. LAST typically manifests as central nervous system symptoms, including tinnitus, agitation, confusion, and seizures, which may progress to cardiovascular issues such as hypertension, tachycardia, hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, and conduction defects, ultimately resulting in cardiac arrest.1 The incidence of LAST has been reported to be 0.27 per 1,000 peripheral nerve blocks (approximately 0.03%; 95% confidence interval, 0.21–0.35) in registries.2 Additionally, the incidence of LAST associated with local infiltration anesthesia, predominantly performed by surgeons, was reported to be 0.7 and 2 per 1,000 local infiltration anesthesia during unilateral total knee and hip arthroplasties, respectively, as noted in a single-center study.3 Notably, non-anesthesiologists are implicated in up to 50% of LAST cases.4 Anesthesiologists generally possess a more profound understanding of the pharmacology of local anesthetics and frequently utilize ultrasound for nerve blocks, but non-anesthesiologists exhibit a comparatively elevated risk of inducing LAST due to this lack of specialized training. Recently, lipid emulsion, initially developed for parenteral nutrition, has become a standard treatment for LAST in cases involving anesthesiologists.5 However, healthcare professionals outside the field of anesthesiology often exhibit inadequate knowledge regarding lipid emulsion treatment for LAST.6 Surveys have indicated that health care professionals in obstetrics and gynecology lack sufficient information concerning safe and effective dosages of local anesthetics, appropriate treatments for LAST, and the lipid emulsion treatment protocol.7 Another survey revealed that 67.3% of dentists, who frequently administer local anesthetics, are unaware of lipid emulsion treatment for LAST.8 Furthermore, the ethical limitations inherent in conducting clinical studies on LAST preclude rigorous examination, thus emphasizing the need to elucidate the clinical features associated with LAST through detailed case report analysis. Despite this necessity, a thorough investigation of case reports detailing LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists remains unknown. Thus, the primary goal of this review is to analyze case reports of LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists, emphasizing the types of local anesthetics used, presumed causes of LAST, indications for local anesthetic administration, and the role of lipid emulsion treatment in LAST management. Another aim is to propose preventive strategies aimed at diminishing occurrences of LAST instigated by non-anesthesiologists through a meticulous examination of related case reports.

METHODS

Relevant case reports associated with LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists were identified through a search of PubMed using the following keywords until December 31, 2023: “lidocaine toxicity,” “bupivacaine toxicity,” “levobupivacaine toxicity,” “ropivacaine toxicity,” “prilocaine toxicity,” and “tetracaine toxicity.” The inclusion criteria for the case reports were as follows: i) case reports of LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists; ii) articles in English language. The exclusion criteria were as follows: i) non-human records; ii) non-case reports and letters; iii) non-LAST cases (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants and antiarrhythmic drugs); iv) systematic reviews, network meta-analyses, and clinical trials; and v) cases of LAST induced by anesthesiologists or pain specialists. A total of 4,137 reports were retrieved (Fig. 1). After excluding, non-human records, non-case reports, irrelevant titles, non-local anesthetic cases, incorrect document types, and reports without abstracts, 53 case reports were acquired for the final analysis (Fig. 1). The total number of patients was 59, as two case reports detailed two patients each, and two other reports included three patients each (Fig. 1). Hence, a total of 59 patients from the 53 case reports were analyzed for various data pertaining to LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists. The following data were extracted from each case report (Table 1): publication year, age, sex, weight, risk factors for LAST, underlying diseases, purpose of using local anesthetics, medical department administering local anesthetics in connection with LAST, route of administration, dosages of local anesthetics, presumed cause of LAST, preventive measures taken, symptoms of LAST, lipid emulsion treatment, and outcomes (Table 1). The presumed causes of LAST were analyzed using the following criteria: if the administered local anesthetic dosages in patients with LAST exceeded the maximum recommended dose (MRD), the presumed cause was classified as overdose of local anesthetic.62 Conversely, if the administered dosages of local anesthetics during nerve blocks, subcutaneous infiltrations, or intravenous administrations were less than the MRD, the presumed cause of LAST was attributed to suspected intravascular absorption. However, when the dosage of local anesthetics was not documented in the case report, the presumed cause was classified as undetermined. Additionally, the patients’ risk factors for LAST—including extreme age (less than 16 years or more than 60 years), low body mass (neonates, infants, and debilitated elderly individuals), and underlying medical conditions (such as cardiac, hepatic, and renal diseases)—were factored into the analysis.4 Subsequently, presumed causes of LAST were categorized into five groups: i) overdose with risk factors, ii) overdose alone, iii) suspected intravascular absorption with risk factors, iv) suspected intravascular absorption alone, and v) undetermined. Data regarding the site of LAST occurrences, supportive treatments administered, physicians or departments recommending lipid emulsion treatment for LAST, kinds of lipid emulsions used, methods of lipid emulsion administration, lipid emulsion dosages, time to symptom improvement after lipid emulsion administration, recurrence of LAST after lipid emulsion administration, and side effects related to lipid emulsion treatment were similarly extracted from each case report (Supplementary Table 1). When determining the medical department responsible for administering local anesthetics associated with LAST, the relevant medical department was identified as that of the medical professional involved in the treatment or procedure associated with the administration. However, if a specific medical department was not mentioned in the case reports, it was categorized as “other.” The risk of bias was assessed for all 53 case reports utilizing the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports.63 It should be noted that rounding percentages for components related to LAST may yield a total that does not precisely equal 100%.

Flow chart depicting the selection process of case reports. A total of 4,137 articles were identified through PubMed searches using a combination of the following keywords (“lidocaine toxicity,” “bupivacaine toxicity,” “levobupivacaine toxicity,” “ropivacaine toxicity,” “prilocaine toxicity,” and “tetracaine toxicity”). Of these, 1,850 were excluded as they were non-human studies and 1,800 were excluded as they were non-case reports or letters. The titles of 487 articles were reviewed and assessed for their relevance to the purpose of this review. At this stage, 374 records were excluded for the following reasons: non-local anesthetic systemic toxicity cases, non-local anesthetic drug cases (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants and antiarrhythmic agents), and incorrect document types (e.g., systematic reviews, network meta-analyses, and clinical trials). After reviewing the abstracts of the remaining 113 records, 77 articles were selected for further inspection, and 36 articles without abstracts were excluded. The eligibility of the remaining 77 papers was then assessed through a full-text review. A total of 24 papers were excluded because they involved local anesthetic systemic toxicity caused by anesthesiologists or pain physicians. A total of 53 articles were included in this review. Among these, two cases reported two patients each, whereas two other cases reported three patients each, bringing the total number of patients included in this systematic narrative review to 59.

Two authors (Lee SH and Yu HK) meticulously reviewed and verified each case report of LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists based on the following criteria: i) clear identification of the practitioner administering the local anesthetic or local anesthetic use during surgical procedures (for example: nerve block or operative site infiltration anesthesia by a surgeon); ii) local anesthetic application in medical procedures typically outside the scope of anesthesiologists in the operating room or pain clinic (for example: topical administration for laser hair removal or dermatological interventions); and iii) instances where local anesthetics were not administered by medical professional (for example: at-home topical application).

From the analysis of the 53 case reports, it was identified that the first clinical report detailing lipid emulsion treatment for LAST due to lidocaine, which was administered for cardiac arrhythmia by a non-anesthesiologist cardiologist, was published in 2011 (Case No: 41). The parameters of the cohort consisting of 18 patients receiving only supportive treatment for LAST prior to 2011 were retrospectively compared with those of another cohort comprising 17 patients who received supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion treatment for LAST between 2011 and 2023 to examine the effect of lipid emulsion on patient outcomes. The parameters extracted from both cohorts included age, sex, medical department involved in administering local anesthetics, purpose of local anesthetic use, the type of local anesthetics, route of administration, presumed cause and symptoms of LAST, treatments rendered, and outcomes (Table 2). Age comparisons between the two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test (Prism 5.0; GraphPad, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Categorical data comparisons between the groups were conducted using either the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The correlation coefficient between the proportions of lipid emulsion treatment administered for total LAST events caused by non-anesthesiologists in 2-year intervals from 2011 to 2023 and elapsed time (consecutive 2-year intervals from 2011 to 2023) was evaluated through linear regression to identify trends in lipid emulsion usage for LAST. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, the P value, which indicates statistical significance in the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, was adjusted using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

RESULTS

Age and sex distribution

The age distribution of patients with LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists was as follows: < 1 year, 13 (22.04%); ≤ 1 – < 16 years, 5 (8.74%); ≤ 16 – < 19 years, 0; ≤ 19 – < 60 years, 29 (49.15%); and ≥ 60 years, 12 (20.34%) (Table 1). Extreme age (< 16 or ≥ 60 years) is one of the major risk factors for LAST.4 Other risk factors can be observed in infant, such as low muscle mass and increased proportions of non-protein binding local anesthetics.4 Therefore, because of this evidence of their association with LAST, we used age groups to classify patients in this study. Among those affected, 29 (49.15%) were men and 26 (44.07%) were women. Data regarding sex were unavailable for four patients (6.78%; Table 1).

Medical department, purpose of local anesthetic use, administration route, and location of LAST

The frequency distribution of medical departments administering local anesthetics associated with LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists was as follows: other (unspecified, 27.12%) > internal medicine (11.86%) > urology (8.47%) > plastic surgery or non-doctor (6.78%) > chest surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, orthopedic surgery, and ear nose throat (5.08%) > general surgery, dermatology, interventional radiology, and pediatrics (3.39%) > emergency medicine, neurosurgery, and dentistry (1.69%) (Fig. 2A). The frequency distribution of purpose of local anesthetic use associated with LAST was as follows: pain control for various procedures (71.19%) > post-operative analgesia (11.86%) > pain relief only (6.78%) > arrhythmia treatment (5.08%) > reduction of intraoperative blood loss (Case No: 37), relief of bladder spasm (Case No: 57), and accidental ingestion (Case No: 50) (1.69%) (Fig. 2B). The routes of administration of local anesthetics linked to total cases of LAST were identified as follows: subcutaneous infiltration (38.98%), topical application (35.59%), nerve block (13.56%), intravenous administration (5.08%), nerve block plus subcutaneous infiltration (5.08%), and unspecified (1.69%) (Fig. 2C). The administration routes for local anesthetics utilized for pain control in various procedures (n = 42), which is the main purpose of local anesthetic usage involved in LAST, were as follows: subcutaneous infiltration (42.86%) > topical application (35.71%) > nerve block (11.90%) > nerve block plus subcutaneous infiltration (7.14%) > unspecified (2.38%). The frequency distribution of the location of LAST occurrences was as follows (Supplementary Table 1): outpatient clinic (35.59%) > operating room (28.81%) > intervention room (16.95%) > home (8.47%) > emergency room (5.08%) > delivery room (3.39%) > intensive care unit (1.69%).

Type of local anesthetic, presumed cause, patient risk factors, and symptoms associated with LAST

The distribution of local anesthetics associated with LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists was as follows: lidocaine alone (59.23%) > lidocaine plus prilocaine (EMLA cream, 11.86%) > bupivacaine or ropivacaine alone (10.17%) > prilocaine alone, lidocaine plus bupivacaine, lidocaine plus mepivacaine, mepivacaine plus bupivacaine, or tetracaine alone (1.69%) (Fig. 3A). The presumed causes of LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists were categorized as follows: overdose of local anesthetic plus the patient’s risk factors for LAST (44.07%) > overdose of local anesthetic alone (23.73%) > suspected intravascular absorption alone (8.47%) and suspected intravascular absorption plus patient’s risk factors (8.47%) > undetermined (15.25%) (Fig. 3B). The frequency of local anesthetics implicated in presumed overdose (n = 40) linked to LAST was as follows (Table 1): lidocaine alone (51.22%) > lidocaine plus prilocaine (17.07%) > bupivacaine alone (12.20%) > ropivacaine alone (7.32%) > lidocaine plus bupivacaine, lidocaine plus mepivacaine, bupivacaine plus mepivacaine, or prilocaine alone (2.44%) (Table 1). The median (interquartile range: 25% to 75%) overdose of single local anesthetics based on body weight was as follows (Table 1): lidocaine (n = 10), 16.13 (10.45 to 32.26) mg/kg; bupivacaine (n = 5), 4.42 (4.39 to 7.5) mg/kg; ropivacaine (n = 2), 3.775 (3.7375 to 3.8125) mg/kg; and prilocaine (n = 1), 6.5 mg/kg. The prevalence of predisposing risk factors (n = 38) related to LAST is outlined in Table 1: i) extreme age (less than 16 years or more than 60 years) alone (55.26%), ii) extreme age with underlying disease or disease alone (10.53%), iii) extreme age plus low body mass, or extreme age plus other, or others alone (5.26%), and iv) extreme age plus low body mass plus other, disease plus other, or low body mass alone (2.63%). The frequency of symptoms associated with LAST as reported by non-anesthesiologists is depicted in Fig. 3C: central nervous system symptoms alone (50.85%) > central nervous plus cardiovascular system symptoms (27.12%) > cardiovascular symptoms alone (11.86%) > central nervous and cardiovascular system symptoms plus others, or others alone (3.39%) > cardiovascular symptoms plus others or unspecified (1.69%). Seizure (19/30, 63.33%) was the most common symptom among the central nervous symptoms alone induced by LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists (Table 1). Other symptoms involving central nervous system alone were drowsiness, altered mental state, and unresponsiveness (Table 1). The symptoms involving central nervous and cardiovascular systems were seizure, loss of consciousness, hypotension, and arrhythmias. Cardiovascular manifestations alone comprised severe bradycardia (the most common), hypotension, pulseless electrical activity, and cardiac arrest (Table 1). The symptoms involving the central nervous and cardiovascular systems and others were lightheadedness, dizziness, palpitations, methemoglobinemia, and cyanosis (Table 1). Other symptoms alone included cyanosis and methemoglobinemia (Table 1). Symptoms involving cardiovascular system and other were methemoglobinemia and intermittent tachycardia (Table 1).

Measure to prevent LAST

Preventive measures against LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists were not undertaken for the majority of patients (48/59 patients, 81.36%) (Table 1). The following preventive strategies were adopted for the remaining 11 patients: local anesthetic with epinephrine (n = 4; 6.78%) > negative aspiration technique during local anesthetic administration (n = 3; 5.08%) > fluoroscopic guidance, ultrasound guidance, serum lidocaine concentration monitoring coupled with vital sign monitoring, local anesthetic dose restriction plus adherence to guidelines (n = 1; 1.69%) (Table 1).

Supportive treatment or lipid emulsion treatment for LAST, rationales for lipid emulsion use, and complications of lipid emulsion treatment for LAST

Among patients experiencing LAST attributable to non-anesthesiologists, supportive treatment combined with lipid emulsion therapy was administered in 17 patients (28.81%), while 42 patients (71.19%) received only supportive care without lipid emulsion (Fig. 4A). The supportive treatments employed for LAST are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. These treatments included: i) oxygen therapy, airway maintenance, seizure management with benzodiazepines; ii) cardiovascular interventions comprising epinephrine, atropine, antiarrhythmic agents, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, advanced cardiac life support, cardiac pacing, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and intensive care; iii) fluid resuscitation; and iv) methemoglobinemia treatment with methylene blue. The predominant method for administering lipid emulsion was bolus dosing, followed by continuous infusion (58.82%), continuous infusion alone (17.65%), and bolus administration alone (11.76%) (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table 1). However, details regarding the method of lipid emulsion administration were unstated for two patients (11.76%) (Fig. 4B). The justifications for lipid emulsion treatment (n = 17) were as follows: presence of LAST symptoms including seizures (Case No: 3, 4, 17; 17.65%) > absence of response to epinephrine (Case No: 1, 14) or inadequate cardiovascular response to advanced cardiac life support (Case No: 15, 25) (11.76%) > identification of LAST attributable to local anesthetic administered intraoperatively by a surgeon observed by anesthesiologists in the post-anesthetic recovery room (Case No: 19; 5.88%). However, reasons for lipid emulsion administration lacked clarity for 52.94% of the cases managed with lipid emulsion (Case No: 8, 13, 20, 24, 30, 31, 33, 41). The types of lipid emulsions used to address LAST are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The predominant formulation utilized was 20% Intralipid (29.41%), followed by Intralipid (11.76%), and 10% Intralipid (5.88%). Nonetheless, some case reports specified the type of lipid emulsion generically, documenting it simply as “20% lipid emulsion” in eight cases (47.06%) and “lipid emulsion” in one patient (5.88%) (Supplementary Table 1). Various dosing regimen regarding lipid emulsion treatment were used (Fig. 4C), with the regimen of 1.5 mL/kg of 20% lipid emulsion, followed by a continuous infusion of 0.25 mL/kg/min of 20% lipid emulsion, as the most frequently employed method of administration (35.29%; Case No: 1, 3, 4, 14, 25, 28; Fig. 4C). The combination of supportive treatment and lipid emulsion resulted in complete recovery from LAST in all treated patients (total n = 17) (Fig. 4D, Table 1). In contrast, supportive treatment alone without lipid emulsion achieved full recovery in 31 out of 42 patients (73.81%) (Fig. 4D, Table 1). Seven patients (16.67%) receiving only supportive treatment for LAST instigated by non-anesthesiologists experienced mortality (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, outcome information was unavailable for four patients (9.52%) who received only supportive treatment (Fig. 4D). The frequency distribution of the physicians or departments recommending lipid emulsion treatment for LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists was as follows (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 1): anesthesiologists (35.29%) > emergency medicine (11.76%) > rapid response team, pediatric intensivist, or poison control center (5.88%). However, information regarding the recommending individuals or departments was not provided for six patients (35.29%) (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 1). The first report of lipid emulsion treatment for LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists was documented in 2011 (Case No: 41; Fig. 5B). The distribution of lipid emulsion treatment for LAST (total n = 17) from 2011 to 2023 was as follows (Fig. 5B, Table 1): 29.41%, 52.94%, and 17.65% for the intervals 2011 to 2015, 2016 to 2020, and 2021 to 2023, respectively (Fig. 5B, Table 1). The coefficient of determination (R2) demonstrating the correlation between the percentage of lipid emulsion treatment administered for all LAST occurrences and elapsed time (in 2-year increments) during the period from 2011 to 2023 was calculated at 0.592 (P = 0.043; Fig. 5C and Table 1), implying an increasing trend in the utilization of lipid emulsion treatment over time. Complication arising from lipid emulsion treatment were generally not reported in most cases (94.12%) (Supplementary Table 1). Specifically, one case with a milky appearance in cardiopulmonary bypass circuits following lipid emulsion treatment was noted (Case No: 13; Supplementary Table 1).

Comparison of supportive treatment alone and supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion treatment for LAST

No significant differences were observed between the supportive treatment alone group and the supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion treatment group regarding age, sex, medical department, purpose of local anesthetic use, type of local anesthetics, route of administration, presumed causal factors, symptoms, or treatments employed (including cardiopulmonary resuscitation or advanced cardiac life support) associated with LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists (Table 2). In contrast, complete recovery from LAST was markedly higher in the group receiving supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion therapy compared to the group receiving only supportive treatment (P = 0.015), with full recovery rates of 100% versus 66.66%, respectively (Table 2).

Assessment of risk of bias in the 53 case reports using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports

The median score obtained of the assessment of risk of bias in the 53 case reports using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports was 8 (interquartile range [25–75%]: 7–8), suggesting a relatively low risk of bias across all data derived from these reports (Supplementary Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study was conducted to analyze case reports of LAST attributed to non-anesthesiologists, focusing on the types of local anesthetics utilized, the underlying causes of LAST, the indications for their use, and the outcomes following lipid emulsion treatment. Taken together, these results indicate that subcutaneous infiltration or topical application of an excessive dose of lidocaine administered by physicians other than anesthesiologists for pain control in various procedures contributes significantly to LAST. Additionally, lipid emulsion treatment leads to complete recovery from LAST in affected patients. The key findings of this study were as follows: i) an overdose of lidocaine, alone or in combination with other local anesthetics, resulted in LAST in 50.85% of patients; ii) subcutaneous infiltration (42.86%) and topical application (35.71%) of local anesthetics, predominantly utilized for pain management in different procedures, primarily produced LAST; iii) the principal symptoms induced by LAST included central nervous system symptoms alone (50.85%; most common symptom: seizure) or symptoms involving both the central nervous and cardiovascular systems (27.12%); iv) lipid emulsion treatment was administered in 28.81% of all LAST cases caused by non-anesthesiologists, with 1.5 mL/kg of 20% lipid emulsion followed by 0.25 mL/kg/min as the most commonly employed (35.29%) dosing regimen for lipid emulsion treatment; and v) the combined treatment of supportive measures and lipid emulsion resulted in full recovery in all patients (100%) with LAST, whereas supportive treatment alone achieved full recovery in 73.81% of patients.

Local anesthetics inhibit voltage-operated sodium channels, leading to analgesia through the blockade of sensory and motor signal.64 LAST induces central nervous system symptoms, characterized by an initial excitatory phase including tinnitus, slurred speech, and generalized seizures, followed by a depressive phase manifesting as coma and respiratory depression.1 Additionally, LAST presents with a progression of cardiovascular symptoms.1 The initial phase is marked by hypertension and tachycardia, succeeded by an intermediate phase involving myocardial depression and hypotension.1 In the terminal phase, symptoms escalate to vasodilation, severe hypotension, ventricular arrhythmias, and asystole.1 The central nervous system and cardiac symptoms associated with LAST, resulting from an overdose of local anesthetics or accidental intravascular injection, stem from sodium channel inhibition within the central nervous system and the inhibition of sodium, potassium, and calcium channels in the heart.164 In the current analysis, 50.85% of patients with LAST exhibited central nervous system symptoms alone, which included seizures, altered mental status, drowsiness, disorientation, and unresponsiveness. Additionally, 27.12% of patients presented with both central nervous system and cardiovascular symptoms, which encompassed loss of consciousness, hypotension, arrhythmia, hallucinations, syncope, dysarthria, tachycardia, and hypertension. Approximately 81.36% (48/59) of patients with LAST displayed either central nervous system symptoms alone or combined with other symptoms in the present analysis. A previous study analyzing case reports and registries regarding LAST indicated that seizures were the most common symptom (53% from case reports or 61% from registries).2 Consistent with this previous report,2 seizure (63.33%) were identified as the most prevalent symptom in cases of isolated central nervous system involvement attributable to non-anesthesiologists in the current analysis. Bradycardia was the most common symptom affecting the cardiovascular system alone documented in the present study (4/7 [57.14%]). However, cardiovascular symptoms are the most frequently reported in pediatric patients experiencing LAST.65 This discrepancy may result from the higher frequency of cardiotoxic bupivacaine use and the administration of local anesthetics under general anesthesia for neonates and infants in prior reports.65 Methemoglobinemia, induced by an overdose of prilocaine, leads to cyanosis, decreased peripheral oxygen saturation, and drowsiness due to the inability of methemoglobin to bind oxygen.66 Other symptoms observed in five patients with LAST in the current analysis, including cyanosis, shortness of breath, and respiratory distress, were attributed to prilocaine-induced methemoglobinemia (8.47% of total patients). The potency of local anesthetics in producing myocardial depression is as follows: bupivacaine > ropivacaine > lidocaine.67 This analysis suggests that more cardiotoxic local anesthetics, such as bupivacaine, were used only in patients receiving supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion.67 Because bupivacaine is highly lipid soluble (log P [octanol/water partition coefficient]: 3.41), it is very responsive to lipid emulsion treatment.46869 These factors likely contributed to the 100% recovery rate from LAST in the group that received lipid emulsion treatment. Collectively, these findings suggest that symptoms associated with LAST are most commonly characterized by central nervous system effects, particularly seizures, which may be linked to the more commonly used and less cardiotoxic local anesthetic, lidocaine, as observed in the current analysis. Furthermore, bupivacaine should be avoided whenever possible to reduce the risk of LAST-induced cardiac arrest.

The MRD of local anesthetics without epinephrine are as follows: lidocaine (4.5 mg/kg, MRD in adult: 200 mg), bupivacaine (2 mg/kg, MRD in adult: 150 mg), ropivacaine (3 mg/kg, MRD in adult: 225 mg), mepivacaine (4.5 mg/kg, MRD in adult: 400 mg), and prilocaine (6 mg/kg, MRD in adult: 400 mg).62 The median overdoses of local anesthetics observed in the current analysis were as follows: lidocaine (16.13 mg/kg), bupivacaine (4.42 mg/kg), and ropivacaine (3.775 mg/kg), which exceeded the MRDs for lidocaine, bupivacaine, and ropivacaine by approximately 3.58, 2.21, and 1.26 times, respectively. A previous study exploring presumed causes of LAST in pediatric patients indicated that intravascular injection of local anesthetics primarily occurs due to anesthesiologists, whereas overdoses of local anesthetics are largely attributed to non-anesthesiologists.65 In agreement with this previous report,65 in the present study, the most common presumed cause of LAST was identified as an overdose of local anesthetic (67.80% of total patients), comprising overdoses with or without patient risk factors for LAST (44.07% and 23.73%, respectively; Fig. 3B). The predominant local anesthetic involved in LAST cases related to overdose in the current study was lidocaine (75%, 30/40), either alone or with other local anesthetics (Table 1). Extremes of age (less than 16 years or more than 60 years) with or without additional risk factors, represented the most prevalent risk factor for LAST (30/38, 78.95%) identified in the current study. The distribution frequencies of patients' risk factors for LAST in the current analysis were as follows: extreme age (less than 16 years or more than 60 years, 21 patients) > underlying diseases (heart failure, n = 4; atrial fibrillation, n = 2; cardiomyopathy, n = 2; coronary artery disease, chronic renal disease, or liver cirrhosis, n = 1) > others (hypoalbuminemia, n = 2; glycogen storage disease, chronic alcohol intake, infection, antipsychotic medication, or gene mutation, n = 1) > low lean body mass alone (n = 1) (Table 1). Together, these results suggest that practitioners administering local anesthetics should adhere to the MRDs (especially for lidocaine) and conduct careful evaluations of patients’ risk factors, including extreme age (infants or individuals more than 60 years old) to mitigate the risk of LAST.

According to the current analysis of case reports of LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists, the primary routes of local anesthetic administration were subcutaneous infiltration and topical application, which accounted for 74.58% of all patients with LAST. Subcutaneous infiltration and topical application of local anesthetics (33/42, 78.57%) were predominantly used for pain management during various procedures in this analysis. LAST is generally underreported. A previous study examining LAST through peer-reviewed journals and online registries from March 2014 to November 2016 reported 47 case reports from peer-reviewed journals and 11 from online registries.2 The primary types of blocks contributing to LAST in this study included penile blocks (23%) > local infiltration analgesia (17%) > neuroaxial blocks (13%).2 However, the differences in block types leading to LAST between the current analysis and previous reports may predominantly arise from variations in practitioner specialties (current analysis: a mix of various departments physician excluding anesthesiologists; previous report: anesthesiologists [49%], surgeons [33%], dentists [9%], emergency medicine physicians [5%], pediatricians [2%], and patient’s self-administration of local anesthetics [2%]).2 The practitioners contributing to LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists in the current study included other medical departments (27.11%), i.e., lacked specific departmental details; internal medicine physicians (11.86%) > urology surgeons (8.47%) > plastic surgeons or non-doctors (6.78%) > chest surgeons, obstetrics and gynecology surgeons, orthopedic surgeons, and ear nose throat physicians (5.08%). Recently, a single-center study reported an incidence of severe LAST (refractory seizures and arrhythmias necessitating lipid emulsion treatment) induced by bupivacaine infiltration analgesia in unilateral total hip arthroplasty at a rate of 2.41 per 1,000.70 A previous study evaluating the locations of LAST occurrences within hospitals documented the frequency of locations as follows: operating rooms (67%) > post-anesthetic care units (14%) > emergency room or wards (8%) > intensive care units (3%).2 However, the most common locations for LAST in the current analysis were outpatient clinics (35.59%), operating rooms (28.81%), and intervention rooms (16.94%). This difference in locations may also stem from differences in practitioner groups. Consequently, these findings emphasize that physicians should exercise caution when performing subcutaneous infiltration or topical application of local anesthetics for pain control in outpatient clinics due to the potential for LAST.

The treatment for LAST includes the following steps: i) airway maintenance, ii) seizure management using benzodiazepines, small dose of succinylcholine when seizure is persistent despite administration of benzodiazepine, and lipid emulsion, which mitigate hypoxia and acidosis, iii) cardiac arrest treatment involving a small dose of epinephrine (1 μg/kg), advanced cardiac life support, lipid emulsion, and amiodarone, and iv) consideration of cardiopulmonary bypass in patients unresponsive to lipid emulsion and vasopressors.45 Similar to previous reports, supportive treatment for LAST observed in the current study encompassed oxygen supply, fluid administration, seizure control with benzodiazepines or propofol, management of cardiovascular symptoms with epinephrine, atropine, antiarrhythmic medications, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, advanced cardiac life support, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (Supplementary Table 1).5 Anesthesiologists now routinely utilize lipid emulsion, initially designed for parenteral nutrition, to manage LAST.5 In the past, lipid emulsion was administered in cases of LAST unresponsive to supportive treatments. However, the contemporary practice involves the utilization of lipid emulsions at the onset of LAST, after establishing airway control to prevent hypoxia and acidosis.45 The recommended dosage for lipid emulsion treatment of LAST, as outlined by the American Society of Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine, includes an initial bolus dose of 1.5 mL/kg of 20% lipid emulsion over 2–3 minutes, followed by a continuous infusion rate of 0.25 mL/kg/min of 20% lipid emulsion.4 In instances of hemodynamic instability, the continuous infusion rate may be increased to 0.5 mL/kg/min.4 The upper recommended dose for the initial administration of 20% lipid emulsion is approximately 12 mL/kg.45 The dosing regimen employing 1.5 mL/kg of 20% lipid emulsion followed by 0.25 mL/kg/min is the most commonly utilized for lipid emulsion treatment in the current analysis. Since anesthesiologists (35.29%) were the predominant physicians recommending lipid emulsion treatment for LAST caused by non-anesthesiologists in this analysis, it can be inferred that this frequent application of lipid emulsion dosing is influenced by anesthesiologist recommendations.4 Moreover, lipid emulsion serves as an adjunctive agent in emergency medicine to ameliorate cardiovascular depression resulting from highly lipid-soluble non-local anesthetics that have no specific antidotes and are unresponsive to supportive treatments.57172 Noteworthy observations from the current analysis concerning lipid emulsion treatment are as follows: first, 100% of patients receiving supportive treatment in conjunction with lipid emulsion treatment achieved full recovery from LAST. In contrast, only 73.81% of patients receiving supportive treatment alone achieved full recovery, with approximately 16.67% succumbing to the condition. Second, as the initial clinical case report of lipid emulsion treatment for LAST induced by non-anesthesiologists was presented in 2011 (Case No: 41), the trend in lipid emulsion treatment for LAST has been analyzed over time since that year. The coefficient of determination between the percentage of lipid emulsion treatment for total LAST cases every two consecutive years from 2011 to 2023 and the elapsed time (two-year intervals) was calculated at 0.592 (R2, P = 0.043), suggesting that lipid emulsion treatment for LAST appears to be more prevalent in recent years compared to the past. Collectively, these results underscore the necessity for 20% lipid emulsion to be readily available for the treatment of LAST in any setting where local anesthetic administration is performed for analgesia or other treatments, such as cardiac arrhythmias.

The mechanism by which lipid emulsion therapy mitigates LAST is thought to involve both direct and indirect actions.5 The well-documented lipid shuttle mechanism, an indirect process associated with lipid emulsion therapy, posits that the lipid phase of the emulsion sequesters the lipid-soluble local anesthetic bupivacaine (log P [octanol/water partition coefficient]: 3.41) from the brain and heart.5 This emulsion, now containing the local anesthetic, is subsequently transported to the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue for detoxification and storage.5 Furthermore, the direct effects of lipid emulsion encompass enhanced inotropic activity, prevention of mitochondrial dysfunction, supply of fatty acids, phosphorylation of glycogen synthase kinase-3β, and reduction of nitric oxide release.5

Preventive measures against LAST include ultrasound-guided nerve blocks, incremental administration of local anesthetics, utilization of the lowest effective doses, needle aspiration prior to the administration of local anesthetics, and the use of epinephrine as a marker for intravascular injection.4 Ultrasound-guided nerve blocks have been shown to diminish the incidence of LAST (seizures: 0.08 per 1,000 nerve blocks) and postoperative neurologic symptoms.73 Furthermore, a single case of LAST necessitating lipid emulsion treatment occurred among the total 2,735 ultrasound-guided nerve blocks performed.74 However, despite the implementation of negative aspiration techniques utilizing local anesthetics, with or without epinephrine, in 52% of pediatric patients with LAST, this approach does not reliably prevent LAST.65 Thus, this method is unreliable.6575 In the current analysis, 81.36% of patients with LAST linked to non-anesthesiologists did not employ any measures for prevention (Table 1). As in previous reports,6575 LAST occurred in seven patients using either epinephrine-containing local anesthetics or negative aspiration techniques, while only one instance of LAST arose from the use of ultrasound guidance. Collectively, these results advocate strongly for the adoption of ultrasound-guided nerve blocks as a preventive strategy against LAST.7374

The limitations of this review are as follows. First, instances of LAST are commonly underreported due to various causes, including medicolegal problems, leading to an incomplete understanding of the true incidence, risk factors, and outcomes associated with the condition. This incompleteness can skew the results and interpretations made from available case reports, potentially minimizing the perceived prevalence or severity of LAST in clinical practice. Second, the analysis employed case reports with varying degree of information (ranging from insufficient to comprehensive data for extraction), as reported on PubMed; consequently, some data necessary for analysis may have been unavailable or unclear, thus impacting the results. Nonetheless, the overall median risk of bias score among the 53 case reports, evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports, was 8 (Supplementary Table 2), indicative of a relatively low risk of bias.63 Third, the prevalence of positively reported case studies regarding lipid emulsion treatment may distort the perception of its effectiveness and safety. Promoting the publication of negative or inconclusive findings is essential to mitigate publication bias. Fourth, in the retrospective comparison of the two groups encompassing supportive treatment alone and supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion treatment, data for the supportive treatment alone group were derived prior to 2011, while data for the supportive treatment combined with lipid emulsion were obtained from 2011 to 2023. Consequently, the more contemporary treatment and diagnostic approaches utilized within the supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion group may impact the results. However, the exclusive use of the more cardiotoxic local anesthetic bupivacaine in only the supportive treatment plus lipid emulsion group should be acknowledged (Table 2). Fifth, in the early stages of reporting LAST, when there were no case reports on LAST, they were well-accepted by academic journals. However, increasing numbers of submissions allowed journals to be more selective, which could have introduced bias in case analyses. To address these limitations, the establishment of an online registry employing a standardized reporting form for LAST cases should be considered.

These findings suggest that an overdose of local anesthetic (particularly lidocaine), administered via subcutaneous infiltration or topical application by non-anesthesiologists in outpatient clinics for pain control of various procedures in patients of extreme age or those with underlying diseases, results in LAST. Therefore, to reduce the incidence of LAST, it is crucial to implement educational programs that encompass the MRDs of local anesthetics, evaluation of patients’ risk factors for LAST, including extreme age or underlying diseases, ultrasound-guided nerve blocks, and lipid emulsion treatment. Moreover, lipid emulsions should be readily accessible for the management of LAST in any environment where local anesthetics are administered. If feasible, the use of bupivacaine should be avoided to decrease the risk of cardiac arrest associated with LAST.
