Smart polymeric nanoparticles for targeted delivery and microenvironment-responsive therapy in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Narayani Prasad Kar, Junyi Lin, Ashkan HassankhaniRad, Wei Li, Alaa R. Aboushanab, Ying Li, Jingjing Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is among the most lethal malignancies, characterized by aggressive biology, a dense fibrotic and immunosuppressive microenvironment, and profound resistance to standard therapies. Smart polymeric nanoparticles (SPNs), engineered to sense and respond to biological cues, present a transformative approach to overcome these barriers. This review highlights recent advances in SPNs tailored for PDAC, including systems designed to actively target tumor cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and cancer stem cells (CSCs), thereby enhancing selective drug delivery and efficacy. SPNs also remodel the desmoplastic stroma or deliver matrix-modulating agents to improve tumor penetration. Furthermore, stimuli-responsive SPNs exploit the unique tumor microenvironment (TME) of PDAC, leveraging pH, hypoxia, or enzymatic triggers to achieve controlled, localized drug release. Beyond these strategies, SPNs have been developed to reprogram tumor immunity, modulate metabolic pathways, and enable precision gene therapy or combination treatments. Incorporating chronotherapy principles, future SPNs are capable of synchronizing drug release with circadian rhythms to maximize therapeutic windows while minimizing toxicity. Emerging concepts, such as integrating biosensors for real-time endogenous signal detection or applying AI-driven design to optimize SPN properties, underscore the future potential of these systems. Together, these multifaceted strategies position SPNs as a powerful platform to tackle the formidable challenges of PDAC and advance toward personalized cancer care.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, with a 5-year survival of only 12.5 % in 2024 [1]. This persistent clinical challenge stems from both diagnostic limitations and intrinsic biological properties of the disease. Most patients present with advanced or metastatic disease at diagnosis, significantly constraining treatment options. More fundamentally, PDAC exhibits profound resistance to all current therapeutic modalities, from conventional cytotoxic agents to newer targeted therapies and immunotherapies.

The unique tumor microenvironment (TME) of PDAC represents the most formidable obstacle to effective treatment delivery [2]. Unlike most solid tumors, PDAC develops within an exceptionally dense and complex stromal compartment that actively contributes to treatment resistance. This fibrotic stroma originates from the pathological activation of pancreatic stellate cells, which transform from quiescent vitamin A-storing cells into highly active myofibroblast-like cells. These activated stellate cells collaborate with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) to produce and remodel excessive extracellular matrix components, particularly fibrillar collagens types I and III, fibronectin, and hyaluronic acid (HA) [3]. The resulting extracellular matrix creates an unusually rigid physical barrier, with tissue stiffness measurements consistently exceeding 8 kPa compared to the 0.2–2 kPa range of normal pancreatic tissue. This dense matrix prevents adequate drug penetration, with most therapeutic agents becoming trapped in perivascular regions and failing to reach cancer cells located deeper within the tumor mass.

The vascular network within PDAC tumors presents another critical barrier to effective therapy. While many solid tumors demonstrate some degree of vascular hyperpermeability that facilitates drug delivery through the enhanced permeability and retention effect, PDAC exhibits fundamentally different vascular characteristics. The extreme mechanical stress generated by the dense stroma leads to widespread vascular compression and collapse [4]. The remaining functional blood vessels show poor endothelial fenestration with significantly reduced pore sizes, typically measuring below 100 nm compared to the 200–2000 nm range seen in more permeable tumor types. These structural abnormalities combine with reduced vascular density to create markedly impaired perfusion, with blood flow rates measuring less than 20 % of those observed in normal pancreatic tissue [5]. In addition, PDAC exhibits exceedingly high interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), creating strong outward fluid flow that hinders drug penetration [6–8].

PDAC has a profoundly immunosuppressive TME that actively blocks effective anti-tumor immunity. The PDAC stroma harbors abundant immunosuppressive cell populations, including M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and regulatory T cells, which create an “immunologically cold” tumor landscape. These cells secrete inhibitory cytokines like TGF-β and IL-10 while expressing immune checkpoint molecules that suppress cytotoxic T cell activity. In addition, the dense fibrotic stroma physically restricts immune cell infiltration and function. The resulting immunosuppressive milieu not only enables tumor immune evasion but also renders PDAC resistant to current immunotherapies.

The molecular complexity of PDAC presents fundamental challenges that extend beyond its physical barriers. The disease exhibits marked genetic heterogeneity dominated by near-ubiquitous KRAS mutations (occurring in >90 % of cases), which drive aggressive tumor progression and confer resistance to therapeutic interventions [9,10]. The KRAS-driven molecular program influences nearly all aspects of PDAC biology, from cellular metabolism to interactions with the microenvironment. Additional common genetic alterations in TP53, CDKN2A, and SMAD4 further contribute to the aggressive phenotype of PDAC and treatment resistance.

To overcome these challenges, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for PDAC [6]. Nab-paclitaxel (Abraxane®), an albumin-bound paclitaxel formulation, was approved in combination with gemcitabine as a first-line therapy, which showed improved survival compared to gemcitabine alone. The albumin carrier enhances drug solubility and may facilitate tumor accumulation through gp60-mediated transcytosis and interaction with SPARC in the stroma. However, the exact role of these mechanisms in the efficacy of Abraxane remains unclear. Liposomal irinotecan (Onivyde®) offers improved pharmacokinetics and biodistribution for irinotecan. Initially approved as a second-line therapy with 5-fluorouracil and leucovorin (5-FU/LV) for gemcitabine-refractory PDAC (NAPOLI-1 trial) [11], Onivyde® has since been incorporated into first-line regimens such as NALIRIFOX (liposomal irinotecan, 5-FU/LV, and oxaliplatin). The liposomal encapsulation prolongs circulation, enhances tumor accumulation via the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, and facilitates sustained release of its active metabolite, SN-38 [11]. The conversion of irinotecan to SN-38 can occur locally within the tumor by carboxylesterases (CES) present in tumor-associated macrophages and potentially tumor cells [11]. Despite these advancements, both Abraxane and Onivyde have shown only modest clinical benefits. Their ability to significantly prolong overall survival remains limited, largely due to insufficient drug delivery and poor tissue penetration in the dense and immunosuppressive PDAC microenvironment.

In light of these limitations, researchers are now developing a new generation of smart polymeric nanomaterials designed to actively respond to the PDAC microenvironment and enhance therapeutic precision [12]. These smart polymeric nanoparticles (SPNs) are engineered with dynamic, programmable structures that can recognize biological signals, interact with specific tumor components, and release their payload in response to internal stimuli (e.g., pH, enzymes, redox conditions) [13] or external triggers (e.g., light or ultrasound) [12,14]. Unlike traditional carriers, SPNs can integrate therapeutic delivery with diagnostic capabilities, adapt to patient-specific needs, and overcome biological barriers more effectively.

This review will highlight recent progress in SPNs systems tailored for PDAC, focusing on smart carriers that ensure safe elimination and tunable release, thereby improving drug accumulation while reducing toxicity [15,16]. We will also explore stimuli-responsive polymers that respond to TME cues and biomimetic systems that enhance biocompatibility and targeting through structural mimicry of natural biological entities [17]. We will detail how the unique chemical and physical properties of these smart polymeric architectures are being harnessed to advance multiple therapeutic strategies in PDAC [18]. These include enhancing targeted drug delivery, enabling novel gene therapy approaches such as RNA interference and mRNA transfection [19,20]. In addition, this review will cover innovative polymer-based immuno-oncology strategies to reprogram the immunosuppressive TME [21], and explore how these advancements are paving the way for personalized nanomedicine, where treatments are tailored to individual patient and tumor characteristics [22]. By dissecting these advanced polymeric strategies, this review aims to provide a forward-looking perspective on how intelligent material design is poised to revolutionize the treatment landscape for one of most intractable diseases in oncology.

Intelligent polymeric design to overcome PDAC delivery obstacles

Leveraging polymer functionality for active tumor targeting

Targeting tumor cells and stromal components

While the EPR effect has guided nanomedicine development for decades, its limitations in pancreatic cancer have become clinically apparent [23,24]. The dense fibrotic stroma, leads to vascular collapse and, consequently, elevated IFP [25]. Most blood vessels in PDAC are non-functional and poorly fenestrated, impeding efficient NP accumulation via passive mechanisms [26]. Recent investigations into alternative transport mechanisms have identified active endothelial transcytosis as a potentially more reliable delivery pathway in PDAC. Unlike passive extravasation through endothelial gaps, transcytosis involves receptor-mediated vesicular transport across intact vascular endothelium. This process appears less susceptible to stromal interference, though the precise relationship between matrix composition and transcytosis efficiency remains an active area of investigation. The discovery of this pathway has prompted a reevaluation of nanoparticle design principles, shifting focus from size optimization for passive accumulation to active engagement of endothelial transport systems.

Modern polymer chemistry enables precise surface engineering of SPNs to facilitate active targeting of PDAC. Common approaches involve covalent conjugation of peptides, aptamers, or antibodies to functionalized PEG termini (e.g., PLGA-PEG-NH2), which display ligands on the nanoparticle surface while retaining stealth characteristics and prolonged circulation [27]. In preclinical PDAC models, surface-decorated SPNs have employed ligands such as iRGD peptides for integrin targeting, anti-EGFR antibodies for receptor-overexpressing cells, and aptamers against EpCAM to enhance tumor specificity. More recently, advanced strategies including dual-ligand nanoparticles, pH-activatable targeting systems, and glycan-based ligands have been investigated to overcome PDAC’s spatial heterogeneity and mucin barrier [28,29].

Moreover, SPNs can be engineered with ligands that selectively bind to surface markers on CAFs, particularly fibroblast activation protein (FAP), which is abundantly expressed in activated CAFs within PDAC tumors but largely absent in normal fibroblasts [30–32]. For example, HPMA (N-(2-hydroxypropyl)methacrylamide) copolymer conjugates have been developed in which doxorubicin is linked to the polymer backbone via pH-sensitive hydrazone bonds and further functionalized with F19 antibody fragments. This design enables selective accumulation in FAP-expressing CAFs within the PDAC stroma [33–35]. Another approach employs polymersome self-assembled from PDMS-b-PMOXA polymer surface-modified with OncoFAP, which is a high-affinity small-molecule FAP inhibitor. A photosensitizer IRDye700DX is loaded in the polymersome. Following tumor accumulation, light activation triggers localized reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to CAF ablation and disruption of the fibrotic stroma [36–41]. FAP-2286, a cyclic peptide with high FAP affinity, has been conjugated to biodegradable polymeric carriers for theranostic applications in fibrotic and tumor-rich tissues, enabling both imaging and therapy [42]. These platforms demonstrate the versatility of polymer-drug conjugates in stroma-targeted delivery, enhancing drug penetration and therapeutic efficacy in dense tumors such as PDAC [43].

These include receptors expressed on tumor cells (e.g., EGFR, CD44, MSLN), stromal components (e.g., FAP, DDR1), and invasive or immune-evasive subpopulations (e.g., CXCR4, uPAR). Ligand-functionalized polymers enable diverse functionalities such as receptor-mediated endocytosis, tumor-specific uptake, stromal modulation, and enhanced penetration across extracellular matrix barriers.

Eradicating CSCs with targeted polymeric nanotherapies

Beyond targeting tumor cells and CAFs, SPNs were functionalized with ligands such as CD44, CD133, and EpCAM to target CSCs [44,45]. CSCs are highly specific and therapeutically resistant subpopulations within the heterogeneous PDAC tumor mass, which drive tumor initiation, metastasis, chemoresistance, and recurrence due to their self-renewal and differentiation capacities. Targeted SPNs facilitate selective delivery upon ligand-receptor interaction and enhance local drug concentration within the CSC niche while minimizing systemic toxicity. These targeted SPNs successfully delivered potent payloads, such as cytotoxic drugs or inhibitors of Notch/Wnt/Hedgehog pathways, directly to CSCs, thereby disrupting the root of tumor recurrence [46,47]. For example, HA, a natural CD44 ligand, has been widely used to decorate nanoparticles (e.g. MP@HA and CNO-HA), enabling selective drug delivery to CD44+ pancreatic CSCs and improving the efficacy of the loaded therapeutic agents (e.g. gemcitabine prodrug) [48–51].

Given the heterogeneity and plasticity of CSC populations, multi-ligand targeting strategies are gaining attention. Dual-functionalized nanoparticles, such as CD44/EpCAM-targeting SPNs, have demonstrated enhanced binding, internalization, and therapeutic efficacy in CSC-enriched PDAC spheroids compared to single-ligand counterparts [52,53]. Dual-targeting polymersomes leverage HA and phenylboronic acid (PBA) functionalization to achieve selective targeting of CD44 and sialic acid (SA) receptors overexpressed on PDAC cells, demonstrating significant efficacy in preclinical models [54]. This nanoplatform exemplifies a rational design to counteract PDAC chemoresistance through multi-ligand targeting and microenvironment-responsive drug release. The 80 % growth inhibition in CD44+ lines highlights its potential for clinical translation (Fig. 1).

Taking together, these active targeting approaches represent significant advances over passive delivery [14,55,56]. However, further optimization of ligand density, spatial orientation, and conjugation stability is essential to maximize receptor binding and facilitate effective cellular uptake and transport.

SPNs for stromal modulation and enhanced penetration

To overcome the dense stromal barrier in PDAC detailed previously, SPNs are being engineered to actively remodel the TME through two primary strategies: direct degradation of ECM components and reprogramming of the CAFs that produce them [57,58].

Enzymatic degradation of the extracellular matrix

A key strategy involves the targeted delivery or in situ activation of enzymes capable of degrading major ECM components. Smart polymeric systems are exceptionally well-suited for this purpose, offering protection to the enzymatic payload during systemic circulation and enabling triggered release within the TME [59]. For instance, SPNs were efficiently engineered to encapsulate ECM-degrading enzymes such as collagenase or hyaluronidase [60]. The use of PEGylated recombinant human hyaluronidase (PEGPH20) has shown that enzymatic depletion of HA can re-expand intratumor vasculature and improve tumor perfusion in preclinical models [61]. In another study, polydopamine nanoparticles co-loaded with hyaluronidase and resveratrol (PDA@Res@HAase) effectively degraded ECM components and improved drug penetration in 3D tumor spheroids [62] (Fig. 2). In addition, engineered polymeric carriers have been used to deliver live attenuated E. coli expressing both collagenase and hyaluronidase, resulting in reduced collagen and hyaluronan levels and improved chemotherapy efficacy in PDAC models [63].

Reprogramming CAF

In addition to enzymatic degradation of the existing matrix, a complementary strategy involves reprogramming the CAFs themselves. Rather than ablating these cells, which can paradoxically promote tumor aggressiveness, this approach aims to revert them to a quiescent or even tumor-inhibitory phenotype [30,64]. Polymeric nanoparticles have proven effective in delivering reprogramming agents such as all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) or vitamin D analogs, improving their stability, bioavailability, and retention within the TME [65,66]. These systems enable sustained, localized drug release, supporting durable CAF reprogramming and ECM normalization.

Various biodegradable polymeric systems such as PLGA, PCL, or chitosan-based nanoparticles have been used to remodel stroma by encapsulating anti-fibrotic agents like TGF-β inhibitors (e.g., galunisertib) or LOX inhibitors, which suppress collagen crosslinking and matrix stiffening [67]. Dual-delivery platforms combining stromal modulators (e.g. Hedgehog pathway inhibitors) with chemotherapeutics in a single nanoparticle construct (e.g., core-shell or multi-compartment micelles) have shown enhanced tumor penetration and antitumor efficacy in PDAC models [20,68–70]. For instance, co-loading cyclopamine and paclitaxel into micellar nanoparticles (M-CPA/PTX) has demonstrated increased microvessel density and reduced matrix stiffness in PDAC models, enhancing drug penetration without ablating the tumor-restraining collagenous framework [71]. This combinatorial approach directly addresses both stromal and cancer cell compartments, maximizing therapeutic efficacy [72].

By actively remodeling the fibrotic stroma, these polymer-mediated strategies convert the TME from a protective barrier into a more accessible landscape for therapy. This represents a critical step toward intelligent nanomedicine in PDAC [73].

Stimuli-responsive SPNs for targeted delivery and controlled release

Stimuli-responsive polymeric nanoparticles have emerged as a critically important strategy to overcome the physical and biological barriers in PDAC. While many solid tumors share common TME features, the PDAC TME is distinguished by its extreme desmoplasia, profound hypoxia, and uniquely dysregulated signaling. SPNs are engineered to leverage these specific pathological conditions as triggers. For instance, polymers designed to respond to the markedly acidic pH (driven by hypoperfusion and aberrant cancer cell metabolism) or to enzymes like matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cathepsins (highly overexpressed to facilitate stromal remodeling and invasion) can achieve precise control of therapeutics release. This approach is particularly necessary in PDAC to enhance nanoparticle accumulation beyond the scarce vasculature, improve penetration through the dense fibrotic stroma, and promote intracellular drug release specifically within tumor cells.

Size and charge-reversal polymeric systems for tumor specific uptake and penetration

The surface charge of SPNs plays a critical role in determining their biodistribution and cellular interactions, a factor of particular importance in PDAC given its dense, negatively charged ECM and stromal barrier. While neutral or slightly negative surfaces, typically achieved through PEGylation, are essential for prolonged circulation, cationic surfaces become advantageous for enhanced cellular uptake once the nanoparticles reach the pancreatic tumor site. This dichotomy is addressed through smart charge-reversal mechanisms where nanoparticles maintain a negative charge under physiological conditions (pH 7.4) but switch to a positive charge in the acidic TME (pH ~6.5–6.8) [74]. Such charge-transforming capabilities can also aid in penetrating the negatively charged HA and collagen networks of the PDAC stroma, further improving drug distribution within the tumor [21].

Common approaches utilize pH-sensitive moieties like 2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride (DMMA) or citraconic anhydride, which hydrolyze in acidic conditions to expose primary amines [75]. For example, DMMA-modified PLGA-PEI micelles demonstrate significant charge reversal at tumor pH, leading to substantially improved uptake in PDAC cells [76,77]. In addition to pH, the specific enzymatic profile of the PDAC TME can also be harnessed for charge conversion. A recent study reported a zwitterionic polymer-SN38 conjugate system that responds to FAP on CAFs and γ-glutamyltranspeptidase (GGT) on tumor endothelial and cancer cells [78]. Enzymatic cleavage of tailored linkers triggers a charge conversion from neutral to cationic, initiating mutual transcytosis across CAFs and cancer cells (Fig. 3). This unique mechanism enables deep tumor penetration and enhanced delivery of SN38 in PDAC models, highlighting the promise of enzyme-responsive charge-switchable nanocarriers for overcoming stromal barriers and enhanced tumor penetration.

In parallel to charge reversal strategies, size-transition approaches have been developed to facilitate deeper tumor penetration. These systems are designed to shrink upon encountering TME-specific triggers, allowing them to navigate through the dense PDAC stroma more effectively. For example, Zhao et al. developed a TGF-β1 inhibitor/paclitaxel nanosystem that undergoes size reduction upon TME entry and MMP-2-cleavable PEG-peptide-PCL micelles that transition from ~100 nm to 10–20 nm after stromal penetration [57]. Another compelling example of a size-transforming system designed for PDAC is the “iCluster” nanoparticle [67]. This smart polymeric clustered nanoparticle possesses an initial size of approximately 100 nm, which is favorable for prolonged blood circulation and extravasation through tumor vascular fenestrations. Once accumulated at the tumor site, the intrinsic extracellular acidity of the PDAC microenvironment triggers the iCluster to discharge its payload of much smaller platinum prodrug-conjugated poly(amidoamine) dendrimers, which are around 5 nm in diameter. This significant size reduction greatly facilitates deeper tumor penetration and enhanced cellular internalization of the therapeutics [79]. Other examples include enzyme-responsive PEG-peptide-PCL micelles that shrink from ~100 nm to ~10–20 nm upon MMP-2 cleavage, promoting diffusion across the stromal barrier [80,81].

The most advanced designs incorporate both size and charge switching capabilities in a single system. Song et al. developed a size and charge dual-switchable nanoparticle, DGD/L@GBI, which co-delivered gemcitabine and dasatinib [82] (Fig. 4). The polymeric nanoparticle was initially shielded by nucleic acid aptamers targeting the PDAC stromal microenvironment. Upon reaching the hypoxic TME, these aptamers were removed, exposing a positively charged core and achieving charge reversal. Concurrently, hypoxia-responsive linkers within the polymer structure triggered a particle size reduction. These dual transformations led to increased intratumoral accumulation, resulting in significant chemosensitization and enhanced infiltration of CD8+ T cells [82].

In addition, SPNs showed promising capabilities to undergo programmed morphological changes (e.g., micelle-to-fiber transitions) in response to TME cues. These shape-shifting capabilities allow dynamic adaptation to different tumor compartments [83]. Spherical forms are suitable for circulation and tumor accumulation, while transitioning to elongated structures triggered by specific biochemical or physical stimuli in the PDAC microenvironment can enhance stromal penetration [84].

These intelligent SPNs exemplify how advanced polymer chemistry can create nanoparticles that sequentially adapt to different physiological requirements, including maintaining stability during circulation, enabling penetration through dense stroma, and facilitating efficient cellular uptake [85]. This helps address the key pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic challenges in PDAC treatment.

Stimuli-responsive polymer systems for promoting drug release

Beyond physical transformations, polymeric systems are designed to release therapeutic payloads in response to biochemical triggers that are especially relevant in the PDAC TME. PDAC is distinguished from many other solid tumors by its dense, hyaluronan-rich stroma, high interstitial fluid pressure, and limited vascular perfusion, all of which restrict drug penetration. These barriers create regions of sustained acidity and hypoxia that can be exploited for controlled drug release. For example, Dereje et al. developed a NIR photosensitizer BrCY7 loaded PEGylated PLGA nanoparticle system, where PEG chains were tethered via acid-labile linkers. Upon exposure to the mildly acidic PDAC microenvironment, these linkers were cleaved, resulting in PEG shedding and surface charge exposure. This pH-triggered transformation enhanced cellular uptake, promoted BrCY7 release and enabled efficient ROS generation under NIR irradiation, even in chemoresistant PDAC cells [75]. PH-sensitive coiled-coil peptide-crosslinked hyaluronic acid nanogels (HA-cNGs) have been developed for CD44-targeted, intracellular protein delivery. These nanogels remain stable at physiological pH but disassemble under acidic conditions, enabling controlled protein release. In MCF-7 cells, HA-cNGs efficiently delivered cytochrome C and saporin, demonstrating enhanced cellular uptake, endosomal escape, and potent cytotoxicity (IC50 = 12.2 nM). This platform highlights the potential of pH-responsive nanogels for tumor-selective protein therapeutics [86,87]. Acid-sensitive micelles composed of PEG-hydrazone-stearic acid (PHC) have been developed for lysosome-triggered delivery of a gemcitabine prodrug (GemC18). Compared to acid-insensitive PEG-amide-stearic acid (PAC) micelles, PHC micelles enabled pH-responsive drug release and enhanced cytotoxicity in tumor cells. In vivo, GemC18-loaded PHC micelles showed superior antitumor efficacy over PAC micelles and free drug formulations, highlighting the advantage of hydrazone-linked micelles for intracellular drug activation and tumor-targeted delivery [88].

PDAC cells exhibit significantly elevated intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels compared to normal pancreatic cells, making it an ideal trigger for redox-responsive drug delivery. Nanocarriers incorporating disulfide bonds exploit this GSH-rich environment to achieve selective cytosolic drug release [17]. One example is Apt/CPP-CPTD nanoparticles, which is a multifunctional redox-responsive platform designed for enhanced delivery in PDAC (Fig. 5). These nanoparticles are composed of a dimeric camptothecin prodrug (CPTD) stabilized by amphiphilic PEG-pPhe and CPP-PEG-pPhe copolymers. Surface decoration with a tumor-targeting aptamer (GBI-10) ensures selective PDAC cell recognition, while a cell-penetrating peptide facilitates stromal penetration and intracellular delivery. Following tumor accumulation, elevated intracellular GSH triggers disulfide bond cleavage in CPTD, inducing a cascade of self-cyclization and elimination reactions that release active camptothecin. This design enables sequential targeting, deep stromal penetration, and on-site drug release within the fibrotic PDAC microenvironment [89].

In PDAC, KRAS mutations and the desmoplastic stroma contribute to persistently high ROS levels, which not only promote tumor progression but also render the tumor microenvironment particularly hostile to conventional therapies. This oxidative environment presents a valuable trigger for selective drug release. ROS-responsive polymeric nanoparticles are designed to remain stable in circulation but undergo structural disassembly or linker cleavage upon ROS exposure, enabling site-specific drug release while minimizing off-target toxicity. Common ROS-sensitive linkers include thioketals, boronic esters, and ferrocene moieties, which degrade or transform in response to hydrogen peroxide or superoxide radicals [90–92]. Ferrocene-based nanoparticles are particularly promising due to their redox sensitivity; however, their biomedical utility has been limited by poor aqueous stability. To overcome this, PEGylated ferrocene nanoparticles (PFNPs) were developed using copolymers containing methacrylic acid and PEG methacrylate at optimized ratios. PEGylation enhanced nanoparticle stability in physiological conditions while preserving ROS responsiveness. Upon ROS exposure, PFNPs disassembled within 8 h, releasing PTX and imaging agents. In vivo, PFNPs achieved ~15-fold higher tumor accumulation than free dye and significantly suppressed tumor growth, reducing tumor volume to ~18 % of the control group. These findings underscore the potential of ROS-triggered PFNPs as a stable and biocompatible platform for tumor-selective drug and imaging delivery [90,93].

Enzyme-responsive SPNs represent another strategy that can be tailored to PDAC-specific biology. For instance, innovative co-polypeptide nanoparticles composed of poly(acetyl L-lysine) and poly (ethylene glycol) blocks have been designed to be intrinsically responsive to histone deacetylase (HDAC) enzymes, which are often overexpressed in PDAC, especially in CSCs [94,95]. These nanoparticles disassemble upon enzymatic deacetylation of lysine residues by HDACs, leading to controlled drug release. When loaded with Napabucasin (a STAT3 inhibitor with relevance to CSC pathways), these HDAC-responsive nanoparticles demonstrated significant cytotoxic effects against pancreatic cancer cell lines (MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1) and, notably, pancreatic CSCs, with HDAC8 triggering over 90 % drug release within 3 h. This approach exemplifies a highly “smart” system where the structural integrity of the nanoparticle is directly modulated by an epigenetic enzyme prevalent in the target cancer cells [94].

Hypoxia is another hallmark of PDAC, arising from its dense stroma, abnormal vasculature, and rapid proliferation, which together produce one of the most hypoxic microenvironments among solid tumors. Polyprodrug micelles incorporating hypoxia-sensitive linkers (e.g., azobenzene, 4,4′-diazobenzene, or 2-nitroimidazole) undergo reductive cleavage under low oxygen conditions, releasing the payloads selectively in hypoxic tumor regions [77,96–98]. These designs can be used for hypoxia-triggered release of diagnostic and therapeutic agents in PDAC models, achieving improved diagnostic sensitivity and therapeutic outcomes. For example, a multifunctional nanoplatform was engineered by encapsulating a hypoxia-responsive molecular probe and a vascular disrupting agent within an acid-degradable metal-organic framework (MOF). In the PDAC hypoxic TME, the molecular probe selectively activated second near-infrared (NIR-II) fluorescence and photoacoustic (PA) imaging signals, enabling precise noninvasive tumor localization. Simultaneously, the system induced synergistic photothermal and photodynamic effects, remodeling the TME and eliciting immunogenic cell death (ICD), which in turn robustly activated the cGAS-STING pathway to sustain anti-tumor immune responses [99] (Fig. 6).

In addition to endogenous triggers, polymeric systems can also be engineered to respond to external stimuli such as ultrasound. A notable example is the perfluorocarbon (PFC)-alginate hydrogel system, which co-delivers gemcitabine (GEM) and indocyanine green (ICG) for ultrasound-triggered, multimodal therapy (Fig. 7). Upon low-intensity ultrasound exposure, the PFC undergoes a liquid-to-gas transition, releasing oxygen to alleviate tumor hypoxia, while ICG generates ROS for sonodynamic therapy (SDT). Meanwhile, GEM is gradually released from the alginate matrix, enabling a synergistic chemo-sonodynamic effect. This system demonstrated effective tumor suppression in patient-derived PDAC organoid xenograft models, highlighting its potential for overcoming hypoxia-associated drug resistance through precise, in situ activation [100]. In addition, dual or multiple stimuli-responsive polymeric micelles offer tumor-specific release with enhanced spatiotemporal precision by integrating responses to multiple pathological cues, such as acidic pH and GSH. The combined triggers enable staged or synergistic release kinetics, which can enhance intracellular drug availability and minimize systemic toxicity. A representative example is the P-RUB system, a dual pH/redox-sensitive micelle self-assembled from rubone-conjugated polymers for the delivery of docetaxel (DTX) to pancreatic tumors [101,102]. Upon accumulation in the TME, the acidic pH induces micelle expansion, while elevated intracellular GSH levels trigger disulfide bond cleavage, releasing the active drug. This dual-trigger mechanism enables precise prodrug activation and effective targeting of both CSCs and bulk tumor cells. To overcome the dense extracellular matrix and heterogeneous barriers in pancreatic tumors, a multiple stimuli-responsive nanoassembly (PLAF@P/T-PD) with a unique core-satellite heterostructure was developed. This system integrates internal pH/redox and external ultrasound responsiveness [103](Fig. 8). The polymersome core, loaded with Taxol and perfluoropentane, undergoes ROS- and pH-triggered size enlargement and charge conversion, facilitating ultrasound-enhanced tumor penetration and controlled Taxol delivery. Concurrently, satellite polyplexes carrying immunostimulatory dsDNA are released and preferentially taken up by dendritic cells, promoting immune activation. In an orthotopic PDAC mouse model, this platform exhibited potent inhibition of both primary and metastatic tumors, demonstrating its promise for overcoming stromal barriers and achieving synergistic chemo-immunotherapy in pancreatic cancer [71,103]. Luo et al. developed a sophisticated microenvironment-responsive micellar system for on-demand, deep tumor penetration and controlled drug release. The design featured PEG-poly(amino acid) micelles functionalized with a fibrin-targeting peptide for stromal accumulation and modified with hypoxia-reducible nitroimidazole, which becomes protonated under acidic conditions to increase surface charge and enhance penetration. Paclitaxel was linked via a disulfide bond, enabling GSH-triggered intracellular release. This approach not only improved drug distribution but also alleviated hypoxia and reduced GSH levels, effectively remodeling the immunosuppressive TME [104]. SPNs for reprogramming the PDAC tumor immune microenvironment and metabolism

Co-delivery of immunomodulators via synthetic SPNs to remodel the immunosuppressive TME

The profoundly immunosuppressive TME of PDAC is a major barrier to effective therapy and contributes significantly to its poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy [106]. SPNs are emerging as versatile tools to reprogram this hostile immune landscape by enabling targeted delivery of immunomodulatory agents, minimizing systemic toxicity, and enhancing anti-tumor immune responses [107].

A key advantage of SPNs lies in their capacity to co-deliver multiple immunomodulatory agents within a single nanoparticle system. Through precise control over polymer composition and architecture, SPNs are flexible to be engineered as multi-compartment structures (e.g., core–shell micelles, polymer–drug or polymer–biologic conjugates) to accommodate cargoes with diverse physicochemical properties. This flexibility enables the synergistic delivery of agents such as STING (Stimulator of Interferon Genes) agonists, Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) including anti-PD-1/PDL1 antibodies or siRNAs [67,108]. These polymeric systems ensure that such potent immune activators are delivered preferentially to the tumor cells or to antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby amplifying local immune activation while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target toxicities [70,109].

For example, Chen et al. developed a smart nanoplatform (nChap) by co-assembling PEG-b-PCL and FHK peptide–modified PEG-b-PCL, together with pH-responsive PCL-b-PAE, to enable targeted co-delivery of CXCL9 and calcipotriol (Cal) for synergistic modulation of chemokine gradients and CAF activity in PDAC [105]. In a Panc02 murine model, Cal@nChap-CXCL9 achieved potent systemic antitumor immunity and significantly amplified chemo-immunotherapy efficacy against advanced tumors (Fig. 9). To overcome the immunosuppressive role of TAMs in the PDAC microenvironment, a mannose-modified, pH-responsive polymeric nanoplatform (M-PNP@R@C) was developed to co-deliver resiquimod (TLR agonist) and cGAMP. This dual-delivery system effectively reprogrammed TAMs from an anti-inflammatory M2 to a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, enhancing antitumor immunity. Notably, cGAMP-mediated STING activation within TAMs downregulated SIRPα expression by modulating fatty acid oxidation, thereby disrupting the CD47–SIRPα “don’t eat me” axis. Combination with anti-CD47 antibodies produced a synergistic blockade, inhibiting metastasis and prolonging survival, especially when further combined with anti-PD-L1 for triple checkpoint blockade [108]. To tackle metastatic PDAC, polydopamine-coated polymeric nanoparticles co-loaded with gemcitabine and the IDO inhibitor NLG919 were developed for combined immunochemo-photothermal therapy. Thicker polydopamine coatings improved photothermal efficiency, biodistribution, and tumor suppression, with laser irradiation further enhancing primary tumor control. In advanced metastasis models, this strategy achieved a notable abscopal effect, inhibiting both primary and distant tumors by amplifying innate and adaptive immunity [110].

Some polymeric nanocarriers themselves exhibit intrinsic immunomodulatory effects when conjugated with therapeutic agents and can simultaneously co-deliver additional immune-active compounds to enable combination immunotherapy. For example, a gemcitabine-conjugated polymer was shown to directly activate the STING pathway, enhancing innate immune signaling within the TME. Co-delivery of a CCR2 antagonist with this platform further reprogrammed immune cell populations by reducing MDSCs and TAMs, thereby amplifying antitumor immunity and improving overall therapeutic efficacy [111].

SPNs for modulating PDAC metabolism

Just as SPNs can reprogram the immune landscape, they can also be engineered to disrupt the aberrant metabolic pathways that fuel tumor growth and create an immunosuppressive niche, representing a complementary and synergistic therapeutic approach. PDAC cells exhibit distinct metabolic reprogramming, frequently relying on pathways like aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) and enhanced glutaminolysis to fuel their rapid proliferation and survive under nutrient-deprived conditions. Inhibiting this metabolic plasticity is a promising therapeutic strategy [112]. Polymeric nanoparticles serve as ideal nanocarriers to deliver inhibitors targeting these aberrant pathways. For instance, inhibitors of glycolysis, such as 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) or 3-bromopyruvate (3-BP), or inhibitors of glutamine metabolism like CB-839 or DON (6-Diazo-5-oxo-L-norleucine), can be encapsulated within smart polymeric matrices [113]. This encapsulation can improve the solubility, stability, and pharmacokinetic profile of these drugs [114]. Furthermore, by co-delivering these metabolic modulators with conventional chemotherapeutics or immunotherapies using multifunctional polymeric NPs, synergistic anti-tumor effects can be achieved by simultaneously targeting multiple vulnerabilities of the cancer cells [115,116].

For instance, Xiao et al. constructed a cationic triblock polymer system that co-delivered the PI3K inhibitor wortmannin and siRNA targeting CXCL1, with a mesothelin antibody facilitating tumor-specific targeting [117]. By simultaneously blocking PI3K/AKT-driven GLUT1 upregulation and remodeling the stroma through CXCL1 silencing, this nanoplatform reduced glucose uptake in both tumor and tumor-associated macrophages, potentially freeing glucose to fuel effector immune cells. Sun et al. developed a supramolecular prodrug nanoplatform that self-assembled via host–guest interactions between cyclodextrin-grafted hyaluronic acid (HA-CD) and adamantane-linked PPa-JQ1 heterodimers, enabling CD44-targeted, GSH-responsive delivery in PDAC [118] (Fig. 10). PPa-mediated photodynamic therapy increased tumor immunogenicity and T-cell infiltration, while JQ1 suppressed c-Myc–driven glycolysis and PD-L1–mediated immune escape, thereby disrupting the metabolic reprogramming that sustains immunosuppression. This dual approach effectively remodeled the tumor metabolic microenvironment, reinforced T-cell infiltration, and led to pronounced tumor regression.

Beyond glycolysis, polymeric systems are actively being designed to disrupt mitochondrial metabolism and amino acid utilization in PDAC. For example, TGF-β receptor inhibitors and mitochondrial PDH inhibitors (such as CPI-613) have been co-encapsulated in MMP2-cleavable polymeric nanoparticles, selectively releasing their payloads within the TME to remodel the desmoplastic stroma and curtail mitochondrial respiration [119]. Elgogary et al. developed PLGA nanoparticles loaded with the GLS1 inhibitor BPTES to disrupt glutamine metabolism in PDAC, modestly slowing tumor growth [120]. However, tumor cells compensated by upregulating glycolysis. Co-administration of metformin, a mitochondrial inhibitor, with BPTES nanoparticles achieved more pronounced growth arrest by concurrently targeting glucose and glutamine pathways. These findings illustrate how polymer-based systems can modulate metabolic reprogramming in PDAC.

SPNs for precision genetic pathway intervention

Advanced polymeric platforms for gene editing and RNA interference

PDAC is driven by genetic mutations (most notably KRAS) and dysregulated signaling pathways that support cell proliferation, stemness, and therapeutic resistance. These features make PDAC an attractive target for gene-silencing strategies such as RNA interference (RNAi) and gene editing via CRISPR/Cas9. However, delivering nucleic acid-based therapies remains challenging due to issues like degradation in circulation, limited cellular uptake, and off-target effects. SPNs offer solutions by protecting payloads, enabling targeted delivery, and facilitating controlled intracellular release [121].

Cationic polymers have been extensively employed to form “polyplexes” through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged nucleic acids like small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or microRNAs (miRNAs). These systems condense and shield nucleic acids from nuclease degradation and aid in cellular uptake. Commonly used materials include chitosan, polyethylenimine (PEI) and its derivatives (known for high transfection efficiency via the “proton sponge” effect facilitating endosomal escape), and synthetic biodegradable poly (β-amino esters) [122]. For example, Cao et al. developed PLGA-PEI micelles modified with 2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride (DMMA) for siRNA delivery [123]. The system remained negatively charged under physiological conditions but converted to a positive surface charge in the acidic tumor environment, which facilitated enhanced cellular uptake of siRNAs [124]. Chen’s group developed pH-triggered PEGylated nanoparticles where siRNA was complexed by PEI and PLG, with a PEG corona that could detach at tumor sites to facilitate cellular uptake [125]. These SPNs deliver siRNAs against oncogenes like mutant KRAS or CSC maintenance genes, with targeting ligands or stimuli-responsive elements further improving precision [126,127].

A triblock cationic polymer, PDMAEMA-co-PNIPAM-co-PMPC (PDNM), was developed as a gene carrier for siKRAS delivery to target mutant KRAS in pancreatic cancer. The PDNM/siKRAS nanoparticles achieved efficient cellular uptake with low toxicity, induced apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and reduced proliferation and migration in vitro, while also suppressing tumor growth in vivo and in patient-derived organoids, highlighting the potential of PDNM for KRAS-targeted gene therapy in PDAC [9]. Recent studies identified LIMS1 as crucial for PDAC survival under oxygen-glucose deprivation by enhancing GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake and promoting HIF1α translation through AKT/mTOR signaling, creating a positive feedback loop that sustains metabolic adaptation. Notably, delivery of anti-LIMS1 siRNAs via a cationic polymeric jetPEI nanocarrier disrupted this network, increasing cell death and suppressing tumor growth, highlighting the promise of polymer-based platforms for targeting metabolic vulnerabilities alongside gene silencing in PDAC [128].

For larger genetic payloads like mRNA or CRISPR/Cas9, lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles (LPNs) integrate lipid biocompatibility and membrane fusion with polymer stability and controlled release [129], offering promise for more complex genetic interventions in PDAC. For example, a lipid-PLGA hybrid NPs was developed to deliver mRNA that locally encode anti-PD-1 antibodies at the tumor site. This approach synergized with a neoantigen-based mRNA vaccine to boost anti-tumor immunity, protect tumor-infiltrating T cells from exhaustion, and minimize systemic toxicity typically associated with checkpoint blockade [130].

SPNs for co-delivery of siRNA and small molecular drug

Polymeric nanocarriers can also be engineered for the co-delivery of siRNA and small-molecule drugs to tackle PDAC, enabling simultaneous gene silencing and chemotherapy within a single platform. This strategy helps overcome resistance mechanisms and remodels the TME to enhance overall therapeutic efficacy. For instance, Huang et al. developed a POEM nanocarrier composed of lipid-derivatized polylysine (PPO) and GEM-conjugated polylysine (PPOGEM), enabling efficient co-delivery of gemcitabine and SERPINB9 siRNA (siSPB9). This design leveraged both charge and GEM-mediated interactions for stable siRNA encapsulation at low N/P ratios (defined as the molar ratio of nitrogen atoms in the cationic polymer to phosphate groups in the siRNA), allowing effective intravenous administration (Fig. 11). Co-delivery of GEM and siSPB9 not only enhanced tumor cell chemosensitivity but also favorably remodeled the immune microenvironment in PDAC models, underscoring the potential of polymeric systems for synergistic drug-gene therapy [131]. Zhao et al. developed a lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticle (LENP) composed of PEGylated phospholipids and cationic ε-polylysine to co-deliver gemcitabine and HIF1α siRNA for treating PDAC. This design protected siRNA from serum degradation and controlled GEM release, enabling effective HIF1α silencing, enhanced GEM cytotoxicity, and significant tumor suppression in Panc-1 xenograft models, while also reducing siRNA-triggered innate immune activation [132]. Tang et al. developed a membrane-camouflaged, dual stimuli-responsive polymeric delivery system for the co-delivery of GEM, erlotinib (Er), and siRNA to tackle pancreatic cancer. This “all-in-one” nanoplatform exhibited pH- and GSH-sensitive release, effectively blocked GEM-Er antagonism, and simultaneously reprogrammed the profibrotic TME via siRNA-mediated modulation. The system significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation and migration in vitro, and upon systemic administration, showed potent suppression of orthotopic and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumors while prolonging survival in vivo [133].

Due to the complicated TME, achieving multifunctionality in SPNs is particularly important for PDAC. An effective therapeutic strategy requires not only targeting cellular populations (e.g., tumor cells, CAFs, and CSCs), but also engineering the microenvironment (by remodeling the dense stroma [134], enabling stimuli-responsive drug release [135], and reprogramming immune and metabolic niches [136]. Furthermore, the efficient co-delivery of different therapeutic agents (e.g. RNA and small molecular drug) is essential. To integrate these diverse functions, polymer blending and the synthesis of single multifunctional copolymers are two major formulation strategies to develop multifunctional SPNs, as demonstrated in the examples above.

The polymer blending strategy involves the physical co-assembly of multiple distinct polymers, each contributing a complementary function, such as pH-responsiveness, ROS-cleavability, stealth properties, or targeting ligands, into a single nanoparticle [137]. This approach offers significant advantages in flexibility and simplicity, as the ratios of each polymer can be readily tuned to optimize performance. Moreover, its modular nature facilitates the efficient co-loading of various therapeutic payloads for combination therapy. However, this method often faces challenges in achieving structural uniformity, since phase separation or uneven distribution of functional groups may occur. Such heterogeneity can compromise stability and reproducibility, presenting barriers for scale-up and clinical translation.

In contrast, the single multifunctional copolymer strategy employs chemical synthesis to integrate all desired functionalities directly into a single polymer backbone. This design offers greater structural precision, as functional groups are uniformly distributed within the polymer matrix, leading to predictable drug release and degradation behaviors [119]. In addition, these systems typically demonstrate higher stability under physiological conditions and improved reproducibility across production batches, which are critical for clinical translation [138]. Nevertheless, the synthesis of multifunctional copolymers is often complex, time-consuming, and less flexible for further modifications.

For PDAC applications, the choice between these strategies is largely context-dependent. Polymer blending may be preferred when rapid development, modularity, and combinatorial delivery are paramount, such as for the co-encapsulation of a stroma-depleting agent and a chemotherapeutic. Conversely, single multifunctional copolymers may be favored when long-term stability, reproducibility, and regulatory compliance are prioritized, making them more attractive for translation toward clinical use. Thus, both approaches offer distinct paths toward overcoming the barriers presented by the PDAC TME, and their selection should be guided by the specific therapeutic objectives.

Conclusion and perspective

SPNs represent a versatile and modular platform with the potential to fundamentally reshape the treatment of PDAC, a malignancy characterized by dense desmoplasia, hypovascularity, and profound therapeutic resistance. By integrating TME-responsive features such as pH-, enzyme-, redox-, or hypoxia-triggered release, alongside active targeting ligands and tunable degradation kinetics, engineered polymeric systems are designed to address multiple biological barriers that limit conventional therapies. Beyond enhancing drug delivery and tumor penetration, SPNs have been engineered to modulate the TME, reprogram metabolic pathways, and serve as carriers for gene therapy or combined gene/drug interventions.

Despite these advances, it is important to recognize that most SPNs remain at the preclinical stage. Polymeric NPs that have progressed into clinical evaluation in PDAC, including paclitaxel-loaded micelles (Genexol-PM®) [139] and cisplatin-loaded micelles (NC-6004) [140], primarily leverage improved solubility, passive tumor accumulation, and sustained drug release rather than dynamic TME-responsiveness. This highlights a critical gap between the sophisticated design of SPNs in the laboratory and their actual clinical translation, where simplicity and scalability often outweigh mechanistic complexity. Therefore, future efforts should focus on bridging this gap by validating the therapeutic advantages of stimuli-responsive SPNs in rigorous preclinical PDAC models, optimizing formulations for large-scale production, and conducting well-designed early-phase trials to determine whether these advanced systems can deliver tangible benefits over conventional nanomedicines.

Chronotherapy, which leverages endogenous ~24-h circadian rhythms governing key cellular processes such as proliferation, metabolism, and DNA repair, has emerged as a promising strategy to optimize the therapeutic index of anticancer agents [141]. In PDAC, these rhythms can influence both tumor cell susceptibility to treatment and host tissue sensitivity, providing a rationale for time-aligned drug delivery. SPNs offer a versatile platform to incorporate chronotherapeutic principles into drug delivery systems. By tuning the degradation rate, permeability, or stimuli-responsiveness of the polymer matrix, SPNs provide a unique platform to enable chronotherapeutic strategies, thereby achieving accurate temporal control over drug release [142].

More advanced SPNs designs integrate temporal regulation with tumor-responsive features. For instance, multilayered SPNs offer a platform to be fabricated with an outer shell that undergoes time-dependent degradation to expose an inner compartment that responds to local tumor cues such as low pH, elevated enzymatic activity, or hypoxia. This sequential release strategy ensures that therapeutic payloads are delivered only when both the timing and microenvironmental conditions are optimal [143]. Emerging approaches also explore embedding “chronosensors” within SPNs, capable of detecting circadian fluctuations in physiological signals such as hormones or metabolic intermediates. These could act as gatekeepers, enabling real-time, endogenous-triggered drug release, pointing to exciting directions for the development of highly personalized, adaptive SPNs [144,145]. Although largely conceptual, preclinical studies support the promise of this approach. For instance, in lung cancer models, paclitaxel-loaded polymeric nanoparticles administered during circadian windows of tumor susceptibility demonstrated improved tumor suppression and reduced systemic toxicity compared to untimed delivery, suggesting similar strategies might be leveraged for PDAC [146].

In addition, integrating artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning into SPN design could markedly accelerate progress. By harnessing large datasets of polymer structures, formulation parameters, and biological outcomes, AI can facilitate de novo design of polymers with optimized responsiveness, drug loading, and tumor selectivity, moving from empirical trial-and-error toward predictive, precision engineering, closely aligned with the broader objectives of personalized oncology.

In summary, through advances in polymer design, temporal and spatial control, computational innovation and the gradual clinical evaluations, SPNs offer significant promise not only for improving outcomes in PDAC but also for setting new benchmarks in individualized cancer therapy.
