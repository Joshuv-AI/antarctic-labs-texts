Metabolomic analyses of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, muscle cramps, and TJ-68 treatment
Authors: Hiroshi Mitsumoto, Ken Cheung, Takashi Matsumoto, Ian Lanza, Björn Oskarsson, Stephen Johnson, Xuan-Mai T. Petterson, Grace E. Jang, Howard F. Andrews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMost patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a fatal motor neuron disease, experience painful muscle cramps. Our recent pilot trial of the Japanese Kampo medicine TJ-68 suggested its efficacy in improving muscle cramps in patients with ALS.ObjectivesThis study analyzed plasma metabolomic changes to identify the underlying mechanisms of muscle cramps in ALS and the effects of TJ-68.MethodsPlasma was obtained from 11 participants with ALS in the repeated crossover trial at five time points (baseline, two placebo phases, and two TJ-68 phases). Metabolites were analyzed using mass spectrometry. Linear mixed-effects models were applied to identify metabolite changes associated with muscle cramps, determine the effects of TJ-68 on metabolites, and predict which participants would respond to TJ-68.ResultsHigher glutamine/glutamate, arginine, and leucine levels were associated with more severe muscle cramps. TJ-68 treatment increased tryptophan and aconitate levels but reduced serotonin and acetylcarnitine levels. Long-chain acylcarnitine levels were correlated with muscle cramp severity, and their levels tended to decrease with treatment. Uric acid, β-aminoisobutyric acid, α-aminoadipic acid, and acetylcholine emerged as predictors of the efficacy of TJ-68.ConclusionThis study identified the metabolite profile of muscle cramps in ALS and the changes in metabolite levels after TJ-68 treatment. Several baseline metabolites were associated with the prediction of the response to muscle cramps following TJ-68 treatment. Uric acid might be particularly useful because of its easy measurement in standard assays. Our study affirms the value of metabolomic technology for future pharmacotherapy and studies in ALS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-026-02499-1.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal motor neuron disease characterized by rapidly progressive paralysis of skeletal muscles. Most patients die within 2.5–4 years after symptom onset because of respiratory failure (Engelberg-Cook et al., 2024; Mitsumoto et al., 1998). Excluding a small proportion of genetic ALS, the cause of ALS is not well understood (Feldman et al. 2022; Hardiman et al. 2017; Oskarsson et al. 2018a). To date, only three disease-modifying medications have been approved for ALS treatment: riluzole (Bensimon et al., 1994; Lacomblez et al., 1996), edaravone (Writing, & Edaravone, 2017), and tofersen (specifically approved for SOD1-associated ALS) (Miller et al., 2022). Although intense research is ongoing to identify disease-modifying treatments for ALS (Paganoni et al., 2022), truly effective medications remain limited. Consequently, clinical trials are important for identifying effective symptomatic therapies that maintain or improve quality of life in patients with ALS (Dorst et al., 2018; Mitsumoto et al., 2014). Successful examples include salivary gland Botox injections (Jackson et al., 2009) and radiation (Kasarskis et al., 2011) to reduce sialorrhea, dextromethorphan–quinidine treatment for pseudobulbar affect (Pioro et al. 2010), and modafinil for fatigue (Rabkin et al., 2009).

Muscle cramps are very common in people with ALS, affecting 57%–95% of patients (Caress et al., 2016; Nicholson et al., 2018; Raymond et al., 2019). In some patients, muscle cramps represent the presenting symptom (Gubbay et al., 1985), preceding muscle weakness (Stephens et al., 2017). Muscle cramps can be associated with severe pain, which affects overall daily activities and quality of life (Baldinger et al., 2012; Weber et al., 2010) and leads to increased muscle damage in ALS measured as CK levels (Gibson et al., 2015). Despite the high prevalence of muscle cramps in ALS, few medications are available to treat them (Bradley et al., 2004; Chandrashekhar et al., 2022). Recent clinical trials of mexiletine for symptomatic treatment demonstrated a significant reduction in muscle cramps in patients with ALS (Oskarsson et al. 2018b; Weiss et al. 2016). However, mexiletine has an FDA black box warning because of safety concerns related to fatal cardiac arrhythmia. There are no well-established safe, effective, and well-tolerated medications for treating muscle cramps in people with ALS (Swash et al., 2019). TJ-68 (generic name: Shakuyakukanzoto) is a Kampo medicine (Japanese herbal medicine) that is widely prescribed in Japan (Okumi & Koyama, 2014; Ota et al., 2020) to treat muscle cramps and pain associated with a variety of conditions (Hinoshita et al., 2003; Hyodo et al., 2006; Kumada et al., 1999; Miura, 1999; Takao et al., 2015). Previously, we evaluated the safety and efficacy of TJ-68 using a novel repeated crossover N-of-1 trial design (Mitsumoto et al., 2023). The results indicated that TJ-68 was safe, and suggested a possible benefit for improving muscle cramps in patients with ALS was noted, although statistical significance was not achieved for the primary outcome (cramps affecting overall daily activity) (Mitsumoto et al., 2025). In this study, we investigated metabolomic changes to understand the underlying mechanisms of muscle cramps in ALS and the effects of TJ-68 treatment. In addition, we aimed to identify specific metabolites that can predict the response to TJ-68.

Methods

Clinical trial design and ethical approval

The original TJ-68 clinical trial was performed at three sites as a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, multi-period crossover (N-of-1) trial with a 1:1 study allocation (Mitsumoto et al., 2025). The study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04998305) and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Columbia University Irving Medical Center and the Mayo Clinic sites. The details of the clinical trial were fully described elsewhere (Mitsumoto et al., 2023, 2025). The present study, involving plasma metabolomic analyses, was conducted within the original TJ-68 prospective controlled clinical trial. Blood samples were collected in EDTA-containing tubes at baseline and at the end of each treatment period for both the placebo and TJ-68 active drug phases (Fig. 1). Plasma samples were obtained via centrifugation of the blood samples and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Metabolomic analysis

All metabolites quantified in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Amino acids (AAs) and related metabolites, neuromodulators, carnitine, acylcarnitines (Acyl-CNs), choline-related metabolites, muscle-related metabolites (creatine and creatinine), and uric acid (UA) were quantified using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates were analyzed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS).

AAs, neuromodulators, and Acyl-CNs were analyzed using an isotope dilution approach on a Quantiva triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to an Acquity Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography system (Waters Co., Milford, MA, USA). Three separate plasma aliquots and methods were used. Both assays used selected reaction monitoring (SRM) under positive electrospray ionization conditions.

For AA and related metabolite analysis, a 20 µL plasma aliquot was spiked with an internal standard mixture containing isotopically labeled AAs. Proteins were precipitated by adding 300 µL of chilled methanol: acetonitrile (1:1). The resulting supernatant was dried and immediately derivatized with 6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate (AccQ.Fluor; Waters Co.). Chromatographic separation was conducted on an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters Co.) before MS detection.

For the analysis of neuromodulators, a 50 µL plasma aliquot was spiked with an internal standard mixture containing isotopically labeled neuromodulators. The sample was processed and analyzed under the same conditions as described above for the AA method.

For analysis of Acyl-CNs, a 25 µL plasma aliquot was spiked with an internal standard mixture of isotopically labeled Acyl-CNs. The samples were extracted using 450 µL cold methanol: dichloromethane (1:1) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, dried, and reconstituted in running buffer. Chromatographic separation was conducted on an Acquity UPLC BEH C8 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters Co.) before MS detection.

Trimethylamine N-oxide and its related metabolites were measured using an isotope dilution technique on a 6500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled to a TX2 LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A 10 µL plasma aliquot was used for the analysis. Plasma samples were spiked with a D9-labeled internal standard mixture, followed by protein precipitation with cold methanol. The resulting supernatant was dried and reconstituted in running buffer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Alltima HP HILIC column (150 × 2.1 mm, 5 μm; Hichrom Ltd., Reading, UK) before MS analysis under positive electrospray ionization conditions. Data acquisition was performed using SRM.

UA, creatine, and creatinine concentrations were quantified using an isotope dilution approach on an Agilent 6460 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled to a 1290 Infinity II quaternary pump (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). A 20 µL plasma aliquot was spiked with an internal standard mixture containing three ¹³C-labeled compounds. The proteins were precipitated by adding 500 µL of ice-cold methanol: acetonitrile (1:1). The resulting supernatant was dried and reconstituted in 100 µL of buffer before LC–MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved using ion-pairing reverse-phase conditions on a Zorbax Extend C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm; Agilent). Detection was performed using negative electrospray ionization with dynamic multiple reaction monitoring.

TCA cycle metabolites were measured using GC–MS under electron impact ionization in positive mode with single ion monitoring, on an Agilent 5977B mass selective detector coupled to an 8890 gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies). A 50 µL plasma aliquot was extracted with 500 µL of chilled methanol: acetonitrile (1:1) following the addition of 20 µL of an internal standard mixture containing uniformly ¹³C-labeled analytes. The supernatant was dried in a SpeedVac and derivatized with ethoxyamine (oximation), followed by silylation using MtBSTFA + 1% tBDMCS (N-methyl-N-(tert-butyldimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide + 1% tert-butyldimethylchlorosilane). The derivatized analytes were separated on a DB-5ms GC column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies) and analyzed with the mass selective detector.

All metabolomic analyses were performed at the Mayo Clinic Metabolomics Core Facility employing stable isotope dilution techniques, as previously described (Dutta et al., 2016; Koeth et al., 2013; Lanza et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2019). Quantification was done using calibration curves generated from authentic standards for each metabolite, which were processed identically to the samples. Quality control samples were analyzed concurrently with study samples to ensure data accuracy and integrity.

Statistical analyses

As muscle cramps can be defined in different ways, we considered three cramp severity metrics from the muscle cramp scale (MCS) (Mitsumoto et al., 2019): total MCS score (expressing overall severity of muscle cramps); MCS question 2 (MCS 2), covering cramp frequency (Oskarsson et al. 2018b); and MCS question 5 (MCS 5), covering cramps affecting overall daily activity, which was used as the primary outcome in our TJ-68 clinical trial (Mitsumoto et al., 2025). All MCS metrics were treated as numerical variables and evaluated longitudinally at each time point for all participants. Overall, we assessed the association of variables using the linear mixed-effects model framework to account for within-subject correlation. The prognostic value of metabolites for cramp severity was examined using changes in metabolite levels as dependent variables and MCS changes as independent variables, with adjustments for fixed TJ-68 and period effects and random subject effects. The effect of MCS changes was tested using the Wald t-test. The composite levels of long-chain (LC)-Acyl-CNs were calculated using principal component loadings and factor analysis: three factors were fitted in the factor analysis, and the scores of the first factor were used as dependent variables in the linear mixed-effects models. To assess the predictive value of metabolites for TJ-68 responsiveness, the interaction effects of TJ-68 and baseline metabolite levels were included in the linear mixed-effects models. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was denoted by p < 0.05.

As MCS 2 increased, glutamate levels increased (p = 0.045), whereas trimethylamine N-oxide levels decreased (p = 0.049). Trimethylamine N-oxide levels decreased as MCS 5 increased from baseline (p = 0.040). The other metabolites listed in Table 2 increased with worsening MCS 5.

Focused analyses of the effects of LC-Acyl-CNs on muscle cramp severity and TJ-68 treatment responsiveness

The levels of several Acyl-CNs were associated with muscle cramp severity (Table 2) and showed a suggestive association with TJ-68 treatment (Supplementary Table S2). This prompted additional analyses focusing on LC-Acyl-CNs, as they are crucial for long-chain fatty acid utilization following binding to carnitine (Longo et al., 2016). LC-Acyl-CNs had a significant association with total MCS in the first factor in factor analysis (p = 0.044) and a marginal association with MCS in the first principal component (Table 4). LC-Acyl-CNs were positively correlated with muscle cramp severity, whereas an inverse association was noted with the response to TJ-68 treatment.

Predictive analyses of metabolites for TJ-68 treatment benefits

Lower β-aminoisobutyric acid (BAIBA; p = 0.0004) and lower UA levels (p = 0.0001) were significantly associated with better TJ-68 efficacy in terms of reducing total MCS (Table 5). Meanwhile, lower alpha-aminoadipic acid (AAAA; p = 0.011), alpha-amino-N-butyric acid (AANBA; p = 0.0059), acetylcholine (p = 0.0076), and choline (p = 0.049) levels were predictive of a treatment effect on muscle cramps. Increased hydroxyproline levels were associated with a better treatment effect on muscle cramps (p = 0.044). When the predictive analyses were performed using MCS 2, we observed similar findings to those seen for total MCS, excluding glutamine (p = 0.044) and alanine (p = 0.047). Based on MCS 5 analyses, reductions in UA (p = 0.0023), alanine (p = 0.0028), proline (p = 0.0085), AAAA (p = 0.011), and AANBA (p = 0.013) levels were significantly associated with improvement in muscle cramps upon TJ-68 treatment. Likewise, elevated glutamine levels predicted a better response to TJ-68 treatment (p = 0.016).

Discussion

Our clinical trial of TJ-68 provided a unique opportunity to study metabolomic changes in a subset of patients with muscle cramps associated with ALS. All patients had at least five muscle cramps per week as an inclusion criterion (Mitsumoto et al., 2025). We compared patients by cramp severity using total MCS, MCS 2, and MCS 5 (Mitsumoto et al., 2019).

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine metabolite changes significantly associated with muscle cramps in ALS. First, glutamate and glutamine emerged as significant metabolites in the statistical analyses. Glutamate is one of the AAs dysregulated in ALS, and it plays a role in excitotoxicity, a putative disease mechanism. This AA is also known to enhance muscle metabolism (Cieslarova et al. 2017; Gautam et al. 2025a; Jia et al. 2021; Parvanovova et al. 2024). Increased glutamate levels are associated with disease progression in ALS (Kumar et al., 2010). Arginine is another AA that was positively correlated with both total MCS and MCS 5, and its relationship with ALS has been previously described (Gautam et al. 2025a; Yusuf et al. 2024). Arginine is metabolized to generate nitric oxide, which plays a critical role in vascular endothelial relaxation (Palmer et al., 1988). However, in pathological conditions such as muscle cramps, in which vascular contraction and reduced blood flow occur, arginine metabolism might be impaired, leading to decreased nitric oxide production. Reduced blood flow can contribute to muscle cramps and hinder the delivery of nutrients to the muscles (Katzberg, 2015). These associations of muscle cramps with glutamate and arginine levels are particularly interesting, and we speculate that these changes are related to the underlying ALS pathophysiology. Skeletal muscles comprise the largest tissue of the human body (approximately 50% of total body mass), and thus, when skeletal muscles undergo uncontrolled severe muscle contractions/cramps because of motor neuron hyperexcitability, significant systemic metabolomic changes can be expected (De Wel & Claeys, 2021; Grapperon & Attarian, 2017). Recent studies of the serum proteome in ALS predominantly recorded changes related to muscle loss and dysfunction, making this assumption reasonable (Chia et al., 2025; Dergai et al., 2025). Nevertheless, it is unclear whether these plasma metabolomic changes are derived from skeletal muscles, the central nervous system, or both.

Acyl-CNs are metabolites formed by the combination of carnitine and fatty acids, with LC-Acyl-CNs being particularly important for transporting fatty acids into mitochondria. Carnitine deficiency has been reported to prolong muscle cramps because of ATP depletion (i.e., a decrease in fatty acid β-oxidation) (Takahashi, 2021). Our study found that higher LC-Acyl-CN levels were positively associated with muscle cramp severity. Serum Acyl-CNs have been proposed as candidate mechanistic biomarkers of mitochondrial damage (McGill et al., 2014); thus, higher LC-Acyl-CN levels might reflect mitochondrial dysfunction, as previously reported in patients with ALS (Smith et al., 2019). Although there are limited reports indicating that Acyl-CN levels increase during ALS and muscle cramps, the importance of carnitine in muscle cramps associated with hemodialysis and liver cirrhosis has been reported to involve fatty acid metabolism and ATP production (Nakanishi et al., 2015; Takahashi, 2021).

Deficient muscle energy availability can lead to a reduction in overall physical activity. ATP production involves the metabolism of carbohydrates, lipids, and AAs. The body compensates for ATP deficiency by catabolizing proteins in tissues through autophagy and utilizing the resulting AAs, such as gluconeogenic AAs (Sartori et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2021). Among the MCS indicators in our study, MCS 5 was associated with the levels of various AAs, suggesting that ATP deficiency attributable to muscle cramps accelerates autophagy as a compensatory response. This is the first report to establish relationships between MCS 5 and various AA levels, although the promotion of muscle protein degradation in ALS and the association between muscle cramps and protein degradation have already been reported (Leger et al., 2006). Among AAs, leucine is generally considered the most anabolic AA because of its ability to independently modulate muscle protein synthesis (Holowaty et al., 2023), and it was previously considered a potential treatment for ALS (Bjornevik et al., 2019). The homeostasis of branched-chain AAs is considered critical for maintaining muscle mass and thus maintaining muscle health (Dai et al., 2021). Finally, our analyses based on total MCS and MCS 5 indicated that trimethylamine N-oxide levels were significantly reduced in ALS and inversely associated with the severity of upper motor neuron signs (Chen et al., 2020). At the muscle level, this metabolite is known to stabilize the actin and heavy meromyosin structure (Kumemoto et al., 2012). The relationships of these metabolites with the mechanism of muscle cramps in ALS remain to be clarified.

The levels of several metabolites, including tryptophan, changed following TJ-68 treatment in patients with ALS and muscle cramps. Tryptophan has a role in regulating skeletal muscle mass; specifically, mice fed a tryptophan-deficient diet exhibited smaller muscles (Ninomiya et al., 2020). In ALS, denervation leads to severe muscle atrophy and muscle weakness, often with progressive systemic malnutrition, which at least superficially might have some similarity to cancer cachexia (D’Amico et al., 2021; Siddiqui et al., 2020). Thus, tryptophan metabolism has been suggested to be a common therapeutic target in these unrelated conditions (Platten et al., 2019). Further, an increase in tryptophan levels might result in the generation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, which is important for a broad range of cellular functions (Lundt, & Ding, 2024). In addition, tryptophan serves as a precursor for serotonin (Wurtman et al., 1980); however, TJ-68 treatment reduced serotonin levels. Serotonin has been linked to the global regulation of motor behavior, local control of motoneuron excitability, and functional recovery of spinal motoneurons in ALS (El Oussini et al., 2017; Sandyk, 2006; Vermeiren et al., 2018). Serotonin participates in muscle activation in humans and serves as a potent regulator of neuronal firing rates, which can influence the force that can be generated by muscles during voluntary contraction (Kavanagh, & Taylor, 2022). Further, serotonergic neuromodulation is a key facilitator of motor unit (MU) discharge and self-sustained firing of human motor neurons (Goodlich et al., 2024). Therefore, we can speculate that muscle cramps occurring in ALS, a prolonged uncontrolled MU facilitation, can be better controlled by suppressing serotonin concentrations (Goodlich et al., 2024).

As previously described, Acyl-CNs and muscle cramps might be linked through mitochondrial dysfunction. Acetyl-CN, which decreased following TJ-68 treatment, reflects the balance between mitochondrial acetyl-CoA production and its use in the TCA cycle. Although increased Acetyl-CN levels may be associated with high mitochondrial metabolic activity (Hagen et al., 2002; Klepochova et al., 2024), they may also occur when the TCA cycle flux is impaired, resulting in the accumulation of excess acetyl groups. Therefore, the observed decrease in Acetyl-CN levels following TJ-68 treatment may be associated with improved use of acetyl-CoA in the TCA cycle and more efficient mitochondrial metabolism. Although some herbal medicines have been demonstrated to improve mitochondrial function (Nahata et al., 2022; Tuo et al., 2023), no such effect has been reported for TJ-68. Although high Acyl-CN levels are associated with greater muscle cramp severity as indicated by MCS, TJ-68 tended to reduce LC-Acyl-CN levels. Taken together, these findings suggest that TJ-68 can exert beneficial effects on mitochondrial function and energy metabolism. Furthermore, the observed increase in the levels of aconitate, a TCA cycle intermediate, also supports the possibility that TJ-68 influences energy production.

A central question of this study was the ability of metabolite changes to predict an improvement in muscle cramps following TJ-68 treatment. Lower BAIBA and UA levels were significantly predictive of TJ-68 responsiveness in all three MCS analyses. BAIBA is considered a myokine, and higher BAIBA levels can stimulate systemic metabolism, including enhancements of muscle activation and osteogenesis (Bottcher et al., 2025; Katano et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024; Sugimoto & Kamei, 2022). Lower BAIBA levels suggest better muscle cramp control, indicating that muscle cramps occur in skeletal muscles, which are generally inactive, more denervated, and more atrophied in ALS. UA levels also emerged as predictive of the ability of TJ-68 to control muscle cramps. UA, a potent antioxidant (Iazzolino et al., 2024; Mitsumoto et al., 2020), was previously found to predict better disease prognosis in ALS (Abraham & Drory, 2014; O’Reilly et al., 2017; Ohnari et al., 2024; Paganoni et al., 2018), suggesting that TJ-68 responders had more advanced ALS. The possibility of UA as a predictive biomarker of TJ-68 efficacy can also be explained by the metabolic direction of hypoxanthine. Hypoxanthine is metabolized to either UA or inosine monophosphate, an ATP precursor, through two different metabolic pathways (Saugstad, 1988). When the former pathway is more dominant, ATP precursors are deficient, making ATP production difficult if TJ-68 promotes energy production. The blood levels of hypoxanthine and inosine monophosphate were not investigated in this study; therefore, these hypotheses need to be further investigated in the future. More importantly, UA has practical utility as a predictor of TJ-68 efficacy because its levels can be easily tested at any clinic. Further studies will be helpful to confirm our findings in clinical practice.

Based on the MCS analyses, lower AAAA levels were significantly predictive of improvement in muscle cramps. Increased AAAA levels are associated with muscle atrophy and weakness (Shida et al., 2025), apparently opposing the trends observed for BAIBA. Nevertheless, heavy exercise in healthy individuals can simultaneously increase AAAA and BAIBA levels (Kusy et al., 2024), suggesting that induced heavy exercise and severe muscle cramps in ALS might share underlying metabolic processes, albeit acting on different cellular sites. Obviously, further research required for clarification. In addition, acetylcholine levels were significantly associated with all analyzed MCS components. When acetylcholine levels decrease, TJ-68 would be expected to better control muscle cramps. As acetylcholine functions as an anti-inflammatory neurotransmitter in the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (Tao et al., 2019), impaired anti-inflammatory activity would be expected to facilitate muscle cramp control by TJ-68. However, it is not clear how acetylcholine influences the effects of TJ-68 treatment, and further research is necessary.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and systemic metabolic alterations occur in patients with ALS (Gautam et al. 2025b). Host metabolic profiles may be influenced by the composition and functional capacity of the gut microbiota. For example, several AAs are metabolized by the gut microbiota (Fan, & Pedersen, 2021). Therefore, crosstalk between the gut metagenome and host metabolome may contribute to the plasma metabolic alterations observed in the present study; however, examining this relationship was beyond the scope of our analysis, as microbiome data were not collected. Future studies will be required to elucidate these interactions.

We successfully demonstrated important changes in metabolite levels in a small number of patients with ALS and severe muscle cramps, suggesting that metabolomic studies can provide new insights into underlying metabolic mechanisms associated with heterogeneous types of ALS (Feldman et al., 2022; Hardiman & van den Berg, 2020; Mitsumoto et al., 1998; Tzeplaeff et al., 2024). Despite the phenotypic diversity of ALS, current clinical trials (Mitsumoto et al., 2014) and most prior ALS metabolomic studies treated ALS as a single disease (Castelli et al., 2024; Lanznaster et al., 2018; Mitropoulos et al., 2018). Furthermore, we lack reliable biomarkers or other means to delineate subtypes of ALS. In contrast to the monolithic approach to disease-modifying pharmacotherapies, a better understanding of molecular subtypes might help clarify ALS subtypes and more rapidly and effectively advance disease-modifying therapies.

ALS has been considered a systemic metabolic disease that affects motor neurons (Blasco et al., 2018; Burg, Van Den Bosch 2023; Dorst et al., 2018; Mitsumoto et al., 2020). Thus, new strategies are needed to study metabolomics and other omics in a more routine, consistent, and collaborative manner. All future clinical trials should include metabolomics and other omics analyses to identify heterogeneous underlying mechanisms and metabolic derangements in ALS (Blasco et al., 2018; Castelli et al., 2024; Goutman et al., 2020; Lanznaster et al., 2018; Lawton et al., 2012, 2014; Mitropoulos et al., 2018).

Our study had several limitations. First, our clinical trial included a small number of participants because of recruitment difficulties, and only a potential benefit for improving muscle cramps was observed (Mitsumoto et al., 2025). Therefore, this metabolomic study should be considered exploratory. Although a future study with a larger sample size is warranted, the present analysis leveraged multiple crossovers in an N-of-1 design, allowing us to include repeated measurements after the placebo and TJ-68 treatment periods and reduce variability by using the participants as their own controls. Second, we did not compare patients with and without muscle cramps. Instead, we compared metabolomic changes between participants with more severe and less severe muscle cramps because all patients had at least five muscle cramps per week due to the trial design. To study the significance of metabolomic changes in patients with ALS and muscle cramps, we might need to compare patients with and without muscle cramps. Third, we found repeated Acyl-CN abnormalities in some metabolomic analyses. These changes suggest underlying abnormalities in the metabolism of fatty acids and other lipids (Lee et al., 2023). However, to verify these insights, future studies incorporating lipidomic analyses are needed.

Conclusions

Our study is the first to highlight metabolite changes in a small number of patients with ALS and severe muscle cramps, representing a subset of ALS cases. In addition, we found certain metabolite changes induced by TJ-68 administration, which is commonly used for the treatment of muscle cramps in Japan. Further, we identified metabolite changes predictive of the response to TJ-68 in ALS muscle cramps. We strongly believe that metabolomic analyses in ALS could be highly useful for future clinical trials and assessments of disease heterogeneity in ALS.
