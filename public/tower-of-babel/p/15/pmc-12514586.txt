Correlation between brain structural features and early motor outcomes from deep brain stimulation in patients with Parkinson disease
Authors: Wenxi Zheng, Limin Lan, Xingzi Xie, Mingxian Huang, Hui Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN-DBS) is an effective neuromodulation therapy for patients with mid-to-late-stage Parkinson disease (PD), demonstrating significant efficacy at the group level. However, therapeutic outcomes across individual patients vary. This study investigated the correlation between brain structural characteristics and the early motor outcomes after administration of bilateral STN-DBS in patients with PD.MethodsA total of 26 patients with PD were assessed 3–6 months after surgery according to Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (UPDRS-III) score. Patients were stratified into a high-improvement group (HMI; n=19) and a low-improvement group (LMI; n=7) based on a 30% improvement rate. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) analysis methods were used to compare the two groups’ gray-matter volume (GMV) and white-matter (WM) tract integrity. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to identify potential neuroanatomical predictors of response to DBS.ResultsAll patients showed significant motor improvement (P<0.001), with a median reduction of 9.20 points in UPDRS-III score. Compared with the HMI group, the LMI group exhibited greater GMV atrophy in the bilateral middle frontal gyrus, left dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate gyrus, orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus, medial superior frontal gyrus, posterior orbital gyrus, right globus pallidus, gyrus rectus, precuneus, and middle cingulate gyrus (P<0.05). The GMV in the bilateral supplementary motor areas (SMAs), bilateral putamen, left supracallosal anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and left lateral geniculate body was positively correlated with the postoperative UPDRS-III score (P<0.05). Tract-based spatial statistics revealed decreased WM tract integrity in the right anterior thalamic radiation, inferior frontal-occipital fasciculus, superior longitudinal fasciculus, and forceps minor (P<0.05). Notably, although LASSO analysis identified the GMVs of the right globus pallidus, precuneus, and left supracallosal ACC as predictors of DBS response, these variables were excluded from the final model due to nonsignificant P values (P>0.05) in multivariate logistic regression.ConclusionsThis study examined the relationship between the GMV and WM microstructure of certain brain regions in patients with PD and early motor improvement after DBS. Our findings clarify the importance of these brain regions in the pathophysiological mechanism of PD and their potential predictive value for DBS motor efficacy and offer new perspectives for promoting the individualization of DBS treatment.Keywords: Parkinson disease (PD), deep brain stimulation (DBS), subthalamic nucleus (STN), brain structural imaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Introduction

Parkinson disease (PD) is a common chronic neurodegenerative disorder characterized by core motor dysfunction (including tremors, rigidity, and bradykinesia) and additionally encompasses a broad spectrum of nonmotor symptoms (1). Although medication can effectively control symptoms in the early stages of the disease, for some patients with mid-to-late-stage PD, medication becomes less effective and side effects worsen, making it difficult to maintain a satisfactory quality of life. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is an effective neurosurgical treatment and is recommended for patients with medication-refractory dose fluctuations or tremor (2). DBS involves the use of implanted electrodes to stimulate specific deep brain nuclei, such as the subthalamic nucleus (STN), providing continuous electrical stimulation to modulate abnormal neural signals. Relief of motor symptoms can be achieved within seconds to hours after DBS is activated (3,4). Several reported cases have confirmed that DBS is significantly effective in improving motor symptoms, reducing medication dosage, and improving the quality of life in patients with PD (5-9). However, the therapeutic effect varies widely across individuals (10). In this study, we employed structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) technology to investigate the correlation between brain structural characteristics of patients with PD and the early improvement of motor symptoms following STN-DBS surgery. Our overall aim was to generate a scientific basis for the individualization of DBS treatment and prognostic assessment. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-603/rc).

Methods

Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and approved by the Ethics Committee of the 900th Hospital of PLA Joint Logistic Support Force (approval No. 2024-089). Informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

Patients who received bilateral STN-DBS treatment at the 900th Hospital of PLA Joint Logistic Support Force from August 2023 to September 2024 were enrolled as research participants.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) meeting the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria for PD (1,11); (II) a Hoehn and Yahr (H-Y) stage (12) between 2.5 and 5 during the medication-off period; (III) passing routine preoperative DBS assessment examination, including comprehensive neurological impairment assessment, neuropsychological assessment, levodopa responsiveness assessment, and head MRI; and (IV) availability of complete clinical and imaging data.

Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) secondary parkinsonism or parkinsonism due to alternate causes; (II) history of severe cranial organic disease, brain trauma, mental illness, or neurological surgery; (III) absolute contraindications to MRI or inability to cooperate with MRI due to significant motor disturbances; (IV) history of severe alcohol or drug abuse; (V) other conditions that could cause motor dysfunction (e.g., musculoskeletal disorders and severe visual impairment); and (VI) loss to follow-up for various reasons.

All patients in the study underwent stereotactic DBS surgery in the Neurosurgery Department of the 900th Hospital of PLA Joint Logistic Support Force, with the stimulating electrodes precisely implanted in the bilateral subthalamic nuclei. Patients were systematically followed up before and 3–6 months after surgery, and various clinical data were collected. UPDRS-III (13) was used to assess patients’ motor function, and UPDRS-III scores were recorded before surgery (baseline) and when stimulation parameters were stable after surgery. Motor function was evaluated in the stimulated-on state and the medication-off state. The improvement rate of motor symptoms for each patient was calculated based on the percentage decrease in the postoperative UPDRS-III score. Patients with PD were divided into a high motor improvement (HMI) group (improvement rate ≥30%) and a low motor improvement (LMI) group (improvement rate <30%), with 30% serving as the threshold. Research methods

MRI acquisition

Cranial MRI data were acquired in all patients with a standard 12-channel phased-array head coil on a 3.0-T Skyra MRI scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany).

High-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted (3D-T1W) structural images were acquired with a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (GR-IR) sequence. The scan parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR), 1,900 ms; echo time (TE), 2.27 ms; inversion time (TI), 900 ms; flip angle (FA), 9°; field of view (FOV), 250 mm × 250 mm; matrix, 256×256; voxel resolution, 1.0×1.0×1.0 mm3; slice thickness, 1.0 mm; and slice spacing, 0 mm.

The diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) sequence was acquired with a single-shot echo-planar (SS-EPI) sequence under the following scan parameters: TR, 7,200 ms; TE, 104 ms; FA, 90°; FOV, 256 mm × 256 mm; voxel resolution, 2.0×2.0×3.0 mm3; slice thickness, 3.0 mm; slice spacing, 3.0 mm; b-value, 0 and 1,000 s/mm2; and number of diffusion gradient directions, 64.

Image preprocessing

For image preprocessing, the image quality was first checked, and the original images that met the requirements were converted from Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format to Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) format via MRIcron software for subsequent processing and analysis.

The 3D-T1WI data were preprocessed with Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) version 12 (SPM12; www/fil.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and Computational Anatomy Toolbox 12 (CAT; www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat) toolkit on the MATLAB R2023b platform (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) (14). Whole-brain images were registered to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, with gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid segmentation and alignment to the Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL) template (15) for normalization. In addition, the built-in automatic anatomical labeling atlas 3 (AAL3v1) (16) was used to further segment the brain regions of interest. Finally, a Gaussian smoothing kernel with a full width at half maximum (FMHW) of 8 mm was used to spatially smooth the gray-matter image to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Empirical quality control was performed under default parameters to ensure that image quality met scientific research standards.

DTI data were preprocessed with the FMRIB Software Library (FSL) (www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl). Specific steps included the extraction of b0 images, skull stripping, and eddy current correction for head motion. The diffusion toolbox of the software was used to extract the diffusion tensor properties of each voxel in all patients, primarily including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD). Finally, a detailed analysis was performed with the tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) method.

Image data analysis

The 3D-T1WI data were analyzed via SPM12 software. A two-sample t-test was performed to compare the gray-matter volume (GMV) between the two groups, with age, sex, disease duration, and total intracranial volume (TIV) serving as covariates. A significance threshold of P<0.001 was set at the voxel level, and multiple comparison correction was performed via the Gaussian random field (GRF) method at the cluster level. The corrected significance threshold was P<0.05, and clusters with more than 30 voxels were considered significant brain regions. The brain regions with significant GMV differences were recorded, including their neuroanatomical location, MNI coordinates, corresponding voxel values, and peak intensities of the voxel regions (T values). Visualization was performed with the BrainNet Viewer plug-in for DPABI software (17), and neuroanatomical regions were identified according to the AAL3v1 atlas. Finally, mean gray-matter data of different brain regions were extracted via the CAT12 toolbox for the subsequent correlation study.

Group statistical analysis was based on the DTI data and conducted with general linear model (GLM) in FSL. Age, sex, and disease duration were used as covariates, and the four diffusion parameters were compared individually by repeating the nonparametric permutation test 500 times. Finally, the results were corrected for multiple comparisons via threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE). Visualization was performed with FSLeyes software, and anatomical regions were identified in the JHU White-Matter Tractography Atlas.

Statistical analysis

Clinical data were analyzed with SPSS version 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and visualized with GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). Quantitative variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and between-group comparisons were performed via the independent samples t-test. The normality of quantitative variables was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test (for sample sizes <50). For nonnormally distributed data, values are reported as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed via the Mann-Whitney. Within-group comparisons (e.g., pre- vs. postoperative UPDRS-III scores) were analyzed via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and associations were evaluated via the chi-squared test (or the Fisher exact test for small sample sizes). Correlation analyses were carried out through use of the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model was implemented through the “glmnet” R package.

Results

General statistics

Between August 2023 and September 2024, 26 patients with PD were enrolled according to the inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria (Figure 1).

Among the 26 patients with PD who underwent bilateral STN-DBS surgery and were included in the study, 16 males and 10 females were aged 56–80 years, with a mean age of 68.04±6.33 years. The duration of PD ranged from 3 to 26 years, with a median of 8.00 (IQR 5.50–11.25) years. The H-Y stage ranged from 3 to 5, including 5 cases of grade 3, 19 cases of grade 4, and 2 cases of grade 5.

At 3 to 6 months after operation, all patients with PD who underwent DBS surgery exhibited varying degrees of improvement in motor symptoms. The median preoperative UPDRS-III score was 24.40 (IQR 21.68–31.03), and the postoperative score decreased to 15.20 (IQR 12.00–17.75), representing a median reduction of 9.20 points. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicated a statistically significant decrease in UPDRS-III scores postoperatively (Z=–4.458; P<0.001) (Figure 2).

Correlation analysis

Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between GMV in different brain regions and the rate of improvement in early postoperative UPDRS-III score after DBS. The results revealed that GMV in the bilateral supplementary motor areas (SMAs), bilateral fusiform nucleus, left supracallosal anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and left lateral geniculate body were significantly and positively correlated with the rate of improvement in UPDRS-III score (P<0.05; Figure 5).

Additionally, age, sex, disease duration, H-Y stage, TIV, total brain GMV, WMV (and its ratio), and whole-brain diffusion parameter values in patients with PD showed no significant correlation with the rate of improvement in UPDRS-III score (P>0.05).

Based on the LASSO analysis, the GMV of the right globus pallidus, precuneus and left supracallosal ACC were selected as predictors for developing a multivariate logistic regression model to predict response to DBS in patients with PD (Figure 6). Discussion

During the follow-up period 3 to 6 months following surgery, we observed significant improvement in motor symptoms across all enrolled patients with PD, with a median reduction of 9.20 points in the UPDRS-III score. These findings strongly support the therapeutic efficacy of STN-DBS in ameliorating motor dysfunction in patients with PD.

Correlation between GMV and motor improvement

When examining the difference in GMV between the HMI group (improvement rate in UPDRS-III scores ≥30%) and the LMI group (improvement rate in UPDRS-III score <30%), we found that the LMI group showed more pronounced atrophy of GMV in brain regions related to the frontal lobes (e.g., bilateral middle frontal gyrus, left dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus orbital, and medial superior frontal gyrus) and in cingulate gyrus (left anterior cingulate gyrus and right middle cingulate gyrus) than did the HMI group. Yim et al. (18) used different grouping criteria and found that the GMV of the bilateral anterior cingulate gyrus and the left middle frontal gyrus in the HMI group tended to be larger than that of the LMI group. Koivu et al. and Jergas et al. also demonstrated that the atrophy of GMV in the frontal cortex and anterior cingulate gyrus can be used as a predictor of poor prognosis after DBS (19-21). Multiple subregions of the frontal lobes play an integral role in motor execution, cognitive performance, and behavioral control (22). In contrast, the anterior cingulate gyrus, which is anatomically adjacent to the prefrontal cortex and projects fibers to the premotor cortex and SMA, plays a role in monitoring motor execution, regulating behavior, and facilitating cognitive control and impulse inhibition (22-26). Atrophy of these areas may lead to impaired functional connectivity of the motor network and reduced efficiency of information transmission, which in turn may affect motor improvement after DBS.

Correlation analysis further revealed a significant positive association between UPDRS-III score improvement rate and GMV in the bilateral SMA, bilateral thalamus, left supracallosal ACC, and left lateral geniculate body. The bilateral SMA plays a pivotal role in planning and executing complex movements, with its GMV integrity being essential for accurate motor output (22). The putamen, a key basal ganglia component, is involved in motor learning and control (27). Karachi et al. (28) found that the bilateral putamen region of patients with worsening freezing gait after DBS had significantly atrophied GMV, which is consistent with the results of our study and further emphasizes that changes in GMV in certain brain regions, such as the putamen, may affect the regulatory effect of DBS on motor circuits. The lateral geniculate body serves as a sensory projection nucleus of the thalamus involved in visual information processing and the motor control of the body through feedforward and feedback mechanisms. Its gray matter state can influence motor control indirectly (29,30). Although the specific mechanism of action is not fully understood, it can be speculated that the state of gray matter in the lateral geniculate nucleus may indirectly affect motor control by influencing the transmission of visual information to motor control centers or through interactions with other motor-related brain regions. In our study, the positive correlation between lateral geniculate nucleus GMV and the rate of improvement in UPDRS-III score suggests that it may play be a critical component in the DBS regulation of motor function.

In this exploratory, small-sample analysis, the LASSO model identified the GMVs of the right globus pallidus, precuneus, and left supracallosal ACC as potential predictors of high treatment response. These findings align with prior neuroanatomical theories linking these regions to motor control and neuroplasticity (31-33). However, the subsequent multivariable logistic regression failed to validate their independent predictive effect. This discrepancy may be attributed to limitations such as insufficient sample size and potential collinearity among variables. Larger-scale investigations or model adjustments (e.g., addressing collinearity and adopting more flexible modeling strategies) are warranted to verify further these preliminary associations.

Correlation between white-matter microstructure and movement improvement

TBSS analysis revealed that when the threshold for the TFCE was relaxed to 0.70, the LMI group tended to have decreased FA values and increased MD and RD values in the right anterior thalamic radiation, inferior frontal-occipital bundle, superior longitudinal fasciculus, and forceps minor as compared with the HMI group. These findings suggest that impaired structural integrity of the affected white-matter tracts potentially arises due to pathological changes such as axonal injury or demyelination.

From a neuroanatomical and functional perspective, the anterior thalamic radiation is a key white-matter tract connecting the prefrontal cortex and thalamus, responsible for integrating and transmitting sensory-motor information. The inferior frontal-occipital tracts connect the prefrontal and occipital lobes, participating in visuomotor processing (34). The superior longitudinal fasciculus connects the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes to the ipsilateral frontal cortex. It is closely associated with transmitting higher neural functions such as spatial cognition and motor execution (35). The forceps minor, a critical component of the corpus callosum, connects to the anterior cingulate gyrus and is involved in the exchange of information between the bilateral cerebral hemispheres.

Abnormalities in the structural integrity of these white-matter tracts can disrupt interhemispheric cooperation during the motor control processes (36). During the early postoperative recovery phase following DBS, such disruptions may impair the patient’s ability to effectively integrate sensory and cognitive feedback into movement planning, ultimately compromising the accuracy and coordination of motor control. These deficits likely contribute to the reduced short-term motor efficacy observed in the LMI group. The white-matter structure of the HMI group appeared to be relatively intact, indicating a practical pathway for transmitting modulation signals generated by DBS stimulation. These modulation signals can be accurately transmitted along the relatively intact tracts to motor-related brain areas, such as the frontal lobe. This facilitates better regulation of abnormal neural activity within the motor loop and more effectively promotes the improvement of motor symptoms in patients with PD (37).

Synergistic association of structural changes in gray and white matter with DBS efficacy

Brain regions such as the frontal lobe and anterior cingulate gyrus, along with white-matter tracts including the anterior thalamic radiation and the inferior frontal-occipital bundle, form an extensive and sophisticated brain connectivity network that is critical to the integration, transmission, and execution of motor information and is closely related to the clinical efficacy of STN-DBS (38-41).

In movement disorders, white-matter microstructural changes typically precede gray-matter atrophy. Pathological changes such as axonal injury and demyelination directly damage white-matter tracts, leading to abnormalities in their structure and function and indirectly affecting the nutrition and uptake of gray-matter neurons, promoting further atrophy (42-44). Gray-matter atrophy in brain regions such as the frontal lobe and anterior cingulate gyrus may also form a vicious circle by affecting connections between neurons in functional areas of the brain network, further exacerbating damage to the white-matter structure. In the LMI group, we observed an interaction between gray-matter atrophy and white-matter microstructural damage. We hypothesize that these pathological changes may synergistically contribute to motor network damage and impaired information transmission, potentially affecting STN-DBS motor efficacy. Therefore, comprehensive preoperative detection of gray-and white-matter structures in specific brain regions may provide valuable reference information for predicting postoperative motor efficacy.

This study found no statistically significant differences between the HMI and LMI groups in terms of TIV, GMV, WMV, their ratios, or the mean values of whole-brain diffusion parameters. These findings are consistent with previous research (18,20). Further analysis revealed that patient age, sex, disease duration, H-Y stage, TIV, GMV, WMV, and their ratios were not significantly correlated with the UPDRS-III improvement rate. These results suggest that the previously mentioned clinical factors have a relatively limited impact on the efficacy of STN-DBS as compared to the structural characteristics of certain brain regions. For in-depth exploration, future research should focus on the synergistic relationship between gray- and white-matter structures. By comprehensively assessing brain structural alterations, clinicians may develop more individualized treatment strategies, thereby maximizing the motor benefits of STN-DBS and improving patients’ quality of life.

Limitations

This study involved several limitations, mainly a small sample size and a short follow-up period, which may hinder the generalizability of the results and their extrapolation to the long-term. Additionally, the LASSO model used for variable selection in this small sample may be prone to overfitting or unstable predictor selection. Furthermore, grouping patients according to the criterion of a 30% improvement in UPDRS-III score was somewhat subjective. Although other grouping criteria (35% and 40%) were attempted, different criteria led to inconsistent results. A 30% improvement rate was finally chosen as the basis for grouping to better distinguish the potential differences between the two groups, but selection bias might have nonetheless been introduced. Studies with larger sample sizes and an extended follow-up period should be conducted to more comprehensively assess the long-term effects of DBS treatment and the relationship between structural changes in the brain.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate a complex, multiregional network linking motor improvement, GMV, and white-matter microstructure in patients with moderate-to-severe PD after bilateral STN-DBS. Key brain regions (e.g., frontal lobe, anterior cingulate gyrus, and supplementary motor area) and white-matter tracts (e.g., anterior thalamic radiation and inferior frontal occipital bundle) form critical nodes influencing DBS efficacy, reflecting their roles in PD neurobiology. In a small-sample exploratory analysis, LASSO modeling identified the right globus pallidus, precuneus, and left supracallosal ACC GMVs as potential predictors of high DBS response. These findings provide new insights for personalized DBS patient selection and highlight the need for larger studies to validate these associations.
