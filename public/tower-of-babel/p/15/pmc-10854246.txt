Management of a Rare Tessier 30 Median Mandibular Cleft Anomaly: A Comprehensive Review
Authors: Sakshi Katyal, Sujata Mohanty, Sanjay Miglani, Panchali Batra
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe median mandibular cleft (MMC) is a rare craniofacial anomaly manifesting as a cleft of the lower lip and mandible, which may extend to the neck to a variable extent and severity. Its management involves a timely, staged, and multidisciplinary approach. Unlike for maxillary cleft lip and palate, the literature on this anomaly is scarce and scattered. Also, guidelines for the management of mandibular cleft are not clearly outlined. Introduction

Craniofacial clefts are developmental malformations that fall into three main categories: (1) median clefts, which can involve any structure in the median plane of the cranium, midface, or mandible; (2) oronasal ocular clefts, which involve the mouth, nose, and eyes; and (3) lateral clefts of face, from the corner of mouth extending to ear.1

“Tessier 30” is a caudal extension of the median cleft which involves the mandible, hence the name median mandibular cleft (MMC). It is a rare anomaly, with very few cases reported worldwide.2,3 It presents as the cleft of the lower lip and mandible in the midline, which may extend to the neck to a variable extent. The expression of median clefts is broadly contemplated into two broad categories: simple and complex types.3 The former is simple midline clefts of the lower lip and mandible with ankyloglossia. The latter has more complex wide clefts with associated tumors and cysts, along with other craniofacial and visceral abnormalities.

The existing literature on MMCs is mostly in the form of case reports, and a comprehensive review of its etiology, clinical presentation, diagnosis, and definite guidelines for its management is deficient. This article aims to consolidate the literature from multiple case reports and the changing trends in the management of MMC. This review may serve as a guide for clinicians in the stage-wise management of this rare craniofacial anomaly.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

A literature search was performed on PubMed, SCOPUS, and Web of Science databases using the term “Mandibular cleft” OR “Tessier 30 cleft.” Filters were used to exclude articles with the author's name “Tessier” and search articles in English only. From the preliminary search of 441 titles, n = 68 articles were hand-filtered as per relevance to MMC from the title and abstract. Among these articles, n = 56 were case reports, n = 2 were articles related to genetic associations, n = 4 syndromes associated, n = 3 discussed the classification of craniofacial clefts, and n = 3 were literature reviews.

Based on an analysis of the literature, a detailed description of the MMC and its management by both traditional and contemporary approaches are discussed below.

Discussion

Previous review articles reported 15–20% cases of the cleft of lower lip only, 15% as cleft mandible only, and 55–60% as cleft of lower lip and mandible amongst Tessier 30 clefts.3

Classification of Median Mandibular Clefts

Median mandibular clefts (MMCs) were first described by Couronne in 1819.4 According to Tessier's classification of craniofacial clefts (1976), facial clefts were categorized into fourteen types based on the location of the cleft on the face. Numbers “0” and “14” are median craniofacial dysgraphia, and Tessier's number “30” is the caudal or mandibular extension of number “0” cleft, whereas “14” is its cranial extension (Fig. 1).1

Tessier's classification of orofacial clefts (1976)

Embryonic Origin

The first branchial arch gives rise to two mandibular processes bilaterally, which grow out and approximate each other in the midline at 5 weeks of intrauterine (I.U.) life.2,5 These mandibular processes fuse in the late embryonic period.5,6 At the 7th month in utero, the mental cartilages ossify into an intramembranous bone of symphysis.7 The fibrous joint of symphysis fuses in infancy from syndesmosis to synostosis.8,9

The development of the MMC is either due to failure of fusion of a pair of first brachial arches or failure of mesodermal penetration in the midline.5 Defects in fusion may just produce a notch of the vermilion with or without the involvement of the mandibular alveolus. Meanwhile, failure of mesodermal penetration may manifest as the absence of hyoid bone, thyroid cartilage, neck muscles, and manubrium in the midline where the cleft extends to the neck.2 This absence of mesodermal migration may result in obstruction by a mass of pluripotent cells, cysts, and tumors associated with MMC.10

It is postulated that aberrations arising early in I.U. life cause more severe defects than those late in embryogenesis. Likewise, mandibular clefts developing during the early embryonic period may cause a wide defect with greater extensions into the neck and involving other branchial arches (Fig. 2). Whereas lack of fusion during the late embryonic period manifests as narrow and less severe midline fusion defects. The severity of the MMC depends on the extent to which various brachial arches are involved.

Associated Genetic Aberrations

Bone morphogenetic protein 4/activin receptor-like kinase 3 deficiency in mice and homozygous 237 kb deletion at 1p31 (LHX8 gene deletion) in humans has been reported to produce defects mimicking the mandible Tessier 30 cleft.11,12

Monroe et al., in their case report, conducted a cytogenetic analysis to rule out mongolism (trisomy G of chromosome 21), but analysis revealed mosaicism 46/47 with aneuploid strain showing a small median centric extra chromosome with maternal trisomy E (small median centric chromosomes 16, 17, 18).13

Clinical Presentation

The MMCs may present with or without lip involvement (Fig. 3).3,14 It may extend to the neck, involving its bones and cartilage, such as hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage.2,9 The strap muscles of the neck are also often atrophic and replaced by the fibrous tissue, causing flexion contracture of the neck (Fig. 4).13,15 This affects the posture and anatomy of the craniofacial skeleton due to an altered growth vector. Due to the lack of differentiation of some tissues, dermoid cysts, ectopic salivary glands, and foregut duplication cysts are also reported in the perioral area.16

Diagnostic Workup

Diagnosis of this developmental defect can be done as early as 17 weeks of prenatal period via ultrasonography (Table 2).17 As soon as a child is reported with MMC, the pediatrician must inspect for other structures involved and also rule out any airway or cardiac involvement (septal defects) by general body evaluation, clinical examination, and radiologic examination.18 Additional facial clefts, such as auricular and transcranial extensions, are essential to be ruled out soon after birth or before attempting the soft-tissue repair.

Management of Patients with Median Mandibular Clefts

A sequential and interdisciplinary intervention is required for a child born with a MMC, as mentioned below. The timeline for the management of patients with MMC is given in Table 2. The following factors should be given attention when treating these patients.

Airway

It is foremost important to secure the airway soon after birth as a mandibular deficiency, ankyloglossia, and MMC-associated tumors and cysts may obstruct the respiratory pathway.10 Respiratory distress may also arise as a postsurgical complication.18 The airway is safeguarded by endotracheal intubation or by elective tracheostomy whenever required.15

Feeding

Unoperated cleft infants are very commonly undernourished, as lip seals are difficult to achieve.13 The feeding route should be established by spoon-feeding or special feeders such as Haberman's nipple and Mead Johnson cleft palate nurser if available.19 General guidelines include holding the infant in a more upright position while feeding.20 As the child picks up the weight appropriate for general anesthesia, he is taken up for lip repair and lingual frenectomy.15 Immediately after repair of the lip, feeding with a syringe, cup, or spoon is recommended to avoid dehiscence of the suture line.20

Surgical Management

Soft tissue repair:

Soft tissue repair of lower lip cleft and ankyloglossia are surgically corrected as soon as possible after birth.7,19 A Minor incomplete cleft of the lower lip is usually repaired with a ”V-excision” of the defect with direct closure.15,21 Reconstruction of the labiomental sulcus is advocated to ensure an anatomical, functional repair with muscular dissection considering the use of triangles (limited Z-plasty) in the region of the vermilion border by Valk et al.

Complete extensive clefts of the lower lip and neck are repaired with Z-plasty, V-Y, and W-plasty to prevent excessive contracture or banding in the scars. The lingual frenum is usually short and may require a frenectomy of the ankylosed tongue from the cleft margins. Associated cysts, tumors, and teratomas are also excised due to their variably enormous size and are perceived as obstructive life threats.10,22

Hard tissue repair:

The hard tissue surgical repair strategy is crucial to the management of the “Tessier 30” cleft, as nearly 80% of the cases reported injunction of the bilateral mandibular process with a medial bony defect.3 There are two schools of thought based on age at which mandibular cleft reduction is performed.3 Traditionally, surgeons choose to repair this defect as a two-staged procedure,7 but the recent trend is following a single-stage repair of both soft tissue and mandibular bone defect in the 1st year of life.3,23

One-stage vs Two-stage Surgical Repair

Around 50% of the “Tessier 30” cases had undergone one-stage repair of both soft and hard tissue, while the rest had more than one-stage repair.3

One school of thought promotes early repair of both hard and soft tissues simultaneously as a single procedure in the 1st year of life3 for a better prognosis of function and development. It involves approximation of the mandibular processes by interosseous wiring to stabilize mandibular processes bilaterally by a fibrous union.16,24 Mandibular permanent incisors develop lingual to deciduous predecessors, leaving room for intraosseous wires and osteosynthesis plates, especially near the inferior cortical border of the mandible.

Latest trends suggest primary mucogingivoperioplasty with resorbable fixation at the time of lower lip repair promotes close approximation of cleft segments with better development outcomes and the need for the least surgical intervention later in life.

The traditional school advocates a two-stage procedure, which is a separate repair of soft tissue (lip and tongue), followed by hard tissue repair (mandibular process reduction and repair with graft) when the child is around 7–8 years of age to prevent damage to developing tooth buds.7,25 Although two-stage procedures have the advantage of shorter anesthesia and fewer respiratory risks associated with extensive surgical procedures.

The latest trends are more inclined towards the early and single-step repair of mandibular cleft by reapproximation with intraosseous wires, sutures, resorbable plates, etc.3 The new procedural innovation also advocates the use of bone morphogenic proteins (Sheckter et al.) in narrow defects to minimize morbidity associated with an additional harvest site; however, this requires more evidence.26 The trends of surgical management are evident in the literature (Table 3). Revision surgeries:

Scars and neck contractures are revised by a plastic surgeon as per the individual's esthetics and functional needs to lessen the deformity by lengthening and changing the orientation of the vertical scar.7,15 It plays a major role in improving the quality of life and confidence of the affected child, especially during adolescence.

Orthodontic Treatment

The harmony between intraoral tissues, tongue, jaws, and facial muscles is disturbed in the mandibular cleft. Bilateral mandibular processes are mobile and collapsed with no definite occlusion (Fig. 5). Malocclusions like anterior open bite, posterior crossbite, and vertical growth pattern25 with backward directed underdeveloped chin have been seen (Figs 4 and 6).9,16

A cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) view of an adult patient with an unoperated MMC; a wide defect is evident with a full complement of permanent teeth in the mandibular anterior segment

Due to altered stomatognathic balance, these patients present with severe sagittal transverse and skeletal disproportionality (Fig. 6). Along with bone grafting for the median defect, the patient may require orthognathic surgery. The literature lacks any discussion on simultaneous or separate bone grafting and orthognathic surgery. A well-planned orthodontic treatment also promotes the overall esthetic and functional outcome. Presurgical orthodontics for decompensation and leveling of arches before orthognathic surgery to address the skeletal malocclusion is required. Virtual surgical planning may be beneficial for complex cases. Orthodontic treatment in concurrence with repair surgeries can promise a better outcome.

Oral Rehabilitation

Iatrogenic Injury to the tooth germ in the mandibular incisor region has been reported after primary repair.7,25 Median bone defect makes the prosthetic rehabilitation of missing teeth difficult. Bone grafting and its successful integration to margins promote the viability of prosthetic replacement.

Speech

Very few studies reported speech abnormalities with MMC. Due to ankyloglottic restriction of tongue movement, articulation of speech may be abnormal.27 In MMC, the lower lip is incompetent, hypotonic, and scarred after lip repair, thus compromising these sibilants and bilabial sounds. Lip repair should be executed well before the speech develops in a child, followed by referral to a speech therapist between 3 and 5 years of age.8

Psychological Care

The psychological impact of the mandibular cleft is major, and parents require counseling soon after birth.28 Regular sessions of the patient and parents at later stages of life are also required to make this journey less traumatic.

Summary

The diagnosis of MMC should involve a thorough clinical, radiological examination along the lines described by Tessier et al., as external cleft may not completely manifest clinically. A thorough general body evaluation to rule out associated anomalies, commonly cardiac, is also necessary. Key aspects in management, such as airway, feeding, speech, surgical repair, guided dental eruption, and esthetics, should be given due importance. Surgeries should be planned and executed at an appropriate age for a better prognosis. Although MMC still has very little incidence, a clearly outlined management protocol addressing the above-mentioned ambiguities in treatment strategy is much needed.
