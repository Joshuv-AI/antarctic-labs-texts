The impact of air pollution on active tuberculosis development from latent tuberculosis infection in rural China: 10-year follow-up of a cohort study
Authors: Yuanzhi Di, Henan Xin, Xuefang Cao, Hongzhi Li, Weitao Duan, Aiwei He, Jun Liang, Limei Zhu, Boxuan Feng, Yijun He, Lingyu Shen, Juanjuan Huang, Zisen Liu, Fang Liu, Shumin Yang, Zuhui Xu, Cheng Chen, Bin Zhang, Jiaoxia Yan, Yanchun Liang, Rong Liu, Tao Zhu, Fei Shen, Tonglei Guo, Zihan Li, Jianguo Liang, Yaqi Zhao, Liqiong Bai, Wei Lu, Qi Jin, Jiang Du, Lei Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of the study was to investigate the impact of major air pollutants on the development of tuberculosis (TB) among adults with latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) in rural areas in China.MethodsBased on a population-based multicentre cohort study conducted since 2013, we aimed to examine the associations between various air pollutants and the risk of active TB development from LTBI. The estimates were assessed using time-dependent Cox regression models, with nonlinearity evaluated by restricted cubic splines. The China High Air Pollutants datasets were used to feature high-resolution, high-quality, near-surface air pollution data in China.ResultsA total of 20 869 participants were followed for 10 years, and 185 incident TB cases were identified. Long-term exposure to PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2 and CO was found to be significantly correlated with increased risk of TB from LTBI development with an adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) between 1.03 (95% CI: 1.01–1.06) and 1.48 (95% CI: 1.38–1.58), while O3 exposure was linked to reduced risk with an aHR of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.83–0.89). Subgroup analysis according to baseline LTBI status suggests that air pollution has an impact on active TB risk for both short-term and long-term infections. Additionally, pollutants generally show positive correlations with each other, except for O3, which was negatively correlated with SO2 and CO. With respect to TB risk, the dose–response relationships showed linearity for PM2.5 but significant nonlinearity for PM10, SO2, NO2, CO and O3.DiscussionLong-term exposure to air pollutants increased the risk of active TB development from LTBI, highlighting the importance of air pollution control in LTBI management allowing for regional and demographic differences.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic respiratory infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB). TB remains one of the leading infectious causes of death among adults globally, making its prevention and control an ongoing and urgent priority in the realm of global public health [1]. In 2023, there were an estimated 10.8 million new TB cases worldwide, with about 741 000 of those in China [2]. About one-third of the global population is infected with MTB, potentially leading to future TB cases [3]. Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) is defined as a state of persistent immune response to stimulation by MTB antigens without evidence of clinically manifested active TB [4, 5]. The lifetime risk of developing active TB for a person with documented latent infection is estimated to be 5–10%, with most cases occurring within the first 5 years after initial infection [6]. To reduce TB incidence and transmission, it is crucial to identify the factors that cause LTBI to become active. Research indicates that various factors can increase the risk of developing TB, including comorbidities, malnutrition, diabetes, smoking and alcohol consumption. Additionally, environmental factors also play a significant role [7]. Research has focused on the relationship between air pollution factors and TB incidence. Popovic et al.'s [8] systematic review summarises the relationship between environmental air pollutants and TB. Lai et al.'s [9] study highlights the different impacts of nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) on the risk of developing TB. Furthermore, a study in South Korea indicates that men with TB are more sensitive to increased concentrations of SO2 [10]. Another study in North Carolina suggests a potential association between particulate matter (PM) exposure and TB risk [11]. A study in Lanzhou, China, indicates a positive correlation between particulate matter 10 µm or less (PM10) and active TB incidence [12]. A nationwide study in China reported that both PM10 and particulate matter 2.5 µm or less (PM2.5) are positively correlated with TB incidence, with relative risks of 1.07 and 1.12, respectively [13]. In South Korea, a study showed that each standard deviation increase in PM10 (5.63 µg·m−3) raises TB risk by 20% [14]. However, the possible reasons for these associations remain unclear. One explanation could be that outdoor PM increases the risk of LTBI, which in turn increases the risk of TB. A population-based multicentre prospective cohort study in China shows that long-term air pollution might be an overlooked risk factor for the progression of LTBI to active TB, especially for those living in developing or underdeveloped areas with poor air quality [15]. Research on the risk associated with progressing from LTBI to active TB disease due to long-term exposure to air pollution is currently limited. To fill this gap, we conducted a multicentre cohort study to explore the association between long-term exposure to air pollution and the prevalence of LTBI and active TB disease among populations in rural areas in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is based on a population-based prospective cohort study (LATENTTB-NSTM) organised and implemented in 2013, aimed at elucidating the dynamics of TB infection and its progression to active disease; quarterly follow-ups were carried out also for 10 years to track the development of active disease until the final visit in 2023. During the baseline survey, we conducted active TB screening among participants and recorded any TB history, excluding those with current or past TB from further analysis. We then performed LTBI testing on the remaining individuals and followed up with those confirmed to have LTBI to monitor their progression to active disease. Comprehensive details regarding the target population, study design, organisation and implementation have been elaborated in prior publications [16, 17].

Eligible participants were identified by door-to-door survey. Eligible participants were rural residents aged 5 years and older with household registration or residence permits for the village, who had lived at the study site for at least 6 months in the past year and could complete the study procedures. They had baseline test results and available air pollution data. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy and incomplete data. Therefore, this study identified a population of 21 832 individuals included in the large-scale multicentre MTB infection survey conducted in 2013. After excluding incomplete information (78 individuals) and lost to follow-up (885 individuals), 20 869 individuals were ultimately included in the cohort.

Survey and health examination

Trained investigators collected baseline sociodemographic information, lifestyle habits and health data from study participants using a standardised questionnaire. This included sex, date of birth, education level (categorised into Primary school or lower, Middle school, High school, College or higher) and household income in the year 2013 (classified into <6000 yuan and 6000 yuan or more [18]). Smoking status was categorised as never-smoked or ever-smoked; alcohol consumption was recorded as yes or no. Close contact history with TB patients, including direct relatives, was noted. Immunocompromised status was evaluated based on immune system diseases or treatments with immunosuppressants. Information on type 2 diabetes, pneumonia, chronic lung diseases (e.g., chronic bronchitis or asthma), cardiovascular diseases (heart disease or stroke) and malignancies was collected. Symptoms of TB (cough, sputum, haemoptysis, night sweats) were recorded during surveys. Professional doctors assessed suspected TB symptoms and conducted physical exams to measure height and weight, and calculate body mass index (BMI), classifying it into normal, underweight, overweight or obese. Fasting venous blood samples were taken for lab tests to determine fasting blood glucose levels and type 2 diabetes status, combined with self-reported medical histories. Interferon-γ release assays (IGRA) were also performed using venous blood samples.

The determination of LTBI and active TB

The cohort collected venous blood samples for the IGRA using the QuantiFERON TB Gold In-Tube test. After completing the IGRA test, the Mantoux method was immediately used to inject 0.1 mL of purified protein derivative containing 5 tuberculin units into the left forearm (Xiangrui; Beijing, China) [19]. The induration diameter (the average of the transverse and longitudinal diameters, in millimetres) of the tuberculin reaction was measured and recorded by trained researchers after 48–72 h. This study uses the IGRA results to determine TB infection status, with a test result not less than the cut-off value of 0.35 IU·mL−1 considered positive [17]. Patients with suspected TB symptoms or chest imaging findings were further examined using sputum smear microscopy, sputum culture or GeneXpert tests. Those who tested positive were confirmed as diagnosed cases. All participants suspected of having TB because of clinical symptoms or radiographic abnormalities were referred for diagnosis according to the national guidelines [20]. For patients negative in all three tests, a 2-week diagnostic anti-inflammatory treatment (excluding anti-tuberculosis drugs) was administered. If there was no response, these patients were considered clinical diagnosis cases and retrospectively confirmed based on their response to subsequent anti-tuberculosis treatment. Additionally, we integrated with the cases registered in the Tuberculosis Information Management System. In this study, active TB cases comprised diagnosed cases, clinical diagnosis cases and system-monitored cases.

Estimates of exposure to air pollutants

The air pollutant datasets employ the extensively utilised and comprehensive China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) datasets, which provide high-resolution, long-term, full-coverage data on ground-level atmospheric pollutants across China [21, 22]. The CHAP datasets encompass near-surface air pollutants including suspended PM and gaseous pollutants. For PM2.5 and PM10, the spatial resolution is 1 km, with coefficients of determination (R2) of 0.92 and 0.89, and root mean square errors (RMSEs) of 10.76 µg·m−3 and 21.12 µg·m−3, respectively. Gaseous pollutants such as carbon monoxide (CO), SO2 and NO2 had a spatial resolution of 10 km before 2019, improving to 1 km thereafter; their R2 values are 0.80, 0.84 and 0.84, with RMSEs of 0.29 mg·m−3, 7.99 µg·m−3 and 4.89 µg·m−3, respectively. The ozone (O3) data feature a spatial resolution of 1 km, boasting a 10-fold cross-validation R2 of 0.89 and an RMSE of 15.77 µg·m−3. Owing to the inability to obtain the precise home addresses and specific boundaries of the villages for all participants, the exposure levels at the location of their respective village committees were used as proxies for the participants’ exposure levels. The coordinates of the village committee points were mapped to the pollutant concentration in the grid cells covering their range, which was then matched to each individual. Pollutant exposure was specifically calculated up to the month of disease onset for patients, using monthly averages.

Statistical analysis

We created two sub-cohorts: one included individuals with LTBI at baseline to study the impact of air pollution on the risk of active disease development from LTBI; and the other included individuals without LTBI at baseline to study the impact of air pollution on the risk of MTB infection and thus active TB development during the observation period. Continuous variables with nonnormal distribution were expressed as median (interquartile ranges (IQR)), and normally distributed variables were expressed as mean±SD. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (%) for baseline characteristics. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test. The distribution of exposure levels to various pollutants during the follow-up period was analysed. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to explore the correlations between exposures to different pollutants. We employed Cox regression models with time-dependent covariates to investigate the associations between long-term exposure to various environmental pollutants and incident TB. For each individual, we defined multiple time windows from enrolment to each follow-up visit and calculated the 1-year, 2-year, 5-year and 10-year moving averages of concentrations to determine long-term exposure levels. The crude model was adjusted solely for baseline age and sex. In contrast, the multivariable model included adjustments for a comprehensive set of factors: age, sex, education level, marital status, history of close contact with TB patients, household per capita income, smoking status, BMI, alcohol consumption and history of pulmonary diseases. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed, incorporating covariates that demonstrated significance in univariate analyses (p<0.05). In addition, subgroup analyses were performed according to age group, sex, marital status, BMI, education level, smoking status, alcohol drinking, household per capita income, history of close contact with TB patients and history of family with TB. Sensitivity analyses were restricted to participants aged over 60 years. To evaluate dose–response relationships between air pollutant exposure levels and TB outcomes, we utilised restricted cubic splines within Cox proportional hazards regression models, setting knots at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of air pollution exposure levels. Nonlinearity was assessed using Wald statistics. Covariates in these dose–response models mirrored those used in the preceding multivariable Cox models.

All statistical computations were executed using R version 4.3.3, with two-sided p-values <0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Institute of Pathogen Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Written, informed consent was obtained from all study participants. For the adolescents aged 5–15 years, written, informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians (IPB-2023–35).

Results

This study included 20 869 participants with 185 new TB cases. Table 1 illustrates the baseline characteristics of all participants. Of the total population, 75.3% were under 60 years old. Females comprised 53.0% of participants, but males accounted for 69.7% of new TB cases. Most participants (79.6%) were married or previously married. There were no significant differences in educational level, diabetes and immunological disease history between groups. Regarding the LTBI population, 78.7% had no prior infection, while 72.4% of those who developed new TB cases had prior LTBI infection. Participants were evenly distributed across four sites, but 47.0% of new TB cases were in Hunan. Among individuals with LTBI, 37.7% (n=1674) had ever-smoked. In contrast, among those without LTBI, 22.3% (n=3670) had ever-smoked (supplementary tables S3–S4).

Distribution and correlation of pollutants

Figure 1 illustrates that except O3, concentrations of the remaining five pollutants exhibited a consistent decline across all regions. Notably, Henan recorded the highest levels of PM2.5, while the most significant reductions in PM10 were observed in both Henan and Hunan. The steepest decrease in SO2 was noted in Gansu, whereas NO2 levels displayed considerable variability over time. Association between air pollutants and TB incidence

From 2013 to 2023, TB incidence rates declined overall in four regions (supplementary figure S1). Overall, the trends were consistent with the decline in air pollutants. Tables 2 and 3 illustrate that an increase of 1 μg·m−3 in PM2.5 is associated with a 5% elevated TB risk for non-LTBI populations (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR)=1.05, 95% CI: 1.03–1.07) and a 6% heightened TB risk for LTBI populations (aHR=1.06, 95% CI: 1.05–1.07). Similarly, each 1 μg·m−3 increment in PM10 correlates with a 2% increased TB risk for non-LTBI populations (aHR=1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) and a 3% rise in TB risk for LTBI populations (aHR=1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04). A 1 μg·m−3 elevation in SO2 is linked to a substantial 22% increase in TB risk for non-LTBI populations (aHR=1.22, 95% CI: 1.16–1.29) and a 21% increase in TB risk for LTBI populations (aHR=1.21, 95% CI: 1.17–1.24). Conversely, every 1 μg·m−3 increase in NO2 is associated with a slight 1% reduction in TB risk for non-LTBI populations (aHR=0.99, 95% CI: 0.95–1.02), while it increases the TB risk for LTBI populations by 3% (aHR=1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06). An increase of 0.1 mg·m−3 in CO significantly augments the TB risk for non-LTBI populations by 44% (aHR=1.44, 95% CI: 1.27–1.62) and elevates the TB risk for LTBI populations by 48% (aHR=1.48, 95% CI: 1.38–1.58). In contrast, each 1 μg·m−3 increase in O3 is associated with a notable reduction in TB risk for non-LTBI populations by 20% (aHR=0.80, 95% CI: 0.74–0.86) and a 14% decrease in TB risk for LTBI populations (aHR=0.86, 95% CI: 0.83–0.89). In different subgroups, the hazard ratios (HRs) were similar with slight variations. Air pollution exposure showed a higher impact on TB risk in LTBI populations, particularly in those with BMI ≥28 kg·m−2, with low income or with a history of close contact (supplementary table S5). In addition, the association between air pollution exposure and the risk of TB development from LTBI was found to be consistent across different seasons (supplementary table S6).

Dose–response associations

The relationship between annual average PM2.5 concentration and health risk is approximately linear, though not statistically significant (p=0.084). In contrast, other pollutants such as PM10, SO2, CO, O3 and NO2 demonstrate pronounced nonlinear associations with health risk (figure 2). Specifically, the nonlinear characteristics are highly significant for PM10 (p=0.0027), SO2 (p<0.001), CO (p<0.001), O3 (p<0.001) and NO2 (p=0.0460).

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the association between multiple air pollutants and the risk of developing TB from individuals with LTBI, based on a large sample, multicentre and long-term follow-up cohort. Our results show that chronic exposure to PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2 and CO increases the risk of active TB, while O3 exposure reduces it. PM2.5 shows a linear dose–response relationship and PM10, SO2, CO, O3 and NO2 exhibit significant nonlinear relationships.

Despite numerous studies, the relationship between air pollutants and TB development remains contradictory. For example, Xiang et al. [23] found that long-term exposure to PM10, NO2 and SO2 promotes TB incidence, while the impact of PM2.5, O3 and NO2 on TB development remains unclear. Dimala et al. [24] used a random-effects model to study the relationship between various pollutants and TB incidence, finding that PM2.5, PM10 and CO were positively correlated with TB, while SO2, NO2 and O3 showed no correlation. A study in Urumqi, China, using a generalised additive model, found that PM2.5, SO2, NO2, O3 and CO increase TB incidence, with CO having the greatest impact. PM2.5, SO2, NO2 and CO raised TB incidence after 3 months, while PM10 did so after 9 months. Overall, increased air pollutants raise TB risk [25]. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that PM10 and NO2 contribute to the spread of TB, but the effects of PM2.5, SO2 and O3 remain inconclusive [26]. Our results showed that long-term exposure to pollutants significantly promotes the development of active TB in patients with LTBI. Based on previous research, the link between LTBI and air pollution is not significant, with air pollution primarily affecting the development of active disease after MTB infection [15]. Therefore, clarifying the stage at which air pollution influences the progression from TB infection to active disease is crucial for TB prevention and control. Studying a cohort without LTBI at baseline allows us to observe the entire process of impact on TB from infection to active disease, offering a valuable research opportunity. Since the results of tables 2 and 3 are similar, our study further confirms that air pollution has a significant impact on the progression of LTBI to active TB, whereas its effect on MTB infection itself is not significant. These findings provide important indirect evidence to further support the view that air pollution mainly influences the risk of disease onset from LTBI but not the risk of MTB infection.

The underlying mechanisms may include the following. PM penetrates the respiratory tract, causing health issues like heart and respiratory diseases. Bioaerosols in particulates help transmit airborne pathogens. Inhaling particulates increases infection susceptibility and spreads diseases, leading to more infectious diseases [27]. PM2.5 enhances susceptibility to MTB infection by compromising lung defense mechanisms, triggering oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, and diminishing immune function [28, 29]. PM2.5 exposure-induced inflammation and cytotoxicity in immune cells diminish the expression of critical immune factors (such as interferon-γ and tumour necrosis factor-α), thereby contributing to the progression of TB [30–32]. Increased exposure to ambient pollutants (PM10, PM2.5, SO2 and NO2) contributed to a faster mutation rate of MTB (MTBMR), indicating that MTB exhibits increased reproductive activity, thus accelerating within-host endogenous re-activation [33]. CO can increase the incidence of TB as it weakens the immune system. Some studies suggest that haem oxygenase, produced by CO, has suppressive effects on the body's immune response and inflammation [34, 35]. SO2 may increase TB risk by damaging alveolar macrophages and reducing immune-active substances. Studies show that 30 min of in vitro exposure to 12.5 ppm SO2 causes apoptosis in 62% of alveolar macrophages and a 63% reduction in reactive oxygen species release [10]. Reactive oxygen species are crucial in inhibiting or killing MTB [36]. Some studies have found a significant correlation between atmospheric pollutants (including SO2) and TB incidence [10, 37]. This association may be attributed to the effects of SO2 exposure on the lung defense system. However, some sources suggest a negative correlation between SO2 and TB incidence in areas with low pollution [38]. Low levels of SO2 exposure might cause oxidative damage to lipids and proteins, activating human cells to combat MTB, thereby reducing the risk of TB [38, 39]. Han noted that NO2 exposure inhibits the formation of neutralising antibodies and the phagocytic activity of alveolar macrophages and blood monocytes, disrupting the humoral immune system [40]. Some studies have shown a significant association between NO2 and TB, consistent with our findings [41]. However, research exploring the impact of outdoor air pollution on TB in South Korea found no association between ambient NO2 concentrations and TB incidence [10]. Another study indicated no significant association between daily outpatient visits for TB and daily NO2 concentrations [38]. The observed relationship between O3 concentrations and TB is unsurprising. Studies show that O3 exposure lowers the MTBMR, suggesting MTB's reproductive activity is limited due to suppressed within-host activation [33]. Additionally, this can be explained by the interaction between O3 and NO2. Nitrogen oxides are consumed during the formation of O3 in the presence of sunlight [42]. Consequently, individuals exposed to high NO2 concentrations often experience lower O3 levels, especially in high-traffic areas [43, 44].

Our study provides compelling evidence that there is a close association between air pollutants and the risk of LTBI progressing to active TB. Despite the overall decline in TB incidence, China still bears a significant burden from this disease. Our large-scale, multicentre epidemiological survey estimated an LTBI rate of 18.1% among individuals aged 5 years and older [45]. Over the past decade, different preventive measures and economic development levels in various regions have led to varying rates of TB incidence decline. Despite the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on reported incidence data, our study found that Zhongmu has seen the fastest drop [46, 47], reaching a low prevalence similar to Jiangsu in the last 2 years. In contrast, Hunan's decline has been slower. The higher rates in Hunan are associated with its agricultural economy, large rural population, low economic development, poor health awareness, poverty, malnutrition, hard labour and poor living conditions [48]. Our subgroup analysis shows that air pollutant exposure is more strongly linked to TB incidence risk in nonsmokers with LTBI, which matches findings from some studies [15, 41]. One reason might be that we only analysed outdoor pollutants, and lower levels of these pollutants may be masked in smokers [49]. According to one study, when PM2.5 doses are high, the dose–response curve levels off, which implies that smokers might be approaching their tolerance threshold [50]. Studies are needed to explore this further. Our research highlights the importance of improving air quality in reducing TB incidence. Specifically, enhancing air quality management and controlling emissions of PM, such as PM2.5 and PM10, as well as gaseous pollutants like SO2, NO2 and CO, can significantly reduce the risk of LTBI developing into active TB. Public awareness campaigns are crucial for educating the population about the risks associated with air pollution and TB. Encouraging individuals to take protective measures, such as wearing masks and reducing outdoor activities during high pollution periods, can help mitigate these risks. Moreover, our findings provide scientific support for incorporating air quality management into TB control strategies. Additionally, implementing strict environmental monitoring and health protection measures for high-risk populations should be part of comprehensive efforts to curb the development of TB.

Our study has several limitations, Firstly, the measurement of gaseous pollutant exposure is based on readings taken in the village centre, which does not consider the daily activity ranges and schedules of the study participants. This could lead to exposure misclassification and does not consider indoor exposures, such as those from solid fuel combustion. Future research could utilise personal wearable devices to enhance accuracy. Secondly, even after adjusting for several potential confounders in the multivariate models, residual confounding from unknown and unmeasured variables cannot be ruled out. It is challenging to separate the effects of socioeconomic factors and air pollution exposure, and the cohort might include multiple family members who share similar living environments and behaviours, potentially leading to inherent confounding. Thirdly, for individuals with LTBI at baseline, the exact time of infection cannot be determined, which may introduce potential bias in the relationships derived from the survival analysis. Lastly, previous studies have linked air pollution to TB recurrence and drug resistance. However, due to the limited sample size, we are unable to further investigate the impact of environmental factors on these aspects in greater detail. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

Our study reveals a close association between air pollutants and the risk of active TB development from LTBI, highlighting the importance of improving air quality to LTBI management. Specifically, strengthening the control of PM, such as PM2.5 and PM10, as well as gaseous pollutants like SO2, NO2 and CO, might significantly decrease the risk of active TB development from LTBI. Similar results, although not significant, were achieved for non-LTBI. Common intervention technologies such as environmental governance may have positive effects on the prevention and treatment of respiratory diseases, which also indicates the importance of the one health concept for TB control. Therefore, TB management strategies should be tailored to local conditions, rather than follow the same pattern in different regions.
