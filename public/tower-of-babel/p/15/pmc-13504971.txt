Recommended intravenous infusion rate of sufentanil during induction of general anesthesia to prevent coughing: a randomized, single-blind, prospective clinical trials
Authors: Quanjun Zeng, Yingqing Liu, Guohong Li, Min Zou, Qiaoling Zhou, Zhikun Xu, Jie Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSufentanil-induced cough (SIC) is a prevalent and potentially hazardous reflex during anesthesia induction. While slow injection is known to reduce SIC, the precise dose-response relationship between infusion rate and cough suppression remains poorly defined. This study aimed to identify the optimal intravenous infusion rate of sufentanil to reduce SCI and to estimate the 50% and 95% cough inhibition thresholds (IS₅₀ and IS₉₅) using probit regression analysis.MethodsA total of 70 adult patients (ASA I–II) scheduled for general anesthesia with tracheal intubation were prospectively enrolled and randomized into seven groups according to different sufentanil infusion rates: Group A, 0.20 mL/s; Group B, 0.225 mL/s; Group C, 0.25 mL/s; Group D, 0.275 mL/s; Group E, 0.30 mL/s; Group F, 0.325 mL/s; and Group G, 0.35 mL/s.All patients received sufentanil at a dose of 0.4 µg/kg (concentration: 5 µg/mL). The incidence and severity of coughing, apnea, hypoxemia, and pruritus within 5 min after injection were recorded. Vital signs—including respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation—were measured at baseline and 5 min after drug administration. Cough severity was classified as: none (0 coughs), mild (1–2 coughs), moderate (3–4 coughs), and severe (≥ 5 coughs) during the 5-minute observatiototal period.ResultsThe incidence of SIC significantly decreased as the infusion rate slowed (P < 0.001). The SIC incidence for rates 0.35, 0.325, 0.30, 0.275, 0.25, 0.225, and 0.20 mL/s were 73.3%, 46.7%, 46.7%, 33.3%, 40.0%, 0.0%, and 13.3%, respectively. Using Probit analysis, the estimated IS₅₀ for cough suppression was 0.310 mL/s(95% CI: 0.288–0.347) and the IS₉₅ was 0.172 mL/s (95% CI: 0.069–0.212). No episodes of severe glottic closure or opioid-induced chest wall rigidity were observed in any group.ConclusionsTo minimize sufentanil-induced coughing in adult patients with normal airway anatomy (ASA I–II, body mass index 16–26 kg/m²), an infusion rate of ≤ 0.172 mL/s (approximately 46 s for 0.4 µg/kg at 5 µg/mL) is recommended, which achieves about 95% cough prevention according to probit regression analysis. However, respiratory depression and apnea remained frequent in this cohort, underscoring the need for adequate preoxygenation, continuous pulse oximetry and capnography monitoring, and immediate availability of airway management equipment during induction with sufentanil 0.4 µg/kg.Trial registrationChiCTR2300067455 (Registered on 9 January 2023 ): https://www.chictr.org.cn/.

Introduction

Sufentanil is a potent synthetic opioid commonly used in the perioperative period. However, intravenous bolus administration can induce coughing, with a reported incidence of approximately 6.1% to 61% [1]. Although most episodes are transient and self-limiting, severe coughing can markedly elevate blood pressure, intraocular pressure, intracranial pressure, and intra-abdominal pressure. In patients with a full stomach, it may increase the risk of reflux and aspiration. Such reactions can be life-threatening, particularly in elderly patients or those with significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities [2].

The mechanisms of opioid-induced cough are multifactorial, involving activation of µ-opioid receptors, vagal stimulation, histamine release, and direct airway irritation [3, 4]. Sufentanil exerts its analgesic effects by binding to µ-opioid receptors and activating both G protein‑dependent signaling and β‑arrestin‑biased pathways; the latter is associated with opioid‑related adverse effects such as respiratory depression, nausea and vomiting, constipation, and dependence. The specific binding mode of an agonist to the µ‑opioid receptor determines which intracellular proteins (e.g., G proteins or β‑arrestins) are recruited, thereby modulating downstream signaling through inhibitory or facilitatory mechanisms [5]. Low‑dose opioids produce analgesia via µ‑receptor activation [6] and can also suppress the cough reflex. However, at higher doses, sufentanil may paradoxically provoke coughing [7]. Painful stimuli activate both nociceptive and pruriceptive pathways; pain masks itch through cross‑inhibition in the spinal cord, and itch is typically perceived only when pain is fully relieved [8]. Throat itching can trigger the cough reflex [9]. A clinical study demonstrated that pre‑injection of 5 µg sufentanil significantly reduced the incidence of cough induced by a subsequent 0.4 µg/kg dose [10]. A neurotransmitter‑mediated link exists among cough, itch, and pain [11]. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that low‑dose sufentanil suppresses cough by enhancing the cross‑inhibitory modulation of itch signaling in the spinal cord, whereas high‑dose sufentanil may attenuate this inhibition, thereby provoking coughing.

The pathophysiology of sufentanil-induced cough (SIC) is complex and not fully understood. It has been proposed that a rapid increase in peak plasma and pulmonary concentrations of lipophilic opioids enhances the excitability of rapidly adapting receptors and bronchopulmonary C-fibers, triggering a transient pulmonary–vagal reflex and forceful coughing [1, 12, 13]. After entering the bloodstream, sufentanil partially binds to plasma albumin and is distributed via the systemic circulation, resulting in a lower concentration of free drug available at receptor sites. This pharmacokinetic profile suggests that reducing the plasma concentration near target receptors may decrease the incidence of sufentanil-induced cough. Liu et al. [14] demonstrated that using a Murphy’s drip during induction of general anesthesia prolonged the infusion time and reduced the peak drug concentration, thereby lowering the risk of cough. Chen et al. further confirmed that both the incidence and severity of cough were positively correlated with the sufentanil dose, and that slowing the rate of administration effectively reduced the drug concentration at effector sites, thus attenuating the cough reflex [15]. Furthermore, several pharmacological interventions, such as lidocaine, dexmedetomidine, and esketamine, have been investigated for their potential to suppress sufentanil-induced cough [16–18]; however, the optimal infusion strategy for sufentanil remains underexplored. To further clarify the relationship between the sufentanil infusion rate and the incidence of cough suppression, and to determine the infusion rate that achieves cough suppression in 95% of subjects, we conducted a randomized, single-blind, single-center, prospective clinical trial.

Methods and materials

Study setting and population

This was a prospective, parallel-group, single-blind, randomized, single-center clinical trial. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences Shenzhen Hospital (approval numbers LL-KT-2022089 and LL-KT-091) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on 9 January 2023 (registration number ChiCTR2300067455). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. The trial was conducted from January 2023 to October 2023 at Shenzhen Hospital of the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences and included patients undergoing general anesthesia with tracheal intubation.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18–65 years; (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II; (3) body mass index (BMI) 16–26 kg/m²; (4) scheduled for general anesthesia with tracheal intubation; and (5) willing to participate and able to provide written informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were: anticipated difficult airway (difficult mask ventilation or Mallampati class III or IV); respiratory disease (including upper respiratory tract infection, pneumonia, bronchial asthma, chronic bronchitis, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease); abnormal hepatic or renal function (values more than twice the upper limit of normal); open ocular trauma; intracranial hypertension; known allergy to anesthetic agents; and recent use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, opioids, or bronchodilators. Patients were also withdrawn from the study if they requested to withdraw, did not cooperate with postoperative follow-up, experienced serious adverse events precluding continued participation, or had incomplete trial data.

Pre-testing and sample size estimation

Previous studies have reported that intravenous infusion of sufentanil at 0.4 µg/kg over more than 20 s significantly reduces the incidence of cough [19]. Based on this, seven groups with different infusion rates were designed: group A (0.2 mL/s), group B (0.225 mL/s), group C (0.25 mL/s), group D (0.275 mL/s), group E (0.3 mL/s), group F (0.325 mL/s), and group G (0.35 mL/s). A pre‑test was conducted using the same procedures as the formal study, except that group allocation was not randomized. The pre‑test results showed cough suppression rates of 100% in groups A and B, 60% in group C, 80% in group D, 40% in group E, 60% in group F, and 0% in group G.The primary endpoint was the incidence of cough suppression, with the goal of achieving suppression in 95% of patients by slow intravenous injection. This increase from the minimal theoretical requirement (n ≈ 4 per group) was made to improve the precision of probit regression estimates and the robustness of between‑group comparisons, using a two‑sided α of 0.05.

Randomization and blinding

Patients who met the inclusion criteria were assigned sequential numbers according to admission order, and 70 patients were then randomly allocated to seven groups of 10 using computer-generated random numbers. Simple randomization without blocking was used because the study involved multiple groups and a fixed total sample size. A single researcher prepared the study medication, calculated the infusion stop time, sealed the medication in opaque envelopes, and labeled each envelope with the assigned infusion rate and stop time. During anesthesia induction, the infusion pump was programmed according to the envelope label. Outcome assessors recorded the incidence of cough and any adverse events. The random sequence generator, participant recruiter, and intervention assigner were three separate researchers. Participants were blinded to their group assignment and the specifics of the administered drug.

Intervention and anesthesia protocol

To maintain drug stability, sufentanil was prepared within 30 min before anesthesia induction. A 100 µg dose of sufentanil was drawn into a 50‑mL syringe and diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 20 mL, yielding a concentration of 5 µg/mL. The total dose for each patient was calculated as 0.4 µg/kg based on body weight. The volume to be administered and the infusion rate were determined according to the group‑specific randomization schedule. The resulting infusion duration was recorded. Any remaining sufentanil was either used for postoperative patient‑controlled analgesia (PCA) or discarded.

Preoperative assessment included history taking, physical examination, and review of the electronic medical record. Written informed consent for the study and for anesthesia was obtained from all participants. All subjects received preoperative fasting instructions in accordance with standard guidelines: abstinence from food for at least 8 h and from clear liquids for 4 h before surgery. After a three‑person verification of medications and equipment, a 20‑G peripheral intravenous catheter (maximum flow rate 45 mL/min) was placed by the circulating nurse. Blood samples were drawn, and intravenous patency was confirmed by the absence of resistance to flushing. Each patient then received 500 mL of lactated Ringer’s solution infused at the maximal drip rate. The patient was placed in the supine position and connected to standard monitors. Non‑invasive blood pressure was measured on the upper arm contralateral to the infusion site, pulse oximetry was applied to a finger on the infusion side, and lead II electrocardiography was used to monitor heart rate and respiratory rate. All vital signs were recorded at 1‑minute intervals.

The infusion pump was pre‑programmed with the patient’s age, height, and weight and placed in standby mode. For Group G, two infusion pumps were prepared. Oxygen was administered via face mask at 6 L/min for denitrogenation. Non‑invasive blood pressure, oxygen saturation (SpO₂), electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate (HR), and respiratory rate (RR) were recorded at 1‑minute intervals, beginning 5 min before and continuing until 5 min after drug administration. The prepared sufentanil solution was connected to the venous access through an extension tube and a three‑way stopcock. The infusion line was primed beforehand to ensure no air remained in the tubing. The lactated Ringer’s infusion was temporarily interrupted during drug administration. The infusion pump was set to deliver sufentanil at a constant rate (mL/h), based on the subject’s group assignment and the calculated infusion duration. Once the infusion was complete, the researcher manually stopped the pump and immediately resumed the lactated Ringer’s infusion at the maximal drip rate. Throughout the procedure, trained observers recorded the number and timing of cough episodes, as well as any occurrences of apnea, pruritus, or other adverse events.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence and severity of cough occurring within 5 min after drug administration. Cough severity was graded by the number of coughs: none (0), mild (1–2 coughs), moderate (3–4 coughs), and severe (≥ 5 coughs), as previously described [15]. Cough latency was defined as the time from the completion of drug administration to the onset of the first cough, excluding the duration of injection. Secondary outcomes included apnea, defined as the absence of chest wall movement with a flat respiratory waveform on the monitor; hypoxemia, defined as a peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) below 90%; and pruritus, defined as either a subjective report of itching or visible scratching. Hemodynamic parameters, including non‑invasive systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, were monitored continuously and recorded at baseline and at 1‑minute intervals for 5 min after administration. Mean values for the pre‑administration period (T1–5) and post‑administration period (T1′–5′) were calculated from the data retrieved through the monitor’s review function.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0). Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed continuous variables and ordinal data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Repeated-measures data were evaluated using repeated-measures ANOVA when the assumption of normality was met; otherwise, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were applied. The Bonferroni correction was used for all pairwise and multiple comparisons. Probit regression was used to model the probability of being cough-free as a function of sufentanil infusion rate, yielding the infusion rates required for 50% and 95% cough suppression (IS₅₀ and IS₉₅) and their 95% confidence intervals; model goodness-of-fit was assessed with the Pearson chi-square test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Recruitment was discontinued once the planned sample size was reached. A total of 106 patients who underwent general anesthesia with tracheal intubation were enrolled. Of these, 36 participated in a pilot phase, during which one participant withdrew owing to difficult intravenous access after drug administration. Subsequently, 70 patients were prospectively enrolled and randomly assigned to seven groups (A–G) in order of admission, with 10 participants initially allocated to each group. Ultimately, 15 patients per group were included in the statistical analysis; for each group, the denominator used in the analysis was the number of participants originally assigned to that group. Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the relationship between the rate of sufentanil infusion and the incidence and severity of sufentanil‑induced cough during induction of general anesthesia. Pharmacological strategies for preventing sufentanil‑induced cough (SIC) have been extensively investigated; recent systematic reviews, network meta‑analyses, and clinical studies have shown that nalbuphine, dezocine, butorphanol, lidocaine, ketamine, esketamine, and dexmedetomidine all significantly reduce SIC [19–23]. However, prophylactic medications carry their own side effects and add to medical costs. The non‑pharmacological approach of optimizing the infusion rate has remained underexplored, and our trial helps fill this evidence gap.

Our findings suggest that, at a fixed dose of sufentanil (0.4 µg/kg), the incidence of SIC is strongly rate‑dependent rather than dose‑dependent. Slower infusion rates (e.g., ≤ 0.172 mL/s at a concentration of 5 µg/mL, a value within the range examined in our cohort [0.069–0.212 mL/s]) likely maintain the transient pulmonary concentration of sufentanil below the threshold required to trigger the pulmonary–vagal reflex and activate airway cough receptors. Based on probit regression analysis, we recommend maintaining the sufentanil infusion rate at or below 0.172 mL/s to minimize the risk of cough. From a practical standpoint, a slower infusion rate does not necessarily prolong the overall induction sequence. At a dose of 0.4 µg/kg and a concentration of 5 µg/mL, an infusion rate ≤ 0.172 mL/s translates to an administration time of approximately 40–60 s, which can be readily integrated into the standard 3–5‑minute preoxygenation period. Thus, slow‑rate sufentanil infusion can be implemented during mask preoxygenation without delaying tracheal intubation or compromising operating room efficiency.

The mechanisms underlying sufentanil‑induced cough are complex and have not been fully elucidated. They may involve multiple factors, including µ‑opioid receptor activation, vagal stimulation, the citrate buffer used as a pharmaceutical excipient, histamine release, increased chest wall muscle tone, and direct airway irritation [24]. During induction of anesthesia, airway patency is typically maintained by suctioning or manual ventilation, and avoiding cough is clinically desirable to ensure procedural safety and patient comfort. After intravenous administration, sufentanil distributes throughout the body and partially binds to plasma albumin, leading to a gradual decline in free drug concentration. Slower administration results in a lower peak plasma concentration, thereby reducing the degree of µ‑receptor engagement [25]. According to the biased agonism hypothesis at the µ‑receptor, this may favor signaling pathways less likely to induce cough, thus attenuating the overall cough response. This pharmacokinetic explanation is supported by studies showing that slower injection of lipophilic opioids is associated with fewer adverse respiratory events [26]. Interestingly, we observed that some patients exhibited delayed coughing or prolonged intervals between coughs. This may be attributable to a pharmacokinetic lag imposed by the blood–brain barrier, which delays drug action at central nervous system receptors. We hypothesize that early‑onset cough results from peripheral receptor activation in the lungs, whereas delayed cough is triggered by central receptor involvement.

In this study, non‑invasive systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) were compared before and after induction across the seven groups; no statistically significant differences were observed between groups (P > 0.05). However, within‑group analysis revealed a significant decrease in respiratory rate following sufentanil administration, indicating notable respiratory depression at a dose of 0.4 µg/kg. Therefore, close monitoring of respiratory function and oxygen saturation is recommended when this dose of sufentanil is administered, and the drug should be given only with appropriate airway management equipment and trained personnel immediately available. The observed respiratory depression is consistent with the known dose‑dependent effects of sufentanil on respiratory drive [26].

Although respiratory rate decreased significantly, oxygen saturation remained stable—a finding that may be attributed to the 5‑minute preoxygenation with 100% oxygen before drug administration. This underscores the importance of adequate preoxygenation in preventing hypoxemia during opioid‑induced respiratory depression. Notably, a significant reduction in diastolic blood pressure was observed in Groups A and B, which received sufentanil at infusion rates of 0.20 and 0.225 mL/s, respectively (P < 0.05). One possible explanation is that sufentanil induces peripheral vasodilation via vagal activation. In contrast, no significant changes in diastolic blood pressure were noted in the other five groups. This may be explained by the occurrence of coughing in those groups, which could have transiently elevated blood pressure and thereby counteracted the vasodilatory effect of sufentanil. Overall, sufentanil at 0.4 µg/kg had minimal impact on hemodynamic parameters under the conditions of this study.

In assessing the safety and tolerability of sufentanil, several adverse events (AEs) were observed, including apnea, respiratory depression, pruritus, and suspected allergic reactions. The incidence of apnea across the study population ranged from 20% to 53.3%, representing a considerable safety concern. In one case, a patient developed hypoxemia, with oxygen saturation dropping to a critical level of 84% despite receiving 100% oxygen at 6 L/min via face mask; such an event, if not promptly recognized and managed, could have resulted in a life‑threatening outcome. Sufentanil is the primary agent used to attenuate the stress response to tracheal intubation but can cause apnea. An insufficient dose, however, may fail to suppress airway reflexes and increase the risk of difficult intubation. Sufentanil‑induced respiratory depression can be readily managed with positive‑pressure mask ventilation or immediate intubation; therefore, this dose remains recommended for patients undergoing general anesthesia with elective endotracheal intubation.

Based on these findings, we do not recommend using this dose of sufentanil for postoperative analgesia without appropriate clinical monitoring and ward‑based observation. This precaution is especially important for elderly patients and those with compromised respiratory function, who may be more susceptible to opioid‑induced respiratory depression [27].

Severe pruritus with involuntary scratching was noted in four patients. Although persistent and difficult to relieve, these reactions were not accompanied by urticaria or abnormal hemodynamic changes, suggesting that the pruritus was not allergic in origin. This is consistent with previous data indicating that sufentanil has a low propensity to induce histamine release or allergic reactions [10]. Nonetheless, clinicians should remain vigilant for rare but serious hypersensitivity reactions [28].

This study has several limitations. First, in order to isolate and better evaluate coughing and other AEs caused by sufentanil, no additional medications were administered to suppress coughing, thereby avoiding confounding effects and unnecessary medical costs or complications.Future studies could explore combined strategies using slow injection plus pretreatment with antitussive agents [3].However, all patients received high-flow oxygen via face mask prior to induction, and vital signs were continuously monitored to allow timely identification and management of any adverse reactions. Second, this was a single-center study conducted over approximately one year. Environmental variables such as altitude, latitude, or seasonal changes were not accounted for, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or geographic settings.Further multicenter studies would be valuable to validate these findings across diverse patient populations and clinical settings.This study utilized a single-blind design due to the practical difficulty of blinding the anesthesiologist to pump settings. However, the use of an objective, physical endpoint (cough count) significantly mitigates potential observer bias.

Conclusion

To minimize sufentanil-induced coughing during general anesthesia induction in adults with normal airway anatomy (ASA I–II, body mass index 16–26 kg/m²), an intravenous infusion rate of ≤ 0.172 mL/s is recommended for sufentanil 0.4 µg/kg at a concentration of 5 µg/mL, which corresponds to an infusion duration of approximately 46 s and was associated with about 95% cough prevention in this study population. Given the substantial incidence of respiratory depression and apnea observed across all infusion rates, adequate preoxygenation with 100% oxygen, continuous pulse oximetry and capnography monitoring, and immediate availability of airway management equipment and trained personnel are essential when using this dosing regimen.
