Person-centered maternity care: adaptation and validation of a tool to measure person-centered maternity care in Sri Lanka
Authors: Mohamed Rishard, MSD Wijesinghe, D. Samaranayake, H. Senanayake, M. Lazzerini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPerson-centered maternity care (PCMC) is recognized as a crucial component in improving maternal health outcomes by addressing patients’ experiences. Although Sri Lanka has made progress in reducing maternal mortality, validated tools to assess PCMC are lacking, limiting the understanding of care quality from the patient’s perspective. This study aimed to adapt and validate a PCMC scale specifically for Sri Lanka’s context.MethodsThe PCMC scale, originally validated in Kenya, Ghana, and India, was adapted through expert reviews and cognitive interviews, followed by psychometric testing. The tool was translated into Sinhala and Tamil. Sixteen experts evaluated the tool for content validity, and 20 postpartum women provided feedback on its contextual relevance. Psychometric analysis included exploratory factor analysis and Cronbach’s alpha to establish construct validity and reliability of the scale.ResultsA total of 400 postpartum women, with a mean age of 27.3 years (SD = 5.51), participated in the study. Multivariate normality checks revealed that all items except Q2, Q14, and Q22 fell within acceptable skewness and kurtosis ranges. The KMO measure of sampling adequacy ranged from 0.512 to 0.891 (overall KMO = 0.811), indicating suitability for factor analysis. Items Q11, Q17, and Q18 had borderline adequacy but were retained. Using principal axis factoring with Oblimin rotation, two factors were extracted based on the Kaiser criterion. Factor 1, named respectful and supportive care, included 14 items (Cronbach’s α = 0.788). Factor 2, communication, autonomy, and privacy, included ten items (Cronbach’s α = 0.708). The refined 24-item scale demonstrated good internal consistency and reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.813). The factor structure reflects the tool’s construct validity, aligning with the goal of capturing the dimensions of respectful maternity care while addressing contextual and cultural factors.ConclusionsThe Sri Lanka-specific PCMC scale provides a culturally tailored measure to assess the experience of care, addressing a crucial gap in maternal health evaluations. This tool enables healthcare providers and policymakers to identify areas for quality improvement in maternity services, aligning with the WHO recommendations for respectful, person-centered care. Future research should explore the utility of this tool in diverse settings in Sri Lanka.

Introduction

Many women die from pregnancy- and childbirth-related causes worldwide. Most contributions come from low- and middle-income countries. A significant proportion of these deaths occurred due to the poor quality of care received by women [1]. Therefore, low- and middle-income countries have increased their focus on improving the quality of care received by pregnant women [2].

According to Tunçalp [3], quality of care can be defined as “efficient, effective, equitable, timely, safe, and person-centered care provided to patients.” To improve the quality of care, focusing on person-centered maternity care (PCMC) is crucial. PCMC refers to care that is respectful and responsive to individual women’s preferences, needs, and values, ensuring their autonomy, dignity, and emotional support throughout childbirth. The core dimensions include effective communication, respect, dignity, privacy, informed choice, and supportive care, fundamentally shifting the focus towards the woman’s experience alongside clinical safety [4]. The World Health Organization (WHO) strongly advocates PCMC as essential for achieving a positive childbirth experience [5]. PCMC has been highlighted in the WHO’s recommendations for positive childbirth experiences (WHO recommendations: Intrapartum care for positive childbirth experiences) [5]. Recent research has shown that better PCMC is associated with improved maternal and neonatal outcomes [6]. Furthermore, most published studies have reported that patient experience is positively associated with clinical effectiveness and safety [7].

Despite being a lower-middle-income country in South Asia, Sri Lanka has been able to track records to achieve a major reduction in the maternal mortality rate (MMR) over the past 60 years, from 1694/100,000 in 1947 to one of the lowest rates in South Asia [8]. Although these milestones have been achieved over the past decade, the MMR has remained steady at 31–39 deaths per 100,000 live births. Therefore, there are criticisms regarding the quality of maternal health in Sri Lanka [9], with suboptimal practices at the community and hospital levels contributing to 38% of maternal deaths in 2017 [8].

Although there is a significant emphasis on improving the quality of maternity care services in Sri Lanka, there is a lack of validated instruments for assessing PCMC in the country. Person-centred/respectful maternity care can be measured with several tools (e.g., RMC [10], MORi [11] MADM [12]), but the PCMC scale [4] is the most comprehensive, explicitly aligned with WHO’s experience-of-care standards, effective communication, respect/dignity, and emotional support while also operationalizing supportive care for routine monitoring. This tool measures PCMC and consists of 30/31 items validated in Kenya, Ghana, India, and many other developed countries. Rishard et al. [9] used this tool to assess the experience of PCMC and its correlation with the provision of care and women’s satisfaction in their study. However, this approach has not been formally validated for use in Sri Lanka yet. Validating this tool formally will help researchers quantitatively measure women’s childbirth experiences across different settings in the country, which will help providers plan future interventions and understand the determinants and consequences of poor PCMC [4]. Therefore, this study aimed to validate the PCMC instrument developed by Afulani et al. [4] for Sri Lanka to measure PCMC in the country.

Methods

We used a standard process to develop the scale. Since the construct of PCMC has been well defined and relevant items have been identified via a proper process of item generation elsewhere by Afulani et al. [4], we skipped the steps of literature review and item generation. The original PCMC version was validated in Kenya, Ghana, and India and served as the basis for the Sri Lankan version. The face and content validities of the scale were assessed. Expert reviews were conducted to assess content validity, and cognitive interviews were conducted using the original scale. The instrument was translated prior to this study. It was translated into two local languages, Sinhala and Tamil. Back-translation was performed to ensure consistency. To ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural appropriateness, we established two independent translation teams for the Sinhala and Tamil versions of this tool. Each team comprised a medical professional with clinical experience in maternity care and a language expert fluent in both English and the respective local languages. After the initial translation, independent back-translation was performed by different bilingual experts with no prior involvement in the initial translation. Discrepancies were reviewed and resolved through consensus among the research team, ensuring the technical accuracy and cultural sensitivity of the final versions.

Expert review

Sixteen experts reviewed the instruments for relevance and comprehensiveness. The panel consisted of six obstetricians, four midwives, two administrators, and four community physicians. They reviewed the 30-item instrument and checked its applicability in the Sri Lankan context. An expert review was conducted to establish content validity in terms of relevance and comprehensiveness [13].

Cognitive interviews

We conducted 20 cognitive interviews with women in the immediate postnatal period (within 24–48 h after delivery, prior to discharge from the hospital). Cognitive interviews were used to determine how participants internalized the items in the instrument, such as the items being interpreted as intended, the wording of the items, and the relevance, context, and length of the instrument [4, 13]. The interviews were conducted in the native language and translated into English. The women provided a few suggestions for improving the tool.

Psychometric analysis

The objective of the psychometric analysis was to ascertain the construct validity and reliability of the tool. A unidirectional Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3 was used. The value 0 indicated never, 1 indicated a few times, 2 indicated most of the time, and 3 indicated all of the time. Negative items were reverse-coded. We used exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess the tool’s construct validity. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) criterion was used to assess the suitability of the items for the factor analysis. We considered a KMO value above 0.5, which is suitable for factor analysis [14]. We selected exploratory factor analysis over confirmatory factor analysis for psychometric validation, as EFA is the recommended first step when adapting scales to novel cultural contexts in which the underlying factor structure is unknown. This approach avoids imposing ill-fitting external constructs and instead identifies emergent dimensions that reflect the local setting [15, 16]. Given that the PCMC scale has never been validated in Sri Lanka’s unique maternity system, EFA allowed the detection of culturally irrelevant items that required removal, which is a critical advantage over CFA’s pre-specified modeling [17].

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the De Soysa Hospital for Women in Colombo, Sri Lanka. The sample size for factor analysis was estimated to be at least 10 subjects per item on the scale [18]. A convenience sample of 400 postpartum women who delivered vaginally at De Soysa Hospital were consecutively recruited for the study. The PCMC tool was administered via face-to-face interviews by trained, independent female data collectors, ensuring standardization and minimizing potential response bias. All interviews were conducted in Sinhala or Tamil, following predefined standard operating procedures. Both the interviewers’ outfit/uniform and her identification card clearly identified her as a nonstaff member. The interviewer introduced herself and the objective of the interview and clarified that the interviews were anonymous. The interview was conducted in a separate area with appropriate privacy.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Eigenvalues higher than one were retained in the factor solutions [19]. We used a scree plot to determine the final factors [20]. As we hypothesized a correlation between these factors, oblique rotation was applied. Cronbach’s alpha values greater than 0.7 are considered sufficient [21]. We used Jamovi software version 2.5 for data analysis [22].

Results

The mean age of the participants was 27.3 (SD = 5.51) years. The majority had an education level above the G.C.E. O/L (38.8%, n = 155), and 46% (n = 184) were Sinhala. Many were unemployed (n = 326, 81.5%). The predominant parity was parity 1 (43.5%, n = 174) (Table 1).

The content validity and clarity of the instrument were established through expert reviews and cognitive interviews. A panel of 16 experts, including six obstetricians, four midwives, two administrators, and four community physicians, reviewed the 30-item instrument for its relevance and comprehensiveness in the Sri Lankan context. Their feedback confirmed the tool’s applicability, ensuring that the items aligned with local clinical and cultural settings.

In addition, 20 cognitive interviews were conducted with women in the immediate postnatal period to assess how well the participants understood and interpreted the items. These interviews, which were conducted in the native language and later translated into English, revealed that the women generally interpreted the items as intended by the researchers. However, a few suggestions were provided to improve the wording, relevance, and clarity of specific items to enhance their appropriateness and comprehensibility. The cognitive interviews emphasized the unique sociocultural context of Sri Lanka. For instance, avoiding medical jargon and direct questions about emotions or physical abuse align with local norms of modesty, while rephrasing ‘autonomy’ reflects linguistic preferences. This participatory refinement ensured that the tool captured locally meaningful constructs of respectful care. The combined results of the expert review and cognitive interviews contributed to refining the instrument, ensuring its suitability for the target population.

The multivariate normality of the items was checked, and we analyzed the kurtosis and skewness of the items, except for items 2 (kurtosis = 198, skewness = 13.6), 14 (kurtosis = 9.18, skewness = 2.86), and 22 (kurtosis = 8.02, skewness=−2.85). All other items were within the normal range. We dropped items 2, 14, and 22 due to high kurtosis and skewness, considering the cutoffs given in the reference texts [16, 23]. The second item refers to the self-introduction of healthcare providers, which is not a common practice in Sri Lanka. Therefore, most participants scored this as ‘0.’ Item 14 refers to talking about feelings that are not normal among women in Sri Lanka; therefore, most participants scored this item as ‘0.’ Item 22 refers to physical abuse, to which many women responded with ‘0.’ The KMO of the sampling adequacy for all the items ranged from 0.512 to 0.891, with an overall value of 0.811, indicating the suitability of the data for factor analysis. The items Q18-delivery support (0.512), Q17-labor support (0.547), and Q11-language (0.561) were close to the cutoff, suggesting borderline adequacy.

We used the principal axis factor technique with Oblimin rotation, as we expected the factors to be correlated. The factors were extracted based on Kaiser’s criteria, where the factors obtained were those above the eigenvalue of 1 [24]. The initial eigenvalues indicated that two factors were retained, following the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues >1). Factor 1 has an eigenvalue of 4.81, and Factor 2 has an eigenvalue of 1.24, together explaining 22.91% of the total variance (Factor 1: 13.36%, Factor 2: 9.55%). The scree plot supports this two-factor solution, showing a clear drop in eigenvalues after the second factor (Fig. 1). The remaining eigenvalues were less than 1, suggesting that these factors did not account for sufficient variance to be retained. The factor loadings in the analysis represent the strength of the relationship between each variable and the extracted factors, with only loadings of 0.3 or higher displayed for clarity [16]. Variables such as Q1 (time to care), Q3 (called by name), and Q26 (crowded) presented very low loadings (< 0.3). When loadings below 0.3 were excluded, the analysis focused on variables with meaningful contributions to the factor structure, simplifying the interpretation and enhancing the clarity of the underlying constructs. Items Q1 (time to care) and Q3 (called by name) may not be relevant in the situation since every mother was attended to immediately, and calling by name is not a usual Sri Lankan practice. Item Q26 (crowded) may not be applicable in this instance since the study was conducted in a tertiary care institution where the number of beds was adequate and the labor rooms were spacious. This analysis aligns with the goal of identifying meaningful underlying constructs in data. Therefore, 14 items loaded on factor one, and 10 items loaded on factor two. We named the first factor “respectful and supportive care” and the second factor “communication, autonomy and privacy”. The 24-item PCMC scale had good internal consistency and reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.813). The Cronbach’s alpha values for the factors were 0.788 (respectful and supportive care) and 0.708 (communication, autonomy, and privacy) (Table 2). We recommend reporting the total PCMC score as a principal metric.

Discussion

The validation of the PCMC tool for Sri Lanka provides significant advancements in measuring the quality of maternity services within the country’s unique cultural and healthcare contexts. This study builds on previous validations of the PCMC tool in other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as Kenya and Ghana, but adapts and refines the tool specifically for Sri Lanka’s healthcare setting.

Comparison with previous validations

Prior research on PCMC measurement, particularly the studies by Afulani et al. [2, 4] in Kenya, Ghana, and India, identified the core dimensions of respectful and person-centered care, including communication, dignity, autonomy, and supportive care​. Studies have shown that incorporating these elements into maternity care can increase patient satisfaction, encourage facility-based births, and improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes [6]. However, while these core dimensions are broadly applicable across LMICs, variations in cultural, linguistic, and healthcare service delivery settings often require the adaptation of such tools to reflect the specific social dynamics and care expectations of different countries.

The factors in our study differ from those in the Afulani et al. [4] and Afulani et al. [2] studies, primarily in terms of their thematic scope and item composition. In our study, three items, Q1 (time to care), Q3 (called by name), and Q26 (crowded), failed to load strongly on any of the factors. This is likely due to the low relevance of these items to the tertiary care setting in which this study was conducted. Furthermore, items Q3 (called by name) and Q26 (crowded) have been shown to have low factor loadings in the initial tool development study conducted in Kenya [4]. Although we had to drop item Q22 related to physical abuse due to high kurtosis and skewness, a study conducted in Ghana, Guinea, Myanmar, and Nigeria revealed that more than one-third of women experienced mistreatment during childbirth in health facilities. Physical abuse, such as slapping, hitting, or pinching, was reported by 14% of women [25]. This has also been reported in state health institutions in Sri Lanka [26, 27].

The first factor of our study, respectful and supportive care, combines interpersonal care aspects, such as being treated with respect and trust, with environmental factors such as cleanliness, water availability, and electricity availability. In contrast, Afulani et al. [4] and Afulani et al. [2] focused narrowly on interpersonal care, excluding operational elements, resulting in more cohesive constructs. Similarly, the factor on communication, autonomy, and privacy integrates items related to communication (e.g., explain exams/procedures) with physical support items such as labor support and delivery support. This differs from other studies that maintain a clear distinction by focusing solely on communication and patient involvement, avoiding overlap with physical or emotional care.

The inclusion of Q6 (visual privacy) and Q7 (record confidentiality) in communication and autonomy is justified by their theoretical alignment with privacy as a facilitator of effective communication and decision-making. These items indirectly support dignity but are primarily operational mechanisms for empowering patient autonomy and fostering trust in communication processes. Similarly, including Q17 (labor support) and Q18 (delivery support) in the Communication, Autonomy, and Privacy factor is justified because these items emphasize communication, collaboration, and patient involvement during labor and delivery processes. These aspects align more closely with autonomy and decision-making than with general physical or emotional care. This categorization improves the interpretability of the tool and better targets the constructs measured by communication and autonomy.

These differences may arise from the broader scope of our study, which aims to capture both interpersonal and environmental dimensions, whereas other studies prioritized narrower, thematically focused constructs. The two-factor structure in our study offers advantages in terms of simplicity, reliability, and practical usability compared with the three-factor structure in Afulani et al. [2, 4]. By consolidating overlapping themes and focusing on broader constructs, our study provides a clearer and more streamlined approach for research and real-world applications. The moderate inter-factor correlation further supports the decision to use the two factors, indicating that the items overlapped. However, the three-factor structure may offer finer granularity for studies requiring a more detailed supportive care analysis.

Moreover, the cognitive interviews conducted in this study provided insights similar to those in the validation study by Naito et al. [28] in Cambodia, where the local context influenced the interpretation and relevance of the PCMC items​. The interviews revealed that Sri Lankan women emphasized the need for healthcare providers to respect their cultural practices and ensure emotional support from family members, reinforcing that PCMC is deeply context specific [9]. This further emphasizes the need for a Sri Lankan-specific PCMC tool that can capture culturally embedded expectations more accurately than the direct adaptation of tools used in other countries.

Unique value of PCMC tool validation for Sri Lanka

The adapted PCMC tool for Sri Lanka holds unique value as it addresses both the nation’s maternal health challenges and its cultural sensitivity to maternity care. Unlike many other LMICs, Sri Lanka has achieved significant milestones in reducing maternal mortality rates over the last six decades. Despite this progress, maternal mortality has plateaued in recent years, with ongoing concerns regarding the quality of maternity care [8]​. Approximately 38% of maternal deaths in 2017 were linked to gaps in community- and facility-based care quality, highlighting the need for tools that not only measure clinical outcomes but also assess women’s experiences of care to identify specific areas for improvement [8, 29].

The incorporation of the PCMC tool into Sri Lanka’s healthcare system offers several practical benefits. First, it provides a systematic means of identifying and addressing deficiencies in respectful, person-centered care across diverse healthcare facilities. For example, tools validated in Pakistan by Hameed et al. [30] demonstrated that even when clinical care may be sufficient, gaps in communication and autonomy can significantly impact women’s perceptions of care quality​. Similarly, the Sri Lankan PCMC tool can help healthcare providers recognize nonclinical aspects that affect patient satisfaction and encourage increased utilization of facility-based care, which is critical for reducing maternal morbidity and mortality in the long term.

Furthermore, the adapted PCMC tool supports Sri Lanka’s health policy objectives by providing a quantitative measure that aligns with the WHO’s recommendations on intrapartum care, which emphasizes person-centered approaches to improve maternal experiences​ [3, 5]. This tool facilitates the identification of best practices and promotes targeted quality improvement initiatives by enabling healthcare administrators to systematically assess and compare PCMC levels across facilities. The Sri Lanka-specific data generated through this tool can inform national maternal health policies, making it a valuable resource for both clinical and policy stakeholders.

Addressing cultural sensitivities and expanding research in Sri Lanka

The cultural and linguistic adaptations included in this study make this tool particularly valuable in Sri Lanka. For example, the translation of the PCMC scale into Sinhala and Tamil ensures accessibility across major language groups, increasing its applicability in diverse clinical settings. As observed in other studies, such as Waddell et al.’s [31] study on shared decision-making in maternity care, adapting tools to the local cultural context is critical for promoting inclusive, respectful and effective maternal care. This culturally adapted tool allows for a more nuanced understanding of maternity care experiences among Sri Lankan women, enabling healthcare providers to offer care that aligns with patients’ expectations and cultural needs.

Moreover, this tool lays the foundation for further research to understand the nonclinical factors influencing maternal health outcomes in Sri Lanka. Studies such as those by Afulani et al. [32] on the Caring for Providers to Improve Patient Experience (CPIPE) intervention highlight the potential for improving both patient experience and clinical outcomes through integrated approaches that address provider well-being and patient-centered care. A Sri Lanka-specific PCMC tool opens avenues for similar research by exploring how factors such as provider burnout, resource limitations, and implicit biases affect the quality of maternity care.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include challenges in generalizability, data collection methods, and scope compared with similar validations in other settings. The sample was limited to a specific teaching hospital, potentially affecting its applicability across Sri Lanka, unlike other broader studies that included various rural and urban settings in Kenya, Ghana, and India​ [4, 32]. Its cross-sectional design limits longitudinal reliability, as shown in studies such as that of Naito et al. [28], which emphasizes the importance of capturing changes over time. Reliance on self-reported data introduces a possible recall bias, and including provider perspectives could mitigate this bias, as Afulani et al. [33] demonstrated by examining biases from multiple viewpoints​. Although valuable, the tool’s focus on resource availability may overlook the systemic quality factors highlighted by Akachi and Kruk [34]. As this is a preliminary validation, we conducted exploratory factor analysis only; we did not perform confirmatory factor analysis, which limits firm conclusions about the latent structure and comparability across subgroups. Future studies should undertake CFA and multi-group invariance testing.

Conclusions and recommendations

In conclusion, the validation of the PCMC tool for Sri Lanka is a significant step in addressing the country’s unique maternal health needs. By critically building on previous studies and incorporating Sri Lanka-specific cultural adaptations, this tool provides an essential resource for evaluating and improving maternal care in Sri Lanka. The study findings suggest that implementing the adapted PCMC tool across Sri Lanka’s maternity facilities can systematically capture the quality of maternal care from the patients’ perspective, enabling targeted quality improvement. As Sri Lanka continues to address its plateauing maternal mortality rate, integrating this PCMC tool into routine maternal health assessments can guide quality improvement efforts and support the country’s commitment to providing dignified, respectful, and person-centered care for all women.
