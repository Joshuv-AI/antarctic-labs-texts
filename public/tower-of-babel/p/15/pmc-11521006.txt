Surgical outcomes of inverted internal limiting membrane flap technique for primary rhegmatogenous retinal detachment coexisting with a macular hole
Authors: Kai-Ling Peng, Ya-Hsin Kung, Tsung-Tien Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the visual outcomes and foveal restoration in eyes with rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) and macular hole (MH) following surgery using the inverted internal limiting membrane (ILM) flap technique.We collected data on eyes with coexisting RRD and MH that underwent surgery using the inverted ILM flap technique between January 2016 and December 2018. The primary outcome measures were visual acuity (VA) and optical coherence tomography findings.Of 308 eyes with MH originating from various causes, 8 (2.6%) eyes diagnosed with coexisting RRD and MH treated using the inverted ILM flap technique were included. The average patient age and axial length were 65.00 ± 12.01 years and 23.66 ± 0.77 mm, respectively. The mean preoperative best-corrected VA (BCVA) was 1.84 (spherical equivalent [SE], counting finger) ± 0.32 logMAR and the mean final BCVA had improved significantly to 1.05 (SE, 20/223) ± 0.67 logMAR (P = .017). The primary retinal reattachment, MH closure, and secondary retinal attachment rates were 75% (6/8), 87.5% (7/8), and 100% (7/7), respectively.The inverted ILM flap technique led to significant improvements in anatomical and visual outcomes in patients with coexisting RRD and MH.

1. Introduction

Primary rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) coexists with macular hole (MH) in approximately 1% to 4% of RRD cases,[1–4] which is characterized by peripheral retinal breaks and macula-off retinal detachment (RD). Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells released from peripheral breaks have been hypothesized to precipitate on the macular surface, creating tangential traction on the central macula in patients with RRD. This process can lead to changes in proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR), such as MH.[1] Treating coexisting RRD and MH conventionally involves sealing of peripheral retinal breaks to ensure adequate attachment of the entire retina, while leaving the MH untreated. Later, the inverted internal limiting membrane (ILM) flap technique used in difficult MH cases[5–7] improved MH closure rate in such complicated cases. In the present study, we evaluated the anatomical and visual outcomes of the inverted ILM flap technique in patients with RRD and coexisting MH.

2. Methods

2.1. Ethics statements

This retrospective study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the hospital’s institutional review board (registered under the number 20-CT6-08).

2.2. Study design and patient selection

We systematically reviewed the medical records of enrolled patients diagnosed with primary RRD coexisting with MH who underwent pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) and ILM peeling using the inverted ILM flap technique. The study period was January 2016 to December 2018. The inclusion criteria were the diagnosis of primary RRD coexisting with MH and a postoperative follow-up period of ≥ 1 month. The exclusion criteria were eyes with RD due to secondary causes such as diabetic retinopathy, trauma, uveitis, a history of vitreoretinal surgery, with or without macular epiretinal membrane, lamellar MH, or pseudohole, and axial length more than 26mm or high myopia less than −6 diopter (D).

2.3. Evaluation and data collection

Each patient underwent a comprehensive preoperative and postoperative ophthalmologic examination, which included the assessment of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) using the Snellen chart, measurement of refraction or axial length (AL), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, indirect ophthalmoscopy, and fundus photography following pupil dilation. The microstructures of the MHs were evaluated using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) (RTVue scanner; Optovue, Inc, Fremont, USA). The collected patient data included sex, age, refractive errors, AL, lens status, preoperative and postoperative BCVA, intraoperative scleral buckling (SB), complications, and follow-up duration. OCT findings such as the minimal preoperative diameters of the MH (measured from the closest point of retinal apposition), postoperative data pertaining to MH closure, presence of a continuous or interrupted external limiting membrane (ELM), and ellipsoid zone (EZ), foveal inner retinal structures, retinal attachment or detachment, and central foveal thickness (CFT), were also collected for analysis.

2.3.1. Surgical techniques of inverted ILM flap for coexisting MH: All surgical procedures were performed by a retinal surgeon using a standard 23-gauge PPV. If epiretinal membranes were present, they were initially peeled, followed by ILM peeling and tamponade with 16% perfluoropropane gas (C3F8). Considering the unavailability of brilliant blue G ophthalmic solution in Taiwan until March 2021, we used 0.05% indocyanine green (ICG) at a volume of 0.5 c.c. to stain the ILM for 20 to 30 seconds, with patients being informed about potential toxicities. In the inverted ILM flap technique, the ILM was not entirely removed but was retained in place with a superior flap surrounding the MH and attached to the MH edges (Fig. 1A–C). The superior ILM flap was inverted and gently positioned using ILM forceps to cover the MH (Fig. 1D). Fluid-gas exchange (FGE) through internal drainage outside the arcade, but inferior to the MH, was used to induce downward flow and maintain the position of the inverted superior ILM flap.

After indocyanine green (ICG) staining, an internal limiting membrane (ILM) flap was directly created using intraocular forceps in a case involving rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) coexisting with a macular hole (MH) (A and B). The lower ILM flap was peeled off along the lower margin of the MH, while the superior ILM flap remained attached to the upper margin of the MH (C). The superior ILM flap was folded downward, pulled flat, and positioned over the MH with the aid of intraocular forceps (D).

2.3.2. Surgical techniques for RRD: In cases where RD presented a more extensive area, multiple peripheral retinal breaks, or proliferative vitreoretinopathy change, encircling silicon bands (style 42, 4.0 mm in width, 1.25 mm in thickness) were sutured post limbus 12 to 17 mm, or silicon oil was injected into the vitreous cavity after FGE, based on the decision of the retinal surgeon. Following the procedure, all patients maintained a face-down position for 2 weeks.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Following vitrectomy using the inverted ILM flap technique with or without SB, primary retinal attachment and MH closure were specifically defined in cases of primary RRD coexisting with MH. These definitions entailed satisfactory peripheral retinal attachment and the observation of both foveal attachment and MH closure on OCT. Secondary retinal attachment was defined as retinal reattachment after the second surgery in patients who had recurrent retinal detachment after the initial procedure.

For data analysis, VAs were converted into logMAR values.[8] Linear regression analyses were performed to compare the final visual outcomes using continuous variables, such as age, AL, duration, minimal MH diameter, final CFT, and follow-up time. Preoperative values and postoperative visual outcomes were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Additionally, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the final visual results across categorical variables, including sex, lens status, intraoperative SB, restoration of the ELM and EZ, MH closure, complications, primary and secondary retinal attachment, and MH closure status. All data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS statistical software (version 20.0; IBM, Armonk). Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics and patient demographics

This retrospective study included 308 eyes with MH of diverse origins subjected to procedures performed by a single retinal surgeon between January 2016 and December 2018. Among these cases, 8 (2.6%) eyes were diagnosed with RRD coexisting with MH, and these 8 eyes underwent pars plana vitrectomy using the inverted ILM flap technique in conjunction with FGE. Of these 8 eyes, 1 eye (12.5%) with a corrugated, mobile retinal surface, indicative of acute RRD. Five cases (62.5%) presented with smooth retinal surfaces, absent of the floating retina. The outward inversion at the retinal break margins was observed in 3 cases (37.5%), alongside the retinal folds (12.5%) and starfolds pattern (12.5%) in 1 case each. With PVR changes present in 7 eyes (87.5%), these further underscore the chronicity and complexity of RRD coexisting with MH. Five (62.5%) concurrently underwent SB, and silicon oil was not used in the initial surgery for any case. The mean patient age was 65.00 ± 12.01 years, and 37.5% (3/8) of the patients were female. The mean AL measured 23.66 ± 0.77 mm. The mean duration from symptom onset to surgery was 1.43 ± 1.87 months. The mean minimum diameter of the MH was 356.68 ± 75.93 μm. The mean follow-up time was 14.88 ± 14.20 months. The mean CFT at the last recorded visit was 260.75 ± 43.88 μm. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the patients with coexisting RRD and MH. No significant differences were observed in the final visual outcomes based on sex, lens status, AL, durations from symptoms onset to surgery, minimal MH diameter, intraoperative SB, final CFT, complications, or follow-up time. However, significant differences were detected between baseline and final visual acuity (VA) (P = .017, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). The mean preoperative BCVA was 1.84 [Snellen equivalent (SE), counting finger (CF)] ± 0.32 log MAR, whereas the mean final BCVA markedly improved to 1.05 (SE, 20/223) ± 0.67 logMAR. Among the 8 patients, 3 (37.5%) experienced complications. One patient developed manageable ocular hypertension effectively treated with antiglaucoma eyedrops. Another patient experienced recurrent RD, although adequate attachment was observed 30 months after the silicone oil injection. Unfortunately, the final patient experienced a recurrence of RD along with MH and cataract and was subsequently lost to follow-up in the third month.

3.2. Postoperative results and optical coherence tomography findings

Primary retinal attachment was achieved in 75% of the cases (6/8), with a mean final BCVA of 0.88 (SE, 20/153) ± 0.67 logMAR. Secondary retinal attachment was achieved in 100% of the cases (7/7), with a mean final BCVA of 0.99 (SE, 20/194) ± 0.54 logMAR. The closure rate of MH reached 87.5% (7/8), yielding a mean final BCVA of 0.94 (SE, 20/174) ± 0.63 logMAR. The sole instance of failure, characterized by recurrent RD coexisting with MH and cataract was lost to follow-up 3 months postoperatively. Eyes with cataracts at last visit accounted for 25% (2/8) of the cases, with a mean final BCVA of 1.85 (SE, CF) ± 0.00 logMAR. Meanwhile, eyes with pseudophakia at the last visit accounted for 75% (6/8) of the cohort, exhibiting a mean final BCVA of 0.78 (SE, 20/120) ± 0.53 logMAR. Three of the 8 patients (37.5%) had a complete ELM at the final examination, with a mean final BCVA of 0.40 (SE, 20/50) ± 0.40 logMAR. Two of the 8 eyes (25%) had a complete EZ, corresponding to a mean final BCVA of 0.20 (SE, 20/32) ± 0.28 logMAR. Table 2 summarizes the OCT findings of the postoperative foveal structures. Despite the absence of statistical significance in the final visual outcomes concerning primary retinal attachment and MH closure, notable distinctions in terms of foveal restoration, specifically pertaining to ELM (P = .016, Mann–Whitney U test) and EZ (P = .021, Mann–Whitney U test) were identified. Furthermore, the final status of the lens was significantly related to the final VA (P = .036, Mann–Whitney U test).

3.3. Visual improvements

The VA results at 1 month (P = .040, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), 6 months (P = .034), 12 months postoperatively (P = .029, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), and the final follow-up (P = .017, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) were significantly better than the preoperative values (Fig. 2). The mean BCVA markedly improved from 1.84 (SE, CF) ± 0.32 logMAR preoperatively to 1.49 (SE, 20/625) ± 0.46 logMAR at 1 month postoperatively, and gradually improved to 1.23 (SE, 20/340) ± 0.54 logMAR at 3 months postoperatively, followed by a steady enhancement to 1.23 (SE, 20/340) ± 0.35 logMAR at 6 months postoperatively. This improvement was maintained with a mean BCVA of approximately 1.0 (SE, 20/200) ± 0.35 logMAR at 12 months postoperatively. For eyes that were pseudophakia at baseline, the mean BCVA improved from 1.80 (SE, CF) ± 0.37 logMAR preoperatively to 1.34 (SE, 20/434) ± 0.52 logMAR at 1 month postoperatively. This improvement continued, reaching 1.19 (SE, 20/312) ± 0.51 logMAR, 1.16 (SE, 20/290) ± 0.42 logMAR, and 1.0 (SE, 20/200) ± 0.42 logMAR at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, respectively, to 0.85 (SE, 20/143) ± 0.46 logMAR. The mean preoperative BCVA in phakic eyes at baseline obviously improved from 1.88 (SE, CF) ± 0.32 logMAR preoperatively to 1.53 (SE, 20/678) ± 0.41 logMAR at 1 month postoperatively. This positive trend persisted, yielding BCVA values of 1.33 (SE, 20/426) ± 0.81 logMAR, 1.30 (SE, 1/20) ± 0.35 logMAR, and 1.0 (SE, 20/200) ± 0.42 logMAR at 3, 6 and 12 months postoperatively, respectively. However, the final BCVA decreased in phakic eyes at baseline, recording a value of 1.25 (SE, 20/358) ± 0.68 logMAR, attributed to the development of cataract in 1 eye followed up for 5 weeks, recurrent RD with persistent MH and cataract in 1 eye followed up for 2 months, and the other 2 eyes underwent cataract surgery after the first surgery, which was followed up for 30 and 28 weeks, respectively.

Visual outcomes in all patients at 1 month postoperatively (P = .044), 6 months postoperatively (P = .043), and the final follow-up (P = .018) were significantly better than preoperative values. Among all patients, the mean preoperative best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved from 2.07 to 1.49 logMAR at 1 month postoperatively, gradually improved to 1.30 logMAR at 3 months postoperatively and 1.12 logMAR at 6 months postoperative. The value remained around 1.1 logMAR at 12 months postoperatively but had slightly worsened to 1.21 logMAR at the final follow-up. In eyes with pseudophakia, the mean BCVA improved from 2.01 logMAR to 1.38 logMAR at 1 month postoperatively, steadily improved to 1.25 logMAR at 3 months postoperatively, and 1.0 logMAR at 6 months postoperatively. The final BCVA in eyes with pseudophakia slightly improved to 0.93 log MAR, although it was worse at 12 months postoperatively (1.30 log MAR) since 3 patients (75%) were lost to follow-up. Among eyes with phakia at baseline, mean BCVA improved from 2.13 logMAR preoperatively to 1.60 logMAR at 1 month postoperatively, gradually and continuously improved to 1.40 logMAR, 1.30 log MAR, and ultimately 1.0 logMAR at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, respectively. The final BCVA in eyes with phakia was worse (1.50 logMAR) since 2 (50%) patients developed recurrent retinal detachment.

3.4. Case presentation

Figure 3 shows a 57-year-old female patient presenting with RRD and an MH in the left eye (Fig. 3A and B). The patient’s left eye showed a preoperative CF. At 1 month postoperatively, her vision improved to 2/200, concomitant with shallow subfoveal fluid and MH closure (Fig. 3C). Ten months postoperatively, the patient underwent cataract surgery. Twelve months postoperatively, her BCVA further improved to 40/200, and OCT confirmed the absence of subfoveal fluid (Fig.3D). At 28 months postoperatively, her BCVA had improved to 20/20, while OCT revealed consistent maintenance of foveal attachment characterized by a well-defined contour (Fig. 3E).

4. Discussion

The coexistence of RRD and MH presents a complex surgical challenge. The success rate of MH closure varies among studies that focus on peeling, non-peeling the ILM or the latest inverted ILM flap technique, as summarized in Table 3. O’driscoll et al[9] involved vitrectomy without ILM peeling and drainage of subretinal fluid through the MH and reported an MH closure rate of 31%. The MH closure rates in the peeling and non-peeling ILM groups were 100% and 28.57 %, respectively, in the study by Singh et al[10] and 90.7% and 33.3%, respectively in the study by Ryan et al. [3] In a distinct study, Shukla et al[11] advised against subretinal fluid drainage through the MH but reported peripheral retinal breaks in 82.4% and 92.9% of patients in the peeling and non-peeling groups, respectively. Furthermore, Shukla et al[11] have reported that 23.1% of cases in the peeling ILM group were classified as type 2 MH closure, defined as an interruption in the continuity of foveal tissue with a flat MH margin,[12] compared to 7.1% in the non-peeling group. Rossi et al[13] indicated that foveal tissue interruption is an open MH, regardless of whether the margin is flat or elevated, based on meticulous spectral-domain OCT assessment. Therefore, the true MH closure rates in the study by Shukla et al[11] should be 59.3% in the peeling ILM group and 35.8% in the non-peeling groups. Without ILM peeling, the MH closure rate only varied from 28.57% to 35.8%, although this rate increased to 59.3% to 100% after ILM peeling. However, we were unable to determine the true rate of MH closure in the studies by Sigh[10] and Ryan et al[3] Najafi et al[1] have reported the primary closure rate of MH in 71% of 15 eyes that underwent ILM peeling. Regarding the latest inverted ILM flap technique, Theodor et al, who peeled and inverted ILM flaps with insertion inside MH under perfluorocarbon liquid, have reported a 100% MH closure rate.[4] Meanwhile, Zhu et al compared ILM insertion and inverted ILM flap techniques with perfluorocarbon liquid assistance, and reported MH closure rates of 73% and 95%, respectively.[14] Liu et al[15] compared ILM peeling and inverted ILM flap techniques, and reported rates of 55.4% and 82.6%, respectively. In our study focused on the inverted ILM flap technique, the MH closure rate reached 87.25%, which was not inferior to the rates observed in the peeling ILM groups in the studies of Shukla et al[11] and Najafi et al[1] and approached the results of ILM flap technique and ILM insertion. All patients with MH closure exhibited continuous foveal tissue on postoperative OCT images. This collective evidence underscores the enhanced outcomes of the inverted ILM flap technique and ILM insertion in achieving MH closure in the presence of coexisting RRD and MH.

The summary of previous studies about RRD coexisting.

Research addressing coexisting RRD and MH commonly highlights its chronic nature, which is associated with elevated PVR rates compared with primary RRD without MH.[1] Patients with severe PVR often require treatment with additional SB and silicone oil to mitigate the risk of reoperation.[1,16] The mean duration from symptom onset to surgery was 1.43 months in our study 43 days in the peeling ILM group and 29 days in the non-peeling group in the study by Shukla et al[11] In addition, the formation of MH after RRD depends on the time required for the PVR process. However, despite SB approaches, the rate of retinal attachment has remained high in previous studies. O’driscoll et al[9] demonstrated primary and secondary retinal attachment rates of 78% and 87%, respectively, by encircling SB and 25% SF6. Singh et al[10] have reported a retinal attachment rate of 100% for strategies without encircling SB, but utilized 20% SF6 or 12% C3F8. Ryan et al,[3] excluding patients with ≥ 6 clock hours of grade C PVR, have reported a primary retinal reattachment rate of 95.9% and secondary retinal reattachment rate of 100% with encircling SB and 10%-18% C3F8 or 20% to 25% SF6. Shukla et al ‘s study,[11] excluding patients with grade B or higher PVR, have reported a retinal attachment rate of 100% in SB encircling surgery supplemented with photocoagulation. However, 26 of 31 patients underwent simultaneous silicone oil placement. A study by Najafi et al,[1] including 29% patients with grade B PVR and 24% with grade C PVR, has revealed a primary retinal reattachment rate of 71% and secondary retinal reattachment of 100% in 15 eyes (88%) treated with SF6 or C3F8, 2 eyes (12%) treated with additional SB, and 2 eyes (12%) treated with silicone oil tamponade. In the studies using the inverted ILM flap technique, Theodor et al[4] documented primary and secondary retinal reattachment rates of 90% and 100%, respectively, in all cases using silicone oil injection. Liu et al[15] have declared retinal reattachment rates of 94.6% and 95.7% in the ILM peeling and ILM flap groups, respectively, accompanied by 360° photocoagulation, cryopexy and silicone oil injection. In our study, the success rates for primary retinal attachment and secondary retinal attachment reached 75% and 100%, respectively, with 62.5% of patients receiving intraoperative SB and 16% C3F8. One eye developed recurrent RD with MH closure yet exhibited a well-attached retina after the second surgery. Although only 1 eye developed recurrent RD and cataracts without MH closure, this patient was lost to follow-up after 3 months. Both patients underwent encircling of the SB during the initial surgery.

Shukla et al[11] have reported that preoperative BCVA among patients with coexisting RRD and MH was 1.8 ± 0.5 logMAR in the peeling ILM group and 1.7 ± 0.5 logMAR in the non-peeling group. Our study presented a worse mean preoperative vision of 1.84 (SE, CF) ± 0.32 logMAR. Singh et al[10] reported the final visual outcomes of 9.2/60 in the ILM peeling group and 12.9/60 in the non-peeling group after the first surgery. Ryan et al[3] reported a mean postoperative vision of 0.75 logMAR (SE, 20/120) in 49 patients, of whom 43 underwent ILM peeling. Shukla et al[11] have reported 0.6 (SE, 20/80) ± 0.2 logMAR in the ILM peeling group and 1.0 (SE, 20/200) ± 0.3 logMAR in the non-peeling group. Najafi et al[1] have reported the mean final vision of 1.28 (SE, 20/400) ± 0.76 logMAR. In studies concerning the inverted ILM flap technique, Theodor et al[4] reported a mean postoperative BCVA of 0.6 (SE, 20/80) ± 0.32 logMAR under the ILM insertion method. Zhu et al[14] found a mean postoperative BCVA of 1.45 (SE, 20/564) ± 0.51 logMAR in the ILM insertion group and 1.31 (SE, 20/270) ± 0.31 logMAR in the ILM flap group. In our study, the mean final BCVA among all cases treated with the inverted ILM flap technique was 1.05 (SE, 20/223) ± 0.67 logMAR. However, the mean final BCVA in cases with MH closure and retinal attachment was 0.88 (SE, 20/153) ± 0.67 logMAR, and cases with pseudophakia exhibited a mean final BCVA of 0.78 (SE, 20/120) ± 0.53 logMAR. The final visual results after using the inverted ILM flap technique in our study were similar to those reported in previous studies of ILM peeling. However, the final vision in ILM insertion cases varied in studies by Theodor[4] and Zhu et al,[14] where the final lens status after surgery, a relevant factor in the final vision, was not indicated.

Most studies on the restoration of foveal microstructures in cases of RRD with MH have focused primarily on MH closure. In the study of foveal restoration after the use of the temporal inverted ILM flap technique for cases with MH alone, Michalewska et al have reported that the ELM and EZ defects remained in 25% and 57% of the eyes at 12 months after surgery postoperatively.[17] Casini et al compared classic and modified inverted ILM flap techniques for cases of MH alone, and reported ELM defects in 20% and 28.21% of cases and EZ defects in 45% and 51.28% of cases at 12 months postoperatively.[18] In our study, the final rates of complete ELM and EZ rate were 37.5% and 25%, respectively, with a mean follow-up of 14.88 ± 14.20 months. These rates may be relatively lower for eyes with coexisting RRD and MH than for eyes with MH alone treated using the inverted ILM flap technique.

Our analysis highlighted the relatively low prevalence of RRD coexisting with MH. Despite the initially poor preoperative vision in these patients, substantial improvements in the final visual outcomes were observed after PPV using the inverted ILM flap technique. The secondary retinal attachment and MH closure rates were 100% in our study. Foveal restoration, including the ELM and EZ, was observed in 37.5% and 25% of cases at the final follow-up, respectively. However, certain limitations of this study need to be acknowledged, including its retrospective design, relatively small sample size, variability, and short follow-up duration.
