As nephrologists, we are fortunate to practice in an era where advances in understanding the pathogenesis of glomerular diseases have led to the discovery and approval of several new treatments. However, these medications require prior authorization, previsit and intensive laboratory monitoring, and concise dosing schedules. To ensure adherence in a busy clinical practice, it is essential to meticulously track each patient from the time a prescription is written until the end of treatment duration. This leads to increased clinician and nursing staff time used for medication management and adds to the disease burden for patients. Thus, automation of tasks may help alleviate health care providers’ burden and refine patient care.

Compass Rose is EPIC’s official care management coordination tool, designed to streamline outreach, patient engagement, and task tracking for patients enrolled in care programs.1,2 For this perspective, we share our experience with Compass Rose having been successfully implemented in our Glomerulonephritis (GN) Clinic at the Mayo Clinic in Jacksonville, Florida, to support the use of seven prescribed medications (sparsentan, iptacopan, targeted release budesonide, belimumab, voclosporin, avacopan, and rituximab) that require extensive prior authorization and close laboratory monitoring as part of our GN Monitoring Program (GMP). Our GMP through Compass Rose has automated and streamlined these tasks with a thorough description of the tasks per drug and indication outlined in Figure 1. For this perspective, we describe its application for these seven medications, using automated enrollment based on defined clinical criteria, optional tasking, and patient stratification through support and service features to coordinate complex medication workflows and enhance care delivery for patients with glomerular diseases.

Glomerular Disease Treatments and Monitoring Requirements

Sparsentan, iptacopan, and targeted release budesonide have been recently US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved for IgA nephropathy. Sparsentan is a dual antagonist of endothelin type A receptor and angiotensin II type 1 receptor.3 Our GMP has automated enrollment, prior authorization with the specialty pharmacy, ensuring initial and monthly liver function testing (as part of risk evaluation and mitigation strategy [REMS]), and pregnancy status management is automated for our clinic nurses. Iptacopan is a factor B inhibitor that blocks activation of the alternative complement pathway.4 Our GMP has automated tasks for prior authorization, reviewing a patient safety guide, REMS monitoring including lipid panel at 1, 3, and 6 months and every 6 months thereafter and ensuring proper vaccinations against encapsulated organisms occur before initiation. Finally, for IgA nephropathy, targeted release budesonide requires initiation through prior authorizing with specialty pharmacy and periodic laboratory monitoring.

Belimumab and voclosporin have been recently FDA approved for lupus nephritis.5 Belimumab is a monoclonal antibody that inhibits B-lymphocyte stimulator (Blys/B-cell activating factor). It requires prior authorization, medication injection teaching, and periodical laboratory monitoring. Voclosporin is a novel calcineurin inhibitor with less need for laboratory monitoring; however, given its recent approval, it requires prior authorization, care coordination with a specialty pharmacy, and frequent laboratory monitoring (every 2 weeks for the first month and monthly thereafter).

Avacopan is a C5a inhibitor, and rituximab is an anti–CD-20 monoclonal antibody and is now the mainstay of induction and maintenance treatment for ANCA vasculitis.6 Rituximab is also standard of care for membranous nephropathy.7 Avacopan requires initiation of therapy through prior authorization, initiation through specialty pharmacy, baseline laboratory results, and every 4-week laboratory testing for the first 6 months with every 3-month laboratory results thereafter. Rituximab requires initial laboratory results including hepatitis serologies, QuantiFERON gold testing for latent tuberculosis, therapy plan initiation, q3 month laboratory monitoring, and then may need redosing to be scheduled at 6-month intervals. Taken together, the workflow and clinical effort needed to start and monitor these medications require considerable time and resources. Thus, automatization of these tasks and providing routine reminders to clinical staff help to optimize care coordination for both patients and clinicians.

Development of Compass Rose Tailored for GMP

Developing the Compass Rose GMP required assembling a multidisciplinary team of stakeholders. This team included the nephrology nurses, advanced practitioner providers, nephrologists, information technology (IT)/software developers, and input from the pharmaceutical industry for the care process of medicine dispensing from the specialty pharmacy. The team developed an ideal workflow mapping for each medication, starting from nephrologist's prescription, through preauthorization steps, prerequisite laboratory tests, vaccination requirement, and subsequent REMS monitoring, to the assessment of side effects throughout the duration of the medication's treatment (Figure 1). The IT team developed the Compass Rose GMP which automatically identifies patients (or can be manually initiated) who have been prescribed one of these seven medicines. The nursing care team then reviews the identified patient list in Compass Rose and enrolls the patients in the GMP. The program provides a digital checklist of tasks for preauthorization of medication, including baseline laboratory results, REMS counseling, and starting the treatment. Subsequently, the Compass Rose GMP sends reminders to the GN care team about the patient's need for follow-up laboratory results at predetermined timelines based on workflow mapping (Figure 1). If a patient is late in performing the follow-up laboratory results, the system alerts the nursing team that the monitoring is overdue, prompting them to contact the patient to get the laboratory results done, preventing nonadherence to the monitoring and management tasks. After completing scheduled tasks, the Compass Rose GMP resets the clock for the next monitoring period to ensure timely safety monitoring of GN medicines.

Our experience with the Compass Rose GMP has been extremely positive. To date, it has identified 76 patients who have been prescribed one of these seven medicines in the past 9 months. In a busy nephrology practice, manually tracking that amount of GN patients treated with these agents is time consuming and increases the care burden on nephrologists, advanced practice providers, and nursing team. In our experience, this GMP has streamlined the process of prescribing and monitoring these newer therapeutic agents.

Developing any new system always comes with challenges. Our initial challenge was getting the nursing team to consider a new automated process of medications monitoring. Demonstrating how the system saves time over manual tracking, laboratory result follow-ups, and patient reminders was crucial. Another key aspect involved input from the IT team to create detailed mapping for each specific medication. In addition, it was necessary for the electronic health record system to accurately identify GN patients prescribed these medications using International Classification of Diseases-10 coding. However, this challenge provided us with an opportunity to incorporate a patient registry feature of Compass Rose tool, which can also be used to identify GN patients for research studies. This GN registry has retrospectively identified 1281 GN patients seen at Mayo Clinic Florida over the past 5 years. We use this registry to prescreen GN patients who may be eligible to participate in the ongoing GN clinical trials.

We acknowledge as a limitation that this report does not include robust preimplementation and postimplementation data to quantify improvements in efficiency or monitoring accuracy compared with the prior manual process. Our intent is to describe our implementation experience, which demonstrates feasibility and potential impact within a real-world clinical setting. Future work will systematically collect comparative data to evaluate time savings, monitoring accuracy, and scalability.

Our experience may be helpful to other nephrology practices in creating a similar care management programs for their GN patients, especially for managing the complexities of newer therapeutic agents. Furthermore, this tool offers potential for broader application in additional nephrology practice areas, such as CKD care management, optimization of kidney stone clinic workflow, and hypertension management.

In summary, we highlight our successful experience with using Compass Rose at the Mayo Clinic Florida GN Clinic as part of our GN Monitoring Program with the goal of streamlining medication prior authorization, initiation, and monitoring for the benefit of both patients and clinicians.
