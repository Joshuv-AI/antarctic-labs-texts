Adolescents’ perspectives on spectacle wear and adherence following a school-based outreach programme in Indonesia: a qualitative study
Authors: Tri Rahayu, Julie Dewi Barliana, Yeni Lestari, Angelina Patricia Chandra, Dhiya Nadira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study is to explore in depth adolescents’ insights regarding experiences of spectacle lens wear and its correlation with self-perception, quality of life, social interactions, adherence and barriers.DesignQualitative design through individual interviews and thematic analysis.SettingMiddle school students in five regions of Jakarta Province.Participants31 middle school adolescents who participated and received free spectacle wear by the Indonesian Ophthalmologist Association.MethodsA set of semistructured questionnaires exploring adolescents’ perception regarding spectacle lens wear, adapted from the PedEyeQ. Interviews were conducted on site or through Zoom and were then transcribed.FindingsThematic analysis identified three themes, as follows: (1) experience with eyeglasses, (2) motivation and encouragement and (3) barriers to usage. This study found that more than half of adolescents received their first spectacles during the outreach programme, with most reporting improved vision and academic performance after spectacle wear. However, adherence varied, as some participants—particularly those with moderate to high myopia and astigmatism—did not use their new glasses due to discomfort, poor fit or dissatisfaction with visual clarity. While initial adaptation often involved dizziness or soreness, most adjusted within a week. The majority recognised the importance of spectacle wear, describing clearer vision, reduced eye strain and improved confidence, though a minority viewed it as unnecessary. Parents played a central role in influencing health-seeking behaviour, and limited prior access to eye examinations and geographic challenges restricted care for several adolescents. Financial concerns were reported by a small proportion, while psychological barriers such as fear of teasing or negative self-perception were the major barrier. Overall, adolescents highlighted both the benefits and challenges of spectacle wear, with motivation shaped by personal experience, parental influence and accessibility of eye care services.ConclusionsFindings showed insight that adolescent understanding regarding eye health is imperative to support adherence. However, psychological barriers act as a major factor that impedes lens wear. Involving parents and teachers in understanding urgency and severity of eye health in adolescents, specifically refractive error and its long-term negative impact, as well as the prominent psychological barriers, may improve adolescent perception and adherence.Keywords: Adolescent, Myopia, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

The International Classification of Disease defines refractive error (RE) as blurring of vision due to a defect in achieving focus of light in the retina.1 Myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism are the three categories of REs.2 Globally, RE is the leading cause of vision impairment, being 88.4 million cases out of 2.2 billion people with vision impairment worldwide.3 90% of the affected population are residents from low-income countries.4 However, the WHO reported that only one-third of people with refractive error have access to spectacle wear.5 Data on RE coverage (REC), defined as degree of unmet needs regarding uncorrected RE, also present as a public health concern as data on urgency of RE coverage are scarce globally. Moreover, effective RE coverage (eREC) data, describing percentage of people with access to appropriate spectacles, are unavailable in low-to-middle countries that already have inadequate access to healthcare.6 7 Two studies in Africa reported similar results, highlighting lack of felt need, affordability, stigmatisation and distance to healthcare facility to be the most prominent barriers to uptake of correction spectacles in rural and low-to-middle income settings.8 9 In Indonesia, national data regarding REC and eREC are currently unavailable. This further highlights the importance of our study in reinforcing the urgency of RE screening and management efforts. In accordance with the gap in REC, a relatively cost-effective intervention, the World Health Assembly in 2021 reached an agreement to increase the number of spectacle wear by 40 percentage points by 2030.10 Our study highlights spectacle provision through school-based outreach in settings where adolescents were unable to independently access corrective spectacles. Moreover, rather than focusing solely on access or coverage, our study aims to explore the distribution of spectacle wear and effective use to better understand whether spectacles provided through outreach are accepted, used and perceived as beneficial by adolescents.

The recent study reported that challenges including education and socioeconomic status present as a significant factor in impeding access to spectacle wear correction.11 Although the aforementioned factors may contribute to undercorrection of RE, perceptions and attitude of spectacle wearers, especially in adolescents, may also contribute to adherence to spectacle wear. Barriers mentioned by adolescents include cosmetic reasons leading to reduction in self-confidence, negative societal perceptions regarding spectacle wear including discrimination by peers and discomfort.12 However, it is also imperative to identify RE severity and its impact on quality of life to adherence with spectacle wear.13

Currently, there is limited research that explores the magnitude of adolescents’ perception towards spectacle wear and its correlation to adherence. This study aimed to identify and explore perceptions of adolescents regarding spectacle wear, as well as its barriers, and how it may correlate with adherence. The research questions were:

Methods

Design

The study design was to collect qualitative interview data from at least 30 early adolescents. The participants were invited to a one-on-one interview through Zoom or onsite which explored the experiences of spectacle lens wear and its correlation with self-perception, quality of life, social interactions, adherence and barriers.

Patient and public involvement

We conducted a patient and public involvement (PPI) group through onsite explanation regarding RE in adolescents and importance of spectacle lens correction. The PPI group was part of a community service event conducted by the Indonesian Ophthalmologist Association (IOA) prior to the collection of interview data. A total of 25 middle-school adolescents from five middle schools in Jakarta province attended the event. During this stage, participants and their accompanying teachers were informed about the interview process and were given the opportunity to express their preferences regarding the mode of interview to ensure comfort. The interview method was intentionally adapted to accommodate participants’ comfort and logistical considerations; consequently, some schools opted for onsite interviews, while schools located on islands or in more remote areas preferred online interviews. Prior to the interviews, both the respondents and their guardians (teachers) were invited to provide feedback on the interview procedures, including aspects that could be improved. This approach aimed to enhance participant comfort, encourage open communication and strengthen the credibility of the qualitative data collected.

Recruitment

Participants were adolescents studying in middle school located in five regions of Jakarta province, being Central Jakarta, East Jakarta, West Jakarta, North Jakarta and Pramuka Island. The middle schools were selected prior to the interviews, during the planning phase of community outreach programmes conducted by the Indonesian Ophthalmologists Association, which included RE screening and the distribution of free spectacles. The selection of schools was recommended by the Education Agencies of each district with the consideration of which schools, demographically and financially, would benefit from the community intervention of free corrective spectacles. The authors collaborated with the middle school teachers in coordinating the children for interview. Written informed consent authorised by the adolescents’ parents, guardians or teachers was obtained prior to the community outreach event. Adolescents with a diagnosis of RE with or without previous spectacle wear history were eligible. A diagnosis of RE and myopia severity was provided by an ophthalmologist during the community outreach event conducted by the IOA mentioned earlier. Exclusion criteria for the study included participants who were unable to take part in the interviews, for example, absence during on-site interview schedules and who were not available to be contacted for online interviews.

Ocular examination procedures

In consecutive order, all participants underwent screening for monocular visual acuity using one line Snellen chart at 3 m under room illumination representing visual acuity of 20/30 (logMar 0.2) by ophthalmology resident. Autorefractive examination in a non-cycloplegic state, followed by subjective refraction, was then conducted by an optometrist using autorefractometer (autorefractor URK-700A, 220V, UNICOS, Korea). Finally, an ophthalmologist examined other ocular pathologies that may cause vision impairment through slit-lamp examinations (type L-0229-E Inami Egg Slit Digital Camera, Inami and Co. LTD, Japan) conducted by ophthalmologist. In this study, myopia was defined as a spherical equivalent (SE) of −0.5D or less in both eyes, diagnosed through subjective refraction. Myopia grading was defined as follows: mild myopia (SE of −0.5D to −3.0D), moderate myopia (SE of −3.01D to −5.99D), and high myopia (SE of < −6.00D).14

Corrective spectacles were then prescribed by an ophthalmologist for all participants diagnosed with RE, noting pupillary distance written in the prescription for each participant, regardless of previous spectacle wear history, followed by production of spectacles by optician. Distribution of corrective spectacles was conducted 2 weeks after the examination, in which a team of medical doctor, optometrist and ophthalmology resident from the IOA were present during the distribution to ensure proper fitting and identify problems regarding the spectacles. Highly myopic students with amblyopia were provided with corrective lenses and a referral note explaining the presence of non–refractive causes of their visual impairment. High index lenses were not provided to highly myopic students due to financial constraints within our study— however, students were encouraged to obtain high index lenses outside of the community outreach event.

Participants

A minimum sample of 30 adolescents was targeted based on common qualitative guidance indicating that 20–30 interviews are typically sufficient to reach thematic saturation in homogeneous groups.15 Purposive sampling was utilised to capture an equal estimated proportion of gender, age and myopia severity. 31 participants completed the interview between July 2025 and August 2025. All but eight participant interviews were conducted on-site at school. Seven interviews conducted online via Zoom were due to accessibility reasons for adolescents from Pramuka Island, where authors had to travel through a 2-hour boat ride to arrive at the school, and one out of the eight preferred to be interviewed via Zoom when the scheduled interview did not align with their time. The interview duration was approximately 30–45 min per participant. The teachers were all present in varying degrees during the interview. A structured topic guide adapted from the PedEye Questionnaires (PedEyeQ)16 was implemented in this study. At the beginning of the interview, the authors confirmed their identities and history of spectacle wear. Participants were then interviewed further, exploring their perceptions on spectacle lens wear. Repeat interviews were not carried out.

Demographic data

Information regarding demographic data was obtained through the written informed consent prior to the interview. The following demographic variables were obtained: age, gender, what class the students were in and home address. Although all of the participants' ethnicity was South-East Asian, differences in these five regions of Jakarta province may provide insights regarding cultural and sociodemographic differences between the regions—highlighting that each region may differ in financial income which may introduce barriers and influence the participant’s perspective.17

Quality and rigour

Semistructured interviews were conducted by three members of the study team: a female consultant ophthalmologist (TR) and two female medical doctors and a research assistant in the Department of Ophthalmology, Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia, Cipto Mangunkusumo General Hospital (APC and DN), who did not previously have contact with the participants prior to the community outreach event conducted by IOA. The topic guide (table 1) was formulated by three ophthalmologist consultants and a member of the IOA Division of Refractive Error Management (TR, YL and JDB) and medical doctors (APC and DN) after a thorough discussion regarding factors and barriers that may contribute to shaping adolescents’ perspective on spectacle wear. APC was trained in conducting qualitative interviews by TR, YL and JDB to ensure appropriate prompts, questioning and intonation were used to ensure a comfortable environment for the participants to openly share their thoughts and feelings regarding the topic. Moreover, TR had published previous qualitative research on public health and community outreach efforts, as well as more than 20 years of clinical experience as ophthalmologist. TR, YL and JDB have also represented the IOA during discussion with the Indonesian government regarding governmental policy on RE screening and management in Indonesia. Three members of the study team (TR, APC and DN) reviewed and transcribed the recorded interviews independently. Then, a discussion with all of the study team was conducted to develop thematic analysis and categorise the interview transcripts by identifying key concepts.18 We acknowledge that the participants’ awareness regarding the community outreach programme conducted by ophthalmologists and medical doctors must be considered during analysis as it may influence how willingly and openly the adolescents respond to questions. Therefore, during the PPI, we repeatedly remind and encourage openness during the interview process. Member checking was not performed in this study; however, interpretation of the interviews was informally discussed with the teachers to navigate and report how the students in their respective schools perceive spectacle wear and visual disturbance.

Results

Participants

A total of 31 middle-school adolescents completed a one-to-one interview with the author. All baseline demographic data were obtained during the interview. The ages of the participants were 13 (35.48%) and 14 (64.51%) years old with the majority (54.83%) being female and 45.16% being male.

Generation of themes

The authors discussed and generated the categorisation of themes in this study. There were three themes: (1) experience with eyeglasses, (2) motivation and encouragement and (3) barriers to usage. Feedback on the findings was not inquired to the participants.

Theme 1: experience and adherence with spectacle wear

More than half of the adolescents included in the study wore spectacles for the first time after being given spectacles during the community outreach event conducted by the IOA (table 2). Out of 17 adolescents wearing spectacles for the first time, 1 (3.22%) participant with mild myopia did not wear the newly given spectacles.

Adherence to spectacle wear

Subtheme 1: benefits of spectacle wear

When interviewed regarding quality of vision before spectacle wear, 26 (83.87%) participants reported vision disturbance, while 5 (16.12%) participants reported no complaints regarding vision before, however, agreed that better quality of vision was experienced after spectacle wear. Out of the 26 participants reporting vision disturbance prior to spectacle wear, 8 (25.80%), 8 (25.80%) and 9 (29.03%) have mild, moderate and high myopia, respectively. They reported difficulties reading the board or presented materials while positioned at the back of the class in school. Some participants with high myopia even reported difficulty in identifying their friends and families while conversing with them.

Personally, I feel everything (my vision) is blurry and unclear. Even my friend sitting next to me sometimes looks blurry. Streetlights appear doubled and with shadows, and other objects also seem to have shadows. Text looks like it’s going up and down. This is because I have high myopia and astigmatism. (SI, female)

However, when asked regarding how they felt when they had vision difficulties prior to spectacle wear, the majority of participants, 25 (80.64%) reported negative emotions such as frustration, irritability, sadness and fear of being mocked by their peers due to their dependency on other classmates and need to adjust their seating distance to the board while studying in class. Moreover, 28 (90.32%) participants highlighted a positive impact on vision after spectacle wear, including a clearer and more contrast vision, aiding them in studying and eliminating the need to squint their eyes due to previous blurriness in vision which caused eye strain.

Subtheme 2: discomfort of spectacle wear

While addressing non-adherence, questions regarding discomfort were asked to the participants. The majority of participants, 14 (45.16%), reported dizziness during initial spectacle wear. Moreover, 6 (19.35%) participants reported soreness in the back of the ear, frequent need to adjust spectacle positioning on the nose and the spectacle being too tightly fitted on their temples. However, all of the participants agreed that adapting to wearing spectacles was not a strenuous target and adapted within 2—7 days of initial spectacle wear.

At first it was difficult because sometimes I would trip while walking due to dizziness, but over time I got used to it and felt comfortable with my glasses. I got used to it in about a week. (MN, male)

Theme 2: motivation and encouragement

When asked regarding understanding of importance of spectacle wear due to RE, the majority of participants, 27 (87.09%), agreed on spectacle wear importance. Importance of spectacle wear was described:

Studying and looking at the whiteboard became easier. I also often use my phone, so wearing glasses helps me anticipate worsening nearsightedness because it feels safer, although I’m not sure if that is actually true. (ZN, female)

However, 4 (12.90%) participants perceived unimportance of spectacle wear with belief that no negative impact will occur when REs are not corrected. Three of the participants were mildly myopic and one participant was highly myopic.

Subtheme 1: attitude towards vision health

When asked whether blurry vision was something that they should be concerned about, 26 (83.87%) of participants answered ‘Yes’, while 5 (16.12%) answered ‘No’. This assessment may provide a picture regarding how adolescents perceive vision-related complaints that may impede their everyday activities.

During interviews, questions regarding whether the participants had suggestions for others adapting with spectacle wear resulted in several statements that depict how adolescents may perceive experience while wearing glasses:

Wear your glasses often and avoid taking them off so you get more used to wearing them. (AD, female)

Get used to wearing glasses so your eyes will not be exposed to dust. Be confident wearing them. If someone makes fun of you, just ignore them. (MM, female)

Have regular eye check-ups and remember to avoid activities that can worsen your vision, such as reading in the dark or using your phone for a long time. Don’t hold your glasses by the lenses because fingerprints will be visible. (SI, female)

Subtheme 2: parental influence

Familiarities with spectacle wear may also be influenced by experience of seeing their parents or siblings wearing glasses. The majority of participants, 26 (83.87%), reported knowing a family member who wears glasses. Parents were also the most mentioned figure in whom the participants reported to when having a vision complaint, 23 (74.19%). Other participants report their vision complaint to teachers (6.45%), siblings or friends (6.45%) and no one (16.12%).

Theme 3: barriers to usage

Subtheme 1: healthcare access

More than half of the included participants, 16 (51.61%), never had their eyes examined prior to the community outreach event conducted by the IOA at their schools. When asked regarding where they would go if they had a health complaint, 24 (77.41%) participants answered a primary healthcare facility, such as private clinics or government-owned primary healthcare centre. Knowledge regarding where to seek eye health services if they had a vision complaint was also assessed during the interview. The majority of participants, 19 (61.29%), answered doctors, 9 (29.03%) participants answered eyewear shop or optics and 3 (9.67%) of the participants did not know where to go. It must be noted that 6 of the included participants were located on Pramuka Island, where eyewear shops or optics are not currently available; therefore, they presented an obvious challenge in accessing spectacle lens correction.

Subtheme 2: financial barriers

Through the conducted interviews, 26 (83.87%) of participants reported that financial condition was not a barrier for them to obtain spectacle lenses. However, 5 (16.12%) reported their parent’s financial status and income as a barrier.

My father worked as a cleaning service and sometimes our family struggles to make ends meet. My need to see clearly could not be a priority for our family. (ER, male)

Subtheme 3: psychological barriers

Adolescents’ self-perception while wearing spectacle lenses was also assessed during the interview, in which 15 (48.38%) participants reported positive impact on their confidence, 10 (32.25%) reported negative impact, and 6 (19.35%) reported no changes on how they view themselves.

I feel more confident and find it easier to communicate with people. (FA, male)

I feel different, like my eyes look smaller. I do not really like it because my eyes appear smaller. I fear that my friends would tease me about it. (SR, female)

Eight (25.80%) participants also reported receiving negative comments about their appearance wearing glasses, including being called ‘geeky’, laughed at and mocked regarding how their faces looked. Only 3 (9.67%) participants reported positive comments regarding their appearances from their peers.

Discussion

In this study, we interviewed adolescents with different myopia severity and qualitatively analysed their perceptions on vision quality, understanding of myopia as well as RE, and the barriers that may present as challenges in acquiring corrective spectacles. Myopia can influence the daily life of patients, such as cosmetic concerns from wearing glasses, limitations in physical activities due to decreased visual function, mental stress and difficulties with social interactions.22 Previous literature has highlighted the impact of myopia on quality of life and psychological well-being of the children and their parents, with increasing debilitating factors in highly myopic children.23 The majority of participants (83.87%) in our study reported vision disturbances prior to spectacle wear, primarily during activities that involved literacy and reading during school. The vision disturbance was in accordance with the negative psychological impact felt by the participants due to blurred vision. Almost all participants (90.32%) highlighted a positive impact in their vision after corrective spectacle use. The compliance rate raises further questions regarding other factors that may impede spectacle wear. Previous studies reported adolescents with older age and children living in urban areas were significantly more likely to be non-adherent and stated reasons including fear of discrimination and lower self-esteem compared with younger-aged children.24 Negative peer pressure, concerns regarding physical appearance and negative perceptions on spectacle wear were several factors that were correlated with non-adherence, as reported in studies conducted in China,25 India,13 Saudi Arabia26 and Nigeria.27 Following spectacle wear, negative self-perception and negative comments by peers were reported to be 32.25% and 25%, respectively. Adolescents’ perceptions and behaviours regarding spectacle wear should be understood within the context of psychosocial development, where self-esteem and identity formation are central. According to Erikson’s theory of psychosocial development, adolescence is characterised by the stage of identity versus role confusion, during which individuals are particularly sensitive to peer evaluation, appearance and social belonging.28 Within this developmental phase, decisions about spectacle wear are not based solely on functional vision needs, but are negotiated through adolescents’ perceptions of themselves and how they believe they are viewed by others. This helps explain why some participants acknowledged clear visual benefits yet limited their spectacle use due to concerns about appearance, fear of teasing or feeling ‘different’ from peers. Recent evidence further supports that self-concept and peer-related factors significantly influence adolescents’ acceptance of visual correction and health-related behaviours.29 In this context, discomfort, stigma and selective spectacle use represent adaptive responses to competing functional, psychological and social demands as barriers rather than ‘just’ non-adherence. Understanding spectacle wear as a developmental and identity-related process provides important insight into why educational support, normalisation within school environments and reassurance from trusted adults are particularly critical during adolescence.

Therefore, alternative refractive correction options, such as contact lenses, may also be considered for adolescents who are reluctant to wear spectacles due to concern about their appearance. A study reported a statistically significant high satisfaction scale of 95%, measured through Piers-Harris Children’s Self-Concept Scale (PHCSCS) after 1 month wear of contact lenses.30 When compared with spectacle lenses, a study consisting of adolescents aged 13—19 years old reported a more positive attitude towards comfort, vision and safety with contact lenses after 6 months of contact lens use.31 However, in settings where hygiene practices have not been adequately evaluated or may be suboptimal, the routine use of contact lenses should be approached with caution. Given the potential risk of adverse effects and the need for proper hygiene education, contact lenses may not be recommended as a primary refractive correction option without comprehensive assessment and appropriate patient education.

Although increasing RE was reported to significantly increase adherence to spectacle wear,13 a similar finding was not concluded in our study, as non-adherence to spectacle wear was comparable in mild and high myopia. Other reasons for non-adherence were dissatisfaction with spectacle wear (discomfort including dizziness and improper fitting) as well as dislike of how they perceive themselves wearing the glasses. Moreover, although the majority of participants (83.87%) reported vision complaints to their parents, less than half (45.16%) of participants had worn spectacles prior to the distribution of spectacles by IOA and more than half of the participants, 16 (51.16%), had never had their eyes examined before. Some students mentioned that their parents were too busy and did not have time to obtain glasses for them. When asked what response their parents gave regarding the complaint, the participants mentioned that their parents only advised them to reduce screen time to combat the complaint. Published studies highlighted that parents may underestimate their children’s complaints regarding blurred vision and its impact on psychosocial and academic performance, thus impeding the need for urgent correction.32

eREC and effectivity of REC during mass screening and community events is also a prominent feature in this study. Almost half of the interviewed participants (14 (45.16%)) had previously worn spectacles. However, only 9 (29.03%) participants passed the vision screening of 20/30 with Snellen chart. Thus, the eREC among the included participants was lower than the estimated global eREC of 65.8% reported in a recent meta-analysis from 75 countries.7 This study also reported that previous history of spectacle wear was a critical factor in adherence to newly given spectacles by the IOA. Only 1 (3.22%) of participants with mild myopia without a history of previous spectacle wear did not wear their newly given spectacles. On the contrary, 6 (19.35%) participants with previous spectacle wear did not adhere to wearing their newly given spectacles, with reasons being their previous spectacles were more comfortable. The wearability of spectacles given out during our community outreach programmes could also be evaluated through the adherence of spectacle wear in our study, although adherence was associated with several factors elaborated in the results section. In a randomised controlled trial, measures of satisfaction and quality of life were comparable between ready-made and custom-made spectacles.33 34 Although our study provided custom-made glasses, the results of our study provided insights that even custom-made glasses needed education and follow-up to better provide understanding regarding spectacle wear to adolescents.

Two out of the three participants not wearing the newly given spectacles were highly myopic and were diagnosed with amblyopia by an ophthalmologist during the IOA event, with best-corrected visual acuity of 20/50 on both eyes. This finding highlights the importance of routine and early vision screening in school-aged children to not only detect uncorrected RE, but also increase early diagnosis of amblyopia and other non-refractive ocular pathologies. However, despite the majority of participants reporting their vision complaint to their parents, over 50% of the included participants had never had their eyes examined prior to the mass screening event conducted by the IOA. This finding pointed out that parents had poor understanding of the importance of vision health and its long-term impact on psychosocial and academic performance of their children. A previous qualitative study highlighted that parents interviewed in Nigeria would be less likely to seek care for complaints not visibly apparent compared with visible ones. Moreover, repeated complaints may support parents’ decision to seek help for their child’s vision complaint.35

Strengths of this study

This study was able to obtain insights from adolescents receiving spectacle correction from five middle schools in different regions of Jakarta, including one located in a rural area. Proportions of myopia severities were also similar within the interviewed participants, providing perceptions that relate to myopia severity.

Limitation of this study

There are several limitations in this study. First, this study has a possibility of selection bias because some of the authors conveying the interviews have previously met the participants during the community outreach programme conducted by the IOA. Second, the financial barriers assessed in this study might be strengthened if data regarding parental income was obtained. Third, spectacles given to the participants during the mass production after the community outreach programme may not be comparable with spectacles produced individually in a standard consultation setting with an ophthalmologist. Fourth, during data collection, the presence of teachers varied across interview settings and may have influenced how openly participants responded. At three schools, teachers were present to some degree where teachers occasionally entered and exited the room or a teacher was positioned at a distance but remained within the room. At one school, although teachers did not sit in on the conversations, they waited nearby and assisted in calling students for their interview sessions. In contrast, no teachers were present during interviews at only one school, where discussions were held in the school auditorium. For one school located in Pramuka Island, interviews were conducted via Zoom due to geographic limitations; in this case, a teacher was present to provide access to the school’s computer and internet connection. We observed differences in participant behaviour during interviews conducted with and without teacher presence. When teachers were present, several adolescents spoke in a lower voice, appeared more hesitant and occasionally looked toward the teacher for implicit approval or confirmation before or during their responses. In contrast, interviews conducted without teacher presence were generally more relaxed, with participants providing longer and more spontaneous responses. These observations suggest that social desirability bias may have influenced some accounts, particularly regarding reported adherence, attitudes towards spectacle wear and expressions of dissatisfaction. Participants may have moderated negative comments or overstated compliance due to the presence of authority figures within the school setting. Finally, as this study involved adolescents (under the age of 18), it did not assess factors that may influence spectacle-wear adherence from the parents’ perspective.

Recommendations: research

The findings of this paper highlight the feasibility of conducting interviews that are flexible to the participant’s available time. Further research regarding insights from children and adolescents may aid in improving delivery of education on childhood myopia, RE correction adherence and the short-term and long-term impact of myopia on academic performance. Additional research on perceptions of parents regarding vision health might also offer better understanding regarding spectacle wear adherence in adolescents.

Implications for clinicians and policymakers

Perceptions of spectacle wear among adolescents may contribute to spectacle wear adherence. Psychological factors include negative self-perception with spectacle wear and negative cosmetic comments by their peers.36 Dissatisfaction with spectacle fitting and discomfort such as dizziness further contributes to non-adherence.37 Therefore, prescription of spectacle wear by eye care professionals must prioritise precise lens power and comfortable fitting to reduce dissatisfaction. Spectacles prescribed and distributed during mass screening programmes should conduct further individual evaluation to minimise discomfort.

The findings in our study reported that the majority of adolescents had prior knowledge regarding where they could go to get their eyes examined, as well as where they could obtain corrective spectacles. Despite demonstrating adequate knowledge, more than half of the participants had never undergone a formal eye examination, indicating a gap between awareness and health-seeking behaviour. The lack of health-seeking behaviour among children may correlate with how their parents perceive vision complaints. Involvement of parents in childhood eyecare regarding benefits of spectacle wear and the negative implications of non-adherence might be helpful to improve adherence.38 Findings from this study suggest that school-based refractive programmes should move beyond spectacle distribution alone and incorporate structured educational components tailored to adolescents’ perceptions of vision problems. Many participants reported clear benefits after receiving spectacles, yet did not initially perceive their visual impairment as problematic. This highlights the need for predistribution and postdistribution counselling or school-based education sessions that help adolescents recognise symptoms of visual impairment, understand the functional and academic benefits of correction, and set realistic expectations regarding adaptation to spectacle wear.

Further studies need to be conducted to investigate how parents' involvement may aid in increasing adherence.39 Collaborative strategies both at the individual and community level are also imperative to facilitate routine vision screening programmes, reducing stigma and promoting adherence. Moreover, given that the majority of participants underwent their first eye examination only during adolescence, the findings underscore the need for earlier, routine vision screening in childhood as a policy priority.28 29 Our study highlights the importance of holistic evaluation on how adolescents perceive vision health and the complex factors contributing to adherence.

Implications to reduce psychological barriers

To effectively reduce psychological barriers to spectacle wear, coordinated efforts among schools, parents and health services are essential. In our study, the IOA collaborated with the Education Agencies of each district to select schools that were most vulnerable and needed free corrective spectacles. Health services, including primary care clinics and outreach programmes, can further contribute by providing clear explanations to adolescents and families about RE, offering individualised fitting and follow-up to prevent discomfort, and collaborating with schools to deliver periodic assessments.28 Structured educational sessions for both teachers and students were not implemented prior to the screening and distribution of spectacles in this study; however, the authors recommend implementation of these initiatives to psychologically prepare students, address misconceptions regarding spectacle use and promote greater acceptance of spectacle wear.

Schools can play a central role by normalising the use of spectacles through routine vision screenings, classroom-based education on eye health and teacher-led encouragement that reinforces positive attitudes toward spectacle wear. Parents may benefit from targeted counselling or informational materials that emphasise the importance of early detection, consistent spectacle use and supportive communication that counters stigma or negative self-perception.38
