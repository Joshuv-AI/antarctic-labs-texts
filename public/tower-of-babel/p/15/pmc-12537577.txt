Injectable hemostatic foam hydrogel for traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage
Authors: Yicheng Guo, Xiaohong Zhao, Ying Wang, Jing Zhou, Tongqing Lu, Gaoxing Luo, Rixing Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traumatic abdominal hemorrhage is critically life-threatening, as uncontrolled bleeding in this region can rapidly lead to death. However, there is a lack of effective methods or materials for emergency hemorrhage control, especially for pre-hospital emergency medical services. Here, we report an injectable hemostatic foam (IHF), a norepinephrine-loaded sodium alginate/carboxymethyl chitosan-based foam, which was inspired by physiological hemostasis processes (vasoconstriction, platelet thrombosis, and blood coagulation). IHF possesses good injectability, can rapidly reach the injury site to create a tamponade effect and provide wound site coverage when injected into abdominal cavity during emergency care. Then, the loaded norepinephrine induces vasoconstriction, and the foam interacts with blood to accelerate the formation of platelet thrombosis and blood coagulation. Upon arrival at the definitive point of care, IHF could be disrupted by urea and removed by negative pressure suction. Rat and rabbit liver bleeding models demonstrated its excellent hemostatic ability, significantly reducing blood loss and bleeding time. What’s more, in the heparinized rat and rabbit liver bleeding models, which were established to mimic systemic coagulation dysfunction secondary to severe trauma, the IHF hemostatic effect was still excellent. The emergency abdominal hemorrhage model created in rat and porcine liver demonstrated the IHF’s effectiveness and safety. Furthermore, no obvious postoperative intestinal adhesion was observed after IHF therapy. Collectively, IHF presents a strategy characterized by low implementation barriers and favorable safety profiles, thereby serving as an effective, safe, and feasible emergency hemostatic intervention for traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

Introduction

Traumatic abdominal hemorrhage is a critical condition caused by war trauma (firearm/explosion injury), car accident injuries, landslides and other disasters, violent attacks, etc. Abdominal trauma accounts for 6 %–14.9 % of total trauma worldwide, and the incidence of major abdominal trauma increased over time [1,2]. Once abdominal hemorrhage occurs, it can cause blood loss exceeding 20 % of blood volume in a short period of time, leading to hemorrhagic shock, which will result in functional failure of important organs such as the liver, kidneys, and brain [3]. Mortality rates linked to traumatic abdominal hemorrhage are 60 %–80 % and 10 % of trauma related deaths are caused by abdominal trauma, according to data from The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) [4,5]. The high mortality rates, high incidence rate and extremely time-sensitive nature underscore the significance of addressing traumatic abdominal hemorrhage.

The treatment for traumatic abdominal hemorrhage is facing severe challenges both in pre-hospital and in-hospital settings, and there is an urgent need for systematic breakthroughs. This challenge originates from its complex pathophysiological characteristics and anatomical environment [6]. The solid organs (liver, spleen, kidney) are adjacent to the hollow organs (intestine, stomach) in the abdominal cavity, and the blood vessel branches are interlaced. Trauma frequently results in complex, multi-site bleeding that is difficult to localize, 44 % patients reported multiple abdominal injuries and may remain asymptomatic until they have lost 50–60 % of their blood volume [5]. Therefore, it requires bulky diagnostic equipment to locate bleeding site [7,8]. However, it is impossible to obtain assistance from such equipment in pre-hospital settings. Even within the hospital, traditional intervention methods have significant limitations: open surgery causes significant trauma, and the incidence of postoperative complications ranges from 19.6 % to 24.2 %; the conversion rate of laparoscopic surgery to open surgery in complex injuries is as high as 5 %–10 %; although vascular embolization is a minimally invasive method, there is a 5.5 % % risk of accidental embolization and a 7.5 %–43 % incidence of post-embolization syndrome [[10], [11], [12], [13], [9]]. In summary, there is an urgent need to develop hemostatic techniques that are convenient, rapid, and easy to operate for the treatment of traumatic abdominal bleeding, suitable for both pre-hospital and in-hospital emergency rescue scenarios.

Currently, lots of advanced hemostatic materials have been developed, including injectable hemostats (like XSTAT) and other reported advanced hemostat under development [[14], [15], [16]]. However, though XSTAT was approved by the CoTCCC and FDA, its use in the abdomen is prohibited [17]. Hemostatic gauze is another highly effective hemostatic device, but the hemostatic effect is limited when dealing with abdominal hemorrhage. The injectable foam is beneficial to deliver hemostatic agents into deep wounds, especially in the abdominal cavity. Currently, the reported injectable foaming hemostatic materials including polyurethane based ResQFoam, chitosan-based foam, foam based on oxidized dextran and so on [[18], [19], [20]]. These hemostatic foams show good hemostatic ability. However, these potential treatments under development have one or more characteristics that could affect their utilities, such as: (i) Difficult to remove after the patient arrive to the hospital, bringing challenges for subsequent treatment; (ii) The lack of coagulation components leads to rebleeding and fails to address the challenge of coagulopathy; (iii) Excessive pressure may lead to necrosis of surrounding organs; (iv) The preparation process is complex, leading to high costs and making it difficult to deploy in pre-hospital environment. For example, ResQFoam has been reported to treat abdominal hemorrhage [21]. However, it brings some complications (including intestinal pressure necrosis and focal intestinal bruising) and subsequently detaches during laparotomy [18,22]. Therefore, developing hemostatic materials for traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage is an urgent matter.

Here, to overcome the abovementioned problems, we describe an injectable hemostatic foam (IHF) for emergency treatment of intra-abdominal hemorrhage. Hydrogen bond crosslinked IHF possesses good injectability and can be injected into the abdominal cavity. Once injected into the abdominal cavity, IHF will form a local tamponade effect and provide wound site coverage without needing to determine the bleeding point. Then, loaded norepinephrine causes vasoconstriction and bubbles of IHF capture blood cells and enrich coagulation factors to accelerate blood clot formation to achieve hemorrhage control. Furthermore, IHF can be removed by negative pressure drainage rather than laparotomy (Scheme 1). To systemically evaluate the hemostatic potential of IHF, rat and rabbit liver bleeding models were used. Results show that IHF could successfully and rapidly control liver hemorrhage, even in such cases of blood coagulation dysfunction induced by heparinization. Furthermore, we developed a non-compressible intra-abdominal hemorrhage model in rat and porcine livers to evaluate IHF’s ability to control intra-abdominal hemorrhage. The rapid hemostatic results demonstrate the prepared IHF possesses promising hemostatic potential for intra-abdominal hemorrhage control.Scheme 1Schematic representation of emergency treatment of traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage using IHF.Scheme 1

Results and discussion

Establishment and characterization of the IHF

In pre-hospital settings, tourniquets, TAX, and XSTAT have been used for limb bleeding, pelvic fracture-related hemorrhage control, and deep-tract or narrow-space trauma hemorrhage control, respectively (Fig. S1A–D) [[23], [24], [25]]. However, due to the fact that the diagnosis of intra-abdominal bleeding often presents significant difficulties, among other issues, there is no effective method available to first responders for temporary control of traumatic abdominal hemorrhage in pre-hospital settings (Fig. S1E) [26]. Currently, injectable foam is considered potential strategy for intra-abdominal hemorrhage control, due to its injectability and effectiveness [27]. But polyurethane-based foam is too hard, which poses a potential risk of respiratory compromise in diaphragmatic injury, overpressure necrosis of the bowel wall, and it needs to be subsequently removed during laparotomy (Fig. S1F) [28,29]. Therefore, according to the characteristics of abdominal bleeding, our design criteria of IHF include: (i) Convenience for injection into the intra-abdominal cavity in pre-hospital settings; (ii) Creation of a local tamponade effect, provision of injury site coverage and internal compression, and acceleration of blood clot formation; (iii) Ease of removal upon arrival at the definitive point of care.

To achieve the above goal, hydrogels crosslinked by hydrogen bonds (a non-covalent interaction force) are ideal candidates [30]. Malic acid is a naturally occurring dicarboxylic acid, it has been reported that could crosslink hydroxyl-containing materials through esterification reaction [31,32]. On the other hand, malic acid also could form cross-links between chitosan chains through hydrogen bonding, owing to the -COOH and -OH groups on the malic acid molecule can act as both hydrogen bond donors (providing H atoms) and hydrogen bond acceptors (receiving H atoms) [33]. Therefore, it can mediate and strengthen the electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding network between polysaccharides through its unique physicochemical properties, thereby forming gel. Thus, malic acid as a crosslinking agent has certain universality. As shown in Fig. 1A, sodium alginate and carboxymethyl chitosan precursor solutions could be crosslinked by malic acid through hydrogen bonds to form stable foam, which endowed IHF with hemostatic ability and injectability. Subsequently, upon the disruption of hydrogen bonds by urea, IHF could be efficiently removed. FTIR spectra and 1H NMR spectra demonstrated the crosslinking and decrosslinking processes (Fig. 1B and C). In FTIR spectra, the formation of hydrogen bonds caused a decrease in vibration frequency and resulted in a red shift (from 3445 cm−1 to 3437 cm−1), while after decrosslinking, a blue shift (from 3437 cm−1 to 3439 cm−1) was also observed. In 1H NMR spectra, the chemical shift of the hydroxyl proton peak shifted towards a lower field after crosslinking. Then, we investigated the influence of crosslinker concentration on foam (Fig. 1D). Without crosslinker, it can still form foam, but the bubbles of the foam were inhomogeneous and the foam was loose. When the crosslinker concentration increased to 0.25 M, a compact and homogeneous foam was obtained. However, when the crosslinker concentration increased to 0.5 M, the foam started to get over-crosslinked and became a hydrogel rather than foam at 1 M malic acid. This phenomenon may originate from that malic acid concentration has affected the strength of electrostatic interaction between carboxymethyl chitosan and sodium alginate by adjusting the pH value by malic acid and providing cross-linking sites. At low concentrations, sodium alginate exhibits a strong negative charge (-COO-), amino groups of carboxymethyl chitosan are protonated (-NH3+), and the ionization of carboxymethyl groups is inhibited, resulting in a strong positive charge on carboxymethyl chitosan. Positively charged carboxymethyl chitosan chains and negatively charged sodium alginate chains will form a dense and uniform three-dimensional network structure under the action of malic acid. At high concentrations of malic acid, some carboxyl groups on the sodium alginate molecular chain are forced to protonate (-COOH), resulting in a significant decrease in their negative charge density. Then, the complex fails to form a uniform network, and instead undergo excessive aggregation, forming irregular and rough aggregates, leading to phase separation or precipitation. This is consistent with our observation of water separation at 1M. The volume of foam was expanded with the help of surfactant (Fig. 1E). Typically, foam is formed with the help of surfactants like sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). However, this foam can only stable for a few minutes and SDS is toxic to humans. Here, we chose ethyl lauroyl arginine hydrochloride (LAE) as surfactant to form foam. LAE, has been approved by the FDA, is a biocompatible amino acid-type surfactant that has been known to degrade into CO2 and urea in vivo [34]. Here, the influence of the amount of surfactant on foam was also investigated. With different amounts of LAE, the volume expansion rate increases, but excessive LAE will lead to the loosening of foam. Therefore, the optimal surfactant concentration is 10 % (w/v).

As shown in Fig. 1F, at 0 M and 0.25 M malic acid, the bubbles were spherical, and the average sizes were 150.05 ± 11.53 μm and 72.14 ± 12.24 μm, respectively (Fig. 1G). At 0.5 M malic acid, the bubbles were irregular and the average size was 162.99 ± 14.68 μm. Though bubbles were founded at 1 M, it was not a typical foam (the average size was 142.98 ± 41.50 μm). Then, we compared different crosslinked foams for their response to gentle stress (Fig. S2A). Only the 0.25 M malic acid-crosslinked foam could retain its integrity after undergoing 50 % compression, while the bubbles of other groups dissipated and the foam collapsed. For intra-abdominal use, requires the foam could fully fill the abdominal cavity after injection. 0.25 M malic acid formed foam was the best candidate for further hemostasis tests. The stability of IHF was further measured under 37 °C and vibrational settings (Fig. 1H–J and Fig.S2B and C). Most importantly, IHF could be reformed by grinding or during injection with a syringe (Fig. S2D). This result not only demonstrated the superior stability of IHF, but also indicated that we could re-form IHF even after long-term storage, which is critical for emergency use.

Blood clotting ability of IHF

To rapidly achieve bleeding control, accelerating blood clotting is crucial [35]. Therefore, we foamed the hydrogel to increase its contact area with blood. We first estimated the blood clotting ability of IHF by monitoring the clotting time when in contact with whole blood (Fig. 3A and B). It takes 5.84 ± 0.28 min to form blood clots for whole blood under normal conditions (Fig. 3C). However, when blood interacts with IHF, the clotting time significantly decreased to 0.93 ± 0.16 min. Prior studies have shown a decrease in clotting time due to the addition of negatively charged particles to polymer hydrogels [36]. The SEM of blood clot is shown in Fig. 3D. In the control group, we can see red blood cells and thin fibrin mesh. However, in IHF-induced blood clot, red blood cells were massively concentrated, and more fibrin was generated between the bubble walls. Subsequently, we conducted statistical analysis on the fibrin diameter and platelet aggregation area of blood clots formed by the two methods based on SEM results (Fig. 3E and F). The results showed that although significantly more fibrin fibers were observed in the IHF group, there was no significant difference in diameter compared to the fibrin fibers in the control group. The area of platelet aggregation in the IHF group was significantly larger than that in the control group. This result perfectly aligns with our hypothesis that the bubbles of IHF would greatly enrich blood cells and coagulation factors, and by interacting with those coagulation factors, accelerate blood clot formation.

Blood clot observation and optical microscopy results further confirmed this assumption (Fig. S5A and B). Around the bubbles, there were aggregated blood cells. We also used rheological tests to assess blood clotting (Fig. 3G and H). The gelling point (clotting point) was around 1 min in the IHF-treated group and 5 min in the normal blood group. Fig. 3I shows the schematic diagram of IHF interacting with blood and promoting blood clotting. The bubbles of IHF greatly enrich blood cells and coagulation factors, and by interacting with those coagulation factors, accelerate blood clot formation.

Biocompatibility and biosafety of IHF

For the transition of IHF into clinical trials, it is imperative to understand the characteristics the biocompatibility and biosafety of IHF. The performance in terms of cytotoxicity, inflammation and toxicity of organ and other aspects is the threshold for the clinical translation of biomaterials [37,38]. Here, we conducted in vitro and in vivo characterizations to evaluate the biocompatibility and biodegradability of IHF. After 72-h co-culture with cell culture media (DMEM + 10%FBS), gelatin sponge, and IHF, the live/dead staining and CCK-8 assay results are shown in Fig. 4A and B. As shown in Fig. 4A, the survival rates of cells cultured on sponge and IHF at different concentrations for 1 day were all >90 %. After the cells were cultured for 1, 2 or 3 days, the absorbance at 450 nm gradually increased, as shown in Fig. 4B, indicating that the number of cells gradually increased. The above results indicate that all samples exhibited good biocompatibility.

The in vivo biocompatibility of IHF was assessed by subcutaneous implantation in rats for 1 day and 2 weeks (Fig. 4C and D). Histological assessment by a blinded pathologist demonstrated that IHF elicits very mild to mild inflammation, comparable to that of a commercially available gelatin sponge (Fig. S6). To further investigate the in vivo biosafety of IHF, we injected IHF into the abdominal cavity and inspected the main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) at 10 min and 2 weeks. Fig. 4E shows the representative H&E staining of the main organs after 2 weeks. H&E staining at 2 weeks revealed no obvious necrosis, congestion, or hemorrhaging. Then, a routine blood test was conducted (Fig. 4F). WBC count, Neu% (neutrophil percentage), Lym% (lymphocyte percentage), and Mon% (monocyte percentage) indicate that there is no obvious systemic inflammatory response after IHF treatment. RBC and PLT tests confirm that IHF is safe. These results demonstrate that IHF possesses good biocompatibility and biosafety.

The in vivo hemostatic performance of IHF in a rat bleeding liver model

The in vivo hemostatic performance of IHF was measured by using a rat liver bleeding model. An injury of 6 mm diameter and 2 mm depth was created, then the bleeding liver received different treatments (gelatin sponge, foam without norepinephrine, IHF, and non-treatment), and the hemostasis time and blood loss were recorded to evaluate the hemostatic performance of each group. Fig. 5A and B, and Movie S5 display the hemostasis process of IHF. When a discoidal wound was made on the rat liver and started to bleed, IHF was injected onto the wound site with a syringe to stop bleeding. Surprisingly, bleeding stopped immediately with minimal blood loss. Fig. 5C shows the hemostasis process with different treatments at different time points. In the control group and gelatin sponge treated group, the hemostasis indices (blood loss and hemostasis time) were far exceeded by IHF. For the control group, the blood loss was 2.08 ± 0.28 g and hemostasis time was 194.67 ± 24.58 s. For the gelatin sponge group, it took 108 ± 18.52 s and lost 1.47 ± 0.063 g blood to stop bleeding. Notably, no significant differences in blood loss or hemostasis time were observed between the IHF-treated group and the foam group without norepinephrine in this bleeding model. Statistics showed the blood loss of foam without norepinephrine and IHF group were 0.24 ± 0.12 g and 0.114 ± 0.026 g, hemostasis time were 35.33 ± 9.29 s and 28 ± 7.94 s, respectively (Fig. 5D and E). For further understanding the difference in hemostatic effect, we checked the wound site by SEM after hemostasis (Fig. 5G). From the SEM images of blood clotting, we can see that there is lots of fibrin meshes after hemostasis in gelatin sponge, foam without norepinephrine, and IHF-treated wounds. However, even though there is fibrin in the control group, the fibrin is much less when compared with the other three groups. As we know, fibrin mesh plays an important role in blocking blood vessels, thereby preventing bleeding. Therefore, the good hemostatic ability of our foam (including foam without norepinephrine and IHF) owes to the physical barriers and the activation of the coagulation pathway to accelerate hemostasis.

According to the SEM results, we counted the fibrin area ratio, and the IHF group showed the highest fibrin area ratio (Fig. 5H). To further validate the function of norepinephrine, H&E staining was conducted on the liver (Fig. 5I). Compared to gelatin sponge, foam without norepinephrine, and non-treatment, the vasoconstriction in the IHF-treated group was obvious. Statistics on blood vessel area showed that the IHF-treated group possessed the minimum area (Fig. S7). We also used the vasoconstriction index (shortest diameter/longest diameter) to evaluate the vasoconstriction. Results indicated that the loaded norepinephrine successfully affected the blood vessel area (Fig. 5J). The above results demonstrated that the designed foam strategy possesses excellent hemostatic capability. Fig. 5K shows the schematic diagram of IHF’s hemostasis and coagulation mechanism.

Hemostatic performance in coagulation-independent hemorrhage models in rats and rabbits

Under severe bleeding conditions, coagulation disorders may occur, so we further validated the effectiveness of hemostasis in cases of coagulation disorders. To mimic coagulation disordered bleeding conditions, heparinized rat and rabbit bleeding models were used to estimate the hemostatic ability of IHF (Fig. 6A). Here, because the blood loss of heparinized rats was very fast, we judged that bleeding for more than 5 min or the rat dying during the experiment was a hemostasis failure, and we terminated the experiment. Fig. 6B displays the hemostasis process of IHF in heparinized rats. Different from the normal rats, when an injury with a 6 mm diameter and a 2 mm depth was caused, the blood flowed quickly. The hemostatic performance of IHF in traumatic abdominal hemorrhage rat and porcine models

Here, we established an animal model in rats and pigs to mimic traumatic abdominal hemorrhage, specifically designed to evaluate the hemostatic efficacy of IHF in this traumatic setting. In a rat model of traumatic abdominal hemorrhage, a tiny hole was created in the upper abdomen to cause injuries to the liver and spleen (Fig. 8A). Fig. 8B andMovie S8 display the hemostasis process. First, a tiny hole (approximately 1 cm) was created to expose the rat liver and spleen, and injuries were caused to the liver and spleen, subsequently followed by immediate suturing of the abdominal incision. Subsequently, IHF was injected the abdominal cavity, and completely filling the cavity. After 5 min of treatment (determined from preliminary experiments), the abdomen was opened at the previously sutured incision to evaluate the hemostatic efficacy of IHF. Through urea-mediated degradation, IHF can be aspirated, thereby facilitating the observation of hemostatic efficacy in the liver and spleen (Fig. 8C). For intra-abdominal use, postoperative adhesions and other side effects should be considered after hemostasis. No obvious postoperative adhesions were observed in all rats undergoing IHF therapy after 1 week (Fig. 8C). If IHF was not removed after hemostasis, slight postoperative adhesions were found at 1 week. Therefore, it is necessary to completely remove IHF to avoid postoperative adhesions. It has been reported that polyurethane foam may cause intestinal pressure necrosis and focal intestinal bruising [41]. Therefore, we checked the main organs (including the heart, liver, stomach and colon etc.), and confirmed that IHF was safe whether it was removed or not (Fig. S10). Additionally, routine blood tests were conducted (Fig. 8D). WBC counts, Neu%, Lym% and Mon% reflect that there was no obvious systemic inflammatory response after IHF treatment. RBC and PLT tests indicated that IHF was safe.

To further evaluate the hemostatic ability of IHF in traumatic abdominal hemorrhage, we conducted an experiment on porcine. Laparoscopy was used to observe the hemostasis process. As shown in Fig. 8E, we established a pneumoperitoneum and then created a liver injury following laparoscopic surgical procedures. Firstly, a 3-cm linear incision was created to evaluate the hemostatic ability of IHF. When the wound was created, blood flowed out rapidly in the control group (Fig. S11A). However, when IHF was used, no obvious blood flow was observed. The hemostasis time in the control group was 13.52 ± 2.02 min, but IHF could stop bleeding in 5.99 ± 1.65 min (Fig. S11B).

After that, we conducted a traumatic abdominal hemorrhage experiment. A 5 × 5 cm cross injury was created, and massive bleeding occurred (Fig. 8F and Movie S9). IHF was injected into the intra-abdominal cavity, it fully filled the abdominal cavity and covered the wound site. After the bubbles of IHF were eliminated during urea-mediated degradation, we checked the injury site and found that the bleeding was completely stopped. The liquid state of IHF was then removed by negative pressure drainage. The IHF was totally removed, and the porcine abdomen was clean (Fig. S11C and Movie S9). We checked one porcine liver by opening the abdominal cavity, the liver was clean and no obvious massive blood was observed after IHF removal (Fig. S11D). This demonstrated that IHF possesses good hemostatic ability and easy to remove. Fig. 8G shows platelet counts, red blood cell counts, prothrombin time, and fibrinogen levels at preoperatively, postoperatively, and 2 weeks. Other indexes are shown in Tables S1–S4. Platelet counts decreased after hemostasis but increased 2 weeks postoperatively. These results indicated that after hemostasis with IHF, the porcine gradually recovered from massive bleeding. There was no obvious rebleeding phenomenon during the 2-week observation. Two weeks after hemostasis, we checked the postoperative adhesions (Fig. S11E). We founded some postoperative adhesions, but through careful checking of the surgical process, we believe that this is a problem that can be overcome by more careful cleaning. These results demonstrate that IHF possesses great potential to solve the problem of traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

Conclusion

In summary, we reported a bio foam material for the emergency hemostasis of traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage. IHF was inspired by physiological hemostasis. When it is applied to the bleeding site, norepinephrine in the IHF immediately induces vasoconstriction of blood vessels to reduce blood loss. Then, it triggers and accelerates primary hemostasis and secondary hemostasis as the bubbles of IHF aggregate platelets and red blood cells. IHF has been demonstrated to have effective hemostatic ability in rat and pig intra-abdominal hemorrhage models. Due to the good injectability, IHF can rapidly reach the injury site to create a local tamponade effect and provide wound site coverage when injected into the intra-abdominal cavity in an emergency hemorrhage model. The loaded norepinephrine induces vasoconstriction, and the foam interacts with blood to accelerate the formation of platelet thrombosis and blood coagulation. After hemostasis, IHF can be disrupted by urea and easily removed by negative pressure suction rather than laparotomy. Considering that the components of IHF are easy to obtain, the fabrication is simple, and IHF is effective in achieving hemostatic control of intra-abdominal hemorrhage, we believe our IHF possesses the potential for emergency treatment of non-compressible intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

Experimental section

Materials

Carboxymethyl chitosan, sodium alginate, ethyl lauroyl arginine hydrochloride, malic acid and Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) were purchased from Macklin Co. (Beijing, China). Urea was obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Norepinephrine was obtained from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). All reagents were used as received.

Synthesis of hemostatic IHF

To prepare a foam precursor solution, 2 g of sodium alginate was added into 100 mL DPBS and stirred rapidly and continuously. After sodium alginate was completely dissolved, 2 g of carboxymethyl chitosan was added into the above solution and a sodium alginate/carboxymethyl chitosan foam precursor solution was obtained. To form the injectable foam, 20 mL the precursor solution, 1 mL 0.25M crosslinker (malic acid), and the amino acid surfactant (ethyl lauroyl arginine hydrochloride) were transferred into a mortar and quickly ground. The obtained foam was sealed in a container and stored at 4 °C. For norepinephrine-loaded foam synthesis, norepinephrine at a 200,000:1 ratio was added into 100 mL DPBS. Then, the foam was loaded into a syringe before use.

Rheology test

Rheological experiments were conducted by TA Instruments (DHR-2). All experiments were performed at room temperature using a parallel plate geometry. The fixed frequency was set at 10 rad/s when varying the stress.

Optical microscopy observation

For optical microscopy observation, the foam was transferred onto a glass slide and observed using a Zeiss inverted microscope. ImageJ was used to analyze the size distributions of the bubbles. Foam was firstly applied to the glass slide and a cover glass was gently placed on top. For bubble size of each crosslinker concentration, we analyzed at least 3 different images. Observation of IHF-blood interaction was similar to the above experiments.

Tube inversion test

5 mL of heparinized rat blood was added into a 50 mL tube, then 15 mL IHF was injected into the tube. The tube was inverted and shaken to observe whether the blood drip down. To broke the hydrogen bonds, 1M urea was added into the IHF. Then, the tube was inverted and shaken to see whether the IHF was able to hold the blood.

Blood clotting test

Clotting time assay was performed according to a previously reported protocol [42]. Briefly, citrated blood was mixed with 0.1 M calcium chloride for 10 s. Then, 30 μL IHF was added into the 96-well plate, followed by 30 μL blood. At fixed time intervals, 100 μL of deionized water was added into examine whether the blood was clotting and record the clotting time.

SEM observation

For SEM, the samples were fixed in 4 % glutaraldehyde for 12 h. Then, samples were dehydrated in a gradient ethanol series (60 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, and 100 %) for 5–7 min at each concentration. After that, samples were dehydrated in a gradient tert-butanol series (60 %, 70 %, 80 %,90 %, and 100 %), for 5–7 min at each concentration. After drying, the samples were sputter-coated with gold and examined by SEM (Zeiss). In order to obtain statistical results, we randomly cut the normal coagulation process blood clots and IHF mediated blood clots after dehydration and drying, and used SEM to observe the internal fibrin and platelet conditions. After obtaining SEM images, use statistical software Image J to measure the diameter of fibrin and the area of platelet aggregation in the field of view.

Cytocompatibility

Cell co-culture was used to assess the biocompatibility of IHF. The NIH 3T3 cells were incubated in DMEM medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum, 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 95 % air, 5 % CO2. Gelatin sponge was used as control. Cells were plated at initial cell density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well. Then, 50 μL IHF was incubated with the cells for 24 h. The cell viability was determined by using a live/dead viability/cytotoxicity kit for mammalian cells.

Evaluation of in vivo biocompatibility and biodegradability

All animal studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Military Medical University of AMU (AMUWEC20224130). Sprague Dawley rats (220–250 g) were used in the study. IHF was prepared and sterilized by UV light for 3 h, firstly. After rats were anesthetized by 1 % pentobarbital, their hair was removed. A 1 cm incision was created on the back of each rat, and 1 mL IHF was implanted (n = 3). A corresponding mass of the gelatin sponge was used as control (n = 3). At 1 day and 2 weeks after implantation, the rats were euthanized. Corresponding subcutaneous regions were harvested and fixed in 4 % polyformaldehyde for 24 h for histological analyses.

Hemostatic performance on bleeding rat liver model

According to a previously reported protocol, Sprague Dawley rats (220–250 g) were anesthetized with 1 % pentobarbital [43]. Then, the abdomen was opened and the rat liver exposed. Pre-weighed filter paper was placed under the liver. A 6 mm diameter and 2 mm depth wound were created by biopsy punch. After it started to bleed, the wound was treated with four treatments to stop bleeding, including self-stopping bleeding (denoted as the control group), gelatin sponge treatment (denoted as the sponge group), foam without norepinephrine treatment (denoted as the foam group), and IHF treatment (denoted as the IHF group) (n = 3). The blood loss and hemostasis time were recorded. Rats were euthanized and the injured liver was harvested and fixed in 4 % polyformaldehyde for 24 h for histological and SEM analyses. The heparinized rat liver model was the same as normal bleeding rat liver model, but before the wound was created, heparin sodium (200 IU/kg) was intravenously administered via tail vein injection and waited for 10 min. Here, we judged that bleeding over 5 min or the rat dying during the experiment was a hemostasis failure and terminated the experiment.

Hemostatic performance in a bleeding model of heparinized rabbits

Heparinized rabbits were induced by following previously reported protocol [44]. Rabbits were first anesthetized with 1 % pentobarbital and the abdomen was opened to expose the liver. Then, heparin sodium (200 IU/kg) was intravenously administered via the marginal ear vein. After 10 min, pre-weighed filter paper was placed under the liver. A 10 mm diameter and 2 mm depth wound were created using a biopsy punch. After it started bleeding, the wound was treated with four treatments to stop bleeding, including self-stopping bleeding (denoted as the control group), gelatin sponge treatment (denoted as the sponge group), foam without norepinephrine treatment (denoted as the foam group), and IHF treatment (denoted as the IHF group) (n = 3). The blood loss and hemostasis time were recorded. Here, we judged that bleeding over 10 min or the rabbit died during the experiment was hemostasis failure and terminated the experiment.

Traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage model in rats

Animals were anesthetized with 1 % pentobarbital. A tiny hole was created in the upper abdomen, cause bleeding on the liver and spleen. Then, IHF was injected into the intra-abdominal from the lower abdomen using a syringe. The abdomen was opened to evaluate hemostasis after 5 min. Then, the IHF was removed, and the rats were observed for 2 weeks to evaluate postoperative adhesions (n = 3).

Traumatic intra-abdominal hemorrhage model in porcine liver

Pigs (about 40 kg) were anesthetized with propofol, and isoflurane was used to maintain anesthesia. Laparoscopic intervention was conducted. To expose the liver, the abdominal wall was bulged by CO2. We first estimated the hemostatic ability of IHF. A 3 cm wound was created, and gauze was used as control. After that, a 5 × 5 cm cross-shaped wound was created, and IHF was delivered to the abdominal till IHF fully filled the abdominal cavity. At set time, the bubbles of IHF were eliminated, and IHF was removed by a negative pressure drainage system. Whole blood was harvested preoperatively, postoperatively, and 2 weeks after operation for routine blood test. After 2 weeks, pigs were euthanized, and the tissues were harvested.

Statistical analysis

For each experiment, at least three samples were tested, and data were presented as means ± SD (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s test for statistical analysis (GraphPad Prism 9.0).
