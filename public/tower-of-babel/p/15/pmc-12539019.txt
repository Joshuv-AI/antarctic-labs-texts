Background

It has been previously stated that up to 20% of patients with invasively proven heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) have natriuretic peptide (NP) levels below diagnostic thresholds [1]. Patients with this type of heart failure are prone to a misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis. Herein, we report a rare case of a flash pulmonary edema patient with a primary diagnosis of HFpEF caused by paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), notably accompanied by normal B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels. HFpEF represents a critical cardiac dysfunction, frequently complicated by acute respiratory failure [2]. The hallmark pulmonary edema develops when elevated left ventricular pressures trigger rapid fluid shifts into lung tissue, severely compromising oxygenation [3]. While pulmonary edema can arise from diverse etiologies—such as neurogenic triggers (e.g., sympathetic storm following intracranial hemorrhage) [4], high-altitude pulmonary edema [5], pharmacotoxic insults [6], or negative pressure-induced airway obstruction [7]—the cardiogenic subtype remains the most clinically consequential due to its abrupt hemodynamic decompensation and high mortality risk [8]. This etiological heterogeneity underscores the critical importance of precise diagnostic differentiation, as targeted management hinges on identifying the underlying pathophysiological mechanism. Through the study of this case, clinicians can strengthen their understanding of atypical heart failure and deepen the understanding of the imaging manifestations of flash pulmonary edema to reduce the number of misdiagnoses and missed diagnoses.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old man without a history of any cardiovascular disease (palpitations, exertional dyspnea, or other heart failure symptoms) was admitted to an inpatient hospital due to a one-week history of exertional chest tightness and dyspnea, accompanied by mild nausea and retching. He also reported occasional nonproductive cough. An electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained at a local hospital showed paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). He denied having any symptoms of infection, dyspnea, chest pain, or other conditions during the past year. On admission, the patient’s N-terminal pro-BNP (NT-proBNP) level was 516 ng/L (normal range: <900 ng/L), and cardiac troponin I level was less than 0.010 ng/mL (normal range: 0.01–0.023 ng/mL). On admission, laboratory tests revealed hypokalemia (serum potassium: 3.27 mmol/L; normal range: 3.5–5.3 mmol/L), with normal sodium, chloride, and calcium levels. Cardiac enzyme profiles—including lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase (CK), MB isoenzyme of CK, and myoglobin—were within normal limits. Thyroid function tests (free triiodothyronine, free thyroxine, and serum thyroid-stimulating hormone) also showed no abnormalities. The admission ECG revealed sinus rhythm without ischemic changes (Fig. 1a). Six hours after admission, he experienced paroxysmal palpitations at 163 bpm, and his ECG revealed SVT (Fig. 1b). A CT scan of the chest indicated multiple patchy ground-glass opacity shadows in both lungs with blurry boundaries (Fig. 1c). His left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 68%. The patient was given diagnostic treatment for heart failure (lyophilized recombinant human BNP and furosemide). After 3 days, a CT scan of the chest showed that the lung lesions had improved (Fig. 1d). After the patient’s condition stabilized, coronary angiography and renal arteriography were performed, and the results showed no obvious stenosis. The intracardiac electrophysiological examination showed a left concealed accessory pathway (Fig. 1e). After radiofrequency catheter ablation at 5 days, there was no recurrence of the SVT. Finally, the patient was diagnosed with HFpEF.

Discussion

According to current guidelines, HFpEF is defined as signs and symptoms of heart failure (i.e., dyspnea or fatigue) with an LVEF ≥ 50% and an elevated pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) ≥ 15 mmHg at rest and/or ≥ 25 mmHg during exercise [9]. In the clinical setting, some patients refuse to undergo invasive PAWP examination, and the diagnosis of HFpEF depends on clinical symptoms, signs and BNP levels. However, previous studies and guidelines have reported that some patients with HFpEF have a normal BNP level [10]. The present case demonstrates this diagnostic paradox—a patient with HFpEF presenting normal BNP level and a normal LVEF and, whose conditions significantly improved after being treated for heart failure.

The pulmonary imaging findings were another clinical feature of this patient. Acute decompensated heart failure can cause flash pulmonary edema. Any factor that provokes an imbalance in pulmonary fluid homeostasis and damages the pulmonary capillary endothelium will accelerate the accumulation of fluid within the pulmonary interstitium and alveoli [11]. Aligns with the present case, Koh et al. reported a Flumazenil-induced HFpEF accompanied normal BNP level and pulmonary edema. After administering antihypertensive and diuretic drugs, cardiothoracic ratio or pulmonary edema was improved [12]. These cases underscored the critical role of definitive etiological identification in guiding pulmonary edema management.

When patients present with flash pulmonary edema, many different diagnoses should be considered based on the risk factors for heart failure alone. Moreover, other potentially life-threatening causes of heart failure should be considered. Potential triggers of flash pulmonary edema include arrythmia, acute coronary syndrome, sepsis, worsening hypertension, medication nonadherence, acute renal artery stenosis, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, acute respiratory distress syndrome, stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy, neurological causes, and pulmonary embolism [3, 13]. It has been reported that left bundle branch block (LBBB) may also contribute to pulmonary edema pathogenesis. Cardiac imaging revealed preserved systolic function at rest, while exercise stress testing triggered sinus tachycardia with LBBB and recurrent pulmonary edema episodes, demonstrating the dynamic nature of this association [14]. In addition, SVT can induce pulmonary edema primarily through acute hemodynamic disturbances that precipitate left heart failure. The underlying mechanisms include that (1) tachycardia-induced shortening of cardiac diastole leads to inadequate left ventricular filling and elevated left atrial pressure, elevated pulmonary venous pressure and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, resulting in fluid leakage from capillaries into alveolar and interstitial spaces; (2) excessive catecholamine release during SVT causes sympathetic overdrive, leading to pulmonary vasoconstriction and increased pulmonary vascular resistance; and (3) the rapid heart rate exacerbates myocardial ischemia by increasing oxygen demand while reducing coronary perfusion time, further impairing cardiac function [15].

While numerous case reports of pulmonary edema exist, no previous publications have documented cases induced by paroxysmal SVT that presented without typical dyspnea symptoms and showed negative BNP levels, representing a clinically atypical manifestation [12, 16]. In this case, the patient with flash pulmonary edema also denied having symptoms of dyspnea. A 3-month post-discharge telephone follow-up revealed no recurrence of palpitations or chest tightness. Therefore, flash pulmonary edema is a medical emergency in which immediate recognition can be life-saving, especially when patients do not have typical clinical manifestations.
