Implementation and Evaluation of a Remote, Questionnaire-Based Model for Monitoring Patients Following Hip or Knee Replacement Surgery
Authors: Stephen D Gill, Hugh Gillies, Sally Beattie, Certificate of General Nursing, Kirsten Porter, Alex Gentle, Andrew Wilkinson, Janak Jaysuriya, Richard Page
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe benefits of long-term follow-up of patients following hip and knee joint replacement surgery are currently debated. This study evaluated a questionnaire-based model for remotely monitoring patients. Primary outcomes included response rates, acceptability to patients, and whether remote review identified patients requiring reoperation.MethodsData were collected over a 7-year period from a large regional teaching hospital in Australia. Hard-copy questionnaires were sent to patients at 1, 5, and 8 years after joint replacement and biennially thereafter. The questionnaire assessed whether patients had pain or concerns regarding their joint replacement and whether orthopedic review was requested. Response rate was determined as the proportion of patients who returned the questionnaire and was considered acceptable if it was not significantly lower than the proportion of patients who attended a 1-year in-person appointment in the 3 years prior to introducing remote-review. Patient preferences for remote versus in-person review were collected from a subgroup of patients (n = 488) using an investigator-designed questionnaire. All reoperations during the study period were examined to determine if they were first identified via the remote-review questionnaire.ResultsThe response rate at 1 year was 69.3% (2,266 responses from 3,268 questionnaires sent), which was significantly lower than historical in-person outpatient attendance rates (82.7%, p < 0.001). A majority of patients (62.1%) preferred remote follow-up to in-person review. Of the 116 reoperations that occurred during the study period, remote review identified 5 patients, representing 0.1% of questionnaire responses. Most complications requiring reoperation were first identified via emergency departments (56.9%) or general practice (31.9%).ConclusionsWhile remote review following joint replacement surgery was consistent with most patients’ preferences for follow-up care, response rates were lower than historical attendance rates for in-person outpatient appointments. Routine remote review was an inefficient mechanism for identifying complications requiring reoperation, most of which were first identified via emergency departments and general practice.Keywords: Arthroplasty, Hip, Knee, Remote consultation

Despite the success of hip and knee joint replacement surgery, complications can occur at any time following surgery and affect approximately 5%–10% of patients;1) hence, reliable methods to identify and treat these complications in a timely manner are necessary. Traditionally, guidelines recommended long-term monitoring of patients following joint replacement to identify complications that can occur many years after surgery.2,3) For example, at our Australian public health service, patients were usually reviewed in person at 3 and 6 months, then at 1, 5, and 8 years, and then biennially for the remainder of their lives. This monitoring schedule placed large demands on patients, caregivers, and outpatient services, challenged our ability to provide timely care to other patients, and identified few complications requiring reoperation.4)

The value of long-term follow-up following joint replacement has been questioned, particularly for resourceintensive methods like outpatient appointments.5) Australian guidelines no longer stipulate regular follow-up of low-risk patients and prostheses, although surgeon discretion is still required. Monitoring patients who might be at greater risk of revision, such as younger, active, and/or heavier patients, those with metal-on-metal articulations, and those with new prostheses without a track record, is recommended.2) Internationally, there is wide variation in recommendations, most of which rely on expert consensus rather than data from clinical studies.6) Although guidelines indicate that follow-up can be done remotely with questionnaires rather than in person, there is limited evidence to support this.2,5)

Barwon Health Orthopaedic Department operates the Barwon Joint Registry (BJR), which is a clinical quality registry that routinely assesses patients before and after joint replacement surgery using a variety of patient-reported outcome measures.7,8) The assessment schedule corresponds to the historical in-person review schedule at our health service (e.g., 1, 5, 8 years, etc.). Consistent with international trends, our service began transitioning away from in-person review after joint replacement in 2017, which afforded the opportunity to implement and evaluate a remote, questionnaire-based model of monitoring patients, which built upon the established infrastructure of the BJR.

The current study describes our experiences establishing a remote model for monitoring patients following hip or knee joint replacement and reports our evaluation of the service with respect to response rates, acceptability to patients, and its ability to identify complications requiring reoperation. A secondary aim was to evaluate whether patient characteristics could predict answers to the remote-review questionnaire.

METHODS

Institutional Human Research Ethics Committee approval was obtained for the study (Barwon Health, ref 19/35). Formal written informed consent was not required due to a waiver by the Ethics Committee.

Setting

The study was conducted at a large publicly funded regional Australian health service that provides healthcare to approximately 350,000 people. In the year preceding the study, the orthopedic department conducted 346 and 217 primary hip and knee replacements respectively, which were completed by a team of 12 orthopedic surgeons. The orthopedic department manages the BJR, which is a clinical quality registry that routinely monitors patients before and after elective joint replacement surgery using a variety of validated patient-reported outcome measures sent to patients in hardcopy via traditional mail. Intermittently, the BJR reconciles its database against the government administered registry of deaths, which was last completed in 2018. Prior to introducing the remote model of care, patients were typically seen in person in the outpatient department at 3 months postoperatively, then at 1, 5, and 8 years, and then biennially thereafter. Patients who were considered by their surgeon to be at higher risk of complication, such as those with metal-on-metal prostheses or prostheses without proven track record, were reviewed at the surgeon’s discretion.

Remote Model of Care

The remote model involved a questionnaire, which was mailed to each patient’s home with a reply-paid envelope. The aim of this Joint Screening Questionnaire (JSQ) (Table 1) was to identify patients who might have a complication or emerging complication, which, once identified, would trigger further assessment and management as required. The 5 JSQ questions were developed locally by the service’s orthopedic team including surgeons, physiotherapists, and staff from the BJR. The JSQ was tested, refined, and approved by patients from the study organization’s consumer involvement committee (“Written Information Simply Explained Committee”) to ensure the questions were understood as intended, which was part of the organization’s standard process for developing written patient information.

Joint Screening Questionnaire

Returned JSQs were screened by administrative staff, and responses were entered into the BJR. Patients whose responses indicated a potential or emerging complication (i.e., answered “yes” or “unsure” to 1 or more questions) were contacted by a surgeon, orthopedic registrar, or orthopedic nurse to clarify their responses, and an outpatient appointment was booked as needed. Patients who did not return the JSQ within 6 weeks were contacted once via phone by administrative staff and asked to return the questionnaire. The JSQ was implemented for patients scheduled for review at 1 or more years postoperatively (e.g., 1, 5, or 8 years). In-person outpatient review continued at 3 months postoperatively.

Data Collection, Evaluation, and Statistical analysis

Feasibility of the remote monitoring program was evaluated using all JSQ data collected since the implementation of the program in March 2017 to March 2024. Response rate was determined as the proportion of patients who returned the JSQ. We considered an acceptable response rate at 1-year follow-up to be equivalent to the proportion of patients who attended their 1-year in-person appointment in the 3 years prior to introducing the remote model of care. Significance was assessed using the chi-square statistic. Patients who returned the JSQ at 1 or 5 years underwent additional analysis to determine the proportion of respondents who received subsequent outpatient review, together with any subsequent changes to patient management as documented in the patient’s medical history, which included reoperation and referral to other healthcare providers (excluding general practitioners) for new treatment such as physiotherapy. Responses at 1 and 5 years after operation constituted almost 70% of the responses; these data were selected for the additional analysis as electronic medical records were available for these patients, from which operation reports and other patient characteristics such as body mass index (BMI) and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score9) could be retrieved.

To determine patient preferences for postoperative monitoring, all 662 patients who were sent the JSQ between February 2020 and February 2021 were concurrently sent an additional questionnaire designed by the investigators to determine the patient’s preferences for inperson versus remote review. Binary logistic regression was used to determine whether a patient’s age, sex (male or female), BMI (according to World Health Organization [WHO] categories), ASA score,9) socioeconomic status (presumed using postcodes linked to the Australian Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) 2021 Index of Relative Socio-economic Advantage and Disadvantage),10) joint replaced (hip or knee), employment status (working or not), and travel time to the outpatient department were associated with patients’ preferences for in-person versus remote follow-up.

To investigate if patient characteristics were associated with JSQ responses, binary logistic regression was used to predict patients selecting “yes” or “unsure” to 1 or more JSQ questions at 1- and 5-year follow-up, using the following predictor variables: age, sex (male or female), BMI (according to WHO categories), ASA score,9) socioeconomic status (presumed using postcodes linked to the Australian SEIFA 2021 Index of Relative Socio-economic Advantage and Disadvantage),10) and joint replaced (hip or knee). Binary logistic regression was repeated with a subgroup of patients (n = 410) who had also indicated their preference for in-person versus remote follow-up (using the investigator-designed questionnaire mentioned previously)—this preference was added as a predictor in the model.

To determine the number and proportion of reoperations occurring at the study site that were triggered via the JSQ, all reoperations (excluding repeat revisions) that occurred at the study site between March 2017 and March 2024, as identified via the BJR, were inspected using patient medical records to determine the pathway by which patients were identified, such as emergency department presentation or general practitioner referral.

Questionnaire data were stored and managed within Socrates and REDCap databases.11,12) Analyses were completed using Microsoft Excel version 16.85 (Microsoft) and IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp.). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

RESULTS

Patient Responses to the JSQ

During the 7 years of data collection, the JSQ response rate at 1 year after operation was 69.3%, which was significantly lower than historical in-person outpatient attendance rates (82.7%, p < 0.001). At 1 year, 610 of 2,266 respondents (26.9%) provided at least 1 response of “yes” or “unsure,” and 228 respondents (10.1%) subsequently received an outpatient appointment, either in person (n = 146, 6.4%) or via telephone (n = 82, 3.6%). Thirty-seven patients (1.6% of respondents) had a documented change in management, most commonly referral to physiotherapy (n = 28, 1.2%) or a pain management clinic (n = 4, 0.2%). One patient was referred to neurosurgery for a lumbar vertebra facet joint synovial cyst which caused knee pain. Four patients (0.2%) received a corticosteroid injection. The 1-year JSQ was not responsible for identifying any patients who subsequently required reoperation of their joint replacement.

At 5 years, 231 of 903 respondents (25.6%) provided at least 1 “yes” or “unsure” response, of which 76 respondents (8.4%) received an outpatient appointment, either in person (n = 48, 5.3%) or via phone (n = 28, 3.1%). Twelve patients (1.3%) had a documented change in management, most commonly referral to physiotherapy (n = 10, 1.1%). Two patients (0.2%) had surgery following a JSQ triggered outpatient review. One patient had a symptomatic neuroma removed that was adjacent to the surgical scar. The other patient had revision surgery for aseptic loosening following a right hip replacement completed at another hospital 10 years earlier. This patient used the 5-year JSQ for their left hip to report symptoms regarding their right hip.

Patient Preferences for Follow-up

Of 662 patients, 488 provided information on their preferred method for postoperative follow-up, of whom 60.2% were female, 64.2% had knee replacements, and the mean age was 69.7 years (standard deviation [SD], 9.6). Remote review was the preferred format for almost two-thirds of respondents (n = 303, 62.1%), using traditional mailout (n = 194, 39.8% of responders), phone or video consultation (n = 55, 11.3%), or electronic mailout (n = 54, 11.1%). Inperson review was preferred by 37.9% of respondents (n = 185). Binary logistic regression indicated that distance travelled was the only significant predictor of patient preference for in-person versus remote care, with increased distance predicting a preference for remote care (B = 0.009, Exp(B) = 1.009; 95% CI, 1.001–1.017). The joint replaced approached significance (p = 0.058); patients with knee replacement were more likely to prefer in-person care. The model was statistically significant, χ2(8)= 15.79 (p = 0.045), and correctly explained 64.5% of cases.

Predicting Patient Responses to the JSQ

Binary logistic regression indicated that knee replacement, higher ASA score, and lower age were associated with an increased likelihood of responding “yes” or “unsure” to at least 1 JSQ question (p < 0.05). At 5 years, ASA score was the only significant predictor of JSQ responses. Increasing ASA score was associated with an increased likelihood of responding “yes” or “unsure.”

The analysis was repeated to include patients’ preferences for in-person versus remote care, including the 410 patients who returned the JSQ at 1 year and returned the survey indicating their preference for follow-up. The model was statistically significant, χ2(7)= 21.7 (p = 0.003), and correctly explained 71.0% of cases. The only significant predictor in the secondary analysis was the patient’s preference for follow-up care. Patients who preferred inperson care, compared to remote care, were 2.6 times more likely to respond “yes” or “unsure” to 1 or more JSQ questions (B = 0.966, Exp(B) = 2.627; 95% CI, 1.687–4.091).

Reoperations

There were 116 reoperations during the 7-year study period, including 70 hips and 46 knees. The mean time since the primary operation was 9.7 years (SD, 8.0) for hips and 10.2 years (SD, 8.9) for knees. Fifty-six reoperations (48.3%) were major, which replaced components fixed to bone on both sides of the joint. The mean patient age at reoperation was 72.3 years (SD, 9.5), and 52 (44.8%) were female. Forty-two patients (36.2%) had trauma preceding the complication, such as a fall. The remaining 74 patients (63.8%) were all symptomatic, with the primary symptoms being joint-related pain (n = 64) or erythema, swelling, or wound discharge (n = 10). The most common complications were infection (n = 41, 35.3%) or aseptic loosening (n = 22, 19.0%) (Table 3). Reoperation for infection most commonly occurred within the first year following the primary operation, and aseptic loosening occurred at 11–20 years (Fig. 1).

DISCUSSION

The current study describes the implementation and evaluation of a remote questionnaire-based model for monitoring patients following hip or knee joint replacement at a large regional Australian hospital over a 7-year period. The most important findings from the current study are as follows. The study found that JSQ response rates were lower than historical outpatient attendance rates. Approximately 70% of patients returned the JSQ at 1 year, which declined significantly thereafter. The JSQ was consistent with most patients’ (62.1%) preferences for remote follow-up rather than in-person review. Greater travel distance to the hospital was the only significant predictor of a preference for remote review. The JSQ identified 5 patients requiring reoperation, representing 0.1% of JSQs returned. Most patients requiring reoperation were identified via an emergency department (56.9%) or general practice (31.9%). Patient preference for in-person follow-up was the strongest predictor of JSQ responses.

Response rates for the JSQ were lower than historical outpatient attendance rates to our service, suggesting that remote review altered patients’ willingness or ability to participate in postoperative monitoring. Another Australian study that used questionnaire-based follow-up of hip and knee replacement patients reported a similar overall response rate (36%) and included patients up to 16 years after joint replacement.13) Internationally, response rates have varied considerably (40% to 92%), which might suggest the influence of contextual and cultural factors.14,15,16) Our 1-year response rate (69.3%) compares favorably with national patient-reported outcome programs that have reported response rates of 62.3% at 6 months in Australia17) and less than 50% in the United Kingdom in recent years.18) In our study, JSQ return rates decreased significantly at 5 years and beyond, which is consistent with inperson review19) and might indicate patients’ declining interest in having a well-functioning joint replacement reviewed and patients being lost to follow-up. The health care needs of patients who did not return the JSQ are unknown, which is a limitation that is not unique to remote review and occurs when patients do not attend in-person appointments.

Implementing the JSQ led to a dramatic reduction in the number of outpatient appointments for monitoring patients following joint replacement. Over the 7 years of JSQ use, just 228 outpatient appointments were booked for over 3,000 patients who received the JSQ at 1 year, with similar proportions at 5 years. Other studies regarding remote review reported similar results with 7%–23% of patients receiving a subsequent outpatient appointment. 13,16,20) Remote follow-up reduces demand for outpatient appointments, though administrator and clinician resources are still required to distribute questionnaires and review patient responses. At our service, the administrative burden was lessened because the JSQ was incorporated into the BJR, which routinely monitors patients with patient-reported outcome measures at 1 year and 5 years, and regularly thereafter. Other studies indicated that remote follow-up had lower costs from healthcare and societal perspectives than outpatient review.16,21,22)

Patient-centered care requires that patient preferences are understood and respected.23) Our study determined that a remote model of care was consistent with most patients’ preferences for follow-up, with traditional hard copy questionnaires being the most popular method, ahead of electronic questionnaires and phone or video links. Travel distance was the only significant predictor of follow-up preference. Not surprisingly, patients with longer commute times to the health service were more likely to prefer remote review. In other studies, patient preferences for remote review varied, with 41% to 70% of respondents indicating a preference for remote review.14,20,24) Preferences will vary according to individual’s circumstances and values. Patients who attend outpatient appointments appear to value this method of follow-up. Barrack et al.24) reported that almost 90% of patients who attended an outpatient appointment for joint monitoring indicated it was worth their time, effort, and cost of attending, despite spending 4 hours on mean to attend the appointment including travel time. In our study, the best predictor of patients’ JSQ responses was a preference for in-person review, suggesting that some patients provided answers that might reasonably be expected to increase the likelihood of subsequent outpatient review. Individualizing care is challenging for health services—providing a variety of follow-up options is logistically more complicated; however, implementing just 1 follow-up method will not suit all patients’ preferences.

The JSQ had limited utility for identifying complications requiring reoperation, with most complications first identified in emergency departments or general practice. Combined with our earlier research and that of others, routine surveillance of hip and knee arthroplasty, whether via questionnaire or outpatient appointments, is inefficient for identifying complications requiring reoperation.4,24,25,26) Currently, follow-up practices vary widely between regions and countries, from discharge at 6 weeks to lifelong monitoring,27) reflecting large variations in published recommendations, which lack high-quality evidence and are based on consensus among experts rather than clinical studies.6) Recently, Kingsbury et al.6) conducted a mixedmethods investigation using literature review, national and multi-centre datasets, interviews and economic modelling to construct recommendations for hip and knee arthroplasty follow-up. For ODEP 10A minimum prostheses, they concluded that it was safe to disinvest in routine follow-up, provided there is rapid access to orthopedic review should it be required. Beyond 10 years, there was insufficient data to guide practice, so the authors recommended that all patients should be given the opportunity to represent for review, which could include remote review. Our results indicated that routine remote follow-up of patients beyond 10 years was no more effective than for patients under 10 years after joint replacement and was low-yield for identifying complications requiring reoperation. Consequently, our organization made an evidenceinformed decision to disinvest in the JSQ for monitoring patients following hip or knee joint replacement surgery.

Primary limitations to the current study include patients who were lost to follow-up via the JSQ and whether they needed reoperation or had a reoperation at another health service. Our follow-up method did not include a radiograph, which some authors suggested is necessary for asymptomatic complications, such as aseptic loosening that might warrant early revision before significant bone loss occurs.26,28) The JSQ combined with a radiograph might have detected a higher proportion of complications. Although we assessed patient preferences for remote follow-up, we did not directly assess satisfaction with the JSQ. Others have reported high satisfaction rates with remote follow-up following joint replacement surgery.16) We compared JSQ response rates to outpatient attendance rates at 1 year because our outpatient data were most reliable at this follow-up time; however, it is possible that attendance rates at 5, 8, and other years would have been similar to JSQ return rates at these time points.

Remote review following joint replacement surgery was consistent with most patients’ preferences for followup care, although response rates were lower than historical attendance rates at outpatient appointments. Routine remote review was inefficient for identifying complications requiring reoperation. The current study provides further evidence to justify disinvesting in routine follow-up of patients with proven prostheses, provided rapid access to orthopedic care is provided via other health services such as emergency departments and general practice. Study results have informed an evidence-based decision at our organization to disinvest in routine follow-up of patients following hip or knee joint replacement surgery.
