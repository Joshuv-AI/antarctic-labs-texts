Imposterism and Self‐Efficacy Among Canadian Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Residents: A National Survey
Authors: Alexander C. Moise, Ying Jie Li, Yvonne Chan, M. Elise Graham, Amanda Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo assess the national prevalence and severity of imposter phenomenon (IP) among Canadian Otolaryngology—Head and Neck Surgery (OHNS) residents and to explore associations between IP and demographic factors.MethodsNational cross‐sectional anonymized online survey was distributed to residents in Canadian OHNS programs in English and French. It included two validated instruments: the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) and the General Self‐Efficacy Scale (GSES). Dillman Tailored Design Method was used for survey distribution over an eight‐week period with four waves of email contact. CIPS and GSES scores were analyzed using t‐tests and ANOVA.ResultsFifty‐six OHNS residents participated (response rate: 34%), including 29 men and 27 women. Mean CIPS score was 61.43 (SD 15.52), with 44.64% within the “significant” impostorism range (CIPS score 61–80). Women reported significantly higher impostor scores than men (mean CIPS: 65.85 vs. 57.31; p = 0.038). Among those who experienced intense impostorism (CIPS > 80), 71.42% were women and 28.57% men. Year of residency was significantly associated with IP (p = 0.024), with PGY‐2 residents exhibiting the highest CIPS scores (71.40) compared to PGY‐5 (48.17). Mean GSES was 31.75 (SD 3.46), with no significant variation across demographic groups. However, self‐efficacy was inversely correlated with IP (Pearson's r = −0.366, p = 0.005).ConclusionIP is prevalent in OHNS residency, particularly among women, and appears associated with lower self‐efficacy and earlier stages of training. These findings underscore the need for wellness initiatives and structured support.Level of EvidenceN/A.

Introduction

Impostor syndrome (IS), also known as the impostor phenomenon (IP), is characterized by persistent feelings of self‐doubt and professional inadequacy despite demonstrable achievements. Individuals with IP frequently believe they have deceived others regarding their competence and live in fear of being exposed as frauds. In the context of medical education, IP is common, with studies reporting prevalence rates ranging from 30% to over 90% among medical trainees [1, 2]. The phenomenon is particularly prevalent among women and those in high‐performance, high‐pressure environments, where clinical decisions and technical competence are constantly scrutinized [3, 4, 5]. The psychological ramifications of IP are substantial, with demonstrated associations to burnout, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and lower self‐esteem [6]. Importantly, IP may negatively affect clinical learning, team dynamics, and ultimately patient care.

Residency training is an especially vulnerable period for the emergence and reinforcement of IP. During this stage, physicians in training are repeatedly evaluated while adapting to steep learning curves and high‐stakes responsibilities. Surgical residents, in particular, are expected to perform at high technical and professional levels while navigating hierarchical structures and frequent transitions between rotations. Previous research has documented the presence of IP in a variety of surgical specialties, including general surgery, neurosurgery, and orthopedic surgery, with alarming rates of moderate to severe IP [7, 8]. Despite the increasing attention to IP in surgical disciplines, no study to date has comprehensively assessed its prevalence and correlates among Canadian Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery (OHNS) residents. Given the intensity of OHNS residency training and the procedural complexity of the specialty, the potential for significant impostor experiences warrants investigation.

Moreover, prior studies have largely been limited in scope by their single‐institution design or focus on gender‐specific or faculty‐level populations, thereby failing to account for specialty‐specific training environments and the full spectrum of residency [9, 10]. This study addresses these gaps by surveying all Canadian OHNS residents using validated tools to assess impostor syndrome and self‐efficacy. The inclusion of residents from both English‐ and French‐language programs allows for a bilingual, nationally representative perspective. Our primary objectives were to determine the prevalence and severity of IP among Canadian OHNS residents, examine its relationship with general self‐efficacy, and identify demographic or training‐related correlates that may place certain subgroups at greater risk. Our hypotheses were that there is a high prevalence of IP among OHNS residents. Certain demographic groups, like women and more junior residents, are at higher risk of IP.

Methods

This was a national, cross‐sectional survey of all currently enrolled residents in Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery (OHNS) programs across Canada. The study was approved by a university‐affiliated research ethics board (#H24‐03795‐A001).

All residents enrolled in any of the 13 accredited Canadian OHNS residency programs were eligible to participate. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The survey was administered in both English and French to ensure accessibility across Canada's bilingual training landscape. Survey administration and data collection were conducted via Qualtrics (Vancouver, 2025), a secure web‐based survey platform that is compliant with relevant Canadian privacy legislation [11]. The survey was open for 8 weeks from March 24 to May 19, 2025.

We obtained the full list of institutional email addresses for OHNS residents through direct communication with program directors and program administrators, using the official distribution lists each program uses to contact their residents. Survey distribution followed Dillman's Tailored Design Method [12]. In week 1, an initial invitation to participate with the survey link was sent out. In week 2 and week 4, reminder emails were sent to non‐responders. In week 7, a final email reminder was sent and in week 8, data collection was closed. Respondents were informed that the survey was anonymous and that all questions were optional. All participants were entered into a draw for a $100 gift certificate.

The survey instrument consisted of three parts. First, the 20‐item Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) was used to measure the degree of impostor feelings [13]. Respondents rated each item on a five‐point Likert scale, generating a cumulative score ranging from 20 to 100. Standard cutoffs were applied to categorize scores as low, moderate, significant, or intense impostorism. Second, the 10‐item General Self‐Efficacy Scale (GSES) was administered to assess residents' confidence in managing challenges and achieving goals [14]. Each item was rated on a four‐point Likert scale, yielding a total score from 10 to 40. Higher scores indicate greater perceived self‐efficacy. Both CIPS and GSES are validated instruments with established psychometric properties. Lastly, a series of demographic and training‐related questions were included, such as age, gender, postgraduate year (PGY), language of training, ethnicity, academic background, and history of accessing wellness or mental health supports. The full questionnaire is available in Supporting Information S1.

Survey questions were translated into French by bilingual researchers and verified through back‐translation to ensure semantic equivalence.

Data were downloaded from Qualtrics and analyzed using SPSS (version 31; IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) [15]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of CIPS and GSES scores. Continuous variables were compared using t‐tests or ANOVA, with nonparametric alternatives (Wilcoxon or Kruskal–Wallis) employed when data were not normally distributed. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. Correlations between impostorism and self‐efficacy were examined using Pearson or Spearman coefficients, depending on normality. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Mean CIPS score was 61.43 (SD 15.52), which is considered significant IP. Based on standardized CIPS scoring employed in previous studies [16], scores 0–40 suggest mild IS, 41–60 being moderate IS, 61–80 indicate significant IS, and 81–100 correlate to intense IS. Table 2 demonstrates CIPS categories for the cohort. Based on CIPS scores, 14.29% of residents reported mild IS, 28.57% moderate, 44.64% significant, and 12.50% intense impostorism. Of those who experienced intense IS, 71.42% were female (Table 2). Despite the high prevalence of moderate to intense impostorism, 76.64% of respondents reported that they had not sought formal support.

General Self‐Efficacy

The mean GSES score for the cohort was 31.75 (SD 3.46). GSES did not significantly differ across gender, ethnicity, relationship status, presence of children, training year, medical school location, or residency program (all p > 0.05, Table 4).

Relationship Between Impostorism and Self‐Efficacy

GSES scores significantly differed across CIPS categories (p = 0.011, Table 5). Residents with mild IS reported the highest self‐efficacy (35.13, SD 3.18), while those with significant or intense symptoms reported substantially lower scores (30.80, SD 2.90 and 30.57, SD 4.35, respectively). Bonferroni post hoc tests revealed significant differences in GSES between those with mild and significant IS (mean difference = 4.32 (95% CI 0.75–7.89), p = 0.01), and between those with mild and intense IS (mean difference = 4.55 (95% CI 0.01–9.10), p = 0.049).

Discussion

This national study is the first to assess impostor phenomenon (IP) and general self‐efficacy (GSE) among Canadian Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery (OHNS) residents using validated bilingual instruments. While our sample size represents 33.73% of the national OHNS resident population, it is comparable to response rates in similar studies [3, 9].

Eighty percent of respondents reported moderate to intense levels of impostorism based on Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) thresholds, consistent with rates previously observed in surgical trainees across specialties, including orthopedic surgery and general surgery [4, 16]. Female residents in our cohort were significantly more likely to report higher CIPS scores compared to male counterparts, echoing results from both Canadian and international studies that highlight the gendered burden of IP in surgery [2, 9, 16]. Importantly, IP is not unique to medicine and has been widely reported across multiple professional fields including academia, law, and the judiciary [2]. The relative lack of representation of women in senior surgical roles may contribute to reduced perceptions of belonging within the specialty. When combined with persistent gender‐based performance expectations, women trainees may experience greater pressure to overperform to counteract stereotype‐driven assumptions which equate surgical competence with traditionally masculine traits. These conditions may intensify IP by undermining confidence and internal validation, even in the presence of external success. Gender disparities in career advancement in academic medicine are well documented, with women less likely to attain senior academic rank or leadership positions despite similar qualifications [9]. These differences are widely recognized to be multifactorial and include factors such as mentorship gaps, implicit bias, and institutional culture [17]. In addition, prior work suggests that women may underestimate their performance despite similar ability and may therefore be less likely to pursue competitive opportunities [18]. In this context, impostor phenomenon may represent one psychological factor that interacts with broader structural influences to shape confidence and career decisions.

The study's bilingual, nationwide design and inclusion of all 13 Canadian OHNS residency programs enhanced its generalizability and relevance to training programs across diverse institutional contexts. Importantly, the presence of impostorism across all training levels and geographic regions suggests that this is not an isolated issue within a single institution but rather a widespread challenge. This Canadian study is also generalizable to other countries like the United States, as our results confirm the results of other American studies [2, 16]. Both of these countries are in North America and have similar medical training systems. IP is a universal phenomenon that does not stop at an international border.

The study in the literature that is the closest to our present study is McCall et al.'s national study of female attending surgeons [9]. Among 387 respondents, 98.7% have experienced IP with a mean CIPS score of 65.0 (SD16.0) [9]. There are several similarities and differences to our present study. Both are Canadian, national wide surveys and both used the same validated outcome measurement tool (i.e., CIPS). Our study, however, evaluated resident trainees, while the McCall et al.'s study evaluated surgical attendings. Our study focused only on OHNS residents, while their study surveyed all surgical specialities; otolaryngologists comprised only 3.7% of their study population. Our study included all gender identities (i.e., men, women, prefer not to answer), while their study only included women. Our female OHNS resident's CIPS score (65.85, SD 15.57) was very similar to the study by McCall et al. CIPS score of female attending surgeons (65.0, SD 16.0). By including male OHHS residents, our study can objectively conclude that female OHNS residents experience more IP. These differences strengthened our study. It added a new dimension to the IP literature by evaluating a unique population that has not been explored in the past.

Level of PGY training was also identified as a significant factor, with PGY2 residents reporting higher IP than PGY‐5 residents. It may be surprising that PGY1 residents did not report the highest CIPS scores. Many Canadian OHNS programs schedule off service rotations in the PGY1 year, similar to the traditional rotating internship year. When OHNS residents finally rotate on to their home otolaryngology service for the first time in PGY2, they may feel more pressure to perform, which may lead to more IP. This finding was confirmed in the McCall et al. study of female attending surgeons where they reported more intense IP the first 5 years of training and IP lessened over time. The authors hypothesized that IP was heightened during transition times in practice, like the transition from residency to staff. Similarly, our study showed heightened IP when transitioning to OHNS residency. However, subgroup comparisons by training level should be interpreted with caution, as estimates were associated with wide confidence intervals, likely reflecting small sample sizes and substantial variability.

Our study also contradicted previous studies on certain risk factors for IP. For example, the McCall et al. study reported that having children under the age of 6, compared with having children older than 6, was associated with more intense IP [9]. They hypothesized that this factor was a proxy for returning from parental leave, which is a major transition and source of stress for professional women. Our study did not report any difference with parental status; however, very few OHNS residents were parents (7.14%). In contrast, in their study, 35% had children less than 6, and 67% were parents [9]. The low numbers in our study may have limited the analysis. Furthermore, the Bravata et al. systematic review of 62 studies on IP reported that ethnic minority groups had higher IP rates [2]. A study from the psychology literature reported a higher level of IP in young African American women [7]. Differences in IP in relation to ethnicity could not be assessed in this study because the number of participants within these subgroups was too small for meaningful analysis. For example, only 4% self‐reported as African, 11% South Asian, and 13% East Asian. Our study did not explore other variables, like religious status and sexual orientation.

General Self‐Efficacy (GSE) refers to the belief in one's ability to execute actions necessary to manage future situations, solve problems, and recover from setbacks [19]. It is a central construct in health psychology and occupational stress research, representing a stable and generalized sense of control over one's environment. High self‐efficacy has been associated with improved stress management, emotional regulation, and performance outcomes across diverse high‐stakes professions. In surgical training, specifically, GSE has been linked to resident wellness and burnout mitigation. A large cross‐sectional study of surgical residents at Stanford University found that high GSE scores were predictive of better well‐being [20]. Additionally, a multicenter study of general surgery residents demonstrated that those with higher GSE reported lower burnout and perceived stress, while another study of vascular surgery residents found that burnout and depression were more prevalent among those with lower GSE [21, 22]. Similar protective effects of GSE have been observed in nurses and firefighters, where high GSE appeared to buffer against psychological strain and emotional exhaustion [23, 24].

We chose to include GSE in our study to better understand its potential protective role in the psychological profile of OHNS residents. IP and GSE often function in tension: the former characterized by self‐doubt and fear of being exposed as inadequate, and the latter by confidence in one's capacity to succeed. The inverse relationship observed in our data (r = −0.366, p = 0.005) underscores the possibility that IP may actively undermine residents' perceived ability to meet clinical and academic demands. This dynamic reinforces the need for residency programs to identify and support trainees at risk, and to cultivate environments that foster self‐efficacy. Ultimately, enhancing self‐efficacy may serve as a key target for interventions aiming to reduce psychological distress and promote sustainable success in surgical training [7, 8, 20, 21]. Potential strategies include mentorship programs, wellness curricula, peer support groups, coaching, and initiatives that explicitly address impostorism and reinforce self‐efficacy. Mentorship, in particular, has been identified as a key protective factor in promoting confidence and mitigating IP among women in academic medicine [6]. Only 25% of the residents surveyed sought support for impostorism and only 60% would be willing to seek support; thus, there is opportunity for residency programs to encourage help‐seeking. Further, fostering a culture that normalizes vulnerability and self‐doubt may help disrupt the cycle of internalized inadequacy often reported by residents [10].

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations that should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes any inference of causality between impostorism and self‐efficacy. Second, the reliance on self‐reported measures introduces the possibility of social desirability or recall bias. Third, non‐response bias may exist if residents who experience more severe impostor feelings or burnout choose not to participate.

Finally, while the national response rate of 33.74% is comparable to similar studies, a higher participation rate would strengthen the generalizability of subgroup analyses and support more robust modeling.

Conclusion

Imposter phenomenon is a common and enduring experience among Canadian OHNS residents, with nearly four in five reporting moderate to intense levels of impostorism. Our findings suggest that these experiences may be inversely associated with general self‐efficacy and disproportionately affect certain groups, including women and earlier‐stage trainees. These results mirror trends seen in other surgical specialties and point to a broader cultural challenge within medical training.

While the presence of impostorism is not new, its persistence across training levels and programs underscores the urgency of developing more effective support systems. Residency is a critical period for identity formation, and unaddressed self‐doubt may erode confidence, compromise learning, and contribute to burnout. Embedding structured wellness initiatives, mentorship frameworks, and opportunities for open dialog into residency curricula may help mitigate these effects and reinforce resilience.

Importantly, impostorism is not simply a reflection of personal insecurity but often a response to systemic pressures and unspoken expectations in hierarchical training environments. By fostering a culture that values authenticity, encourages help‐seeking, and normalizes experiences of self‐doubt, surgical training programs can begin to shift the narrative around competence and success.
