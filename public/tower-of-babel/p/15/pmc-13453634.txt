Meckel's Diverticulum Causing Small Bowel Obstruction in Male Patients: A Case Series
Authors: Anchal Kumari, Chongtham Anil Kumar Singh, Mohim Thakur, Manish Kumar, Ajay Dhiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Meckel's diverticulum is a congenital abnormality of the gastrointestinal tract, resulting from an incomplete obliteration of the vitello-intestinal duct. Generally, Meckel's diverticulum is asymptomatic, but it can present as intestinal perforation, obstruction, hemorrhage, and diverticulitis. These complications can develop through various mechanisms such as volvulus, adhesions, intussusception, and internal hernia. Small bowel obstruction due to a meso-diverticular band of Meckel’s diverticulum is a rare complication. Herein, we present a case series of three male patients presenting with intestinal obstruction due to a band of Meckel’s diverticulum.

Introduction

Fabricius Hildanus first described the term Meckel’s diverticulum in 1598 [1,2]. Out of all acute surgical admissions, small bowel obstruction accounts for 20%, with the most common cause being postoperative adhesions, followed by hernia [2,3]. Meckel's diverticulum is the most common congenital anomaly of the gastrointestinal tract with a 2% incidence, two feet away from the ileocaecal junction, and two inches in length [3,4]. Most of the cases are incidentally diagnosed at radiological imaging or at the time of surgical exploration. It is twice as common in males as compared to females and usually contains two types of tissue: gastric and pancreatic. It is a true diverticulum as it contains all the layers of the bowel wall. It is caused by incomplete obliteration of the proximal part of the omphalomesenteric duct at the sixth to 10th week of gestation [2-4]. The rate of developing complications is 4-40% [4,5]. The most common presentation is rectal bleeding in adults [6]. Symptomatic Meckel’s diverticulum usually presents in patients with age younger than 50 years, male gender, diverticulum more than 2 cm in length, and broad-based diverticulum. Major complications include rectal bleeding, diverticulitis, bowel perforation, and obstruction. Other complications are torsion of diverticulum [7], enterolith formation, tuberculosis, foreign bodies, parasites, and tumors such as angioma, lipoma, leiomyoma, neurofibroma, carcinoid, adenocarcinoma, and sarcoma. Various mechanisms for bowel obstruction include volvulus along mesodiverticular band, intussusception, adhesions, and Littre’s hernia. In cases of ectopic mucosa in diverticulum, a radionuclide scan with technetium-99m is used to detect gastric mucosa [8]. Herein, we are reporting three cases of Meckel’s diverticulum presenting within a six-month period. All three cases presented in emergency with features of intestinal obstruction.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 47-year-old male patient presented with diffuse abdominal pain, constipation, and vomiting for three days. On examination, there was tachycardia; per abdomen, diffuse tenderness was present, and bowel sounds were absent. There were multiple air-fluid levels on abdominal X-ray suggestive of bowel obstruction (Figure 1). The patient was posted for exploratory laparotomy. Intra-operatively, there was a Meckel's diverticulum 40 cm proximal to the ileocaecal junction with a broad base, and the diverticulum adhered to the mesentery, forming a distal ileal stricture (Figure 2). Ileal loops distal to the stricture were collapsed, forming a transition point. Resection of Meckel's diverticulum and end-to-end anastomosis of the ileal segment was performed. The postoperative period was uneventful.

Case 2

A 27-year-old male patient presented with diffuse abdominal pain and vomiting for four days. There was diffuse tenderness, exaggerated bowel sounds, and there were multiple dilated bowel loops on abdominal X-ray suggestive of bowel obstruction (Figure 3). Patient was planned for surgical exploration. Intra-operatively, there was a Meckel's diverticulum 90 cm from the ileocecal junction adhered to the parietal wall, causing bowel obstruction, which caused kinking of bowel leading to bowel obstruction proximal to the diverticulum (Figure 4). Resection of the ileal segment containing Meckel's diverticulum with end-to-end anastomosis was performed. The postoperative period was uneventful and the patient was discharged on the seventh postoperative day.

Case 3

A 26-year-old male patient presented with abdominal pain and multiple episodes of vomiting. On presentation, the patient was maintaining blood pressure; there was tachycardia and guarding was present all over the abdomen. There were multiple air-fluid levels and dilated bowel loops on abdominal X-ray, and on CECT abdomen, there was a transition point in the distal ileum with dilated proximal bowel loops. The patient was planned for surgical exploration and there was a Meckel’s diverticulum 30 cm from the ileocecal junction, which formed adhesions with the ileum causing bowel obstruction (Figure 5). Resection of the ileum containing Meckel’s diverticulum with end-to-end anastomosis was performed. The postoperative period was uneventful.

Discussion

Meckel’s diverticulum is named after Johann Friedrich Meckel, who described its embryonic origin in 1809 [1,2]. It is a common congenital anomaly with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1, and it is a true diverticulum [2,3]. The average length of Meckel’s diverticulum is 3 cm but it can range from 1 to 10 cm. It is usually found within 100 cm from the ileocaecal junction on the antimesenteric border of the ileum [1,2]. Mostly, Meckel’s diverticulum is asymptomatic and the risk of complication is 4-40% [2]. Intestinal obstruction is the second most common complication [2,3]. Torsion of the diverticulum is an uncommon complication, accounting for 0.5% of symptomatic cases [4]. Other complications include diverticulitis, hemorrhage, and perforation [5,6]. It is difficult to diagnose Meckel’s diverticulum, as symptoms and signs are nonspecific. It usually presents with symptoms such as rectal bleeding and intestinal obstruction [1,2 ]. X-rays of the abdomen were suggestive of dilated bowel loops and an air-fluid level which has a limited role in the diagnosis of Meckel’s diverticulum [4,5]. Ultrasonography and computed tomography are also nonspecific but sometimes they can demonstrate a fluid-filled, blind ending structure in the right lower quadrant, with findings that are similar to those of appendicitis [3,4]. For symptomatic Meckel’s diverticulum, treatment is resection and anastomosis of the diverticulum-containing small bowel [1,7,8].

Conclusions

Meckel’s diverticulum is the most common congenital anomaly of the gastrointestinal tract, but it is difficult to diagnose preoperatively as abdominal symptoms and signs are nonspecific. Radiological imaging such as abdominal X-ray and contrast-enhanced computed tomography abdomen are usually nonspecific, showing only signs of bowel obstruction. Early surgical intervention is required to prevent the gangrene of the bowel. A diagnosis of Meckel’s diverticulum should be kept in mind for a young patient presenting with symptoms of a bowel obstruction.
