Trends and projections of cervical and subcervical spinal cord injury burden in China: An observational study analysis for the GBD 2021
Authors: Tao Wang, Youjiang Min, Shunpu Wang, Le Li, Min Wang, Quanhe Qiu, Weiping Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI) and subcervical spinal cord injury (SSCI) are severe central nervous system disorders with high disability and mortality rates, imposing a substantial burden on patients and society. To assess the incidence and prevalence and trends of spinal cord injury (SCI) in China, we analyzed data from the global burden of disease 2021 study. Variations in the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), prevalence rate (ASPR), and years lived with disability rate (ASYR) for CSCI and SSCI were evaluated using Joinpoint regression and age–period–cohort models, and long-term trends were projected with ARIMA models. From 1990 to 2021, CSCI’s ASIR increased from 3.12 to 3.23 per 1,00,000 and ASPR from 68.95 to 72.16 per 1,00,000, while ASYR decreased from 29.84 to 27.65 per 1,00,000, with estimated annual percentage changes of –0.14, –0.17, and –0.63, respectively. For SSCI, ASIR increased slightly from 2.95 to 2.98 per 1,00,000, ASPR declined from 80.89 to 79.53 per 1,00,000, and ASYR decreased markedly from 22.25 to 13.96 per 1,00,000, with estimated annual percentage changes of –0.32, –0.48, and –2.14, respectively. Both prevalence and incidence were consistently higher in men. Joinpoint analysis revealed declining ASIR trends from 1990 to 2011, followed by increases between 2011 and 2021, with ASPR showing fluctuations and ASYR declining overall, while SSCI rebounded after 2010. Age–period–cohort analysis suggested that period and cohort effects decreased with age, whereas incidence rates increased. ARIMA projections indicated that by 2050, the ASIR and ASPR of CSCI will decline to 2.97 and 66.62 per 1,00,000, respectively, and those of SSCI to 2.93 and 76.66 per 1,00,000. These findings indicate a gradual downward trend in the burden of CSCI and SSCI in China; however, population size and aging continue to drive increases in absolute incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability. The burden of CSCI remains higher than that of SSCI, particularly among men and older adults, underscoring the need for strengthened prevention and rehabilitation strategies in high-risk groups.

1. Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is one of the most severe complications of spinal trauma, primarily caused by falls from heights and traffic accidents. It may lead to multisystem problems and the irreversible loss of motor and sensory function.[1,2] In addition to severely lowering the patients’ quality of life, SCI causes long-term health loss and high medical cost.[3] SCI is typically classified into cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI) and subcervical spinal cord injury (SSCI) based on the injury location, with distinct differences in the severity of functional impairment, complications, and rehabilitation outcomes. CSCI often results in complete or incomplete quadriplegia with marked muscle atrophy, severely impairing fine motor coordination. Additionally, problems with the diaphragm and upper parts of the body (thorax) often lead to breathing and heartbeat problems.[4–6] In contrast, the main symptoms of SSCI are lower limb paralysis and bladder/bowel dysfunction, with relatively milder effects on cardiopulmonary function.[7,8] Surgical decompression, medication, and rehabilitation therapy are the mainstays of current SCI treatment approaches.[9] Surgical treatment effectively alleviates spinal cord compression; however, the timing and extent of surgery critically influence the prognosis, and risks persist.[10] Pharmacological interventions can partially suppress secondary injury responses; however, their efficacy remains limited, and specific treatments that promote neural regeneration are lacking.[11] Rehabilitation therapy aids in limb function recovery but struggles to significantly improve systemic complications.[12] Despite recent advances in SCI treatment, overall patient outcomes remain suboptimal, with most patients requiring prolonged or repeated hospitalizations, underscoring the need for further epidemiological and disease burden research. Notably, countries and regions differ greatly in the economic burden and epidemiological features of SCI. Currently, most studies focus on the United States and Europe, while independent research targeting China and other developing countries is relatively limited. Prior research has shown that the yearly cost of SCI in the US was over $4 billion in 1990, and it was increasing annually.[13] In contrast, the average total hospitalization cost for patients with SCI in China is $11,500.[14] This evidence demonstrates that SCI poses significant challenges to public health and socioeconomic development in China.

Through the methodical collection and modeling of epidemiological data across age, sex, time, and geographic dimensions, the global burden of disease (GBD) database offers a solid basis for estimating the disease burden.[15] As the latest global epidemiological dataset, GBD 2021 has been optimized in terms of data coverage and methodology, enabling a comprehensive assessment of disease incidence, prevalence, years lived with disability (YLDs), and their temporal trends.[16] Previous studies have revealed the overall burden of SCI in China but did not distinguish between CSCI and SSCI or provide forecasts of future trends. Given that CSCI often leads to more severe neurological damage and higher rehabilitation needs, differentiating between its subtypes is of significant clinical and public health importance.[17] Using data from GBD 2021, we conducted a thorough analysis of the epidemiology and illness burden of CSCI and SSCI in China from 1990 to 2021. It also forecasts SCI incidence and prevalence over the next 30 years, aiming to deepen understanding of the disease burden in China and inform other countries.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources

Data on Chinese CSCI and SSCI were sourced from GBD 2021. The GBD 2021 comprehensively assessed the burden of 371 diseases and injuries and 88 major risk factors across 204 countries and regions. A unified framework was proposed to systematically evaluate global disease burden, covering core metrics such as incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALYs.[18] Relevant data are accessible through the GHDX, with data sources, methodology, and statistical modeling systematically described in previous studies.[19] In the GBD study, SCI includes both CSCI and SSCI, classified under “injury nature” according to ICD-10. Corresponding codes are S14.0–S14.159, T91.3, S24.0–S24.159, and S34.0–S34.139.[20] All data were publicly available, no additional ethical approval was required.

2.2. Observational indicators

This study assessed the disease burden of CSCI and SSCI using cases, prevalence, incidence, YLDs, and their age-standardized rates. YLDs quantify the health burden of SCI by estimating the total number of years of disability resulting from SCI, incorporating the prevalence and disability weights.[17] The 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) were used to evaluate the heterogeneity of these ratios.[21]

2.3. Statistical analysis

Joinpoint software (v4.9.1.0) was used to analyze temporal trends in the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and ASYR for SCI, calculating the annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change while estimating the 95% UI to assess disease burden dynamics.[22,23] Trends in CSCI and SSCI incidence and prevalence by age, time period, and cohort were examined using a Poisson-based APC model. By quantifying age, period, and cohort effects, this method makes it easier to identify differences in disease burden between age groups and cohorts.[24] Additionally, this study further applied ARIMA models for time-series prediction of ASIR and ASPR.[25] Models were fitted to historical data, with stability assessed through residual analysis, Ljung–Box tests, and autocorrelation/partial autocorrelation functions (ACF/PACF) to project disease burden trends over the next 30 years. All statistical analyses except Joinpoint analysis were conducted using R software (v4.4.2).

3. Results

3.1. Incidence, prevalence, and YLDs of CSCI and SSCI in China

As shown in Table 1, between 1990 and 2021, the number of CSCI cases increased from 35,399 (95% UI: 25,982–48,628) to 52,454 (95% UI: 34,148–81,575), with the ASIR marginally increasing from 3.12/1,00,000 (95% UI: 2.28–4.37) to 3.23/1,00,000 (95% UI: 2.16–4.94). The EAPC was −0.14 (95% UI: −0.40 to 0.13), indicating a stable overall incidence. Males had a much greater incidence rate than females (4.31 vs 2.11 per 1,00,000). During the same period, the number of SSCI cases increased from 33,953 (95% UI: 24,481–47,595) to 46,910 (95% UI: 29,417–73,988). The ASIR increased slightly from 2.95 per 1,00,000 (95% UI: 2.09–4.35) to 2.98 per 1,00,000 (95% UI: 1.92–4.55), remaining largely unchanged with an EAPC of −0.32 (95% UI: −0.64 to 0.01). The incidence in males was consistently higher than that in females (3.59 vs 2.31 per 1,00,000 individuals). Overall, the incidence of CSCI was slightly higher than that of SSCI. Regarding prevalence, the number of CSCI cases increased from 7,85,139 (95% UI: 7,30,492–8,49,161) to 13,15,929 (95% UI: 12,16,024–14,27,664), ASPR rose from 68.95/1,00,000 (95% UI: 64.37–74.34) to 72.16/1,00,000 (95% UI: 66.82–78.28), while EAPC was −0.17 (95% CI: −0.41 to 0.06), indicating a generally stable overall trend. Male incidence rates were significantly higher than female rates (93.24 vs 50.10/1,00,000), with a slight downward trend observed among women. In contrast, the number of SSCI cases increased from 9,09,125 to 14,50,348, but the ASPR slightly decreased from 80.89/1,00,000 (95% UI: 75.14–87.77) to 79.53/1,00,000 (95% UI: 73.18–87.26). with an EAPC of −0.48 (95% UI: −0.77 to −0.19). The prevalence in males remained higher than that in females (95.01 vs 63.18 per 1,00,000), with a more pronounced decline among women than among men. Regarding YLDs, CSCI YLDs increased from 3,42,208 to 4,99,615 cases, while ASYR decreased from 29.84/1,00,000 (95% UI: 21.47–37.58) to 27.65/1,00,000 (95% UI: 19.59–35.92). with an EAPC of −0.63 (95% UI: −0.88 to −0.37). The decline was greater among females (EAPC = −0.87) than that among males (EAPC = −0.47). The absolute number of YLDs for SSCI remained largely unchanged (2,52,152 vs 2,52,222), but the ASYR decreased significantly from 22.25 per 1,00,000 (95% UI: 15.51–28.99) to 13.96 per 1,00,000 (95% UI: 9.71–19.24), with an EAPC of −2.14 (95% UI: −2.50 to −1.79). Similarly, the decline was greater among females (EAPC = −2.39) than among males (EAPC = −1.96).

3.2. Gender differences in CSCI and SSCI burden across age groups

Figure 1 depicts sex- and age-specific trends in CSCI incidence, prevalence, and YLDs for 1990 and 2021. In 1990, incidence peaked in males aged 20 to 24 and in females under 5, with males showing higher rates. By 2021, the peaks had shifted to 50 to 54 and 65 to 69 years, respectively, with the gender gap narrowing, although the overall incidence remained higher in men (Fig. 1A, D). Regarding prevalence, both sexes peaked at 35 to 39 years in 1990 and were delayed to 50 to 54 years by 2021 (Fig. 1B, E). Regarding YLDs, males consistently exceeded females; both sexes peaked at 35 to 39 years in 1990, shifting to 50 to 54 years for males and 55 to 59 years for females by 2021 (Fig. 1C, F). Figure 2 illustrates the changes in SSCI incidence, prevalence, and YLDs by sex and age groups from 1990 to 2021. In 1990, the highest incidence was under 5 years for girls and between 20 and 24 years for males. Males generally exhibited higher incidence rates before the age of 65 years, whereas females showed higher rates at age 75 years and above. By 2021, male incidence exhibited a double peak (ages 30–34 and 50–54), whereas female incidence peaked at ages 55 to 59 and 65 to 69, with females surpassing males after 80 years of age (Fig. 2A, D). Regarding prevalence, both sexes peaked at ages 35 to 39 in 1990, followed by a decline; by 2021, the peaks shifted to ages 50 to 54 for men and 55 to 59 for women (Fig. 2B, E). YLDs consistently exceeded those of women; while both genders peaked at ages 35 to 39 in 1990, peaks were delayed to ages 50 to 54 for men and 55 to 59 for women by 2021, with women surpassing men after age 80 (Fig. 2C, F).

3.3. Trends in incidence, prevalence, and YLDs rates for CSCI and SSCI

Figure 3 shows sex-specific ASIR, ASPR, and ASYR for CSCI and SSCI across all-age groups, 1990 to 2021. Overall, the number of new cases, number of people living with the conditions, and number of YLDs for CSCI and SSCI all showed an upward trend, and the number of men is generally higher than that of women. The ASIR reached a brief peak in 2008 but generally showed a slow decline or remained largely stable (Fig. 3A, D). The ASPR exhibited fluctuating U-shaped patterns (Fig. 3B, E). The ASYR showed a pronounced downward trend, with a steeper decline among females than among males (Fig. 3C, F). These results imply that while the total burden of SSCI and CSCI has increased over time, the health impact has decreased.

3.4. Joinpoint regression analysis of CSCI and SSCI

Figure 4 displays the Joinpoint regression trends for the ASIR, ASPR, and ASYR of CSCI and SSCI. For ASIR, CSCI showed a decline from 1990 to 2011 (APC = −0.80, P < .001), followed by an increase from 2011 to 2021 (APC = 1.86, P = .008; Fig. 4A and Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589). The SSCI showed a similar pattern, declining from 1990 to 2011 (APC = −1.16, P < .001) and increasing from 2011 to 2021 (APC = 2.20, P = .006; Fig. 4D and Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589). For ASPR, CSCI trended upward from 1990 to 2001 (APC = 0.29, P = .001), declined from 2001 to 2004 (APC = −4.74, P < .001), showed a nonsignificant decline from 2004 to 2007 (P > .05), and rose from 2007 to 2018 (APC = 1.21, P < .001), with acceleration from 2018 to 2021 (APC = 1.21, P < .001; Fig. 4B and Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589). SSCI’s ASPR remained stable between 1990 and 2001 (APC = −0.02, P > .05), declined from 2001 to 2005 (APC = −5.25, P < .001), showed a nonsignificant increase from 2005 to 2010 (APC = 0.06, P > .05), increased from 2010 to 2018 (APC = 1.51, P < .001), and accelerated from 2018 to 2021 (APC = 3.13, P < .001; Fig. 4E and Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589). For ASYR, CSCI showed varying changes: stable from 1990 to 1995 (APC = −0.31, P = .140), nonsignificant increase from 1995 to 2000 (APC = 0.46, P = .127); decline from 2000 to 2005 (APC = −4.23, P < .001), stability from 2005 to 2009 (APC = −0.56, P = .241); increase from 2009 to 2018 (APC = 1.02, P < .001), and acceleration from 2018 to 2021 (APC = 2.48, P < .001; Fig. 4C and Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589). SSCI showed a sustained decline then upturn, declining from 1990 to 1998 (APC = −1.00, P < .001), 1998 to 2001 (APC = −1.92, P = .053), 2001 to 2005 (APC = −7.25, P < .001), and 2005 to 2010 (APC = −2.59, P < .001); increasing from 2010 to 2018 (APC = 0.50, P = .002), and accelerating during 2018 to 2021 (APC = 2.66, P < .001; Fig. 4F and Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589).

3.6. ARIMA forecasting

To predict the ASIR and ASPR trends for CSCI and SSCI during the ensuing 3 decades, the ARIMA model was utilized. Overall, the results imply that both the ASIR and ASPR for CSCI and SSCI will exhibit a downward trend over the next 3 decades (Figs. 7 and 8). Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589 presents the parameters of the best-fitting models, along with their corresponding AIC, BIC, and Ljung–Box test P-values. The Ljung–Box test findings show that all models’ residuals exhibit white noise characteristics, suggesting a good degree of fit to the real data and optimal model stability. The Q–Q, ACF, and PACF plots further verified the normality of the residuals (see Figs. S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589). Specifically, for CSCI, projections indicated declining trends in overall and male ASIR over the next 30 years (Fig. 7A, B), whereas the female ASIR was projected to remain relatively stable (Fig. 7C). Overall and male ASPR also showed declining trends (Fig. 7D, E), whereas the female ASPR remained stable (Fig. 7F). For SSCI, projections indicate that the overall ASIR will slightly decrease or stabilize over the next 30 years (Fig. 8A), with male ASIR showing a downward trend (Fig. 8B) and female ASIR remaining stable (Fig. 8C). Both overall and male ASPR exhibited downward trends (Fig. 8D, E), whereas the female ASPR is projected to increase (Fig. 8F). ARIMA indicates that by 2050, the CSCI ASIR and ASPR will reach 2.97/1,00,000 and 66.62/1,00,000, respectively, and the SSCI ASIR and ASPR will reach 2.93/1,00,000 and 76.66/1,00,000, respectively, in the high-risk. Detailed annual projections are presented in Tables S5 and S6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q589.

4. Discussion

This study analyzed Chinese CSCI and SSCI burdens based on GBD 2021 data and projected trends over the next 30 years. The findings showed that in 2021, China’s CSCI incidence, prevalence, and YLDs totaled 52,454, 13,15,929, and 4,99,615, respectively, while SSCI were 46,910, 14,50,348, and 2,52,222, respectively. Both CSCI and SSCI exhibited notable increases in incidence, prevalence, and YLDs compared with 1990. Although the ASIR and ASPR remained largely stable, the ASYR exhibited a marked downward trend. CSCI consistently imposed a higher disease burden than SSCI, with greater burdens observed among males and middle-aged and older populations. EAPC analysis and ARIMA forecasting further indicated downward trends in both the ASIR and ASPR for CSCI and SSCI during 1990 to 2021 and 2022 to 2050. Nevertheless, with China’s growing and aging population, both prevalence and incidence are projected to increase, and SCI will continue to pose a significant public health challenge.

China is a populous emerging nation that is rapidly expanding its economy and society. Rising economic levels and demographic shifts have led to increased vehicle ownership and traffic, resulting in frequent road accidents. Concurrently, the rise in extreme sports and high-risk recreational activities has contributed to a sustained increase in SCI incidence in China.[26,27] In 2008, China experienced multiple extreme natural disasters, notably the Wenchuan earthquake, alongside concurrent blizzards and typhoons, resulting in a significant surge in SCI cases and an abnormal peak in the ASIR. Overall, the SCI prevalence rate exhibited pronounced fluctuations, following a U-shaped trend. Following China’s accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001, rapid economic development and improved healthcare conditions contributed to a certain degree of reduction in incidence rates. The SARS epidemic in 2003 led to a significant decrease in public activities, further driving down the incidence rates. However, since 2008, the ASPR has shown a gradual upward trend owing to accelerated population aging, numerous traffic accidents, and ongoing expansion in the building and industrial sectors. Previous studies have indicated that the ASIR and ASPR for SCI globally showed no significant changes between 1990 and 2019, whereas the ASYR continued to decline. This aligns with our findings, indicating that improvements in medical care and rehabilitation contributed to a reduced disease burden.[20,28] Notably, our study revealed rising ASIR for CSCI and SSCI in China after 2010, which differs from patterns seen in countries such as the United States. In these regions, the incidence of traumatic SCI has steadily declined since 2000, primarily due to more comprehensive preventive measures, advanced medical capabilities, and improved rehabilitation protocols.[6,29] In contrast, China’s rapid urbanization, surge in motor vehicle numbers, and expansion of high-risk occupations, such as construction, have further increased traffic accidents and falls from heights, leading to a gradual increase in ASIR.[14] This divergence suggests that China must draw on developed countries’ experiences while adapting to its unique socioeconomic context to formulate and implement targeted prevention strategies and optimize rehabilitation services, thereby effectively reducing the long-term disease burden of SCI.

Our study indicates that ASYR for CSCI and SSCI is declining, while ASIR and ASPR remain stable. The ASIR and ASYR were higher for CSCI than SSCI; however, the ASPR for CSCI was lower than that for SSCI. The long-term stability of SCI ASIR and ASPR in China may be related to the lack of significant fluctuations in the incidence rates of sports injuries and traffic accidents in recent years.[30] Previous studies have indicated that CSCI accounts for over 50% of traumatic SCI cases, significantly higher than SSCI,[31,32] and CSCI has a relatively higher incidence. This is primarily attributed to the high prevalence of acute traumatic events, such as falls and traffic accidents.[30] Among children, approximately 80% of SCIs occur at the cervical spinal cord level, potentially due to the larger relative size of the head compared to the body and the weaker strength of the paravertebral muscles and ligaments.[33] Age and sex significantly affect the disease burden of SCI. We found that the incidence rates of both CSCI and SSCI increased with age, with the prevalence showing a similar trend, peaking at approximately 70 years of age. With aging, older adults experience diminished vascular function, weakened immunity, increased chronic diseases, and reduced balance and reaction times, elevating the risk of falls and osteoporosis-related injuries, thereby increasing the incidence of SCI.[34] Additionally, the disease burden is significantly higher in men than in women, partly due to the higher proportion of men in high-risk occupations and their greater exposure to traffic accident risks as the primary driving population.[32–35] Concurrently, physiological structural differences and hormone-related neural repair capacities may contribute to sex disparities.[36,37]

According to Joinpoint regression analysis, since 2011, both the ASIR and ASPR for CSCI and SSCI have been increasing, suggesting a minor increase in incidence and prevalence rates. Overall, the ASYR for both CSCI and SSCI decreased significantly since 1990. The CSCI showed a marked increase after 2009, whereas the SSCI shifted to an upward trend starting in 2010 after a prolonged decline. The notable drop in the ASYR suggests a declining total illness burden for both CSCI and SSCI, despite comparatively steady incidence and prevalence rates. The average annual percentage change values showed notable sex-based disparities; females exhibited smaller increases or even negative values, whereas males showed positive values. This implies that more potent preventative and therapeutic strategies are required for women, indicating the need for enhanced attention to both males and females. After adjusting for biases, the APC analysis revealed declining incidence rates for CSCI and SSCI among individuals aged 0 to 10 years, followed by a gradual increase after age 10. This trend may be correlated with adolescents and adults engaging in high-risk activities and experiencing traffic accidents.[38] After 2011, period effects led to increases in frequency and prevalence, peaking among the elderly. This may be associated with the heightened risk of fall-related and traffic accident injuries and accelerated population aging.[38] Cohort effects indicate an overall decline in the incidence and prevalence risks of CSCI and SSCI starting from earlier birth cohorts, suggesting that improvements in medical care, enhanced preventive measures, and optimized rehabilitation systems have significantly reduced the burden of SCI.

ARIMA model projections indicate that ASIR and ASPR for CSCI and SSCI will decline over the next 30 years. The ongoing development of road safety management regulations, enhancements to trauma emergency systems, and heightened public knowledge of preventive measures are probably all directly responsible for this drop. However, due to population growth and aging, the total number of SCI cases and affected individuals is expected to rise, keeping the disease burden high. Further analysis revealed significant sex disparities in both CSCI and SSCI. For CSCI, ASIR, and ASPR among males, a decline is projected, whereas the rates among females are expected to remain relatively stable. This may be related to the aging female population and the higher prevalence of degenerative spinal disorders, particularly postmenopause, where women face increased susceptibility to non-fracture injuries, resulting in a slower decline in disease burden compared with men.[39] For SSCI, both overall and male ASIR and ASPR showed declining trends, whereas the female ASPR showed an increasing trend. This may be explained by the higher cumulative burden of chronic illnesses and longer life expectancy in women.[40] Additionally, because SSCI primarily manifests as lower limb paraplegia, it has a higher long-term survival rate than the more fatal CSCI. Consequently, female patients are more likely to accumulate long-term functional impairments and rehabilitation needs, leading to an upward trend in ASPR rather than a decline in these rates.

Despite the novel insights our study provides into the disease burden of CSCI and SSCI in China from 1990 to 2021, it has some limitations. First, the absence of provincial-level data in China prevented further analysis of regional epidemiological characteristics of SCI. Second, the GBD 2021 database did not provide mortality information for SCI, impeding a more thorough evaluation of the disease burden in China. Future studies should conduct larger-scale and more representative epidemiological research to further analyze the disease burden of SCI and its risk factors across Chinese provinces while incorporating key epidemiological indicators, such as mortality rates. This will provide more precise data to support SCI prevention, control, and decision making in public health.

5. Conclusion

China’s total CSCI and SSCI illness burdens have declined. However, owing to the large population base and accelerated aging, the incidence, prevalence, and YLDs will continue to increase, making the disease burden significant and unignorable. The burden of CSCI is particularly heavy and may lead to higher disability rates. The burden is generally higher among men and older adults than among women and middle-aged individuals. Although the incidence and prevalence of SCI in China is anticipated to decline over the next 30 years, further strengthening of preventive measures is necessary to ensure this decline. Implementing targeted interventions that consider sex and age differences is essential for effectively mitigating the long-term societal and public health impacts of SCI.
