Environmental and Inflammatory Factors Drive Perinuclear‐Antineutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibodies (pANCA) in Ulcerative Colitis: A European Twin Study
Authors: Daniel Bergemalm, Karin Amcoff, Marie J. Pierik, Jean‐Frederic Colombel, Severine Vermeire, Lennart Bodin, Marie Carlson, Jonas Halfvarson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPerinuclear‐antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (pANCA) have been identified in familial ulcerative colitis (UC), but the mechanism underlying their expression remains elusive. We assessed the role of genetic predisposition, environmental factors and systemic subclinical inflammation in the development of pANCA in a twin cohort with UC.MethodsA total of 48 twin pairs (Leuven, Belgium n = 4, Maastricht, The Netherlands n = 6 and Örebro, Sweden n = 38) with UC were included. Among these, 18 were monozygotic (3 concordant and 15 discordant for UC) and 30 were dizygotic (1 concordant and 29 discordant for UC). P‐ANCA was detected through standardised ELISA, an indirect immunofluorescence assay and DNase treatment. In addition to high sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP), 92 inflammatory protein markers were measured in serum by proximity extension assay.ResultPerinuclear‐ANCA was present in 15/52 (29%) of UC twins vs. 4/44 (9%) healthy twin siblings (p = 0.02). No agreement in the presence of pANCA or their levels was observed between twin siblings in monozygotic pairs discordant for UC [intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) = 0.09] or dizygotic pairs (ICC = −0.20). Female sex was associated with an increased likelihood of pANCA (odds ratio, OR 5.25; 95% confidence interval, CI 1.36–20.30) and higher ANCA levels (ratio of geometric means 1.86; 95% CI 1.18–2.93). Active smoking was associated with lower concentrations of ANCA (ratio of geometric means 0.31; 95% CI 0.14–0.68) and potentially reduced the likelihood of pANCA (OR 0.20; 95% CI 0.03–1.34) in twins with UC but not in their healthy siblings. In healthy twin siblings, significant correlations between ANCA levels and hs‐CRP, CDCP1, IL17 A, CXCL9 and IL5 (correlation coefficients 0.36–0.41, p‐values < 0.05) were observed.ConclusionFemale sex and tobacco smoking outweighed genetics regarding the generation and levels of pANCA and ANCA antibodies. The correlations between ANCA levels and inflammatory markers in healthy twin siblings suggest that pANCA may result from subclinical inflammation.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic disease characterised by an inflammation that starts in the rectum and extends to the colon [1]. In accordance with the preclinical period in Crohn's disease (CD), increasing lines of evidence indicate that the onset of symptoms in UC is preceded by a preclinical period [2, 3]. It has been hypothesised that this period is initiated by interactions between genetic and environmental risk factors, eventually resulting in barrier dysfunction and subclinical inflammation [4]. No diagnostic marker defines the initiation of this preclinical period. Still, perinuclear‐staining anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (pANCAs) are found in 24%–85% of patients with clinically established UC [5]. An increased prevalence of pANCAs and increased levels of ANCA have also been observed among healthy first‐degree relatives of patients with the disease. Aggregation of neutrophil autoantibodies in families with UC was first reported by Shanahan et al. [6], who observed ANCA in 68% of patients with UC and 16% of their clinically unaffected family members but only in 3% of healthy controls. Their findings were confirmed by Seibold et al., who detected pANCAs in 70% of patients with UC and 30% of their relatives but in none of the healthy controls [7]. The presence of pANCAs in healthy relatives of patients with UC may reflect activation and propagation of early key pathogenic mechanisms triggered by genetic and environmental risk factors [6, 7]. Alternatively, these markers are triggered by a subclinical inflammation or identify healthy relatives who will proceed and develop symptoms and a UC diagnosis but who still are in their preclinical phase of the disease [8, 9]. However, studies on pANCAs in families with UC have not been designed to differentiate between the influence of genetic and environmental risk factors. Twin methodology allows comparisons between monozygotic and dizygotic twin pairs, enabling the assessment of the respective influence of genetic predisposition and environmental factors. Therefore, we performed a European twin study to assess the impact of genetic predisposition and environmental exposure on the occurrence of pANCA and ANCA levels in UC. Additionally, we correlated ANCA levels in the healthy twin siblings with inflammatory protein markers to examine the influence of subclinical inflammation in their development.

Material and Methods

Twin Population

Mono‐ and dizygotic twin pairs, in which at least one sibling had been diagnosed with UC, were invited to participate at the University Hospital in Leuven, Belgium, Maastricht, the Netherlands and the University Hospital, Örebro, Sweden. After obtaining written consent, information on basic demographics and smoking habits was collected. Smoking was defined as daily use for ≥ 6 months, and smoking status was categorised as never‐smoker, ex‐smoker or active smoker. Patient medical records were scrutinised to verify a diagnosis of UC and determine disease phenotype, according to the Montreal Classification [10]. Twin pairs with a mixed inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) diagnosis, that is, one twin being diagnosed with UC and the twin sibling with Crohn's disease, were excluded from the study. The Swedish twins in this study were a subset of twins with IBD from a previously described cohort [11]. Zygosity of Swedish twins was determined by DNA analyses or a questionnaire on intra‐pair similarities in childhood and opposite sex status [12, 13]. An earlier validation has shown ≥ 98% agreement when comparing questionnaire data to genotyping [13]. For the twins from Leuven and Maastricht, genotyping for zygosity determination was performed using the AmpFLSTR Profiler Plus ID PCR Amplification Kit (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

The local ethics committees approved the study.

Laboratory Assay, pANCA

Serum samples were obtained, stored at −70°C and sent to the Prometheus Laboratories (CA, USA), where they were analysed for pANCA in a blinded fashion. pANCA were detected by (I) a standardised ELISA analysis, in which the results of individual sera were expressed in EU/mL, (II) an indirect immunofluorescence assay and (III) DNase treatment, as previously described [14]. To be classified as positive, samples had to reveal a perinuclear binding of ANCA in the indirect immunofluorescence, which subsided after DNase treatment and had an ANCA level > 12.0 EU/mL.

Markers of Inflammation

Previously generated data on the relative concentrations of 92 proteins (Proseek Multiplex Inflammation I Probe kit 96 × 96, Olink Bioscience, Uppsala, Sweden) were retrieved for the Swedish twins [2]. Normalised log2 values corresponding to relative protein quantities were used. Proteins were excluded if signals were below the limit of detection (LOD) in > 80% of the samples. Values below the LOD were substituted in the remaining proteins with a fixed value set to LOD/√2. Eighteen proteins had quantified levels below the limit of detection in > 75% of samples and were excluded from further analyses. Levels of hsCRP were measured using a particle‐enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay (Cardiac C‐Reactive Protein [Latex] High Sensitive, Roche Diagnostics) at Uppsala BioLab, Uppsala Clinical Research Centre, Uppsala, Sweden.

Statistical Analyses

Logistic regression models were employed to analyse the relative risk of positive pANCA status with allowance for dependence within the twin pair. The analyses were performed using UC status as the main explanatory factor and smoking and sex as additional covariates. Interaction effects between UC status, smoking and sex were also introduced and tested in these models. Thus, the outcome parameter is an odds ratio (OR), along with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and the p‐value for testing OR = 1.0. For smoking, the OR is depicted in three states, with never smoking serving as the reference category for both former and current smoking statuses. p‐values are shown for each OR and smoking as a composite variable.

The concentration of ANCA in UC twins and healthy twin siblings was analysed using a mixed regression model with allowance for dependence within the pairs. The analysis was executed in a way equivalent to the analysis of pANCA, that is, with UC status as the main factor and smoking and sex as additional covariates. To comply with the statistical assumptions of the regression model, the analysis had to consider the skewed distributions of ANCA. Therefore, we performed the calculations based on the elogarithm of the concentrations and then transformed the results back to the original scale, as previously elucidated [15]. Hence, the effect size is a ratio based on geometric means.

The kappa statistic was analysed to address the question of agreement of pANCA status within the twin pairs. In the absence of agreement, kappa is close to zero, and kappa close to +1.0 indicates a very high agreement. A negative Kappa value indicates disagreement. As described previously, agreement of ANCA levels within the twin pairs was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on the elogarithm of the concentrations according to Dunn [15, 16]. The ICC ranges from −1.0 to 1.0, with a low value (< 0.4) reflecting poor agreement. In contrast, a high value (> 0.8) indicates very good to excellent agreement [16]. ICC and kappa have similar interpretations regarding agreement within mono‐ and dizygotic twin pairs.

The correlation of inflammatory proteins with ANCA levels was explored using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. The statistical software packages used were STATA version 15.1, SPSS version 26, SAS version 9.4 and Graph Pad Prism 7.

Results

Twins

Altogether, 48 twin pairs participated in the study (Leuven n = 4, Maastricht n = 6 and Örebro n = 38). Eighteen twin pairs were monozygotic (3 concordant and 15 discordant for UC), and 30 pairs were dizygotic (1 concordant and 29 discordant for UC). Thus, 52 twins had UC and 44 were unaffected twin siblings. The median disease duration since diagnosis of UC was 20.5 (range 5–55) years. pANCA Status and Concentration of ANCA in the Different Twin Groups

Perinuclear‐staining ANCA was present in 15/52 (29%) twins with UC compared to 4/44 (9%) healthy twin siblings, corresponding to an OR of 4.05; 95% CI 1.23 – 13.33; p = 0.02. Consistently, the median concentration of ANCA was 9.7 (1.5–138.5) EU/mL in twins with UC and 5.1 (0–30) EU/mL in their healthy siblings, with a significant difference in the ratio of geometric means (ratio 2.36; 95% CI 1.49 – 3.73; p = 0.0004). There was no difference in the prevalence of pANCA among the healthy siblings when monozygotic pairs [1/15 (7%)] were compared to dizygotic pairs [3/29 (10%)], OR 0.62; 95% CI 0.06 – 6.52; p = 0.69.

pANCA Status and ANCA Concentrations According to the Phenotype of Ulcerative Colitis

There was no difference in pANCA status or ANCA concentrations according to the extent of inflammation (Table 1). Altogether, pANCA was present in 7/20 (35%) twins with ulcerative proctitis (E1) or left‐sided colitis (E2) and in 8/28 (29%) twins with extensive colitis (E3) (p = 0.54, Fisher's exact test). The corresponding median ANCA concentration levels in these two groups were 10 EU/mL and 9 EU/mL (p = 0.49, Mann‐Whitney test). No difference was observed when pANCA status and ANCA levels were analysed according to age at diagnosis when age was defined as ≤ 16, ≥ 17−≤ 39 or ≥ 40 (see Table 1) (p = 0.90, chi‐square test) and (p = 0.81, Kruskal‐Wallis test).

The occurrence of pANCA did not differ by the history of past colectomy. Perinuclear‐staining ANCA was present in 5/12 (42%) UC twins who had undergone colectomy and in 10/36 (28%) UC twins with no history of colectomy (p = 0.50, Fischer's exact test). No difference was noted in the median concentration of ANCA between UC twins with past colectomy [9 (range; 2–139) EU/mL] and UC twins without any history of colectomy [10 (range; 2–93) EU/mL]. The ratio of geometric means was 1.12 (95% CI 0.55 – 2.30; p = 0.75).

Agreement on pANCA Status and ANCA Levels Within Twin Pairs

To assess the influence of genetic predisposition and shared environmental risk factors on pANCA status and ANCA levels, twin pairs were stratified by their zygosity and intra‐pair agreement was examined. No agreement in pANCA status was observed among discordant monozygotic pairs (ĸ = −0.12) or discordant dizygotic pairs (ĸ = −0.18). Levels of ANCA within each twin pair, stratified by zygosity, are depicted in Figure 1. In accordance with our results on pANCA status, no agreement in levels of ANCA was observed in discordant monozygotic pairs (ICC = 0.09) or discordant dizygotic pairs (ICC = −0. 20). In monozygotic pairs concordant for UC, there was an agreement in levels of ANCA (ICC = 0.72), but only three twin pairs were included in the analysis. The analysis of ICC for dizygotic concordant twin pairs was not possible due to the inclusion of only one twin pair (Figure 1).

To analyse the impact of smoking status and sex on the likelihood of positive pANCA status, we constructed an extended logistic regression model. This model accounted for the interdependence within twin pairs and considered UC status as the primary explanatory variable, while incorporating smoking and sex as additional covariates (see Table 3A). The relative risk of positive pANCA was increased among twins with UC compared with their healthy twin siblings, OR = 3.85 (95% CI 1.17 – 12.73; p = 0.03). The prevalence of pANCA was higher in females than in males (OR 5.25, 95% CI 1.36 – 20.30; p = 0.02). The analysis did not reveal a substantial impact of smoking. However, stratifying for disease status and analysing twins with UC and their healthy siblings independently, an interaction between disease status and smoking status was detected. Among twins with UC, none of the active smokers were pANCA positive (Figure 2a). In contrast, 11/30 of the never‐smokers were pANCA positive, indicating a protective role of smoking on pANCA status in twins with UC. The stratified logistic regression supported this tendency (Table 3A).

Impact of Subclinical Inflammation on ANCA Levels in the Healthy Twin Siblings

Correlations between CRP and ANCA levels were assessed to reveal any association between subclinical inflammation and ANCA in twin siblings not affected by the disease. There was a statistically significant correlation between high‐sensitive CRP and ANCA levels (p < 0.05, corr = 0.37) in the healthy twins (Figure S1A). To characterise the subclinical inflammation in the healthy twins, we used proximity extension assay to assess the serum levels of 92 markers of inflammation. Significant correlations were found between ANCA level and C‐X‐C motif chemokine 9 (CXCL9, corr = 0.40), CUB domain‐containing protein 1 (CDCP1, corr = 0.41), Interleukin‐17A (IL17 A, corr = 0.41), and Interleukin‐5 (IL5, corr = 0.36) (Figure S1B–E).

Discussion

The mechanism underlying the development of pANCA in the preclinical phase of UC is largely unknown. By analysing a European cohort of twins, we disentangled the influence of genetic predisposition, environmental risk factors and subclinical inflammation on the occurrence of pANCA. Only 1/15 healthy monozygotic siblings among pairs discordant for UC was pANCA positive compared to 3/29 healthy dizygotic siblings. Additionally, no similarity in ANCA levels was observed among discordant monozygotic pairs (ICC = 0.09) or discordant dizygotic pairs (ICC = −0. 20). These findings suggest that genetic predisposition does not play a predominant role in the development of pANCA. In contrast, the most firmly established protective environmental factor, that is, active smoking, appeared to have a protective effect against the development of ANCA in twins with UC and potentially pANCA as well. At the same time, female sex was associated with increased risks. Importantly, correlations between ANCA levels and several proinflammatory proteins, including CRP, in the healthy twin siblings suggest that ANCA may be secondary due to subclinical inflammation and not a primary event in the preclinical phase of UC.

The detection of antibodies against proteins in the nuclear lamina of neutrophils (known as pANCA) was first documented in patients with UC in 1990 [17, 18]. Since then, several studies have validated the correlation between pANCA and UC [19, 20, 21]. Nevertheless, the causation of ANCA formation, especially pANCA, remains largely elusive. In contrast to some autoimmune disorders [5], UC‐associated ANCA is detected as an atypical perinuclear staining in indirect immunofluorescence [22]. This atypical pANCA seems to target various proteins, and the target antigen likely represents an unidentified complex conformational epitope. There are few studies focussed on identifying mechanisms associated with the development of pANCA. Intriguingly, the presence of pANCA several years before the diagnosis of UC and the increased occurrence among first degree‐relatives of IBD patients have been reported [3, 6, 8, 9]. The latter observation suggests that exposure to genetic and environmental risk factors could potentially contribute to the development of pANCAs. However, our results demonstrate that genetic predisposition does not contribute to the production of pANCAs. This observation is supported by a previously published case series of 12 twin pairs with UC [23]. Moreover, it is partly in line with a recent study of pre‐diagnostic serum samples from the US Department of Defence Serum Repository [3]. Torres et al. examined the predictive capacity of pANCAs by analysing 599 serum samples from 199 individuals within the US Army who later developed UC. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve ranged from 0.59 at 5 years to 0.61 at 1 year before diagnosis of UC [3]. However, it is possible that genetic factors still play a role in determining the level of increase in ANCA levels among individuals who develop UC. The reason behind this is the high degree of agreement in ANCA levels (ICC = 0.72) observed among the three monozygotic twin pairs concordant for UC.

A key finding of this study is the identification of smoking as a protective factor for ANCA and potentially pANCA in UC. Several studies have demonstrated that UC predominantly affects non‐smokers and former smokers, and that active smoking exerts a protective effect against developing UC [24, 25, 26]. To our knowledge, the extent to which smoking may influence the formation of pANCAs is unknown. In the current study, smoking patterns seemed to interfere with the risk of positive pANCA status, as none of the eight UC twins who were active smokers were pANCA positive. In contrast, 37% of the never‐smokers with UC were pANCA positive. Unfortunately, we were unable to explore potential dose‐response relationships, as quantitative data on cumulative smoking exposure, such as pack‐years, were not available. The precise mechanism by which smoking inhibits the development of ANCA and potentially pANCA in UC can only be a matter of conjecture. The composition of tobacco smoke is complex and may exert its effects through various mechanisms, including the alteration of gut microbiota composition [27], induction of epigenetic modifications and modification of protein function via post‐translational modifications [28].

The protective effect of active smoking on ANCA production in UC contradicts the previously reported effect of smoking on the formation of anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs) and ACPA‐positive rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [29]. However, consistent with the association between female RA patients and positive ACPA status, we observed an association between female sex and increased risk of positive pANCAs. Prior studies involving sex‐stratified analyses of UC patients have not identified a statistically significant difference in the prevalence of pANCA between males and females [30, 31]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a statistically significant association between both the development and concentration of pANCA and sex in patients with UC. Several reports indicate that positive pANCA status may represent a subgroup of UC patients characterised by an aggressive disease course requiring early colectomy [30, 31, 32]. However, we could not confirm this hypothesis given that no difference in pANCA status or levels of ANCA was seen when twins were stratified and compared based on past colectomy.

In preclinical UC, pANCA and systemic inflammation characterised by, for example, elevated hsCRP and CXCL9 precede the diagnosis by years [2, 3, 9, 33], suggesting that pANCA is driven by subclinical inflammation specific to UC. In agreement with these findings, we discovered positive correlations between inflammatory biomarkers (hsCRP, CXCL9, IL17 A, IL5, CDCP1) and ANCA levels in healthy twins. CRP is a well‐established biomarker of disease activity in UC, and previous studies have reported elevated levels of CXCL9, IL‐17A, and IL‐5 in both newly diagnosed and prevalent UC patients. [34, 35]. Elevated inflammatory markers have also been described in relatives of patients with UC [2, 36]. Our results extend these findings and support the hypothesis that the development of pANCA is driven by a UC‐specific inflammatory response, triggered by exposure to genetic and environmental risk factors during the early preclinical phase of the disease. Alternatively, the systemic inflammatory response may be a consequence of an immune activation that leads to the generation of pANCA and precedes clinical disease.

The relevance of these inflammatory markers in at‐risk individuals is further supported by recent data from the Crohn's and Colitis Canada Genetics Environment Microbial Project (CCC‐GEM) cohort, where elevated levels of CXCL9 and CDCP1 were associated with a future diagnosis of IBD in healthy first‐degree relatives of patients with CD [37].

This study is limited by the small number of twin pairs, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals for several comparisons. The retrospective design is also a limitation as serum samples were not collected at the time of diagnosis or at a predetermined point during the follow‐up period. Furthermore, the absence of longitudinal samples limited our capacity to examine temporal relationships between environmental factors, inflammation and the development of pANCA. Still, the acquisition and storage of serum samples were identical within pairs, which minimises the potential for differential bias.

We cannot exclude the possibility that some healthy twin siblings might develop UC. The occurrence of concordance for UC is infrequent among twin pairs, especially dizygotic pairs [11], which likely decreases the likelihood of twins being inaccurately classified as healthy. Our analysis of non‐shared environmental factors was restricted to tobacco smoking and other environmental factors during adulthood. However, stochastic factors may also play a significant role in the generation of pANCA.

In conclusion, our data show that genetics has a minor influence on the development of pANCA. More likely, UC‐associated subclinical inflammation increases the risk of ANCA development. Smoking patterns, representing a non‐shared environmental factor, had a greater impact on the progression of pANCA status and ANCA levels. A possible protective effect of active smoking against the occurrence of pANCA was observed in twins with UC but not in their healthy twin siblings. Consistent with a previous report highlighting the heightened risk of ACPAs in female twins with RA [29], our findings show that being female is associated with an elevated risk of pANCA.
