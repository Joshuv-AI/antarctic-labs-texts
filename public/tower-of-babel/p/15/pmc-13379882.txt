Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy in Athletes
Authors: Alwaleed Aljohar, Matthew D Williamson, Reid A Mitchell, Thomas M Roston, Zachary Laksman, Andrew D Krahn, James McKinney, Nathaniel Moulson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExercise recommendations in athletes with arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy are shifting toward an individualized and genotype-informed approach. Specifically, athletes with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy represent a subgroup in whom the risk of disease progression and sudden cardiac death with continued high-intensity endurance exercise is considered higher. Contemporary practice, guided by consensus statements, emphasizes multidisciplinary care and shared decision-making to inform sports participation advice and overall disease management. We highlight these principles and the clinical complexities faced when applying guideline recommendations into real-world practice through 2 illustrative cases.Key words: arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, athletes, exercise, shared decision-making, sports participation

Shared Decision-Making Outcomes and Management of 2 Athletes With ARVC

AED = automated external defibrillator; ARVC = arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy; EAP = emergency action plan; ICD = implantable cardioverter-defibrillator; PVC = premature ventricular contraction; RV = right ventricle; SCD = sudden cardiac death; SDM = shared decision-making; TFC = Task Force Criteria.

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is a heart muscle disorder characterized by a spectrum of clinical phenotypes, and it is an important cause of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in athletes.1,2 Initially described as a condition predominantly affecting the right ventricle (RV), the definition has since expanded to include biventricular and left-dominant forms under the broader ACM umbrella.2 A range of disease-causing genotypes have been identified, and contemporary management is shifting toward a genotype-informed approach. Exercise counseling constitutes a central component in management, as vigorous intensity or competitive sports participation, particularly endurance-based sports, may promote disease progression and is associated with an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias and SCD.1,3 This association is most clearly established in the RV-dominant phenotype (ie, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy [ARVC]), especially in individuals harboring mutations in the PKP2 gene.1,3 A shared decision-making (SDM) approach for exercise recommendations is now considered standard-of-care for athletes with cardiovascular disease,4 and current sports cardiology guidelines endorse the incorporation of genotype in guiding exercise recommendations.5,6

Here, we present 2 cases of athletes with classic ARVC, illustrating the clinical challenges encountered when applying the current guideline recommendations. We outline the importance of individualized risk assessment for continued sports participation within the frameworks of SDM and multidisciplinary care.

Case 1

A 53-year-old male endurance runner (∼70-100 miles [110-160 km]/wk for >20 years) presented with exertional syncope. Family history was unremarkable. An electrocardiogram revealed T-wave inversions in V1-V5 and inferior leads (Figure 1). Echocardiography demonstrated mild biventricular dilatation and reduced systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction: 42%; RV systolic function mildly reduced). Exercise stress testing revealed a 4-beat run of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia at peak exercise. Holter monitoring showed 2% multifocal premature ventricular contractions (1,651 per 24 h). Coronary computed tomography angiography revealed mild nonobstructive disease in the right coronary artery. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed a mildly dilated left ventricle with preserved systolic function (indexed end-diastolic volume: 116 mL/m2, ejection fraction: 56%), a severely dilated RV with moderately reduced systolic function and global hypokinesis (indexed end-diastolic volume: 169 mL/m2, ejection fraction: 36%), and no definitive late gadolinium enhancement in the myocardium (Video 1). Genetic testing was negative. A definite diagnosis of gene-elusive ARVC was fulfilled (2 major criteria: reduced RV function with global hypokinesis and T-wave inversions; 1 minor criterion: >500 premature ventricular contractions per 24 h).2 The estimated 5-year risk for fatal ventricular arrhythmias was 9.5% (arvcrisk.com), for which a primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was recommended. An SDM discussion regarding exercise recommendations was initiated. The patient expressed a preference to continue participating in endurance exercise. The risk of disease progression was emphasized, and reduction in exercise volume and intensity was recommended.5,6 After this SDM process, the patient ultimately decided to limit endurance training to general health recommendations (150-300 min/wk of moderate aerobic activity), with incorporation of guideline-based levels of resistance training.7 A dual-chamber ICD was inserted, and no recurrent arrhythmic events occurred during a 2-year follow-up period.

Case 2

A 17-year-old competitive male basketball player with a family history of ARVC was found to have a truncating variant in the PKP2 gene during family screening (c.1978C>T, p.[Gln660∗]). The proband is his mother, who had recurrent ventricular tachycardia requiring 2 ablation procedures and ICD insertion. He was asymptomatic, and his electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm with T-wave inversion in leads V1-V3 (Figure 2). Echocardiography revealed normal biventricular size and function. Exercise stress testing did not reveal significant arrhythmias. On Holter monitoring he had 1% (1,475 per 24 h) multifocal premature ventricular contractions. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed normal left ventricular size (indexed end-diastolic volume: 92 mL/m2) and function, mildly dilated RV (indexed end-diastolic volume: 119 mL/m2) with preserved systolic function, no regional wall motion abnormalities, and no late gadolinium enhancement. A definite diagnosis of ARVC was fulfilled (2 major criteria: T-wave inversions and positive genetic testing; 1 minor criterion: >500 premature ventricular complexes per 24 h).2 The 5-year risk for ventricular arrhythmia was estimated at 27% (arvcrisk.com), and a primary prevention ICD was recommended. An SDM approach to return-to-play was undertaken. The athlete's initial goals were to complete his last year of high-school basketball and to compete at the collegiate level. Medical recommendations, consistent with contemporary clinical guidelines,5 were to avoid participation in competitive sports over the longer term. Ultimately, respecting the goals of the patient and balancing the risks of continued sports participation, the patient, with family input, decided to complete his final year of competitive high school basketball followed by transition to recreational physical activity for health and wellness goals. A detailed emergency action plan (EAP) was developed, which included the purchase of an automated external defibrillator until ICD insertion was completed.

Discussion

These cases highlight the clinical challenges of contemporary management of athletes with ARVC. Key management goals include appropriate risk stratification and mitigation strategies for SCD, along with prevention of disease progression. Guideline-based management strategies include antiarrhythmic therapy and ICD insertion. Restriction from competitive sports has been a mainstay of management for athletes with ACM, especially in cases of classic ARVC.2 More recently, there has been a paradigm shift recommending SDM for return-to-play decisions.4, 5, 6 This includes the use of disease-specific risk scores and the individualization of exercise recommendations informed by the integration of genotype, phenotype, and sport type. Regardless of the outcome of a return-to-play decision, best possible therapies should be implemented. In athletes who continue participation in vigorous intensity exercise, a detailed management plan including a personalized EAP is recommended.5,6,8

Unrestricted vigorous-intensity exercise, particularly endurance-based sports, may promote disease progression and increase the risk of ventricular arrhythmias in patients with ARVC.1,2 Most evidence stems from cohorts predominantly harboring the PKP2 mutation,3 which are typically associated with RV-dominant disease.2 The RV is more susceptible to the effects of high-intensity, high-volume exercise, likely given its thinner free wall and marked elevation in pulmonary artery pressure during exercise compared with rest.1,4 Other genotypes within the broader ACM spectrum have variable degrees of RV involvement, and recent observational studies suggest that the harmful effects of exercise may not uniformly apply to all genotypes.1 This variability likely reflects the broader and more heterogeneous disease spectrum compared with classic ARVC. Accordingly, the updated 2024 Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) and 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines now recommend incorporating the genotype into exercise counseling.5,6 Both guidelines agree that athletes with PKP2-ARVC remain at increased risk of disease progression and arrhythmias with continued unrestricted vigorous intensity sport and exercise.5,6 However, the HRS statement provides more granular recommendations for those with non-PKP2 variants through graded risk assessment of disease progression, arrhythmias/SCD, and disease penetrance in family members based on the underlying genotype (eg, desmoplakin, lamin A/C, phospholamban), as well as those with gene-elusive disease.6

A patient-centered approach in an SDM framework has become the standard-of-care in sports cardiology.5 Its core principles include sharing medical knowledge, respecting athlete autonomy, adopting a team-based mindset, and transparent communication.4 This approach was shown to reduce decision conflict and decision regret in ARVC patients and their at-risk relatives when counseled about exercise.9 Medical recommendations should indicate the degree of potential cardiac risk, based on available evidence, expert opinion, or both, with emphasis on areas of uncertainties. These principles were reflected in the SDM process of the athlete in case 1. The 2025 ACC/AHA guidelines indicate that athletes with non-PKP2 variants “can consider” competitive sports participation after appropriate risk assessment.5 However, the athlete in this case exhibited high-risk features with predominant RV involvement, and the likelihood of disease progression with continued high-intensity exercise was judged to be high. Importantly, non-PKP2 ACM represents a heterogeneous disease, and the risk of progression varies by underlying genotype, including in nonfamilial or gene-elusive disease.1 For master athletes who develop later-onset gene-elusive ARVC, cumulative exposure to lifelong endurance exercise may induce a phenotype referred to as “exercise-induced ARVC.”1,10 This is essentially a diagnosis of exclusion that requires detailed quantification of lifetime exercise dose combined with expert clinical judgment. Contrary to the ACC/AHA framework, the HRS recommendations provide more definitive guidance in this setting. Specifically, athletes with gene-elusive ARVC (presumably exercise-induced) are considered at elevated risk of disease progression and arrhythmias and should be counseled that continued vigorous endurance exercise is harmful.6 This position is supported by the potential benefit of modifying disease trajectory through the removal of the environmental stimulus (ie, sustained endurance exercise), but evidence remains limited.10 In this athlete, understanding disease mechanisms and integrating risk profile and phenotype were central to counseling against continued high-intensity exercise. This ultimately led to a shared decision to significantly reduce exercise exposure.

Quantifying a balanced exercise prescription for athletes wishing to maintain some degree of endurance training in this context remains challenging. Furthermore, athletes who choose to continue high-intensity endurance exercise despite counseling for reduction may require closer surveillance with more frequent follow-up and a personalized EAP as part of SDM.8 In contrast, the guidelines provide clearer direction regarding exercise in PKP2-ARVC, where disease progression is more likely.5,6 While SDM promotes athlete autonomy and respects core values, the potential harm of unrestricted vigorous intensity exercise must be clearly communicated. Importantly, the risks of continued exercise appear to be cumulative over years,3 whereas the short-term impact remains largely unknown. These nuances were discussed as part of the SDM process for the athlete in case 2. Emphasizing the difficulty in quantifying short-term risk may help athletes make decisions about temporary participation, even in otherwise traditionally prohibitive clinical scenarios, offering a potential compromise that aligns with shared goals, as exemplified in this case.

Other important considerations inform SDM and individualized care in this realm. First, ARVC has a variable disease course and phenotypic expression. Incorporating the phenotype was central to SDM in both athletes. For example, the athlete in case 1 demonstrated advanced structural manifestations and high-risk syncope. Regardless of whether his presentation reflected exercise-induced ARVC or true gene-elusive disease, continued high-intensity endurance exercise was considered to increase risk to a prohibitive level, therefore recommended against. Conversely, the athlete in case 2 exhibited mild phenotypic disease manifestations in the absence of high-risk features. This lower risk phenotype allowed for the consideration of short-term continuation of competitive sports participation over 1 year (rather than the originally intended 5 years). In both cases, the decisions were carefully curated in a multidisciplinary approach by sports cardiologists and disease-specific experts (eg, specialists in cardiovascular genetics, electrophysiology, and/or heart failure) to enhance precision and quality of care. Second, longitudinal reassessment and risk stratification remain essential during follow-up.5 This applies equally to athletes who elect to continue competitive sports participation to monitor for disease progression as well as to those who initially restrict exercise, as clinical impression, response to therapy, or improved risk profile may justify reconsideration of exercise intensity over time. Third, risk stratification for fatal ventricular arrhythmias and SCD is a cornerstone of management for all ARVC patients.2 The discussion for ICD insertion frequently arises in the evaluation of athletes; however, contemporary guidelines do not recommend ICD insertion for the sole purpose of return to play.5,6 Conversely, a return-to-play decision should not be swayed by the presence or absence of an ICD. Finally, for athletes who ultimately elect to return to competitive sports after SDM, a personalized EAP is essential and should be developed for all athletes as part of their management plan.8

Conclusions

The cases highlight the paradigm shift toward an individualized and genotype-informed approach for athletes with ARVC. Exercise recommendations require careful risk assessment and thoughtful integration of genotype, phenotype, and sport type to construct a personalized management plan through an SDM process. In athletes with ARVC, management includes a detailed and personalized EAP for those who return to play, in addition to ongoing disease surveillance, and appropriate SCD risk assessment with consideration of ICD insertion as indicated.
