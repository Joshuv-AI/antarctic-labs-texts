Development and validation of a prognostic model for colon cancer based on mitotic gene signatures and immune microenvironment analysis
Authors: Changhao Gu, Lulu Jin, Xiaoyan Lv, Cheng Wang, Congle Wen, Xiuxiu Su
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMitotic processes play a pivotal role in tumor progression and immune responses. However, the correlation between mitosis-related genes, clinical outcomes, and the tumor microenvironment (TME) in colon cancer remains unclear. This study aims to develop a prognostic and therapeutic significance model for colon cancer based on mitosis-related genes.MethodsRNA expression profiles and clinical data of 453 colon cancer patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Mitosis-related genes were selected from the MsigDb database. The gene model was constructed using differential analysis, univariate and multivariate Cox regression, and Lasso regression analyses. The predictive model was validated using data from the GSE17536, GSE17537, and GSE39582 datasets. Predictive accuracy was evaluated via Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, while nomograms were developed by integrating clinical and pathological features. Gene set enrichment analysis explored biological processes and pathways linked to the model. TME was assessed using ESTIMATE, and the proportion and function of immune cells were analyzed through CIBERSORT. Drug sensitivity analysis was conducted using the CTRP database.ResultsA predictive model based on 17 mitosis-related genes (KIFC1, CCNF, EME1, CDC25C, ORC1, CCNJL, ANKRD53, MEIS2, FZD3, TPD52L1, MAPK3, CDKN2A, EDN3, NPM2, PSRC1, INHBA, BIRC5) was created. The model exhibited robust predictive performance across both training and validation cohorts. Nomograms for predicting 3-, 5-, and 7-year survival rates in colon cancer (COAD) patients were generated. The model's correlation with immune cell infiltration and function was highlighted.ConclusionThe mitosis-related gene model serves as a valuable indicator for predicting survival outcomes in colon cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01421-2.

Introduction

Globally, colorectal carcinoma is one of the most common oncological malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. Its development and progression are driven by a complex interplay of genetic mutations, cellular interactions, and environmental factors [2]. Despite advances in detection and treatment methods over recent decades, many cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to a five-year survival rate between 15 and 30% [3, 4]. In contrast, early diagnosis can elevate survival rates to approximately 85% [4].

The emergence of predictive models based on gene expression has markedly enhanced the ability to assess colon cancer prognosis. Nonetheless, the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying CRC outcomes present challenges for single-gene or single-factor models, often leading to suboptimal accuracy. Multi-gene models, on the other hand, have generally shown improved efficacy in predicting cancer prognosis. For example, models that incorporate pyroptosis-related gene expression have demonstrated their relevance in forecasting immune responses and clinical outcomes in colon cancer patients [5]. Furthermore, the prognostic value of immune-related genes has been particularly notable in early-stage CRC [6]. One such model, developed using differential protein expression between cold and hot CRC tumors, serves as a standalone prognostic indicator for stage II CRC (T3-4N0M0) [7]. Another model, which includes 30 genes related to demethylation, has proven effective in predicting the survival risk of colon cancer patients and in assessing the tumor immune microenvironment [8]. Despite these advances, the focus of most current multi-gene models on large and diverse gene sets can limit their stability and applicability, as they often encompass broad and complex biological processes.

Mitotic division is a critical process within the cell cycle involving the replication and division of cells. Dysregulation of mitosis is one of the primary contributors to the unchecked proliferation of tumor cells. As a complex signaling cascade, mitosis can serve as a new therapeutic target for cancer treatment [9]. In recent years, mitotic catastrophe (MC) has gained significant attention for its role in tumor development. Inducing MC in tumor cells has been used to optimize clinical cancer treatment and reverse multidrug resistance in some tumors [10]. For example, inducing MC in oral cancer cells has shown potential as a promising therapeutic approach [11]. Other studies also reveal a relationship between MC and the prognosis of breast [12] and prostate cancers [13].

Despite these findings, the role of mitosis-related genes in the prognosis of COAD (colon adenocarcinoma) patients and immune response in colon cancer remains inadequately explored. Our model focuses on the critical biological process of mitosis, enabling more accurate predictions of tumor proliferation and patient prognosis, thereby demonstrating greater potential in personalized treatment. This study aims to establish prognostic modeling of mitosis-associated genes as an independent factor influencing colon cancer prognosis. The study seeks to improve prognostic classification and assess immunological infiltration in colon cancer patients, predict drug sensitivity, unveil the underlying pathogenesis of colon cancer, deepen the understanding of the cancer's immune microenvironment, and ultimately promote precise clinical intervention strategies, particularly in personalized medicine and immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

High-throughput RNA sequencing datasets, along with corresponding clinical information for patients diagnosed with Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD), were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. This dataset contains 453 COAD tissue samples and 41 normal tissue controls, with comprehensive clinical characteristics meticulously documented (see Table 1). The Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) is widely recognized as one of the most extensively utilized repositories of well-annotated gene sets. Genes associated with mitosis were identified by querying the keyword “mitotic” on the MSigDB platform (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). A univariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was then used to identify genes significantly associated with patient survival (P-value ≤ 0.1). An intersection selection step extracted a core set of prognostic genes from the differentially expressed gene pool.

Development and assessment of a mitotic-associated gene prognostic model

The Lasso Cox regression method was used to identify mitosis-associated genes crucial for COAD prognosis, leading to the development of an evaluative risk model. This model computes a risk score for each patient based on the expression levels of selected genes, classifying them into high or low-risk groups using the median score as the cutoff. Survival outcomes across these risk groups were compared using the survival package in R. An independent cohort from the GEO database was analyzed to validate the model’s prognostic reliability.

Comprehensive prognostic evaluation and nomogram development

A comprehensive analysis using multivariate Cox regression was conducted to elucidate the relationships between risk scores, patient survival, and clinical characteristics. The accuracy of these predictors was evaluated through Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A predictive nomogram was created based on this analysis, and its validity was confirmed through Kaplan–Meier survival plots and calibration curves, demonstrating its applicability.

Exploration of molecular functions and pathways

A detailed functional enrichment analysis was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases to identify molecular functions and pathways related to the risk model. This analysis differentiated the biological functions and signaling pathways between the high and low-risk groups.

Correlation between risk model and the tumor microenvironment

The relationship between the risk model and the Tumor Microenvironment (TME) components, including stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores, was assessed using the ESTIMATE method. Correlations were quantified via Spearman rank correlation, with statistical significance set at a P-value of 0.05.

Immune cell infiltration and analysis of potential drugs in COAD

The relationship between the risk model and tumor immunity was examined by comparing immune cell infiltration patterns and immune checkpoint gene expression between high and low-risk groups using the CIBERSORT algorithm. The Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP, http://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp/) was utilized for therapeutic drug screening. The prognostic model was applied to cell line expression, dividing the cells into high and low-risk groups.

Examination of KIFC1 protein expression and single-cell expression profiles

The expression level of the KIFC1 protein was determined using immunohistochemistry (IHC) images from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Furthermore, the Cancer Single Cell Expression Map (CancerSCEM, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/cancerscem) facilitated analysis of KIFC1's expression across different cell subtypes in colorectal cancer.

Analytical methodologies

All data analyses were executed using R (version 4.2.2). The Wilcoxon and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for binary and multivariable group comparisons, respectively. Survival differences were assessed through Kaplan–Meier plots and log-rank tests, while Fisher's exact test was used to compare clinical and immune response classifications. Correlation and regression analyses used Spearman rank correlation, with a P-value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Validation of the mitotic related gene model

The TCGA cohort served as the training set, with patients classified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on median risk scores to assess the distribution of risk scores, survival statuses, and heatmap profiles (Fig. 3A–C). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients in the low-risk group had significantly better overall survival (OS) than those in the high-risk group (p < 0.001, HR = 3.83,95%CI 2.34–6.28)(Fig. 3D). Functional enrichment analysis of the risk model

Functional enrichment analysis was conducted on genes associated with the risk score using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses to clarify potential biological functions and pathways. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high-risk and low-risk groups were identified and examined in both GO and KEGG analyses. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in processes such as cartilage development and spliceosomal tri-snRNP complex assembly in Biological Processes (BP). In Cellular Components (CC), the apical plasma membrane showed notable enrichment. Molecular Function (MF) analysis indicated significant enrichment in receptor ligand activity and signaling receptor activity.

KEGG pathway analysis found that DEGs were largely enriched in pathways like cell adhesion molecules (Fig. 5A–B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) highlighted the enrichment of biological functions, including the calcium signaling pathway, cell adhesion molecules, ECM-receptor interaction, phagosome, and PPAR signaling pathway.Fig. 5Functional Analysis of the 17 Mitotic-related Gene Model. Functional Analysis of the 17 Mitotic-related Gene Model. A GO enrichment analysis. B KEGG enrichment analysis. C–D GSEA enrichment analysis

In the functional enrichment analysis, we identified a series of important biological processes and signaling pathways related to the prognosis of colorectal cancer patients. Firstly, the calcium signaling pathway plays a critical role in the regulation of various cellular functions, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Abnormal calcium signaling may lead to abnormal tumor cell proliferation and enhanced anti-apoptotic capabilities. The PPAR signaling pathway is closely associated with lipid metabolism and cell differentiation, exhibiting dual roles in colorectal cancer; it may inhibit tumor growth, but under certain conditions, it can also promote the survival of tumor cells and their adaptation to unfavorable microenvironments. Additionally, cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) and ECM-receptor interactions play crucial roles in the interactions between tumor cells and their microenvironment. Abnormal expression of CAMs may affect the invasive and metastatic abilities of tumor cells, while changes in the extracellular matrix can directly impact the survival and adaptability of tumor cells. The phagosome pathway plays an important role in immune responses, particularly by promoting antigen presentation and the clearance of apoptotic cells. The function of phagosomes in the tumor microenvironment may be related to mechanisms of tumor immune evasion, thereby affecting patient prognosis. In terms of gene expression and regulation, the assembly of the spliceosomal tri-snRNP complex is closely related to RNA splicing, and abnormal RNA splicing is often associated with cancer development and maintenance. Abnormal assembly of the spliceosome may lead to the generation of specific isoforms of tumor-related genes, thereby influencing the behavior of tumor cells. The enrichment of the apical plasma membrane plays an important role in maintaining cell polarity and signal transduction, and its abnormalities may be related to enhanced migration, invasion, and metastatic capabilities of tumor cells.

These results emphasize the significant functional enrichment of DEGs in supporting signal transduction, cell function regulation, intercellular interaction, and microenvironment adaptation (Fig. 5C–D).

Analysis of potential drugs in COAD

The Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) was utilized for potential therapeutic drug screening. Forty-two colon cancer cell samples and 545 drugs were initially evaluated. The prognostic model was applied to analyze cell line expression and classify them into high-risk and low-risk groups. Differences in drug sensitivity between these groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test.

Significant differences were observed (P < 0.01), with compounds displaying higher sensitivity in the high-risk group. Expression of KIFC1 in different cell subtypes in Colon Cancer

The expression of KIFC1 in Different Cell Subtypes in colorectal cancer (CRC) single-cell samples was analyzed using the Cancer Single Cell Expression Map database (CancerSCEM, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/cancerscem). KIFC1 gene expression in 16 single cell samples was analyzed, and it was observed that KIFC1 expression level was significant in the following cell types: such as the expression in CD4 + Tcm, CD4 + Tem, CD8 + effector T cells, etc. (Fig. 7C). This broad expression pattern of KIFC1 reveals a possible multifaceted role in colon tumor development and progression. The high expression in malignant cells, in particular, may indicate that this gene is a potential therapeutic target, and its inhibition may affect tumor cell survival and spread.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized RNA expression profiles and clinical data from 453 colon cancer patients in the TCGA database to develop a predictive model based on mitosis-related genes. This model was independently validated using the GSE17536, GSE17537, and GSE39582 datasets and was further combined with clinical and pathological features to create nomograms that can predict the 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year survival rates for patients with Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD). The model, constructed on mitosis-related genes, exhibited strong predictive performance in both the training and validation cohorts.

Eukaryotic cells deploy complex mechanisms to minimize errors during chromosome segregation and cell division. Cells will not proceed to mitosis without first completing DNA replication and repairing any newly replicated DNA if damaged [14].When severe chromosomal aberrations or mitotic spindle dysfunction occur, mitotic catastrophe represents an irreversible cell cycle arrest, rendering cells unable to complete mitosis. The cell death caused by mitotic catastrophe can occur through apoptosis or necrosis and may also trigger autophagic cell death [10].Mitotic catastrophe is considered a tumor-suppressive mechanism, preventing the survival of cells that cannot complete mitosis due to defects in the mitotic apparatus, DNA damage, or mitotic checkpoint errors [9].

KIFC1: This motor protein is involved in the retrograde transport of microtubules. KIFC1 is upregulated in cancer cells following CA induction as a compensatory mechanism, aiding cancer cells in generating additional centrioles to avoid cell death. Its aberrant expression is linked to the invasive and metastatic potential of cancer cells [15].CCNF: A crucial member of the F-box and cyclin protein families, CCNF mutations or dysfunction can lead to cell cycle dysregulation. There is evidence that F-box proteins exert control over cell cycle mediators, which may either promote or inhibit cancer progression [16].EME1 participates in DNA repair, particularly during mitosis. Dysfunction can result in genomic instability, a critical factor in cancer development. EME1 clears reversed-fork Holliday junctions to support DNA replication in cancer cells, potentially contributing to their proliferation [17].CDC25C: This key enzyme regulates the cell cycle transition from G2 to M phase, overseeing the progression into mitosis [18]. Elevated expression of CDC25C is observed in various cancers, including gastric [19] and lung cancers [20].ORC1 is a protein complex with six highly conserved subunits necessary for initiating DNA replication in eukaryotic cells [21]. ORC1, the largest subunit, is crucial for DNA replication and cell cycle regulation. Abnormal ORC1 expression can impair DNA replication, promoting tumorigenesis. CCNJL: A member of the cyclin family that regulates cell cycle progression and potentially affects tumor cell proliferation and survival. Evidence indicates that CCNJL is upregulated in liver cancer, serving as a potential diagnostic biomarker [22].ANKRD53 localizes to the microtubules of the mitotic spindle, modulating their polymerization and cytokinesis. Aberrant methylation of the ANKRD53 gene may cause an increased frequency of anaphase lagging chromosomes [23]. MEIS2: As part of the TALE superfamily, MEIS2 plays a crucial role in regulating Hox activity by directly binding with posterior Hox proteins or indirectly with Pbx to form a homeoprotein-DNA complex. It plays a vital role in the pathogenesis of human cancer [24, 25]. FZD3: A component of the Wnt signaling pathway, FZD3 influences cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation. The Wnt pathway is activated in various cancer types.TPD52L1: TPD52 family proteins are associated with cell proliferation, being upregulated in several tumors and potentially serving as tumor markers [26, 27]. MAPK3: An integral member of the MAPK/ERK pathway, MAPK3 plays a pivotal role in cell proliferation and cancer progression.CDKN2A: A tumor suppressor protein, CDKN2A prevents the degradation of the p53 tumor suppressor by interacting with the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase MDM2. CDKN2A mutations can cause aberrant cell cycle processes across various cancers [28]. EDN3: The balance of endothelin receptor activity determines gene expression, cell division, and differentiation. In cancer cells, this balance is disrupted, leading to tumor progression and invasion into neighboring tissues [29]. NPM2: a histone-binding chaperone, is crucial for chromatin remodeling, genomic stability, ribosome biogenesis, DNA replication, and transcription regulation. It is implicated in cancer development, progression, prognosis, and outcomes [30, 31]. PSRC1, a microtubule-associated cancer protein, is involved in multiple signaling pathways. Its overexpression is seen in various cancers, including colorectal cancer [32]. INHBA: As part of the TGF-β superfamily, INHBA is associated with promoting EMT, cell proliferation, and the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor environment [33]. BIRC5: A member of the IAP family, BIRC5 is typically expressed in tumors and embryonic tissues. Its high expression is linked to poor prognosis across multiple malignancies and is closely associated with tumor cell differentiation, proliferation, infiltration, and metastasis [34, 35].

Moreover, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed biological processes and pathways significantly associated with the Mitotic-related risk score, which are notably enriched in calcium signaling pathways, cell adhesion molecules, ECM-receptor interactions, phagosomes, and PPAR signaling pathways. This enrichment underscores that DEGs are significantly involved in supporting signal transduction, regulating cellular functions, and facilitating cell–cell interactions and microenvironment modulation.

Patients in the high-risk group displayed significantly higher Estimate and Stromal Scores than those in the low-risk group (P < 0.05). This finding indicates that patients with higher risk scores possess tumors characterized by a more intricate cellular composition and increased stromal cell presence.

EMT (epithelial-mesenchymal transition) is a process in which stationary epithelial cells acquire a motile mesenchymal phenotype, contributing to cancer invasion and metastasis [36, 37]. It plays a crucial role in cancer stemness, immune evasion, metabolic reprogramming, and therapy resistance [38–40]. Elevated EMT scores in the high-risk group emphasize their enhanced metastatic and invasive characteristics, which are critical for accurate prognostic assessment.

Interactions among different components of the tumor microenvironment influence tumor growth and metastasis and promote chemotherapy resistance [41]. Chemotherapy resistance mechanisms in tumors are complex, involving interactions across numerous signaling pathways [42]. Accurate identification of drug-sensitive patient populations is essential. In our study, we analyzed drug sensitivity in high- and low-risk groups. High-risk patients demonstrated increased sensitivity to several compounds, including vorinostat, CAL-101, and EGCG. The predictive model's assessment of colon cancer drug treatment sensitivity aims to improve COAD treatment strategies.

The mitosis-associated gene prognostic model developed in this study demonstrates significant advantages in predicting the survival of patients with colon cancer. Existing multi-gene models, such as those based on immune-related genes, may exhibit variability in predictive power depending on the dataset and patient population due to the functional diversity of the involved genes. By focusing specifically on genes closely related to mitosis, our model directly links cell cycle regulation with tumor proliferation. This specificity enhances the model's predictive accuracy for tumor behavior and patient prognosis, with performance metrics demonstrating strong predictive capabilities. Moreover, our model has shown robustness across multiple independent datasets.

In terms of clinical application, this model holds substantial potential. First, it can serve as a complementary tool to existing clinical indicators, such as TNM staging, aiding in the identification of high-risk patients and informing the development of personalized treatment strategies. Furthermore, the analysis of the relationship between the genes in our model and the tumor microenvironment suggests that this model may be useful in predicting patient responses to specific therapies, such as immunotherapy, thus offering new perspectives for personalized treatment. Given the model’s focus on cell proliferation mechanisms, it may be particularly suited for developing personalized treatment plans, such as identifying patients who may benefit from specific anti-mitotic agents.

Despite the numerous advantages of our model, we acknowledge its limitations. Primarily, this study is based on data from TCGA and other publicly available databases. While these datasets provide a wealth of information, their representativeness may be limited. For instance, the TCGA patient cohort is predominantly from Western countries, with a relatively small sample size, which may affect the model's applicability to broader, more diverse populations across different racial and geographic backgrounds. Additionally, although we employed various statistical analyses to control for potential confounding factors, some limitations remain inevitable, such as sample heterogeneity, incomplete clinical information, and the inherent constraints of sequencing technologies. To further establish the clinical value of this model, future studies should validate it in more diverse populations and assess its utility across different clinical settings. Moreover, the implementation of this model will require careful consideration of how to integrate it into existing clinical workflows and how to overcome potential technical and economic barriers. Through these efforts, we hope that this model will play a crucial role in improving survival rates and optimizing treatment decisions for colon cancer patients.

In conclusion, we identified seventeen prognostic genes associated with mitotic signaling in Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD) and developed a robust prognostic model capable of independently predicting survival outcomes. This model is associated with the immune microenvironment and can differentiate the immune status of COAD patients. More importantly, it can provide new therapeutic options for COAD patients in various risk categories.

Although our model has demonstrated excellent performance in existing datasets, further research is essential. Future studies should focus on experimentally validating the functions of key genes in the model, including through cell-based assays and animal models, to confirm their specific roles in tumor biology. Conducting clinical trials in broader and more diverse populations is crucial to evaluate the model’s effectiveness in real-world clinical settings. These future research directions will help to further solidify and expand the findings of this study, providing more reliable foundations for early detection and personalized treatment of colon cancer.

Limitation

There are several limitations to this study. The prognostic model based on mitotic gene signatures and immune microenvironment analysis was developed using data from colon cancer patients in the TCGA dataset, and its validation was limited to the GEO datasets. More experimental validation is necessary to reinforce the applicability of the model. Additionally, the impact of the model on improving patient quality of life needs to be verified through clinical practice. Future research should focus on identifying these subgroups through stratification studies, as the prognostic model may be more sensitive and specific for certain populations.
