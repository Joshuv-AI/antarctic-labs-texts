The effectiveness of digital nudging in improving dietary quality: A systematic review of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Lan Ge, Xuewei Guan, Leiwen Tang, Qianyin Zhu, Yumei Zhong, Meijuan Lan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital nudging is increasingly being used in the field of dietary quality change. However, existing reviews have not systematically evaluated the comparative effectiveness of different digital nudging strategies on dietary quality, particularly within the context of randomized controlled trials.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to systematically review the scientific literature on the effectiveness of digital nudging in improving dietary quality, as observed in randomized controlled trials.DesignA systematic review of randomized controlled trials.Information sourcesPubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane, Embase, VIP, CNKI, and Wanfang database.MethodsA structured search was conducted by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The initial search spanned studies from the inception of each database to July 7, 2024, and an updated search was performed from July 8, 2024, to April 30, 2025. The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (ROB 2) was used to assess the risk of bias in the trials.ResultsA total of 22 trials were included in the review, with most studies showing a high risk of attrition bias. Among them, seven were conducted in the United States, 13 in Europe, and 2 in Asia, all involving adult participants aged 18 years or older. Nutritional labeling (n = 11), increased product accessibility (n = 5), framing effects (n = 4), and priming nudges (n = 1) were each associated with improvements in dietary quality. In particular, combined nudging strategies, especially the combination of labeling and availability enhancement (n = 5), demonstrated stronger effects than single nudges. The effectiveness of digital nudging interventions was also influenced by factors such as individuals’ dietary preferences, contextual settings, and socioeconomic status.ConclusionsDigital nudging, particularly with nutritional labels and increasing accessibility of products, showed the most consistent positive effects, improving food choice and dietary intake.Clinical trial registrationThe review protocol was registered in PROSPERO under the number CRD42024560677.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24069-1.Keywords: Digital nudging, Dietary quality, Food choice, Nutrients intake, Randomized controlled trial, Review

Introduction

As an important factor affecting human health and quality of life, dietary quality has attracted much attention [1–3]. Dietary quality, defined as the extent to which a diet meets nutritional requirements and aligns with health guidelines [4], is a critical determinant of human health and quality of life. High-quality diets have been shown to reduce the risk of chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes [2]. However, recent studies indicate a persistent gap between individuals’ nutritional knowledge and their actual dietary behaviors [5].

This knowledge-behavior gap is well explained by behavioral economics, particularly dual-process theories of decision-making. These theories posit that individuals’ choices are influenced by both deliberate (reflective) and intuitive (automatic) cognitive processes [6]. Based on this understanding, Thaler and Sunstein introduced the concept of “nudging” which involves subtly altering the choice architecture to steer behavior while preserving individual freedom of choice. Nudging leverages cognitive biases to bridge the gap between knowledge and behavior [7], and numerous studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in promoting healthier food choices in real-world settings [8, 9]. With the rise of digital technology, food choices are increasingly made in digital environments such as virtual supermarkets and online food ordering platforms. Consequently, nudging strategies have extended beyond the physical realm into digital contexts [10]. In 2016, Weinmann et al. introduced the concept of digital nudging, defining it as the use of user-interface design elements to guide individuals’ behavior in digital choice environments [11]. Digital nudging enables a range of advanced strategies—such as personalized defaults, real-time feedback, and adaptive interface modifications—facilitated by big data analytics and machine learning. These approaches have shown promising results in influencing user behavior effectively.

Recent studies have explored the use of digital nudging to improve dietary quality. For example, modifying the availability of lower-energy meals [12], setting healthier food options as defaults [13], and enhancing the visual appeal of healthy recipes [14] have all been shown to influence food choices in digital settings. As digital environments continue to evolve, digital nudging is emerging as a scalable and increasingly influential tool for promoting healthier eating behaviors [15].

Recent reviews have highlighted a growing interest in digital nudging within dietary contexts; however, there remains a lack of clarity regarding which specific nudging strategies are most effective [16]. Moreover, responses to digital nudging appear to vary across user characteristics and settings [17]. For instance, individuals with higher socioeconomic status (SES) were more responsive to nutritional labels when selecting low-energy staple foods in virtual full-service restaurants, whereas similar labeling interventions had no measurable effect in virtual fast-food environments [18]. Given the increasing adoption of digital food environments, systematically evaluating the effectiveness of different digital nudging strategies is of growing importance. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically review all scientific knowledge obtained to date from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) about the effects of digital nudging on dietary quality.

Materials and methods

Study protocol and registration

We conducted this systematic review following the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and checklist [19]. The review protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024560677).

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic literature search in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, VIP Database, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Database. Two consecutive searches were conducted and limited by date, first from the inception of each database to July 7, 2024, and again from July 8, 2024, to April 30, 2025, in an updated search. The following keywords were included: “digital nudging”, “libertarian paternalistic”, “dietary behaviors”, “dietary intake”, and “dietary habits”. RCTs were included. We imposed no language restrictions. The updated search strategy was consistent with the original search. A complete database search strategy is shown in Supplemental Material.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants

Participants must be adults aged 18 years or older. We excluded studies that reported results from only one gender, as men and women may respond differently to dietary interventions [8, 20–22]. Including only mixed-gender samples helps reduce potential gender-related bias and improves the generalizability of the findings. Studies in participants who were exclusively patients of a specific medical ailment, such as neoplastic disease, were excluded. Similarly, studies were excluded if they targeted a population with a specific health condition that may impact dietary intake, e.g., those with disordered eating or cancer.

Intervention

This review focused on interventions that align with the definition of digital nudging [11]. Studies using one or more of the nudging strategies in digital choice environments are eligible, and studies that implement interventions in real-world settings were excluded.

Comparison

The control group received no nudging, in contrast to the group exposed to nudging. If the control condition reflected real-world practices, it was considered as non-nudge, as a nudge refers to a deliberately implemented modification of the choice architecture, rather than a mere reflection of pre-existing or naturally occurring default conditions [23].

Outcomes

Main outcomes related to dietary quality include food choices and dietary intake [4]. We excluded studies where diet measurements were self-reported, as self-reported data lacks objectivity and is susceptible to recall bias.

Study type

Only RCTs were included, as RCT was considered to provide the most substantial evidence for intervention effect. By employing randomization, RCTs significantly reduce potential biases and confounding factors, providing clearer and stronger evidence regarding the causal relationship between digital nudging strategies and improvements in dietary quality.

Data extraction and synthesis

Full texts were independently reviewed by the author team (X.G. and L.G.). Disagreements were resolved by a third-party evaluation (Y.Z.). Information extracted from the included trials was tabulated and included the following: the year the article was published, names of the authors, country, sample sizes, demographics such as sex and age, digital environment, interventions, outcomes measured, and principal findings.

A meta-analysis was not performed due to the heterogeneity of the included trials for variations in intervention types and outcome measures. Therefore, a narrative synthesis approach was employed to analyze and interpret the findings qualitatively.

Risk of bias of the included trials

The risk of bias of included trials was rigorously evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB 2) tool, a standardized instrument developed by the Cochrane Collaboration for systematic assessment of bias domains in RCTs [24]. The tool includes information on (i) selection bias (random sequence generation and allocation concealment), (ii) performance bias, (iii) detection bias, (iv) attrition bias, (v) reporting bias, and (vi) other bias.

Results

Study selection

A total of 41,427 records were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates, 34,802 titles and abstracts were screened. Of these, 1,684 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 17 studies met the inclusion criteria. A subsequent update of the systematic search identified five additional RCTs, which were also included. Figure 1 presents the outcomes of the study selection process.

Risk of bias

Most of the trials had a high risk of attrition bias. As some studies have not adequately reported methods for handling attrition (such as whether intention-to-treat analysis was conducted or missing data was imputed), it is challenging to determine the potential impact of attrition on the results. Consequently, the risk of attrition bias has been assessed as high (Fig. 2).

Study characteristics

Study design and intervention characteristics

The characteristics of the study included in the systematic review are presented in Table 1. This review included 17 articles and 22 trials in total, with 4 articles describing more than one study [12, 18, 25, 26]. Of these studies, ten were conducted in the UK [18, 26–30], six in the USA [25, 31–33], and three in the Netherlands [34–36], while the remaining trials were carried out in Singapore [37], Uruguay [38](and China [39]. Altogether, these 22 trials involved 33,352 participants, with sample sizes ranging from 106 [37] to 8,780 [28] individuals.

Among the 22 trials, five main interventions were utilized: displaying nutrition labels [12, 18, 25, 26, 29, 31, 36–38], increasing accessibility of products [12, 18, 26, 29], salience nudging [35], framing effect [27, 32], and priming nudging [39]. Twelve trials simulated online ordering at a restaurant [18, 25–27, 29, 30, 32, 38], and ten trials simulated online supermarket [28, 29, 31, 35, 36, 39] or grocery shopping [33, 34, 37]. For the assessment of outcomes related to improving dietary quality, twelve trials used the number of selected target foods as the primary outcome [26, 27, 29, 31–35, 37–39], while ten trials calculated the nutrients of the selected food.

Effectiveness of digital nudging

Nutritional labels

Nutrition labels provide essential nutritional facts and product attributes to consumers. A total of sixteen trials explored how different nutritional labels affect dietary quality, revealing both similarities and differences in their effectiveness. These trials can be categorized based on the type of nutritional labels used. Specific nutritional value labels were employed in four trials [12, 18, 28, 30]. Luick et al. and Tanasache et al. found that providing calorie labels reduced total calorie and energy in simulated online food delivery platforms. While Marty et al. [12, 18] found that energy labels improved the choice of high-energy foods in full-service restaurants, their impact was insignificant in fast-food settings. Four trials used image labels [25, 38]. Gugliucci et al. [38] and Musicus et al. [25] reported that image labels increased healthy food choices and reduced sodium content, whereas Parkin et al. [26] found no significant increase in vegetarian options. Two trials utilized nutrition scoring labels [29, 37], and Braga et al. gamified the nutrition scoring label by presenting it as many crowns, which led to an improvement in healthier food choice.

Increasing accessibility of products

Accessibility is enhanced by reducing barriers to specific behavioral choices and increasing their visibility, guiding individuals toward desired decisions more effortlessly. Seven trials explored the impact of increasing the accessibility of products on dietary quality. Marty et al. [12, 18, 29] explored the impact of increasing the accessibility of energy-dense foods on the selection of food energy content in a virtual supermarket, a virtual full-service restaurant, and a virtual fast-food restaurant. The results showed that it effectively reduced the caloric content of food choices. Parkin et al. [26] increased the proportion of vegetarian options on the menu and found that when the proportion of vegetarian options on the menu was 50%, it did not affect promoting vegetarian choices. However, when the proportion was adjusted to 75%, the likelihood of selecting vegetarian options increased. Bacona et al. [27] provided a separate vegetarian menu, where the proportion of vegetarian options was 100%, and found that it did not influence occasional vegetarians’ selection of plant-based dishes.

Salience nudging

Salience nudging refers to influencing people’s decisions by designing how information is presented to highlight key information and capture attention. Two trials explored the effects of salience nudging on dietary quality [27, 35]. Bacona et al. [27] observed an increase in the choice of plant-based options, but Hoenink et al. [35] found that only combining the price salience nudge with product discounts effectively promoted the choices of healthier products.

Framing effect

Framing effect refers to the psychological phenomenon where variations in the presentation format of information (framing) influence human decision-making and judgment. Two trials explored the effect of framing effect on dietary quality [27, 32]. On a descriptive menu, the name of the vegetarian dish was changed to “Fresh seasonal risotto primavera”, which improved the selection of vegetarian options [27]. Hua et al. [32] changed the description of the reduced main course and finally increased the choice of healthier products.

Priming nudging

The priming nudging refers to physical, verbal, or sensational cues in the environment that unconsciously drive decisions, and aims to activate individuals’ automatic mental associations. Chen et al. [39] activated participants’ awareness of the health benefits of sustainable diets and their concern for the environmental benefits of sustainable diets, respectively, through different word search tasks. They found that health-benefit priming and environment-benefit priming had a small effect size on increasing sustainable food choices, respectively.

Combined nudging

Eight trials explored the effectiveness of combined nudging strategies in improving dietary quality, grouped into three categories. Five trials employed a combination of nutritional labeling and availability modifications. Marty et al. [12, 18, 29] demonstrated that increasing the availability of lower-energy options combined with nutritional labels significantly reduced caloric selection, with availability changes showing stronger effects than labels alone. Jansen et al. [36] found that Nutri-Score labeling combined with healthy alternative recommendations significantly improved the nutritional quality of food choices. Similarly, Katare et al. [33] reported a substantial increase in plant-based food selections through the combination of carbon footprint labeling and product categorization. One trial [34] combined personalized digital just-in-time (JIT) nudging, demonstrating a significant increase in healthier food selection online.

Discussion

This review aimed to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of digital nudging strategies in improving dietary quality, based on evidence from randomized controlled trials. Our findings illustrate that digital nudging is beneficial in promoting healthier dietary choices, although its effectiveness may vary depending on the application context, strategy combinations, and characteristics of the target population.

Nutrition labels, through innovative user interface elements and presentation formats, significantly improved consumers’ dietary quality. However, its effectiveness is constrained by the position of labels [40], the type of labels [41], and the SES of the target population [42]. Unlike offline nudging strategies, digital nudging emphasizes the use of various user-interface design elements in digital environments to achieve the desired behavioral change [40]. Nutrition labels are placed near food images or displayed when the mouse hovers over images, reducing the effort required by users to access nutritional information [43]. However, it is worth noting that the type of labels and the context in which they are used have a significant impact [10]. Image labels can significantly improve consumers’ intake of nutrients such as sodium [25] and energy [38]. Nevertheless, the labels’ effectiveness will diminish if they are too common in daily life to attract people’s attention [26]. To address this, Gugliucci [38], Musicus [25], and Marty et al. [12, 18] designed new image labels that highlight unhealthy nutrient content to improve food purchasing behavior. Numeric labels objectively present the nutrient content, significantly improving dietary quality for individuals with higher SES, but have limited impact on food choices among those with lower SES, who may not prioritize or be aware of recommended nutrients intake levels [44–46]. Therefore, numeric labels may not have universal applicability. Braga et al. [31] introduced an innovative nutritional scoring system in an accessible format to convey the overall nutritional value of foods, and effectively improved dietary choices. These labels attract attention by presenting the nutritional value of foods more clearly and simply [47]. They address the limitations of both image and numeric labels, and the digital environment provides significant convenience for their innovation, indicating a promising future for their development.

Increasing product accessibility has been shown to influence food choices, but determining the appropriate proportion in different contexts remains a challenge. Studies by Marty et al. [12, 18] highlighted the role of healthy options accessibility as a key determinant in consumer food choices. Especially when faced with multiple options, consumers often gravitate toward options that are more accessible or more visible [48]. Although increasing the accessibility of healthy products has proven effective, studies have not determined the precise reduction of unhealthy products needed to achieve the desired effects [49]. Three trials implemented a 1:3 ratio (e.g., three healthy options for every unhealthy option offered), resulting in significant improvements [12, 26]. This ratio may balance the dominance of healthy choices with consumers’ freedom of choice to some extent, thus guiding consumers to make healthier choices without causing strong resistance. While one study only presented the intended products and did not achieve the expected results [27]. This suggests that while reducing access to unhealthy products may help steer consumers toward healthier products, excessive restrictions may lead to consumer resistance and even increase cravings for unhealthy products [50]. Therefore, how to reduce unhealthy products while maintaining consumer satisfaction and freedom of choice remains a complex issue.

Salience nudges, particularly when combined with other strategies, have shown potential in influencing food choices, but the effectiveness in virtual supermarkets and the ability to promote healthier choices remain areas for further exploration. A systematic review found that combining different types of nudges may more effectively influence food purchasing behavior, especially in environments like supermarkets, which are saturated with food-related cues [51]. For a long time, people have been accustomed to shopping in physical supermarkets. As virtual supermarkets gradually become a part of daily life, a question arises: does the shopping experience in virtual supermarkets resemble that of physical stores? Moreover, how can salience nudges be better utilized to help people choose healthier products? This remains an issue that warrants further exploration in future research.

Two trials demonstrated that through framing effect, emphasizing the sensory enjoyment and health benefits of food, people can be subtly guided to make healthier dietary choices. However, this way shows significant variation in effectiveness across different consumer groups and it tends to work better for consumers who do not frequently eat vegetarian options, while it may have a counterproductive effect on those who regularly consume vegetarian food [52]. Moreover, framing effect leads to different behavioral results through the different presentation of information [53, 54]. Vegetarians have more practice and selection experience for vegetarian food, and they are more likely to make rational decisions, so they are not easily influenced by framing effect [55]. Also, there is no evidence that framing effect improving diet quality in the long term.

In our included studies, only one trial explored and demonstrated the effectiveness of priming nudging to sustainable food choice. Unlike other nudge strategies, it actively engages users in learning and self-regulation, potentially leading to more sustainable behavior change. However, such interventions may require higher levels of user engagement, which poses implementation challenges in broader populations. Future studies should explore the integration of priming nudging and other nudge strategies to leverage the strengths of both approaches in diverse dietary contexts.

Notably, combined nudging strategies on improving dietary quality demonstrated a stronger effect compared to a single nudge strategy. Interventions targeting multiple behavioral determinants simultaneously—such as cognitive awareness through labeling and environmental restructuring through increased availability—may yield greater improvements in dietary outcomes compared to single-component nudges. Additionally, the study by de Vries et al. [34] provided a novel perspective by integrating just-in-time (JIT) nudging with personalized content and format. This demonstrated that personalizing nudges based on users’ food motivations (e.g., health vs. price) and information processing styles (text vs. image) can further enhance nudge effectiveness. Unlike static combinations (e.g., labeling and availability), JIT nudging illustrates the potential of temporally and cognitively tailored nudges to influence food choices in real time, underscoring the scalability and relevance of personalized digital nudging in dietary interventions.

The study has some limitations. Firstly, most of the included studies were short-term interventions focused on immediate or short-term outcomes related to dietary changes. Therefore, this systematic review cannot draw conclusions about the long-term effects of digital nudges. Secondly, the digital nudging strategies included in this review were limited in number and exhibited considerable heterogeneity. Future research should employ a broader range of digital nudging to assess further their effectiveness in improving dietary quality. Thirdly, articles that reported a disease status, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, were excluded, but some included studies did not describe the disease status of the participants, and we cannot be sure that all the included participants are free of a particular disease. In addition, while the control group in our analysis did not receive any form of nudging, this design limited the ability to compare digital nudging with other types of nudging strategies. Finally, the included studies measured and evaluated different nutritional outcomes while examining different nudging strategies (either alone or in combination with other strategies). The diversity of assessments of nutritional outcomes and the use of different measures limit the ability to compare outcomes or synthesize evidence.

Conclusions

In this review, we systematically collated the available evidence from RCTs on digital nudging and its impact on dietary quality. Overall, current evidence shows the benefits of digital nudging in improving dietary quality, especially with nutritional labels and increasing accessibility of products. However, it should not be applied as a one-size-fits-all nudge across different target groups and different contexts. Dietary behaviors are influenced by contextual factors, making it essential to adapt digital nudging strategies to specific settings, such as schools or workplaces. Future research should examine how these strategies perform in different environments and identify barriers to their effectiveness, such as individual preferences, cultural differences, or practical challenges in implementation. Addressing these issues can improve the development of digital nudging to support healthier dietary quality.
