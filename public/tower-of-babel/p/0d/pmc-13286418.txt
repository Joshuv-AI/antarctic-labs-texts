Vaccine effectiveness against COVID-19 in-hospital mortality in people living with HIV across SARS-CoV-2 variant waves: Analyses of data from the WHO Global Clinical Platform
Authors: Seth Inzaule, Ronaldo Silva, Nathan Ford, Soe Soe Thwin, Jassat Waasila, Alimuddin Zumla, Meg Doherty, Janet Diaz, Silvia Bertagnolio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There are limited data on the impact of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines on mortality among people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (PLHIV) across different severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 variants. We assessed the impact of the COVID-19 vaccine in reducing in-hospital mortality among PLHIV across various severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 variant waves. We analyzed individual-level data from 159,150 patients hospitalized with COVID-19 across 43 countries. Flexible parametric survival models were used to estimate the association between COVID-19 vaccination and in-hospital mortality, accounting for HIV status. Among HIV-negative individuals, COVID-19 vaccination reduced the risk of in-hospital mortality by 38 to 42% across all variant waves compared to their unvaccinated counterparts. In PLHIV, the protective effect of vaccination was less pronounced. Vaccinated PLHIV exhibited higher mortality risks compared to unvaccinated HIV-negative individuals: 41% higher during the Omicron wave (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.41, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.05–1.89) and no significant difference during the pre-Delta and Delta waves. However, vaccinated PLHIV consistently showed lower mortality risks than unvaccinated PLHIV, with mortality reductions of 60% (aHR 0.40, 95% CI 0.31–0.51) during the pre-Delta period, 50% during Delta (aHR 0.50, 95% CI 0.33–0.75), and 54% during Omicron (aHR 0.46, 95% CI 0.36–0.59). While COVID-19 vaccination significantly reduced inpatient mortality among PLHIV, mortality risks remained elevated compared to HIV-negative individuals, particularly during the Omicron variant wave. Overall, vaccination still offered substantial protection against death for PLHIV compared to unvaccinated PLHIV. These findings underscore the need for targeted vaccination strategies, including booster doses, to protect high-risk populations such as PLHIV, in alignment with World Health Organization recommendations.

1. Introduction

People living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (PLHIV) who contract coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) face a heightened risk of severe disease and mortality due to compromised immunity and a high prevalence of comorbidities, such as tuberculosis, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases, all of which are associated with severe or fatal COVID-19 outcomes.[1,2] Despite an overall decline in COVID-19 cases, over 1.91 million new cases and 33,089 deaths have still been reported globally since the start of 2025.[3] The ongoing risk of emerging, more transmissible variants underscores the need for continuous vigilance; in 2025, World Health Organization (WHO) declared the Omicron subvariant (JN.1) as a variant of interest due to its rapid spread, and currently 5 more Omicron sub-variants are closely being monitored.[4]

COVID-19 vaccines have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing severe disease and mortality worldwide. By December of 2023, global vaccination coverage reached 67% for primary doses and 32% for at least 1 booster dose.[4] While COVID-19 vaccines have been widely administered, underrepresentation of PLHIV in clinical trials[5,6] limits our understanding of vaccine responses in this population. PLHIV often exhibit comparatively reduced immune responses to vaccines due to immune dysfunction, as observed with other vaccines such as those for hepatitis A and B, Mpox, and Streptococcus pneumoniae.[7] HIV infection progressively depletes CD4 + T-cells, impairing both B-cell and cytotoxic T-cell function.[8,9] This compromised immune response can reduce the quantity, quality, and durability of protective antibodies generated through natural infection or vaccination.[10,11] While some studies have reported comparable immune responses to vaccines between PLHIV and HIV-negative individuals, others have shown reduced and short-lived immune responses among PLHIV.[12–14]

The emergence of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) variants, including Omicron subvariants such as JN.1, highlights the variability in vaccine effectiveness against severe disease and mortality. Previous studies have shown variability in the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in reducing severe disease and mortality against emerging variants. Some studies report reduced vaccine efficacy in preventing new infections during the Omicron wave compared to previous strains, with further variations observed among the Omicron sublineages.[15,16] However, data on vaccine impact specifically on mortality among PLHIV during different variant waves remains limited. This study aimed to address this gap by evaluating the impact of COVID-19 vaccination on in-hospital mortality among PLHIV compared to HIV-negative individuals across pre-Delta, Delta, and Omicron variant waves.

2. Methods

2.1. Data sources

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of anonymized, individual-level data from 159,050 hospitalized adult and pediatric COVID-19 patients across 43 countries who contributed vaccine and clinical data to the WHO Global Clinical Platform for COVID-19.[3] The countries contributing data were: Bangladesh, Belgium, Bolivia, Brazil, Canada, Colombia, Ecuador, Estonia, France, Gambia, Germany, Ghana, India, Indonesia, Iran, Israel, Italy, Japan, Jordan, Kuwait, Malawi, Malaysia, Mexico, Netherlands, New Zealand, Nigeria, Norway, Pakistan, Peru, Philippines, Poland, Portugal, Russia, Singapore, South Africa, Spain, Sudan, Switzerland, Turkey, Ukraine, United Kingdom, United States of America, and Zimbabwe (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). The list of facilities that contributed to this analysis is provided on the WHO Global Clinical Platform for COVID-19 webpage.[17]

The design and implementation of this platform have been described previously in detail. Briefly, the WHO Global Clinical Platform collects data through standardized case report forms (CRFs) or mappings from local databases. Participating countries submit data electronically, with local datasets standardized to the WHO CRF structure before transfer for centralized analysis. The CRFs include standardized epidemiological and clinical patient data collected at hospital admission, throughout hospitalization, and at discharge. Data collected encompass demographic characteristics, medical history, vital signs, laboratory results, clinical treatments, hospital course, co-infections, immunization status (including vaccine type and doses), information on reinfection, use of oxygen or mechanical ventilation, and outcomes (e.g., hospital discharge, death, or intensive care unit admission).

2.2. Statistical analysis

We used the Royston–Parmar flexible parametric survival regression model to evaluate the association between HIV infection and COVID-19 vaccination, and in-hospital mortality during 3 distinct variant periods: pre-Delta (primarily Alpha, Beta, Gamma), Delta, and Omicron waves. These variant waves were defined as follows: Pre-Delta: Up to August 1, 2021, Delta: August 2, 2021, to October 3, 2021, and Omicron: November 15, 2021, to October 1, 2023. These periods correspond to times when the respective variants predominated (> 90% of circulating cases) globally, based on country-specific genetic sequencing data from the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data, which had been expanded to include SARS-CoV-2 data.

The primary exposure was a composite variable combining HIV infection and COVID-19 vaccination status (≥ 1 dose of any SARS-CoV-2 vaccine), creating 4 groups: HIV-negative/unvaccinated (reference), HIV-negative/vaccinated, HIV-positive/unvaccinated, and HIV-positive/vaccinated.

We used flexible parametric survival models (Royston–Parmar models) with natural cubic splines (3 degrees of freedom) to model the baseline hazard, with cluster-robust standard errors to account for clustering by country (31 clusters). This approach relaxes the proportional hazards assumption while providing interpretable hazard ratios. Potential confounders were identified using directed acyclic graphs and clinical relevance, with age, sex, and severity at admission included as essential adjustment variables. Additional covariates were selected based on their prevalence (> 80% of cases), low collinearity with other variables (correlation coefficient < 0.8), and univariate association (P < .10) with in-hospital mortality or the primary exposure. Conditions considered for inclusion as covariates included: chronic comorbidities (asplenia, asthma, chronic cardiac, kidney, liver, pulmonary, or neurological disease); lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking); endocrine/metabolic conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, obesity); malignant neoplasms; and tuberculosis (current or prior infection). Final multivariable models retained covariates with P values < .05.

Secondary analysis stratified results by CD4 + T-cell count categories (< 200 cells/µL, 200–499 cells/µL, and ≥ 500 cells/µL) among PLHIV were assessed using Cox regression.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R version 3.6.3 (RStudio).

2.3. Ethical considerations

The analysis plan was submitted to the WHO Ethical Review Committee, which granted a waiver from ethical review due to the passive and anonymized nature of the clinical surveillance. Additional ethical approval was obtained from relevant institutional or national review boards where required.

3. Results

3.1. Patient Characteristics

Of 1126,192 hospitalized COVID-19 patients included in the global database, a total of 159, 150 with data on HIV status and COVID-19 vaccination were included in the study. Of these were included in the analysis, distributed across the pre-Delta (n = 86,276), Delta (n = 34,978), and Omicron (n = 37,896) variant waves. Among these, 10,154 patients (6.4%) were co-infected with HIV. The proportion of PLHIV was significantly higher during the Omicron wave (9.9%) compared to the Delta (7.2%, P < .001) and pre-Delta (4.5%, P < .001) waves.

Overall, 24% of hospitalized patients had received at least 1 dose of a COVID-19 vaccine. Vaccination rates were highest during the Omicron wave (39.9%, 15,132/37,896), followed by the Delta (34%, 11,880/34,978) and pre-Delta waves (13.3%, 11,465/86,276). PLHIV had lower vaccination rates (18.2%, 1848/10,154) compared to HIV-negative individuals (24.6%, 36,629/148,996, P < .001). This disparity was evident during the Delta (7.4% vs 35.8%, P < .001) and Omicron (28.7% vs 41.2%, P < .001) waves but not during the pre-Delta wave (12.7% vs 13.3%, P = .2969). There were, however, regional differences in vaccination across variant waves and by HIV status (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1).

Data on vaccine schedules were available in only 39% of the vaccinated patients, the majority of whom (11,759/15,126) had only 1 vaccine dose. Among those with data on vaccine doses, PLHIV (28.5%, 247/867) were more likely to have had > 1 vaccine dose than HIV-negative individuals (21.9%, 3120/14,259) (P < .001). Data on the type of vaccine were available for 21.3% of the vaccinated patients, the majority having received either the viral vector Janssen vaccine (38.7%, 3170/8183) or the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) Pfizer vaccine (31.7%, 2516/8183). Data on time since last vaccination were available for only 43% of vaccinated PLHIV and 15.3% of vaccinated HIV-negative individuals.

3.2. In-hospital mortality

Overall, 17.1% (27,163/159,050) of hospitalized patients died during their hospital stay. Mortality rates were highest among unvaccinated PLHIV (25.2%, 2095/8306), followed by unvaccinated HIV-negative individuals (17.8%, 20,033/112,367), vaccinated PLHIV (17.7%, 327/1848), and vaccinated HIV-negative individuals (12.9%, 4714/36,629).

In-hospital mortality rates varied by both COVID-19 variant and HIV status (Table 1). Pre-Delta Wave: Unvaccinated PLHIV had the highest mortality rate (29.9%), followed by vaccinated PLHIV (21.9%), unvaccinated HIV-negative individuals (20%), and vaccinated HIV-negative individuals (16.1%). Delta Wave: Mortality rates were 25.2% (unvaccinated PLHIV), 18.1% (vaccinated PLHIV), 19.3% (unvaccinated HIV-negative), and 15.4% (vaccinated HIV-negative). Omicron Wave: Rates decreased across all groups: 19.1% (unvaccinated PLHIV), 15.7% (vaccinated PLHIV), 8.5% (unvaccinated HIV-negative), and 8.2% (vaccinated HIV-negative). Tables S2a,S2b,S2c,S3a,S3b,S3c,S4a,S4b and S4c, Supplemental Digital Content 2 and Figures S1,S2 and S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3 show early (< 7 days) vs late deaths (> 7 days) across the different variants. Unvaccinated PLHIV had a high rate of early deaths vs later deaths (22.1% vs 10.1%, 19.1% vs 7.6%, 14.1% vs 5.8%) compared to vaccinated PLHIV (9.1% vs 14.1 %, 9.6% vs 9.4%, 8.2% vs 7.9%), unvaccinated HIV-negative individuals (8.8% vs 12.3%, 11.35% vs 9.0%, 5.2% vs 3.5%), and vaccinated HIV-negative individuals (6% vs 10.8%, 5.7% vs 10.3%, 3.9% vs 4.5%) during pre-Delta, Delta, and Omicron variant waves, respectively.

3.3. Impact of vaccination on mortality risk

Vaccination significantly reduced in-hospital mortality for HIV-negative individuals compared to their unvaccinated counterparts, with adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) of: Pre-Delta Wave: 0.60 (95% CI 0.57–0.64, 40% reduction); Delta Wave: 0.62 (95% CI 0.58–0.66, 38% reduction); Omicron Wave: 0.58 (95% CI 0.53–0.64, 42% reduction) (Fig. 1).

Among PLHIV, vaccination conferred mortality reductions compared to unvaccinated PLHIV: Pre-Delta Wave: aHR 0.40 (95% CI 0.31–0.51, 60% reduction); Delta Wave: aHR 0.50 (95% CI 0.33–0.75, 50% reduction); Omicron Wave: aHR 0.46 (95%CI 0.36–0.59, 54% reduction) (Table 2).

However, vaccinated PLHIV faced an elevated mortality risk compared to unvaccinated HIV-negative individuals during the Omicron (aHR 1.44, 95% CI 1.08–1.93) wave, with no significant difference during the Pre-Delta (aHR 1.38, 95% CI 0.97–1.96) and Delta waves (aHR 0.70, 95% CI 0.47–1.04).

Unvaccinated PLHIV consistently exhibited substantially higher mortality risk than unvaccinated HIV-negative individuals across all variant waves: Pre-Delta Wave: aHR 1.97 (95% CI 1.81–2.16, 97% increase); Delta Wave: aHR 2.46 (95% CI 2.12–2.85, 146% increase); Omicron Wave: aHR 2.43 (95% CI 2.10–2.81, 143% increase).

In a sensitivity analysis, we assessed for: i) interaction between HIV infection and vaccination status with in-hospital mortality; ii) impact of unknown HIV status on in-hospital mortality; and iii) impact of COVID-19 vaccination on in-hospital deaths in the pediatric population. In the model with the interaction term, there was a trend towards varying vaccine effects by HIV status, but the interaction terms were not significant across all variant waves (Figs. Mortality reduction in vaccinated PLHIV was 28% compared with 40% among vaccinated HIV-negative individuals during the Pre-Delta waves, 57% vs 40% during Delta, and 42% vs 40% during Omicron waves.

Vaccinated individuals with unknown HIV status had, however, a higher but nonsignificant risk of in-hospital death during Pre-Delta compared to unvaccinated HIV-negative individuals, and a lower but nonsignificant risk during Delta and Omicron waves (Fig. S5, Supplemental Digital Content 6). The magnitude and direction of the hazard ratios, however, remained unaffected for both HIV-negative individuals and PLHIV when individuals with unknown HIV status were included in the model.

Unvaccinated PLHIV < 18 years had an increased risk of in-hospital mortality (aHR 6.26, 95% CI 2.99–13.1), while vaccinated HIV-negative pediatric individuals had a lower but nonsignificant risk of in-hospital death compared to unvaccinated HIV-negative pediatric individuals (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

3.4. Mortality by CD4 + T-Cell count

4. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that the protective effect of COVID-19 vaccination against in-hospital mortality varied between PLHIV and HIV-negative individuals across SARS-CoV-2 variant waves. While vaccination significantly reduced mortality risk in both groups, the magnitude of protection was less pronounced among PLHIV, particularly those with advanced immunosuppression. Nonetheless, vaccination remained a crucial intervention, with vaccinated PLHIV consistently experiencing a 50 to 60% lower risk of death compared to their unvaccinated counterparts across all variant waves. Vaccination consistently conferred substantial survival benefits for PLHIV, even in those with low CD4 counts.

Implications of lower vaccine effectiveness in PLHIV

Our findings align with prior studies reporting reduced vaccine effectiveness among PLHIV compared to HIV-negative individuals. Clinical trials and immunological studies have consistently demonstrated that PLHIV, especially those with CD4 counts below 200 cells/μL or high comorbidity burdens, exhibit weaker immune responses to COVID-19 vaccines. A South African study, for example, observed reduced mortality protection from the Janssen vaccine in PLHIV healthcare workers compared to HIV-negative controls.[14] Similarly, a systematic review involving 33 studies confirmed that immune responses to COVID-19 vaccines were generally lower among PLHIV, even after 2 doses.[18]

The suboptimal vaccine response in PLHIV is likely due to persistent immune dysfunction, including immune activation, immunosenescence, and a reduced capacity to mount polyfunctional T-cell responses.[10,18–20] These factors, coupled with a higher prevalence of comorbidities in this population, contribute to the increased risk of severe outcomes observed in our study, particularly in those with low CD4 counts.

Variability in vaccine effectiveness across variant waves

Vaccine effectiveness was also influenced by the dominant SARS-CoV-2 variant. Our findings indicate lower protection against in-hospital mortality during the Omicron wave compared to Delta, reflecting the greater antigenic divergence of Omicron from the original vaccine strains. Our findings align with the growing broader literature showing decreased efficacy against Omicron subvariants compared to earlier strains like Delta.[15,16,21,22] This highlights the role of immune imprinting and the pressing need for adaptable vaccine designs to keep pace with viral evolution. Omicron extensive mutations, particularly in the receptor-binding domain of the spike protein, enhance its immune evasion capabilities and reduce neutralizing antibody responses. Emerging variants, such as BA.2.86 and JN.1, have demonstrated significant immune escape capabilities due to mutations in the receptor-binding domain of the spike protein.[15,16,21,22] This adds complexity to vaccine protection, particularly in populations with already compromised immune systems, like PLHIV.

Despite these challenges, vaccination remained beneficial for PLHIV across all waves. Booster doses have been shown to enhance immune responses, even against Omicron sublineages, and are critical for maintaining vaccine efficacy in this population. Our findings underscore the importance of booster strategies, especially for PLHIV with low CD4 counts, those with opportunistic infections, or individuals not on effective antiretroviral therapy.

Public health implications

The higher mortality risk observed among unvaccinated PLHIV, even compared to vaccinated HIV-negative individuals, emphasizes the need for equitable vaccine access and targeted public health strategies. Countries should prioritize vaccination and booster campaigns for PLHIV, particularly in resource-limited settings. Optimized vaccine schedules and the use of high-efficacy platforms, such as mRNA vaccines, could further enhance protection for this vulnerable group.[23–27] Evolving data continue to highlight that high comorbidity burdens in PLHIV exacerbate the challenges in vaccine-induced protection. Ongoing research suggests tailoring vaccination schedules and integrating other interventions, like monoclonal antibodies and antivirals, for better outcomes. The importance of booster doses has been emphasized, with newer vaccines designed to tackle circulating Omicron subvariants. Evidence suggests a short-lived peak in effectiveness, which diminishes over months. This underscores the necessity of regular boosters, especially for PLHIV with low CD4 counts.

Future Directions

The newer monovalent COVID-19 vaccines targeting the XBB.1.5 variant have shown promising results. Early data suggest they provide 54% effectiveness against symptomatic infection in immunocompetent adults, with stronger responses shortly after booster administration.[28] Although specific data for PLHIV is limited, these findings reinforce the potential benefits of updated vaccine formulations in vulnerable populations, including those with HIV. Further research is needed to explore the immunological mechanisms underlying the observed differences in vaccine effectiveness across SARS-CoV-2 variants. Studies should also investigate the impact of vaccine type, dosing intervals, and prior SARS-CoV-2 infections on outcomes in PLHIV. Tailored vaccination schedules, including dose optimization and extended intervals between primary and booster doses, should be evaluated to maximize vaccine efficacy in PLHIV, particularly those with advanced immunosuppression.

4.1. Limitations of the study

Our study was limited by several factors. First, a significant proportion of participants lacked data on vaccination or HIV status, which could have potentially introduced bias if the missing data were not random. However, sensitivity analyses that included participants with unknown HIV status showed a similar trend as that of PLHIV on the impact of vaccine on in-hospital mortality. Hence, it’s likely that potential bias due to missing data was likely minimal. Moreover, a significant proportion of data was collected at the time when vaccination coverage was low, especially during the pre-delta period, further reducing the potential influence of missing vaccination data. We, however, were also unable to assess the impact of vaccination among pediatric PLHIV due to sparse vaccine data in this population. This limitation is, however, consistent with global vaccination patterns, where children were largely deprioritized for immunization owing to the comparatively lower burden of severe COVID-19 disease in this age group. Further studies are therefore needed to evaluate the impact of COVID-19 vaccination among children living with HIV.

Second, we were unable to evaluate the influence of vaccine type, number of doses, timing of vaccination, or prior SARS-CoV-2 infection on vaccine effectiveness among PLHIV. Previous studies have reported stronger humoral immune responses and a lower rate of breakthrough infection among PLHIV vaccinated with mRNA vaccines compared to those vaccinated with inactivated or adenovirus vector-based vaccines.[23,27] Similarly, boosted doses have been associated with improved immune responses among PLHIV[27,29] while longer duration between prime and booster doses has also been linked to enhanced immunogenicity.[24] Residual confounding related to healthcare access, treatment practices, and socioeconomic differences may therefore persist. Additional research is therefore required to determine whether vaccine type and booster dosing strategies reduce the differential effect observed between PLHIV and the HIV-negative population.

Third, our analysis was restricted to hospitalized patients and therefore may not fully reflect the broader impact of vaccination in preventing SARS-CoV-2 infections and milder disease in the community. Moreover, some countries contributed data derived from heterogeneous sources including national registries, convenience samples of hospitals, or sentinel clinics. This coupled with differences in hospitalization criteria and access and quality of health care by country may result in potential reporting biases. Moreover, most data were from Africa (in particular South Africa), which potentially limits the generalizability of these findings to other contexts. Additional community-based studies are needed to determine whether COVID-19 effectiveness in preventing hospitalization and severe disease differs between PLHIV and HIV-negative populations in the community.

Lastly, while we used variant-specific periods informed by genomic surveillance data, the dynamic nature of the pandemic and potential misclassification of variants could have influenced our findings. In particular, the inability to distinguish between specific SARS-CoV-2 variants circulating during the pre-Delta period limited more granular insights on their individual effects on clinical outcomes. Further studies incorporating patient-level genomic data are needed to better characterize the relationship between specific variants, vaccination, and COVID-19 outcomes among PLHIV.

5. Conclusions

Our study represents one of the largest to date assessing COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness against mortality among PLHIV. Our findings highlight significant differences in vaccine protection between PLHIV and HIV-negative individuals across SARS-CoV-2 variants. While vaccination reduced mortality risk for PLHIV, the magnitude of protection was lower compared to HIV-negative individuals. These findings underscore the need for tailored strategies to optimize vaccine impact among PLHIV, including booster doses and additional interventions.
