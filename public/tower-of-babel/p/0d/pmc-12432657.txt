Shared genetics of lung cancer and type 2 diabetes: a large-scale genome-wide cross-trait analysis
Authors: Ze Wang, Hongsheng Deng, Enting Ji, Yao Lu, Weixiang Lu, Jianxing He, Wenlong Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer (LCA) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) are major global health burdens, with accumulating evidence suggesting a genomic link. This study systematically investigates the shared genetic architecture between LCA subtypes [small cell lung cancer (SCLC), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD)] and T2D using large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS).MethodsWe conducted genome-wide genetic correlation analyses using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) and local genetic correlation analyses via heritability estimation from summary statistics (HESS). Pleiotropic variants were identified using the pleiotropic analysis under composite null hypothesis (PLACO), and functional annotation was performed with functional mapping and annotation of genetic associations (FUMA). Pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were carried out using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and GO, and tissue-specific enrichment patterns were evaluated with LDSC for the specific expression of genes analysis (LDSC-SEG).ResultsOur analysis revealed significant positive genetic correlations between T2D and LCA subtypes, particularly in SCLC and LUSC. Genome-wide genetic correlation analyses indicated the strongest correlation between LCA and T2D. Local genetic correlation analysis identified several genomic regions with suggestive significance. A total of 50 independent genomic risk loci were identified as pleiotropic loci, involving 34 unique chromosomal regions. Notably, the 9p21.3 region (CDKN2B-AS1) was identified in all pairwise trait analyses. Functional annotation highlighted deleterious variants in genes such as PROX1-AS1, BPTF, APOE, APOC1, TOMM40, TCF7L2, and PVRL2. Pathway analyses indicated significant enrichment in chromatin organization and metabolic regulation.ConclusionsThis study provides robust evidence of a shared genetic architecture between LCA subtypes and T2D, highlighting potential therapeutic targets. Future research should focus on validating these findings in larger cohorts and exploring the functional roles of the identified genes in the co-occurrence of these conditions.Keywords: Lung cancer (LCA), type 2 diabetes (T2D), genome-wide cross-trait analysis

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study represents the first comprehensive genome-wide cross-trait analysis investigating the shared genetic architecture between lung cancer (LCA) subtypes and type 2 diabetes (T2D), revealing significant genetic correlations and shared biological pathways.

What is known and what is new?

• Epidemiological studies have shown associations between T2D and increased LCA risk, but the genetic mechanisms underlying this relationship were not well understood.

• This study identifies significant positive genetic correlations between T2D and LCA subtypes (particularly small cell lung cancer and lung squamous cell carcinoma), 50 independent pleiotropic loci, and shared biological pathways involving chromatin organization and metabolic regulation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings suggest potential shared therapeutic targets and indicate that T2D patients with specific genetic variants may benefit from enhanced LCA surveillance. The identification of shared biological pathways provides new directions for developing targeted treatments for patients with concurrent conditions.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LCA) represents the leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, resulting in approximately 1.8 million deaths annually (1). It comprises three major subtypes, small cell lung cancer (SCLC), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). Type 2 diabetes (T2D), a complex metabolic disorder affecting more than 463 million adults globally, is characterized by hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and progressive β-cell dysfunction (2). Accumulating epidemiological evidence supports a significant association between LCA and T2D, including the major subtypes SCLC, LUSC and LUAD (3-5). A prospective study of two America cohorts demonstrated that T2D was associated with a 27% increased risk of LCA [hazard ratio (HR) =1.27; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.12 to 1.45] (6). The finding was further substantiated by analysis of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data, which revealed an even stronger association, with T2D conferring a threefold increased risk of LCA [odds ratio (OR) =3.032; 95% CI: 1.015–9.054] (7). The consistent demonstration of this relationship across transcriptome data suggests potential shared genetic mechanisms contributing to the co-occurrence of T2D and LCA, warranting investigation into subtype-specific biological mechanisms (7).

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 400 genetic loci associated with T2D and more than 45 risk loci for LCA, revealing genetic factors influencing disease development (8,9). These large-scale genetic discoveries enable the utilization of genome-wide cross-trait analysis—a compiled analytical strategy that can determine shared and distinct genetic architecture to provide novel insight into mechanism of disease development. Such analysis encompasses multiple approaches: genetic correlation analysis to estimate overall and local genetic correlation, cross-trait meta-analysis to identify shared loci, and functional enrichment analysis to elucidate possible biological pathways.

Despite the fact that there have been established epidemiologic associations between LCA subtypes and T2D, these advanced statistical genetic approaches have yet to be systematically applied to examine their genetic relationships. While several studies have explored genetic associations, there is a lack of comprehensive cross-trait analyses incorporating multiple analytical methods and considering different LCA subtypes (10-12). Furthermore, the extent of genetic overlap between these conditions, its potential subtype-specific patterns, and the role of tissue-specific gene expression and pathway enrichment in mediating these genetic associations remain largely unexplored.

In this comprehensive genetic analysis, leveraging the largest available GWAS summary statistics, we performed systematic cross-trait analyses between T2D and LCA subtypes (LUAD, LUSC, and SCLC) through multiple complementary statistical genetic approaches to investigate shared genetic architecture at various biological levels. Specifically, we first conducted genome-wide genetic correlation analyses using linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression (LDSC) to assess overall genetic overlap, followed by local genetic correlation analysis via heritability estimation from summary statistics (HESS) to identify regions harboring shared genetic effects (13,14). We then employed the pleiotropic analysis under composite null hypothesis (PLACO) to detect specific pleiotropic variants and loci between T2D and each LCA subtype (15). Based on these findings, we performed extensive pathway analyses using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) databases to characterize the biological mechanisms underlying the shared genetic components, supplemented by LDSC for the specific expression of genes analysis (LDSC-SEG) to evaluate tissue-specific enrichment patterns (16,17). These multi-layered analyses provide a systematic framework for understanding the genetic relationship between T2D and LCA subtypes, as illustrated in Figure 1. We present this article in accordance with the STREGA reporting checklist (18) (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2025-184/rc).

Methods

GWAS summary statistics

In the study, summary data from the hitherto largest GWAS of LCA and T2D were leveraged for genetic analyses, both exclusively focused on individuals of European ancestry. The detailed information for the GWAS data of both traits is shown in Table S1.

GWAS summary statistics of LCA (LCA, SCLC, LUSC, and LUAD, corresponding to GCST004748, GCST004746, GCST004750, and GCST004744) were downloaded from the NHGRI-EBI GWAS catalog (19). A total of 85,716 European individuals from the International Lung Cancer Consortium (ILCCO) were analyzed (20). ILCCO is an international research group that established and implemented LCA research from different geographical areas, were used as epidemiological summary-level data. Subgroup analyses were stratified according to the pathology classification of SCLC and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Specifically, LCA was divided into LUAD (3,442 cases, 14,894 controls) and LUSC (3,275 cases, 15,038 controls). Details of each GWAS were described in Table S1.

GWAS summary statistics of T2D were obtained from the DIAbetes Genetics Replication and Meta-analysis (DIAGRAM) consortium (https://diagram-consortium.org/downloads.html), which represents the most comprehensive T2D GWAS meta-analysis data, encompassing 32 European cohorts of ~74K cases and ~824K controls (21). Individuals with diabetes were identified using multiple diagnostic criteria either independently or in combination, including World Health Organization (WHO) 1999 standards, glycosylated hemoglobin, type A1C (HbA1C) ≥6.5%, random blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, diabetes medication situation, medical record documentation, International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes and self-report diagnosis (22). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Statistical analyses

Global and local genetic correlation

To evaluate the genetic relationship between LCA subtypes and T2D, we implemented a two-tiered correlation analysis approach. First, we employed cross-trait LDSC to assess genome-wide genetic correlations between each LCA subtypes and T2D (13). The final estimates ranged from −1 to 1, with −1 indicating a perfect negative genetic correlation and 1 indicating a perfect positive genetic correlation. We used pre-computed LD scores obtained from ~1.2 million common single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in European ancestry represented in the HapMap3 reference panel, which were commonly believed to be of high imputation quality. A Bonferroni-corrected P value threshold of 0.05/4 was used to represent statistical significance.

Subsequently, recognizing that global genetic correlations might be masked by counterbalancing local genetic effects, we conducted fine-scale analysis using HESS to quantify local genetic correlations across the genome (14). For this analysis, the summation of these local genetic correlations across all LD blocks provided an alternative measure of global genetic correlation for each LCA subtype-T2D pair. The genome was segmented into 1,703 independents LD blocks in LCA-T2D, LUSC-T2D, and LUAD-T2D analyses; while 1,700 LD blocks in SCLC-T2D analysis. Statistical significance for local genetic correlations was determined using Bonferroni-corrected thresholds: P<0.05/1,703 for LCA-T2D, LUSC-T2D, and LUAD-T2D analyses, and P<0.05/1,700 for SCLC-T2D analysis.

Pleiotropic analysis to dissect genetic overlap

For genetic overlap observed in trait pairs, we performed the PLACO to investigate pleiotropic effects at the variant level. This analytical approach aims to identify genetic variants that influence the risk of two diseases, offering greater statistical power than typically methods (15). It detects pleiotropic loci by testing each variant under a composite null hypothesis, which posits that a variant is either unassociated with both traits or associated with only one of them. Prior to analysis, we conducted quality control by excluding SNVs showing allele inconsistencies and those with Z2 values exceeding 80 in the GWAS datasets. We identified significant pleiotropic variants using a genome-wide significance threshold of P<5×10−8.

Genomic loci characterization and functional annotation

Genomic loci identification and functional annotation to characterize significant pleiotropic SNVs identified through PLACO analysis, we implemented functional mapping and annotation of genetic associations (FUMA) to delineate independent variants, define genomic risk loci, and elucidate their functional implications. The analysis incorporated LD information derived from the 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 European reference panel (23). Independent SNVs were demarcated using an r2 threshold of 0.60, whereas lead SNVs were ascertained using an r2 threshold of 0.10 within a 1 Mb window. Adjacent genomic risk loci were merged when the physical distance between lead SNVs was less than 250 kb.

Functional annotation was executed utilizing multiple analytical approaches, including ANNOVAR for variant classification, Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD) scoring, and RegulomeDB assessment, all implemented through FUMA. SNVs exhibiting CADD scores exceeding 12.37 were designated as potentially deleterious. Furthermore, SNVs demonstrating genome-wide significance (P<5×10−8) in individual trait GWAS were subjected to FUMA annotation for comparative assessment.

Gene-based analyses

We performed gene-level and gene-set analyses utilizing MAGMA v1.6, integrated within the FUMA framework, based on GWAS summary statistics (24). The gene-level analysis aggregated SNV-level statistics to evaluate the cumulative association of variants within each gene with the phenotype of interest. Gene-set analysis aggregated individual genes into functional clusters to elucidate the involvement of specific biological pathways in the genetic architecture of the phenotype. Tissue-specific expression patterns were investigated using Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) data through FUMA implementation.

Pathway enrichment analyses

The KEGG and GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the OmicShare platform (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/home/report/koenrich.html). To systematically investigate the biological functions of differentially expressed genes. GO functional enrichment analysis included three domains: biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. KEGG was employed to identify enriched biological pathways associated with differential RNA-binding protein (RBP) expression.

Histone modification enrichment analysis

To investigate tissue and cell-type-specific enrichment patterns, we employed LDSC-SEG methodology to assess per-SNV heritability enrichment (16). The analysis utilized pre-computed annotations derived from the Roadmap Epigenomics Project, encompassing multiple chromatin marks (DNase hypersensitivity, H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K9ac, and H3K36me3) (25). The 489 cell-type-specific annotations were stratified into 9 primary categories (adipose, central nervous system, digestive system, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal and connective tissue, immune and blood, liver, pancreas, and others). Statistical significance was determined using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P<0.05/489).

Results

Shared genetic architecture and correlations between LCA subtypes and T2D

The sample size for each GWAS ranged from 663,053 to 898,130 (Tables S1,S2). We found distinct patterns of shared genetic architecture between LCA subtypes (LCA, SCLC, LUSC, and LUAD) and T2D through SNV-heritability enrichment analysis. Specifically, LCA subtypes showed more concentrated genetic effects compared to T2D, with approximately 60% of their total SNV-heritability explained by just 20% of the genome regions (Figure 2A). This concentrated pattern was particularly evident in SCLC and LUSC, suggesting these subtypes might share stronger genetic components with T2D in specific genomic regions. In contrast, T2D showed a more dispersed genetic architecture, requiring approximately 40% of the genome to account for the same proportion of heritability.

Global genetic correlation analysis using both LDSC and HESS revealed significantly positive correlations between LCA subtypes (LCA, SCLC, and LUSC) and T2D. Notably, the genetic correlation between LCA and T2D was the strongest, followed by SCLC and LUSC (Figure 2B, Table S3).

Motivated by these findings, the local genetic correlation was further explored using HESS. Unfortunately, no significant local genetic correlation was identified between LCA subtypes and T2D after correcting for multiple testing (P<0.05/1,703 for LCA-T2D and LUSC-T2D; P<0.05/1,700 for SCLC-T2D). Suggestive significance (P<0.05) was observed for LCA-T2D at eight genomic regions, for SCLC-T2D at five genomic regions and LUSC-T2D at six genomic regions (Table S4; Figure S1).

Shared loci between LCA subtypes and T2D

A total of 976 SNVs were identified as potential pleiotropic variants by PLACO in 3 trait pairs (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tlcr-2025-184-1.xlsx). FUMA identified 50 independent genomic risk loci as pleiotropic loci, involving 34 unique chromosomal regions, with significant enrichment in pancreatic tissue (Figure 3; Tables S5,S6; Figures S2-S4). Notably, one pleiotropic region [9p21.3, mapped gene: CDKN2B-AS1 (OMIM: 613149)] was identified in all pairwise traits, suggesting mutual pleiotropic effects of this locus across these traits. The top three identified SNVs, predicted to be deleterious variants with CADD scores lager than 12.37, were linked to both LCA subtypes and T2D (Table S6). Specifically, rs79687284 and rs340874, both located at 1q32.3 within the ncRNA intronic region of PROX1-AS1:PROX1, exhibited CADD scores of 17.72 and 13.3, respectively. These variants suggest a strong likelihood of disrupting the splicing or expression of PROX1-AS1, potentially affecting biological pathways shared by LCA and its subtype SCLC, as well as T2D. Another variant, rs1976052, located at 17q24.2 in an intronic region of BPTF, had a CADD score of 14.1, indicating a potential impact on BPTF function, which is involved in transcription regulation and chromatin remodeling. These alterations in gene function could influence processes such as apoptosis, immune escape, and insulin sensitivity, contributing to the pathogenesis of both LCA, SCLC, and T2D (26,27).

In addition, the top SNVs in 50 FUMA-annotated pleiotropic loci revealed complex genetic relationship between LCA subtypes and T2D (Table S7). Among these, 19 of 50 top SNVs (38%) showed concordant effects, where risk alleles were associated with either increased or decreased risk for both LCA subtypes and T2D. In contrast, 31 of 50 top SNVs (62%) showed discordant effects, where risk alleles had opposite effects on LCA subtypes and T2D, suggesting these diseases may share genetic components but through distinct biological mechanisms.

Prioritization of candidate pleiotropic genes

MAGMA gene-based analysis of 36 candidate pleiotropic genes located within or overlapping the 34 pleiotropic loci identified 75 significant pleiotropic genes, of which 28 genes were detected across multiple trait pairs (Table S8). These 75 pleiotropic genes exhibited differential expression patterns across various tissues, including pancreas, brain, and prostate (Figures S5,S6). Notably, eight genes were consistently identified across all trait pairs, with 5 of them (APOE, APOC1, TOMM40, TCF7L2, PVRL2) predicted to have deleterious effects (CADD scores >12.37). Further analysis revealed APOC1 (rs56131196) in region 19q13.32, which plays a crucial role in lipid metabolism and immune regulation, and serves as a prognostic biomarker both in LCA and T2D, suggesting its involvement in the metabolic-immune axis underlying the comorbidity of LCA and T2D (28-30).

Chromatin and cellular mechanisms shared between LCA subtypes and T2D

Gene set enrichment analysis identified significant pathway enrichments in both KEGG pathways and GO terms based on MAGMA results. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant overlap between metabolic and cancer-related pathways (Figure 4A,4B, Tables S9,S10). Notably, pathways linking cancer and glucose metabolism were prominently enriched, including Maturity onset diabetes of the young (P=5.66×10−3) and pathways in cancer (6 genes, P=2.11×10−2). The analysis also highlighted cellular mechanisms common to both conditions, such as cellular senescence (3 genes, P=2.79×10−2) and endocrine resistance (3 genes, P=7.78×10−3). Of particular relevance, hepatocellular carcinoma pathway (3 genes, P=3.06×10−2) was enriched, suggesting common mechanisms between different cancer types and metabolic disorders.

GO analysis revealed 20 significantly enriched terms (P<0.05), predominantly involving chromatin organization and metabolic regulation (Figure 4C, Table S11). The most significantly enriched GO terms included structural constituent of chromatin (GO:0030527, P=9.12×10−7), protein-DNA complex (GO:0032993, P=9.78×10−7), and pancreas development (GO:0031016, P=8.20×10−6). Notably, multiple chromatin-related process genes were enriched, including nucleosome assembly (GO:0006334), chromatin assembly (GO:0031497), and chromatin remodeling (GO:0006338).

The convergence of these pathways, particularly in chromatin organization and metabolic regulation, suggests shared molecular mechanisms between LCA subtypes and T2D. The enrichment of both endocrine resistance and cellular senescence pathways further supports the interconnected nature of these conditions, potentially through dysregulation of chromatin-mediated gene expression and cellular metabolic processes.

Shared chromatin regulation in LCA subtypes and T2D

Motivated by the significant enrichment of chromatin-related processes identified in MAGMA-based gene-set analysis, we further investigated the tissue/cell-type-specific enrichment patterns in histone modifications between LCA subtypes and T2D using LDSC-SEG (Tables S12,S13). Despite none of the tissue/cell-type-specific enrichment withstood multiple correction (P<0.05/486) for LCA subtypes, several suggestive signals (P<0.05) provided valuable insights into potential regulatory mechanisms. Among these, LCA showed the most extensive enrichment in immune-related tissues, particularly in T cells with H3K4me1 modifications. SCLC demonstrated enrichment in peripheral blood cells with H3K36me3 modifications and various tissue-specific H3K4me patterns. LUSC exhibited significant enrichment in fetal thymus H3K36me3 and brain-specific H3K4me3 modifications.

For T2D, the analysis revealed strong enrichment in pancreatic islets with multiple histone modifications, including H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K4me1, and H3K9ac. Notably, there was overlapping enrichment of H3K4me modifications between LCA subtypes and T2D, particularly in metabolically active tissues. Both conditions showed significant enrichment in H3K4me3 modifications across multiple tissues, suggesting a shared epigenetic regulatory mechanism.

The convergence of histone modifications, especially H3K4me patterns, in both conditions suggests that chromatin-mediated gene regulation may serve as a molecular bridge between LCA subtypes and T2D pathogenesis.

Discussion

In this comprehensive genome-wide cross-trait analysis, we provide the first systematic investigation of shared genetic architecture between LCA subtypes and T2D. Our findings reveal significant positive genetic correlations, particularly between LCA, SCLC, and LUSC with T2D. Further comprehensive analyses highlighted the pleiotropic genetic variants, loci, and genes, biological pathways and a potential genetic basis associated with chromatin regulation. These results provide novel genetic molecular insights into the epidemiological association between these conditions and suggest potential therapeutic implications.

The genetic correlation findings from our study are largely consistent with those from conventional epidemiological research, and also offer new perspectives in several areas. Our findings, consistent with previous analyses demonstrating increased LCA risk among T2D patients in observational study, provide the first comprehensive assessment of positive genetic correlations between T2D and LCA subtypes, leveraging summary statistics from the largest available gene database-GWAS (3). While previous study has reported associations between T2D and overall LCA risk, our subtype-specific analysis reveals a more nuanced picture of these relationships (3). The enhanced statistical power from our enlarged sample sizes enables more precise estimation of genetic correlations, addressing limitations of previous studies that lacked sufficient power to detect subtype-specific associations.

Notably, in accordance with limited prior studies specifically investigating the relationships between glucose metabolism and SCLC, our analysis revealed the strongest genetic correlation with this subtype, aligning with epidemiological evidence showing particularly elevated risk associations between T2D and neuroendocrine-derived tumors (31). This finding suggests potential shared pathogenic pathways between T2D and SCLC, possibly involving dysregulation of glucose metabolism and neuroendocrine signaling networks (32). The identification of this stronger correlation with SCLC compared to other subtypes not only provides new directions for investigating the biological mechanisms underlying the T2D-LCA relationship but also has potential implications for both risk stratification and therapeutic strategies in patients with concurrent T2D and LCA, particularly given SCLC’s poor prognosis among LCAs.

In addition to the genetic correlations identified between T2D and LCA subtypes, results from cross-trait meta-analysis suggest that the observational link may largely be explained by potential pleiotropic variants affecting both traits independently. Here we highlight seven shared genetic loci with interesting findings. The first of these is PROX1-AS1, which encodes a long non-coding RNA that has been implicated in both glucose metabolism regulation and tumor progression (26,33). Studies have shown that PROX1-AS1 influences insulin sensitivity in metabolic tissues and its dysregulation has been linked to enhanced proliferation and invasion in various cancer types (34,35). The second locus involves BPTF, a chromatin remodeling factor that plays crucial roles in both T2D risk and LCA growth (27,36). BPTF has been shown to regulate T2D development through modulation of adiposity-mediated pathways, while its aberrant activation in cancer cells promotes tumor growth through epigenetic reprogramming (37). This dual functionality suggests a potential mechanistic link between metabolic dysfunction and cancer susceptibility. The APOE/APOC1/TOMM40 gene cluster represents another significant shared locus. APOE, well-known for its role in lipid metabolism, has been increasingly recognized for its involvement in both glucose homeostasis and cancer progression (38). Recent studies have demonstrated that APOE variants affect insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake, and also influence tumor microenvironment and metastasis. Similarly, APOC1 affects both lipid metabolism and tumor growth (29). Mechanistically, APOC1 regulates lipid homeostasis by modulating cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) activity during high density lipoprotein (HDL) metabolism and concurrently influences immune cell polarization, particularly macrophage activation states (39,40). APC1-driven lipid dysregulation triggers chronic low-grade inflammation characteristic of T2D (41). This inflammatory milieu establishes a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment conducive to LCA initiation and progression. Conversely, cancer-associated metabolic reprogramming further exacerbates insulin resistance via inflammatory cytokine feedback loops, thereby reinforcing a self-sustaining pathological cycle (42). TOMM40 impacts mitochondrial function, a process central to both lipid metabolism and cancer cell survival (43-45). TCF7L2, one of the strongest genetic determinants of T2D risk, emerges as another shared locus (46). TCF7L2 encodes as a transcription factor that mediates Wnt signaling and regulates pancreatic β-cell function (46). In cancer contexts, TCF7L2 promotes anoikis resistance, facilitating metastatic progression (47). The pleiotropic effects of TCF7L2 variants stem from their impact on shared biological processes—including inflammation, oxidative stress, and Wnt pathway dysregulation—that drive both metabolic dysfunction and tumorigenesis (48-50). Clinical, these variants not only impair insulin secretion and glucose homeostasis but also correlate with poor prognosis in NSCLC (50). The final shared locus, PVRL2, encodes a cell adhesion molecule with dual implications in metabolism and cancer. suppressing T-cell activation. Recent study has revealed role of PVRL2 in immune evasion by suppressing T-cell activation and regulating immune checkpoint interactions (51). High expression of PVRL2 on both CD14+ and CD45− cells in LCA suggests a potential clinical benefit in targeting PVRIG in indications where this pathway plays a significant role.

The identification of these shared genetic loci extends beyond mechanistic insight, providing clear avenues for clinical translation. The pleiotropy of apolipoproteins (APO) in lipid metabolism suggests that genotype-guided lipid-lowering therapies like statins or PCSK9 inhibitors could yield a dual therapeutic benefit, mitigating cardiometabolic risk while also reducing LCA risk, particularly in APOE ε4 allele carriers (52,53). Similarly, since TCF7L2 drives T2D susceptibility via the oncologically-targeted Wnt/β-catenin pathway, repurposing Wnt inhibitors (e.g., LGK974) is a compelling strategy for patients with shared genetic predisposition (54). Moreover, the shared CDKN2B-AS1 locus holds significant promise as a molecular biomarker to refine risk stratification, directly informing clinical decision, such as intensifying LCA screening for high-risk diabetic patients (55).

Our pathway analyses revealed significant enrichment in both metabolic and cancer-related pathways, particularly highlighting the role of chromatin organization. The enrichment of multiple chromatin-related GO terms, coupled with tissue-specific enrichment patterns in histone modifications, suggests that epigenetic regulation is a crucial link between metabolic disruption and cancer development. The overlapping enrichment of H3K4me modifications between LCA subtypes and T2D, particularly in metabolically active tissues, provides potential mechanistic explanations for how metabolic perturbations might influence cancer risk through altered gene regulation.

We acknowledge several limitations. First, as the statistical power of our GWAS analysis was restricted to the sample sizes of each of LCA subtypes, the genetic correlation between T2D and additional LCA subtypes may be discovered with larger sample sizes. Second, tissue/cell-type-specific enrichment patterns should be interpreted with caution due to the varying quality and availability of tissue-specific epigenetic data. Third, our analysis was restricted to European ancestry populations, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, while our identification of shared genetic loci provides mechanistic insights supported by existing biological evidence—including APOE-mediate dysregulation of lipid metabolism pathways in both diseases and TCF7L2’s validated associations with both T2D and LCA—functional experimental validation remains essential to establish definitive causality and fully elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms (46,50,56,57). Future work should focus on following directions. Large, prospective longitudinal clinical studies are needed to define whether T2D patients carrying specific genotypes are at increased risk for developing particular LCA subtypes. The use of gene-modified mouse models, e.g., knock-in/knock-out of identified risk genes, would be important to define the role of specific genes and rare genetic variants in disease development. Integration of other omics data, particularly metabolomics and single-cell transcriptomics, could provide additional insights into the biological mechanisms underlying these complex traits. Moreover, investigating whether improved glycemic control reduces LCA risk and progression in patients with specific genetic profiles could provide novel clinical prevention strategy.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrate shared genetic architecture between LCA subtypes and T2D, with strongest associations observed in SCLC and LUSC. Two pleiotropic deleterious variants (rs79687284 and rs340874), previously identified as distinct signals influencing T2D risk, could serve as potential biomarkers for risk stratification, suggesting that T2D patients harboring these variants may benefit from enhanced LCA surveillance. Furthermore, our identification of shared biological pathways provides mechanistic insights into the link between metabolic dysfunction and LCA development, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for patients with concurrent conditions.
