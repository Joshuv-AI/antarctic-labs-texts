Study on the effect of nursing intervention based on the broaden-and-build theory on the psychosocial adaptability of patients with minimally invasive ablation of liver cancer
Authors: Jinxing Li, Jing You, Dan Zhang, Xuemin Jing, Xiaojing Lian, Li Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the effect of nursing intervention based on the broaden-and-build theory on the psychosocial adaptability of patients with minimally invasive ablation of liver cancer.MethodA total of 100 patients with liver cancer undergoing minimally invasive ablation were selected and randomly divided into two groups. The control group consisted of 50 patients who received routine nursing intervention and Health education. The intervention group consisted of 50 patients who received nursing intervention based on the broaden-and-build theory in addition to routine nursing intervention and Health education. Data were collected and compared using the Self-rating Anxiety Scale, the Self-rating Depression Scale, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, the Strategies Used by People to Promote Health self-report and the Gratitude Questionnaire 6. Significance was verified by the independent samples t-test, repeated measures analysis of variance and χ2 test, and confounders were controlled for using randomised grouping, baseline comparability analysis and single-blind design.ResultsAfter 3 and 6 months of intervention, patients in the intervention group showed significantly greater improvement in anxiety scores (28.16 ± 8.24 vs. 33.04 ± 8.02; 32.16 ± 8.24 vs. 36.18 ± 7.09), depression scores (25.73 ± 9.18 vs. 35.91 ± 10.99; 29.73 ± 9.18 vs. 37.33 ± 9.13) and sleep quality scores (10.22 ± 2.95 vs. 11.33 ± 2.21; 12.16 ± 2.87 vs. 13.53 ± 2.14) than those in the control group (all p < 0.001) and greater improvement in self-efficacy (125.98 ± 12.81 vs. 115.49 ± 20.06; 115.53 ± 12.12 vs. 102.31 ± 26.47) and gratitude levels (40.20 ± 2.08 vs. 35.87 ± 6.29; 39.44 ± 2.31 vs. 35.13 ± 6.10) (all p < 0.05). This suggests that the nursing intervention based on the extended construct theory of gratitude effectively improved the psychological health and quality of life of patients undergoing minimally invasive ablation for hepatocellular carcinoma.ConclusionNursing intervention based on the broaden-and-build theory can effectively reduce the negative emotions of patients with minimally invasive ablation of liver cancer by improving sleep quality, self-efficacy and gratitude level.Trial registrationThe study was registered on http://www.chictr.org.cn ‘ChiCTR2400093603’ on 9 December 2024.Keywords: Broaden-and-build theory, Negative emotion, Self-efficacy, Liver cancer

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a common malignant tumour in China, and its morbidity and mortality rank fifth and second, respectively, among all malignant tumours [1]. The number of new patients per year with liver cancer in China is approximately 370,000, accounting for nearly half of the world’s cases and making it the country with the heaviest burden of liver cancer globally [1–3]. The incidence of liver cancer is insidious, and most patients are in the middle or late stages or metastasis at the time of diagnosis. The prognosis is poor, and palliative treatment is often used in clinical practice. The 5-year overall survival rate of HCC in China is only 14.1%, and the 5-year survival rate for HCC globally is approximately 18% [4, 5]. Despite treatment, up to 70% of patients with liver cancer develop tumour metastasis or recurrence after 5 years, which makes patients worry about disease progression throughout the treatment and recovery process [6]. Patients must face the blow of malignant tumour diagnosis and bear the adverse reactions of anti-tumour therapy, such as surgery or interventional therapy, resulting in negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression, which often lead to a serious reduction in sleep quality [7, 8].

Currently, in China, minimally invasive ablation has become one of the main methods for the treatment of liver cancer because of its small trauma, high safety and repeatability. However, based on the characteristics of high mortality and high recurrence rate of liver cancer, most patients have a variety of negative emotions and increased psychological pressure during treatment, resulting in difficulty in falling asleep, poor sleep quality and low quality of life; this can seriously affect patients’ treatment compliance, thus interfering with the treatment effect [9]. Therefore, effective postoperative nursing intervention, reducing patients’ negative emotions and improving their sleep quality are current clinical priorities [10, 11]. The focus of this nursing intervention study is to provide a sound postoperative nursing intervention to reduce the negative emotions of the patients and to improve the quality of their sleep.

The broaden-and-build theory proposed by Fredrickson is a cornerstone in the field of positive psychology, with its core focus on revealing how positive emotions enhance individual well-being through two major processes: ‘broadening’ and ‘building’ [12]. The theory posits that positive emotions ‘broaden’ an individual’s cognitive and behavioural range, making their thinking more flexible and their perspective broader, thereby enabling them to explore and attempt more possibilities. Upon this foundation, the ‘building’ process occurs, wherein individuals accumulate enduring personal resources, such as social support, coping skills and physiological resources, through these broadened experiences. These two processes work together to form a positive upward spiral: ‘positive emotions → resource accumulation → enhanced capacity for positive emotions’. The accumulated resources further strengthen the individual’s buffer against the impact of negative emotions, enhancing their sense of happiness and psychological resilience [13]. Gratitude, as a specific positive emotion, holds a unique position within this theoretical framework. It is not only one among many positive emotions but also an emotional experience that can effectively trigger the ‘broadening’ and ‘building’ processes. Gratitude guides individuals to focus on the kindness of others, the beauty in life and their own good fortune, thereby breaking through the potential cognitive limitation that ‘negative situations equal disaster’, thus achieving the ‘broadening’ of attention. Simultaneously, gratitude experiences strengthen interpersonal connections, accumulate social support resources and enhance self-worth, constituting psychological capital—this is the embodiment of the ‘building’ process [14].

The extended conceptual definition of gratitude transcends its level as a fleeting emotional experience, being understood as a stable cognitive pattern and behavioural tendency formed through sustained practice and internalisation. Its core lies in the individual’s habitual tendency to extract and focus actively on positive meanings, contributions from others or resources they possess, particularly from life experiences that may contain negative elements [15]. This cognitive pattern is not just an emotional response but also a positive psychological resource that enables individuals to ‘broaden their positive perception range from negative situations and build sustainable psychological adaptation capabilities’. Based on the broaden-and-build theory, the positive effects of gratitude have been substantiated from multiple perspectives. Research findings [16] indicate that gratitude can promote positive cognitive construction, enhance social connections and elevate life evaluation and positive expectations, thereby strengthening psychological resilience and alleviating negative emotions. Its therapeutic effects are not limited to the psychological level but can also improve the physiological and social adaptation of patients with chronic diseases, such as cancer, by improving mental health, coping skills, social support and quality of life, helping them better cope with the challenges of illness. However, these findings should be viewed cautiously; gratitude differs from other positive emotions (such as joy and pride), with its uniqueness lying in its greater emphasis on the perception and response to external positive factors, making it more targeted in building social resources. Additionally, the effects of gratitude vary among individuals and may be moderated by factors such as cultural background and the severity of the disease—they are not a ‘universal’ solution. Therefore, in specific contexts, such as interventions for patients undergoing minimally invasive ablation for liver cancer, personalised gratitude interventions should be designed based on the characteristics of the patients, and personalised guidance should be provided to reduce cultural adaptation barriers, aiming to activate the broaden-and-build effects of gratitude and promote their physical and mental well-being more effectively.

This study is the first to apply the extended construct theory of gratitude to the care of Chinese patients with minimally invasive ablation of HCC to improve their negative emotions and sleep quality. It aims to fill the gaps of long-term efficacy, acculturation and standardised interventions in this field in China.

Study participants and methods

Study participants

This was a randomised controlled study with a minimum sample size of 80 cases calculated by PASS software. The recruitment period was 1 March 2024 to 31 August 2024, during which patients who received minimally invasive ablation therapy for HCC and met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were screened in our hospital by the convenience sampling method; those who met the eligibility criteria were told in detail about the purpose of the study, the process and the risks and were enrolled in the study after signing the informed consent form. A total of 100 patients were enrolled; the single-blind method was used, in which only the patients were blinded, in order to avoid assessment bias and ensure the objectivity of the results. The patients were randomly divided into a control group (conventional care) and an intervention group (extended construct theory of gratitude care) according to a randomised numerical table, in order to assess the latter’s impact on the patients’ psychosocial adaptation. Fifty patients in the control group received conventional nursing interventions and Health education; 50 patients in the intervention group received nursing interventions based on the extended construct theory.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with HCC diagnosed by imaging or pathological biopsy who underwent minimally invasive ablation; (2) Child–Pugh B or above; (3) age ≥ 18 years; (4) blood ammonia < 54 µg/dl; (5) no mental illness and language disorders, no psychotropic drugs and treatment of Hepatic encephalopathy drugs within 3 months; (6) ability to use mobile devices, such as cell phones, proficiently for remote communication and information reception needs.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with severe cognitive, audio-visual dysfunction, hepatic encephalopathy or abnormal psychological behaviour; (2) patients unable to communicate with or actively cooperate with medical staff.

The dropout reasons were as follows: (1) patients withdrew; (2) during the study period, patients experienced gastrointestinal bleeding or other serious changes in their condition, which led to the inability to continue follow-up or death.

This study was approved by the hospital ethics committee (ethical number LL-2024–002-K), and all participants signed informed consent.

Study methods

The nursing programme in this study included two stages: in-hospital nursing intervention and out-of-hospital continuous nursing. In the out-of-hospital continuous nursing, the intervention programme continued until 3 months after discharge in both groups.

Control group

Patients in the control group received routine nursing intervention and health education. The specific procedures were as follows: upon admission, routine nursing (such as monitoring vital signs and postoperative wound care) and health education (such as post-ablation precautions and dietary guidance) were conducted. For anxiety and depression caused by treatment effects and postoperative recovery, targeted psychological counselling was provided to guide patients to understand their condition rationally and build confidence in recovery. For patients with sleep disorders, personalised sleep schedules were developed and monitored for compliance. For those whose sleep quality did not improve after following the schedule, medication intervention was administered according to medical advice. Upon discharge, discharge instructions (such as key points for home care) were given. After discharge, monthly telephone follow-ups were conducted, including inquiries about physical and mental condition, answering questions, health education and reminding patients of follow-up appointments (see Table 1).

Intervention group

As well as the routine nursing provided to the control group, the intervention group received additional nursing intervention based on the broaden-and-build theory, divided into three phases:

Team formation and training—the team consisted of the attending physician, head nurse, oncology specialist nurse, primary nurse, psychological counsellor and nursing expert (detailed roles for each member are shown in Table 2). Before the intervention, the psychological counsellor conducted systematic training for the team, covering the core concepts of the broaden-and-build theory, standardised procedures for gratitude interventions (such as guidance on gratitude journaling) and strategies for addressing common psychological issues. A test was administered after the training to ensure operational consistency.

Implementation of personalised intervention plans—the intervention period spanned from admission to 6 months post-discharge. The forms of intervention included outpatient follow-ups (once a month), telephone follow-ups (every 2 weeks, with fixed times between 9 and 10 am or 3 and 4 pm), WeChat interactions (for real-time question answering) and monthly online knowledge lectures. The core intervention measures included guiding patients to keep a daily gratitude journal (e.g. ‘One small warm thing experienced today’), sharing one positive experience weekly in a WeChat group (e.g. ‘Improved physical strength after treatment’) and participating in one gratitude-themed sharing session monthly (either offline or online). After each intervention, the time for the next session was scheduled, and the content was adjusted based on patient feedback (e.g. providing one-on-one guidance for those with difficulty expressing themselves).

Intervention/evaluation indicators and evaluation methods

Patient data were collected at admission, 1 month, 3 months and 6 months after discharge at the start of the study intervention.

General information

General information includes gender, age, religious belief, marital status, education level, type of household registration, occupational status, payment method of medical expenses, caregivers and Child–Pugh classification. In this study, these general information variables were used as covariates or for subgroup analyses, as appropriate, to explore their impact on patients’ psychosocial adjustment and intervention outcomes.

Main outcome measures

The Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [17] and Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS) [18] were used to evaluate the psychological status of the two groups of patients. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [19] was used to evaluate the sleep quality of the two groups. The SAS and the SDS Both contain 20 entries, with a 4-point scale for each, and total scores range from 20 to 80 points for assessing the severity of anxiety and depression, respectively; the higher the score is, the more severe the degree of anxiety or depression. The SAS and SDS are scored on a similar scale with a 50-point cutoff, with scores ≤ 50 considered normal and scores > 50 considered mild, moderate or severe anxiety or depression, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the SAS self-report scale was 0.89, with a reliability coefficient of 0.777 and a validity coefficient of 0.69; the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the SDS self-report scale was 0.93.

The PSQI assesses the quality of sleep in the past month through 19 self-assessment items and five other-assessment items, with a global score of 0–21, with higher scores indicating poorer quality of sleep and with a cutoff score of 5. A score of ≤ 5 indicates better quality of sleep, whereas a score of > 5 suggests sleep disorders. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the PSQI was 0.87.

Secondary indicators

The Strategies Used by People to Promote Health (SUPPH) [20, 21] was used to evaluate the patient’s self-efficacy, and the Gratitude Questionnaire 6 (GQ-6) [22] was used to evaluate the gratitude level of the two groups.

The SUPPH questionnaire was developed by American researchers Lev and Owen [20] in 1996. In 2011, the Chinese scholar Qian Huijuan [21] comprehensively considered Chinese national conditions and synthesised the questionnaire and finally formed a ‘C-SUPPH’ suitable for Chinese cancer patients. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the C-SUPPH total scale was 0.970, the Guttman split-half coefficient was > 0.9 and the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the subscale was > 0.8.

The GQ-6 scale was compiled by foreign scholars McCullough et al. [22] and translated and revised by Chinese scholars Wei et al. [23] in 2011. The higher the total score of the questionnaire is, the higher the degree of gratitude of the individual. The questionnaire is widely used in China and has good reliability and validity. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the GQ-6 scale was 0.81.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS 26.0 statistical software package was used for statistical analysis. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov method was used to test for normality. The measurement data that met the normality criteria were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). The independent sample t-test was used for group design. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for repeated measurement data. The count data were expressed as frequency (n) or rate (%). The χ2 test was used for those that met the conditions, and the Fisher exact probability method was used for those that did not meet the conditions. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

There were 50 cases in the control group, comprising 34 men and 16 women with an average age of 61.73 ± 10.56 years. There was no significant difference in gender, religious belief or marital status between the two groups (all p > 0.05, see Table 3).

Repeated measures analysis of variance showed that the main effects of time for SAS, SDS, PSQI, SUPPH and GQ-6 (F = 159.898, 153.545, 203.085, 99.803, 62.424, all p < 0.001) and the main effect of treatment (F = 1521.077, 1054.046, 2738.793, 2729.726, 7.877, all p < 0.001), and interaction effects (F = 93.213, 106.031, 13.152, 15.314, 16.260, all p < 0.001) were all significant, indicating that the improvement trend in the intervention group was superior.

Comparison of self-efficacy and gratitude level

Before the intervention, the difference in SUPPH and GQ-6 scores between the two groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05, see Table 5). Three months after the intervention, the SUPPH score (125.98 ± 12.81) was significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group (115.49 ± 20.06) (F = 99.803, p < 0.001), and the GQ-6 score (40.20 ± 2.08) was significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group (35.87 ± 6.29) (F = 62.424, p < 0.001); 6 months after the intervention, the SUPPH and GQ-6 scores were significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group. The SUPPH (115.53 ± 12.12) and GQ-6 (39.44 ± 2.31) scores were further elevated compared with those at 3 months, and the differences with the control group were more significant (all p < 0.001). Repeated measures ANOVA showed a significant time × treatment interaction effect (SUPPH: F = 15.314, p < 0.001; GQ-6: F = 16.260, p < 0.001), suggesting that the elevation effect of the intervention group increased over time.

Discussion

Currently, minimally invasive ablation for liver cancer is one of the main treatments for the disease in China because it is less invasive, safer and more reproducible [9]. Due to the high mortality and recurrence rate of liver cancer, patients often suffer from tremendous psychological pressure during the treatment process, leading to negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression, which in turn trigger sleep disorders, reduce the quality of life, affect treatment adherence and ultimately interfere with the treatment effect. Furthermore, patients often fear the recurrence of cancer or disease progression [10]. The focus of this nursing intervention study is to provide a good postoperative nursing intervention to reduce the negative emotions of the patients and to improve the quality of their sleep [11].

The findings of this study are consistent with the core predictions of the broaden-and-build theory. Millstein et al. proposed [15] that the ‘broadening effect’ of positive emotions can break through an individual’s ‘defensive cognitive narrowing’ (such as patients with cancer focusing solely on ‘risk of death’), while the ‘building effect’ can accumulate coping resources. In this study, the intervention group recorded ‘the patience of medical staff in answering questions’ and ‘the care and company of family members’ through gratitude diaries. This act of broadening the perception of positive events gradually broke down the cognitive narrowing of ‘disease equals hopelessness’. Moreover, the sustained sharing via WeChat and expressions of gratitude reinforced social connection resources, which directly resonates with the mechanism in the theory that ‘positive emotions build social support networks’ [13].

This study confirms that nursing intervention based on the gratitude-broaden-and-build theory significantly outperforms conventional care, particularly at the 3rd and 6th months post-intervention, where anxiety and depression decreased, whereas sleep quality, self-efficacy and gratitude levels concurrently improved. This indicates its potential to enhance the psychosocial adaptation of patients undergoing minimally invasive ablation for liver cancer durably. This advantage stems from the theory’s threefold function [24, 25]: (1) ‘broadening’, achieved through WeChat sharing of positive experiences, guides patients to shift their focus from the lesion to remaining abilities and resources, breaking the cycle of catastrophising thoughts; (2) ‘building’, accomplished by repeatedly practicing to solidify fleeting gratitude into a stable cognitive pattern such as ‘illness also contains gifts’, continuously accumulating psychological capital and social support; (3) ‘sustained release’ transforms positive emotions into protective factors against stress, further reinforcing the upward spiral.

At the neurobiological level, gratitude training synchronously activates the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, insula and grey matter networks, strengthening long-term goal setting and emotional regulation [26–28]; simultaneously, it downregulates excessive hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis reactivity, lowers cortisol levels and promotes the release of dopamine and serotonin, forming a physiological–emotional positive feedback loop [29–31]. As gratitude levels increase, patients become more prone to experiencing positive emotions, such as optimism and contentment, leading them to adopt positive coping strategies proactively, thereby buffering negative emotions and improving sleep [32–34]. It should be noted that individual differences (personality, culture, disease severity) can influence the magnitude of benefits; therefore, the intervention must be implemented long-term and personalised to maintain neuroplasticity and the sustained gains in psychological capital.

As a personality trait in positive psychology, gratitude focuses on the patient’s own latent and relatively stable positive emotional tendencies, rather than just a transient experience of gratitude. To some extent, it is transformed into a positive force to help patients pay attention to positive things, form a positive way of thinking and promote physical and mental health [35, 36]. Gratitude is a positive emotional experience that includes thankfulness, appreciation and repayment and has a wide range of adaptive functions [37]. Through the researchers’ positive explanation of the disease, recalling the past with gratitude value, watching gratitude series videos and recording gratitude diaries, patients are aware of the help of others in their daily life. This multiple gratitude experience improves the brain’s coding and memory of positive events, so that patients can form positive cognitive preferences and pay more attention to positive factors [38]; it arouses individuals’ perception of gratitude emotional traits. Thus, the patient’s gratitude level is improved as a whole.

Although conventional care is effective in improving patients’ symptoms and promoting rehabilitation, it has limitations in enhancing psychosocial adaptation; its low-cost intervention is limited, requiring more resources and time to meet expectations. In this study, we focused on the gratitude intervention, which centres on relational gratitude, guiding patients to pay attention to the good things in life and express their gratitude to construct positive cognition and enhance the sense of well-being, which is simple and clinically useful. Conventional care relieves immediate negative emotions but does not systematically build positive resources. An intervention based on the broadening–constructing theory, however, helps patients develop their own positive cognitive strategies through the broadening–constructing cycle, thus maintaining the improvement 6 months after their discharge from the hospital.

This study has limitations, such as a small sample size, short study duration and limited follow-up time, and it is restricted by the single-centre research and resource investment. Future studies should expand the sample size, extend the study and follow-up time, increase resource investment and explore personalised intervention protocols, mechanism studies and cross-disease applications. This will further improve the application of the broaden-and-build theory in psychosocial adaptation interventions for patients undergoing minimally invasive ablation for HCC and provide a reference for other chronic disease fields.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily confirmed that nursing intervention based on the extended construct theory of gratitude can effectively reduce negative emotions and improve the sleep quality, self-efficacy and gratitude level of patients undergoing minimally invasive ablation for HCC. This provides a new way of thinking and an effective means of improving the psychosocial adaptability of patients, with important practical significance. However, due to its limitations, future studies need to further expand the sample size, extend the study and follow-up time, increase the investment of resources and explore the possibilities of personalised intervention programmes, in-depth investigation of the intervention mechanism and cross-disease application. This would provide a more comprehensive and in-depth application of the extended construct theory of gratitude to the field of HCC and other chronic diseases, thus offering a more comprehensive and in-depth scientific basis and a reference for the application of the extended construct theory of gratitude in liver cancer and other chronic diseases.
