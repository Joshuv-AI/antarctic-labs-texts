Introduction

The escalating world population has heightened the necessity for fundamental resources: clean water, sustainable food, and innovative energy sources, encompassing comforting heat, chilling cold, versatile electricity, and efficient fuel [1,2]. As the reliance on fossil fuels grows to address shortages, the earth suffers from increased pollution, blanketing the skies and warming the planet. Using renewable resources has become the choice of humanity as a result [3]. Solar energy and biomass are two highly practical renewable sources, ideal for generating electricity and fuel due to their widespread availability [4,5]. A possible solution could involve using biomass pyrolysis and gasification, followed by the conversion of the synthesis gas produced [6,7]. To ensure the cost-effectiveness of the final fuel and electricity products, it is, however, necessary to develop processes that demonstrate appropriate energy and exergy efficiency.

The application of cogeneration systems is known as an efficient solution to boost the productivity of processes because they yield various products like water, electricity, heat, cold, and fuel while enhancing the system's efficiency [[8], [9], [10]]. Numerous in-depth studies have been meticulously conducted in this burgeoning field. For example, Using biomass and solar power, Kermani Alghorayshi and colleagues engineered a hybrid system to generate 34.547 MW of electricity, 783 m3/h of freshwater with a clean, crisp taste, and synthesis gas, realizing a 52 % desalination efficiency and 35.51 % exergy efficiency through MED technology [11,12]. Abbasi and colleagues combined solar, wind, a Rankine steam cycle, and multi-stage flash reverse osmosis desalination to produce 57.63 MW of electricity and 2505 m3/h of crystal-clear drinking water [13]. Additionally, combining different manufacturing processes with hybrid renewable energy sources can improve efficiency and overall performance. In one study, Kermani Alghorayshi et al. demonstrated that by combining solar and wind energy, they achieved an output of 52.14 MW of electricity, 1199 m3/h of fresh water, 11.3 MW of cold, and 58.86 tons of salt per hour, achieving energy and exergy efficiencies of 57 % and 50.1 % [14].

Unlike solar and wind energy, which are renewable and directly usable, biomass necessitates either combustion or conversion into synthesis gases before it can be harnessed [15,16]. Gasification is one of the most promising technologies for producing renewable fuels from biomass, a globally accessible renewable energy source [17,18]. The stable synthesis gas created through biomass gasification has applications in direct use, biochemical processes, and biofuel production [19,20]. Carotenuto et al. performed a numerical comparison, contrasting the efficiency of combined heat and power (CHP) generation using combustion and gasification methods, all fueled by residual woody biomass. The first configuration's gas turbine is fueled by the raw, unfiltered exhaust from direct combustion; the second uses cleaner, processed syngas created through gasification. With combustion displaying an approximate 40 % efficiency boost, the results also indicate gasification yields fewer greenhouse gas emissions [21]. Copa Rey et al. performed a detailed technical analysis, carefully examining the integration of Biomass Integrated Gasification-Gas Turbine (BIG-GT) technology into a sugarcane mill, fueled by the fibrous byproduct, bagasse. Among four proposed configurations, electricity generation efficiency improved by 5.9–12.6 %, a noticeable increase in output. Configuration IV, reliant only on in-mill biomass and the crackling burn of supplemental straw, yielded a modest efficiency gain of 5.9 % but severed the tie to external fuel sources [22].

When synthesis gas is produced adequately in both quantity and quality, the required fuel can be generated through the addition of fuel production processes [23,24]. In a study by Xu et al. [25], DME production through CO₂ capture boasted energy and exergy efficiencies of 49.54 % and 41.12 %, respectively, and a 7.12-year return on investment. Ciccone et al. developed and assessed a biomass-based polygeneration system, its heart humming as it produced DME, methanol, hythane (with up to 30 % H₂), alongside heat and power. Efficient multi-product generation was supported by the production of syngas with an H₂/(CO+CO₂) ratio of 0.97 at 950 °C and under low-pressure O₂/steam conditions [26]. Scognamiglio et al. employed Aspen Plus simulation to evaluate one-step DME synthesis, focusing on CO₂-enriched syngas from biomass gasification and its impact on process efficiency. Optimal conditions—220 °C, almost too hot to touch, and 50 bar of pressure—led to the highest CO conversion, while an H₂/COₓ ratio of 3 achieved a balance between reaction efficiency and H₂ usage, despite negative CO₂ conversion [27].

The cost of fuel is vital when it is produced from biomass. To achieve proper energy and exergy efficiency, the biomass type and process design are crucial. The design must be precise, as syngas and biogas production is highly sensitive to parameters such as the CO:H₂ and O₂:biomass ratios. Yan et al. reported a 51.7 % efficiency increase and a cost reduction of 0.0501 $/kW with optimal H₂O/O₂ and O₂/biomass ratios of 2.8 and 0.22, respectively [28]. Kofler et al. studied DME production from agricultural residues (wheat straw) and forestry residues (bamboo) utilizing one-stage and two-stage synthesis systems that were operated using wind and solar energy sources. The two-stage process showed higher efficiencies, but the bamboo-fed one-stage system achieved the lowest levelized cost of fuel (115 €/MWh), using up to 45 % less hydrogen and electricity with minimal operational complexity [29]. Sun & Aziz proposed two biomass-based methanol production routes via chemical looping: biomass chemical looping gasification (B-CLG), which uses a gasification process, and biomass chemical looping hydrogen production (B-CLHP), focused on hydrogen creation. Both achieve ∼60 % energy efficiency, but B-CLHP is more economically feasible, costing only 383.59 USD/t for methanol with a quicker payback period of 8.29 years; B-CLG, in contrast, demands 479.46 USD/t and a lengthy 18.52 years to recoup costs [30].

New research underscores the significance of techno-economic optimization in hybrid renewable energy systems. As an illustration, studies have evaluated combined PV/biogas/wind/hydrogen systems for eco-friendly transport [31]. Furthermore, the energy and economic viability of hydrogen refueling stations that are PV-assisted has been studied [32]. Additionally, stand-alone PV/wind/battery systems for hydrogen production have been the subject of optimization approaches like HOMER [33]. Despite this, there is a lack of research specifically examining biomass-derived dimethyl ether (DME) and methanol production alongside solar energy input and CO₂ capture. The goal of this study is to close this existing gap by proposing a new hybrid method for converting biomass into DME, which also has better energy recovery and is more environmentally friendly.

Even with great progress in biomass valorization and solar-assisted chemical processing, few studies have merged pyrolysis, autothermal gasification, and carbon capture into a full solar-powered energy recovery system. The innovative aspect of this research is the synergistic integration of biomass pyrolysis and syngas upgrading for DME and methanol production, coupled with power generation via dual steam cycles, and solar energy as the main heat source. In addition to improving environmental sustainability via solvent-based CO₂ capture from hot combustion gases, recovered heat integration across subsystems improves exergy efficiency. This comprehensive strategy offers an all-encompassing solution for clean fuel production, power generation, and greenhouse gas reduction—showcasing technical innovation and economic viability.

Novelty

This research introduces a new combined system that uses thermochemical biomass conversion alongside solar thermal energy and post-combustion CO₂ capture to concurrently produce bio-based DME, methanol, and electricity. By combining biomass pyrolysis with syngas purification, this method provides several technological benefits, including greater product yield and purity, enhanced energy and exergy efficiency through process heat recovery, and a reduction in impurities, which in turn facilitates CO₂ capture and minimizes greenhouse gas emissions.

To optimize the system's performance, this approach uses a detailed 4E analysis (energy, exergy, economic, and environmental) compared to standard biofuel methods, along with dual power recovery loops and internal heat integration. Due to its high conversion efficiency, capacity to create numerous biofuels, and compatibility with solar thermal integration, we selected this method, which presents a scalable, low-carbon solution. The process is shown to be viable from both a technical and economic standpoint, as demonstrated by the techno-economic evaluation, which emphasizes its environmental and economic benefits.

This design makes sure that the technical benefits of integrating pyrolysis with syngas, as well as the new elements of the hybrid system, are clearly set apart from systems that have been reported before.

Dry biomass, entering a pyrolysis reactor at 12,400 kg/h, experiences thermal decomposition at approximately 800–960 °C and 2.5 bar, as detailed in Table 3. The vapors and gases are channeled to an oxidation reactor (6.3 m in length, 0.23 m in diameter) which operates at 605 °C and 2.2 bar. Oxygen is provided at a rate that is 1.2 times the feed rate. Subsequently, the stream is channeled into a gasification reactor (7.56 m × 0.5 m), functioning at 1000 °C and 2 bar, resulting in syngas abundant in CO, H₂, CO₂, and H₂O.

The temperature of the hot syngas, exiting at nearly 1200 °C, is brought down to roughly 180 °C using a series of heat exchangers. A Rankine steam cycle, fueled by the recovered heat (steam at 445 °C, 310 t/h), produces 108.26 MW of electricity. This electricity powers compressors and auxiliary components, while also cooling the syngas to 65 °C (refer to Table 3). Parabolic collectors are used in a parallel solar-assisted cycle to heat HITEC molten salt to roughly 550 °C, which then transfers heat to a secondary steam turbine. The two cycles work in tandem to boost energy recovery, which also enables the selling of extra electricity.

The syngas undergoes compression in three steps, reaching roughly 33 bar after cooling, with intercooling used to save energy. Methanol and DME are produced simultaneously when the compressed gas enters the DME synthesis reactor (254 °C, 32 bar, 6 m × 1.5 m). Under these circumstances, the reactor converts approximately 18 % of the reactants, producing 2699.8 kg/h of DME and 559.4 kg/h of methanol, with a H₂/CO ratio of 1.92 (as detailed in Table 3). In the downstream distillation columns, products are separated and purified, and the DME column reboiler at 170 °C uses surplus syngas heat for more energy integration.

Kinetic parameter selection and model limitations

The accuracy of simulations involving pyrolysis and gasification is highly influenced by the chosen kinetic parameters and an understanding of the models' restrictions. This research used thermogravimetric experiments (TGA/DTG), along with both model-free (KAS/FWO) and model-fitting (Coats–Redfern) techniques, to determine activation energies (Eₐ) and pre-exponential factors (A). The activation energies for cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin were 198, 132, and 220 kJ/mol, respectively, at the component level, mirroring published data.

The simulation's purpose dictated the use of either basic global models or more intricate methods, including the DAEM and CPD models, with typical values hovering around 165 kJ/mol. The kinetic constants for char gasification were modified using correlations based on the Random Pore Model (RPM) and Modified Random Pore Model (MRP). To further differentiate intrinsic kinetics from apparent kinetics, transport limitations were addressed, leading to an average activation energy of 143 kJ/mol.

The parameters were adjusted and checked using data from experiments and computer models. To find the most influential parameters on the projected process performance, we conducted more sensitivity and uncertainty analyses.

Results & discussion

This section is dedicated to a comprehensive review of all the simulation results, in addition to examining the parameters that influenced the simulation process, and further presenting the outcomes of the energy, exergy, and economic analyses conducted on the reactor.

Validation

The simulation's validity was checked by comparing the biomass component mass percentages with those reported in the original paper. Simulation and experimental results closely agree, with all component errors below the acceptable 5 % threshold (table S8). The greatest error, 4.50 %, was observed in the H₂O data, whereas the sulfur (S) data was perfectly accurate. The measured percentages of H₂, CH₄, and C showed good agreement with the reference data, with errors of only 3.79 %, 3.85 %, and 0.84 %, respectively (Table 6). The model's accuracy confirms its reliability in predicting biomass composition and related processes. Consequently, the simulation's dependable results make it a suitable foundation for future in-depth analyses.

The results of present process

Thermodynamic and economic performance is promising with the integrated process. The Brayton cycle uses 560.43 MW of 620.38 MW solar heat input to generate 108.26 MW of electricity, with 67 % solar efficiency (table S9). Although the turbine efficiency is a relatively modest 19.3 %, the system's overall energy and exergy efficiencies are 50.22 % and 48.97 %, respectively, suggesting high-quality energy integration across the cycles. Motive steam drives the process at 30 bar pressure and 310 tons/h flow. A maximum of 14.18 MW of exergy is destroyed, suggesting effective thermodynamic optimization. A ratio of 1.92 is maintained for H₂/CO in the syngas, allowing optimal conditions for the following synthesis.

The production side of the system yields 5108 kg/h of syngas, resulting in 2699.84 kg/h of bio-DME and 559.44 kg/h of methanol. Liquid products are obtained from syngas with 41.2 % conversion efficiency, DME selectivity of 84 %, and methanol selectivity of 16 %. At 0.5435 ton/MW, the CO₂ emissions are minimal; also, it uses carbon capture. Electricity generation costs are economically estimated to be 0.0543 $/kW. It costs 636.81 $ to produce a ton of bio-DME, and 382.09 $ for a ton of methanol. The financial viability and long-term sustainability of the process are confirmed by a 526.65 M$/ton biomass NPV and a 5.52-year payback.

A linear relationship was observed between the feed rate and the thermal energy needed in biomass pyrolysis, starting at 26.57 MW and culminating at 53.14 MW — a substantial increase in energy demand (Fig. 5b). Electricity, a valuable co-product that can be sold, is generated by recovering a portion of this energy using a Rankine cycle. Downstream of the gasification process, a second Rankine cycle captures heat from the hot syngas stream, converting this thermal energy into electricity for powering the plant’s compressors; the intense heat radiated from the syngas was palpable. By functionally separating the two power cycles, the system achieves higher energy efficiency and reduces its need for external electricity, leading to lower operating costs and a smaller environmental footprint.

The results show that while increased biomass input boosts product and power output, it also leads to decreased conversion efficiency, higher energy needs, and greater greenhouse gas emissions, necessitating careful process optimization to mitigate these negative impacts.

The impact of biomass moisture

The research methodically investigated how biomass moisture impacted the system's function. Moisture significantly affects system metrics like exergy efficiency. As moisture rose from 10 % to 40 %, exergy efficiency decreased from about 50.8 % to 43.5 % (Fig. 6a). This decline correlated with an increase in exergy destruction, from 13.56 kW to 14.9 kW, representing more energy losses from evaporating the excess water. As a result, total energy efficiency declined from 52.7 % to 45.2 % because the increased moisture's latent heat demand reduced the energy for useful conversion.

The impact of biomass moisture on the performance of the present process.

The biomass moisture had a negative correlation with the syngas/DME production. Syngas production fell sharply from 6647 kg/h to 4669 kg/h, and DME output also went down, from 3180.6 kg/h to 2234.1 kg/h (Fig. 6b). Unlike the other, methanol production increased, moving from 597.6 kg/h to 657.3 kg/h. According to selectivity analysis, this shift happened: DME selectivity declined from 84.2 % to 77.3 %, and methanol selectivity increased from 15.8 % to 22.7 %.

This behavior is explicable by looking at the gasification–synthesis system's thermodynamics and reaction mechanism. Increased moisture in the feedstock elevates steam's partial pressure, thereby accelerating both steam reforming and the water-gas shift reactions (CO + H₂O ⇌ CO₂ + H₂). Fig. 6c illustrates the increase of the H₂/CO ratio from 1.82 to 2.74. Methanol synthesis (CO + 2H₂ ⇌ CH₃OH) is thermodynamically preferred with a higher H₂/CO ratio, and simultaneously, extra steam prevents methanol dehydration (2CH₃OH ⇌ CH₃OCH₃ + H₂O), moving the equilibrium from DME towards methanol. According to Gibbs free energy, more hydrogen reduces the impetus for DME creation, thus making methanol the more stable intermediary.

Concurrently, the disadvantages of increased moisture in terms of the environment and energy were obvious. Emissions of CO₂ climbed from 0.532 t/MW to 0.797 t/MW, whereas the pyrolysis phase demanded considerably more energy (37.14 MW → 89.69 MW), which was mainly used to vaporize moisture. These findings underscore the significance of either pretreating or drying biomass. This improves the efficiency of the process, lowers greenhouse gas emissions, and optimizes the production of DME and methanol in combined pyrolysis-gasification systems.

The impact of pyrolysis reactor temperature

The system's response to a 100 °C temperature increase in the pyrolysis reactor (from 740 °C to 840 °C) showcased a clear compromise: improved energy efficiency came at the cost of decreased exergy efficiency (figure S1.a). Although the exergy efficiency decreases, falling from 53.4 % to 47.2 % as indicated by a rise in exergy destruction (11.66 kW to 15.61 kW), likely from less efficient processes, this is counterbalanced by a significant improvement in overall energy efficiency—a jump from 42.5 % to approximately 51.8 %—indicating better energy utilization despite the exergy losses. The data shows a trade-off: higher temperatures cause more energy loss as heat, but simultaneously boost the transformation of input energy into productive output. The higher thermal demand to sustain the elevated temperatures is evident in the rise of required pyrolysis energy from 32.8 MW to 41.9 MW.

As temperatures increase, the efficiency improvements lead to a substantial rise in biomass conversion rates—from 84 % to nearly 100 % (99.45 %). This improvement points to a significantly more efficient decomposition of the feedstock, which is palpable through the accelerated process and increased yield. However, CO₂ emissions see a modest rise from 0.53 to 0.55 t/MWh, indicating a subtle increase in gasification and oxidation reactions due to the higher temperatures. The slight uptick in emissions, from 0.53 to 0.55 t/MWh, points toward more vigorous gasification and oxidation processes at elevated temperatures. A marginal increase in CO₂ emissions, from 0.53 to 0.55 t/MWh, is observed, likely caused by the intensified chemical reactions at higher temperatures. This research underscores the critical need to fine-tune reactor temperatures; higher temperatures boost biomass conversion and energy efficiency, but this comes at the cost of increased exergy destruction and potentially harmful emissions, demanding a careful balance for sustainable pyrolysis and gasification.

The impact of first reformer reactor temperature

Increasing the reference temperature from 750 °C to 1000 °C gradually improved both exergy and energy efficiencies, a clear indication of enhanced overall system performance (figure S1.b). While the overall exergy efficiency shows a small gain, climbing from around 49.17 % to 51.12 %, a corresponding, though slight, increase in total system exergy destruction is observed—from 11.89 kW to 13.04 kW. The overall energy efficiency shows a similar improvement, increasing from a baseline of 52.07 % to a more efficient 54.13 %, representing a tangible energy saving. The data reveals that increased reference temperatures improve the system's energy conversion, yielding more useful output, even with a small increase in exergy destruction. Higher temperatures appear to boost thermodynamic performance, as suggested by this trend. This is likely because the gasification process benefits from more thorough, efficient reactions at these elevated temperatures.

However, raising the reference temperature from 750 °C to 1000 °C not only decreased syngas production—from 5940 kg/h to 5048.5 kg/h—but also reduced biomass conversion to syngas, dropping from a robust 47.9 % to a less efficient 40.7 %. Simultaneously, CO₂ emissions per unit energy drastically fall from 0.91 to 0.29 t/MWh, indicating a cleaner process with significantly lower greenhouse gas emissions at elevated temperatures. The reduction is substantial, showcasing a marked improvement in environmental impact. This shows that higher temperatures lead to more efficient conversion processes that generate less carbon dioxide, but result in a smaller syngas yield; the increased heat literally burns off more of the reactants. Balancing the effects of maximizing energy output with minimizing emissions is crucial for optimizing system efficiency and environmental sustainability in biomass gasification.

The impact of DME reactor temperature

A clear decline in the overall system performance, specifically in exergy and energy efficiencies, was observed when the DME synthesis reactor temperature was increased from 220 °C to 280 °C; the change was immediately apparent in the reduced operational efficiency of the system (figure S1.c). The exergy efficiency falls from about 52.16 % to 47.58 %, representing a noticeable decrease, while the total exergy destruction shows a minor increase, rising from 12.25 kW to 13.09 kW. A considerable reduction in energy efficiency is observed, with a decline from 56.16 % down to 51.23 %. Higher synthesis temperatures drive this trend, resulting in significant thermodynamic irreversibility and substantial energy losses within the system, a consequence of increased molecular disorder. At the same time, a significant reduction in CO₂ emissions—from 1.07 to 0.34 t/MWh—was observed at elevated temperatures, pointing to a cleaner process marked by enhanced reaction selectivity and conversion, evidenced by reduced air pollution.

DME production yields see a substantial rise, starting at 984 kg/h (220 °C), reaching a high point of 2250 kg/h at 260 °C, then tapering off slightly as temperatures increase further. In the process of converting biomass to DME, the trend remains consistent, with maximum levels of approximately 18.15 % achieved at around 260 °C, while DME selectivity peaks at 86.4 % (figure S1.d). A dramatic decrease in methanol selectivity was observed; it fell sharply from 62.2 % to only 36.4 %, indicating a substantial shift in preference. Additionally, as temperatures climbed, the syngas increasingly favored DME conversion, while methanol production simultaneously decreased, thus highlighting this shift. These results emphasize the critical role of optimized synthesis reactor temperature in maximizing DME yield and system efficiency, while simultaneously reducing unwanted byproducts and emissions; higher temperatures may increase the rate of reaction, but this can also lead to increased undesired byproducts.

The impact of gasification reactor temperature

As the gasification reactor temperature climbed from 800 °C to 1100 °C, a subtle decrease in the system's overall exergy efficiency was observed—from 53.01 % to 48.35 %—accompanied by a slight rise in total exergy destruction, from 12.45 kW to 13.31 kW (figure S2.a). The heat radiating from the reactor intensified with this temperature increase. The overall energy efficiency decreased from 57.07 % to 52.06 %, signifying a tangible loss of efficiency as the gasification temperature rises, possibly due to increased entropy. Despite this, syngas production showed an initial increase, rising from 5001.5 kg/h at 800 °C to a maximum of 5642 kg/h at 1000 °C, only to slightly decrease with further temperature increases. The slight decrease was noticeable at higher temperatures after peaking. The data suggests that around 1000 °C, syngas yield peaks; temperatures significantly above or below this point decrease syngas production. A significant reduction in CO₂ emissions, from 0.99 to 0.31 t/MWh, was observed at higher temperatures (figure S8b). This improvement, marked by a quieter hum from the machinery, points to more complete gasification and lower carbon intensity.

Concurrently, biomass conversion to DME improved, climbing from 14.8 % to about 18 %, indicating a positive correlation between temperature and efficiency. At higher temperatures, a substantial shift occurred: DME selectivity jumped from 70.6 % to 85.5 %, while the simultaneous drop in methanol production and selectivity confirmed the preferential formation of DME (figure S8c). The hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide ratio (H₂/CO) dropped from approximately 2.94 to 1.79, indicating a change in syngas composition that could affect subsequent catalytic reactions. This shift may have been accompanied by subtle changes in temperature and pressure. Higher gasification temperatures, while decreasing overall system efficiency, significantly improve the selectivity and yield of the desired product, DME, highlighting the need for careful temperature control to optimize both process performance and product purity.

Study limitation and future study

Despite the promising results of the proposed solar-assisted biomass conversion system, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current model relies on steady-state simulations and ideal operating conditions, which may not fully capture the variability in solar irradiance and biomass feedstock properties over time. Real-world operational challenges, such as solar intermittency, system control complexity, and biomass logistics, were not dynamically modeled. Additionally, the environmental analysis primarily focuses on CO₂ emissions and does not encompass a full life cycle assessment (LCA) [62,63], including upstream impacts like biomass cultivation, transportation, and construction of solar infrastructure. Moreover, the economic model assumes fixed market prices and stable policy conditions, which may vary significantly in real deployment scenarios.

Future work should include dynamic simulation [64,65] and control strategies [66,67] to assess system performance under fluctuating solar and operational conditions. Integration of energy storage systems (e.g., thermal batteries) may help mitigate solar variability. A comprehensive LCA is recommended to evaluate broader environmental impacts, including water use, land footprint, and resource depletion. Furthermore, pilot-scale implementation and experimental validation would provide valuable insights into practical performance, scalability, and operational reliability. Exploring alternative CO₂ capture methods and optimizing catalyst performance for DME and methanol synthesis can also enhance system efficiency and sustainability.

Conclusion

This study developed an integrated solar-assisted thermochemical system. It combines biomass pyrolysis, autothermal gasification, syngas upgrading, power generation, and solvent-based CO₂ capture. Bio-DME production was 2699.84 kg/h, methanol production was 559.44 kg/h, and 108.26 MW of electricity was produced, resulting in 50.22 % energy efficiency and 48.97 % exergy efficiency. A 0.5435 ton/MW limit was placed on carbon emissions, and the payback period was 5.52 years with a 526.65 M$ NPV per ton of biomass, according to the economic analysis. The data proves the suggested multi-product biorefinery system is technically feasible, environmentally beneficial, and economically viable. The summarized results of the system performance are as follows:

•DME production increased proportionally with the biomass feed rate from 8680 to 17,360 kg/h (2201.4 to 3696.7 kg/h), alongside higher syngas and methanol. On the other hand, a higher biomass input produced somewhat reduced conversion efficiencies (DME/biomass and MEOH/biomass), revealing a capacity scaling versus conversion performance trade-off, and CO₂ emissions rose moderately, going from 0.38 to 0.76 t/MWh.
