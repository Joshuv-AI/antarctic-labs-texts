A quasi-experimental study evaluating the effectiveness of a male-specific tuberculosis screening and diagnosis intervention in Uganda (IGNITE)
Authors: Jasper Nidoi, Tom Wingfield, Rachael Thomson, Justin Pulford, Winters Muttamba, Marc Henrion, Ranga Solomon Owino, Edrine Akera, Bruce Kirenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite men having a higher burden of TB, they are frequently missed by TB services due to social, behavioural, and health system factors that limit healthcare access. In Uganda, where there are no gender-specific guidelines for TB, we implemented and evaluated a gender-specific intervention to find and treat men with TB.MethodsWe conducted a quasi-experimental study comparing TB notifications before (January–June 2023) and after (July–December 2023) the intervention at both intervention (Gombe and Mityana hospitals) and control (Luwero and Kiboga hospitals) sites. The intervention package aimed to enhance TB screening documentation by using checklist stamps, distribute TB educational materials, and introduce male-friendly TB clinics with evening opening hours, health education delivered by male champions, and integrated screening for TB, HIV, diabetes, and hypertension. We extracted data from TB registers and fitted a quasi-Poisson regression model, adjusting for catchment population dynamics by gender, to assess the intervention’s effects on TB case notification rates.ResultsOverall, 6,415 people in the participating hospitals were presumed to have TB, and 53.3% of these were females. Post-intervention, TB notifications increased by 46.3% (Female 0%, Male 88.4%) in Gombe and 36.4% (Female 26.3%, Male 45.1%) in Mityana; while they reduced by 26.7% (Female 28.8%, Male 25.5%) in Luwero and by 15.4% (Female + 23.9%, Male − 31.8%) in Kiboga. The baseline case notification rate was 246/100,000 population. Post-intervention analysis demonstrated that the intervention significantly increased TB case notification rates (RR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.03–2.22), and males had a higher notification rate than females (RR: 1.5, 95% CI: 1.23–1.83). The treatment initiation rates pre- and post-intervention ranged between 97.4% and 100% in women, and 91.4% to 97.7% in men.ConclusionsGender-specific interventions for screening people with TB and linking them to care can be mutually beneficial at improving notifications among men and women.Trial registrationThe trial was registered retrospectively on the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR202509682831484) on 29th September 2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28658-6.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is preventable, treatable and curable, yet it is the leading cause of infectious disease death [1]. In 2024, the estimated number of people who developed TB globally was 10.7 million, of whom 2.4 million were not reached by available TB care services [1]. TB is a disease deeply entrenched in social and economic inequalities, disproportionately affecting populations in low- and middle-income countries. Socioeconomic factors such as poverty, food insecurity and overcrowded housing are strongly associated with both the risk of TB infection and adverse treatment outcomes [2–5].

Substantial evidence, including from TB prevalence surveys and global TB reports, indicate that men bear a higher TB burden compared to women, are less likely to access diagnostic and treatment services, and constitute the majority of undiagnosed or untreated cases, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia [6–9]. With an average of 2.6 prevalent cases per notified man with TB compared to 1.6 in women [7], men are estimated to account for about two-thirds of all individuals with TB who are missed by care services [8, 10]. As a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, it is estimated that 68% more men missed out on care compared to women in 2020 [11].

Gender influences healthcare access. Childbearing and women’s role as caregivers in the family increases their overall contact with health services primarily through antenatal care and immunisation services [12, 13. Conversely, gender norms associated with masculinity often frame illness or healthcare seeking as unmanly [14–16], leading some men to downplay symptoms [17]. While women may recognise and report multiple, usually non-specific symptoms, men are more likely to minimise or overlook early signs of illness, typically delaying care until more alarming and visible indicators such as blood in sputum appear, prompting health seeking at more advanced stages of disease [13, 18–20. Men often forego healthcare due to long waiting times at busy public health facilities, which may conflict with their work obligations and income-earning priorities [8, 21]. These gendered factors intersect with other social stratifiers to predispose men to TB. Men, for example, have more social contact with people with TB [22], especially from congregate settings such as prisons [23] or mines [24]. Tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption, both more prevalent among men than women [25, 26], increase the risk of TB, poor treatment outcomes and may mask the physical signs of TB, leading to delayed healthcare-seeking [3, 27–30].

Ending TB is a key global health priority [31–33], necessitating identification of people with TB who are missed by routine care services using patient-centred approaches to stop onward transmission within communities [34, 35]. In a year, it is estimated that a single individual with pulmonary TB can infect 5 to 15 people and models of social contacts attribute more than half of these infections to contact with adult men [34]. The 2018 United Nations High Level Meeting on TB called for recognition that men have a higher prevalence of TB [32]. The second meeting in 2023 reinforced and expanded global commitments to enhance TB care access, including explicit recognition of the need for gender-responsive approaches and strategies to reach men and other underserved groups [33]. Systematic screening for TB is an essential public health strategy aimed at identifying individuals with TB early, particularly among high burden populations, including populations with limited access to TB care services or those at elevated risk [36]. This study aimed to evaluate a co-created health facility-based TB screening intervention [37] designed to increase TB notifications by improving access to TB screening and care services among men over a one-year study period.

Methods

Study setting

This work is part of the wider IGNITE (Improving TB case detection using a GeNder-specific TB screening InTErvention in urban public health facilities in Uganda) study within the LIGHT consortium (Leaving no-one behInd; transforming Gendered pathways to health for HIV & TB) [37, 38]. IGNITE was a sequential exploratory mixed methods study [39] employing both quantitative methods (quasi-experimental multiple time series study) and qualitative methods (participatory workshops with intervention co-creation process, observation and key informant interviews).

We purposively selected general hospitals serving a peri-urban population, located at least 50 km from the capital city of Kampala, as health facilities within it were already implementing facility-based case finding strategies. These hospitals were located along different major highways leading out of Kampala to avoid overlapping catchment areas and had similar TB notification rates in 2021. This ensured a balanced comparison of facilities in terms of TB caseload while targeting distinct populations at each site and minimising external influences from ongoing interventions. The selected study sites were Gombe General Hospital and Mityana General Hospital, which functioned as intervention sites, and Luwero General Hospital and Kiboga General Hospital, as control sites. The hospitals vary in bed capacity, ranging from 100 to 150 beds, and primarily cater to peri-urban populations whose livelihoods are predominantly reliant on subsistence agriculture [40].

Study design

The objective of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of a health facility-based TB screening intervention targeting men in increasing the number of people with TB notified, disaggregated by sex. The study employed a quasi-experimental design with a before-and-after comparison at the intervention sites, complemented by concurrent observations at control facilities to account for temporal trends unrelated to the intervention [41]. The study period was 1st January to 31st December 2023 in all facilities. The initial six months, from 1st January to 30th June, served as the control period, and we implemented the intervention from 1st July to 31st December. Although blinding was not undertaken, data collection in the control facilities began after the intervention period, on 15th January 2024, to minimise the risk of contamination or behavioural changes among healthcare workers that could have arisen from awareness of the intervention. The data base was locked on 29th June 2024.

Study intervention

We adopted the UK’s Medical Research Council framework for developing complex interventions [42]. Results from the co-creation process, which utilised participatory methods among healthcare workers, TB survivors, policymakers, and researchers to develop the IGNITE intervention, are published elsewhere [37]. We reviewed the proposed components against established theories of healthcare access [43] and literature on gender-specific strategies to refine its operational components. The study intervention included male-friendly services, enhanced systematic provider-initiated TB screening at all service delivery points, and the distribution of information, education, and communication materials on TB to address male-specific barriers to TB care access. These are summarised in Table 1, matched with key dimensions and individual capabilities of access outlined in Levesque’s framework of access to healthcare [43].

Summary of intervention components

Male-friendly services

The intervention adopted strategies proven to enhance male involvement in healthcare settings [44–47]. This included establishing men’s corners where male TB survivors acted as male champions. The TB focal person at the intervention facilities identified a male TB survivor, already volunteering at the facility, to act as the male champion. The male champion participated in the intervention training described below. Each facility also designated a space for a men’s corner, near the TB clinic. Men presumed or diagnosed with TB referred to the TB clinic were then directed to the men’s corner for health education with the male champion, covering topics such as sexual reproductive health, healthy lifestyles, TB, and HIV. To accommodate work schedules and increase male engagement, clinic operating hours were extended to up to 6:30 pm on weekdays. All people presumed to have TB were informed about the average turnaround times for sputum results and the extended service hours of the clinic, enabling them to schedule return visits effectively [48 ,49].

Men often cite long waiting times, multiple visits, and the need to navigate several service points as barriers to care [37, 50]. Therefore, the TB clinics offered integrated services to make TB care services attractive, promote wellness and reduce waiting times. They offered provider-initiated HIV testing and counselling, as per current guidelines [51], complemented by comprehensive clinical examinations that included measurements of vital signs like blood pressure. People seeking care were also screened for diabetes. A lay healthcare worker (HCW) offered a random finger-prick blood glucose test to those who were diagnosed with TB, had symptoms suggestive of diabetes, or were aged at least 40 years and had risk factors such as obesity, physical inactivity and family history of diabetes. People with elevated blood pressure or random blood sugar readings, as well as those with HIV, were linked to available care services within the health facility for confirmatory tests and further management. TB treatment was initiated for all people with TB.

Systematic provider-initiated TB screening

Active case finding tools developed by the National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Programme (NTLP) were distributed to the health facilities, including the TB facility-based case finding guide and stamps to 10 departments at each facility to support the systematic identification of individuals presumed to have TB for further evaluation. The facility-based case finding stamps, shown in Additional file 1, summarise symptomatic TB screening steps into a checklist. Lay or professional HCW workers used the stamps on the records of people seeking care to promote and track TB screening. Individuals presumed to have TB were further evaluated by health facility staff using the national TB screening, diagnosis, and management algorithm, which included chest X-rays, sputum and urine testing [52, 53]. Digital X-rays machines within the facilities were also used to screen for TB.

Information, education and communication material

We leveraged established communication structures to boost awareness, demand, and uptake of TB and male-friendly services through information, education, and communication materials. Cough monitors are trained volunteers, stationed within health facilities, particularly in outpatient and inpatient departments, where they screen people seeking care for cough or other TB-related symptoms and refer them for further evaluation using the national TB diagnostic algorithm. They also support documentation and follow-up of people under care within the facility. In the community, trained volunteer HCWs known as village health teams (VHTs) identify individuals with symptoms suggestive of TB, provide health education, and facilitate their referral to health facilities for screening. They also support treatment adherence and contact tracing. For this study, routine health education was delivered using cough monitors within the facility and VHTs in the community. Informational materials on TB and available male-friendly services included a banner at the hospital entrance and TB clinic, as well as flyers based on the NTLP’s ‘TB GUIDE AT HOME’. Flyers were distributed within the health facility and community by cough monitors and VHTs. Individuals within the facility or community who reported TB-like symptoms, requested additional information, or mentioned knowing someone, particularly men, with TB-like symptoms were given the informational materials.

Intervention training

In July 2023, ahead of intervention implementation, key HCWs needed to implement the intervention successfully were mapped out with the facility’s TB focal person. At each facility, we identified 30 HCWs to ensure representation from each hospital department, health educators, staff working at the TB clinic, hospital administration and district leadership, all of whom were trained on active case finding. Additionally, we identified eight HCWs from each facility who would have the primary responsibility of providing male-friendly services and distributing the information, education, and communication materials. These included the TB focal person, two cough monitors, a male TB champion, a radiographer, a laboratory technician, and 2 VHTs. These HCWs received additional training on male-friendly services and were each given a facilitation allowance of UGX 100,000 (approximately USD 27.8) per month from July to December 20,233.

Study population

The unit of assignment into intervention and control arms was the health facility. The study population consisted of all people seeking care at the sites during the one-year study period. Additional sociodemographic, cost, and health-related quality of life data were collected from all newly diagnosed people with TB at the intervention sites who were aged ≥ 18 years and were able and willing to provide informed consent during the intervention implementation period.

Sample size and sampling procedure

We conducted a simulation-based power analysis by estimating the power of the study across a range of potential effect sizes, defined as the relative increase in monthly TB notifications. We explored effect sizes ranging from 1.1 to 1.5, in increments of 0.025, with a baseline notification rate of 23 TB notifications per month, based on facility data. We modelled for two intervention sites and two control sites with six timepoints before and six timepoints after the intervention, totalling 12 timepoints per site. We modelled notifications using a quasi-Poisson distribution to estimate the power for each effect size by running 10,000 simulations, assessed for statistically significant effects. At an effect size of 1.325, the study had 75.8% power, and at an effect size of 1.350, the power was 82.1%. Therefore, the study achieved 80% power to detect an effect when the cumulative increase in TB notifications was approximately 34%. We show the plot of power as a function of the effect size in Additional file 1.

Data collection

Across intervention and control sites, all people presumed to have TB were systematically line-listed from the presumptive TB register and subsequent diagnostic investigations were cross-verified using entries in the TB laboratory register. Treatment records for people notified with TB were compiled from the Unit TB register to create a single record for each person. Records of people with TB in the Unit TB register that could not be matched to a record in the presumptive TB register by name or record number were also entered. Participants were categorised by sociodemographic variables such as sex and age according to routine health system records at the point of patient registration. Socio-demographic and behavioural data collected from newly diagnosed people with TB included occupation, education, employment, marital status, cigarette smoking, alcohol use and socioeconomic status (SES) measured an asset index for wealth [54]. Research assistants collected this data on password-protected Android tablets with access to the project’s Data Management System created in REDCap. Real-time validation checks were programmed in the database. Data was uploaded daily with weekly data quality checks to validate responses and check for consistency.

We obtained routinely collected data on hospital attendance, TB screening, and quarterly TB notifications between 2020 and 2022 from the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS 2), an electronic Health Management Information System. We obtained sex disaggregated data from DHIS 2 when available. Facilities used varied methods to estimate their catchment populations; therefore, we estimated this from published district population projections [55, 56] adjusted by the facility’s proportional contribution to TB notifications in that district. These were absolute values for the recorded number of people notified with TB from each facility, divided by the district’s overall notification during a financial year, obtained from DHIS 2. We performed a sensitivity analysis in which the catchment population was adjusted by the proportional facility attendance.

Data analysis

The primary outcome variable of the study was the sex-stratified number of people with TB notified. Secondary outcome variables include sex-stratified proportions along the TB care cascade i.e., the proportions of those screened among those eligible, identified as presumptive TB among those screened, evaluated for TB among those identified as presumptive TB, notified with TB among those evaluated and initiated on treatment among those notified with TB. The unit of analysis were individuals. The intervention was a between-participants factor, while other independent variables were within-participants factors.

We used descriptive statistics to summarise the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of people presumed to have TB and those with TB. Age was categorised because the relationship between TB and age is non-linear, peaking at 25–34 years both globally and in Uganda [57]. All records had complete data on TB notification and sex. For other variables such as age or HIV status, participants with missing data were excluded from analyses involving stratification. No imputation was performed, and these individuals were included in overall TB notification counts. Missingness was assumed to be at random after assessing its distribution across facilities. Socioeconomic inequities were assessed by constructing an SES index and categorising participants into quintiles using existing national scores for urban populations54. Differences in SES distribution between men and women were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Overall deviation of the SES distribution from an equal quintile distribution was assessed using a chi-square goodness-of-fit test.

We used a quasi-Poisson model for count regression to estimate the effect of the intervention on TB notification rates, adjusting for the intervention, sex and facility, offset by the catchment population disaggregated by sex. Exploratory analyses were conducted to assess whether the data met the assumptions of quasi-Poisson regression models. TB notification counts followed an over dispersed Poisson distribution, which justified the use of a quasi-Poisson regression model to account for overdispersion. We interpolated the district population for 2022 and 2023 to calculate monthly catchment populations per facility. As the catchment population data were only disaggregated by facility and sex, we only included these independent variables in the model. To assess seasonality across years, the temporal trends in quarterly TB notifications by facility from 2020 to 2023 were also assessed. Corresponding to a 95% confidence interval, we interpreted p-values < 0.05 as showing that the corresponding variable is significantly associated with TB notification. We analysed all data using RStudio, 2023.06.1, Build 524 for Windows (© 2009–2023 Posit Software, PBC, Boston, Massachusetts, US) [58]. We used the TREND Checklist in Additional file 2 for reporting non-randomised evaluations of public health interventions [59].

Results

Descriptive statistics

Across the study sites, a total of 6,415 people were presumed to have TB over the study period, 53.3% of whom were female (3,419) as shown in Table 2. All facilities had a similar trend except Kiboga, where 52.0% of those presumed to have TB were male. The median age of people presumed to have TB was 35 (IQR 21–50), and the median age was comparable across facilities, ranging from 30 years (IQR 14–46) in Kiboga to 37 years (IQR 22–55) in Gombe. Across all facilities, the median age for males was higher than females. Adults aged 25–34 constituted the largest age group among people presumed to have TB in Gombe, Mityana and Luwero, ranging from 16.6% to 20.3% while in Kiboga, the highest proportion of people presumed to have TB were children under 15 years, comprising 26.5%. HIV co-infection among those presumed to have TB was high, particularly in Mityana, where nearly half (48.6%) were HIV positive compared to Gombe (28.5%), Luwero (32.8%), and Kiboga (29.3%). The HIV positivity rate among females was consistently higher across all facilities. Across the facilities, more people entered TB care from the OPD and TB units.

Intervention sites: Gombe and Mityana

The number of people completing each step of the TB cascade increased post-intervention. Hospital attendance increased in both facilities. The number of people screened after the intervention increased for both males and females in Gombe, while in Mityana, the number of females screened increased, while that of males decreased. A higher percentage of men than women were presumed to have TB and notified with TB in both facilities across both periods. Comparing the pre-intervention to post-intervention period, the number of people notified with TB increased by 40.2% (214 to 300), with increases higher amongst males (61.4%, 114 to 184) than females (16%, 100 to 116).

In Gombe, there was a 46.3% (Female 0, Male 88.4%) increase in people notified with TB from 82 (Female 39, Male 43) to 120 (Female 39, Male 81). Postintervention, Gombe showed a 6.2% increase in total attendance from 34,400 to 36,522, with an increase in the screening rate from 57.4% to 74%. Although the percentage of people presumed to have TB remained constant at 3.7%, there was an increase in the diagnosis rate from 11.2% to 12.1% among those presumed. The treatment initiation rate decreased from 98.8% to 93.3% post-intervention.

In Mityana, there was a 36.4% (Female 26.2%, Male 45.1%) increase in people notified with TB from 132 (Female 61, Male 71) to 180 (Female 77, Male 103). While Mityana had a 10.9% increase in the recorded facility attendance from 50,901 to 56,452, the screening rate and presumption rate decreased from 77.4% to 72.3% and 2.2% to 2.0% respectively. Conversely, the diagnosis rate rose from 16.9% to 22.8% postintervention. The treatment initiation rate remained high during both periods at 97.7% and 97.8%.

Control sites: Luwero and Kiboga

In the control sites, the facility attendance and screening numbers increased overall. Both Luwero and Kiboga had an increase in the number of males and females screened, with the least increase observed among females in Luwero. However, the number of people completing subsequent steps in the TB cascade decreased during the intervention period. Males accounted for a higher proportion of people presumed to have TB and had a higher diagnostic yield in both facilities across both periods. Overall, the number of people notified with TB decreased by 21.1% (Female 5.7%, Male 28.8%) from 317 (Female 105, Male 212) to 250 (Female 99, Male 151).

In Luwero, there was a 26.7% (Female 28.8%, Male 25.5%) decrease in people notified with TB from 161 (Female 59, Male 102) to 118 (Female 42, Male 76). In the pre- and post-intervention periods, the facility exhibited similar attendance figures (57,554 versus 57,984) and screening rates (87.0% versus 88.9%). The TB presumption rate was low during both periods, at 1.8% in the pre-intervention period and 1.5% post-intervention period. Diagnosis rates dropped in the post-intervention period from 18.4% to 16.0%. Treatment initiation rates were high, improving from 97.5% to 99.2% in the post-intervention period.

In Kiboga, there was a 15.4% (Female + 23.9%, Male − 31.8%) decrease in people notified with TB from 156 (Female 46, Male 110) to 132 (Female 57, Male 75). In the post-intervention period, attendance increased by 4.2% from 54,741 to 57,026, with the screening rate rising from 76.2% to 86.1%. Although the presumption rate decreased from 1.8% to 1.3%, the diagnosis rate increased from 20.3% to 21.2%. The treatment initiation rate was 97.4% in the pre-intervention period and 98.5% in the post-intervention period. A plot of monthly notifications by sex and facility is shown in Fig. 1.

Socioeconomic characters of people with TB

Of the 300 people with TB notified at the intervention sites during the intervention period, 24 were under 18 years of age (6 in Gombe and 18 in Mityana), 83 declined or could not provide consent (29 in Gombe and 54 in Mityana), and 193 were enrolled for the collection of additional SES data (85 in Gombe and 108 in Mityana). Sociodemographic characteristics of the enrolled adults with TB are presented in Table S.2 in Additional file 1. Risk behaviours for TB showed marked sex differences: smoking and alcohol use were almost exclusively reported by men, with alcohol use notably higher in Mityana, where nearly half of participants reported alcohol consumption and men reported frequent use. Participants reported a mean of 3.4 (SD 2.1) alcohol use days per week, with a 95% confidence interval of 2.9–4.0 days while the mean smoking days were 5.5 (SD 1.9), with a 95% CI of 4.6–6.4. Educational attainment was generally low, with primary education being most common and very few participants having tertiary education, especially among men. Livelihoods were dominated by informal employment and agriculture across both sites, with pronounced gendered patterns; women were more likely to report household work, while men were more commonly engaged in agriculture, sales, and production or construction work. Most participants were informally employed, underscoring economic vulnerability. Marital status differed by site and sex, with higher proportions of divorced or separated individuals in Mityana and a greater proportion of widowed women in Gombe.

An assessment by SES in Table 4 showed that among women with TB, 22.2% were in the poorest socioeconomic quintile compared with 16.5% of men, while a slightly higher proportion of men were in the richest quintile (8.3% vs. 5.6%). The distribution of socioeconomic status did not differ significantly by sex (p = 0.846). However, the overall distribution of TB cases across SES quintiles deviated significantly from an equal distribution, with a greater concentration of cases in the middle and lower quintiles (χ² = 27.65, df = 4, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This assessment examined the effectiveness of a gender-specific intervention designed to address barriers faced by men with TB, who are often missed by routine TB care services [6, 9, 60]. The intervention included targeted health education, peer engagement and male-friendly services as critical strategies for increasing men’s participation in TB care [7].

The results show that overall, the intervention significantly increased the TB case notification rate by 51% (RR 1.51, 95% CI 1.03–2.22, p = 0.038). The overall increases in TB notifications further underscored that: Gombe’s TB notifications rose by 46.3%, driven entirely by male notifications, while Mityana reported a 36.4% increase across both genders, with the increase among males almost double that among females. Comparatively, control facilities experienced a noticeable decline in TB case notifications during the post-intervention period, which was more pronounced among men. The plot of quarterly TB notifications from 2020 to 2022 showed similar seasonal and contextual trends across facilities. In 2023, upward trends in quarterly notifications were noted in Gombe and Mityana only post-intervention. These findings imply that the increases observed in the intervention sites occurred despite a broader temporal decline in TB notifications, indicating that the intervention may have counteracted this downturn, potentially by improving care-seeking behaviour or case detection, particularly among men. Although previous research in Uganda indicates that seasonality has a limited effect on overall TB notifications [61], the fluctuations observed in the monthly notification trends may nevertheless reflect seasonal variation, with higher numbers typically occurring during the dry seasons. Their perceived role and other contextual factors affecting notifications were explored qualitatively and will be published separately.

The analysis also highlighted gendered trends. In Gombe and Mityana, which implemented the intervention, male notification rates increased, with a statistically significant rise observed in Gombe (RR 1.84, 95% CI 1.28–2.69, p = 0.001) and a borderline increase in Mityana (RR 1.32, 95% CI 0.98–1.80, p = 0.07). In contrast, the male notification rates declined significantly in control facilities (Luwero RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.97, p = 0.03; Kiboga RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53–0.95, p = 0.021). These results suggest that the intervention increased notifications with some gendered effects among the targeted male population. Previous studies have emphasised the need for targeted, context-specific strategies to engage men in care, such as tailoring service hours, messaging content, and engagement strategies to the realities of men’s lives and work responsibilities7. Given the documented underutilisation of health services by men [15, 49, 62–64], these findings likely reflect the efficacy of the targeted efforts to overcome barriers to healthcare access. However, after adjusting for other factors, the interaction term for the intervention’s effect and male sex showed no association.

Overall, these findings suggest that this gender-specific approach, while primarily designed to address barriers faced by men, can lead to broader improvements in TB care that benefit both men and women. This could partly explain why, although trends for improved notifications among men were observed, this did not demonstrate a statistically significant interaction between intervention and sex. Relatively few studies report on gender-specific interventions for TB in sufficient detail to establish whether this dual benefit is common. Where data is available from other programs, interventions designed to engage women, such as antenatal based strategies or maternal health programmes, often demonstrate less spillover benefits for men [65]. Furthermore, several components of the intervention reflected broader improvements in quality of care rather than being gender-specific, and this likely contributed to the observed benefits among women as well. Thus, combining male-focused strategies with broader improvements in service delivery can be mutually beneficial for both men and women, ultimately enhancing access and promoting equity. Future studies can also explore the mechanisms through which gender-specific strategies produce system-wide benefits, including whether adaptations designed for men indirectly improve service delivery for women.

Similar improvements in TB notifications and notification rates have been observed in intervention studies. One study in Northern Uganda that combined quality improvement with active case finding strategies observed quarterly TB notifications improved by 30.4% from 171 to 223 per 100,000 population over a 6-month period [66]. In Zambia, active case finding activities implemented in a health facility and its catchment area observed an increase in TB notification rates by 60.6% from 246 per 100,000 population to 394 per 100,000 population in the last year of the intervention [67]. This study, however, differed from the current study in that almost equal numbers of the 18,194 people screened were from the health facility and its catchment community (facility: 9,846 (54.1%), community: 8,348 (45.9%)). Another active case finding intervention study based in 725 health facilities observed a 9% increase in TB notification rates two years post-intervention compared to pre-intervention rates (RR = 1.09, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.12) [68]. Although some elements of the TB cascade were disaggregated by sex, the effects of the interventions on notifications were not sex disaggregated in the studies reviewed. There is an urgent need for such retrospective analyses on existing data and consideration of gender-disaggregation in future study designs.

People with TB in this study comprised of socioeconomically disadvantaged adults with TB, characterised by high HIV burden, gendered livelihood patterns, low SES and male exposure to behavioural risk factors for TB. The HIV clinic came second only to the OPD as a care entry point for people presumed to have TB. Furthermore, the HIV burden among people presumed to have TB and those with TB ranged from 28.5% to 69.8%%, higher than the national prevalence of 5.8% (Females: 7.2%, Males: 4.3%) [69] and the TB/HIV co-infection rate of 26.6% [70]. This is in line with global recommendations for routine TB screening among people living with HIV, a population with heightened vulnerability to TB [3].

The TB care cascade data highlights some improvements in TB screening and diagnosis, particularly among men, following the intervention. The odds of TB notifications among men were 50% higher compared to women presumed to have TB. These findings are similar to other studies [7], underscoring men’s vulnerability to TB due to higher rates of exposure and delayed healthcare seeking behaviours [71]. Post-intervention data revealed that while screening rates increased in Gombe, Kiboga and Luwero, there was a decline in Mityana. Gombe, which had the lowest screening rate of 57.4% increased to 74.0% post-intervention, while Mityana, which had the highest screening rate of 77.4% experienced a slight decline to 72.3% post-intervention. This suggests that the intervention is most effective in settings where baseline screening rates are low. All facilities, except for Luwero in the post-intervention period, failed to meet the 100% TB screening target established by the NTLP for people seeking care at OPDs. Overall, females constituted a higher percentage of those presumed to have TB compared to males across most facilities. This trend may be attributed to women’s greater access to or utilisation of healthcare services [62], driven by their involvement in ANC, immunisation, and the care of sick family members, especially children [12]. Women also have increased vulnerability to intersecting health issues such as HIV[69], which was notably more prevalent among females than males, especially in Mityana. However, the unique pattern observed in Kiboga, where males had a higher engagement with TB-related health services, suggests the influence of local socioeconomic and cultural factors, prompting further examination. Additionally, Kiboga had a high proportion of children presumed to have TB, which may indicate recent household or close-contact transmission and possible missed adult TB cases [72], underscoring the need to further explore these patterns using age-disaggregated data in future research.

Furthermore, differences in outcomes between facilities underscore the need for process evaluation to understand how the intervention delivered its impact to guide localised strategies. For instance, Mityana exhibited a higher increase in TB notification rates post-intervention compared to Gombe, suggesting variability in how intervention strategies were received or implemented. Whereas the facility attendance was relatively stable across the before and after periods in Gombe, the number of people screened increased from 19,751 to 27,019, suggesting that the intervention’s effect was primarily on screening. In Mityana, whereas both the facility attendance and number of people screened increased, the number of people presumed to have TB reduced from 862 to 797, but the number of people notified increased from 132 to 180, suggesting improvements in effective identification of people presumed to have TB. In the logistic regression analysis, the odds of being notified with TB in Mityana were 1.74 times those in Gombe, supporting this finding. These differences may necessitate adjustments to the intervention, tailored to the specific social and operational context of each facility. We undertook a process evaluation assessment [73] to explore the intervention’s implementation processes and underlying contextual factors. Participatory action research methods were also used to explore contextual nuances in the findings and will be published separately. Cost-effectiveness assessments are integral to the evaluation of active case finding interventions [74] and the IGNITE study will evaluate the cost-effectiveness of this intervention package. These results will be published separately.

Limitations

There were some limitations to the study. Firstly, intervention and control sites were not randomly selected, which could introduce selection bias and affect the generalisability of the results. Despite this, the quasi-experimental design enabled the examination of the intervention’s effect while controlling for external influences that could impact outcomes. Another key limitation of the Poisson regression analysis is that we only adjusted for sex, as catchment population data were disaggregated by sex alone. However, TB risk is influenced by a range of socioeconomic and biological factors, including age, HIV status, income level, and other contextual determinants [3]. Future evaluations would benefit from more granular population data, allowing for a more comprehensive adjustment of relevant risk factors. The regression model assumed that the effect of each facility is constant across all observations within that facility. Without accounting for within-facility correlation as a random effect, there may be some bias in the estimates. However, with only four clusters, the trade-off between bias and the reliability of random effects estimation favoured using fixed effects.

Conclusion

This gender-specific intervention targeting men successfully enhanced TB screening, diagnosis, and treatment initiation rates among men, a group traditionally underserved in TB care, with mutually beneficial improvements to notifications among women. The findings support the continuation and expansion of gender-specific interventions, which could include further customisation to address local needs and continuous evaluation to refine and optimise impact. This approach not only aids in controlling TB among high-risk populations but also contributes to the broader public health goal of reducing TB transmission in the community.
