The Utility of the Functional Sphincter Area: A Novel Functional Lumen Imaging Probe Parameter for the Assessment of Anal Sphincter Structure and Function in Fecal Incontinence
Authors: Alexander O’Connor, Donghua Liao, Matthew Davenport, Abhiram Sharma, Dipesh H Vasant, Asbjørn M Drewes, Edward Kiff, John McLaughlin, Karen Telford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsThe functional lumen imaging probe (FLIP) is a unique tool to measure the anal sphincter during distension. Using detailed geometric data generated by FLIP, we present the functional sphincter area (FSA), a novel measurement of sphincter morphology with potential clinical utility in the investigation of fecal incontinence (FI).MethodsParous female patients with FI presenting to a tertiary pelvic floor unit were prospectively recruited. FLIP measurements were obtained alongside endoanal ultrasound and high-resolution anorectal manometry.ResultsThirty-one parous female patients with FI (median age 62) were recruited. On endoanal ultrasound, 14 (45.2%) were found to have a sphincter defect in either the external sphincter (n = 10), or in both the external and internal sphincter (n = 4). The FSA demonstrated a consistent pattern of change during distension of the FLIP bag in all patients. At rest, the FSA was greater in those without anal sphincter defects at 30 mL (72.6 vs 53.2 mm2; P = 0.029) and 40 mL (71.5 vs 45.5 mm2; P = 0.048) bag volumes. The same pattern was observed during voluntary squeeze at 30 mL (67.8 vs 42.8 mm2; P = 0.044) and 40 mL (61.2 vs 38.6 mm2; P = 0.040) bag volumes. By contrast, there were no differences in symptom severity or manometry measurements between the 2 groups.ConclusionsThe FSA may offer a clinically useful parameter and, in combination with other FLIP measurements, could become a useful tool to assess the structure and function of the anal canal in FI.Keywords: Anal canal, Fecal incontinence, Manometry, Pelvic floor disorders

INTRODUCTION

Fecal continence is maintained by coordinated interactions between the anorectum, the anal sphincter, and the pelvic floor orchestrated by central and peripheral neural activities.1 Deficiencies in these components can lead to fecal incontinence (FI), a devastating condition affecting 8% of the population.2,3 However, anal sphincter dysfunction is considered a principal cause, particularly following obstetric or iatrogenic injury.4,5 High-resolution anorectal manometry (HRAM) is the established test of sphincter function.6 However, HRAM uses fixed-diameter catheters to measure the pressure generated by the anal sphincter muscle under isometric conditions. This may explain why assessing sphincter function with HRAM only demonstrates a limited association with the severity of anal sphincter defects in patients with FI.7

The functional lumen imaging probe (FLIP) is a unique tool to assess the resistance of the anal sphincter during distension,6,8-10 which has been proposed as the main determinant of its function. 11-13 It generates a dynamic geometric profile of the whole anal canal at different distension volumes with the ability to measure the cross-sectional area (CSA) over an 8 cm length in combination with pressure measurements.8 In doing so, FLIP can examine the length-tension relationship of the sphincter muscle to describe its function and has therefore been proposed as an alternative tool to HRAM.9,14 Furthermore, new metrics, such as the functional sphincter area (FSA), can now be extracted from the traditional FLIP measurements. We therefore hypothesised that this advanced FLIP measurement of the anal canal could be used to identify differences in sphincter function between patients with anal sphincter defects and those without. If so, this could be used to support the use of FLIP in the investigation of FI and address the limitations currently experienced with HRAM.

This study of FI patients describes the FSA as a novel FLIP parameter to assess the anal sphincter in those with and without evidence of anal sphincter defects. In addition, conventional FLIP and HRAM parameters were explored in the same patients. The primary aim is to investigate if differences exist in measurements with these devices between parous female patients with and without anal sphincter defects.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This prospective observational study received approval from the local research ethics committee (Greater Manchester West, United Kingdom; Ref: 19/NW/0633). Patients with FI attending the pelvic floor unit were identified prospectively and were invited to take part in this study. Patients were excluded if they were under the age of 18, lacked cognitive capacity to provide informed consent, were pregnant, or had active inflammatory bowel disease or a history of anorectal cancer. All patients provided written informed consent.

The severity of FI was assessed using the Vaizey incontinence score.15 A score of 0 indicates no incontinence symptoms, whilst the maximum score of 24 suggests severe FI symptoms. The impact of FI on quality of life was assessed with the Manchester Health Questionnaire.16 Scores range from 0, indicating no impact, to 900 indicating the most severe impact on an individual.

Endoanal ultrasound (EAUS) investigations (3D 9052 Transducer, BK Medical, MA, USA) were performed by a consultant radiologist with an interest in pelvic floor dysfunction. Patients were considered to have an anal sphincter defect if there was evidence of previous sphincter trauma on EAUS including the presence of a scar, or sphincter disruption in either the external anal sphincter (EAS) or internal anal sphincter (IAS) or both (Fig. 1). The standardized London protocol was used for HRAM measurements17 with a 14 Fr water-perfused catheter containing 10 circumferential pressure sensors spaced at 8 mm intervals (Mui Scientific, ON, Canada). Mean resting pressure, maximum squeeze pressure, and incremental squeeze pressure were recorded in mmHg. In addition, the functional anal canal length was also recorded, defined as the length of the anal canal in which pressure exceeded rectal pressure by > 5 mmHg, recorded in cm.18

Functional Lumen Imaging Probe Measurement

Analysis of the functional lumen imaging probe data

Data from the FLIP measurements were analyzed using a customized MATLAB subroutine (R2022a; Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA). The data can be used to construct profiles of the anal canal at any bag volume.22 A smoothed diameter distribution along the anal canal was obtained using one-dimensional interpolation at any time point (Fig. 2A). Parameters were calculated at the area of minimum CSA, including the yield pressure, closing pressure, pressure-strain elastic modulus, and distensibility index (DI) during rest and voluntary squeeze, which have been described previously. 9,20,21,23 In addition, the bag pressures at each bag volume during rest and maximum squeeze were used to calculate the increase in (incremental) bag pressure. Resting measurements were calculated as an averaged measurement from the final 10 seconds of the 30 second resting period, and the squeeze function was during the 10 second squeeze.

Measuring the Functional Sphincter Area

The pressure and diameters along the anal canal were analyzed during the initial state at the second distension to 10 mL, and at each bag volume of 20, 30, 40, and 50 mL (Fig. 2). At the initial state, the proximal and distal limits were defined at the points where the diameters clearly started to increase.19,22 Using these defined limits, the configuration of the anal canal can be determined using a technique that has previously been described with the proximal and distal anal canal defined as the points 75% of the distance from the middle to the proximal and distal limits.22 During every distension after the initial state, the middle of the anal canal was redefined as the middle of the narrow zone of the anal canal at half of the distension volume. Proximal and distal ends of the narrow zone were defined as the locations with CSA equal to 110% of the narrowest CSA (Fig. 2B). The proximal and distal anal canal points were also recalculated by assuming the proximal and distal anal canal lengths during distension are the same as that at the initial state (proximal anal canal length: length from the middle to the proximal limit of the anal canal; distal anal canal length: length from the middle to the distal limit of the anal canal). The FSA, measured in mm2, represents the area contained by the outside of the FLIP bag wall and the line drawn between the proximal and distal anal canal, termed the functional anal canal length (Fig. 2B). The FSA was considered to represent the area of tissue contained by the outside of the FLIP bag wall as it increases in volume. The FSA was measured at rest and during voluntary squeeze. The CSA was calculated from the radius of the FLIP bag measured from the center of the catheter to the wall of the bag.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Version 29.0 (IBM, NY, USA). Data are presented as median (interquartile range) unless otherwise stated. The Kruskal-Wallis test and the Mann-Whitney U test were used for comparisons between independent samples of non-parametric data. Statistical significance was considered at the P < 0.05 level. A formal sample size calculation was not possible given the exploratory nature of this first study to investigate a novel FLIP parameter.

The FLIP measurements were easy for the investigator to perform and were well tolerated by patients. However, in 1 patient, distension to 50 mL bag volume was not tolerated due to discomfort. There were no other adverse events related to the study investigation.

The Functional Sphincter Area

As the FLIP bag filled with conductive solution and the volume increased, the FSA changed in size. A consistent finding in all patients was an FSA that began to increase in size as the FLIP bag inflated from 10 to 50 mL bag volume. This increase in FSA continued until a point was reached where the minimum CSA started to increase in size, as the anal canal opened. The pressure observed at this point, as the narrowest part of the anal canal began to open, is commonly described as the ‘yield pressure’. As the anal canal continued to open, the FSA then decreased in size until the maximum bag volume was reached (50 mL) (Fig. 3). There was no difference in the median rate of change of the FSA between patients with and without an anal sphincter defect (2.02 [1.21-2.67] mm2/s vs 2.00 [1.82-2.73] mm2/s; P = 0.518). In all measurements, the yield pressure, or pressure at which the FSA began to decrease in size occurred between 30 to 50 mL bag volume.

Functional Sphincter Area Measurements in Anal Sphincter Defects

Of the 31 included patients, 17 (54.8%) had no evidence of a sphincter defect on EAUS, whilst 14 (45.2%) were found to have a sphincter defect in either the EAS in isolation (n = 10), or in both the EAS and IAS (n = 4). There were no differences between the groups with and without anal sphincter defects in median age (60 vs 63; P = 0.922), weight (69.5 vs 71.0 kg; P = 0.399), Vaizey score (14 vs 16; P = 0.173), Manchester Health Questionnaire score (459.17 vs 505.00; P = 0.830), parity (2 vs 2; P = 0.830), or vaginal deliveries (2 vs 2; P = 0.710).

The differences in FLIP measurements between the 2 groups are presented in Table 2. At rest, the FSA at 30 mL (72.6 vs 53.2 mm2; P = 0.029) and 40 mL (71.5 vs 45.5 mm2; P = 0.048) bag volumes were greater in those with an intact anal sphincter complex. At 50 mL bag volume, there was a trend towards a reduced FSA in patients with evidence of anal sphincter defects (40.5 vs 22.0 mm2; P = 0.101). Fig. 4 demonstrates an example of FSA recordings in two patients with and without an anal sphincter defect. With the exception of yield pressure (26.4 vs 17.6 mmHg; P = 0.040), there were no other differences in FLIP measurements at rest between the 2 groups (P > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

The anal sphincter complex is an important component of the continence mechanism,1 and its dysfunction from iatrogenic or obstetric injury is often implicated in the aetiology of FI in female patients.24 This prospective observational study of parous females describes a new dynamic parameter of the whole anal canal that may be of interest in the assessment of anal sphincter morphology, the functional sphincter area. This area was lower in patients with sphincter defects, indicating a reduced sphincter volume. In addition, the yield pressure is reduced in this group, indicating an anal canal that opens more easily at a reduced pressure. To date, most studies with FLIP have reported measurements of the anal canal obtained at a single point along its length; at the minimum measurable diameter (or cross-sectional area) at a particular static bag volume using the distensibility index.21,25 By contrast, the FSA combines geometric measurements from the whole anal sphincter to quantify the sphincter area and has identified reduced measurements in patients with anal sphincter defects. These findings may help to explain the mechanism behind FI in this group with a small volume anal sphincter, that opens at a lower pressure. No differences in HRAM parameters, including the functional anal canal length, were seen between patients with anal sphincter defects and those without.

FLIP provides a detailed geometric and pressure profile of the whole anal canal, and it has previously been used in the investigation of FI.21,22,26 The DI (minimum CSA/bag pressure; mm2/mmHg) is typically used to describe the resistance of the anal sphincter to distension and is calculated at the point of minimum CSA at a particular bag volume.10,27 Using this parameter alone, authors have described FLIP to be a complimentary tool to HRAM.26 This measurement taken at rest, or during voluntary squeeze, is unable to differentiate between patients with intact sphincters and varying degrees of sphincter injury.6 Indeed, the DI does not always correlate with the symptoms of FI.6 In this present study, no differences were seen in the DI between patients with and without sphincter defects. However, data can be downloaded from the FLIP device to generate additional parameters in post-acquisition analysis.19 These additional parameters seek to quantify the function of the anal canal during opening and closing. During inflation of a FLIP bag to 50 mL, the yield pressure has been described as the pressure at which the narrowest part of the anal canal just starts to open.19 This is lower in patients with FI,20 and those treated with neoadjuvant therapy for anal or rectal cancer.23 In this study, the yield pressure was reduced in patients with anal sphincter defects. It suggests that this parameter, measuring the resistance to distension of the anal canal, may offer additional clinical utility above that provided by DI alone at a static bag volume, and may help to explain the mechanism of FI in this group.

EAUS is the gold standard technique to assess the anatomy of the anal sphincter complex and, whilst it is a well-tolerated technique, it is invasive and requires the use of specialist equipment and experienced technicians.28 Its role in the routine investigation of FI is unclear and it is only recommended on a case-by-case basis, particularly where a sphincter repair is being considered, or to plan subsequent deliveries following obstetric trauma.29,30 Further work with FSA could see FLIP proposed as a first-line test prior to EAUS to provide both a functional and structural assessment of the anal sphincter complex in FI. In select patients, without suspicion of an anal sphincter defect, an additional EAUS investigation could therefore be avoided. Further work is now required to generate a normal dataset to inform the clinical utility and reliability of the FSA and other FLIP measurements in the assessment of FI. This should include whether the FSA can correlate with the severity of sphincter injury, including combined IAS and EAS injuries, and if it corresponds with patient reported symptoms of FI. It is acknowledged that the intra- and inter-rater reliability and repeatability of the novel parameters presented here have not currently been explored and should be the subject of future work in healthy controls and symptomatic patients. Indeed, when the reliability of HRAM was explored, the variation seen in repeated measurements of pelvic floor function affected the precision of the test, which could have implications in the clinical utility of these tools.31

This is the first study to use FLIP to quantify the ability of the whole anal sphincter to resist distension. However, in calculating the FSA, and indeed other geometric measurements with FLIP, it is assumed that the FLIP bag is cylindrical inside the anal canal. This has been demonstrated in healthy volunteers using a customized magnetic resonance compatible FLIP catheter,32 but remains to be demonstrated in symptomatic patients with a sphincter defect, thus representing a limitation of this study. In addition, this study is limited by its single center design with small overall numbers with 1 patient unable to tolerate distension to 50 mL bag volume. The use of a water-perfused HRAM device rather than a 3D solid-state catheter may also limit the ability of HRAM to detect difference between the 2 groups. EAUS remains operator dependent and despite the experienced radiologists in our institution there may be instances of missed external sphincter injuries in our study. Finally, the FLIP parameters described can currently only be generated in detailed post-acquisition analysis limiting the generalisability of the findings. Future work therefore is required to explore the accuracy of FLIP in identifying anal sphincter defects, with the generation of ‘normal’ data from healthy volunteers and in parous women without FI symptoms. If proven to be clinically useful, these additional parameters should be incorporated into the FLIP device to provide ‘real-time’ measurements as the bag inflates. This would avoid the need for detailed post-acquisition analysis which currently limits the routine clinical use of these measurements.

In conclusion, in patients with anal sphincter defects, the FSA measured with FLIP may offer a parameter of clinical utility. In combination with other measurements previously described, FLIP could be a valuable tool to assess the structure and function of the anal canal. Future work is now required to define the diagnostic utility of FLIP and its measured parameters in the assessment of FI and anal sphincter trauma.
