Brain-derived 5-hydroxymethylcytosine epigenetic scores are related to Alzheimer’s disease pathology and cognitive decline
Authors: Zhou Zhang, Ana W Capuano, Alana Beadell, Shinya Tasaki, Xiaolong Cui, Rupal I Mehta, David A Bennett, Chuan He, Wei Zhang, Zoe Arvanitakis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytosine modifications play critical roles in gene regulation and disease pathogenesis. Elucidating novel epigenetic contributions to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) could advance diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic strategies. 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), a stable and dynamic DNA modification, has emerging links to AD pathophysiology and potential uses as a biomarker. Cognitive decline, a hallmark of AD progression, varies across individuals and is not fully explained by classic pathological markers. Here, we aimed to develop and validate brain-derived 5hmC-based epigenetic scores to distinguish AD from non-AD pathology and examine their relationship to individual cognitive trajectories.MethodsGenome-wide 5hmC profiles were generated using 5hmC-Seal and next-generation sequencing on 1016 postmortem human brain prefrontal cortex samples from well-characterized, deceased participants in a longitudinal, clinical-pathologic research study on aging. Samples were processed in independent training and validation sets. Genomic features (e.g., gene bodies, enhancers) were summarized, followed by differential analysis and machine learning-based feature selection to construct classification models distinguishing AD from non-AD.ResultsAfter quality control and batch correction of 5hmC data, 1005 participants were included, with 655 classified as having AD and 350 as non-AD, according to NIA-AA neuropathologic criteria. In the training set (n = 859), 136 candidate gene bodies and 96 enhancers were selected based on variability and relaxed significance thresholds (p < 0.1). Pathway enrichment analyses implicated cardiovascular function, endocytosis, and MAPK signaling pathways. Using these features, we developed machine learning models that distinguished AD from non-AD with high performance in both the training set (AUC = 87.0%; 95% CI 84.2–89.7%) and validation set (n = 146; AUC = 91.4%; 95% CI 86.6–96.2%). Moreover, the resulting AD-score was significantly associated with rates of global and five domain-specific cognitive decline.ConclusionThis study extends prior work by translating brain 5hmC profiles into epigenetic scores that distinguish AD pathology and reflect individual cognitive trajectories. These findings highlight the potential of brain-derived 5hmC modifications as biomarkers for AD and as tools to advance research on disease progression, offering a new direction for epigenetics-informed clinical applications in AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00401-025-02950-y.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Cognitive decline, 5-Hydroxymethylcytosine, Epigenetics, Biomarker

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a common disease that affects approximately 7 million individuals in the United States and more than 55 million people worldwide, thus representing a major health burden globally [43]. Age is the most recognized risk factor for AD and AD-related dementia, among other risk factors such as family history and lifestyle factors [59]. Although recent large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 70 loci associated with AD risk [3], genetic factors alone do not fully explain the etiology of AD, with SNP-based heritability estimates suggesting that common genetic variation accounts for approximately 20–30% of AD risk [38]. Of note, there is growing scientific evidence that a healthy lifestyle, including dietary patterns, regular physical activity, cognitive engagement, and vascular risk management, may help delay or prevent the clinical expression of AD [36, 39]. Although pathological hallmarks, such as extracellular β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques and hyperphosphorylated intraneuronal paired helical filament tau-containing neurofibrillary tangles in the brain, have been proposed as biomarkers for AD, their clinical use requires positron emission tomography (PET) scan or biochemical analyses of biofluids such as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [7]. Moreover, while these biomarkers reflect underlying pathology, they do not fully capture the clinical heterogeneity or predict the trajectory of cognitive decline that characterizes the disease.

Cognitive decline is a hallmark clinical feature of AD, often preceding overt dementia by several years [48]. Accurately assessing cognitive decline is crucial for early diagnosis, prognosis, and will assist in the development of targeted interventions. Traditional biomarkers such as β-amyloid and tau pathology provide important diagnostic information but have limited utility, as of yet, in forecasting individual cognitive decline [4, 24]. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify novel molecular markers that are associated with both neuropathological changes and clinical cognitive trajectories.

While DNA methylation, specifically 5-methylcytosine (5mC), has been investigated in brain tissue samples for AD, most previous studies were limited by either sample size or constraints of the technical platforms employed [17, 50, 60]. In particular, the conventional DNA methylation profiling platforms, including the bisulfite conversion-based Illumina 450 K or 850 arrays, cannot distinguish 5mC from other major cytosine modification types, specifically 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), which has been shown to feature a distinct genomic distribution and gene regulatory roles from 5mC [14, 22, 54]. To address this technical gap, our team has implemented an innovative and robust epigenetic profiling technique, 5hmC-Seal, that uses covalent chemical labeling to specifically enrich for 5hmC-containing DNA fragments, followed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) [34, 47]. Our team and the research community have successfully applied the 5hmC-Seal technique in biomarker discovery for various human complex diseases, including cancers and diabetic complications [11, 20, 41, 55].

In our previous pilot study, taking advantage of the Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP) [6], we demonstrated the feasibility of detecting and characterizing 5hmC patterns in the circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA), focusing on a small group of individuals with and without diabetes mellitus, an important risk factor for AD dementia [2]. This work established the potential of 5hmC as a minimally invasive biomarker for AD risk stratification. More recently, a larger study using samples from the Religious Orders Study and the Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) [5, 6] profiled genome-wide 5hmC in postmortem brain tissue, generating an atlas of 5hmC modifications associated with AD pathology and offering insights into potential mechanisms underlying AD pathogenesis [61]. However, that work primarily focused on cross-sectional associations with neuropathologic features and did not develop classification models or investigate the relationship between 5hmC profiles and longitudinal cognitive data. As a result, important questions remain regarding the clinical utility of brain-derived 5hmC profiles for individual risk prediction and their association with cognitive trajectories.

In the present study, our objective was to advance the understanding of brain 5hmC variation in AD by developing and validating brain-derived 5hmC-based epigenetic scores that not only distinguish AD pathology but are also associated with cognitive decline, including in specific cognitive domains. We generated new genome-wide 5hmC profiles for 149 postmortem brain tissue samples and combined these with a reprocessed subset of 867 previously profiled samples [61] from ROSMAP [5, 6]. To ensure a unified analysis, all data were processed using standardized pipelines and normalization procedures. We performed differential analysis to identify AD-associated 5hmC features, conducted pathway enrichment analyses, and used machine learning-based feature selection to derive classification epigenetic scores. Finally, we evaluated the association between these 5hmC-based scores and longitudinal cognitive trajectories. Together, these findings build upon prior 5hmC studies and demonstrate the potential of brain 5hmC profiles as biomarkers for neuropathologic AD and as indicators of associations with clinical expression, including cognitive decline.

Methods

Data sources

The Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) [4] are longitudinal, epidemiologic clinical-pathologic cohort studies focused on common chronic conditions of aging, with an emphasis on cognitive decline and the risk of AD [5, 6]. For the current study, we collected postmortem frontal cortex samples from ROSMAP study participants of European ancestry. Participant data were obtained under standardized ROSMAP protocols approved by the Rush University Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) and include demographic variables (age, sex, years of education), technical covariates (postmortem interval), and clinical–pathologic measures—neuropathologic AD diagnosis [44, 52], APOE genotype [58], and previously published slopes of cognitive decline [15]. To improve clarity, in this study, “AD” was defined as the presence of Alzheimer’s disease pathology according to NIA‑AA criteria [23], and “non‑AD” was defined as the absence of such pathology.

Data on 5hmC from a total of 1117 brain samples from the ROSMAP [4] were available from a previously published study [61]. To ensure technical consistency between datasets, we restricted the analysis to samples generated with paired-end (PE) sequencing, matching the sequencing protocol used for the 149 newly profiled samples at the University of Chicago (UChicago). Thus, the final dataset included 1016 brain samples, consisting of 867 PE samples from the prior study [61] which formed the training set, and 149 newly generated PE samples from UChicago, which served as the independent validation set (Table 1). Following quality control, 11 samples with fewer than 100,000 total unique aligned 5hmC sequencing reads were excluded, leaving 1005 samples, consisting of 859 in the training set and 146 in the validation set for further analysis (Fig. 1). Among the total samples, 655 persons (65%) met the NIA-AA pathology criteria for AD [23].

The newly profiled samples at the University of Chicago were processed following the same ROSMAP standardized protocols. All procedures were approved by the Rush University Medical Center IRB, and the IRB approval also covers the UChicago-generated dataset.

Genome-wide profiling of 5hmC in genomic DNA

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from ~ 5 mg of fresh-frozen dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) brain tissue. For the UChicago batch, DNA was extracted from brain samples using the QIAamp Fast DNA Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and immediately stored at -80 °C, while for the prior study, gDNA was extracted using a phenol–chloroform protocol, as previously described [61]. All samples were processed in a random order within the set, and technicians were blinded to participant data. The detailed protocol for preparing 5hmC-Seal libraries has been described in our previous publications [19, 34, 47]. Briefly, 100 ng of gDNA per sample was enzymatically fragmented to 200–400 bp in size, and the fragments were ligated to Illumina-compatible adaptors. T4 bacteriophage β-glucosyltransferase was used to transfer an engineered glucose moiety containing an azide group to the hydroxyl group of 5hmC in the input DNA. The azide group was chemically coupled to biotin using a biotin-PEG-alkyne linker in a “Click chemistry” cycloaddition reaction. Streptavidin beads were used to specifically capture the 5hmC-containing DNA. Finally, the captured DNA was extensively washed, PCR-amplified, and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeqX machine for the validation set and the Illumina HiseqX platform for the training set. Although DNA extraction and sequencing platforms differed between sets, the technical robustness of the 5hmC-Seal approach in gDNA has been consistently demonstrated in our previous studies [19, 34]. Furthermore, potential batch effects related to extraction or sequencing differences were assessed and corrected using the COMBAT algorithm prior to downstream analysis [27].

Bioinformatic processing and summarization

The basic bioinformatic processing was aimed to provide a robust summarization of the genome-wide 5hmC data and facilitate data analysis/result interpretation, as described in our prior publication [34]. Briefly, the adapter sequences were removed from raw sequencing reads using Trimmomatic [8]. Low-quality bases at the 5' and 3' ends were trimmed based on phred score to a minimum length of 30 bp. The sequencing reads were aligned to the human genome reference (hg19) using Bowtie2 with the end-to-end alignment mode [33]. Read pairs were concordantly aligned with fragment length ≤ 500 bp and with an average ≤ 1 ambiguous base and up to four mismatched bases per 100 bp length. Alignments with Mapping Quality Score ≥ 10 were counted based on the genomic feature (gene body or enhancer) using featureCounts [35], without strand information.

For summarization at the gene body level, as annotated by GENCODE [21], 5hmC-enriched reads within each gene body were aggregated into a single count value, representing the total 5hmC abundance across all sites within that feature. This approach prevents overrepresentation by any single high-coverage site and captures the integrated 5hmC signal for the entire feature. Raw counts were then normalized using DESeq2’s variance stabilizing transformation (VST) [34], which adjusts for library size differences, stabilizes variance across the dynamic range, and ensures comparability between samples. For summarization at the enhancer level, we used brain-derived enhancers from the Roadmap Epigenomics Project [32], specifically state 7 of the core 15 chromatin states in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (E073), defined by five chromatin marks (H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H3K9me3), and processed them similarly to gene bodies.

To address potential batch effects between datasets generated on different extraction protocols and sequencing platforms, we applied the COMBAT algorithm [27] to the normalized data prior to downstream analysis.

Differential analysis between AD and non-AD

The summarized genomic features (e.g., gene bodies, enhancers) were individually compared between AD and non-AD in the validation set using a logistic regression adjusted for age at death, sex, postmortem interval (PMI), education, and APOE genotype (AD indicator ~ 5hmC + age at death + sex + postmortem interval + year of education + APOE genotype).

Machine learning-based feature selection and modeling

We aimed to construct an integrated epigenetic score for AD, and validate it in the independent validation set. Following our previous publications [2, 11], we employed five-fold cross-validation and elastic net regularization within a logistic regression framework to select informative 5hmC features and formulate a classification rule by computing the probability of AD (Fig. 1). Although elastic net can theoretically be applied to whole-genome data, we pre-selected the most informative features before modeling to improve interpretability and reduce overfitting. This involved excluding features with no variation across samples. Specifically, we first ranked all features by variance across all samples and selected the top 2000 most variable features. Among these, we applied a less stringent cutoff (p < 0.1) based on differential analysis in the training set to identify candidate markers. To further mitigate overfitting, we constructed subsets from the training set using the following steps: (1) randomly sampling four times the number of AD (n = 83) and non-AD (n = 63) from the UChicago dataset, resulting in subsets of n = 332 AD and n = 252 non-AD from the training dataset; (2) training the model using this training set with the candidate features; (3) repeating this process 1000 times; and (4) retaining the features consistently selected by at least 95% iterations to build the final logistic model. This resampling ensured that the AD/non-AD ratio in each training subset matched that of the independent UChicago validation set, thereby mitigating the impact of the modest difference in overall proportions between the original training and validation datasets. For a particular feature type (e.g., gene bodies), we computed a 5hmC-based classification score for AD as follows: score = 11+e-(β0+∑βkGk), where β0 is the intercept, βk is the coefficient from the final multivariable logistic model for the k-th feature, and Gk is the normalized 5hmC level of the k-th feature. We constructed the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve in the validation set and calculated the area under the ROC curve (AUC) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Each genomic feature type (e.g., gene body, enhancer) was tested separately. Additionally, we constructed an integrative ensemble model by entering the classification scores from the gene body-based model and the enhancer-based model as input variables in a final logistic regression, generating a combined probability score referred to as the AD-score.

We used a classification threshold of 0.5 to evaluate how well the 5hmC-based models distinguish between AD and non-AD samples in the validation set. Sensitivity and specificity were computed based on this threshold, and AUROCs between training and validation samples were compared using the DeLong test.

Association of the 5hmC‑based AD‑score with longitudinal cognitive decline

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the 5hmC-based AD-score, we examined its association with longitudinal cognitive data available for all participants, collected annually in the ROSMAP cohort over a median of 9 years (range 1–22) from 19 neuropsychological tests used to generate composite cognition scores [15, 53]. Specifically, we assessed the association between the 5hmC-based AD-score and previously published cognitive decline slopes [15, 53]. These slopes—estimated in earlier work with linear mixed‑effects models that adjusted for age, sex, and education—quantify changes in global cognition as well as in five specific domains: episodic memory, working memory, semantic memory, perceptual speed, and visuospatial ability. For the present study, we regressed each pre‑computed slope on the AD‑score; a more negative regression coefficient indicates that a higher AD‑score is associated with a faster rate of decline in the corresponding cognitive measure.

Exploration of functional insights

We used clusterProfiler [57] to explore the functional relevance of the candidate marker genes (gene bodies) for the enrichment of canonical pathways maintained at the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (KEGG) [28]. Pathways with at least ten gene counts and a 0.2 false discovery rate (FDR) were considered significant. To understand the system-level relationships among those epigenetically altered genes, we used the CEMiTool [42] to construct co-modification networks based specifically on 5hmC modification patterns. This targeted approach was chosen to capture modules of genes exhibiting coordinated 5hmC variation in AD, providing insight into epigenetic regulation that may not be apparent from genetic or transcriptomic signals alone. Those differential genes with a p < 0.01 under the logistic regression model distinguishing AD from non-AD in the training set were used for the co-modification network analysis. In addition, we searched the protein–protein interactions (PPI) from the Human Reference Interactome (HuRI) [37] map to identify potential hub genes for the differentially modified gene bodies.

To complement the epigenetic findings with transcriptional evidence, we integrated transcriptomic data to explore whether differential 5hmC patterns were associated with gene expression differences between AD and non-AD. For a subset of 740 samples with available RNA-seq data [49], we performed a differential gene expression analysis using a logistic regression model adjusted for age at death, sex, PMI, education, and APOE genotype (AD indicator ~ RNA + age at death + sex + PMI + years of education + APOE genotype). We tested whether the differentially expressed genes between AD and non-AD were enriched for genes with differential 5hmC modification. To evaluate if differential 5hmC-modified genes are linked with differential gene expression between AD and non-AD, we conducted a permutation-based overlap analysis. Genes were independently ranked based on their significance in 5hmC modification levels from 5hmC-Seal data and differential expression from RNA-seq data. For various thresholds (e.g., top 10, 20, 30, …, 1000 genes), the top-ranking genes from both datasets were selected, and the number of overlapping genes was determined as the “Observed” overlap. To establish a null distribution, we performed 1000 random sampling iterations for each threshold, randomly selecting an equivalent number of top-ranking genes from each dataset and recording the overlaps as “Permutation” overlap. For each threshold, we calculated the mean of the Permutation overlaps and conducted a one-sample t-test to assess whether the Observed overlap was significantly different from the mean Permutation overlap.

In addition, to further assess the relevance of our differentially 5hmC-modified genes to AD, we integrated genome-wide association study (GWAS) data. We downloaded associations for AD traits from the National Human Genome Research Institute–European Bioinformatics Institute (NHGRI-EBI) GWAS Catalog [46] and filtered them to retain reported and mapped genes. This process yielded 2747 unique AD-associated genes. We then examined the overlap between these GWAS-identified genes and differentially 5hmC-modified genes identified in our training set. Using a hypergeometric test with a background of approximately 20,000 total genes, we evaluated whether the observed overlap exceeded that expected by random chance (p < 0.05). To ensure a more stringent assessment, we also tested the 109 mapped genes reported in the GWAS study by Bellenguez et al. as a reference set [3].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses, including differential analysis and feature selection, were performed under the R Statistical Environment. To assess the discriminative performance of the AD-scores, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated, and an AUC was computed with 95% CIs.

To assess the robustness and generalizability of the 5hmC-based AD-score across different subgroups, we conducted stratified analyses based on key demographic and technical factors, including age at death, sex, years of education, and PMI. For continuous variables (age at death, education, and PMI), we selected cutoffs that were based on sample medians and rounded to the nearest integer for consistency and interpretability. Specifically, we used 90 years for age (median = 90.9), aligning with a rounded, interpretable threshold commonly used in aging and AD research [13]; 16 years for education (median = 16); and 7 h for PMI (median = 6.9). AUC comparisons between groups were performed using DeLong’s test, with p-values > 0.05 indicating no statistically significant differences across strata. Additionally, age as a continuous variable was evaluated independently for its discriminative performance, and an AUC was computed. To evaluate potential differences in the distribution of AD and non-AD cases between the training and validation sets, we compared proportions using a chi-square (χ2) test, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Normalization of 5hmC-seal datasets and sample grouping

We processed raw 5hmC‑sequencing data from two sources: newly generated samples profiled at UChicago and previously published ROSMAP samples. The genome-wide data were corrected for batch effect using COMBAT (Supplementary Figure S1a, b) [27]. Although the proportion of AD cases (Table 1) differed slightly between the full training (66.6% AD) and validation (56.9% AD) sets (χ2 test, p = 0.03), the balanced resampling strategy used in our modeling step ensured matched AD/non-AD ratios in each training iteration. The genomic distributions of the genome-wide 5hmC data across the two datasets showed enrichment in gene bodies and depletion in flanking regions, consistent with previous studies (Supplementary Figure S1c). Brain-derived enhancers showed a depletion of 5hmC relative to their flanking regions (Supplementary Figure S1d).

Differentially 5hmC-modified gene bodies in pathologic AD

In the training set, 636 gene bodies displayed a trend of differential modification (p < 0.05) between AD and non-AD, as determined using a logistic regression model (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 1). Similarly, in the validation set, 2419 gene bodies showed a trend of differential modification (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 2).

To refine the feature selection process and improve model generalizability, we incorporated both statistical significance and biological variability. Specifically, we calculated variance for each gene body across all samples (training and validation combined) using VST-transformed counts and retained the top 2000 most variable features, providing a balance between capturing sufficient biological signal and reducing dimensionality to mitigate noise and overfitting. Within these 2000 features, we applied a relaxed differential analysis threshold (p < 0.1) using only the training set to avoid information leakage. This yielded 136 candidate gene bodies for downstream modeling (Fig. 2c, d). Clustering analysis of these 136 gene bodies revealed distinct patterns separating AD and non-AD in both training and validation samples (Fig. 2e, f).

Recognizing the importance of enhancer regions in gene regulation, we performed the same analysis on brain-derived enhancers (Supplementary Figure S2a, b) and identified 96 candidate 5hmC-modified enhancers (p < 0.1) in the training set (Supplementary Figure S2c–f), ranked by their variation. These enhancers were retained to develop an enhancer-based model.

Functional annotation of those 136 candidate gene bodies revealed enrichment in AD-related KEGG pathways (Fig. 2g), including Adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, Axon guidance, Dilated cardiomyopathy, Endocytosis, Focal adhesion, MAPK signaling pathway, and Rap1 signaling pathway (Supplementary Table 3). These pathways play essential roles in cellular processes such as synaptic function, neuronal communication, and responses to cellular stress, all of which are disrupted in AD pathology. For instance, cardiovascular-related pathways like adrenergic signaling and dilated cardiomyopathy underscore the link between cardiovascular health and cognitive function [31]. Meanwhile, pathways like endocytosis and MAPK signaling are important for amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing and neuronal survival, which are central to AD progression [40, 56].

A co-modification analysis further identified one interaction/co-modification module encompassing multiple differentially 5hmC-modified genes (p < 0.01), many of which have documented links to AD (Fig. 2h). For example, CEP72 (encoding Centrosomal Protein 72; Supplementary Figure S3), a component of our final model, has genetic variants that have been linked to peripheral neuropathy [18, 29], which may be associated with and even precede dementia [9, 45]. Additionally, RNF2 (encoding Ring Finger Protein 2), also a hub gene in this analysis, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, as accumulating evidence demonstrates that abnormal RNF protein-mediated ubiquitination is involved in AD progression [10]. Another hub gene, UBR3 (encoding Ubiquitin Protein Ligase E3 Component N-Recognin 3), which is also a component of our final model, has been associated with other neurological and developmental disorders, although direct AD associations remain to be elucidated [1]. These collective findings emphasize the functional relevance of 5hmC modifications in pathways central to AD biology.

Performance of a 5hmC-based machine-learning model for AD

Elastic net regularization applied to the 136 candidate gene bodies through logistic regression, combined with 1000 bootstrap iterations of balanced random sampling from the training set, identified 18 gene bodies that were retained in at least 95% of iterations. These reproducibly selected gene bodies were used to construct the gene-based model using the entire training set (Supplementary Figure S4a). This model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 65.1% (95% CI 60.6–69.7%) in the training set and 63.0% (95% CI 53.9–72.2%) in the validation set (Fig. 3a), indicating a modest discriminatory power. A separate enhancer-based model was generated in parallel, also using elastic net regularization applied to the 96 candidate enhancers. A total of 86 enhancers were retained (Supplementary Figure S4b), resulting in a model that demonstrated notably stronger performance: AUC = 85.7% (95% CI 82.7–88.6%) in the training set and 91.7% (95% CI 87.2–96.3%) in the validation set (Fig. 3b).

AD, presence of Alzheimer’s disease pathology according to NIA‑AA criteria; non-AD, absence of Alzheimer’s disease pathology according to NIA‑AA criteria

When examining the detection of true AD cases based on a classification threshold of 0.5, the gene body-based score achieved a sensitivity of 94.0% in the entire training set (Supplementary Figure S5a), and a sensitivity of 78.3% in the validation set (Supplementary Figure S5b). By comparison, the enhancer-based score demonstrated a sensitivity of 84.3% in the entire training set (Supplementary Figure S5a), and a sensitivity of 72.3% in the validation set (Supplementary Figure S5b).

To enhance overall classification accuracy, we combined the gene body-based and enhancer-based scores into an ensemble score (5hmC-based AD-score; Fig. 3c). This final classifier provided improved discrimination between AD and non-AD samples, with an AUC of 87.0% (95% CI 84.2–89.7%) in the training set and 91.4% (95% CI 86.6–96.2%) in the validation set. The AD-score improved overall accuracy to 79.5% and 81.5% in the training and validation sets, respectively, outperforming both the gene body model (62.2% and 62.3%) and the enhancer model (75.7% and 82.2%). Moreover, AD-score values showed a clear separation between the AD and non-AD groups (p < 0.001; Fig. 3d).

Association between AD-scores and cognitive decline

Given the strong discriminatory performance of the 5hmC-based AD-score in distinguishing AD from non-AD pathology, we next evaluated whether the AD-score was also related to clinically meaningful cognitive decline based on the longitudinal cognitive data. Specifically, we examined the association between AD-scores and cognitive decline across multiple cognitive measures. Regression analyses revealed significant negative associations between the AD-score and the slopes of cognitive decline for global cognition (β = − 0.0467, p < 0.001; Fig. 4a), as well as for all five cognitive domains, specifically episodic memory (β = − 0.0576, p < 0.001; Fig. 4b), working memory (β = − 0.0085, p = 0.038; Fig. 4c), semantic memory (β = − 0.0008, p = 0.007; Fig. 4d), perceptual speed (β = − 0.0533, p < 0.001; Fig. 4e), and visuospatial ability (β = − 0.0137, p = 0.002; Fig. 4f). Higher 5hmC-based AD-scores were consistently related to faster cognitive decline across all cognitive measures examined.

Stratified analysis

We stratified the samples by key demographic and technical covariates, including age at death, sex, education, and PMI, to evaluate the performance of the 5hmC-based AD-score across these strata. While age is widely recognized as an important risk factor for AD, its classification capability alone was limited, yielding an AUC of 61.9% (Fig. 5a). Despite this, the AD-score maintained robust and consistent performance across all stratified categories. Specifically, comparisons between age groups (< 90 vs. ≥ 90 years; Fig. 5a), sex (female vs. male; Fig. 5b), education levels (< 16 vs. ≥ 16 years; Fig. 5c), and PMI (< 7 vs. ≥ 7 h; Fig. 5d) revealed no significant differences in the AD-score’s discriminative ability. DeLong’s test yielded p-values exceeding 0.05 for all comparisons, supporting the generalizability of the model across these subgroups.

Functional relevance of the 5hmC signatures for AD

In a subset of 740 individuals with available RNA-seq data (740/1016, 73%), we conducted a differential expression analysis using a logistic regression model adjusting for age at death, sex, PMI, education, and APOE genotype. This analysis identified 2315 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with p < 0.01. To explore the potential relationship between these DEGs and differential 5hmC modifications, we performed a permutation-based overlap analysis. We calculated the observed overlap between the top-ranking differentially 5hmC-modified genes and DEGs at various thresholds. These observed overlaps were then compared to a null distribution generated by 1000 random samplings of the same number of genes. When focusing on fewer than ~ 300 top-ranking differentially modified genes, the observed overlap was comparable to or lower than expected by chance, indicating no significant enrichment. However, for thresholds exceeding ~ 300 top-ranking genes, the observed overlap significantly exceeded the null expectation (p ≤ 0.05), suggesting a meaningful association between differential 5hmC modifications and gene expression changes in brain tissue (Supplementary Figure S6).

Furthermore, among the 636 differentially 5hmC-modified genes identified in our study, 52 genes overlapped with the 2747 AD-associated genes from the publicly available NHGRI–EBI GWAS dataset [46]. This overlap suggests a potential shared involvement of these genes in AD-related pathways or mechanisms. However, a hypergeometric test conducted to assess the statistical significance of this overlap yielded a p-value greater than 0.05, indicating that the observed enrichment of differentially 5hmC-modified genes among GWAS-identified AD genes is not statistically significant. Similarly, when testing the 109 mapped genes reported by Bellenguez et al. [3], the hypergeometric test also yielded a p-value greater than 0.05, further indicating a lack of statistically significant enrichment.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the epigenetic landscape of AD by examining genome-wide 5hmC modifications in brain tissues from individuals with and without AD pathology and relating these data to AD pathology and cognitive decline. While a recent study [61] established a large-scale atlas of brain 5hmC patterns and highlighted their potential regulatory roles in AD pathogenesis, it primarily focused on identifying differentially hydroxymethylated regions (DHRs) and characterizing their epigenetic and transcriptional context. In contrast, our study advances the field by developing and validating brain-derived 5hmC-based epigenetic scores that not only distinguish AD pathology but are also related to cognitive decline, providing a direct link to clinically relevant outcomes. Although a direct feature-level comparison (e.g., DHRs vs. gene body) between the two studies is not feasible, both converge on widespread 5hmC alterations at AD-associated loci, underscoring the importance of epigenetic regulation in AD pathogenesis.

Our analysis identified consistent differential 5hmC modifications in gene bodies across both the training and validation datasets, suggesting that these epigenetic changes are robust features of AD. Notably, the enhancer regions exhibited even more pronounced differences between individuals with and without AD. The enhancer-based model achieved superior classification performance compared to the gene body-based model, highlighting the critical role of enhancers in regulating gene expression and their sensitivity to disease-related epigenetic alterations. This is consistent with prior evidence that 5hmC is preferentially enriched at tissue-specific enhancers and dynamically regulated in the brain, where it plays a key role in activity-dependent gene expression [26, 30]. Such properties may render enhancer-associated 5hmC patterns more sensitive to AD-related regulatory disruptions than gene body modifications. Our finding that enhancer-associated 5hmC alterations were more pronounced than those in gene bodies adds functional context to existing brain 5hmC atlases [61], highlighting specific regulatory loci that may be especially sensitive to AD-related dysregulation and valuable for targeted biomarker development. The 5hmC-based AD score generated from the ensemble model combining both gene body and enhancer features further improved discrimination, underscoring the complementary information provided by these genomic regions. While few studies have explored the integrative modeling of 5hmC features in AD, our prior work in glioblastoma [12] demonstrated that combining enhancer- and gene body-derived epigenetic features, including histone modification-defined enhancers, significantly improved classification performance. This supports the novelty and utility of integrating diverse regulatory features in disease classification frameworks.

To further explore the clinical relevance of the 5hmC-based AD-score, we examined its association with cognitive decline across multiple cognitive composite measures [15, 53]. Our findings demonstrate a significant negative association between the 5hmC-based AD-score and cognition, including in the global measure and across all five cognitive domains assessed (Fig. 4). Higher AD-scores were associated with faster rates of decline in global cognition and each cognitive domain, including episodic memory, perceptual speed, and others. Although the effect sizes linking the AD-score to cognitive decline are modest in absolute terms, this is expected given that cognitive composites are z-scored and reflect annualized change in late-life cohorts [15, 53]. Importantly, the consistency of these associations across all cognitive domains aligns with prior ROSMAP studies of typical yearly decline [25, 51], supporting the robustness and biological relevance of our findings. Together, this consistent pattern across domains suggests that the 5hmC-based AD-score captures widespread neurologic effects rather than isolated deficits. These results indicate that brain-derived 5hmC profiles are associated with neuropathologic features of AD and reflect individual variability in clinical cognitive trajectories, supporting the need for further research into their potential diagnostic value and insight into disease progression.

In parallel, we examined the biological pathways associated with differentially modified 5hmC gene bodies. Enrichment analyses revealed that these genes are involved in pathways critical to neuronal function and AD pathogenesis. In particular, pathways such as MAPK signaling, endocytosis, and axon guidance—essential for synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival, and amyloid precursor protein processing [16]—were prominently implicated, highlighting the biological relevance of the identified 5hmC alterations. The involvement of cardiovascular-related pathways like adrenergic signaling and dilated cardiomyopathy also points to a systemic component of AD, linking cardiovascular health to cognitive function and neurodegeneration [31, 40, 56]. To further explore coordinated regulation, we constructed co-modification networks focusing on genes with significant 5hmC differences between AD and non-AD. This targeted approach allowed us to identify epigenetic modules specifically reflecting disease-associated 5hmC variation, providing insight into regulatory relationships that may not be apparent from genetic or transcriptomic datasets alone. Notably, several hub genes, such as CEP72, RNF2, and UBR3, have been previously implicated in neurodegenerative processes and related conditions. For example, CEP72 has been associated with peripheral neuropathy, which often precedes dementia, while RNF2 and UBR3 are involved in ubiquitination processes that are crucial to neuronal survival and function [1, 9, 10, 18, 29, 45]. It is important to note that our network analysis was based exclusively on genes showing significant 5hmC modification signals. While this approach allows us to focus on the epigenetically co-modified gene sets most relevant to our study, it does not capture genes implicated by other modalities. As such, the network presented here should be interpreted as reflecting one layer of AD biology (i.e., epigenetic co-modification), rather than a comprehensive map of all pathogenic mechanisms. Future integration of 5hmC co-modification networks with genetic and transcriptomic networks will provide a more complete systems-level understanding of AD pathogenesis.

In line with this systems-level perspective, we also examined how the observed 5hmC alterations intersect with transcriptional changes in AD brain tissue. Although the top 300 genes show minimal direct overlap, enrichment becomes evident at broader thresholds (> 300 genes), where the overlap between differentially expressed genes and those with altered 5hmC significantly exceeds random expectation (p ≤ 0.05), suggesting a potential role of 5hmC in modulating AD-related transcription. Furthermore, our findings emphasize the importance of enhancer regions, where pronounced 5hmC alterations were detected. Enhancers, as key regulatory elements, orchestrate gene expression in a cell-type-specific manner. Disruption of enhancer 5hmC patterns could have far-reaching effects on neuronal function, suggesting a pivotal role for these regulatory regions in disease development. Our results also provide a broader perspective on the complex interplay between epigenetic modifications and transcriptomic changes. While the strongest differentially modified 5hmC sites may not immediately translate into changes in transcriptional activity (e.g., 300 top-ranking differentially modified genes), our permutation-based analysis indicates that a broader set of differential 5hmC modifications likely contribute to transcriptomic alterations in AD brain tissue. This observation underscores the cumulative and potentially synergistic impact of epigenetic dysregulation on gene expression in the context of AD.

Interestingly, our findings also highlight a key distinction between epigenomic and genomic approaches to AD research. While our study demonstrates the functional relevance of 5hmC modifications in AD pathology, the identified 5hmC-modified genes do not substantially overlap with GWAS-identified genetic risk loci [3]. This discrepancy may reflect the different layers of biological regulation captured by these approaches. Whereas GWAS identifies genetic variants associated with disease susceptibility, 5hmC modifications may mediate downstream effects of these variants or represent independent epigenetic contributions to disease processes. This distinction reinforces the need for integrative analyses that combine epigenomic and genomic datasets to unravel the complex mechanisms driving AD pathology.

Despite the strengths of our study, including the use of independent datasets and rigorous analytical methods, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size, while sufficient for initial discovery, may limit the generalizability of the results. This is particularly relevant for some strata in our subgroup analyses, where smaller group sizes may reduce power to detect subtle differences. Larger studies with more diverse populations are needed to validate our findings and assess their applicability across different demographic groups, including across sex, race, a spectrum of age, as well as mixed pathologies. Second, the brain epigenetic data are collected at a single time point (postmortem) and preclude conclusions about causality or the temporal dynamics of 5hmC changes in relation to disease progression. While our findings demonstrate strong associations between 5hmC profiles and cognitive decline, the prognostic utility of postmortem-derived epigenetic markers is inherently limited, as methylation patterns may change after cognitive assessment. Analyses of prospectively and longitudinally collected data with linked antemortem biospecimens (e.g., blood) will contribute to expanding our understanding of these epigenetic modifications (e.g., whether epigenetic modifications precede clinical symptoms or arise as a consequence of neurodegeneration), and to rigorously evaluating their potential for prognostic applications. Third, the non-AD group in our study was defined solely by the absence of Alzheimer’s disease pathology according to NIA-AA criteria and may include individuals with other neuropathology, such as vascular brain injury or Lewy body pathology. Such comorbid conditions are common in aging cohorts and may occur in both AD and non-AD groups. While our primary focus was to distinguish AD from non-AD pathology, these co-occurring pathologies could influence 5hmC profiles and should be explored in future work. Additionally, potential confounding factors, such as medication use, lifestyle variables, and comorbidities, could influence 5hmC patterns and were not fully accounted for in this study. Although we corrected for known confounders and batch effects, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Notably, the training and validation cohorts were processed using different DNA extraction protocols and sequencing platforms. While this required batch-effect correction, it also provided a stringent test of the robustness of our 5hmC-based signature. The successful validation across these technical differences underscores its potential clinical applicability, as real-world implementation will inevitably involve diverse laboratory conditions. Finally, while our bioinformatic analyses on postmortem human-derived brain cortical samples suggest functional relevance of the identified 5hmC modifications, experimental validation is also necessary to confirm their biological impact and to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which they contribute to AD pathology.

Future research should focus on validating these epigenetic markers in larger, independent cohorts and exploring their potential as diagnostic tools. Integrating 5hmC data with other omics approaches, such as 5mC, transcriptomics, and proteomics, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular changes in AD. Functional studies using cellular and animal models are needed to investigate the causal relationships between 5hmC modifications and neuronal dysfunction and dementia. In addition, extending 5hmC profiling to circulating cfDNA offers an important opportunity to develop minimally invasive biomarkers for early detection and monitoring of AD, bridging discoveries in brain tissue to clinical application. Finally, exploring therapeutic strategies that target epigenetic modifications may offer new avenues for intervention in AD.

Conclusions

Our study advances the field by developing brain-derived 5hmC-based epigenetic scores that not only distinguish AD pathology but are also associated with the clinical expression of AD, cognitive decline, moving beyond descriptive atlas-building. By identifying 5hmC alterations and linking them to both biological pathways and clinical trajectories, we highlight the translational potential of brain epigenetic profiles as candidate biomarkers for AD, while noting that prognostic applications will require validation in prospective, antemortem studies. These findings lay the foundation for future efforts to translate brain epigenetic signatures into minimally invasive tools, such as those based on circulating cfDNA.
