Covalent Insertion of a Mn(Salen) Type Complex in Cross‐Linked Protein Crystals: Design of an Enantioselective Artificial Epoxidase
Authors: Manel Boukhallat, Ismail Benhamed, Jade Arnone, Stann Van Baaren, Clara Rinaldi, Patrice Catty, Caroline Marchi‐Delapierre, Christine Cavazza, Stéphane Ménage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArtificial enzymes represent a promising alternative for performing non‐natural reactions in biocatalysis. Here, we illustrate the potential of cross‐linked enzyme crystals (CLEC) to achieve enantioselective epoxidation through the generation of an artificial enzyme obtained by direct covalent anchoring of a manganese complex as an artificial active site within a protein. Enantiomeric excess (ee) of up to 90% on cis‐β−methylstyrene was measured when the covalent binding yield was maximized, thanks to the remarkable behavior of the crystals. The structure of the modified enzyme, NikA, is provided. This work adds to the growing body of examples highlighting the advantages of CLEC in oxidation catalysis.

Introduction

Green chemistry promotes the development of chemical processes that prioritize safety and energy efficiency, reduce toxicity, and minimize waste [1, 2]. Biocatalysis plays a key role in achieving these goals and has been a major inspiration in the development of organic synthesis for industrial applications. However, the scope of biocatalysis is limited, and it is still far from meeting human needs. Efforts to increase the stability of enzymes under harsh catalytic conditions are still required. Significant progress has been made in combining biocatalysis and chemical catalysis, leading to the development of new possibilities for synthetic routes.

To go further, the design of artificial metalloenzymes has provided new‐to‐nature catalytic activities for the efficient production of valuable products [3, 4, 5]. Among the various strategies developed so far for designing artificial metalloenzymes, the de novo active site reconstitution strategy—involving the insertion of an organometallic (or inorganic) complex into a protein scaffold—has proven to be successful [6, 7, 8]. In recent years, the design of biohybrid catalysts, based on NikA—a nickel import protein from Escherichia coli—combined with various metal complexes via supramolecular interactions, has enabled the catalysis of diverse alkene transformations. This is achieved either by enhancing the catalytic properties of a complex when it is inserted into the protein, or by generating new reactivity [9]. The question of the stability of these biohybrid catalysts prompted us to design a heterogeneous version, in which cross‐linked protein crystals act as a solid host, exhibiting physical properties similar to those of mesoporous systems [10]. To achieve this objective, the inorganic complex was incorporated into cross‐linked NikA crystals [11], called CLEC‐NikA. The resulting catalysts are highly active and robust in environments that are too extreme for natural enzymes in solution (e.g., very low pH, oxidative conditions, high temperatures, and organic solvents) with turnover numbers exceeding 30,000 for an oxidative cleavage reaction [9]. Going one step further, the aim here is to apply this “in cristallo” approach to enantioselective reactions such as epoxidation, a key organic process for introducing oxygen atoms into molecules.

Despite their high efficiency and stereoselectivity, classical enantioselective epoxidations still depend on stoichiometric amounts of oxidants (e.g., alkylperoxides) and require further development to meet sustainability criteria [12, 13, 14, 15]. In nature, enzymes, such as monooxygenases or peroxygenases, achieve a high enantioselectivity using iron or a flavin cofactor [16, 17, 18, 19]. The emphasis is on the fact that the oxidant is either O2 or H2O2, but the presence of a FAD reductase enzyme is required. While dioxygen is the ideal oxidant in green chemistry, few catalytic systems are available for enantioselective epoxidation. For example, the pioneering work by Mukaiyama involves a high loading (2%–5%) of Mn(salen) and uses an aldehyde (isobutyraldehyde (IBA) or pivalic aldehyde) as co‐reactant to convert only conjugated olefins [20]. In described systems, the drawbacks are the high catalyst loading (due to its moderate stability), the need of imidazole derivatives as additives, the limitations to mostly cyclic conjugated olefins and a moderate chemoselectivity, with the exceptions of a case in which the solvent is oxidized into an intermediate oxidative species and high efficiency achieved by TAML iron (III) species, active at 80°C in 20 h [14, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26].

The minimum requirement for a complex to bind to NikA is the presence of a carboxylate that interacts with an arginine (Arg137) present in the binding pocket [27]. This anchoring strategy was used to design a series of iron@NikA artificial metalloenzymes in solution and crystal forms. These hybrids were effective in catalyzing oxidation reactions such as hydroxychlorination of alkenes or sulfoxidation [9]. However, they showed poor enantioselectivity. In this work, we have decided to develop a novel, durable bioinspired artificial monooxygenase, based on the CLEC‐NikA system combined with a manganese complex, more accurate than iron for enantioselective epoxidation reactions [14]. We then selected Mn(salen) complexes—known as the prototype of the inorganic catalyst for enantioselective epoxidation—for de novo active site reconstitution, due to the high versatility of the ligand in terms of chemical modifications and the wide range of oxidants that can be used in catalysis. However, we have previously demonstrated that Mn(salen) cannot bind to the NikA cavity, even in the presence of a carboxylate on the phenol moieties, due to its planar structure [27]. In fact, the geometry of the complex also plays a key role in its supramolecular insertion into the NikA cavity. Inspired by this, we developed a new approach in which the artificial active site (Mn(salen)) was covalently attached to the protein at specific positions. Our strategy was based on incorporating a maleimide moiety into the Mn(salen) structure (MnL*4, Scheme 1), which is capable of generating a covalent C─S bond via a Michael addition to a cysteine residue. This approach is of particular interest in the context of NikA as it does not contain any cysteine residues in its sequence. This enables the insertion of the Mn(salen) to be controlled in a single chosen position by site‐directed mutagenesis, a prerequisite for achieving efficient enantioselective control. Furthermore, we decided to bind the complex to the protein directly inside the crystals. In the field of artificial enzymes, bioconjugation chemistry in protein crystals has rarely been reported. Examples include surface functionalization for antigen‐antibody interactions, the generation of patterned multienzyme (Catalase and HRP) networks on crystalline support, or the embedding of a ruthenium complex in LEAFY crystals [28, 29, 30]. Conversely, many bioconjugations have been achieved prior to crystallization [30, 31, 32]. In this study, we present different strategies for creating a CLEC catalyst based on NikA to produce enantioselective epoxidation using a modified Mn(salen) complex MnL*4. The following section describes our approach to achieve an enantioselectivity of 90 ± 5% for the catalytic epoxidation of cis‐β‐methylstyrene, using the crystalline form of the His416Cys NikA mutant incorporating MnL*4 ( CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C) as the artificial active site.

Synthetic procedure of MnL*4: (a) 2 equiv. NEt3, MeOH, r.t., 2 h; (b) 1) 1 equiv. (COCl)2, 0.01 equiv. DMF, THF, r.t. 1 h; 2) 3 equiv. NEt3 in ACN, 1 equiv. N‐(2‐aminoethyl)maleimide.HCl, r.t., 16 h; (c) MnII(OAc)2•4H2O 1 equiv., LiCl 3 equiv., EtOH, reflux, 5 h.

Results and Discussion

Hybrid Preparation and Characterization

The original ligand L*4 synthesis was adapted from a general procedure (Scheme 1) [33]. First, L*1 was obtained in a quantitative yield from the condensation of L‐2,3‐aminopropionic acid with o‐vanillin. The ligand L*4 was obtained in a quantitative yield as well by the activation of the carboxylate moiety in L*1 using oxalyl chloride in THF with a catalytic quantity of DMF, followed by the addition of 2‐aminoethlymaleimide and triethylamine [34]. L*4 is recovered by filtration and engaged in complexation assays with no further purification. 1H, 13C NMR and ESI‐MS are in agreement with the expected formulation and the expected sample purity (see Supporting Information).

MnL*4 was prepared from mixing equimolar quantities of MnII(OAc)2•4H2O, the ligand L*4, and LiCl in absolute ethanol. MnL*4 precipitated from the solution at room temperature and was recovered by filtration as a brown, hygroscopic solid. The spectroscopic characteristics of MnL*4 are in agreement with the presence of a Mn(III) with a CT band at 410 nm (ε = 2000 M−1 cm−1) and an elemental analysis in agreement with the formulation as MnL*4Cl•6H2O (Figure S1). The ESI/MS in MeOH displayed the expected fragment at m/z 601 attributed to [L*4MnCl(H2O) + H+]+ and m/z 633 (with the addition of a coordinated MeOH to the previous fragment)(Figures S2 and S3). The poor solubility of the complex excluded further characterization.

Considering the biomolecule scaffold, a cysteine needs to be inserted into NikA to ensure the covalent binding of the Mn(salen) moiety. Several residues, located in the cavity or in the crystal channels, have been targeted, but only the histidine 416 to cysteine mutant will be described in this study. The cysteine will be positioned in the opposite site of the cavity from Arg137, in order to conserve the binding environment of the preceding catalytic systems. The H416C mutant was constructed by site‐directed mutagenesis. The mutant was produced in E. coli, and its purification was carried out based on the purification protocol of the NikA WT protein, previously described [35], except that 1 mM of dithiothreitol was added to the purification buffer in order to prevent the formation of intermolecular disulfide bridges.

The next step involves forming a covalent bond between the Mn complex and the protein. As a first approach, we tested the Michael addition reaction in solution. Accordingly, apo‐NikA WT and apo‐H416C, at 100 µM in 25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, were incubated overnight with a tenfold excess of MnL*4 solubilized in the same buffer but with an extra 10% of DMF, at room temperature. The aliquots were then centrifuged at 15 000 rpm for 5 min and desalted on a NAP10 resin column, to discard any excess of free MnL*4. The C─S bond formation was deduced from ESI‐MS studies under denaturating conditions (Figure S4). While the MnL*4@NikA‐WT samples provided the fragment of the apo protein (m/z 56302), the spectrum of MnL*4@H416C displayed a unique peak at m/z 56618, which was m/z 332 higher than the apoprotein mass (Figure S5). The different behavior between the two samples indicates (i) that only the H416C mutant is chemically modified; (ii) the mass difference in the mutant hybrid does not correspond to the expected MnL*4 total mass but to an altered complex bound to the protein via the maleimide group, in which the imine groups have been hydrolyzed (m/z = 332 for C9H17N4O6Mn for L*4’Mn(H2O)3 , L*4’ = C9H14N4O3, see Figure S6 for a chemical representation), probably resulting from the acidic denaturating conditions. A precise quantification of the metal content in the protein was performed using SEC‐MALLS analysis and ICP‐AES, revealing an equimolar ratio between the protein and the metal in the H416C hybrid.

Although evidence for the C─S bond was provided, the instability of the salen core may prevent catalysis. We then turn to a heterogeneous methodology. The protocol used above in solution was adapted to covalently bind MnL*4 to the H416C mutant directly inside the crystals.

Structural Characterization of NikA‐H416C Mutant and MnL*4@H416C

As previously described for NikA‐WT [36], a twofold molar excess of Fe(III)‐EDTA was added to the H416C mutant, prior to its crystallization, to yield reproducible orthorhombic crystals within a week. The crystal structure, solved at 2.54 Å resolution, displayed the cysteine at position 416. The sulfur atom does not interact with the Fe‐EDTA present in the cavity, with a distance Fe─S of about 10 Å (Figure 1A). This geometry should facilitate the reaction of this cysteine with the maleimide moiety present in MnL*4.

In order to form the MnL*4@H416C hybrid in cristallo, crystals of FeEDTA@H416C were soaked overnight at 25°C in a stabilizing solution (100 mM HEPES buffer at pH 7.0, 2.1 M ammonium sulfate) containing 10 mM MnL*4. The crystals were then washed in the stabilizing solution before being cryoprotected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. With this procedure, x‐ray fluorescence revealed only traces of Mn compared to Fe, which is consistent with the presence of Fe(III)‐EDTA in the NikA cavity (Figure S7). In agreement, the corresponding x‐ray structure revealed the absence of MnL*4 bound to the Cys416 and the presence of Fe(III)‐EDTA bound to Arg137. A second method was then developed, in which the crystals were soaked in the stabilizing solution containing 10 mM MnL*4 at 40°C overnight under stirring, after which they were washed and frozen, as described above. In this case, the x‐ray fluorescence spectrum showed intense peaks for Kα1 and Kβ1 bands of Mn, even though Fe is still present (Figure S7). Although XRF is not a quantitative method, it is evident that the manganese content has increased when the crystals were soaked at 40°C. The large decrease in the Fe: Mn ratio suggests the replacement of Fe‐EDTA from the crystals to the benefit of the insertion of MnL*4.

Unexpectedly, despite the overnight 40°C soaking, the crystals were still diffracting, allowing the determination of the x‐ray structure at 1.81 Å resolution (Figure 1B). Two molecules (called A and B) were identified in the asymmetric unit. In both molecules, the electron density map displayed a well‐defined F°‐Fc omit map in the cysteine environment corresponding to the maleimide moiety covalently bound to the sulphur atom of the Cys416 residue. Once bound to the cysteine, the maleimide loses its aromaticity and appears to be slightly distorted in the electron density, and is modelled with an occupancy of 0.8. The electron density points towards the cavity from which the Fe‐EDTA seems to be expelled (Figure 1B).

However, the electron density is not clear enough to position the MnL*4 unambiguously, to determine its occupancy accurately, and to check its integrity. Nevertheless, no additional electron density for an extra complex stabilized by supramolecular interactions could be detected in the x‐ray structure, in agreement with the binding of one complex to each NikA protein, via a covalent interaction with CyS416. Crystallographic data statistics are summarized in Tables S1 and S2.

Because the electron density was not sufficiently defined in the X‐ray structure, AlphaFold 3 was used to model the binding mode of MnL*4 covalently bound to Cys416 in the H416C mutant (Figure 1C). Several models bind to the cavity. In all cases, the complex adopts similar conformations. The most favorable model is shown in Figure 1C. In this configuration, the Mn(III) complex adopts a distorted square pyramidal geometry with two coordinating N atoms coming from the ethylenediamine moiety and two phenolic oxygen donor atoms. The coordination sphere is completed by a chloride ion as an axial ligand. As observed in the crystal structure (see above), the complex is covalently bound to the Cys416 residue. However, only a few additional interactions contribute to the stabilization of the complex within the protein cavity. Two hydrogen bonds are formed between Arg137 and both a phenolic oxygen and the methoxy group. In addition, previous studies suggest the presence of CH–π interactions between the side chain of Trp100 and the ethylenediamine moiety [27]. One phenol ring may also engage in π‐stacking interactions with Trp398 and Tyr402, although this is not clearly evident in the AlphaFold model.

Catalytic Studies

The synthesis of reproducible crystals of H416C and the possibility of anchoring MnL*4 in the crystals opened up the possibility of carrying out in cristallo catalysis using the CLEC technology [10, 37]. In a general procedure, crystals were selected under a microscope based on their similar size, and transferred into a 500 µL Eppendorf. The measurement of the protein concentration of 20 crystals results from five sets of SEC MALLS measurements as previously described [10].

In this study, to prepare the CLEC, the crystals were incubated in 1% (v/v) of fresh glutaraldehyde for 2 h, which was sufficient to stabilize the crystals while preventing glutaraldehyde polymerization in solution. Two different methods were used to synthesize the CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C heterogeneous catalyst, differing in the order of the two required steps: soaking (S) and cross‐linking (C). In the CS∆ method (cross‐linking followed by soaking at 40°C), 100 H416C crystals were cross‐linked prior to an overnight soaking at 40°C with a 100 mM solution of MnL*4 in DMF. The CLECs were transferred to MeCN, in which they are stable at least for several weeks at room temperature, and tested in catalytic assays. The S∆C method (first soaking at 40°C, then cross‐linking) was developed after it was observed that treating H416C crystals at 40°C did not affect their crystallinity (see above). Accordingly, the soaking step in the crystallization solution containing 2% DMF to ensure the solubility of the complex at 5 mM was performed prior to the cross‐linking step.

Catalytic epoxidations at room temperature were performed using two different oxidants in acetonitrile to ensure the solubility of the alkenes. Each catalytic result is the average of at least three sets of experiments based on the reactivity of 100 crystals in suspension in 40 µL CH3CN. As a first approach, an oxygen donor oxidant was chosen. While H2O2, TBHP, or NaOCl were ineffective, peracetic acid successfully oxidized styrene in the presence of CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C. The method of catalyst preparation was an essential factor both for efficiency and selectivity (Table 1). First, the CS∆ method enabled the catalytic formation of styrene oxide as the major product, with a chemoselectivity of 81%. Second, CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C is more efficient than CLEC‐FeEDTA@NikA‐WT, attesting that the catalysis is controlled by the inserted complex. Moreover, MnL*4 alone was less efficient and less chemoselective (50%), highlighting the role of the crystalline protein, as observed in our earlier studies [10, 37]. Third, the CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C catalyst generated via the CS∆ method exhibited remarkable stability, as a decrease in activity was only observed after three successive runs. This corresponds to an estimated turnover number (TON) of 1800, with styrene oxide remaining the major product throughout the cycles. Fourth, the selectivity of the reaction depended on the temperature, as only epoxide was observed under a temperature kept below 15°C, but at the expense of the yield, which fell by a third. Disubstituted alkenes or less enriched styrene derivatives were transformed exclusively into their corresponding epoxide, but to a lesser extent. Fifth, in any case, no enantiomeric excesses were obtained.

Several remarks can be made: (i) the use of a strong oxidant is less harmful to the protein than expected, due to its stabilization in the CLEC state; (ii) the protein scaffold is essential for the selectivity of the reaction for several reasons. First, the method of the catalyst preparation has a great influence on the catalytic activity, probably by a more efficient insertion of the complex in the CLEC than in the crystals with the CS∆ methodology, correlated with the result issued from XRF. Indeed, soaking the CLEC in DMF at 40°C improves the solubility of the MnL*4 complex, thereby enabling the use of a large excess during the incorporation process. Second, the selectivity toward epoxide is not due to the epoxide hydrolysis but to a direct competitive formation of the corresponding diols, as shown by the absence of epoxide transformation when this oxirane was used as substrate under standard conditions and the different epoxide /diols ratio as a function of the starting alkene. Moreover, the presence of diols is quite unexpected, as no Mn(salen) catalysts are reported to have such capacity so far. We may suggest that the combination of the protein environment and the binding of the acetate, produced during the process, in the coordination sphere of MnL*4, may create a new catalyst with a different reactivity.

To reach the enantioselectivity, an alternative oxidation strategy was explored, implying a change of oxidant. We then decide to use Mukaiyama‘s procedure for the epoxidation process, starting with the CS∆ methodology. Accordingly, 100 CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C were oxygenated at room temperature and one atmosphere pressure in the presence of 2000 equivalents of the chosen alkene together with 10,000 equivalents of IBA in a Man on the Moon millireactor, for 2 h in acetonitrile. Disubstituted alkenes were selected to ensure the chemoselectivity of the reaction, and only epoxides, not diols, were observed, regardless of the conditions. Cis‐β‐methylstyrene is transformed into an exclusive trans epoxide (dr 100%) with a 26% yield but nearly 500 TON (see Table 2). Conversely, when MnL*4 alone was tested, the yield fell to 5% as expected in the absence of an imidazole additive. This result demonstrated the role of the protein that is able to activate the Mn complex and emphasized the synergistic properties of the hybrid catalyst. Nevertheless, an ee of 60% (dr 100%) was estimated when MnL*4 alone was used in vitro, while a racemic mixture was obtained with CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C, thus indicating that incorporation of the Mn(salen) complex by the CS∆ method was deleterious to the enantioselectivity. Several reasons can be evoked: several orientations of the complex inside the crystals, a modification of the catalyst, and the presence of an adventitious extra complex. This last hypothesis was assessed by the use of CLEC‐MnL*4@NikA‐WT, generated by the CS∆ method, which was found to be slightly active. As a very low yield and no ee were measured, this suggests the presence of non‐covalently bound MnL*4, which is deleterious to enantioselectivity. Moreover, conducting the same experiment with a racemic catalyst ( CLEC‐MnL4@H416C or MnL4 alone) resulted in the formation of the racemic epoxide, but with a lower yield. This limited overall efficiency may be attributed to a selective interaction between the protein and the R enantiomer of the complex, causing a decrease in active catalyst concentration.

In order to reduce the quantity of potential non‐covalent MnL*4 binding, we modified the method of preparation of CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C by (i) reducing the catalyst concentration in the soaking solution by a factor of five, and (ii) soaking first at 40°C as was done when the effective anchoring was observed by XRD (see above and Figure 1B,C), followed by cross‐linking (S∆C methodology). Under these conditions, the resulting crystals were much lighter in color (pale yellow compared to brown, see Figure S8), suggesting a lower complex content. We were not able to determine with reliability the Mn content in the CLEC, preventing any definitive conclusions about the impact of Mn concentration in the crystals on the reactivity. However, the crystal structure of MnL*4@H416C, prepared by a soaking step at 40°C, strongly suggests the presence of only one complex per NikA when the catalyst is prepared following the S∆C methodology.

The oxidation of cis‐β‐methylstyrene with MnL*4@H416C, prepared following the S∆C methodology, led to an astonishing result, as even if the yield dropped by half in line with a supposed lower complex content, the ee could reach up to 90% ± 10% (Figure S9). This suggests that the increasing enantioselectivity of the reaction is the result of a synergistic interaction between the ligand and the biomolecule.

As usually observed when exploring the literature upon Mn(salen) based catalysis, trans‐alkenes did not react. Indeed, reaction with trans‐β‐methylstyrene led to traces of the oxidation product with both MnL*4 or CLEC‐MnL*4@H416C prepared by the CS∆ methodology.

Finally, and somewhat surprisingly concerning epoxidation reactions, the reaction is indeed stereospecific but leading only to the trans epoxide diastereomer instead of the expected cis one when starting from the cis‐β‐methylstyrene. Such stereospecificity is quite unusual, as the trans epoxide has only been reported once; however, it was formed as a mixture with the cis diastereomers [38], while most of the Mn(salen) complexes provide cis‐epoxides [14].

Our catalytic system is more efficient than any homogeneous metal catalysts for epoxidation following the Mukaiyama process with a high enantiomeric excess and chemoselectivity in the case of cis‐β‐methylstyrene [14]. Conversely, if we consider only the ee, the MnSalen grafted on glass beads has better turnover numbers (TON) (1.2 105) [39]. Moreover, MnPorphyrin grafted on graphene oxide has a large TOF (up to 4900) while keeping ee up to 100%, but for 1‐octene [40]. In addition, a chiral indanol‐substituted Mn porphyrin grafted on Fe3O4 nanoparticles is capable of aerobic oxidation, giving 100% ee, albeit with reduced chemoselectivity due to the concomitant formation of aldehyde and poor diastereoselectivity [41]. Concerning biocatalysis, several enzymes such as the styrene monooxygenase or chloroperoxidases provide also 100% ee, but the yields remain modest, unless performed in vivo [18, 42, 43]. To conclude, our system is relevant by its original construction, reaching high enantioselectivity and stereoselectivity, but with modest yield efficiency.

Concerning the observed inversion of configuration, several pathways may be evoked except the Bartlett mechanism, in which a concerted O transfer occurs [44]. Consequently, the metal‐assisted activation of a peracid, issued from autoxidation of the IBA with dioxygen, may take place [45]. It is at this stage impossible to discriminate the pathways between a radical or a carbocation pathway, resulting from a 1+1 electron or two electrons pathways. Nevertheless, they have in common the subsequent attack of the resulting high valent Mn═O (MnV or MnIV) to generate either a long‐lived radical intermediate Mn‐alkoxy bond substrate‐centered intermediate or its cationic analog. The latter will collapse to form the trans epoxide. We propose that the protein scaffold controls the lifetime of the intermediate.

Conclusion

To conclude, we have then designed an enantioselective in cristallo artificial enzyme with a 90% ee for the epoxide of cis‐b‐methylstyrene. The challenge of achieving enantioselectivity has been met through the covalent anchoring of the catalyst in protein crystals. The possible chemical transformation into protein crystals is then accessible at least for Michael additions. Moreover, the catalytic hybrid is capable of activating dioxygen without the need for additional additives, the protein effectively acting as a surrogate. The hybrid proved to be highly stable, as it remained active under harsh conditions, achieving up to 1800 TON in the presence of peracetic acid. Similarly, the protein crystals showed remarkable robustness, retaining their crystallinity after overnight incubation at 40°C in the soaking buffer. This work represents a new contribution to the few reported examples demonstrating the power of in cristallo reactivity.
