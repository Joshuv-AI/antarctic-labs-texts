The association between sarcopenia index and cognitive function among Chinese older adults
Authors: Xiao-Ming Zhang
Published: 2024

Introduction

The relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment has been extensively studied. Previous research has identified sarcopenia as a risk factor for cognitive decline in older adults. In 2023, Bian et al. [1] investigated this association among elderly individuals and found that baseline sarcopenia was linked to worse cognitive function, with an odds ratio of 1.65 compared to those without sarcopenia. This study inspired us to explore whether the sarcopenia index, calculated using serum creatinine and cystatin C, correlates with poorer cognitive function. The sarcopenia index has been proposed in prior studies as a potential proxy for sarcopenia and can be easily obtained from blood samples, offering significant potential for application in older populations [2].

Methods

Study population

This longitudinal cohort study utilized data from four waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS): wave 1 (2011, baseline), and three follow-up surveys in 2013, 2015, and 2018. The data, publicly available on the CHARLS website, included 4,134 older adults aged 60 years and above who underwent serum creatinine and cystatin C testing. Cognitive function was assessed across four domains: orientation, memory, computation, and drawing. Orientation was evaluated by asking participants to state the current year, month, day, and day of the week. Memory was assessed through immediate and delayed word recall tasks. Computation and drawing abilities were evaluated by performing calculations and drawing a clock, respectively. The total cognitive function score ranged from 0 to 30 points, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. Detailed information on these assessment methods is available in a previous study. The sarcopenia index was calculated using the formula: SI = (Serum Creatinine/Cystatin C) * 100, as reported in prior research [3].

Covariates

Additional covariates were extracted from the 2011 baseline survey, including age, gender, education, marital status, drinking history, smoking history, and the total number of chronic diseases. The chronic diseases considered included cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric problems, arthritis, dyslipidemia, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, asthma, diabetes, and hypertension.

Statistical analysis

Cognitive function was measured over time in the same participants across four waves, treating the data as panel data. A generalized estimating equation was used to explore the association between sarcopenia index quartiles and cognitive function in a fully adjusted model, with results presented as regression coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Adjusted Model 1 did not include any variables, while Adjusted Model 2 accounted for age, gender, education, marital status, current smoking status, current alcohol consumption, time, and the total number of chronic diseases.

Results

In the unadjusted model, Quartiles 2, 3, and 4 of the sarcopenia index, with Quartile 1 as the reference, showed significant positive associations with baseline cognitive function (β = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.43 to 2.33, p < 0.0001; β = 2.74, 95% CI: 2.30 to 3.18, p < 0.0001; and β = 3.96, 95% CI: 3.51 to 4.42, p < 0.0001, respectively). After adjusting for potential confounders, these associations remained significant, with β values of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.51 to 1.29, p < 0.0001), 1.01 (95% CI: 0.60 to 1.41, p < 0.0001), and 1.49 (95% CI: 1.06 to 1.92, p < 0.0001), respectively, as shown in Table 1.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated a positive association between the sarcopenia index and cognitive function among older adults in a longitudinal analysis. These findings are consistent with previous research, suggesting that the sarcopenia index may serve as a useful proxy for sarcopenia in predicting cognitive function, in line with the AWGS 2019 criteria [1,4]. Additionally, a recent study found that the lowest sarcopenia index quartile was associated with a higher risk of cognitive impairment compared to the highest quartile (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.37–0.90), corroborating our results [3]. Unlike studies using imaging and physical tests to assess sarcopenia, the sarcopenia index, derived from blood tests reflecting kidney function, offers advantages in speed and convenience, making it practical for hospital settings.

In conclusion, the sarcopenia index holds significant promise for early screening of older adults at risk for cognitive decline. Implementing early interventions such as cognitive training, exercise, and nutritional programs for these individuals may improve their prognosis.
