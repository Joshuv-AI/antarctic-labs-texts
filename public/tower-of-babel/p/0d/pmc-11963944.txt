Physical Activity Modifies the Risk of Incident Cardiac Conduction Disorders Upon Inflammation: A Population‐Based Cohort Study
Authors: Haiyan Zhao, Man Li, Dan Wu, Shuohua Chen, Chenrui Zhu, Yulong Lan, Hongmin Liu, Yuntao Wu, Shouling Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence suggests a central role for inflammation in cardiac conduction disorder (CCD). It is unknown whether habitual physical activity could modulate the inflammation‐associated risks of incident CCD in the general population.Methods and ResultsThis population‐based cohort was derived from the China Kailuan study, including a total of 97 192 participants without prior CCD. The end points included incident CCD and its subcategories (atrioventricular block and bundle‐branch block). Systemic inflammation was indicated by the monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR). Over a median 10.91‐year follow‐up, 3747 cases of CCD occurred, with 1062 cases of atrioventricular block and 2697 cases of bundle‐branch block. An overall linear dose‐dependent relationship was observed between MLR and each study end point (all P‐nonlinearity≥0.05). Both higher MLR and physical inactivity were significantly associated with higher risks of conduction block. The MLR‐associated risks of developing study end points were higher in the physically inactive individuals than in those being physically active, with significant interactions between MLR levels and physical activity for developing CCD (P‐interaction=0.07) and bundle‐branch block (P‐interaction<0.05) found. Compared with those in MLR quartile 2 and being physically active, those in the highest MLR quartile and being physically inactive had significantly higher risks for all study end points (1.42 [95% CI, 1.24–1.63], 1.62 [95% CI, 1.25–2.10], and 1.33 [95% CI, 1.13–1.56], respectively, for incident CCD, atrioventricular block, and bundle‐branch block).ConclusionsMLR should be a biomarker for the risk assessment of incident CCD. Adherence to habitual physical activity is favorable for reducing the MLR‐associated risks of CCD.Keywords: cardiac conduction disorder, inflammation, monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, physical activitySubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease

Cardiac conduction disorder (CCD), a common condition that may occur at any level of the cardiac conduction system, mainly comprises atrioventricular block (AVB), left and right bundle‐branch block (LBBB and RBBB), and nonspecific intraventricular block. In the European population, despite the inconclusive prevalence, it was reported that approximately 9% of individuals had AVB, 1 , 2 8% had RBBB, 3 and the prevalence of LBBB was roughly 2% in those aged >55 years. 2 In China, the overall prevalence of AVB in the overall population is approximately 5.85%, with a consistent increasing trend as age increases. 4 Abnormalities in cardiac conduction disrupt normal electrical depolarization of the heart, and their progression can lead to life‐threatening rhythm disturbances and heighten the risk of adverse major cardiovascular outcomes. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 For example, both left and right BBBs increase the risk of heart failure and sudden death. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Despite therapeutic advances with pacemaker implantation that improve the prognosis, its complications and treatment costs inflict a heavy toll on health care systems. 11 There is a pressing need to investigate modifiable risk factors and formulate early active interventions for at‐risk groups.

To date, the causes or risk factors for cardiac conduction defects remain incompletely elucidated. Apart from the currently known risk factors, including advanced age, 3 , 4 , 12 male sex, 4 hypertension, 8 ventricular hypertrophy, 13 and insulin resistance and diabetes, 14 evidence has accumulated for a mechanistic link between inflammation and cardiac arrhythmia. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Inflammatory processes are proposed to engage in arrhythmogenesis via mechanisms involving myocardial fibrosis and reconstruction. Recent findings from the Kailuan cohort demonstrated a significant linear relationship between inflammation assessed by hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) and CCD onset. 19 In addition to hsCRP, blood‐based biomarkers derived from leucocyte subsets (eg, the monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio [MLR]), have been increasingly used to assess systemic inflammation and predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes, 20 including cardiac arrhythmia. 18 Data from the UK Biobank showed that among 478 524 individuals free of arrhythmia at baseline, an imbalance between lymphocyte and monocyte counts was significantly associated with incident various arrhythmias. 18 It has been increasingly recognized that leukocytes can be arrhythmogenic either by altering tissue composition or by interacting with cardiomyocytes. 21

In contrast to inflammatory risk factors, physical activity has increasingly been highlighted as an efficacious lifestyle management for primary prevention of CCD onset. 22 A recent population‐based cohort study stressed that physical activity, rather than smoking or drinking habits, significantly lowered the risk of incident CCD. 22 Adherence to physical activity potentiates the amelioration of systemic inflammation 23 , 24 and benefits cardiovascular health. 24 , 25 However, less is known about the association between inflammation assessed by MLR and incident CCD in the Chinese general population or about the modifying effect of physical activity on the inflammation–CCD association.

In this context, we hypothesized that habitual physical activity is capable of partly counteracting the risks of incident CCD with exposure to inflammation. To address the knowledge gap, a subanalysis based on data from a population‐based, real‐life, prospective cohort in China (Kailuan study) was performed.

METHODS

Data Availability

The authors take responsibility for the integrity and accuracy of the data analysis and are willing to share the data upon reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Study Design and Participants

The Kailuan study (registration number: ChiCTR‐TNC‐11001489) is an ongoing, large‐scale, community‐based, prospectively designed cohort that aims to investigate the risk factors for cardiometabolic diseases. Approved by the Kailuan General Hospital Ethics Committee, China (2006‐05), the Kailuan study has issued health examinations every 2 years since 2006. All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion in the study. Details of the Kailuan study have been previously described. 26 , 27 For the current subanalysis, 117 212 participants who participated in the first physical examination in 2006/2007 or 2008/2009 and had complete monocyte count, leukocyte count, and ECG data were recruited. Among them, we excluded individuals who missed the follow‐up visits (n=9307), had preexisting CCD (n=3498) or cardiovascular diseases (including myocardial infarction, fibrillation, or heart failure) (n=1046), or had incomplete data on physical activity and socio‐demographics (n=6169), leaving a total of 97 192 participants for the current analysis. Outcome Ascertainment

The primary study end point was CCD, defined as a composite of first‐degree, second‐degree, and third‐degree AVB, RBBB (including complete and incomplete RBBB) and LBBB (including complete and incomplete LBBB), left anterior fascicular block, or left posterior fascicular block, as well as nonspecific intraventricular block. 19 Detailed definitions of the end points are listed in Table S1. The secondary end points were AVB and bundle‐branch block (BBB) (including RBBB, LBBB, and nonspecific intraventricular block). Standard 12‐lead ECGs were recorded for all participants by strictly standardized procedures in the health examinations conducted every 2 years since 2006. Briefly, subjects comfortably rested in the supine position in a quiet room for 5 minutes before a 12‐lead ECG was taken from 7:00 am to 9:00 am. ECG measurements and diagnoses were completed by 2 ECG specialists. The diagnosis of CCD and its subsets was in accordance with the ECG diagnostic criteria of the Minnesota Code and the relevant American Heart Association guidelines. 28 , 29 Follow‐up started at the end of the baseline visit and ended at the incidence of any CCD, death, or the last available follow‐up visit, whichever came first.

Exposure Assessment

Inflammation, as assessed by MLR, was calculated as the monocyte count/leukocyte count ratio. 20 Monocyte count and leukocyte count were obtained from the routine blood panel, which was tested using a complete blood count analyzer (Sysmex XT‐1800i; Sysmex) at the central laboratory of Kailuan Hospital. As previously described, fasting (8–12 hours) venous blood samples were drawn and transferred into vacuum tubes containing EDTA at 7:00 am to 9:00 am on the health examination day and were tested on the same day. 30 Participants were first categorized into 4 subgroups according to MLR quartiles. Physical activity was assessed by 2 sets of questions: (1) Whether the participants were manual workers or not. The Kailuan cohort is a profession‐specific cohort in a coal mining city (Tangshan City, China). The coal miners were all men who engaged in coal mining and carrying heavy groceries for 8 hours per day and 5 days per week. The work intensity of these workers was strong and met the current criterion of vigorous‐intensity physical activity according to The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans. 31 (2) Physical activity intensity, frequency, and duration. In the health examinations in 2006/2007 and 2008/2009, with different intensities of physical activity, we had specific questions on the duration and frequency of each set of physical activity. Then, we calculated the total amount of time spent on moderate‐ and vigorous‐intensity physical activity. In the present study, physical activity was divided into 2 subgroups, namely, physically active (PA) and physically inactive (PI), with PA being defined as male manual workers (all coal miners were men in the Kailuan community who were engaged in vigorous‐intensity work for 40 hours per week) and who were accumulating at least 80 minutes per week of moderate, vigorous, or a combination of moderate and vigorous physical activity according to the self‐reported frequency of physical activity from a standard questionnaire. 32 , 33

Covariable Assessment

Information on potential confounders (including socio‐demographics, lifestyle factors, family history of diabetes, history of medication use and medical diseases) was collected at baseline and thereafter updated every 2 years via standard questionnaires. Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and blood pressure, were conducted by trained nurses or physicians. Biological tests of lipid profiles, fasting blood glucose, uric acid, creatine, and hsCRP were also conducted by an automatic analyzer (Hitachi 747; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. 34 Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg or having a history of hypertension or using antihypertensive drugs. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L or <7.0 mmol/L but with a confirmed history of diabetes or medication use of hypoglycemic drugs. Smoking was defined as at least 1 cigarette per day in the past year. 35 Alcohol consumption was defined as the consumption of ≥2 standard drinking volume per day for men and ≥1 standard drinking volume per day for women (1 standard drinking volume is equivalent to 120 mL of wine, 360 mL of beer, or 45 mL of liquor) for ≥1 year. 35 The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated in accordance with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula. 36

Statistical Analysis

We used multiple imputation by chained equation techniques to account for missing data under the missing‐at‐random assumption. Baseline characteristics were described as mean±SD, median together with interquartile range (IQR), or number and percentage, when appropriate. Intergroup comparisons across MLR quartiles were conducted by 1‐way ANOVA or Kruskal‐Wallis tests for continuous variables and Pearson χ2 tests for categorical variables.

Unadjusted incidence rates (per 10 000 person‐years) and Kaplan‐Meier curves were used to present the absolute risk of the study end points. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, and no variable violated the proportional hazards assumption. Cox proportional hazards models were used to model time to event for estimation of hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs of incident CCD upon MLR‐indicated inflammation and physical activity, as alone or jointly, adjusted for potential confounding variables. Covariates for inclusion in the multivariable model were potential confounders of physical activity and well‐established risk factors for cardiovascular disease. 37 Mode 1 included sex (men/women), age (continuous), smoking habit (yes or no), alcohol consumption (yes or no), body mass index (<24, 24–28, ≥28 kg/m2), diabetes (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), dyslipidemia (yes or no), estimated glomerular filtration rate (continuous), and log‐transformed (hsCRP) (continuous, limited to simply MLR or physical activity exposure). Model 2 was further adjusted for medication use of antihypertensives (yes or no), lipid‐lowering drugs (yes or no), and antidiabetics (yes or no) on the basis of Model 1.

We also used restricted cubic splines with 3 knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles to flexibly model the association between MLR and the study end points in the entire population and stratified by physical activity categories. In the spline models, the covariables included in Model 2 were adjusted. We tested for potential nonlinearity by using a likelihood ratio test comparing the model with only a linear term against the model with linear and cubic spline terms. 38 , 39

Thereafter, we fitted interactions to investigate the modifying effect of physical activity on CCD risk with exposure to MLR‐indicated inflammation. Likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate the multiplicative interaction (INTm) between MLR quartiles and physical activity subgroups in the fully multivariable‐adjusted Cox models. With a significant INTm found, multivariable adjusted models for MLR‐associated CCD risks were then repeated in 2 different levels of physical activity (active and inactive). We further examined the risks of CCD upon joint exposure to MLR and physical activity. Based on the physical activity (PA and PI) and MLR (quartiles) grouping, the entire sample was stratified into 8 mutually exclusive groups.

To assess the robustness of the study results, several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, to minimize potential confounding by cardiovascular events, we excluded the observation subjects who had new myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, or heart failure during the follow‐up process. Second, to account for reverse causality, we excluded CCD onset within the first heath examination (approximately 2 years) of follow‐up. Third, to account for the competitive risk of death, the Fine‐Gray model was used to analyze the difference in the risk of heart block between the different groups after controlling for the competing risk of death. Fourth, because the definition of physical activity was intertwined with the manual worker status, we also conducted sensitivity analyses by treating manual worker and reported physical activity as separate variables and to repeat the analysis using the nonmanual workers alone. Additionally, to examine the risk heterogeneity, we performed several subgroup analyses across sex, age, body mass index, prevalent hypertension, and diabetes subgroups.

All statistical analyses were performed with SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R software (version 4.1.2; R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, except for interaction testing, where a P value <0.1 was considered significant. 40 , 41

RESULTS

The mean age was 49.81±12.26 years, and the majority were men (78 003 [80.26%]). During a median 10.91 (IQR, 8.45–12.59) years of follow‐up, 3747 cases of any CCD occurred, of which 1062 cases were AVB (mostly first‐/second‐degree AVB, only 15 third‐degree AVB cases), and 2697 cases were BBB. The overall incidence of heart conduction disorders was 38.18 per 10 000 person‐years. We first addressed the risks of incident CCD and its subcategories (AVB and BBB) with separate exposure to MLR‐indicated inflammation and physical activity. We used 3‐node restricted cubic splines to flexibly model and visualize the relationship of MLR with CCD, AVB, and BBB onset.

As shown in Figure 2, an overall linear dose‐dependent association between MLR and incident CCD, AVB, or BBB was observed, despite J‐shaped patterns (all P for nonlinearity ≥0.05). When MLR was tested as a categorical variable (Table 2), MLR quartile 2 had the lowest incidence (34.88 per 10 000 person‐years) compared with other MLR quartiles (37.14, 37.38, and 42.44 per 10 000 person‐years, respectively, for quartiles 1, 3, and 4). This was in line with the risk trend indicated in the RCS plot. In this regard, we used MLR quartile 2 as the reference group. In the multivariable adjusted model (Model 2), compared with MLR quartile 2, only the highest level of MLR was significantly associated with incident CCD (hazard ratio [HR], 1.19 [95% CI, 1.09–1.30]) and BBB (HR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.01–1.24]), whereas both lower and higher levels of MLR were significantly associated with incident AVB (HR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.06–1.52]; HR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.04–1.49]; and HR, 1.41 [95% CI, 1.18–1.68]), respectively, for MLR quartiles 1, 3, and 4]. In regard to physical activity, compared with PA, PI had a higher incidence of the study end points except for BBB. In the multivariable model, the PI‐associated risks were 1.15 (95% CI, 1.06–1.25), 1.23 (95% CI, 1.07–1.43), and 1.10 (95% CI, 1.00–1.21) for incident CCD, AVB, and BBB, respectively.

Then, we addressed the modifying effect of physical activity on the MLR‐associated risk of conduction block. The trends in the dose–response association between the MLR and all study end points across different physical activity subgroups were similar to those in the overall population, with a markedly higher MLR‐associated risk among the PI participants (Figure 2). Figure 3 shows the cumulative incidence of the study end points across physical activity subgroups. The log‐rank test demonstrated a statistically significant difference in the cumulative incidence of CCDs across MLR quartiles in the 2 groups (all P<0.05). There were significant interactions between MLR quartiles and physical activity in the development of CCD (P‐INTm=0.07) (Table 3). Relative to MLR quartile 2, the highest MLR levels had higher absolute risks (incidence and multivariable adjusted risks) across both physical activity subgroups. Notably, the MLR‐associated risks were significantly higher among the PI individuals than among the PA individuals (HR, 1.31 [95% CI, 1.12–1.52] versus HR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.02–1.27]). In terms of the analyses for secondary end points, the MLR‐physical activity interaction was significant in developing BBB (P–INTm <0.05) but not AVB (P–INTm=0.34). Despite a nonsignificant interaction, adherence to physical activity tended to decrease the MLR‐associated risk for AVB; the HRs in MLR quartile 4 were 1.34 (95% CI, 1.08–1.66) for PA versus 1.58 (95% CI, 1.17–2.14) for PI. In contrast, the MLR–physical activity interaction was statistically significant in the developing BBB. Physical activity significantly reduced the MLR‐associated risks of BBB (HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.94–1.22] for PA versus HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.02–1.47] for PI).

Additionally, in the sensitivity analyses, we repeated the analysis of association between joint exposure to MLR and physical activity by excluding the study subjects who had new myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, or heart failure during the follow‐up process or excluding CCD onset within the first heath examination of follow‐up or in the competing risk model (Tables S3 through S5). The results remained similar to the primary results of the main analysis. When defining physical activity according to solely reported physical activity patterns or manual work, the risk of incident CCD associated with MLR exposure appeared to be higher among the nonmanual workers or PI adults, without statistically significant interaction found. Nonetheless, among nonmanual workers, adherence to habitual physical activity appeared to reduce the risk of incident CCD with exposure to MLR, consistent with the main findings (Tables S6 through S8).

Furthermore, there were no significant risk heterogeneities across different sex, age, BMI, prevalent hypertension, or diabetes subgroups; tests for the multiplicative interaction were all nonsignificant (P‐interaction >0.1) (Tables S9 through S13).

DISCUSSION

Based on data from a real‐life, large‐scale, community‐based prospective cohort in China, we demonstrated a significant dose‐dependent association between MLR‐indicated inflammation and incident cardiac conduction abnormalities. Specifically, habitual physical activity significantly reduced the MLR‐associated risks of CCD and its subcategories (AVB and BBB), with a significant interaction between MLR and physical activity found.

Consistent with the significant positive association between inflammation indicated by MLR and enhanced CCD risk, previous findings from the UK Biobank supported that the imbalance between lymphocyte and monocyte subsets was a significant risk factor for bradyarrhythmia. 18 Additionally, the close link between other inflammatory biomarkers (eg, hsCRP or other variable blood cell subset‐derived inflammatory parameters) and cardiac arrhythmia from the Kailuan and UK Biobank cohorts substantiated the close association between inflammation and cardiac arrhythmia. 18 , 19 Given the extensive involvement of inflammation in a wide range of cardiometabolic diseases, it may not be surprising that inflammation was significantly associated with incident CCD. Cardiac conduction is the outcome of complex interactions between cellular electrical activity, electrical cell‐to‐cell communication, and the cardiac tissue structure. 42 Myocardial fibrosis is an important pathophysiological contributor and has been linked to arrhythmogenesis. 15 , 43 Most likely involved in delayed action potential propagation, afterdepolarizations, reentry, and increased ectopic automaticity, fibrosis potentiates interference with all the basic electrophysiological mechanisms leading to cardiac arrhythmia. 42 Converging studies have demonstrated a mechanistic link between inflammation and myocardial fibrosis. 15 , 44 , 45 , 46 Aside from myofibroblasts and cardiomyocytes, several immune cell types, including monocytes, macrophages, and lymphocytes, may contribute to the fibrotic process by producing proteases that participate in matrix metabolism. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines, such as IL (interleukin)‐1, IL‐10, tumor necrosis factor‐α, and multiple chemokines, are secreted in the cardiac interstitium and play distinct roles in activating the fibrotic response. 15 These inflammatory responses can promote the formation and development of myocardial fibrosis via electrical and structural remodeling. Anti‐inflammatory treatment was proposed to afford benefits for mitigating CCD burdens. 15 The ALLHAT (Antihypertensive and Lipid‐Lowering Therapy for Prevention of Heart Attack Trial) demonstrated that administration of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibition can prevent conduction disease partly through its anti‐inflammatory and antifibrotic properties. 13

As a hematological parameter derived from the widely available routine blood panel, the MLR reflects the opposing but complementary balance between innate immune processes and the adaptive immune response in the body and has been established as an alternative biomarker for assessing inflammation. 20 Converging studies have demonstrated a strong association between MLR and atherosclerotic diseases and an independent predictor of cardiovascular death and all‐cause death. 20 , 47 Our findings, together with the results from the UK Biobank study, 18 highlight the usefulness of MLR as a convenient and cost‐efficient biomarker candidate for predicting CCD. Nevertheless, the specific mechanisms underlying the increased susceptibility to CCD augmented by elevated MLR levels remain unexplored. The complex interaction between immune cells, such as neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and macrophages, contributes to the appearance of inflammation. 48 Monocytes and their derived macrophages are the most numerous leukocytes in the heart and may promote destruction of the arterial wall and the myocardium, leading to CCD onset. 49 , 50 The NLRP3 (NACHT, LRR, and PYD domains‐containing protein 3) inflammasome and its downstream IL‐1β in monocytes and macrophages have been demonstrated to drive arrhythmogenesis. 51 , 52 T lymphocytes elicit cell‐mediated immunity, and subsets of T lymphocytes may produce cytokines such as IL‐2 or IL‐17, exacerbating neutrophilic inflammation and thereby being involved in the fibrotic response. 53 B lymphocytes may engage in cardiac arrhythmias via autoantibody‐targeting specific calcium, potassium, or sodium channels on the surface of cardiomyocytes. 54 Although the importance of immune cells in the pathological process of conduction block has been increasingly recognized, 55 there is a lack of appreciation in the underlying mechanisms of arrhythmogenesis involving circulating monocyte and lymphocyte dynamics. A detailed deciphering of the cell–cell interactions of leukocyte subsets is merited to understand inflammation‐related pathophysiology in arrhythmogenesis.

Additionally, our study extends the understanding of the effects of physical activity on inflammation‐associated CCD risk. CCD is associated with pronounced morbidity and mortality. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Nonetheless, currently, the only available therapy for CCD is pacemaker implantation, with no established prevention strategies. 22 There is increasing interest in the modifiable risk factors for conduction dysfunctions. Habitual physical activity has long been suggested as a main nationwide strategy for maintaining cardiovascular health. 23 , 24 In a recent study focusing on the CCD‐preventive benefits of lifestyle modifications, only habitual physical activity, but not smoking or drinking patterns, was associated with a risk amelioration of CCD. 22 Although the mechanisms involved in the reduced risk of CCD by adherence to physical activity remain incompletely understood, it is likely that physical activity should afford CCD‐preventive benefits through targeting the key pathogenic factors of CCD. In an experiment‐based study, exercise training in mouse models increased the expression of fibroblast growth factor 21 and inactivated the transforming growth factor‐β1 (TGF‐β1)‐Smad2/3‐Matrix Metalloproteinases2 and 9 (MMP2/9) signaling pathway, thereby alleviating cardiac fibrosis and oxidative stress and resulting in cardiac function improvement. 56 In addition to ameliorating cardiac fibrosis, habitual physical activity may mitigate the risk of cardiac arrhythmia mechanically by improving insulin signaling 57 and reducing overall inflammation. 24 , 25 Further exploration is warranted to investigate the related pathways underlying the pathophysiology of inflammation‐related cardiac arrhythmia and its biological interplay with physical activity.

The study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effect modification by physical activity on inflammation‐associated risks of incident CCD. Additionally, our results highlighted the usefulness of MLR for risk assessment of conduction block. As a convenient and cost‐efficient biomarker in both cohort and clinical settings, MLR has the potential to improve the risk prediction of CCD and merits attention. Other merits of this study included the large sample size, the long period of follow‐up, the prospectively designed and real‐world cohort, and the high quality of data processing and collection.

The study also has certain limitations. First, the study was conducted primarily among the Han Chinese population in northern China, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the entire country and other ethnic groups. However, the relative homogeneity in the occupational and environmental exposures among community‐dwelling adults should reduce potential confounding factors. Second, there was marked male skewness in the study participants, which may have limited the extrapolation of the findings, yet the sex‐stratified analysis may compensate for this limitation to some extent. Third, CCDs were diagnosed based on the ECG measurements taken at the medical checkup, without including individuals with CCD episodes between health examinations, which may have decreased the efficacy of the analysis. Fourth, physical activity was assessed by a self‐reported pattern, which may inevitably have led to recall bias and resulted in a failure to quantify the volume of physical activity. Fifth, in the present study, PA was defined as a manual worker or having ≥80 minutes of moderate‐ or vigorous‐intensity physical activity weekly according to the information attained in the health examinations in 2006/2007 and 2008/2009. Although we have carefully controlled the classification of physical activities, some degree of inconsistency with current criteria 58 is inevitable. Nonetheless, previous studies in the Kailuan study have shown a significant association between a high volume of physical activity and lower cardiometabolic risks. 32 , 59 , 60 , 61 Moreover, manual workers who met the current criteria of PA 58 predominated the majority of the all‐forms of PA individuals in our study (56.33%), strengthening the reliability of our findings. Evaluation of the reproducibility of existing findings in other populations and CCD‐purpose studies is needed.

CONCLUSIONS

In a real‐life, prospective cohort in China, our results pointed out a previously unappreciated role of habitual physical activity in reducing the risk of bradyarrhythmia with exposure to MLR‐indicated systemic inflammation. The MLR should be an important alternative inflammatory biomarker for the risk assessment of cardiac conduction abnormalities. Adherence to physical activity is imperative to reduce the risk of incident CCD.
