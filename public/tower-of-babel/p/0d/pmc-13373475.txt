Influence of body mass index on disease severity and treatment outcomes in primary aldosteronism
Authors: Marta Araujo-Castro, Jessica Goi, Jacopo Burrello, Silvia Monticone, Paolo Mulatero, Elena Bersano, Holger Schneider, Erik Johann Petersenn, Stephanie Zopp, Virginia Capuzzo, Ana María García Cano, Elena Mena Ribas, Alicia Sanmartín Sánchez, Patricia Martín Rojas-Marcos, Miguel Paja Fano, Patricia Díaz Guardiola, Ángel Rebollo Román, Alicia Rizo Gellida, Marga González-Boillos, Jorge Gabriel Ruiz-Sanchez, Emilia Gómez-Hoyos, Mònica Recasens, Rebeca Barahona San Millan, María José Picón César, Carolina M Perdomo, Cristina Robles Lázaro, José María Recio, María Calatayud, Cesar Gonzalvo Diaz, Cristina Lamas, Joaquín Serrano, Theodora Michalopoulou, Susana Tenes Rodrigo, Fernando Jaén Aguila, Nuria Muñoz Rivas, Eva María Moya Mateo, Rogelio García Centero, Ana Torres, Sonsoles Gutiérrez-Medina, Aster Adel Gamil

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess the impact of body mass index (BMI) on disease severity, cardiac remodeling, and clinical and biochemical outcomes after surgical and medical treatment in patients with primary aldosteronism (PA).MethodsThis international retrospective study included patients diagnosed with PA in Germany, Italy, and Spain. Patients with available BMI, serum potassium, blood pressure, and basal plasma aldosterone concentration at diagnosis were included. PA severity was classified using the Primary Aldosteronism Severity Classification.ResultsA total of 1752 patients were analyzed at baseline; 604 (34.5%) had obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2). Patients with obesity were older, more frequently male, and had a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, left ventricular hypertrophy, and obstructive sleep apnea. PA severity according to the Primary Aldosteronism Severity Classification did not differ significantly between patients with and without obesity; however, the prevalence of severe PA increased progressively across BMI categories (MH test for linear trend: χ2(1) = 3.70, P = .054). Following adrenalectomy, the probability of complete hypertension resolution decreased with increasing BMI, whereas biochemical cure rates were similar. Among patients treated with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, obesity was associated with a lower likelihood of complete clinical response, without significant differences in biochemical response.ConclusionIn patients with PA, obesity is associated with a higher burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities and increased prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy at baseline, as well as with less favorable clinical outcome after both surgical and medical treatment.

Primary aldosteronism (PA) is one of the most common causes of secondary hypertension, accounting for more than 5% of all hypertensive cases and up to 20% of resistant hypertension [1].

Obesity represents a major global public health challenge and is strongly associated with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [2]. Adipose tissue secretes several adipokines, including adiponectin, leptin, osteopontin, and resistin, which may stimulate aldosterone production independently of the aldosterone-secreting adenoma [3]. Obesity-related aldosteronism has therefore been proposed as a contributor to cardiometabolic outcomes [4]. Both obesity and hypertension induce maladaptive cardiac remodeling, leading to left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and hearth failure. The coexistence of obesity and PA could therefore be expected to amplify cardiovascular damage [5] and disease severity.

Recently, the Primary Aldosteronism Severity Classification (PASC) was developed to stratify PA severity based on biochemical and clinical parameters and to support clinical decision-making [6]. Nevertheless, data on determinants of PA severity beyond disease laterality remain limited. Given that higher body mass index (BMI) has been associated with increased plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC), it could be hypothesized that patients with overweight or obesity would present with higher PASC scores [4]. To date, however, no studies have specifically examined the relationship between BMI and PA severity using the PASC system.

In addition, previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding the impact of obesity on surgical outcomes. Although some investigations have suggested that obesity negatively affects hypertension resolution after adrenalectomy [7-13], others have failed to demonstrate a significant association [14]. Data on the influence of BMI on outcomes of targeted medical therapy are even more limited. The Primary Aldosteronism Medical Outcome (PAMO) study found that female sex was associated with better clinical outcomes after targeted medical treatment for PA, potentially because of shorter hypertension duration, lower BMI, fewer antihypertensive medications, and lower burden of comorbidities than in men; however, the independent role of BMI across different categories was not specifically analyzed [15].

Overall, evidence regarding the impact of obesity on the effectiveness of adrenalectomy and, even more so, on the clinical and biochemical response to targeted medical therapy in PA remains limited. Therefore, the primary aim of the present study was to assess the impact of BMI on PA severity, as defined by the PASC system. Secondary objectives included evaluating the prevalence of LVH depending on the BMI classification and the influence of BMI on clinical and biochemical outcomes following both surgical and targeted medical treatment with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) in patients with PA.

Methods

Study design

This international retrospective study included patients diagnosed with PA in Germany (Munich), Italy (Turin), and Spain (37 centers belonging to the SPAIN-ALDO Registry). Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years at PA diagnosis; (2) available BMI data at baseline; and (3) available serum potassium, blood pressure (BP), and basal PAC at diagnosis, allowing PASC [6] (Table 1).

Of the 1923 patients in the database, 171 were excluded because of missing BMI or baseline clinical data. The final cohort comprised 1752 patients for baseline analysis. Follow-up analyses assessing treatment outcomes included patients who received targeted treatment (adrenalectomy or MRAs) and had available biochemical and clinical outcome data (n = 1143). In addition, we have included a control group of patients with obesity and essential hypertension (EH) in whom PA, pheochromocytoma and hypercortisolism were ruled out. Cardiometabolic profile was compared between the group of patients with PA and obesity and the group of EH and obesity. The control group was extracted from the ADRENAL-INCIDENTALOMA database of the Ramón y Cajal Hospital [16]. Of the 638 patients included in the database, 205 had mild autonomous cortisol secretion and 225 were nonhypertensive. Thus, the final cohort of patients with obesity and EH in the control group was 209.

The study was approved by the local research ethics committees to each center and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definitions

PA diagnosis and management followed contemporaneous clinical practice guidelines [17, 18]. Information about subtype differentiation between unilateral and bilateral PA based on adrenal venous sampling (AVS) was available in 1078 patients (62%), with a rate of successful procedure equal to 81%. Of the 872 patients with a selective AVS, 492 (45.8%) were classified as having unilateral PA and 380 (35.4%) as having bilateral PA. Subtype classification was based on institution-specific thresholds.

BMI was calculated at diagnosis and categorized as follows: normal weight (BMI < 25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25-29.9 kg/m2), obesity grade I (BMI 30-34.9 kg/m2), obesity grade II (BMI 35-39.9 kg/m2), and obesity grade III (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2). For primary analyses, patients were classified as with or without obesity (BMI ≥30 vs <30 kg/m2).

PA severity was assessed using the PASC classification [6], categorizing disease as mild, moderate, or severe based on BP, serum potassium, and basal PAC (Table 1).

Echocardiographic data obtained at the time of PA diagnosis were available for 1359 patients. LVH was defined as left ventricular mass index >95 g/m2 in women and >115 g/m2 in men, in accordance with the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging [19].

Surgical and medical outcomes

Available information about treatment modality was reported in 1260 patients: 445 underwent adrenalectomy and 815 received medical treatment with MRA. Among patients treated surgically, clinical outcomes data were available for 411 and biochemical outcomes data for 342. Among patients treated with MRA, clinical outcomes were described in 704 and biochemical outcomes in 448 cases. Clinical and biochemical responses to treatment were classified as complete, partial, or absent according to the Primary Aldosteronism Surgical Outcome [12] for surgically treated patients, and the PAMO [15] criteria for medically treated patients. Complete clinical response was defined as a normalization of BP without antihypertensive medications after adrenalectomy; partial clinical response as an improvement in BP control and/or reduction in the number or dosage of antihypertensive drugs and absent response as unchanged or worsened BP despite surgery. Complete biochemical response was denied as normalization of potassium levels, aldosterone-renin ratio, and suppression of autonomous aldosterone secretion and absent response as persistent biochemical evidence of PA after surgery [12]. In relation to PAMO outcomes, complete biochemical response was defined as correction of hypokalemia (if present pretreatment) without potassium supplementation, and normalization of plasma renin activity (PRA)/direct renin concentration (DRC) and complete clinical response as normal BP without additional antihypertensive medications [15].

Clinical and biochemical data were collected at baseline (before initiation of targeted treatment) and at long-term (the last available visit, with at least a period of 6 months of follow-up) follow-up visits.

Baseline information included: age, sex, duration of hypertension, BMI, systolic and diastolic BP, various blood tests (PAC, PRA, DRC, potassium, creatinine, lipid profile, fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin A1c) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (calculated by Cockcroft–Gault equation); history of hypokalemia (K+ ≤3.5 mmol/L) at any time before diagnosis; active smoking habit; presence of cardiovascular comorbidities, dyslipidemia, diabetes type 2, obstructive sleep apnea, or mild autonomous cortisol secretion (defined as cortisol after 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test >1.8 µg/dL without cushingoid features) [20]; oral potassium supplementation; and number of antihypertensive drugs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA.15 (StataCorp. 2017. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. All data are expressed as the mean ± SD or median (25th–75th percentile), as appropriate. Student t-test was used to compare continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables between 2 groups. For the estimation of odds ratios (OR) and CIs, logistic regression analyses were performed. Mantel-Haenszel test was performed for the evaluation of linear trends for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression estimated variable associations after adjusting for confounders.

Influence of BMI on PA severity

Based on PASC category, 13.1% (n = 230) of patients had mild PA, 72.9% (n = 1277) moderate PA, and 14.0% (n = 245) severe PA. PA severity distribution did not differ significantly between patients with and without obesity (severe PA: 15.4% vs 13.2%, P = .216). Similarly, no significant differences were observed between groups in the individual PASC components related to serum potassium levels or basal PAC (Table 4), but BP components were more severe in patients with obesity than without obesity.

Influence of BMI on the prevalence of LVH and on surgical outcomes

Echocardiographic data were available for 1359 patients. LVH was present in 48.7% (n = 662) of the cohort and was more prevalent in patients with obesity than in those without (56.3% vs 45.2%, P < .001). The association remained significant after adjustment for age, gender, and systolic BP (adjusted OR 1.34; 95% CI, 1.05-1.70).

Surgical decisions were based on AVS in 223 patients (50%) and in radiological results in the other cases. Among the 445 patients who underwent adrenalectomy, complete resolution of hypertension was achieved in 112 (27.3%), improvement in 234 (56.9%), and no clinical response in 65 (15.8%). In relation to biochemical outcomes, 69.3% achieved complete response, 11.4% partial response, and 19.3% had no biochemical response.

The probability of complete hypertension resolution after adrenalectomy was significantly lower in patients with obesity than in those without obesity (18.9% vs 32.5%, P = .002). In contrast, no differences were detected in the rate of complete biochemical response (72.2% vs 67.5%, P = .357). When BMI was analyzed by category, the likelihood of hypertension resolution decreased progressively with increasing BMI (MH test for linear trend: χ2(1) = 20.50, P < .001) (Fig. 3).

Influence of BMI on hypertension resolution.

There were 70 patients (22.4%) of the 312 cases with available information on presurgical treatment with MRA that were treated with spironolactone or eplerenone before adrenalectomy. Pretreated patients had a higher probability of complete biochemical response than nonpretreated (69.5% vs 50%, P = .008), whereas no differences were detected in complete clinical response (33.2% vs 30.2%, P = .653).

In the multivariate analysis, we found that a lower BMI (OR 0.94 [0.89-0.99], 0.022), female sex (OR 3.01 [1.71-5.30], <0.001), and noncardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease at PA diagnosis (OR 2.44 [1.38-4.30], 0.002) were independent predictors of hypertension resolution (Table 6).

Influence of BMI on response to medical therapy

Of the 815 treated with MRA, clinical outcomes were described in 704 and biochemical outcomes in 448 cases. Partial clinical response was the most frequent outcome (58.8%), followed by no response (24.4%) and complete response (16.7%). Complete biochemical response was achieved in 48.2%, partial response in 17.6%, and no response in 34.2%.

Patients with obesity were significantly less likely to achieve a complete clinical response compared with those without obesity (10.2% vs 20.7%, P < .001), but with no differences in complete biochemical response (49.7% vs 47.3%, P = .615). The proportion of complete clinical response to MRA decreased as BMI increased (Table 7). Similarly, complete clinical response rates declined with increasing PA severity (32.8% in mild, 15.0% in moderate, and 9.0% in severe, MH test for linear trend: χ2(1) = 24.08, P < .0001).

Discussion

In this large international cohort of patients with PA, obesity was associated with a higher cardiometabolic burden, increased prevalence of LVH, and less favorable clinical outcomes following both surgical and medical treatment. Although overall PA severity according to PASC did not differ markedly by obesity status, increasing BMI was associated with a higher prevalence of severe disease and progressively worse clinical responses to targeted therapy.

Patients with obesity presented with a more adverse cardiometabolic profile at diagnosis, including higher rates of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and microalbuminuria. These findings are consistent with previous reports [7-13] and reinforce the concept that obesity amplifies the cardiovascular and metabolic consequences of PA beyond BP elevation alone. Notably, patients with obesity also required a greater number of antihypertensive medications [21] and had higher systolic BP at diagnosis, suggesting a more resistant hypertensive phenotype.

Contrary to initial expectations, BMI was not associated with a higher basal PAC or lower renin parameters in our cohort. This observation suggests that the detrimental cardiovascular effects observed in patients with obesity and PA are unlikely to be driven by higher circulating aldosterone levels alone. Instead, obesity may enhance aldosterone-mediated tissue damage through downstream mechanism, including mineralocorticoid receptor overactivation, inflammation, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and adipokine dysregulation [22]. In other words, the influence of obesity on the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system appears to be highly variable among individuals, and obesity-related hormonal alterations are not uniform. The weak positive correlation between BMI and serum potassium levels likely reflects clinical heterogeneity and does not appear to mitigate the increased cardiovascular risk associated with obesity.

A major finding of this study is the strong and independent association between obesity and LVH in PA. We found that LVH was more prevalent in patients with obesity, even after adjustment for age, sex, and systolic BP. Moreover, LVH prevalence increased progressively across BMI categories and PA severity grades, supporting a synergistic interaction between excess adiposity and aldosterone excess on adverse cardiac remodeling. These results reinforce the concept that LVH in PA is a multifactorial process, influenced by both hemodynamic load and direct myocardial effect of aldosterone [23], which may be further exacerbated by obesity-related metabolic stress.

Regarding treatment outcomes, obesity was associated with a lower probability of complete response after adrenalectomy. Importantly, biochemical cure rates were comparable across BMI categories, suggesting that surgical correction of excess aldosterone is equally effective regardless of body weight. The dissociation between biochemical and clinical outcomes suggests that persistent hypertension after surgery in patients with obesity is driven predominantly by obesity-related vascular and metabolic factors, rather than residual hyperaldosteronism. Our results are further supported by evidence from the Japan Primary Aldosteronism Study [24]. Nakamaru et al [24] demonstrated that obesity is an independent predictor of persistent resistant hypertension after adrenalectomy in patients with unilateral PA, even in presence of biochemical cure. In that cohort, patients with postoperative resistant hypertension were significantly more obese than those who achieved better BP control, and a BMI ≥25 kg/m2 independently predicted persistence of resistant hypertension after surgery. These findings are clinically relevant because they support current guideline recommendations favoring adrenalectomy in unilateral PA, while emphasizing the need for realistic expectations regarding BP normalization in patients with obesity.

We also observed that female sex and noncardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease at PA diagnosis were independent predictors of hypertension resolution that is in line with the previously reported by other authors [25, 26]. Another interesting finding is that we observed that presurgical treatment with MRA was associated with a higher probability of complete biochemical response after adrenalectomy. These results are in line with the reported in the SPAIN-ALDO, where at long-term follow-up, patients pretreated with MRA exhibited better postsurgical biochemical outcomes (81.7% had complete biochemical response vs 57.1% of nonpretreated patients; P = .004) [27]. Thus, it is possible that the long-term blockade of the mineralocorticoid receptor may persist even after MRA withdrawal in pretreated patients.

MRAs are highly effective in controlling BP, correcting hypokalemia, and reducing aldosterone-mediated organ damage. In some patients, especially those with obesity and multiple metabolic comorbidities, medical therapy combined with weight reduction may substantially improve BP control and overall cardiovascular risk profile. Furthermore, obesity itself contributes to hypertension through several aldosterone-independent mechanisms, including sympathetic activation, insulin resistance, inflammation, vascular stiffness, obstructive sleep apnea, and renal dysfunction [28]. Therefore, reducing BMI before surgery could theoretically improve the probability of postoperative clinical success. Nonetheless, current guidelines continue to recommend adrenalectomy as the preferred treatment for unilateral PA because surgery removes the source of autonomous aldosterone secretion and offers the highest likelihood of durable biochemical cure [17]. In contrast, MRA therapy blocks the effects of aldosterone but does not eliminate autonomous hormone production. Importantly, prolonged exposure to excess aldosterone may contribute to progressive and potentially irreversible cardiovascular, renal, and vascular remodeling. For this reason, postponing adrenalectomy solely to achieve weight reduction could also delay definitive treatment of the underlying endocrine disorder.

Similarly, among patients treated with MRAs, obesity was associated with a reduced likelihood of achieving complete clinical response, whereas biochemical response rates were not significantly affected. The progressive decline in clinical response with increasing BMI underscores the limitations of aldosterone-targeted therapy alone in patients with obesity and PA. Together, these results highlight obesity as a key modifier of treatment response, affecting BP control independently of biochemical disease correction. However, we must consider that there are other antihypertensive medications that can be used during the follow-up that might affect clinical and biochemical outcomes. In this regard, biochemical outcome assessment can be difficult in patients whose medications can substantially increase renin concentration, such as diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, or angiotensin II receptor antagonists, or decrease renin such as β-adrenergic blockers. Thus, PAMO criteria for the assessment of biochemical outcomes should be interpreted with caution in patients who do not have a suppressed renin concentration or renin activity at baseline; or whose suppressed renin is caused by β-adrenergic blockade [15].

The rate of absent biochemical response after adrenalectomy and medical therapy was quite high in our cohort: 19.3% in the surgical arm and 34.2% in the medical arm. These results may be influenced by several factors. First, limitations in subtype classification may contribute to incomplete response after adrenalectomy as we found that AVS was performed in only 50% of the cases who underwent adrenalectomy. In addition, postoperative biochemical assessment may vary according to timing, sodium balance, renal function, and concomitant medications. In medically treated patients, interpretation of biochemical response is intrinsically more complex. MRAs block receptor activation but do not suppress aldosterone secretion, and adequate MR blockade frequently induces compensatory increases in aldosterone concentrations and variable renin responses depending on MRA type, dose, treatment adherence, and duration of therapy. Therefore, “biochemical nonresponse” during MRA therapy may be influence by all these factors, especially the dose and type of MRA [29]. Finally, the retrospective multicenter nature of the study may introduce heterogeneity in diagnostic protocols, biochemical assays, follow-up duration, treatment strategies, and application of Primary Aldosteronism Surgical Outcome/PAMO criteria, potentially contributing to variability in response classification.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design precludes causal interference and may be subject to residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. For example, because of the retrospective nature of the study, it is difficult to determine the true duration of PA before diagnosis. Many patients were likely exposed for years to hypertension and associated metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidities, as well as to multiple therapeutic interventions, including antihypertensive agents, MRAs, antidiabetic, lipid-lowering, and weight-control treatments. These interventions may indeed influence several clinical and biochemical parameters evaluated in our study, including BP, aldosterone-related indices, cardiovascular remodeling, and metabolic status. Furthermore, in our study, aldosterone measurements were obtained as part of the routine diagnostic evaluation and were not based on repeated serial determinations specifically performed according to PASC assessment protocols [6]. Therefore, the reported basal PAC values reflect the biochemical status at the time of clinical evaluation and may not fully capture the known intraindividual variability of aldosterone secretion [30]. Moreover, we are aware that aldosterone levels may fluctuate according to circadian rhythm, day-to-day variability, seasonal factors, sodium intake, and concomitant treatments. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution. Second, the multicenter and international nature of the cohort introduces heterogeneity in diagnostic workup, treatment strategies, and follow-up protocols. Finally, information on longitudinal weight changes and posttreatment lifestyle interventions was not available, precluding assessment of their potential impact on outcomes. Despite these limitations, the large sample size and comprehensive clinical characterization strengthen the robustness and clinical relevance of our findings.

For a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that BMI should be considered when counseling patients with PA regarding expected treatment outcomes. Although obesity should not preclude surgical or medical therapy, it identifies a subgroup of patients at higher cardiovascular risk and with a lower probability of complete clinical remission. Integrating weight management strategies and comprehensive cardiovascular risk reduction into the management of PA may therefore be essential to optimize long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

In patients with PA, obesity is associated with a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, increased prevalence of LVH, and less favorable clinical outcomes following both adrenalectomy and medical treatment. Although overall disease severity according to the PASC classification does not differ substantially between patients with and without obesity, increasing BMI is associated with a higher prevalence of severe disease and progressively lower rates of hypertension resolution after targeted therapy.

These findings underscore the role of BMI as a relevant modifier of cardiovascular risk and treatment response in PA. Incorporating weight-focused intervention and comprehensive cardiovascular risk management into the care of patients with PA may be essential to optimize long-term outcomes.
