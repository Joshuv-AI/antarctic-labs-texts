Depletion of Bone Marrow Hematopoietic Cells in Ebolavirus-Infected Rhesus Macaques
Authors: David X Liu, Bapi Pahar, Donna L Perry, Huanbin Xu, Timothy K Cooper, Louis M Huzella, Randy J Hart, Amanda MW Hischak, John Bernbaum, Marisa St Claire, Russell Byrum, Richard S Bennett, Travis Warren, Michael R Holbrook, Lisa E Hensley, Ian Crozier, Connie S Schmaljohn
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pathophysiology of long-recognized hematologic abnormalities in Ebolavirus (EBOV) disease (EVD) is unknown. From limited human sampling (of peripheral blood), it has been postulated that emergency hematopoiesis plays a role in severe EVD, but the systematic characterization of the bone marrow (BM) has not occurred in human disease or in nonhuman primate models. In a lethal rhesus macaque model of EVD, 18 sternal BM samples exposed to the Kikwit strain of EBOV were compared to those from uninfected controls (n = 3). Immunohistochemistry, RNAscope in situ hybridization, transmission electron microscopy, and confocal microscopy showed that EBOV infects BM monocytes/macrophages and megakaryocytes. EBOV exposure was associated with severe BM hypocellularity, including depletion of myeloid, erythroid, and megakaryocyte hematopoietic cells. These depletions were negatively correlated with cell proliferation (Ki67 expression) and were not associated with BM apoptosis during disease progression. In EBOV-infected rhesus macaques with terminal disease, BM showed marked hemophagocytosis, megakaryocyte emperipolesis, and the release of immature hematopoietic cells into the sinusoids. Collectively, these data demonstrate not only direct EBOV infection of BM monocytes/macrophages and megakaryocytes but also that disease progression is associated with hematopoietic failure, notably in peripheral cytopenia. These findings inform current pathophysiologic unknowns and suggest a crucial role for BM dysfunction and/or failure, including emergency hematopoiesis, as part of the natural history of severe human disease.

Ebolavirus (EBOV), a nonsegmented, negative-strand RNA virus in the family Filoviridae, causes severe and often fatal multisystem organ dysfunction or failure in humans and nonhuman primates (NHPs).1 Despite significant progress over many decades, gaps in the current understanding of the pathophysiology of EBOV disease (EVD) remain, including those related to some of the longest-recognized clinical features.1,2 Pancytopenia (leukopenia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia), described even in early cases, is typical and characteristic in the peripheral blood of severe EVD patients and NHP models.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Recently, albeit from limited sampling, higher-resolution characterization of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from EVD patients has queried the role of emergency hematopoiesis from the bone marrow (BM) as a feature of severe disease.8,9 Despite these historical and more recent signposts of the role of BM in pathogenesis, systematic characterization of the structure and function of this important organ during EVD is unavailable.

BM is the primary site of hematopoiesis in three classes of peripheral blood cells: white blood cells (leukocytes), red blood cells (erythrocytes), and platelets (thrombocytes).10 Though BM involvement in EVD was postulated in some of the earliest case reports,11 molecular and histopathologic characterization has not been possible, except in rare cases, as sampling of BM tissue, whether in the clinical or postmortem setting, poses considerable challenges, including biosafety, and cultural, ethical, and resource-availability concerns.8,12,13 BM in human EVD has been histopathologically evaluated rarely, reported only once, and the number of samples summarized in that report was unclear; however, those limited data described normocellular marrow, focal necrosis, intracytoplasmic monocytic inclusion bodies, and/or EBOV antigen immunostaining positivity in BM from EVD patients.14 With regard to closely related viruses, a patient with related Sudan virus disease who died on day 8 after the onset of symptoms presented with granulocytic precursor cell vacuolization in the BM.6 To overcome the challenges in human specimen availability, animal models are crucially required to structurally and functionally characterize the BM in EVD toward understanding the pathophysiology of hematologic abnormalities and other potentially related EVD manifestations.

For decades, NHPs have been used extensively as the model of choice to explore the pathogenesis of a severe disease that includes characteristic pancytopenia. Although EBOV RNA or antigen detection in BM monocytes of EVD animal models, including NHPs, has been reported, BM involvement has not been systematically studied.15, 16, 17 Similar to findings from many previous reports, hematologic abnormalities were observed in EBOV-exposed rhesus macaques (RhMs) in a recent large-scale natural history study conducted in our laboratory, the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID).16,18,19 The opportunity was used to systematically characterize the histopathologic and immunohistochemical alterations in the BM of RhMs with the terminal disease compared to uninfected control subjects. Furthermore, the associations between BM findings and hematologic alterations during disease progression were investigated. These findings improve our understanding of how EBOV infection affects the BM and disrupts blood cell production.19

Materials and Methods

Virus and Animals

The information on virus, animals, and study design have been published previously.7 Briefly, 21 Chinese-origin RhMs were used in this study. Of those, 15 were split equally into five groups (3 RhMs per group) by prescheduled euthanasia day 0 (D0; uninfected control), D3, D4, D5, and D6, and at EBOV postexposure days (dpe) 0, 3, 4, 5, and 6, respectively. Six additional RhMs were designated as a terminal disease (T) control group and euthanized when they reached the end-stage EVD criteria (between 5 and 8 dpe). All RhMs were inoculated with 1 mL of 1000 PFU/mL Kikwit strain of EBOV administered intramuscularly in the left lateral triceps muscle at 0 dpe, except in 3 RhMs at D0 (uninfected control) that were administered a sham inoculation of 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline in the same anatomic location.

All RhMs used in this research project were cared for and used humanely according to the following policies: the US Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care and Use of Animals20; the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals21; and the US Government Principles for the Utilization and Care of Vertebrate Animals Used in Testing, Research, and Training.22 In addition, all NIAID–Integrated Research Facility animal facilities and programs are accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International. This study was performed in the Biosafety Level 4 Laboratory at the NIH/NIAID–Integrated Research Facility, Fort Detrick (Fredrick, MD).

Macroscopic and Microscopic Examination

All monkeys were euthanized and necropsied, as mentioned in the previous paragraph. Complete postmortem examinations were performed on all RhMs, and gross findings were documented. Tissues were fixed for >72 hours in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histopathology and other assays. Microscopic examination was performed on the routinely prepared hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained, paraffin-embedded, processed tissue sections. The observations were scored by ordinal categories of severity of normal, minimal, mild, moderate, and severe.23

Slides were scanned using an Aperio AT2 whole-slide scanner at ×40 magnification (Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, IL). The scanned images were analyzed by QuPath software version 0.3.2 (https://qupath.github.io, last accessed March 4, 2022). Briefly, the built-in annotation tool was used to randomly select 10 small regions of interest (100 to 200 cells per area) from a control slide, to establish the parameters for detecting nucleated cells on H&E-stained slides or positive cells on IHC-stained slides. Parameters were established by comparing the positive cell counts between the manual and the build-in tool (positive cell detection) in the selected areas. The parameter values and thresholds were fine-tuned until the maximal accuracy between the two positive cell count methods was reached. The optimized parameters and thresholds for nucleated or positive cell detection were automated using the scripting function and applied to all regions of interest.25, 26, 27 To analyze whole slides, a built-in annotation tool was used to outline the BM islands along the endosteum in an entire slide, so that the cortical and trabecular bones or significant artifacts (tissue folds) were excluded from regions of interest. To minimize bias due to variations in the size of the sternum and the spaces of and shape of the medullary cavity separated by trabeculae, the cellularity (mean nucleated cells/mm2) was calculated as the total nucleated cells/total detected area (mm2) on whole H&E slides. The positivity of IHC (mean positive cells/mm2) was calculated as total positive cells/total detected area (mm2) per slide. For the semiquantification of megakaryocyte (MK) emperipolesis, the number of MKs with emperipolesis and the number of MKs were counted manually in 10 randomly selected high-power (40× objective) fields per H&E slide per animal. The percentage of MKs with emperipolesis was calculated as total MKs with emperipolesis/total MKs.

RNAScope in Situ Hybridization

EBOV genomic or antigenomic (replicative intermediate) RNA was detected on 4-μm sections of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue collected at necropsy, using the manual in situ hybridization RNAscope for EBOV-VP40 genomic V-Z-EBOV-Mayinga-Zaire (catalog number 507141; Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA) and EBOV-VP35 antigenomic V-EBOV-VP35-sense (catalog number 527491; Advanced Cell Diagnostics) as previously described.23

Transmission Electron Microscopy

As previously described, BM samples from paraffin-embedded blocks were processed for transmission electron microscopy.28 Briefly, 70- to 80-nm–thick sections were collected on 200-mesh copper grids and post-stained with Reynold's lead citrate. Samples were examined using the FEI Tecnai Spirit Twin transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) operating at 80 kV.

Hematology

Tripotassium EDTA anticoagulated blood (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ) was analyzed on the Sysmex XT-2000iV hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) to measure different peripheral blood cell populations.

Statistical Analysis

The Dunnett multiple comparisons test was used to compare the mean value of D0 (baseline, 0 dpe) to other groups (or dpe) of D3 (3 dpe), D4 (4 dpe), D5 (5 dpe), D6 (6 dpe), and T (from 5 to 8 dpe). Correlation between parameters was determined with the two-tailed Pearson correlation method. All statistical analyses and graphical presentations were performed in Prism software version 9.1.1 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). P ≤ 0.05 was used to define statistical significance.

Results

EBOV Is Detected in Bone Marrow as Early as 3 dpe; CD68+ Monocytes/Macrophages Are the Primary Target Cells

To track early viral infection in BM of RhMs after exposure to EBOV, the virus antigen and RNA were examined by IHC and in situ hybridization. EBOV-VP40 and -GP antigens were detected as early as 3 dpe in a small number of BM cells [the morphology appears as either a single or multifocal macrophage(s)/monocyte(s)] (Figure 1, A and B). At 4 dpe onward, numerous BM cells were positive for EBOV-VP40 (Figure 1C), -GP antigen, and genomic RNA (Figure 1D) on IHC and in situ hybridization, respectively. EBOV replicative intermediate (antigenomic) RNA was detected in many BM cells from the T RhMs (Figure 1E). To verify which cell types were affected by EBOV, BM cells from T RhMs were immunostained for EBOV-VP35 and CD68 (a pan-macrophage or M1 marker) and other cell markers (Table 1). Based on the findings, EBOV-VP35 was predominantly colocalized with CD68+ marker (Figure 1F), indicating that CD68+ monocytes/macrophages are most EBOV-infected cells in the BM of EBOV-infected RhMs.

EBOV Is Detected in MKs but Not in Erythroid or Myeloid Hematopoietic Cells

To determine whether hematopoietic progenitor cells, including MKs, are infected by EBOV, BM cells from EBOV-exposed RhMs were examined using IHC, in situ hybridization, confocal microscopy, and transmission electron microscopy. Surprisingly, as early as 4 dpe (D4), EBOV-VP40 antigen was detected in the cytoplasm of a few MKs (Figure 1C). With disease progression, more MKs were positive for EBOV-VP40 (Figure 2A), EBOV-GP (Figure 2B), and EBOV-VP35 (Figure 2C) antigen and genomic RNA (Figure 2D) by IHC and in situ hybridization, respectively. However, EBOV inclusion bodies and replicative intermediate RNA were not observed in MKs.

Notably, MKs (CD41 marker) were highly colocalized with EBOV-VP35 expression in these BM samples (Figure 2E). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the presence of mature, filamentous EBOV particles in the demarcation channels of MKs in BM from EBOV-infected RhMs (Figure 2F). In contrast, EBOV antigens, viral RNA, and viral particles were not detected in erythroid (CD71+) and myeloid (MPO+) cells.

Progressive Bone Marrow Hypocellularity in EBOV-Infected RhMs

As the disease progressed, the most significant histopathologic observation was generalized hypocellularity of the BM, which became moderate to severe at 5 dpe (D5) and thereafter (Figure 3, A–C). Compared to controls, BM cords were more conspicuous, with dilated sinusoids lined by prominent, elongated endothelial cells and decreased cellularity of hematopoietic elements. In addition, BM cords presented a loosely arranged stroma with edema, fibrin, microhemorrhage, and many monocytes/macrophages compared to D0 controls. As BM hypocellularity advanced, a majority of the remaining hematopoietic cells were immature myeloid precursors characterized by hypolobulated nuclei (pseudo-Pelger form), irregular cell borders, and an increased nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio (Figure 3, D and E). Consistently, on semi-quantification by image analysis (SQIA), BM cellularity dropped at 3 dpe (D3) onward to 5 dpe (D5; P < 0.05), 6 dpe (D6; P < 0.01), and T (P < 0.01) compared to D0 controls (Figure 3F).

All Three Hemopoietic Stem Cells Are Depleted in the BM of EBOV-Infected RhMs

To investigate the impact of EBOV infection on BM progenitor cells, CD71, MPO, and CD41+ cells were quantified by histopathology and IHC analysis. BM CD71+ erythroid cells decreased markedly at 3 dpe and then dropped to undetectable levels in T RhMs (Figure 4, A–C). The remaining erythroid cells displayed features of less dense, eccentric nuclei, and more eosinophilic cytoplasm. SQIA confirmed that the mean number of CD71+ cells decreased significantly at D5, D6, and T compared to D0 controls (P < 0.001, P < 0.001, and P < 0.0001, respectively) (Figure 4D). Anemia was detected in these EBOV-infected RhMs,19 although there was no statistically significant correlation between the peripheral red blood cell count and BM CD71+ cells (data not shown). However, EBOV-infected RhMs presented with significant reticulocytopenia at D4, D5, D6, and T (P = 0.0072, P < 0.0001, P < 0.0003, and P < 0.0001) (Figure 4E). BM CD71+ cell count and the peripheral reticulocyte count were positively correlated (P < 0.0001, r = 0.935) (Figure 4F).

MPO+ cells decreased markedly at D3 (3 dpe) onward (Figure 5, A–C). SQIA confirmed that the mean number of MPO+ cells/mm2 decreased markedly from D0 to D3 and significantly at D4, D5, D6, and T compared to D0 controls (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.05, and P < 0.001) (Figure 5D). Similar to findings from previous studies of EVD in NHPs, the RhMs with EVD in this study also showed a significant increase in neutrophil count.16, 17, 18 However, after the peak at 4 dpe, the absolute neutrophils declined markedly afterward (Figure 5E). Moreover, SQIA quantification confirmed qualitative histopathologic observations, indicating an increase in the myeloid/erythroid precursor ratio (MPO+/CD71+) by day, reaching its highest point (approximately 900:1) at T in RhMs (Figure 5F).

The number of hematopoietic MKs (CD41+) typically decreased each day in EBOV-infected RhMs, but the severity of the decrease varied among individuals. Most MKs displayed irregular cytoplasmic outlines, fewer cytoplasmic granules, and nuclei that were hypolobulated and/or peripheralized. MKs were decreased gradually from D3 onward (Figure 6, A–C). SQIA confirmed that the mean number of CD41+ cells steadily decreased and became significantly lower at D5, D6, and T compared to D0 controls (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.01) (Figure 6D). Additionally, a statistically significant positive correlation between BM CD41+ cells and the peripheral blood platelet count19 was observed (P < 0.0001, r = 0.7905) (Figure 6E).

BMs from EBOV-Infected RhMs Present with an Increase in Megakaryocyte Emperipolesis, Release of Immature Hemopoietic Cells, and Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis but No Significant Change in Cell Apoptosis

A number of histopathologic findings in the BM of EBOV-infected RhMs, especially at T, were particularly noteworthy. First, MK emperipolesis increased markedly at 5 dpe and onward, although they were observed occasionally in the BMs of D0 monkeys. In comparison to D0 controls, in which MKs usually engulfed a single cell, the MKs from T RhMs engulfed more cells, ranging from three to eight immature myeloid and/or erythroid cells (Figure 7A), and were EBOV-VP35 antigen positive (Figure 2C). SQIA confirmed that the percentages of MKs with emperipolesis were significantly greater at D5, D6, and T compared to D0 controls (all, P < 0.0001) (Figure 7B). Second, many immature MKs and myeloid cells (immature hemopoietic cells) were observed within dilated BM sinusoids, especially in BM from monkeys with end-stage EVD. These MKs presented with hypolobulated, round, and compact nuclei occupying almost the entire cytoplasm (Figure 7C), and were also EBOV-VP40 antigen positive (Figure 2A). Another remarkable histopathologic finding, as observed in a previous study,19 was a marked increase in hemophagocytosis, where numerous activated macrophages phagocytized one or more myeloid and/or erythroid precursor cells in their swollen cytoplasm (Figure 7D).

Discussion

Though the depletion of BM cells in an EVD convalescent patient was suggested as early as 1976,11 BM morphologic and immunobiochemical examinations are extremely rare for obvious reasons. It has been questioned for decades whether emergency hematologic abnormalities are associated with BM failure in acute EVD, despite the frequent observation of peripheral cytopenia, especially in severe disease in humans and lethal NHP models, including EBOV-infected RhMs in this study.3,4,6,11,14,16,17,29 Nonetheless, more recent flow cytometric or cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) evaluation in a few human patients and peripheral profiling in the NHPs in this study (enabled via CyTOF and single-cell RNA sequencing) suggest the peripheral footprints of (ineffective) emergency hematopoiesis and BM dysfunction as features of severe EVD.8,9 This study in the RhM model demonstrated for the first time that EBOV infection causes significant BM hypocellularity by reducing all three hematopoietic lineages during acute infection. Collectively, these data suggest that hematologic disorders in patients with EVD could result from a depletion of BM hemopoietic cells.

Thrombocytopenia is the most common hematologic finding in EVD patients and NHP models, although the mechanism is unclear.30,31 Platelet consumption as a part of disseminated intravascular coagulation has been hypothesized previously.31 However, the responses and functionalities of BM hematopoiesis during EVD progression have not been studied, despite that EBOV particles were observed in the MKs in EBOV-infected RhMs by electron microscopy 3 decades ago.17 This study reconfirmed that EBOV was detected in BM monocytes/macrophages and MKs as early as 3 and 4 dpe, respectively. The observation of severe BM hypocellularity, including a significant reduction in MKs, suggests that BM failure may play a major role in thrombocytopenia. First, a significant decrease in BM MKs that correlates with thrombocytopenia suggests insufficient production of platelets from the BM. Second, the absence of MK proliferation in the BM is evidenced by the negative correlation between BM MKs and BM Ki67+ cells. Finally, the observation of many immature MKs in the BM sinusoids suggests an early release of immature MKs into the peripheral blood. Thrombocytopenia associated with direct or indirect involvement of MKs has been associated with viral infections.32, 33, 34 For example, dengue virus can infect BM MKs, leading to a considerable decrease in mature MK count, ultimately hindering the production of platelets (in the BM) and leading to thrombocytopenia.32,35 In this study, EBOV particles, antigens, and genomic RNA were detected in the MKs, but EBOV inclusion bodies and replicative intermediate RNA were not detected. Whether MKs are directly infected by EBOV or phagocytize EBOV particles with infected cells through MK emperipolesis is not clear. Further interrogation of the specific interaction between MKs and filoviruses in vitro and in vivo is warranted.

Anemia has been observed in acute EVD patients and NHP models.4,36, 37, 38 Also, persistent anemia is a common clinical manifestation in EVD convalescent patients.37,38 Although the EBOV-infected RhMs in this study also presented with anemia, a statistically significant correlation was not observed between BM erythroid cell depletion and anemia. The absence of a correlation could have been due to several factors, such as the small size of RhMs per time point, the severe dehydration observed in EBOV-infected RhMs (potentially artificially elevating peripheral blood counts), or that disease-related anemia originates elsewhere mechanistically, such as red blood cell destruction or blood loss.19,39 Similar to the findings from a previous study,29 this study also showed peripheral reticulocytopenia, which was notably positively correlated with a progressive decrease in BM erythropoietic (CD71+) cells. In addition, BM erythropoietic cells did not appear to be proliferating, at least as proxied by the negative correlation between BM erythropoietic (CD71+) and Ki67+ cell positivity. Taken together, anemia with an insufficient reticulocyte response and a decrease in erythroid progenitors suggests erythropoietic dysfunction and deficiency in the erythroid cell regeneration process in the BM.40 EBOV RNA and antigen were not detected in erythroid precursors; any interaction would likely be mediated by bystander cells or a local or systemic cytokine milieu. This concept is poorly studied, but an asymmetric negative effect of EBOV antigen on in vitro erythroid (but not granulocytic or monocytic) colony formation has been reported.41

A significant reduction in BM MPO+ cells by IHC indicates a decline in myeloid cells from promyelocytes to mature neutrophils.42 Neutrophilia is the most common clinical manifestation in EVD patients and NHP models, particularly in the early stages.16,18,43 According to the findings from this study and previous research, it is evident that there is an issue with the replenishment of neutrophils from the BM, which begins to decline noticeably starting at 4 dpe.5,17 Histopathologically, immature neutrophils were also observed in the BM in this study and in the peripheral blood of both EVD patients and monkeys in previous studies.16,43 In our previous study, single-cell sequencing (of these particular NHPs) revealed increased immature neutrophils in the peripheral blood.9 This study showed a negative correlation between the number of BM myeloid progenitors and Ki67+ cells. These data suggest insufficient myeloid regeneration and an early release of immature neutrophils in EBOV-infected RhMs. The generation of unproductive and immature neutrophils as part of emergency myelopoiesis may produce inadequate and potentially immunopathologic innate antiviral defenses against acute EBOV infection.44

The myeloid/erythroid ratio was increased in this study, particularly in the RhMs at terminal stages. While the exact cause of this finding is unclear, a decrease in red blood cells as early as 1 dpe in these RhMs indicates that EBOV infection may affect erythroid cells before myeloid cells in the BM.19 It is important to consider the impact of MPO positivity in BM monocytes. MPO, which is in the heme peroxidase-cyclooxygenase superfamily, is primarily expressed by myeloid cells but can also be found on activated monocytes.42,45 Consistent with previous findings of activation of monocytes in the blood and resident macrophages in tissues,19 the observation of a significant increase in the presence of BM CD163+/CD68+ monocytes/macrophages in EVD macaques suggests that MPO on monocytes may have a crucial impact on the myeloid/erythroid ratio. Although this study did not differentiate the effects of neutrophil and macrophage-mediated MPO production, future research will investigate this distinction more thoroughly. Notably, these NHPs have previously been described (by single-cell RNA sequencing and CyTOF profiling) to have an unusual population of circulating CD14–CD16– double-negative monocytes with a high expression of MPO and other neutrophil genes that suggest emergence from marrow early myeloid progenitors, rather than from circulating monocytes.9

Based on the morphologic observations, BM hypocellularity features a combination of hemophagocytosis, MK emperipolesis, and the early release of immature hematopoietic cell populations. Hemophagocytosis has been observed in multiple organs, including BM in EVD RhMs in this study and EVD patients with similar features of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis/macrophage activation syndrome.19,46 One of the typical morphologic features is hemophagocytosis, in which the activated macrophages/monocytes engulf lymphocytes, neutrophils, and erythrocytes in the spleen, lymph node, and Kupffer cells in the liver, as well as in the BM, especially in late-stage EBOV-infected RhMs.19 Although the mechanism by which BM hemophagocytosis contributes to BM hypocellularity is unknown, it appears to be a typical response to sustained or severe inflammation with hypercytokinemia, especially in the presence of a high concentration of interferon (IFN)-γ.47 Studies in animals have demonstrated that administering anti–IFN-γ antibodies to hemophagocytic, lymphohistiocytic mice can hinder macrophage activation and thus prevent hemophagocytosis, pancytopenia, and other associated manifestations.48, 49, 50, 51

Even though it is a relatively common phenomenon in the healthy BM of humans and other mammals, MK emperipolesis was significantly greater in terminal EBOV-infected RhMs versus uninfected controls. The mechanism and significance of this phenomenon remain undetermined. Thrombocytopenia and hemorrhage can hemostatically result in a greater need for platelets, leading to a significant increase in emperipolesis.52, 53, 54 A recent study suggests that emperipolesis represents a novel pathway employed by myeloid and hematopoietic cells to influence the composition and generation of circulating platelets through interactions with MKs.55 EBOV virions have been detected in MKs in this and a previous study by transmission electron microscopy.17 However, the significance of EBOV MK infection is unknown. Studies have confirmed the capacity of dengue virus to infect human K562 MK cultures in vitro56 and to cause a significant reduction in BM MK populations and ultimately generate thrombocytopenia in in vivo humanized mice.35

The observation of many immature MKs and other hemopoietic cells in the BM vascular sinusoids suggests that immature hematopoietic cells are released early in response to high demand.52 The mechanism and significance of this process remain unknown. However, emergency hematopoiesis, an expansion of hematopoietic stem cells and myeloid progenitors in response to any physiologic insult, including infection, has been well-described.57,58 High parameter single-cell RNA sequencing and CyTOF characterization of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from a small number of EVD patients and these specific NHPs suggests evidence for this mechanism.8,9 Emergency hematopoiesis in the setting of sepsis results in the production of myeloid-derived suppressor cells that have immunosuppressive effects.59 Various factors could be involved in the defect in BM hematopoiesis associated with EVD. First, the direct infection of MKs could suppress the differentiation and survival of hematopoietic cells. Second, when IFN-γ levels are high, they can hinder the self-renewal, proliferation, and differentiation of hematopoietic cells in BM, ultimately inhibiting hematopoiesis.60, 61, 62, 63 Indeed, in a previous study, macrophage activation (increased sCD163) with hypercytokinemia, including cytokine IFN-γ, has been reported in these EVD RhMs.19 Also, the significance of the structural and physiologic disruption to the integrity of the BM microenvironment caused by EBOV infection cannot be ignored. For successful hematopoiesis, the integrity of the BM microenvironment must be maintained to keep hematopoietic stem cells in place and provide the hematopoietic growth factors necessary for growth, differentiation, and maturity of these cells along specific lineages.10 According to the findings from recent studies, IFN-γ can potentially harm BM stromal cells, leading to damage in the microenvironment maintenance necessary to support hematopoietic stem cells.64 As a result, these factors can lead to decreased platelet counts and the production of immature red blood cells. Of interest, in RhMs infected with Bundibugyo virus (a closely related filovirus), lethality (with sustained innate immunity and lack of an appropriate adaptive immune response) has been associated with greater expression of myeloid-derived suppressor cell transcripts.65 It has been observed in pigs that African swine fever virus infection leads to a decline in MKs and an increase in the release of immature hematopoietic cells from the BM.66

A limitation of this study was the lack of flow-cytometry data or other high-parameter characterization of the BM due to the inadequate availability of BM samples. Fortunately, data from high-parameter peripheral flow cytometry and CyTOF/single-cell RNA sequencing were available from the same NHP data set to inform the interpretation of these data.9,19 Although biomarkers used in this study are specific to each hematopoietic cell lineage, more biomarkers may provide additional information about the changes in BM associated with EBOV infection.67 Even though this study used 21 RhMs, the data may be subject to some variations due to the small sample size of only 3 RhMs per group.

Nonetheless, this first systematic characterization of the BM in a lethal NHP model of EVD demonstrated that EBOV infection leads to pathologic consequences that include depletion of BM hematopoietic cells along with severe BM hypocellularity in the setting of simultaneously correlated peripheral thrombocytopenia and reticulocytopenia. BM dysfunction/failure, including emergency hematopoiesis, likely underlies the hematologic abnormalities characteristic of EVD and may also contribute to immune dysregulation and immunopathology. These findings inform current pathophysiologic unknowns and argue for continued work to elucidate both the causes and consequences of EBOV-associated damage and dysfunction in BM, highlighting the need for further characterization of the host–pathogen interaction in this historically underattended organ.
