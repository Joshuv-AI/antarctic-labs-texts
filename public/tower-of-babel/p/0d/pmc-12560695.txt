Does Fiber Orientation Distribution Really Outperform Diffusion Tensor Imaging-Based Tractography in the Clinic? A Diagnostic Test Accuracy Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Intraoperative Tractography
Authors: Johannes H. Reilly, Mitra L. Neymeyer, Meghan R. Forrest, Stephen Schüürhuis, Peter Vajkoczy, Julia Onken, Thomas Picht
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Tractography of the corticospinal tract (CST) is routinely used for neurosurgical planning and navigation. It allows the noninvasive, patient-specific visualization of fiber tracts which can be used for surgical planning and risk stratification. This systematic review and meta-analysis sought to synthesize the accuracy of CST tractography by comparing diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)–based and fiber orientation distribution (FOD)–based tractography approaches to direct (sub)cortical electrical stimulation of motor fibers.METHODS:We systematically searched 4 databases (MEDLINE Ovid, Embase Ovid, Web of Science, CENTRAL) on 01.10.2024. Studies were included if they were (1) cross-sectional (2) original research studies that (3) had participants with motor-eloquent pathologies undergoing surgery, (4) performed intraoperative mapping of the CST, (5) compared stimulation sites with tractography, and (6) reported measures of test accuracy, e.g., sensitivity and specificity. Risk of bias was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 tool. Data were analyzed using a fixed-effect logistic regression model to derive pooled effect estimates of sensitivity and specificity of DTI-based and FOD-based tractography.RESULTS:We included 16 studies of which 12 used DTI-based, 2 used FOD-based, and 2 used both DTI-based and FOD-based tractography. For DTI-based tractography, sensitivity ranged from 25% to 100% and specificity from 55.6% to 100%. For FOD-based tractography, sensitivity ranged from 36% to 79% and specificity from 77% to 98%. Clinically, 10 studies reported functional outcomes, although the time of postoperative testing varied: 1 study reported a decrease, 6 a preservation, and 3 an improvement in motor function at follow-up.CONCLUSION:Overall, incomplete reporting prevented a quantitative comparison of DTI-based and FOD-based tractography. Although 2 studies indicate that FOD-based tractography may be superior to DTI-based tractography for sensitivity in a clinical setting, evidence to this end is, as of yet, insufficient. Future cross-sectional studies evaluating multiple tractography approaches within one setting are recommended.

Neuro-oncological surgery is undergoing a paradigm shift toward preserving the core connectivity in the brain.1 Anatomically, the core connectivity is underpinned by white matter fiber tracts which are visualized using tractography. Damage to fiber tracts may result in temporary or even permanent functional deficits,2 severely reducing patients' quality of life and oncological prognosis.3

Consequently, surgical planning increasingly relies on accurate tractography methods—not only for lesions adjacent to tracts but also for deep brain stimulation or when approaching deep lesions through eloquent white matter.4 The application of tractography in this context is particularly challenging since it is performed in regions affected by mass effects and edema resulting from tumor growth.5

It remains uncertain which tractography method offers the most accurate representation of the anatomo-functional relationship between tractography and white matter tracts. This hinders the ability to adopt a specific method as the clinical standard. Previous studies investigated this by comparing direct electrical stimulation of the corticospinal tract (CST) with its tractography as CST mapping can be performed asleep and reliably produces quantifiable motor responses.

Mapping through Direct Electrical Stimulation: The CST can be mapped by applying a current to the brain parenchyma through direct (sub)cortical electrical stimulation (DES).6 If this stimulation elicits a motor response (e.g., motor-evoked potentials measured by electrodes in target muscles), it is an indication that a fiber tract passes through the stimulated tissue. Although DES is the most reliable and objective method, the gold standard, to locate the CST, it is invasive and not suitable for presurgical planning.

Tractography uses computational approaches to visualize the CST perioperatively using diffusion magnetic resonance imaging data. The accuracy of tractography may be evaluated by comparison with DES: true (false) positives are response-evoking DES points that do (not) overlap with tractography and false (true) negatives are non–response-evoking DES points that do (not) overlap with tractography. Broadly, two approaches to tractography can be differentiated based on how diffusion data are preprocessed: diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)–based and fiber orientation distribution (FOD)–based tractography.

DTI-based tractography: After the development of diffusion-weighted imaging, one of the first mathematical models developed and validated to model the diffusivity of water was DTI.7 DTI relies on fitting a Gaussian distribution on the diffusion direction of water where the peak corresponds to the main diffusion direction per voxel and, by extension, the vector of the fiber tract. However, through this Gaussian constraint, DTI models assume a single-fiber orientation per voxel which causes anatomically implausible fiber reconstructions in regions of complex fiber arrangements, e.g., crossing, interdigitating, or fanning fibers.8

FOD-based tractography: An alternative, later developed model that does not impose a Gaussian constraint is the FOD model. FOD-based tractography considers multiple diffusion maxima per voxel, e.g., by applying spherical deconvolution, which separates the diffusion signal into contributions from individual coherent fiber populations using a response function.9 Thereby, nondominant diffusion orientations per voxel are captured, partially overcoming the limitations of DTI-based tractography in regions where the local diffusion pattern is too complex to be captured by a single diffusion direction per voxel.9

Clinical practice: Owing to its relative simplicity and availability, DTI-based tractography remains a commonly used method in clinical practice despite its limitations. However, studies increasingly suggest that FOD-based tractography may provide a more reliable alternative for both research and clinical purposes, despite longer scan times to acquire sufficient diffusion directions and preprocessing steps that are not (yet) commonly available in neuronavigation systems.10

While FOD-based approaches may be associated with larger tract volumes11 and thus may increase the number of false-positive streamlines,5 they likely achieve higher accuracy than DTI-based tractography because an estimated <90% of voxels contain more than 1 fiber orientation. This presents a serious limitation to DTI-based tractography.

Objectives: Our systematic review and meta-analysis aim to review the available evidence and where possible, quantitatively synthesize the accuracy of DTI-based and FOD-based tractography to visualize the CST in individuals undergoing surgery for motor eloquent lesions. Because motor responses can be reliably and objectively measured, this meta-analysis will focus on studies comparing DES of the CST with its tractography.

METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis was preregistered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024587285; available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42024587285). All protocols for this systematic review were developed in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy.12,13 This review was performed using CADIMA systematic review manager (https://www.cadima.info; for review14). Detailed methods and additional data from this review can be found on our GitHub repository (https://github.com/meghanrforrest/sr_fod-dti-tractography).

Search Strategy

Our search strategy was developed by an information specialist (M. R. F.). The following electronic publication databases were searched from inception to October 1, 2024: MEDLINE Ovid, Embase Ovid, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) in the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. No filter was applied for publication date or language.

In addition to a search of electronic publication databases, we performed forward and backward citation screening of included references using Citationchaser15 and conducted grey literature searches in the Bielefeld Academic Search Engine (https://www.base-search.net) and the Center for Research Libraries Library Catalog (https://catalog.crl.edu). The full search strategy is detailed in the supplementary materials (Supplemental Digital Content 1. Methods, http://links.lww.com/NS9/A81).

Selection Criteria

We included all records in our sample which (1) were original research, (2) were cross-sectional, (3) had human participants undergoing brain surgery (Population), (4) tractography was performed (Index test) and compared with (5) DES of motor fibers and intraoperative neuromonitoring of motor evoked potentials (Reference test), and (6) reported measures of the diagnostic test accuracy16 of CST tractography (sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values).

Records were excluded if they were not original research (e.g., review articles, commentary, and editorial). However, these records were retained and reviewed for references to other records that could be eligible for inclusion in our review. We did not exclude records on the basis of language or publication date.

Record Selection

Potential duplicates flagged by CADIMA were manually reviewed and removed by either J. H. R. or M. R. F. The remaining records underwent a 2-stage screening process: title/abstract screening and then full-text screening. Both stages of screening were performed by 2 independent reviewers. Records screened during title/abstract screening (performed by J. H. R. and either M. L. N. or M. R. F.) that appeared to meet all inclusion criteria, or for which eligibility was uncertain, underwent full-text screening by 2 independent reviewers (J. H. R. and M. L. N.). Discrepancies during screening were resolved through consensus among the 2 reviewers who made the initial decision.

A full record of exclusion reasons is available in our GitHub repository (https://github.com/meghanrforrest/sr_fod-dti-tractography).

Extraction

Data of each article were manually extracted by 2 independent reviewers (J. H. R. and M. L. N.) using CADIMA. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the reviewers that made the initial decisions.

The following data were extracted from each record:-Population characteristics (age, sex, sample size, type of tumor)-Tractography parameters (diffusion imaging gradient directions, b-value, voxel size, and method of tractography creation)-Index test (i.e., FOD or DTI)-Measures of test accuracy (e.g., sensitivity, specificity, and contingency tables of true/false positives and negatives)-Functional outcomes after surgery as assessed by clinical tests (e.g., modified Rankin Scale and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale)

If data were missing or unclear, the corresponding author of the article was contacted through email. In case of no reply, a follow-up email was sent 1 week after the initial email. Corresponding authors were granted a total of 2 weeks to respond.

Critical Appraisal

Included studies were assessed for risk of bias by querying Retraction Watch Database (http://retractiondatabase.org/RetractionSearch.aspx) to confirm none of the included studies have been retracted.

The quality of evidence of all included articles was evaluated by 2 independent reviewers (J. H. R. and M. L. N.) using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) critical appraisal tool.17 Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus among J. H. R. and M. L. N.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was based on Schlattmann's tutorial16 and performed in R Studio (https://posit.co) version 2024.09.1 + 394 (R packages: forestplot_3.1.5, abind_1.4-8, checkmate_2.1.0, rje_1.12.1, lme4_1.1-35.1, emmeans_1.10.5, dplyr_1.1.4, meta_8.0-1, mada_0.5.11, metafor_4.6-0, numDeriv_2016.8-1.1, metadat_1.2-0, Matrix_1.6-4, mvmeta_1.0.3, ellipse_0.5.0, mvtnorm_1.2-4, readxl_1.4.3). All studies that passed full-text screening and reported a 2 × 2 contingency table were eligible for meta-analysis. Contingency tables counted true positives and false negatives (indicating the number of stimulation points that overlapped with tractography) and false positives and true negatives (indicating the number of stimulation points that did not overlap with tractography).

Sensitivity and specificity were analyzed separately using univariate fixed-effects and random-effects logistic regression models with 95% CIs. A random-effects logistic regression model was used if the assessment of heterogeneity, through I2, the Wald type test, and critical appraisal, indicated interstudy variability in effect estimates. To assess the overall diagnostic performance of tractography, the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) was calculated. The higher the discriminatory performance of tractography, the higher the DOR value. Next, a bivariate random-effects logistic regression model was conducted to analyze the relationship between sensitivity and specificity, accounting for interstudy variability. The effect of varying accuracy thresholds on discriminatory power was investigated by plotting a scatterplot of sensitivity vs 1-specificity and summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) curve and the performance quantified through the (partial) area under the curve ((p)AUC). For pAUC, the calculation is restricted to the observed false-positive rates. Thus, unlike AUC, the pAUC avoids extrapolation beyond the observed data. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots of the DOR.

The number of included studies was expected to be small. In such cases, pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity may vary greatly depending on the regression model. A sensitivity analysis assessed robustness of pooled estimates independent of model choice by using different models. The results of all models are reported.

Heterogeneity was assessed through I2, H, and Cochrane Q statistic. P-values below an alpha level of 0.05 were considered statistically significant. An overview of the results of each analysis will be provided in tables displaying (1) cohort demographics, (2) diffusion imaging and tractography parameters, (3) intraoperative mapping parameters, and (4) accuracy measures extracted from each study.

Risk of Bias

No articles were retracted as of 01.12.2024. All studies presented medium-quality to high-quality evidence based on critical appraisal using the QUADAS-2 tool (Supplemental Digital Content 1. Table 2, http://links.lww.com/NS9/A81). Regarding the QUADAS-2 domain “patient selection,” there was uncertainty in 10 studies whether a consecutive or random patient sample was selected, and for the domain “flow and timing,” it was unclear in 4 studies whether all included patients were analyzed.

The main source of potential bias across studies arose from the QUADAS-2 domains “index test” and “reference standard” and pertained to (1) uncertainty whether tractography was performed preoperatively or postoperatively and (2) if performed preoperatively, whether the tractography was displayed in the neuronavigation during surgery. Displaying tractography in neuronavigation is a helpful and routine procedure in surgeries of motor-eloquent areas; however, it may influence where surgeons conducted mapping. Thus, the number of true positives and true negatives may be inflated. Indeed, visual inspection of funnel plots (Supplemental Digital Content 1. Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/NS9/A81) suggests asymmetry, potentially indicative of publication bias. Although there was no statistical evidence for publication bias in the meta-analyzed studies (t = −1.96, df = 4, P = .122), the statistical test may be underpowered due to the small sample size, and the possibility of publication or selective-reporting bias remains.

Meta-Analysis of Accuracy of DTI-Based Tractography

The heterogeneity of effect sizes between studies was not statistically significant (Q(5) = 4.94, P = .423, I2 = 0%). In combination with the critical appraisal and assessment of publication bias, this suggests that any variation between studies may be attributed to sampling error and not to differences in methodology or patient population. Based on this, a fixed-effects logistic regression model was deemed appropriate.

Assuming a fixed effect, i.e., the same underlying true sensitivity and specificity in every study, the pooled estimates for sensitivity and specificity were 84.45% (95% CI: 0.812, 0.872) and 99.03% (95% CI: 0.980, 0.995), respectively (Table 5). The fixed-effect DOR is 258.72 (95% CI: 123.624, 541.466), indicating discriminatory ability of tractography in identifying electrophysiologically confirmed tracts. As sensitivity analyses, random-effects logistic regression and bivariate random-effects logistic regression models, as well as stratified analyses, revealed comparable results, suggesting that the pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity are robust (Supplemental Digital Content 1. Results, Supplemental Digital Content 1. Tables 3-4; forest plot in Supplemental Digital Content 1. Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/NS9/A81).

Accuracy of DTI-Based Compared with FOD-Based Tractography

Two articles used both DTI-based and FOD-based tractography within the same sample24,30 enabling a direct comparison of specificity and sensitivity. Both studies reported shorter raw distances of DES points and FOD-based compared with DTI-based tractography,24,30 especially for positive DES points.30 Consequently, FOD-based tractography was found to have higher sensitivity than DTI-based tractography.24,30 Although Radwan et al30 report higher specificity for DTI-based approaches, the ROC curve analysis and Youden index identified a shorter optimal cutoff distance for FOD (5.5 mm) compared with DTI (13.5 mm), suggesting that FOD-based tractography is able to reach an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity at shorter distances to the tract than DTI-based tractography.

FOD-based tractography may outperform DTI-based tractography not primarily due to its larger tract volume but its ability to display tracts in regions affected by edema. While both studies reported higher tractogram volumes for FOD-based compared with DTI-based tractography,24,30 post hoc analysis indicated that the improved sensitivity of FOD over DTI was not solely due to larger tractogram volumes.30 Rather, FOD may outperform DTI because of its ability to navigate edematous regions where fractional anisotropy decreases and mean diffusivity increases. In these regions, CST tractography was reported to have a thinned appearance24 and DTI-based tractography algorithms may fail to determine a principal diffusion direction, leading to missing streamlines.30

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantify the diagnostic accuracy of DTI-based and FOD-based tractography compared with DES for patients undergoing surgery for motor-eloquent pathologies. Per our inclusion criteria, the articles identified in our systematic review were cross-sectional studies that use DES during surgery of motor eloquent lesions. This systematic review identified 16 studies meeting all inclusion criteria, of which 6 were able to be included in a meta-analysis. All studies had similar risk of bias. The study22 with the highest risk of bias scored “high” on the domains “index test” and “reference test.”

The present findings provide evidence that both DTI-based and FOD-based tractography have high sensitivity/specificity, reaching 100%/100% and 79%/98%, respectively. DTI-based tractography had a pooled estimated sensitivity of 84.45% and specificity of 99.03%. Importantly, the 2 studies24,30 that used both DTI-based and FOD-based tractography, thus enabling a direct comparison, found FOD-based tractography to have evidence of higher sensitivity than DTI-based tractography.34,35

The statistical accuracy of tractography as described in this meta-analysis appears to align with the previously reported increase in preservation of motor function after surgery with compared with without tractography.36 Of the included studies, 10 assessed motor function before and after surgery, by evaluating functional outcome scores (modified Rankin scale,20,25 medical research council score,19,22,25,28,31 Karnofsky Performance Score,19,21 World Health Organization performance status,28 unclear18,26,32) for different time points (from 24 hours postsurgery19,28 up to ≥3 months19,22,25,28). All studies included in our meta-analysis used DTI-based tractography. Only 1 study reported a decrease in postsurgical motor function,20 6 reported a preservation,18,21,25,28,31,32 and 3 reported an improvement.19,22,26 This suggests that tractography is not only statistically accurate but also a clinically meaningful tool for preserving motor function in surgery.

Limitations

This systematic review aimed to review the available evidence which might benefit from additionally comparing tractography methods. It was not possible to statistically compare FOD-based to DTI-based tractography because (1) the sample size was small, (2) only 2 studies jointly evaluated DTI-based and FOD-based tractography24,30 (Tables 3 and 4), and (3) no study reported a 2 × 2 contingency table for FOD-based tractography (Table 4), preventing a calculation of pooled estimates of accuracy of FOD-based tractography. The fact that clinical studies often evaluate only a single out of multiple possible methods is a recognized problem in meta-research which is amplified when systematic reviews synthesize each method separately.37

By only examining one method per study, multiple different methods are found to be potentially useful in clinical practice. However, without evaluating multiple methods in similar patient cohorts, one cannot conclusively compare the accuracy estimates between methods and determine which method is most beneficial.37 This is further complicated by variations in sample age and pathologies, the use of different thresholds for calculating accuracy, and inconsistent methods for brain shift correction. For example, the current systematic review found thresholds varying from 0 mm27,33 to 10 mm,19 as well as brain shift corrections ranging from prophylactically expanding the coordinates of the stimulation points by 1 cubic centimeter23 to intraoperative MRI-based tractography.22 Although the heterogeneity in patient population and methodology reflects the current clinical application of tractography, it is essential to remember that a synthesis of such data must be interpreted with caution. Until more homogenous data are available, a meta-synthesis can only provide an indication of the directionality of results.

To address this, future studies evaluating surgical applications of tractography may (1) include more than 1 tractography method, (2) report 2 × 2 contingency tables for each method, and (3) apply a common threshold value across methods. A study to evaluate DTI-based, FOD-based, navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation–based DTI, and navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation FOD–based tractography with navigated ultrasound correction for brain shift is currently being conducted.38

CONCLUSION

Tractography offers unique advantages for neurosurgery as it currently is the only noninvasive method of patient-tailored visualization of critical fiber tracts that need to be spared to preserve function. By visualizing the relation between pathology and tracts, the surgical access route and resection strategy can be planned preoperatively and evaluated intraoperatively by overlaying tractography onto the navigation systems or even the surgical microscope.39,40 Furthermore, tractography aids in patient communication and counselling when stratifying surgical risks.41 Hence, an understanding of which tractography method best serves surgical planning and navigation purposes is essential.

This systematic review and meta-analysis summarized intraoperative evaluations of tractography indicating that both DTI-based and FOD-based tractography have high sensitivity and specificity. However, only 2 studies directly compared DTI-based and FOD-based tractography, showing evidence of higher concordance between DES and FOD-based compared with DTI-based tractography. This systematic review therefore reveals that as of yet intraoperative evidence remains too limited to confidently conclude that FOD is more useful for clinical purposes than DTI-based tractography. Further research directly comparing multiple tractography methods within the same cohort is highly recommended.
