Optimized FDA Blood Pump: A Case Study in System-Level Customized Ventricular Assist Device Designs
Authors: Canberk Yıldırım, Kağan Uçak, Ali Madayen, Tansu Gölcez, Hakan Ertürk, Özgür Uğraş Baran, Kerem Pekkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe design and development of ventricular assist devices have heavily relied on computational tools, particularly computational fluid dynamics (CFD), since the early 2000s. However, traditional CFD-based optimization requires costly trial-and-error approaches involving multiple design cycles. This study aims to propose a more efficient VAD design and optimization framework that overcomes these limitations.MethodsWe developed a system- and component-level ventricle assist device optimization approach by coupling a lumped parameter cardiovascular physiology model with parametric turbomachinery, volute design, and blade path generation packages. The framework incorporates pump hydrodynamic losses and is validated against experimental data from six distinct blood pump designs and CFD simulations. The optimization framework allows for the specification of both physiology-related and device-related objective functions to generate optimized blood pump configurations over a large parameter space.ResultsThe optimization was applied to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) benchmark blood pump as the baseline design. Results showed that an optimized FDA pump, maintaining the same cardiac output and aortic pressure, achieved a ~ 32% reduction in blade tip velocity compared to the baseline, resulting in an ~ 88% reduction in hemolysis. Additionally, an alternative design with a 40% reduction in blood-wetted area was generated while preserving the baseline pressure and flow.ConclusionThe proposed optimization framework improves device development efficiency by shortening the design cycle and enabling hydrodynamically optimized pumps that perform well across diverse patient hemodynamics. The optimized pump designs are available as open-source resources for further research and development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10439-025-03834-8.Keywords: Ventricle assist devices, Optimization, Computational fluid dynamics, Lumped parameter modeling, Benchmark devices, FDA blood pump, Cardiovascular engineering

Introduction

State-of-the-art ventricle assist device (VAD) design and optimization primarily rely on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) studies. Studies available in literature were conducted to improve the pump designs in terms of pressure head, pump flow, efficiency, and hemolysis performance by iteratively changing the blade parameters, such as thickness, inlet/outlet angle, wrap angle, splitters, and tip clearance [1–5]. These investigations usually utilized a trial-and-error approach based on a considerable number of design cycles and required multiple CFD runs to reach the desired design specifications. Alternatively, several researchers integrated CFD with optimization algorithms to test a wider range of design alternatives with less computational cost and time, to satisfy hydrodynamic and hemolysis objectives [6–10]. However, none of these studies include all the design parameters; generally only blade parameters or main impeller dimensions are aimed at being optimized in CFD. Therefore, a new design framework which includes the lumped parameter model (LPM) as a preliminary design tool, and CFD for detailed investigation is proposed in this study.

Although, there are available LPM-based pump design models in literature [11–13], they mainly focused on satisfying the required pump head and flow rate through extremely simple pump design modules. For example, current 1D models rely on the empirical quadratic [14, 15] or linear [16, 17] pump characteristic equations, which limit the number of VAD design parameters that are considered in device optimization. Additionally, these studies typically optimized only for steady design point conditions with constant pressure and flow, can experience altered hemocompatibility under time-periodic, physiological conditions. This highlights the importance of frameworks, which enable optimization under dynamic and hemodynamically realistic boundary conditions, addressing a crucial gap in current pump design practices. To overcome these limitations, a physiology-coupled system-level pump design tool was developed as described in this paper. The proposed design process allows key cardiovascular hemodynamics to be integrated into the optimization process through novel objective functions. Furthermore, it utilizes the full Euler turbomachinery equation for pump modeling, nozzle/volute design and blade path generation which automatically computes the pump characteristic curves for any choice of design parameters without the need for empirical data.

The widespread adoption of CFD in VAD design and optimization has raised concerns about the credibility of CFD results in high-risk clinical decision-making [18]. Therefore, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) researchers and collaborators developed a novel benchmark centrifugal blood pump [19], to standardize CFD studies considered for VADs by providing reliable validation datasets. This pump has been further investigated in literature in terms of CFD hemolysis, hydrodynamic performance [20–22] and performed particle image velocimetry (PIV) experiments [23]. Besides providing significant data for hydrodynamic and hemolytic performance of FDA pump, these studies revealed that it lacks a smooth hydrodynamic operation at design and creates a higher blood damage environment due to its simple design and several hydrodynamically unoptimized geometrical features. Thus, as another aim of this study, our team decided to re-visit FDA benchmark critically and optimize its volute, blades, impeller, inlet, nozzle, and shroud in a comprehensive manner using the optimization tool we introduce here, which leverages a patient-specific, target-based cardiovascular lumped parameter model (LPM) to guide pump design.

We hypothesized that the proposed framework would lead to significant improvements in pump performance by broadening the optimization concept through a system-level pump design tool, which can optimize both VAD geometric parameters and in the future can incorporate peripheral circulation specifications like vascular adaptation and remodeling. This tool was tested to present an alternative FDA pump with improved hydrodynamic and hemolytic performance. Our results showed that, the developed tool is promising to be a strong candidate for the preliminary optimized design step that typically takes place before the detailed CFD design effort, by offering physiology couple without multiscale modeling and reducing the trial-and-error optimization steps.

Lumped Parameter Left Ventricle Failure Circulation Model

Our previous neonatal congenital heart disease [24] and Fontan circulatory lumped parameter (LPM) solver [15] are modified to investigate an adult left ventricle (LV) failure patient with in silico-implanted FDA pump as an LVAD. LV failure is simulated in the model for the ‘acute state’ by modifying the LV chamber compliance in the LPM, which leads to a decrease in stroke volume and ejection fraction (EF). In this study, coronary circulation and left/right pulmonary circulation branches were added to the recent version of our model as well. Fig. 1 demonstrates the developed LPM network for mechanically assisted LV failure circulation.

FDA Pump Modeling

For the parametric pump modeling, an explicit form of Euler’s turbomachinery equation (Eq. 1) is utilized via pump inlet and outlet velocity triangles [25]. In comparison, current literature, including our previous pump studies [14, 15], utilize quadratic-tuned or empirical blood pump characteristic curves as pump equations, which limits the representation of pump-specific design parameters. Differently, in the present parametric Euler framework, each design dimension is separately represented, as in Eq. 1. Therefore, these parameters can be optimized for defined ranges based on predetermined hemodynamic or design targets, as the main prominent feature of the proposed model.

ΔPpump is the pump head equal to the pressure difference between inlet and outlet compliance chambers where the pump is in silico implanted (in this study, LV and aorta compliance chambers, respectively). ΔPloss stands for the pump losses including tubing, impeller and volute friction [26], diffuser friction and separation [27], blade loading [28], wake region mixing [29], leakage between the rotation and stationary parts [30], leakage from the blade pressure side to suction side [31], blade separation [32], blade incidence angle [33], and recirculation into the impeller [31]. Index 1 represents the inlet of the pump, while index 2 represents the outlet. Qpump is the pump flow rate and w is the rotational speed. Other impeller and blade design parameters which are not represented in the governing equation explicitly but have a role in the loss calculations are shown in Fig. 1 on the baseline FDA pump drawings.

FDA Pump System-Level Design Optimization

Circulation system-level (LPM) design optimization has been conducted with an in-house developed tool, which searches the global optimal values of design parameters based on specified objective functions. Except rd,i, which is determined indirectly using the other parameters, and f and g, which are held constant in this study, all design parameters represented in Fig. 1 are included in the optimization procedure. For this aim, each design parameter was changed within a range around the baseline FDA pump values, one at a time to observe their effects on changes in the normalized objective function. In this study, the objective function fNx for a target optimal pump is constructed as a composition of healthy cardiac output (CO) and aortic pressure with blade tip velocity, and as another optimization case, blood-wetted area of the device, as these are among the indicators of hemolysis, thrombogenicity, and hemocompatibility [34, 35]. Equation 2 quantifies deviations from target hemodynamic values under both healthy and LV failure (LVF) conditions without pump support, ensuring robust LVAD performance across physiological states. To balance variables with different scales, the function uses normalization based on nadir (worst acceptable) and utopia (ideal) values. This prevents any one metric from dominating the optimization. Such multi-objective normalization is standard in global optimization, supporting designs that meet diverse physiological requirements.

where Xi represents the objective parameters of the optimization and i=1⋯N where N is the number of objectives that constitute the composite objective function. Subscripts LVF, FDA, and healthy represent the value of associated objective in LV failure, FDA pump operation, and healthy circulation states, respectively. Xix¯ defines the relevant objective’s value for a set of pump design parameters x¯. we is the weighting factor corresponding to each individual objective and the sum of all weights equals to one. Finally, x¯ is the set of pump design parameters to be optimized.

A genetic algorithm is used for the optimization of fNx. Optimization algorithm initializes with a population of design parameter sets and evolves them through selection, crossover, and mutation, evaluating each using the fNx, while different pump designs are iterated until the change in fNx of the recent pump is smaller than the convergence criteria ε1 (Optimization Algorithm). Similar types of global optimization algorithms are widely used in the literature [6, 9], including in our own earlier works [36, 37]. For each pump design iterated during the optimization, an operation point is determined by revising the pressure of each compliant chamber under pump operation until the difference between the pump and system (LPM) pressures is smaller than a convergence criterion ε2 (Physiology Match Constraints). These pump–physiology match and pump optimization algorithms are shown in Fig. 1.

Volute is designed after the determination of main dimensions via optimization by assuming the pressure and the average velocity in volute to be nearly constant. Once the optimal design parameter set is found, volute is divided into several cross-sections. Average velocity at each cross-section is set to be the same as the diffuser inlet velocity calculated through Qpump and rd,o, based on the Stepanoff method assuming the cross-sections are circular [38]. Thus, this approach for volute optimization is uncoupled from the pump optimization, but other “coupled” volute design approaches can easily be implemented in this scheme.

Detailed Design Considerations CAD (LPM to 3D CAD Model)

Inlet dome design: Keeping the average velocity constant as much as possible from the pump inlet to the blade passage is crucial for hydrodynamic performance and reduced hemolysis. Thus, a proper inlet guide passage is designed with a dome in the middle of impeller, creating a conical area between impeller and shroud. This conical flow area is controlled through tuning parameters f and r1 to keep it constant plus to ensure constant average blood velocity. The comparison of flow fields through the improved inlet guide passage with the baseline FDA design inlet is shown in Fig. 2. For an unbiased comparison, the leakage gap between the blade upper surface and shroud is kept at 1 mm in all optimized pumps as in the baseline FDA design. Likewise the impeller plate height is kept same (4 mm).

Volute cross-section design: The volute inlet cross-section was designed as trapezoidal, and the other volute cross-sections are designed using Bezier curves. Thus, once the volute cross-sectional areas are computed via Stepanoff method, each section of volute is transformed into the Bezier curves defining smooth transition with the shroud and hub and preserving the computed hydraulic area. For this optimization, the trapezoidal cross-section at the volute inlet, a gap of 0.5 mm (parameter g in Fig. 1) is set as constant for all designed pumps for simplicity, which can also be varied in the optimization if needed. Importantly, these Bezier curves at each cross-section is constructed preserving the taper angle of blades to minimize wake regions at impeller to volute flows.

While designing the volute, these Bezier curves were transformed into the points (coordinates) from LPM to a computer aided design (CAD) system, for each cross-section at each angle of volute, and connected via Bezier curves at each cross-section. Designing the volute in CAD by generating the Beziers at each angle of volute is laborious. Thus, we compared the volute profile obtained via cross-sections at each 10 degrees of volute with the following cases in Geomagic software: (a) cross-sections at each 30 degrees of volute, (b) cross-sections at each 20 degrees of volute, and (c) a refined one to the inlet and outlet of volute from 20 degrees to 5 degrees. As demonstrated in Fig. 3, the refined profile yields a very similar shape with the design having a cross-section at each 10 degrees. Thus, such refined design method was used in this study for the volute generation.

Blade shape: Optimization tool provides only the optimal dimensions for design parameters listed in Fig. 1; thus, there is a further step required to determine whole blade shape and curvature. For this aim, the blade to be generated is divided into segments, and Eq. 1 is solved to obtain inlet and outlet angle of each blade segment. ΔPpump is initially assumed as distributed to the segments equally, but updated by simultaneously solving the Euler turbomachinery equation for each segment through an implicit solver until convergence is achieved across all segments, and the constant Qpump is used for each blade segment. Once the inlet and outlet angles are calculated for each segment, segment chords are drawn using the average of inlet and outlet angles. As the final step, blade curvature is generated by the connecting segment chords from pump inlet to outlet. The blade thickness is reduced equally from both the suction and pressure sides along the chord, reaching ~ 0.3 mm at the blade outlet tip, as seen in detail A at the top of Fig. 2. Blade inlet curvature is circular with its diameter equal to blade thickness.

Volute tongue and diffuser: Different than the baseline FDA, the volute tongue is also designed to be smoother using a fillet, as seen in the details C at the bottom of Fig. 2. The volute spiral was constructed in SolidWorks using Bezier curves based on coordinates from the design framework. A loft operation connected the spiral outlet to the diffuser inlet, forming the volute tongue. A fillet with a ranging radius between 0.5 and 1 mm ensures a smooth transition to the circular diffuser, which was completed by lofting between its inlet and outlet. It also shows that the Bezier curve at the volute outlet is softly connected with the diffuser inlet using a loft preserving the cross-sectional area constant and ensuring the absolute velocity outflow vector is perpendicular to these cross-sections. As another remark, diffuser collects all the flow at the volute outlet to minimize the losses different than the baseline design in which the diffuser is located at a portion of volute outlet, as seen in the second row of Fig. 2 (detail B).

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)

To verify the LPM predictions, we developed a CFD model in OpenFoam and validated it rigorously against experimental data from the literature [23], as well as our own PIV experiments [39]. The Multiple Reference Frame (MRF) method is utilized to handle the rotating frame. The simpleFoam solver in OpenFOAM [40] was employed, utilizing a steady-state solver with the SIMPLE pressure–velocity coupling algorithm. Second-order spatial discretization was applied for improved accuracy. Menter’s k-ω-SST [41] model was selected for RANS turbulence modeling, since this model provides reliable results in adverse pressure gradients that would be critical to assess separation cases. We have ensured that the first cell height provides low y+ distribution in all solid surfaces. In all numerical simulations, the y+ plus remained below unity on all surfaces assigned to no-slip wall boundary conditions, while the maximum y+ was less than 3. Therefore, we have maintained first cell calculations in the laminar sub layer.

A grid consisting of hexahedral boundary layer elements and tetrahedral core elements was generated for the numerical simulations. To ensure grid independence, three grid configurations were tested, with cell counts doubled (2.5, 5, and 10 M cells), and the pressure head created by the pump was monitored. With a difference less than 2% between the 5 and 10 M grids, the 5 M grid was selected for further modeling.

Alongside solving the system of Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations, two additional scalar equations were solved to estimate the flow residence time (RT) [42] and the hemolysis index (HI) [43]. Both transport equations were implemented in OpenFOAM using the scalarTransport feature of the solver. In both equations, the diffusion constant was set to a very small value to omit the diffusion terms. For the RT equation, since the governing equations in simpleFoam are in non-conservative form and also divided by density, a unity source term was added. The HI model is based on a power-law approach with porcine blood constants and von Mises scalar stress. The validation of these models and the results obtained will be discussed next.

Investigation of Detailed Flow Pathways via CFD: Baseline FDA Pump vs. the Optimized Designs

A numerical model has been created for both baseline FDA and the optimized case (Optimization Case 1). Figure 8 presents the comparison of velocity, RT, and HI CFD contours for both FDA pump and optimized case.

Baseline FDA pump vs. the optimization case 1 comparison of velocity magnitudes (top row), volumetric averaged residence time (middle row) and hemolysis index (bottom row)

This figure shows that the volume of the optimized pump has reduced by about 48% (40.9 cubic centimeters for optimized vs. 78.3 cubic centimeters for baseline FDA pump), due to overall reduced dimensions in the optimized design. Furthermore, lower rotational speed (1876 RPM vs. 2500 RPM) in the optimized design has led to reduced velocity in the pump. The reduced dimensions combined with the reduced rotational speed reduce the tip speed. This leads to reduced stress exerted on the blood elements flowing through the pump. For instance, the peak velocity in the mid-blade plane for the baseline pump is about 8.1 m/s, while with a reduction of 41%, this value is about 4.8 m/s in the optimized pump. On the other hand, despite reduced speeds in the optimized pump, the volumetric average of the residence time has decreased in this (0.288 vs. 0.522 s, 45% decrease). This suggests reduced local flow entrapments within the device and the optimization process has improved washout characteristics. The optimized pump potentially minimizes the risk of thrombus formation by enhancing flow-through efficiency. Finally, the HI has drastically decreased in the optimized design, as seen in Fig. 8. While the mass-weighted average of HI at the outlet is ~ 3.55 for the baseline FDA pump, this value reduces to about ~ 0.4 in the optimized design (leading a 89% reduction).

Discussion

Optimization of blood-wetted devices traditionally focused on component-level (3D) or detailed shape optimization studies primarily using CFD or structural solvers, e.g., inlet and outlet canula optimization of VADs [48] or coronary artery stent shapes to achieve atheroprotective wall shear stress patterns [49]. In this traditional approach, the hemodynamic requirements such as blood flow rate and pressure drops are fixed as boundary conditions and were treated to be independent of the device geometric parameters. However, there is no guarantee for the optimized device component to work exactly at these fixed flowrate and pressure values when implanted to the actual patient. Thus, as an important progress, the present approach eliminated this problem by including the physiological parameters into the optimization loop allowing realistic device-vascular coupling. Most importantly, this feature allows the introduction of special “physiological objective functions” where chronic vascular adaptations and remodeling that occur following device implantation can also be modeled in a fully coupled way to the device geometry and other device design parameters.

In this study, FDA centrifugal blood pump is aimed to be optimized to provide healthy level of CO (5.2 l/min) and mean aortic pressure (92 mmHg) with lower blade tip velocity (constituting the Objective Set 1) and blood-wetted area (constituting the Objective Set 2), as an indicator of lower blood damage. Seven different optimization cases via changing bounds of design parameters and objective functions were also conducted as introduced in Tables 2 and 3. Table 1 shows that the target healthy CO and mean aortic pressure were reached with optimization Case 1 pump with 32% lower rotational speed than the baseline FDA pump. Convergence of the normalized objective function in optimization Case 1 through generations is plotted in the Supplementary Information. Alternatively, Case 5, another optimal case determined for the objective function of blood-wetted area showed that the same healthy level hemodynamics with the baseline FDA pump can be reached with an optimized pump having even a smaller blood-wetted area of 40%. Overall, these are amazing performance improvements that would be very difficult to be discovered without the present optimization framework.

To understand and quantify the influence of design parameters’ on the reduced blade tip velocity objective, a detailed parametric sensitivity study was conducted. Effects of selected design parameters are plotted and summarized via a Pareto chart displayed in Supplementary Information. It demonstrated that the chosen objective function is most sensitive to r2 and w, as expected due to their direct effect on tip velocity. Secondary to these, key parameters, b1, b2 and zp were also identified to have significant effect on the objective function among the other pump dimensions. Sensitivity study also showed that diffuser and volute design parameters have relatively less effective compared to the impeller parameters on the objective function. Intrinsically, design parameter sensitivities differ for each objective, and it is important to determine optimization search bounds for each constructed objective function. While not considered in this initial work, our tool also enables us to formulate a wide range of hemodynamic and cardiovascular design objective functions, such as lower blood-wetted area to reduce thrombogenicity, target ventricular EF values or pressure–volume curve shapes, end-organ hemodynamic targets like pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio, cerebral and coronary flow, hepatic/renal conditions, and oxygen saturation levels. Furthermore, our model can optimize blood pumps for operation under any desired exercise condition of the patient [50].

As further discussions on Objective Set 1 cases (Table 3), modifications in range of w revealed that impeller outlet radius and rotational speed are inversely changed through the optimization as expected (Case 2). In Case 3, reduced blade inlet and outlet angles increase the mean blade length and consequently blade loading and recirculation losses. These losses were compensated by selecting a smaller radii/larger length diffuser and increasing the rotational speed. On the other hand, Case 4 can operate lower speeds than the other cases due to its smaller impeller inlet radius, which also causes a lower radial velocity at the pump inlet. In this case, impeller outlet blade width was decreased to create a corresponding reduction in the outlet radial velocity to maintain the target aortic pressure and CO.

Considering Objective Set 2, main contributors to the pump blood-wetted area are determined to be the impeller top and bottom surfaces, followed by the shroud inner surface, and finally the volute inner surface. In Cases 5 and 6, impeller surface area was slightly decreased, yet, it does not significantly contribute to the reduction of blood-wetted area due to the same search range bounds of these design parameters with Case 1. Therefore, the algorithm used an alternative way to minimize the wetted area, by reducing the inner surface of Bezier cross-sections in volute via reducing the diffuser inlet and outlet radii (Table 3). Due to the constant average velocity assumption used in this study, volute shape can be indirectly manipulated through the diffuser design. The volute in the baseline pump has a constant radius and connects to the diffuser through a needle-shaped passage with a much smaller cross-sectional area compared to the volute. As seen in Fig. 8, this leads to a sudden acceleration of the flow in the baseline pump. This abrupt acceleration increases velocity gradients, which in turn elevate hemolysis. However, in the optimized pump, the volute has a spiral form designed to accommodate the accumulation of flow. Based on this design, no sudden changes in flow speed are visible within the volute, as shown in Fig. 8. Additionally, this figure illustrates that the smooth integration of volute and diffuser ensures a smooth flow transition between these two regions. Finally, in Case 7, the search bounds for all parameters were adjusted to a lower range to observe the broader impact of these ranges on the objective function. As foreseen, it generated the smallest case design with an extreme reduction in the blood-wetted area of 61% than the FDA pump, but with the highest rotational speed to achieve the target hemodynamics. Reduced blood-wetted area or device volume is a widely desired clinical need in neonatal and pediatric mechanical support applications, not only for achieving compact devices but to eliminate neonatal blood reactivity issues.

The LPM-Euler turbomachinery formulation employed in this study is based on a major assumption where relative flow pathways are considered parallel to the device blade surfaces. Thus, this formulation will predict the performance at the design operating conditions with the best accuracy, with an error of 2.5%. However, we observed that due to our detailed loss prediction modules, we were also able to predict the off-design operating points acceptably well with a maximum error of 15%, despite the lack of an operable design point in FDA pump due to the straight blades. As an example, baseline FDA pump curves simulated via our model for rotational speeds of 2500 and 3500 rpm were compared with the literature, as seen in Fig. 5. It shows that both LPM and CFD results have a good agreement with the literature ranges given by Hariharan et al. [23]. The differences observed between our LPM and CFD results for the introduced conditions can be summarized as follows: 6% in Condition 1 (2.5 l/min, 2500 rpm), 1% in Condition 2 (2.5 l/min, 3500 rpm), 9% in Condition 3 (4.5 l/min, 3500 rpm), 14% in Condition 4 (5.2 l/min, 2500 rpm), 2% in Condition 5 (6 l/min, 2500 rpm), and 15% in Condition 6 (6 l/min, 3500 rpm). The mean absolute error across all conditions was ± 8% at off-design operating points. As expected, the deviation between LPM and CFD slightly increases at the off-design regions from the design operating point, due to empirical errors in loss formulations. Nevertheless, both LPM and CFD results are observed in the range of our previous PIV research on FDA pump [39] and literature [23]. Additionally, Fig. 3 demonstrates that the velocity magnitude along radial line of the blade passage and blade orientation-based velocity fields obtained through CFD for the baseline FDA pump at Condition 2 matched well with the in vitro studies. This figure also demonstrates that the simulated HI for all introduced FDA pump conditions are consistent with literature-based CFD studies and fall within the standard deviation range observed in porcine blood experiment measurements.

Figure 4 also presents the further validation of our CFD model with literature numerical [44] and experimental data [23]. The figure demonstrates that the relative HI varies significantly with operating conditions, even when the design remains the same. Additionally, the absolute HI values were observed to be 1.39, 4.06, 3.74, 4.54, 5.34, and 8.28 for Conditions 1 through 6, respectively, further revealing the substantial impact of operating conditions on HI. A similar study was conducted for the Case 1 design using Condition 5 from Hariharan et al. [23] as the reference. It was observed that the absolute HI is 83% lower at the design condition compared to its operation under Condition 5.

CFD results presented in Fig. 8 show the evident of various performance improvements at the component-level through optimization and detailed hydrodynamic design. The velocity contour plots revealed that the baseline pump exhibits regions of flow separation between blades and in the downstream of the diffuser. Additionally, a low velocity region is observed in the hub region of the impeller. In contrast, the optimized design displays a more uniform velocity distribution with minimized low-speed zones. The smoother transition of velocity magnitudes across the impeller and volute suggests improved flow streamlining in the pump, which contributes to improved hemodynamic performance.

Similarly, when the RT contours are investigated closely (Fig. 8), the baseline pump shows a region of locally elevated RT caused by flow separation in the diffuser downstream, which is completely diminished in the optimized design. Both pumps operate in the same flow rate. However, due to reduced volume, the optimized pump has a reduced flush time (volume divided by volumetric flow rate): 0.488 seconds for the optimized pump and 0.899 seconds for baseline (46% decrease).

Improvements in velocity and RT show their additive effect in the reduction of HI. The baseline pump exhibits elevated HI values near the leading edges of the impeller blades and at high-velocity gradient regions. This suggests that shear-induced hemolysis is a concern in the baseline design. In contrast, the optimized pump significantly reduces the peak HI values, particularly near the impeller blades. The smoother transition in velocity and lower shear regions indicate that the optimized pump better mitigates RBC damage, leading to improved biocompatibility. Considering all these three variables investigated by CFD in detail, it is confirmed that the optimized design has considerably improved the performance via fulfilled optimization objectives.

The performance of the optimized Case 1 design was also compared with that of a clinically relevant pump, the CentriMag (Abbott, IL, USA), at operating conditions of 5.2 L/min and 82 mmHg. CentriMag is one of the clinically approved pumps with the lowest blood damage parameters. CFD simulations for both pumps showed that our Case 1 design could deliver the same flowrate and pressure as the CentriMag, while operating at 11% lower rotational speeds. Additionally, similar blade tip speeds (~4.6 m/s) and hemolysis indices (~0.4) were observed under these common conditions. Notably, the optimized pump exhibited a residence time (RT) that was 3% shorter than that of the CentriMag. These findings indicate that even a benchmark pump such as the FDA design can be enhanced to reach the performance level a clinically approved device.

Although the optimization cases were conducted for full support of LV in this study, the proposed tool can investigate the partial support cases as well. However, it was observed that the LV in the selected failure state is unable to maintain adequate levels of CO and arterial pressure through partial support alone. A moderate acute LV failure state was used in this study, yet our model can analyze any severity of LV failure. Moreover, the proposed network has the flexibility to investigate any other congenital diseases or patient profiles via modifying the network.

As an additional consideration, applying the algorithm to mixed-flow or axial-flow pumps like the HeartMate III requires modifications to account for their 3D flow paths and velocity triangle geometry changes. Adaptation of the equations for such cases has already been initiated for our right ventricle assist device design research [51], and further development is ongoing.

Despite all these novelties it brings, the introduced model still has limitations. Firstly, since this is a 2-element LPM, the impedance factor in vessels is neglected. Secondly, while we demonstrated blood damage improvements using rigorously validated CFD results, further in vitro and blood tests are desired and ongoing through multiple optimized configurations. These will confirm a more robust hydrodynamic and hemolysis analysis framework. Finally, loss calculations, particularly friction factor estimation, might be improved via evaluation of the continuous test data to obtain a more accurate, comparable model with the CFD and in vitro studies even in off-design conditions.

In overall, considering all its flexibilities and advantages, this research has potential to contribute the mechanical circulatory support design process by serving primarily the preliminary design step, which may minimize the trial-and-error cycle at the detailed CFD design and experimental investigation phases. Conventional CFD-based, iteration-driven pump design and optimization can take several months. In contrast, the proposed framework delivers a comparable level of design insight within just a few days. Additionally, recent studies have shown a link between hydraulic dissipation and hemolysis [52, 53]. This would reduce the CFD analyses time significantly since HI calculations require an additional passive scalar equation to be solved numerically. Therefore, particularly for achieving the objective of truly customized patient-specific blood-wetted devices, this approach will comprehensively save time and prototyping resources significantly during the design process. As the first demonstration of this integrated design approach, the benchmark FDA blood pump has been significantly optimized with improved hydrodynamic profiles and hemocompatible detailed design features. This benchmark alternative is made openly available (10.5281/zenodo.15076514) and can be considered especially in precise device development projects. This approach also allows us to consider new circulatory assist devices that are optimized for patient’s specific physiology, which will be the focus of our future work.
