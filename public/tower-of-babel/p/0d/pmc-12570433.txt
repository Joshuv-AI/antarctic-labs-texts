The prevalence and experience of upward bullying of nurse managers in China: a multicenter mixed methods survey
Authors: Shuang Wang, Jia He, Qin Cao, Yang Liu, Zining Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have shown that upward bullying affects both organizational efficiency and the harmony of the work environment. However, the prevalence of upward bullying and its contributory factors remain unclear in Chinese nurse managers. This study aimed to explore the prevalence of upward bullying and its contributory factors as experienced by nurse managers in Chinese healthcare settings, together with an investigation of their specific experiences.MethodsThis study used a comprehensive, sequential, explanatory mixed methods design. The research was carried out in 12 provinces of China and included nurse managers working from various healthcare settings. The data collection involved an online survey completed by 210 nurse managers during the quantitative phase, followed by 17 semi-structured interviews during the qualitative phase. Our comprehensive approach, using both quantitative and qualitative methods, allowed us to gain a deep understanding of the prevalence and experiences of upward bullying among nurse managers, providing a robust foundation for our findings.ResultsOverall, 24.76% of nurse managers reported experiences of upward bullying and 40.38% of cases were classified as severe. Their upward bullying experiences were classified into nine categories and refined into four themes (perception of upward bullying by nurse managers, facilitators of upward bullying, calls for organizational support, and recovery and growth).ConclusionsA considerable proportion of nurse managers in China experience upward bullying, which affects their physical and mental health and management effectiveness, impacting healthcare quality. Developing a supportive environment emphasizing respect, open communication, and professional development is crucial to mitigate upward bullying. Such a setting can help nurse managers cope with upward bullying and improve work culture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03977-y.Keywords: Nurse managers, Nursing, Upward bullying, China, Mixed methods

Background

Workplace bullying is widely recognized as a harmful phenomenon in healthcare [1]. Among its forms, upward bullying, where subordinates bully their managers has recently gained attention [2–6]. This involves behaviors such as open disrespect, questioning leadership, and deliberate non-cooperation, challenging traditional power dynamics and undermining organizational efficiency [7–10]. Studies from the United States have suggested a rise in upward bullying experienced by nurse managers, with the prevalence ranging from 19.42% to 28% [11, 12]. Upward bullying negatively impacts the effective management and decision-making abilities of nurse managers [8], diminishes their capacity to maintain a positive work environment, disrupts nursing processes, and adversely affects patient experiences [13, 14]. Furthermore, upward bullying leads to considerable emotional distress, decreased job satisfaction and may even provoke thoughts of resignation among nurse managers, increasing management turnover and inducing instability in the healthcare setting [15]. Given this significant impact, it is essential to investigate upward bullying experienced by nurse managers to improve management practices and foster a supportive work environment.

However, there is insufficient existing research on the topic, limiting our comprehensive understanding of this phenomenon. First, most studies to date have concentrated on lateral bullying among nurses or downward bullying by senior nurses toward junior staff [16]. Nurse managers are key intermediaries between management and the nursing staff, and thus face challenges distinct from those of other nursing staff, leading to different responses to bullying. Therefore, further research focusing on nurse managers is needed for an in-depth exploration of their psychological and behavioral responses to upward bullying. Second, the reliance on a single research method has also limited the understanding of upward bullying [8, 11, 12, 15]. While quantitative surveys can reveal the prevalence of upward bullying, they struggle to explore its complex socio-psychological mechanisms. Qualitative research, although capable of providing in-depth insights into individual experiences and feelings, is highly subjective. Mixed methods combines the strengths of both, thereby providing a more comprehensive and profound understanding [17]. Third, cultural differences also play a role. Studies on upward bullying have focused on Western countries [5, 11, 12, 16], and minimal research has been undertaken in Eastern cultures, particularly within the Chinese healthcare setting [8]. In the post-pandemic era, the rising demand for healthcare services in China has led to considerably higher workloads and psychological pressure for nurses, creating fertile ground for the occurrence of upward bullying [18]. The hierarchical management structure and the culture within nursing teams may further exacerbate this issue [8]. Eastern cultures, which strongly emphasize collectivism, respect for authority, and interpersonal harmony, may shape distinct coping strategies for upward bullying [19]. Thus, an exploration of upward bullying within an Eastern context is crucial for gaining deeper insights across different cultures, as well as for developing intervention strategies that are culturally sensitive and locally relevant.

In light of these research gaps, this study aims to explore the prevalence and its contributory factors of upward bullying among nurse managers in Chinese healthcare settings, as well as their real experiences. The findings of this study are instrumental in proposing practical and effective solutions for improving management practices, thereby enhancing the quality of nursing management and fostering a harmonious work environment, particularly in the context of the growing demand for healthcare services in China. It should be noted that the current study forms part of a broader research program on upward bullying among Chinese nurse managers and represents the second phase following an exploratory qualitative study [8].

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a sequential explanatory mixed methods design, consisting of a quantitative phase followed by a qualitative phase (Fig. 1) [20]. The quantitative survey assessed the prevalence of upward bullying and was used to identify participants for the qualitative interviews. The qualitative phase further explored the nature, meaning, and perceived impact of the experience [17]. This article adhered to the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study checklist [21]. The research team included a nurse researcher, three nurse managers, and a PhD student in medicine and philosophy, ensuring scientific rigor and practical relevance. The datasets used in this study are entirely independent from those of our earlier qualitative study [8], with no overlap in participants, settings, or recruitment period.

Flowchart of explanatory sequential study design

Study setting and sampling

This study took place at 12 provinces and municipalities in China with diverse healthcare systems and cultural backgrounds to reduce sampling bias and enhance the representativeness and applicability of the findings.

Participants for the quantitative study were recruited through a combination of convenience sampling and informal peer referral [22], aiming to capture the wide-ranging experiences within the broader nursing community. The hospital’s liaison office issued recruitment notices to consortium units. Electronic questionnaires were distributed via WeChat, and participants also referred peers through social networks to diversify the sample.

Nurse managers who met both the inclusion and exclusion criteria were eligible to participate in the quantitative phase. Inclusion criteria: (i) Certified nurse managers; (ii) ≥ 6 months in continuous management role; (iii) Informed, voluntary consent. Exclusion criteria: individuals clearly uncomfortable with the topic or concerned about privacy.

Using the formula n = (z²·p·(1–p))/E², a required sample size of 185 was calculated based on a 14% expected prevalence of upward bullying, as reported by the Workplace Bullying Institute (2021) from the United States [23], and a significance level of 0.05.

Purposive and voluntary sampling were used for the qualitative methods [24]. Nurse managers identified in the quantitative phase as having experienced upward bullying were selected, ensuring at least one participant per province. Interviews continued until data saturation and regional coverage were both achieved; otherwise, additional participants were recruited [25].

Quantitative data collection and analysis

An online cross-sectional survey was conducted from August to September 2024. To minimize bias and encourage truthful responses, strategies were adopted to improve data validity and reliability. First, anonymous questionnaires were used to protect the participants’ identities. In addition, language was framed carefully to create a non-threatening environment, helping participants respond without pressure.

Demographic data were collected, including gender, age, marital status, education, professional title, department, and years of service, and other relevant characteristics. These variables were chosen based on prior research that has established their links to bullying [26]. Several instruments have been developed to assess workplace bullying, differing in length, structure, and applicability. Examples included the Negative Acts Questionnaire-Revised [27], Short Negative Acts Questionnaire (SNAQ) [28], Leymann Inventory of Psychological Terror [29], and Workplace Bullying Scale [30]. The SNAQ was selected in our study for its brevity, reliability, and sensitivity to subtle forms of upward bullying like passive resistance. Developed by Notelaers et al. [28], the SNAQ is a nine-item instrument that assesses social exclusion, malicious behavior, and work interference without explicitly using the term “bullying”. It includes three components: person-related (2 items), work-related (6 items), and physical intimidation (1 item). The original version showed good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.78) and validity and differentiation [28], whereas in this study, it demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.944. Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 5 = Daily), reflecting experiences over the past six months. A SNAQ score below 14 indicates no bullying and A SNAQ score of 18 or higher indicates severe bullying [31].

Quantitative data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize continuous variables, whereas percentages were used to describe categorical variables. Normality of the SNAQ scores was assessed using Q-Q plots (Fig. 2). If normally distributed, scores were expressed as mean ± standard deviation; otherwise, as median and interquartile range. Moreover, independent t-tests were conducted to compare continuous variables between the two groups. For comparisons involving multiple sample means, one-way analysis of variance was applied when the data met the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance; otherwise, the rank sum test was used. The test level α = 0.05 was used.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in October 2024 by three researchers (SW, JH, QC) with no direct hierarchical ties to participants. To minimize bias, researchers maintained neutrality and applied reflexivity throughout data collection and analysis. To ensure consistency, all researchers received pre-interview training on procedural protocols, question intent, and probing techniques. A pilot interview phase was conducted to calibrate interview styles and refine question phrasing for clarity and relevance. During the formal interview period, the team held regular meetings and adopted an iterative strategy to continuously adjust the direction and emphasis of subsequent interviews based on insights from earlier sessions.

Participants for the qualitative interviews were identified from the quantitative phase. Although participants were not asked to provide their names, the questionnaire included an optional item at the end inviting them to voluntarily leave their contact information (e.g., phone number, WeChat account, or email) for potential follow-up interviews. Depending on participants’ locations, interviews were conducted either face-to-face or by telephone and lasted 30 to 40 min, allowing for an in-depth exploration of their experiences. To ensure anonymity, each participant was assigned a unique code. Both verbal responses and non-verbal cues were documented. Data saturation was achieved after the 17th interview.

Qualitative data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis [32]. Transcripts were completed within 24 h of each interview and verified by participants. Two researchers (SW and YL) independently coded and cross-checked and reconciled discrepancies through discussion. Themes were developed collaboratively and illustrated with participant quotes.

The interview outlines were developed based on the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) model, which explores how knowledge and attitudes shape behavior [33]. This theory was chosen as it provides a comprehensive understanding of the complex dynamics involved in workplace bullying. A pretest with two nurse managers was used to refine the script prior to formal interviews. Table 1 presents the outlines of the formal interview.

Mixed methods and analysis

Integration of quantitative and qualitative data occurred at both the design and analysis stages. During the design phase, quantitative data were first used to identify potential interviewees who had experienced upward bullying for subsequent qualitative exploration. In addition, key variables such as experiences of upward bullying, prior training, and perceived organizational support were aligned with the KAP framework to guide the development of the interview outline.

In the analysis phase, we employed thematic triangulation, a widely used strategy in mixed methods research to cross-validate and enrich insights across data sources. Specifically, qualitative and quantitative findings were systematically compared to identify patterns of convergence, divergence, and complementarity.

Ethical considerations

The Ethical Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology approved this research (No. S044, Date:27 April 2022). Informed consent was obtained electronically for the survey and orally for interviews, with assurances of voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time. To enhance ethical oversight and critical reflection during the qualitative phase, a PhD student (ZNZ) with a background in medical philosophy was invited to participate in the design, implementation, and interpretation of the interviews.

Nurse managers experience being bullied by subordinate nurses

Transcripts of nurse managers’ experience of upward bullying were classified into nine categories and refined into four themes. These themes included nurse managers’ perception of upward bullying, facilitators of the occurrence of upward bullying, calls for organizational support, and recovery and growth.

Theme 1: nurse managers’ perception of upward bullying

Category 1: low sensitivity to upward bullying

Most nurse managers perceived upward bullying as covert.

At first, I thought they were just delaying tasks or ignoring messages unintentionally, but repeated incidents revealed their dissatisfaction with me…… (N1, 35, F)

Category 2: the subjectivity of perception of upward bullying

Many noted that perceptions of upward bullying were subjective. Even when typical behaviors occurred, they didn’t label them as “bullying”.

When subordinates challenge my decisions, I see it as work disagreement, not bullying…… (N9, 46, F)

Theme 2: facilitators of the occurrence of upward bullying

Category 1: nurses’ personal factors

Some nurse managers believed nurses’ experiences and perceptions influenced their bullying behavior.

A nurse who bullied me had been mistreated by a former manager. I think she projected that onto me…… (N7, 37, F)

Some didn’t see behaviors like speaking ill as bullying…… (N1, 35, F)

Many agreed bullying often arose when personal interests were threatened.

When I reassigned workloads, some nurses who previously had lighter duties began resisting me…… (N2, 52, F)

Many noted that misunderstanding of management work contributed to upward bullying.

They believed I only made rules to restrict them. Some even excluded me from group chats…… (N3, 38, F)

Others believed personality also contributed to bullying.

Some nurses were emotionally unstable, and when challenged, they became frustrated and showed disrespect, not only to me…… (N12, 37, F)

Category 2: organizational culture factors

Most nurse managers stated that negative factors, such as excessive workload, insufficient organizational support, and a lack of emphasis on workplace bullying in the work environment, were triggers for the occurrence of upward bullying.

In the emergency room, nurses are under heavy stress. They can’t vent to patients, so they turn their frustration on me…… (N4, 34, F)

There’s no real system to protect victims or hold bullies accountable…… (N8, 34, F)

Theme 3: call for organizational support

Category 1: foster good psychosocial safety climate

Almost nurse managers emphasized the need for a respectful, communicative, and psychologically supportive environment.

Environmental factors shape behavior. I try to build a respectful team culture by showing kindness…… (N11, 41, M)

Psychological counseling groups can help both nurses and nurse managers process negative emotions…… (N6, 35, F)

Category 2: clarify responsibilities and establish reward and punishment system

Some nurse managers believed that clarifying roles and responsibilities was seen as key to reducing workplace unfairness and bullying.

I update detailed work responsibility guidelines to reduce misunderstandings and avoid role ambiguity…… (N5, 47, F)

Many noted that fair punishment reinforced behavioral norms, while rewards motivated staff.

While punishment isn’t ideal, it reminds staff to think twice before acting inappropriately…… (N16, 32, F)

Category 3: provide professional training

All nurse managers emphasized the need for training on bullying awareness and response.

I seek training that teaches real-world conflict resolution while preserving relationships…… (N14, 42, F)

Nurses also need education to recognize inappropriate behaviors and understand their impact…… (N15, 37, M)

Theme 4: recovery and growth

Category 1: psychological adjustment and self-reflection

Upward bullying caused emotional stress among most nurse managers, affecting their performance despite efforts to stay professional. Over time, they recovered.

At first, I felt constantly compared to my predecessor and doubted myself. Later, I regained confidence with help from experienced managers…… (N16, 32, F)

I used to react defensively to complaints, but I learned to reflect and view issues from their perspective, which helped me manage emotions better…… (N8, 34, F)

Category 2: career growth and enabling

Some nurse managers gained self-efficacy and confidence by repeatedly managing conflicts and power struggles.

As a new nurse manager from another department, I was initially viewed as idealistic and questioned about my competence. Through self-study and hands-on work, I earned recognition and grew professionally…… (N10, 36, M)

Some nurse managers believe these experiences can enhance their ability to predict upward bullying.

I developed prediction thoughts regarding these occurrences. For instance, if I notice a nurse struggling emotionally, I arrange time off to help them decompress before issues escalate…… (N7, 37, F)

Merged results

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings revealed multiple factors contributing to upward bullying. Quantitative data showed that nurse managers who received relevant training or organizational support experienced less upward bullying. Qualitative data not only confirmed these findings but also identified additional influences, such as limited awareness of bullying and adverse work conditions, including poor team collaboration and unclear role boundaries. Overall, upward bullying appears to result from the interplay of individual, organizational, and cultural factors.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of upward bullying experienced by nurse managers in China and their lived experiences with it. The findings support that upward bullying is a prevalent but under-recognized phenomenon within Chinese healthcare settings. Organizational support and relevant training emerge as protective factors, while nurse managers’ subjective perception and cultural norms significantly influence how bullying is interpreted and managed. These insights enhance our understanding of the psychosocial and cultural mechanisms underlying upward bullying in hierarchical healthcare contexts.

In addition to these findings, this study also advances beyond our earlier qualitative work [8]. First, it is the first to report prevalence and severity of upward bullying among Chinese nurse managers, providing generalizable evidence of its seriousness. Second, using a sequential explanatory mixed methods, it both confirms and extends prior insights, generating richer qualitative data with a more refined interview guide. Third, by integrating quantitative breadth and qualitative depth, the study moves from descriptive exploration to confirmatory explanation, yielding more robust and broadly applicable understanding.

Notably, this study highlights a striking disconnect between the objective occurrence of upward bullying and nurse managers’ subjective recognition of it. Although many nurse managers reported behaviors consistent with upward bullying, few consciously labeled these experiences as such or sought formal recourse. This aligns with earlier findings that nurses often perceive bullying as an expected part of their job, which reduces their willingness to report it and leads them to internalize such experiences as a normal occupational hazard [34]. However, our study further highlights how these perceptual barriers are reinforced by cultural expectations, particularly in Confucian influenced contexts where endurance and harmony are regarded as excellent traditions [19]. This cultural framing not only suppresses reporting but also normalizes hostile behavior, ultimately weakening institutional responsiveness. Prior research has highlighted the role of “cultures of silence” and “bullying normalization” across diverse healthcare systems [35], suggesting that the problem is not limited to individual awareness, but embedded in broader organizational and sociocultural environments. In the Chinese context, acknowledging bullying may be further complicated by concerns over professional reputation, leadership image, and institutional harmony, making systemic reform essential for change.

Another key finding of this study is the protective effect of organizational support and relevant training in mitigating upward bullying. This result is consistent with existing literature demonstrating that organizational support buffers against workplace mistreatment and enhances employee resilience [36]. This phenomenon can be interpreted through an integrated lens of psychosocial safety climate [37] and perceived organizational support [36]. Psychosocial safety climate reflects the organizational climate regarding psychological safety and fair conflict management. When the workplace encourages respectful communication and signals intolerance toward mistreatment, such as through clearly defined reporting procedures or visible support from senior leadership, it reduces the interpersonal cost of speaking up [37]. Within such a climate, nurse managers are more likely to perceive organizational support, which enhances their confidence to interpret difficult behaviors as problematic rather than as pressures they must silently endure [36, 38]. Training further strengthens this process by equipping managers with the skills to identify subtle bullying behaviors and the knowledge that the organization stands behind them. Together, psychosocial safety climate and perceived organizational support create a context in which upward bullying is not only less likely to persist but also more likely to be addressed early and constructively.

Interestingly, the interviews revealed that some nurse managers perceived the experience of upward bullying as not entirely harmful, but accompanied by a degree of post-traumatic growth. Such growth is inseparable from psychological resources, which are crucial for maintaining or regaining functioning under stress and for transforming negative encounters into opportunities for development [39]. However, our findings also caution against overemphasizing psychological resources as a default coping mechanism in the absence of structural solutions. Psychological resources, therefore, should be understood not as an expectation placed upon individuals, but as a possible outcome supported by a safe, responsive, and well-structured work environment [40].

In conclusion, this study not only confirms earlier findings [8, 11, 12, 15], but also makes a distinct contribution by highlighting the cultural specificity of upward bullying. It demonstrates how individual perceptions, organizational support, and culturally rooted norms influence the recognition and management of bullying, thereby clarifying why such behaviors often remain hidden and difficult to address. By integrating cultural and psychological perspectives, the study advances current understanding and offers a practical foundation for developing targeted, culturally appropriate interventions and strengthening nurse manager well-being in hierarchical healthcare systems.

Limitations and future directions

This study has certain limitations. First, although efforts were made to recruit participants from diverse regions, the use of convenience sampling and peer referral may have introduced selection bias. In addition, the underrepresentation of male nurse managers limited the analysis of gender differences, and individuals who declined participation may have held stronger negative experiences that were not captured. Third, within China’s collectivist culture, middle-level managers may downplay negative experiences or avoid open conflict to maintain harmony, which could further constrain the findings. Finally, the use of SNAQ cut-off values derived from a Swedish context may not fully reflect cultural perceptions of bullying severity, and future research should validate these thresholds for the Chinese setting.

Beyond methodological improvements, future research could conduct intervention studies to assess effectiveness and carry out cross-national comparisons to identify differences across cultural contexts and healthcare systems. It may also incorporate psychological theories to more systematically examine the antecedents and consequences of upward bullying.

Implications for practice

The findings are of great significance for improving the workplace climate and the well-being of nurse managers.

Reflect on leadership styles

Authoritarian leadership can provoke resistance, silence, and even retaliation [41]. Therefore, nurse managers should actively reflect on and adjust their leadership approaches in high-pressure or tense interpersonal environments. Reducing directive communication while increasing negotiation and supportive interaction, and adopting non-coercive methods, can effectively ease confrontational dynamics, enhance team stability, and reduce the occurrence of upward conflict [42, 43].

Promote respect for leadership and professional boundaries

Training should guide nurses to respect managerial authority while maintaining clear professional boundaries. It can help them practice professional communication even when disagreements arise, avoid turning opinion expression into passive resistance, improve emotional management, and strengthen their ability to recognize and respond to subtle workplace violence, such as emotionally driven opposition to institutional arrangements [44].

Strengthen organizational support and psychological resources

It is recommended to foster a culture that encourages open expression, accepts feedback, and acknowledges managerial difficulties to enhance the psychosocial safety climate of nurse managers [37]. This can be achieved by establishing institutional support mechanisms, such as dedicated platforms for nurse manager support and non-punitive reporting and consultation channels. These initiatives provide safe pathways for managers to express stress, help them move away from silence and passive compliance, and enable clearer judgment and more active expression when facing challenges. In addition, interventions should focus on strengthening individual psychological resources to enhance resilience and coping capacity.

Conclusions

This study revealed the high prevalence and complex impact of upward bullying among nurse managers in China, highlighting its urgency in healthcare management. Organizational support and training were associated with lower levels of perceived bullying. Qualitative findings identified four themes: limited sensitivity to recognizing bullying, contributing individual and contextual factors, lack of organizational support, and the process of recovery and growth. These findings underscore the need to foster a supportive environment and a psychological safety climate in the workplace. Based on these results, healthcare institutions are advised to implement structured anti-bullying systems, including leadership development, conflict resolution training, and secure reporting channels, to better equip nurse managers to handle workplace conflict. Future research should explore the moderating role of culture and assess the effectiveness and adaptability of interventions across diverse healthcare settings.
