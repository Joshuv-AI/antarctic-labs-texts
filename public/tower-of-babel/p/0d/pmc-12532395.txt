Latent class analysis of oral symptoms in stroke patients and exploration of associated
Authors: Huanhuan Zhu, Fengyin Zhang, Simin Li, Qinghua Wen, Xiaoyue Wang, Lihong Xian, Juan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aims to identify the potential categories of oral symptoms in stroke patients and to explore their associated influencing factors.MethodsA convenience sampling method was employed to collect demographic and oral symptom data from hospitalized stroke patients admitted to the neurology and neurosurgery departments of a tertiary hospital in Guiyang, Guizhou Province, between January 2025 and March 2025. Latent class analysis was applied to classify oral symptoms, and univariate analysis along with multivariate logistic regression was conducted to identify factors associated with each class.ResultsThree distinct classes of oral symptoms were identified: Structural and Masticatory Dysfunction (21.8%), Oral Mucosal Lesions (34.6%), and Poor Oral Hygiene (43.6%). The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that the influencing factors varied across different oral symptom classes (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe findings indicate substantial heterogeneity in oral symptoms among stroke patients. Tailored oral care strategies should be developed based on the characteristics and determinants of each class to improve oral health outcomes and overall quality of life in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06977-1.

Introduction

Stroke is an acute cerebrovascular event characterized by impaired brain function due to disrupted cerebral circulation, and is marked by high incidence, disability, recurrence, and mortality rates [1]. In recent years, the incidence of stroke has continued to rise, making it one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide [1]. Post-stroke patients frequently experience varying degrees of neurological impairment, affecting motor, speech, swallowing, and cognitive functions [2]. Oral health problems are common post-stroke complications, affecting nearly 80% of patients [3].

Due to facial muscle paralysis, impaired jaw–tongue coordination, dysphagia, and consciousness disorders, stroke patients are highly susceptible to poor oral hygiene, manifested by gingival bleeding, xerostomia, tooth mobility, difficulties in denture use, and masticatory dysfunction [3, 4], which can severely compromise nutritional intake and even lead to aspiration pneumonia and other complications [5]. Oral problems in stroke patients not only represent a major contributor to reduced quality of life but also constitute a potential risk factor during the rehabilitation process [6]. However, current research predominantly focuses on individual oral symptoms or intervention outcomes, with a lack of systematic analyses addressing the overall profile of post-stroke oral health. Existing approaches are largely limited to univariate analyses or simple group comparisons, which fail to capture the combinatorial patterns among symptoms and inter-patient variability, thereby overlooking the inherent complexity and heterogeneity of oral health in this population.

Latent Class Analysis (LCA) is an unsupervised clustering technique used to identify latent subgroups within a sample, particularly suited for modeling complex phenomena involving multiple categorical variables [7]. Compared with conventional grouping techniques, LCA simultaneously accommodates multiple categorical variables and estimates class membership probabilities, thereby revealing patterns of symptom co-occurrence and patient classification. This capability is particularly suited to the complex scenario of coexisting oral symptoms examined in the present study [8]. Accordingly, this study employed an LCA framework, hypothesizing that the oral symptoms of stroke patients could be classified into distinct latent categories with significant interclass differences, and that the distribution of these categories would be significantly influenced by key demographic characteristics, disease-related factors, and lifestyle behaviors. The ultimate aim is to inform precision oral care strategies and risk stratification in stroke patients.

Method

Study participants

This cross-sectional study used a convenience sampling method to recruit stroke patients from the neurosurgery and neurology departments of a tertiary hospital in Guiyang, Guizhou Province, between January 2025 and March 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of stroke consistent with the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke (2019) [9]; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) ability to provide informed consent and communicate effectively. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with critical illness or severe comorbidities preventing participation; (2) incomplete or missing clinical data. According to Kendall’s sample size estimation method [10], the recommended sample size ranges from 10 to 20 times the number of independent variables. This study included 10 variables in the LCA model, yielding an estimated sample size of 100 ~ 200 participants. Based on previous studies, a minimum of 200 participants is required to fit an optimal latent class model. Considering a 10% nonresponse or invalid rate, the minimum required sample size was set at 220. A total of 234 participants were ultimately included in the study. This study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (Approval No. 2025-011).

Research tools

The research tools consist of the General Information Questionnaire(See the Supplementary Material 1), the Kayser-Jones Brief Oral Health Status Examination, and the General Oral Health Assessment Index.

General information questionnaire

A self-designed general information questionnaire was used to collect participants’ demographic and health-related data. The questionnaire covered Age, Gender, Educational level, Marital status, Place of residence, Per capita monthly income, BMI, Smoking and Drinking, History of chronic diseases (e.g., Hypertension, Hyperlipidemia, Diabetes, Coronary heart disease), and post-stroke functional impairments (e.g., Motor dysfunction, Dysphagia, Disturbance of consciousness). In addition, stroke-related variables were collected, including Stroke type, Number of stroke episodes, and Days since onset.

The Kayser-Jones brief oral health status examination (BOHSE)[11]

The BOHSE was utilized to assess oral symptoms and conduct latent class analysis among stroke patients. The instrument consists of 10 items, including lymph nodes, lips, tongue, mucosa, gums, saliva, teeth, dentures, chewing, and oral cleanliness. The translated version demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.873 and a test–retest reliability of 0.775. Inter-rater reliability was 0.737, which was higher than that of the original version, indicating the robustness of the Chinese BOHSE.

The general oral health assessment index (GOHAI)[12]

The GOHAI was used to perform multivariate logistic regression analysis on the oral symptom categories among stroke patients. The scale consists of 12 items covering physiological, psychological, and social functions related to oral health problems. It is scored on a five-point Likert scale, with a maximum score of 60. The instrument is self-administered, and higher self-assessment scores indicate better oral health status, whereas lower scores reflect poorer oral health status.The Chinese version of the GOHAI demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.81 and a test–retest reliability of 0.866.

Data collection and quality control

Data collection was conducted by three uniformly trained investigators. The training covered survey procedures, questionnaire completion guidelines, communication skills, and key precautions to ensure consistency in understanding and standardized implementation. Prior to the formal survey, investigators explained the study objectives, procedures, and precautions to participants. After obtaining informed consent, face-to-face interviews were conducted to complete the questionnaires. For participants with limited literacy or comprehension, investigators provided assistance in explanation while ensuring that responses were given voluntarily and independently. Upon completion of data collection, all responses were double-entered into an electronic database by two independent researchers and cross-checked for accuracy. Following data cleaning, statistical analyses were performed using statistical software. A total of 240 questionnaires were distributed to eligible participants. Six questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete responses, resulting in 234 valid questionnaires for analysis (valid response rate: 97.5%).

Statistical analysis

LCA was performed using Mplus 8.0, with the 10 items from the BOHSE serving as observed indicators. The analysis began with a one-class model and progressively increased the number of classes to determine the optimal solution. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), and Entropy. The smaller the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, the better the model fit. Entropy ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater classification accuracy. Likelihood ratio tests, including the Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (LMRT) and the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), were also considered. The final model was selected based on a combination of statistical fit, interpretability, and clinical relevance, prioritizing models with both strong fit and meaningful classification. Data management and statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were described using medians and interquartile ranges. Between-group comparisons were conducted using t-tests or the Kruskal-Wallis H test, as appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with latent classes of oral symptoms in stroke patients. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics of stroke patients

A total of 234 stroke patients were included in this study, with a mean age of 64.41 ± 14.32 years, and 60.3% were male. The majority of participants had an education level of junior high school or below (66.2%), and 84.6% were married. Most patients resided in town (42.3%) or urban districts (30.3%), and 47.4% reported a monthly household income per capita between 1000 and 3000 yuan. A total of 54.3% were current or former smokers, and 49.6% reported current or past alcohol consumption. The most common chronic conditions were hypertension (77.4%) and hyperlipidemia (38.5%). Ischemic stroke was the predominant type (65.8%), and 79.5% of patients were experiencing their first stroke episode. The mean time since onset was 10.8 days, and more than half of the patients (51.7%) had motor dysfunction. The average GOHAI score was 40.04 ± 9.34, indicating generally poor oral health status.

Latent classes of oral symptoms in stroke patients

LCA was performed to model oral symptoms in stroke patients, comparing models with one to five classes. With increasing class numbers, the values of AIC, BIC, and adjusted BIC decreased progressively, indicating improved model fit. Entropy reached 0.928 in the three-class model, suggesting high classification accuracy. The LMR test and BLRT were statistically significant for models 1 through 3 (P < 0.001), while the LMR test for the four-class model was not significant (P = 0.286), suggesting no further improvement in model fit beyond three classes. Additionally, the class probability distribution in the three-class model was well balanced (21.8%, 34.6%, 43.6%), avoiding the emergence of excessively small classes and ensuring interpretability and practical relevance. Therefore, the three-class model was selected as the optimal solution for categorizing oral symptoms in stroke patients. Detailed model fit indices are presented in Table 1. In terms of symptom characteristics, the first class was predominantly characterized by chewing difficulties, along with issues related to natural teeth and dentures; therefore, it was designated as “Structural and Masticatory Dysfunction” (n = 51; 21.8%). The second class exhibited a higher prevalence of lip dryness, thickened tongue coating, gingival bleeding, and mucosal ulcerations, leading to its designation as “Oral Mucosal Lesions” (n = 81; 34.6%). The third class demonstrated the poorest oral hygiene and reduced salivary flow, and was thus labeled “Poor Oral Hygiene” (n = 102; 43.6%) (see Fig. 1).

Multivariate analysis of factors associated with latent classes of oral symptoms in stroke patients

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, several variables were significantly associated with latent classes. Compared with the Poor Oral Hygiene group, patients residing in rural areas (OR = 7.914, 95% CI: 1.048 ~ 59.784), with a per capita monthly income below 1000 yuan (OR = 9.386, 95% CI: 1.170 ~ 75.295), current smokers (OR = 6.411, 95% CI: 1.456 ~ 28.234), and those with coronary heart disease (OR = 4.370, 95% CI: 1.003 ~ 19.045) were more likely to be classified into the Structural and Masticatory Dysfunction group. Compared with the Poor Oral Hygiene group, patients in the Oral Mucosal Lesions group were older (OR = 1.096, 95% CI: 1.037 ~ 1.158), had an educational level of junior high school or below (OR = 5.218, 95% CI: 1.262 ~ 21.571), a per capita monthly income < 1000 yuan (OR = 9.110, 95% CI: 1.617 ~ 51.319), consumed alcohol (OR = 6.506, 95% CI: 1.500 ~ 28.224), had diabetes (OR = 5.920, 95% CI: 2.036 ~ 17.217) or coronary heart disease (OR = 5.042, 95% CI: 1.332 ~ 19.092), had a longer time since onset (OR = 1.077, 95% CI: 1.027 ~ 1.129), and were more likely to present with dysphagia (OR = 50.668, 95% CI: 6.926 ~ 370.646). First-ever stroke was identified as a protective factor (OR = 0.170, 95% CI: 0.044 ~ 0.655), suggesting that recurrent stroke patients were more likely to be classified into the Oral Mucosal Lesions group. Discussion

The findings indicate substantial inter-individual variation and distinct latent class patterns in oral symptoms, suggesting notable heterogeneity in oral health conditions. Using latent class analysis, three subgroups were identified: Structural and Masticatory Dysfunction (21.8%), Oral Mucosal Lesions (34.6%), and Poor Oral Hygiene (43.6%). Patients with structural and masticatory dysfunction often had tooth loss, denture use, and chewing difficulties, leading to nutritional limitations and reduced quality of life [13]. Oral mucosal lesion patients experienced ulcers, pain, or burning in lips, tongue, and gingiva, sometimes with secondary infections affecting daily activities [14]. Those with poor oral hygiene showed reduced salivary flow, halitosis, and plaque accumulation, increasing the risk of caries and periodontal disease [4].

In this study, patients with structural and masticatory dysfunction typically exhibited tooth loss, high denture use, and chewing impairment. Key associated factors included smoking, rural residence and/or low income, and comorbid coronary heart disease. Smoking is a major risk factor, consistent with previous findings [15]. It contributes to periodontal destruction, alveolar bone resorption, and reduced salivary secretion, leading to tooth mobility, loss, and mastication difficulties. Harmful smoke components also damage the oral mucosa, causing dryness, pain, and discomfort. Regular oral examinations, hygiene education, and dietary supplementation (vitamin C and calcium) can strengthen periodontal health and reduce smoking-related damage. Rural residence and low income increase risk due to limited healthcare resources, financial constraints, and low oral health awareness [16]. Inadequate access to dental care and preventive services, inability to afford high-quality oral hygiene products, and poor dietary patterns promote caries and periodontitis, further impairing mastication. Long-term consumption of coarse or hard foods may exacerbate tooth wear and periodontal strain. Comorbid coronary heart disease is linked to poorer oral health, with higher rates of caries, periodontitis, and enamel defects [17]. Medication side effects (e.g., gingival bleeding from anticoagulants) may further compromise oral health. Stroke patients with this comorbidity should monitor oral conditions closely and manage drug-related effects [18].

Factors associated with oral mucosal lesions included advanced age, low education level, alcohol consumption, comorbid chronic diseases (diabetes, coronary heart disease), swallowing dysfunction, and recurrent stroke or long disease duration. Advanced age [19] and low education [20] reduce mucosal regenerative capacity, increase vulnerability to injury, and delay healthcare-seeking. Older individuals may face immune decline and poorer oral hygiene, while those with lower education often lack preventive knowledge. Targeted oral health education and affordable dental services are essential for these groups. Alcohol consumption damages the mucosa through direct irritation, immune suppression, and reduced salivary secretion [21], increasing infection risk and potentially oral cancer. Limiting or ceasing alcohol use, maintaining hygiene, and regular dental checkups are recommended. Comorbid diabetes [22] and coronary heart disease [23], impair healing through vascular and immune dysfunction, while medication side effects and xerostomia further increase lesion risk. Strict chronic disease control and regular oral monitoring are needed. Swallowing dysfunction leaves food residue, promotes bacterial growth, and reduces salivary flow [24], leading to xerostomia and secondary infections [25]. Recurrent stroke and prolonged disease duration contribute to persistent inflammation, infection, and mechanical irritation from poor oral function [26]. Therefore, individualized oral care plans, gentle mouth rinses, and adequate nutrition should be implemented to mitigate these risks.

First-time stroke onset was identified as a significant factor associated with Poor Oral Hygiene, aligning with previous findings [3]. These patients mainly exhibited reduced salivary secretion and poor oral cleanliness, with milder symptoms in other areas such as the lips, tongue, and teeth, suggesting that oral problems were primarily due to impaired self-cleaning capacity and suboptimal hygiene practices, rather than evident structural damage. Patients in the acute phase of their first stroke often receive clinical and nursing care that prioritizes neurological recovery, leading to the neglect of oral hygiene maintenance [26]. Moreover, first-time stroke patients often lack structured rehabilitation awareness, limited understanding of post-stroke oral health management, and inadequate experience, which result in poor oral care routines and worsening hygiene conditions [6].

Given the heterogeneity of oral symptoms among stroke patients, intervention priorities should be tailored accordingly. At the patient level, oral health education should be customized to symptom patterns [27]—for example, enhancing self-care skills in those with poor oral hygiene, providing individualized dietary guidance for masticatory dysfunction, promoting smoking cessation and alcohol reduction, and initiating early swallowing rehabilitation with hygiene instruction for dysphagia. At the clinical level, comprehensive oral health assessment should be embedded in both acute and rehabilitation phases, with particular focus on patients with chronic comorbidities; this requires training healthcare teams in early detection and management of oral problems and fostering multidisciplinary collaboration [28]. At the system level, priorities include expanding access to oral healthcare in rural and resource-limited areas [29], integrating oral health indicators into stroke follow-up, subsidizing preventive care and quality hygiene products for high-risk groups, and raising public awareness of the link between oral health and stroke prognosis to reduce disparities and improve long-term outcomes.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the use of convenience sampling resulted in a relatively homogeneous sample source, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to stroke patients from other regions. Second, although face-to-face interviews helped improve data completeness, they may have introduced social desirability bias, as some participants might have adjusted their responses to align with perceived expectations. Furthermore, the general information questionnaire used in this study had not undergone systematic validity and reliability testing, which could affect the precision of certain measurements. Future studies could adopt more representative random sampling strategies and incorporate longitudinal research designs to further examine interaction effects, dynamically monitor changes in latent class membership and associated factors, and thereby provide a more comprehensive understanding of the heterogeneity in oral health status among stroke patients.

Conclusion

This study revealed that stroke patients exhibit heterogeneous oral symptoms influenced by multiple factors. Three latent categories of oral symptoms were identified: Structural and Masticatory Dysfunction, Oral Mucosal Lesions, and Poor Oral Hygiene. Healthcare providers should pay close attention to oral symptoms among community-dwelling stroke patients and promote awareness of oral health management. The findings support the implementation of individualized oral care interventions and underscore the importance of stratified and categorized management. However, the study is limited by its cross-sectional design and single-center sampling, which restrict the ability to capture the dynamic progression of oral symptoms. Future research should adopt multicenter designs, stratified sampling, and longitudinal follow-up to further explore the trajectory of oral symptom evolution and evaluate intervention outcomes in stroke patients.
