Recurrent atrial flutter after radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation in a patient with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy undergoing repetitive transcoronary ablation of septal hypertrophy

Introduction

Performing catheter ablation for Atrial fibrillation (AF) in patients with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) is a great challenge due to a higher rate of recurrent atrial arrhythmia [1]. Transcoronary ablation of septal hypertrophy (TASH) is a safe and effective therapy for HOCM by relieving outflow tract obstruction [2]. However, there are significant anatomical variations in the interventricular septum in patients with HOCM, which may lead to unsuccessful initial alcohol ablation and the need for multiple procedures [3]. Performing catheter ablation and TASH respectively in these patients can be tricky but such a comprehensive and respective clinical treatment would be beneficial for patients in the long term [4]. Herein, we reported a case of a patient with HOCM who had undergone TASH twice and developed atrial flutter after radiofrequency ablation for AF due to pulmonary vein reconnection. This case of HOCM is unique because of its complex complications and multiple complex atrial arrhythmias. The treatment of HOCM was successful and the postoperative follow-up results was good. Case report

A 71-year-old female started experiencing exertional dyspnea with palpitations 12 years ago. Echocardiography revealed asymmetric hypertrophy of the left ventricular wall with significant thickening of the interventricular septum (22.8 mm), resulting in severe obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract (Fig. 2A) and systolic anterior movement of the mitral valve (SAM) (Fig. 2B). The patient was diagnosed with HOCM. During the course of the disease, the clinical symptoms remained after drug treatment, accompanied by nonpersistent ventricular-tachycardia and hypotension. Therefore, the patient underwent TASH and simultaneous single-chamber implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) implantation in March 2019 (Fig. 1C&D). Two and a half years after the procedure, the patient’s symptoms recurred, and echocardiography indicated worsening left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. She was sent to the catheter lab again and coronary angiography revealed that the original ablated septal branch had reconnected, and repeat TASH procedure was performed via the same coronary artery (the first septal branch) (Fig. 2E&F). However, a few days after TASH, the patient returned to the hospital for a visit due to recurrent palpitations and fatigue. AF was observed on the electrocardiogram when the symptoms of palpitation occurred (Fig. 3A). Thus, radiofrequency ablation for AF was performed, and successful circumferential pulmonary vein isolation (CPVI) was achieved during the procedure (Fig. 3B). However, 3 years after radiofrequency ablation for AF, the patient experienced recurrent tachycardia, and an electrocardiogram suggested a pattern of persistent atrial flutter. Because of the patient’s interstitial lung disease, the use of antiarrhythmic drugs was contraindicative, and multiple antiarrhythmic drugs were ineffective. Finally, a repeat radiofrequency ablation operation was performed. During the operation, an intracardiac electrocardiogram pattern of atrial flutter was recorded after a coronary sinus electrode was placed (Fig. 4A). Electrical reconnection of the pulmonary vein was detected (Fig. 4B). The left atrial substrate map revealed a large area of low voltage in the posterior wall (Fig. 4C). A dual-loop macroreentry was visible on the activation map, with one loop moving around the posterior wall and the other around the left pulmonary ridge (Fig. 4D). After ablation of the left pulmonary ridge, the cycle length of the atrial flutter was prolonged (Fig. 4E). Finally, the atrial flutter terminated with linear ablation on the roof (Fig. 4F). At the three-month postoperative follow-up, the patient’s heart function recovered well, with recent echocardiography showing a significant reduction of the interventricular septum thickness from 22.8 to 14.6 mm, and no paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea occurred. The patient’s symptoms were relieved, and her exercise tolerance was significantly improved. Postoperative ECGs revealed sinus rhythm. (Fig. 5A&B).

Discussion

We reported a successful case of AF patient with HOCM, exhibiting exciting results stemming from appropriate procedural treatment. Firstly, previous studies have shown that patients with HOCM have higher rates of recurrent atrial arrhythmias and need for repeat AF ablation after initial ablation. Clinicians should pay more attention to remote ECG monitoring and dynamic ECGs follow-up after AF ablation, which makes it possible to record recurrent atrial arrhythmias in time. We could also further discuss about extending the course of anti-arrhythmic drug use after AF ablation in these patients. Secondly, the mechanisms of AFL after AF ablation include the following types: (1) Reentrant circuit caused by pulmonary vein reconnection; (2) Mitral isthmus-dependent atrial flutter; (3) Roof-dependent atrial flutter; (4) Atrial flutter revolving around the left appendage; (5) Anterior wall scar-related atrial flutter; and (6) Tricuspid isthmus-dependent atrial flutter. Occasionally, the epicardial muscle bundle may be involved in atrial flutter after AF ablation [6]. In this case, the activation mapping suggested roof-dependent atrial flutter [5, 7]. After ablation of the left pulmonary ridge, the circle length was prolonged. So Dual-loop macroreentry was identified, with one loop moving around the posterior wall and the other around the left pulmonary ridge. For this type of macroreentry, linear ablation connecting the two CPVI rings on the roof can terminate tachycardia. Ensuring permanent electrical isolation of pulmonary veins is the foundation for reducing the risk of recurrent atrial arrhythmia. Atrial flutter ECGs were in accordance with the characteristics of roof-dependent atrial flutter, and the F-wave is vertical in leads II/III/avF and V1 but inverted in leads avL/avR. [8, 9] This case is consistent with previous findings. Thirdly, the patient underwent TASH twice. The reason for failure of TASH could be explained that the balloon catheter maybe sent to the septal branch too deep and there are significant anatomical variations in the interventricular septum in patients with HOCM.

Conclusions

Performing catheter ablation and TASH respectively in patients with HOCM associated with AF would be tricky. But taking such a comprehensive and respective clinical treatment would be beneficial to patients in the long term. This case inspired us about catheter ablation of AF patients with HOCM from the following aspects: Firstly, CPVI is still the cornerstone for AF ablation in these patients, either acute or long-term isolation. Secondly, appropriate linear ablation, such as roof line, might be beneficial as to avoid recurrent atrial arrhythmias in the future. Thirdly, remote ECG monitoring might be taken into consideration for timely tracking. In addition, we could further discuss about extending the course of anti-arrhythmic drug use after AF ablation in these patients.
