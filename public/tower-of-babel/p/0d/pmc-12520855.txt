INTRODUCTION

Advances in veterinary medicine, preventive health programs, and individualized nutrition have significantly extended the lifespan of dogs and cats [1,2]. As companion animals more frequently reach senior and geriatric life stages, veterinarians face the dual challenge of managing age-related diseases and addressing the underlying biological processes that drive organ-specific decline. This shift has prompted a transition from survival-focused care toward strategies that preserve healthspan—maintaining physiological function, resilience, and quality of life in aging pets [1,2,3,4].

Despite these advances, veterinary geriatric care remains hindered by inconsistent life-stage terminology, limited availability and/or adoption of validated biomarkers, and fragmented approaches to prevention and management. Definitions of “senior” and “geriatric” vary by species, breed, and expected lifespan—commonly referring to the last 20%–25% of life expectancy or, in cats, ages beyond ~10 years—yet they often fail to capture biological variability and functional status [3,4]. Harmonized, species-appropriate staging systems are needed to improve diagnostic precision, guide prognostication, and facilitate communication among veterinary teams and caregivers.

A comprehensive understanding of aging in companion animals requires integrating evolutionary theories—such as programmed senescence and stochastic damage—with mechanistic insights from geroscience. Cellular hallmarks, including genomic instability, telomere attrition, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cellular senescence, are increasingly recognized as drivers of organ deterioration and chronic disease susceptibility [1,5]. These processes converge in systemic syndromes such as immunosenescence, inflammaging, sarcopenia, and reduced physiological reserve, which increase vulnerability to multimorbidity and frailty [2,6].

This review synthesizes organ-based pathophysiology of aging in dogs and cats, combining comparative, species-specific data from Sections 1 and 2 with structured diagnostic–therapeutic frameworks from Sections 3 and 4. Covered systems include gastrointestinal, hepatic, integumentary, urinary, cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine, musculoskeletal, neurologic, sensory, neoplastic, and immune systems, as well as multimorbidity and frailty syndromes. By linking biological mechanisms to clinical expression, the review aims to support proactive, integrated, and individualized geriatric veterinary care [6].

METHODS

This narrative review was prepared through a structured synthesis of peer-reviewed literature, authoritative veterinary references, and clinical guidelines on aging and age-associated diseases in dogs and cats. Comprehensive searches of PubMed, Web of Science, and CAB Abstracts used combinations of dog, cat, aging, geriatric, pathophysiology, organ system, biomarkers, and disease-specific terms. English-language articles meeting criteria for species relevance, mechanistic insight, or clinical applicability were included, with seminal earlier works referenced when foundational.

Sections 1 and 2 outline definitions and staging of aging, key evolutionary and mechanistic theories, and cellular hallmarks—genomic instability, telomere attrition, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and senescence—linked to systemic processes such as immunosenescence, inflammaging, sarcopenia, and frailty. Sections 3 and 4 present organ-based syntheses for dogs and cats, covering major systems and the syndromes of multimorbidity and frailty, with emphasis on pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnostics, therapeutics, monitoring, prevention, and breed predispositions.

Information from diverse sources was critically appraised and integrated to compare species, connect biological mechanisms to clinical expression, and inform practical management strategies. Structured comparative tables condense key findings to support translation of aging biology into proactive, individualized geriatric care.

During manuscript preparation, ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI, USA) was used for language assistance, including grammar correction, stylistic refinement, and organization of complex material. All scientific content, data interpretation, and referencing were verified by the authors, who assume full responsibility for the accuracy and integrity of the final manuscript.

OBSERVATIONS

Sections 1 and 2 present the conceptual and mechanistic framework for aging in dogs and cats, reviewing definitions and staging, species and breed variability, and the relationship between longevity and disease risk. Evolutionary theories are linked to cellular hallmarks—genomic instability, telomere attrition, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and senescence—that contribute to systemic processes such as immunosenescence, inflammaging, sarcopenia, and frailty. Relevant biomarkers, preventive strategies, and potential interventions are outlined for their clinical utility.

Sections 3 and 4 provide species-specific, organ-based summaries covering gastrointestinal, hepatic, integumentary, urinary, cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine, musculoskeletal, neurologic, sensory, neoplastic, and immune systems, along with multimorbidity and frailty syndromes. For each system, key pathophysiologic changes, clinical features, diagnostics, therapeutics, monitoring, prevention, and breed predispositions are summarized.

Across both species, shared mechanisms underlie organ vulnerability, but differences exist in prevalence, presentation, and progression. The comparative, organ-by-organ approach, supported by structured summaries, links biological mechanisms with clinical application, highlighting the need for harmonized staging, proactive screening, and integrated care to extend healthspan and maintain quality of life in aging dogs and cats.

Section 1. Aging in dogs and cats: overview

Defining aging and senescence in companion animals

The global rise in pet ownership and an increasing tendency towards humanization of pets have resulted in a greater focus on improving pet health and extending longevity. This review aims to introduce the terminology in use around aging, to discuss causes of aging according to the comparative literature, to provide an overview of the current state of knowledge about aging in dogs and cats, to outline the disease conditions associated with aging in dogs and cats, and to summarize possible anti-aging strategies that may be applicable to pets.

Aging can be defined as the time-related deterioration of physiological functions necessary for survival and fertility. The characteristics of aging—as distinguished from diseases of aging (such as cancer)—affect all individuals of a species [7]. Senescence, in biological terms, refers to a cellular process characterized by a stable state of cell cycle arrest and the secretion of inflammatory molecules [7,8]. The general senescent phenotype is characteristic of each species. For example, the familiar human senescent phenotype includes graying hair, sagging and wrinkling skin, joint stiffness, osteoporosis, loss of muscle fibers and muscular strength (sarcopenia), loss of height, memory loss, deterioration in vision and hearing, and the slowing of sexual responsiveness [8]. The senescent phenotype in dogs is graying of the haircoat (especially on the face), reduction in skin elasticity, joint stiffness, slow but progressive weight loss, sarcopenia, and lenticular sclerosis [5,9]. The phenotype is similar in cats; however, sarcopenia is often very pronounced on the head, thinning and decreased elasticity of the skin is increasingly noticeable with advancing age, and graying of the haircoat may be quite subtle or absent and lenticular sclerosis is uncommon [10].

Body size, breed variation, and longevity patterns

Maximum lifespan is a characteristic of each species [7]. It is the maximum number of years a member of that species has been known to survive. For example, the maximum human lifespan is estimated to be 121 years [7]. An average lifespan for cats is 12–18 years, but some live well into their 20’s, and occasional cats have a verified age of 30+ years [11]. For dogs, maximum lifespan is very dependent on breed, because of the enormous variation in body weight and body mass amongst different breeds [12]. There are about 450 official dog breeds, the most of any mammal. Canine phenotypes began diversifying rapidly in the Victorian era, when humans took control of breeding, replacing natural selection. Most breeds were derived from small numbers of founders within the last 200 years. Since then, dog breeds have undergone rapid phenotypic change and have been subjected to artificial selection by humans. The skull, body, and limb proportions among breeds display more phenotypic diversity than can be found within any other species. Breeds possess distinct traits related to morphology, including body size, skull shape, tail phenotype, fur type, and colour, as well as distinct behavioral traits. In nature, bigger animals live longer [13]. In mammals generally, life expectancy is inversely related to body mass except in dogs, where the inverse is observed. This unusual pattern of body size and lifespan in dogs, in contrast to other mammals, is illustrated in the following figures, which compare general mammalian trends in median longevity (Fig. 1) with breed-specific data for dogs (Fig. 2) and cats (Fig. 3) [14,15,16,17].

Lifespan, healthspan, and measuring aging

Life expectancy, the length of time a member of a species can expect to live, is not characteristic of a species, but of populations. It is usually defined as the age at which half the population still survives [7]. For example, in 1900, 50% of American women were dead by age 58. In 1980, 50% of American women lived past age 81. Thus, the phenomena of the senescent phenotype and the diseases of aging are much more common in most human populations today than they were a century ago. Life expectancy calculations require large databases that record the age at death of individuals. Unfortunately, such data is not available for pets. The life expectancy of dogs and cats, however, appears to have increased over the past few decades, likely due to most pets living in safe environments and receiving regular, modern veterinary care. Many veterinary clinics report that the average age of pets seen at their clinic(s) is increasing, and this is sometimes used as a proxy for life expectancy.

A term that has more recently entered the geroscience and medical vocabulary, in contrast to lifespan, is healthspan. Healthspan is the period of life during which the individual is healthy, rather than simply physically alive [18,19]. A common working definition is that healthspan is the period of life spent in good health, free from the chronic diseases and disabilities of aging. This definition implies that healthspan is a length of chronological time beginning at birth and ending at some subsequent time when an individual is no longer in good health and/or is suffering from diseases or disabilities of aging. Conceptually, this definition allows us to consider healthspan as a quantitative continuous variable, in the same way that we consider lifespan. This would, in principle, be useful for determining how different genes, environmental parameters, or interventions impact healthspan in a quantitative way. It would also allow us to measure the relative impact of such factors on healthspan compared to their impact on lifespan. This is important, since some interventions have been suggested to increase healthspan without increasing lifespan. It is theoretically possible that other interventions could increase lifespan without increasing healthspan to a proportional or greater extent, which would be undesirable. For this reason, evaluation of anti-aging strategies must make some attempt to assess healthspan, not just lifespan.

Chronological vs. biological age

In veterinary medicine, the terms senior and geriatric are often used interchangeably, yet they represent distinct stages of the aging process [20,21,22,23,24]. Senior typically refers to the early aging phase in which animals may not show clinical signs of aging but are considered at increased risk for age-related disease [22,23]. By contrast, geriatric implies advanced aging, characterized by the onset of functional decline, comorbidities, or geriatric syndromes such as frailty or cognitive dysfunction [20]. This differentiation aligns with the human medical model, where 'senior' may denote individuals over 60–65 years old, while ‘geriatric’ implies the presence of aging-related impairments or vulnerabilities [20,21,23,24].

Chronological age—the number of years lived—is inadequate as a sole marker of aging status [25]. Biological age, encompassing assessment of physiological function, immune competence, mobility, and cognitive performance, provides a more accurate assessment of an animal’s aging trajectory [25]. Tools such as frailty indices, muscle condition scores, or epigenetic clocks (e.g., DNA methylation markers) are being explored for more precise classification of aging in veterinary medicine [25]. Additional molecular markers such as oxidative damage (e.g., 8-OHdG), telomere attrition, and senescence-associated gene expression (e.g., p16INK4a) are being explored for clinical relevance [26]. Until validated measurement tools of biological age are routinely available, however, ‘senior’ and ‘geriatric’ remain the major classifications of older pets. Biological age is particular to species, breed and body size. Cats are typically considered senior at 11–14 years and geriatric at 15 years or older. However, the subtlety of clinical signs of aging in cats, compared to dogs, often delays recognition [27]. Small-breed dogs are often considered senior around 7–9 years and geriatric beyond 11–13 years, while giant-breed dogs may reach geriatric status as early as 6 years due to accelerated physiological aging [25].

Breed-based classification and standardization

Breed-specific data from longitudinal cohorts, such as the Dog Aging Project, underscore the need for size- and breed (genetics)-adjusted aging thresholds [28]. In addition to genetic and physiological factors, modulation of the gut microbiome through various ‘biotics’ (e.g., probiotics, prebiotics, postbiotics) has emerged as a promising adjunctive strategy for influencing aging trajectories in dogs and cats [29]. In clinical practice, distinguishing senior from geriatric is important for preventive screening, diagnostics, and therapeutic planning [20,22,23]. Senior wellness exams may include baseline diagnostics and lifestyle modification counseling, whereas geriatric care often involves management of multiple concurrent conditions, monitoring of frailty markers, and palliative strategies [20]. Some veterinary associations have published life stage guidelines (e.g., American Animal Hospital Association, American Association of Feline Practitioners), but terminology varies, especially in multinational contexts [27]. Until validated biological markers are widely available and adopted, there is growing consensus for the adoption of standardized age categorization. Standardized terminology improves communication, clinical decision-making, and cross-study comparability in aging research [24,27,30]. Due to wide breed variations, the classification of dogs as ‘senior’ or ‘geriatric’ must be adjusted based on breed and body size [3,24,30,31,32].

For example, one proposal would categorize adult dogs as:

• Small breeds: Senior at ~8 years, geriatric ≥ 12 years

• Small breeds: Senior at ~8 years, geriatric ≥ 12 years

• Medium breeds: Senior at ~7 years, geriatric ≥ 10 years

• Medium breeds: Senior at ~7 years, geriatric ≥ 10 years

• Giant breeds: Senior at ~5 years, geriatric ≥ 7–8 years

Breed-adjusted life stage charts, now adopted in some practices and by major pet food companies, help tailor nutrition, screening, and preventative care protocols [3,15,24].

Section 2. Pathophysiology of geriatric diseases

Integrating theories into a veterinary geriatric care framework

Understanding aging solely as a chronological inevitability limits clinical impact. Synthesizing insights from evolutionary, cellular, and systems-level aging theories, however, provides a foundation for transforming veterinary care into a preventive, predictive, and proactive discipline [5]. Veterinary geroscience seeks to operationalize measures of oxidative damage, inflammaging, cellular senescence, and stem cell exhaustion into concrete clinical strategies—e.g., earlier screening, nutritional modulation, and geroprotective therapies [33].

Some biomarkers of aging are now measurable and need validation in dogs and cats [34]:

• 8-OHdG and F2-isoprostanes for oxidative DNA/lipid damage [35]

• 8-OHdG and F2-isoprostanes for oxidative DNA/lipid damage [35]

• p16INK4a and γH2AX as markers of senescence [36]

Toward a systems-based geriatric care model

Veterinary aging care is shifting toward a systems-biology model, emphasizing physiologic reserve and frailty status over age in years. Tools being used by veterinarians to classify patients as robust, vulnerable, or frail include:

Impact on vaccination and disease susceptibility

Immunosenescence has practical implications for veterinary practice. Vaccine efficacy is often reduced in older animals, prompting consideration of revised immunization protocols tailored to geriatric physiology [44]. Annual antibody titers (serological monitoring), may be warranted to assess immune protection, particularly against core viral pathogens such as canine distemper, parvovirus, and feline panleukopenia [44]. Geriatric dogs and cats are also more susceptible to opportunistic infections, such as urinary tract infections, periodontal disease, and respiratory illnesses [44]. Reduced mucosal immunity, including compromised secretory immunoglobulin A levels, weakens first-line defenses in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts [44]. Moreover, the incidence of immune-mediated disorders—including hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and chronic enteropathies—may paradoxically increase, possibly reflecting dysregulation rather than suppression of immune pathways [44].

Gastrointestinal and hepatic system

Gastrointestinal (GI) and hepatic disorders are common in aged dogs and often contribute to chronic weight loss, anorexia, and poor coat condition. Conditions such as chronic enteropathy, protein-losing enteropathy, colonic dysmotility, dysbiosis, hepatic dysfunction, and pancreatitis frequently coexist and impact systemic health. Multifactorial etiologies and subtle clinical signs in early stages often lead to delayed recognition and management [45,46,47,48,49,50]. Age-related changes in the GI tract include decreased gastric acid secretion, reduced pancreatic enzyme activity, impaired mucosal immunity, and altered motility. These factors contribute to malabsorption, dysbiosis, and chronic low-grade inflammation. ‘Leaky gut’ phenomena and microbial imbalance have been implicated in systemic effects. Hepatic aging involves reduced hepatocyte regenerative capacity, oxidative stress, and impaired detoxification, often resulting in hepatic lipidosis or chronic hepatitis [45,51,52,53,54,55,56,57].

These pathophysiologic changes and disease entities are summarized in Table 1, which outlines the major gastrointestinal and hepatic conditions associated with aging in dogs, including clinical signs, diagnostics, and breed-specific considerations.

Integumentary system

Dermatologic disorders are frequently encountered in aged dogs and often reflect underlying systemic disease, immune dysregulation, or chronic inflammation. Common conditions include seborrhea (oleosa/sicca), xerosis, pyoderma, Malassezia dermatitis, flea allergy dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, and cutaneous neoplasia. Age-associated immune decline and skin barrier impairment predispose older dogs to recurrent skin infections and inflammatory dermatoses [58,59,60,61,62,63]. Skin aging involves epidermal and dermal thinning, reduced sebaceous and sweat gland function, impaired keratinocyte turnover, and weakened skin barrier integrity. Immunosenescence, chronic inflammation, and hormonal alterations further compromise cutaneous homeostasis, leading to increased susceptibility to pathogens and allergens [58,60,62]. Urinary system

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) and associated urinary disorders are prevalent in aged dogs, particularly those over 10 years of age. CKD is a progressive, multifactorial syndrome that affects systemic homeostasis and contributes to significant morbidity. Hypertensive nephropathy, renal secondary hyperparathyroidism (RSHP), and uremic syndrome are common complications. Timely identification, dietary and pharmacologic intervention, and long-term monitoring can improve quality of life and survival [64,65,66,67,68,69,70,71,72,73]. CKD in dogs is typically associated with irreversible nephron loss due to chronic interstitial nephritis, glomerulosclerosis, and tubular atrophy. Progressive glomerular hypertension and proteinuria exacerbate damage to the remaining nephrons, perpetuating a cycle of fibrosis and functional decline. RSHP develops in later stages due to phosphate retention, impaired vitamin D metabolism, and compensatory PTH elevation. Uremic syndrome reflects multisystem dysfunction caused by toxin accumulation [68,69,70,73,74,75,76]. A more detailed overview of aging-associated urinary tract conditions in dogs—including diagnostic markers and treatment plans—is presented in Table 3.

Cardiovascular and respiratory system

Cardiovascular and respiratory disorders are prevalent, yet frequently underdiagnosed, in aged dogs. Common cardiac conditions include myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD), dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), arrhythmias, and systemic hypertension. On the respiratory side, chronic bronchitis, tracheal collapse, and pulmonary fibrosis are often observed. These disorders significantly affect exercise tolerance, respiratory efficiency, and overall well-being. Early detection and tailored therapy can extend longevity and improve quality of life [22,77,78,79,80,81,82,83,84,85,86]. MMVD involves progressive degeneration of the mitral valve leaflets, resulting in mitral regurgitation and volume overload of the left atrium and ventricle. DCM is marked by myocardial thinning and impaired systolic function. Arrhythmias can arise from atrial or ventricular conduction abnormalities. Systemic hypertension contributes to end-organ damage, including cardiac hypertrophy and renal injury. Respiratory diseases such as bronchitis and fibrosis stem from chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and airway collapse [81,86,87,88,89,90,91,92,93,94,95]. The systemic impact of cardiovascular and respiratory aging is in Table 4, which summarizes key disease presentations, monitoring protocols, and breed-related risks.

Endocrine system

Endocrine and metabolic disorders are frequently encountered in aging dogs and include hypothyroidism, hyperadrenocorticism (Cushing’s syndrome), diabetes mellitus, and obesity. These conditions are often interrelated and can significantly affect morbidity and longevity. Comprehensive management involving nutritional, medical, and lifestyle strategies is required to optimize outcomes in senior dogs [96,97,98,99,100,101,102]. Hypothyroidism is typically caused by autoimmune thyroiditis or idiopathic follicular atrophy, leading to decreased metabolic rate. Cushing’s syndrome is due to excess cortisol from a pituitary or adrenal tumor or pituitary hyperplasia and results in protein catabolism, immune suppression, and insulin resistance. Diabetes mellitus is usually due to insulin-deficiency or insulin-resistance, and is often associated with obesity, which is itself characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation and dysregulated adipokine secretion. Age-related hormonal shifts and mitochondrial dysfunction further exacerbate these conditions [98,100,101,102,103,104,105,106,107]. Musculoskeletal system

Musculoskeletal disorders such as osteoarthritis (OA), sarcopenia, and intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) are major contributors to decreased mobility and quality of life in aged dogs. These conditions often develop insidiously and may go unrecognized until significant impairment has occurred. Early identification and multimodal intervention are essential to preserve function and comfort in senior dogs [108,109,110,111,112,113,114,115,116]. OA is characterized by articular cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, subchondral bone remodeling, and osteophyte formation. Sarcopenia, common in aging dogs, involves age-related loss of muscle mass and function, often exacerbated by chronic inflammation and disuse. IVDD is caused by degeneration and herniation of intervertebral discs, leading to spinal cord or nerve root compression. Obesity contributes to these disorders through mechanical overload and increased inflammatory cytokine production [117,118,119,120,121,122,123,124,125]. To aid in practical application, Table 6 highlights the musculoskeletal conditions most frequently observed in senior dogs, including early indicators and evidence-based interventions.

Neurological system and cognition

Neurological and cognitive disorders are increasingly recognized in aging dogs due to rising longevity and greater caregiver awareness. Canine cognitive dysfunction syndrome (CCDS) shares similarities with human Alzheimer’s disease and significantly impacts daily functioning and welfare. Other neurologic conditions include brain aging, IVDD, and secondary neurologic decline due to systemic illness or neoplasia. Early behavioral screening and integrative care strategies are essential for preserving cognitive function and quality of life [20,126,127,128,129]. Aging canine brains exhibit structural and biochemical changes including β-amyloid plaque accumulation, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. These changes lead to synaptic loss, hippocampal atrophy, and cholinergic deficits, all contributing to impaired cognition and behavior. Vascular compromise and neuroendocrine dysregulation further exacerbate neurologic aging. Some cases involve coexisting metabolic or neoplastic diseases affecting the central nervous system [20,126,127,130,131,132]. Age-related denervation and remodeling of the neuromuscular junction result in fiber-type switching (from type II to type I fibers), reduced firing frequency, and progressive atrophy of fast-twitch fibers, which are essential for strength and balance [133]. These neuromuscular alterations explain common clinical signs such as reluctance to jump, reduced gait speed, and difficulty climbing stairs or jumping in aging dogs and cats [134,135]. The complex neurologic and cognitive changes seen in aged dogs are summarized in Table 7, with an emphasis on clinical detection, progression, and supportive care.

Sensory system

Sensory decline, particularly involving vision and hearing, is common in aged dogs. It is often underrecognized, however, due to gradual onset and the animal’s ability to adapt. These deficits impact behavior, communication, and quality of life [136,137,138,139]. Age-related ocular changes include lens thickening, reduced aqueous production, corneal degeneration, and retinal thinning due to photoreceptor loss. Frequent ocular conditions diagnosed clinically include lenticular sclerosis, cataracts, keratoconjunctivitis sicca (KCS), progressive retinal atrophy, and glaucoma. Presbycusis, or age-related hearing loss, is a prevalent condition. Hearing loss results from degeneration of cochlear hair cells, neuronal apoptosis, and alterations in central auditory pathways. Both visual and auditory impairment are exacerbated by oxidative damage, impaired repair mechanisms, and concurrent systemic illness [139,140,141,142,143]. Neoplastic and immune system

Neoplasia and immune-mediated disorders are significant health concerns in aged dogs. Common malignancies include lymphoma, mast cell tumors, hemangiosarcoma, soft tissue sarcomas, and oral melanoma. Immune-mediated diseases such as immune-mediated hemolytic anemia and immune-mediated thrombocytopenia may occur alone or alongside neoplastic processes. Aging-related immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation contribute to disease risk [33,44,144,145,146]. Cumulative DNA damage, impaired apoptosis, oxidative stress, and persistent inflammation promote oncogenesis in older dogs. Immunosenescence results in diminished immune surveillance and dysregulated cytokine signaling, facilitating tumor progression and autoimmunity. Neoplastic transformation is often multifactorial, involving environmental, genetic, and endocrine contributors [147,148,149]. Multimorbidity and frailty syndromes

Multimorbidity and frailty are emerging paradigms in geriatric veterinary medicine, describing dogs with multiple chronic diseases and functional decline. These syndromes reflect an accumulation of health deficits that exceed the body’s adaptive capacity, often resulting in reduced resilience and increased vulnerability to adverse outcomes. They represent a critical challenge in aging dogs and demand an integrated and proactive approach to care [22,33,150,151,152]. Key mechanisms include chronic systemic inflammation (‘inflammaging’), oxidative stress, immune senescence, and endocrine dysregulation. Mitochondrial dysfunction, loss of proteostasis, gut microbiome disruption, and neuroendocrine decline further contribute to progressive organ and functional deterioration. The interplay of physical inactivity, malnutrition, and chronic disease amplifies frailty in aged dogs [22,151,153]. Sarcopenia involves loss of type II muscle fibers and reduced neuromuscular signaling. Chronic low-grade inflammation in aging animals (inflammaging) also exacerbates muscle breakdown through the upregulation of tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6, and myostatin, which activate proteolytic pathways like the ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy-lysosome systems [116,154]. Furthermore, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction reduce ATP availability and impair the regenerative potential of satellite cells, limiting muscle repair capacity [126,127]. Chronic inflammation, inactivity, and hormonal changes further contribute to musculoskeletal decline in geriatric dogs. Gastrointestinal and hepatic system

Gastrointestinal and hepatic disorders are commonly encountered in aging cats and are associated with anorexia, malnutrition, and reduced quality of life. Common conditions include hepatic lipidosis, chronic enteropathy, constipation, chronic pancreatitis and nutrient malabsorption. These disorders are often underdiagnosed due to the subtlety of early clinical signs, yet they play a pivotal role in the development of sarcopenia, frailty, and immune dysfunction in geriatric patients [118,155,156]. Aging results in reductions in gastric acid secretion, pancreatic enzyme activity, and intestinal motility, leading to impaired digestion and absorption of essential nutrients such as B vitamins and fat-soluble vitamins. Additionally, age-related shifts in gut microbiota composition contribute to dysbiosis and systemic inflammation. These factors predispose cats to hepatic lipidosis, particularly when anorexia occurs in overweight individuals, and promote chronic gastrointestinal dysfunction [155,157,158]. Integumentary system

Dermatologic disorders are frequently observed in geriatric cats and often reflect a combination of intrinsic skin aging, diminished grooming behavior, and chronic systemic disease. Common conditions include seborrhea, xerosis, alopecia, feline hyperesthesia syndrome, and cutaneous neoplasia. These issues may present with subtle or behavioral signs, and are frequently overlooked during routine care despite their impact on comfort and skin barrier function [10,159,160,161]. Aging skin undergoes structural and functional changes, including epidermal thinning, reduced sebaceous gland activity, and impaired wound healing capacity. These changes lead to increased transepidermal water loss, barrier dysfunction, and higher susceptibility to microbial invasion and chronic inflammation. Diminished grooming and alterations in the skin microbiome further exacerbate dermatoses in senior cats [62,160,161,162]. Urinary system

Urinary disorders, particularly chronic kidney disease (CKD), are among the most prevalent and impactful conditions affecting geriatric cats. CKD frequently coexists with hypertension, proteinuria, and metabolic disturbances, contributing to progressive multisystemic decline. Due to the insidious onset of clinical signs, routine wellness screening is essential for early detection and intervention [163,164,165]. The primary histopathological lesion in feline CKD is chronic interstitial nephritis, characterized by nephron loss, interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and glomerulosclerosis. Compensatory hyperfiltration in surviving nephrons leads to glomerular hypertension and proteinuria, accelerating renal damage. Systemic consequences include anemia, metabolic acidosis, and accumulation of gut-derived uremic toxins such as indoxyl sulfate and p-cresol sulfate [163,164,165]. Cardiovascular and respiratory system

Cardiovascular and respiratory diseases are relatively common in geriatric cats and often present with vague or nonspecific clinical signs. Sedentary cats need little cardiorespiratory reserve due to inactivity. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most prevalent cardiac condition, frequently accompanied by systemic hypertension and, in some cases, arterial thromboembolism. Chronic bronchitis and feline asthma are the primary respiratory concerns, often underdiagnosed due to their subtle progression [166,167,168,169]. HCM in cats involves concentric thickening of the left ventricular myocardium, leading to diastolic dysfunction, elevated left atrial pressure, and potential thromboembolic complications. Age-associated vascular changes and endocrine comorbidities contribute to systemic hypertension, which in turn can exacerbate cardiac, renal and ocular damage. Chronic bronchial disorders are characterized by airway inflammation, smooth muscle hypertrophy, and mucus accumulation, resulting in progressive respiratory compromise [167,168,169,170]. Endocrine and metabolic system

Endocrine and metabolic disorders are frequently diagnosed in aging cats, with obesity, diabetes mellitus, and hyperthyroidism being the most common conditions. These disorders contribute to systemic complications including hepatic lipidosis, renal dysfunction, and cardiovascular stress. Subtle or overlapping signs often delay diagnosis, underscoring the importance of early screening and comprehensive metabolic assessments [171,172,173]. Obesity in senior cats is characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and dysregulated adipokine secretion, notably involving leptin and adiponectin. Hyperthyroidism results from nodular hyperplasia of the thyroid gland(s), leading to increased metabolic rate and multisystemic stress. In diabetic cats, pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and peripheral insulin resistance impair glucose metabolism, often exacerbated by obesity and age-related changes in the gut microbiota [171,174,175]. Musculoskeletal system

Musculoskeletal disorders are increasingly recognized in aged cats and are often underdiagnosed due to the species’ stoic nature and subtle behavioral changes. Osteoarthritis and sarcopenia are the most common age-associated conditions, contributing to chronic pain, reduced mobility, and decreased quality of life. Early identification through mobility scoring and musculoskeletal assessment is essential to preserve function in geriatric patients [176,177,178]. In n cats, OA is characterized by progressive cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodeling, synovitis, and joint capsule fibrosis. Sarcopenia involves the loss of type II muscle fibers and impaired neuromuscular signaling, often exacerbated by chronic inflammation and reduced physical activity. Obesity-related inflammation and hormonal shifts, such as decreased insulin-like growth factor-1, further contribute to musculoskeletal decline in senior cats [176,179,180]. Neurological system and cognition

Neurological and cognitive disorders are increasingly recognized in senior cats, particularly those over 11 years of age. Feline cognitive dysfunction syndrome (CDS) is the most prominent condition, characterized by behavioral changes and declining spatial and social awareness. Brain tumors, such as meningioma, must also be considered during diagnostic evaluation for an aged cat with central neurologic signs. Other age-associated disorders include seizure disorders and vestibular dysfunction. Early detection and environmental support can significantly enhance quality of life [181,182,183]. Cognitive dysfunction in aged cats is associated with β-amyloid accumulation, oxidative stress, and synaptic degeneration in the cortex and hippocampus. Neuroinflammation, cholinergic neuronal loss, and ventricular dilation further contribute to the progression of cognitive decline. Similar to canine CDS, the condition reflects multisystem neurodegenerative aging processes [182,183,184]. Sensory system

Sensory decline, including vision and hearing loss, is common in aging cats and may contribute to behavioral changes, disorientation, and reduced interaction. Key conditions include lenticular sclerosis, cataracts, retinal degeneration, and presbycusis. These changes are often gradual and may go unnoticed without proactive evaluation [185,186,187]. Ocular aging involves lenticular fiber compaction (sclerosis), photoreceptor degeneration, and reduced tear production, which may predispose to KCS. In the auditory system, presbycusis results from degeneration of cochlear hair cells and neural pathways, leading to progressive sensorineural hearing loss. These sensory deficits can affect spatial orientation and quality of life in geriatric cats [186,188,189]. Neoplastic and immune system

Neoplastic and immune-mediated disorders are significant contributors to morbidity and mortality in aged cats. Common tumors include lymphoma, squamous cell carcinoma, mammary carcinoma, and cutaneous mast cell tumors. Immune dysregulation in older cats may also manifest as anemia or chronic inflammation. Early detection and individualized treatment are essential to improving outcomes [190,191,192]. Age-related tumorigenesis is driven by cumulative genetic mutations, impaired DNA repair, and exposure to environmental or viral carcinogens such as feline leukemia virus. Immunosenescence reduces T cell surveillance and impairs antitumor immunity. Chronic inflammation and hormonal factors may also contribute to the development of neoplastic and immune-mediated conditions in senior cats [191,193,194]. Multimorbidity and frailty syndromes

As in aged dogs, multimorbidity and frailty syndromes are prevalent in aged cats, reflecting the cumulative burden of chronic diseases, functional decline, and nutritional deficits. Frequently concurrent conditions include CKD, hyperthyroidism, sarcopenia, neoplasia, and cognitive dysfunction. These syndromes are major predictors of poor outcomes and reduced resilience to stressors. A comprehensive geriatric assessment is essential for early identification and proactive management [155,156,195]. Frailty arises from multisystem dysregulation involving chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, hormonal imbalance, and neuroendocrine decline. Progressive loss of physiological reserve impairs the ability to maintain homeostasis under stress. Nutritional deficiencies, oxidative stress, and reduced activity accelerate the frailty trajectory in senior cats, which may be compounded by appetite reduction. [155,196,197]. DISCUSSION

The progressive extension of lifespan in dogs and cats has intensified the clinical relevance of aging biology in veterinary practice. Aging should be approached as a biologically driven, organ- and species-specific process, not merely a chronological milestone [1,2,3,4]. Mechanistic insights—ranging from genomic instability and telomere attrition to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cellular senescence—offer explanatory frameworks for the multisystem vulnerabilities observed in senior and geriatric patients [1,2,5,6,9]. These processes converge in systemic syndromes such as immunosenescence, inflammaging, sarcopenia, and frailty, which amplify the risk of multimorbidity and complicate clinical management [10,11].

Comparative analysis of canine and feline organ systems reveals both shared and divergent patterns of age-related change. While certain mechanisms are conserved, species-specific differences in prevalence, clinical expression, and progression underscore the importance of tailored diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [12,14,15,16,17]. The structured, organ-based synthesis in this review provides a practical reference for integrating biological knowledge into case management, enabling earlier detection and more targeted interventions.

Several limitations in current geriatric veterinary care remain evident. Inconsistent life-stage definitions, limited availability of validated biomarkers, and fragmented disease surveillance hinder timely intervention [3,4,14,15,16,17]. Broader adoption of harmonized staging systems, integration of emerging biomarkers, and longitudinal monitoring could improve prognostic accuracy and therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, proactive screening for multimorbidity and frailty should be prioritized, as these syndromes have a disproportionate impact on quality of life and clinical decision-making [18,19,20,21,22,23,24].

Ultimately, aligning clinical practice with advances in geroscience will require not only application of mechanistic insights but also a shift toward preventive, individualized, and systems-based care. This approach has the potential to extend healthspan, delay functional decline, and sustain the quality of life for aging companion animals [20,21,22,23,24].
