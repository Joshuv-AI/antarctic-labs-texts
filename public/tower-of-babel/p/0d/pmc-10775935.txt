Treatment of Aggressive Periodontitis in a Patient with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus: A Case Report with 8 Years Follow-up
Authors: Pradeep Koppolu, Kizhakke Veetil Sheethi, Lingam Amara Swapna, Mandapathi Priyanka, Rawa Kamal Abdelrahim, Aravinda Basireddy, Obada Mandil
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with oral desquamative lesions is one of the rare clinical entities. Periodontal disease and SLE display various mechanisms and possess a wide range of pathological characteristics. The tissue destruction mechanism of periodontitis and autoimmune diseases share similar pathways, and mounting reports studied the association between these two entities. The present case is of a 24-year-old female patient who complained of generalized widening of spaces in between the teeth. Along with it, She suffered from loss of hair, weakness, edema in the legs as well as arthralgia. The patient was identified to be suffering from SLE according to the American Rheumatism Association and European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology criteria 1 year before she reported to the dentist. She suffered from hair loss, weakness, arthralgia as well as edema in the legs. Based on the oral, clinical, and radiographic findings, she was diagnosed with aggressive periodontitis case. After nonsurgical periodontal treatment, the flap was reflected, debridement was done, after root conditioning with tetracycline, bovine osseous xenograft was placed in all the sites where ever there is angular bone loss, later sutured with interrupted direct loop suturing technique with 4-0 silk suture. Clinical and radiographic evaluation was done every 6 weeks to check the progress of the treatment. 6 months and 8-year follow-up revealed satisfactory clinical and radiographic outcomes. Based on the present case report and the previous literature, we recommend the use of xenograft in treating aggressive periodontitis patients.

INTRODUCTION

Systematic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease with the characteristic feature of immune-related inflammation and affects all organs with a wide range of severity.[1,2] The clinical features display frequent acute or chronic inflammation periods and elicit malfunction of various vital organs such as skin, kidneys, and joints. Meanwhile, in SLE patients, there exist some manifestations, which include honeycomb plaques, cheilitis, varying oral ulceration such as erythematous patches, oral lesions as a result of lichen planus, and xerostomia.[3,4] The previous report indicates that SLE patients displays a greater frequency of dental carious teeth and a greater number of lost teeth.[5] Thus, the above-stated clinical consequences may lead to a negative impact on their oral health and also affects their quality of life.[6] Mounting recent reports highlight the significant association between SLE and the risk of periodontitis. The first article regarding the association between SLE and periodontitis was first reported in 1981 in a 17-year-old girl who presented with spontaneous bleeding and edematous gingiva.[7] In an alternative case report, a 35-year-old Korean girl with SLE displayed generalized gingival swelling, and in this case absence of predisposing aspects for chronic periodontitis was interpreted by the authors in favor of a direct association between the periodontal status and SLE.[8] However, inflammation is regarded to be the foremost source of disease in SLE, including many organs and tissues. Treatment frequently includes removal of deposits by scaling and root planing, improving oral hygiene, as well as antibiotic therapy. Surgical therapy is needed whenever angular defects are noticed. Maintenance therapy is crucial to accomplish improved results. In the present case report, we present the surgical management of aggressive periodontitis in SLE patients with xenograft with a follow-up for 8 years.

CASE REPORT

A 24-year-old female patient visited a private clinic in Hyderabad, India, in 2014 and complained of generalized widening of spaces in between the teeth. One year before, the patient was diagnosed to be suffering from SLE according to the American Rheumatism Association and European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology standards. She suffered from loss of hair, weakness, edema in the legs as well as arthralgia. Routine laboratory investigations revealed an elevated sedimentation rate and anemia (Hb 7.4 g/dl). Serological analysis showed a positive Coombs’ test (lgG and C3) and raised levels of antinuclear antibody. Along with these findings, a butterfly rash was noticed on the cheeks [Figure 1]. On clinical examination, the plaque and calculus deposits were minimal [Figure 2]; an average of 5.2 mm clinical attachment levels and 6.7 mm of pocket depths were recorded, and the radiograph revealed an angular bone loss in all the right and left mandibular premolars and molars. Based on the clinical and radiographic findings, the case was established as aggressive periodontitis. Patient consent was obtained and after performing the nonsurgical periodontal therapy, the flap was reflected in relation to (irt) #35, #36, #37, and debridement was done [Figure 3], after root conditioning with tetracycline, bovine osseous xenograft (Bio-Oss) was placed in all the sites where ever there is angular bone loss, irt #35, #36, 37 and irt #45, #46, #47 later sutured with interrupted direct loop suturing technique with 4-0 silk suture. No antibiotics were prescribed to the patient. The patient was asked to use chlorhexidine 0.2% mouthwash for 2 weeks. The sutures were removed after 10 days and the patient was instructed to follow routine oral hygiene instructions properly. The radiograph revealed bone formation in all the sites where the bone graft was placed.

DISCUSSION

SLE is a chronic condition with multiorgan inflammatory disease associated with immune system dysfunction. The etiology of SLE is mainly due to the deposition of autoimmune antibody complexes in connective tissue of various organs, which may lead to altered immune response. The SLE is usually observed in the female population and the prevalence is as high as 90%. The occurrence of SLE may alter orofacial structures and functions through various processes. The major complications encompass xerostomia, burning, and painful oral mucosal lesions. Further, reduced salivary flow can also prelude to periodontal disease in SLE patients. A previous report by Mutlu et al.[9] states that SLE patients displayed significantly lower periodontal probing depths than of the healthy controls. This might be due to the use of corticosteroids and NSAIDs for the treatment of SLE. A previous report by Keller et al.[10] shows that chronic use of corticosteroids might lead to osteoporosis and damage the osseointegrated dental implants. The present case report describes the effective management of SLE patients with aggressive periodontitis using bovine-derived xenograft. In the present case, the teeth in which the xenograft was placed to compensate for the defect, did not show any bone loss during the 8 years of follow-up. Meanwhile, patients with steroid therapy are susceptible to osteoporosis, and it is a difficult task to recover such patients using conventional treatment. Patients on steroid therapy are often osteoporotic. The SLE patients are prescribed antimalarial, steroids, and immunosuppressive drugs, contingent on their respective clinical circumstances. These drugs are responsible in decreasing the inflammatory process, which aggravates the periodontal tissue destruction. In addition, the dosage is enhanced when outbreaks are experienced by SLE patients. Bio-Oss elicits an osteoconductive surface and further provides a source for osteoinductive factors, which enables to have proper regeneration of the bone.[11,12] Repetitive dental follow-ups of patients with SLE (with or without arthritic treatment) will be use for early diagnosis of periodontal disease.[13] It is imperative to cautiously observe the jawbone before proceeding with placing a graft or a dental implant because of the jeopardy for osteonecrosis secondary to the use of steroids. Dental check-ups must be considered a routine in diagnostic appraisal and clinical management of patients suffering from SLE. The treatment of aggressive periodontitis is more challenging compared to that of chronic periodontitis since the present case is coupled with SLE it has a strong genetic tendency as a risk factor which made it difficult to execute the planning of the treatment. The key to successful management lies in the initial diagnosis of the disease and employing the treatment modality mentioned in the case, followed by thorough enduring maintenance therapy.

CONCLUSION

Within the confines of the present case report, it can be clinched that the combination of proper periodontal treatment along with the usage of bovine-derived xenograft resulted in a significant improvement in both the hard and soft-tissue parameters from baseline data to 8-year duration. There was no complication during the surgical procedure or in the follow-ups. Based on the present case report and the previous literature, we recommend the use of xenograft in treating aggressive periodontitis patients.
