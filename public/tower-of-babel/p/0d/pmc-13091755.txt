Capicua mediates interleukin-2 withdrawal–induced death of activated CD8+ T cells
Authors: Jun-Ge Shi, Ye Xu, Wen-Hao Hang, Xin-Yue Zhang, Zhen-Wu Ma, Shu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During the course of an immune response, antigen-reactive T cells undergo clonal expansion and are subsequently eliminated by cytokine withdrawal-induced cell death (CWID), an intrinsic apoptotic program essential for immune homeostasis. However, the mechanisms orchestrating CWID remain poorly understood. Here, we performed genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens in CTLL-2 cells, which identified the transcriptional repressor Capicua (CIC) as a critical mediator of CWID. CIC mediates CWID but not apoptosis induced by staurosporine or etoposide. Deficiency of CIC attenuates IL-2 withdrawal-induced death of mouse primary CD8+ but not CD4+ T cells. CIC promotes CWID via a BIM-independent pathway, and simultaneous deletion of CIC and BIM fully abrogates CWID. Although CIC is activated upon IL-2 withdrawal to repress its target genes, its role in CWID is independent of its transcriptional repression. Instead, CIC promotes CWID by facilitating Cullin-3/KEAP1–mediated BCL2 degradation and disrupting the BAX-BCL2 interaction. In a Listeria monocytogenes infection model, CIC-deficiency suppresses apoptosis of activated CD8+ T cells, resulting in their increase in the later phase of infection. Our findings suggest that CIC plays a vital role in CWID through a noncanonical mechanism, providing new insights into how the immune system limits uncontrolled inflammation and immunopathology.

Introduction

T cells are key components of the adaptive immune system, essential for maintaining host health and protecting against diverse diseases (Chopp et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2018). T cells reside in peripheral lymphoid organs until they encounter foreign antigens presented by antigen-presenting cells, after which they migrate to sites of inflammation and undergo clonal expansion to defend against the pathogen (Krammer et al., 2007). After the potential infection has been cleared, most activated T cells are eliminated through apoptosis to maintain immune homeostasis, whereas only a small pool develops into memory T cells to ensure rapid responses upon reinfection (Hildeman et al., 2002a, Hildeman et al., 2002b). Cytokine withdrawal-induced death (CWID) is a major self-regulating apoptotic mechanism that contributes to the contraction of effector T cells (Lee et al., 2023). CWID is triggered by a decline in cytokines (mainly IL-2) in the local environment at the late phase of an immune response, which induces expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BIM (Lee et al., 2023). The accumulated BIM then overwhelms the pool of anti-apoptotic BCL2 and triggers BAX/BAK-dependent pore formation, thereby activating the intrinsic apoptosis pathway (Czabotar and Garcia-Saez, 2023). CWID is an important checkpoint that controls the number of active T cells and limits inflammation during an immune response. Dysregulation of CWID has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several autoimmune disorders, including systemic lupus erythematosus (Crispín et al., 2011; Rodríguez-Rodríguez et al., 2020; Rosetti et al., 2022). However, how CWID is orchestrated and whether additional pathways beyond the classical model are involved remain unclear.

Capicua (CIC) is an evolutionarily conserved transcriptional repressor that plays essential roles in diverse developmental and physiological processes (Lee, 2020). CIC exists in two isoforms, CIC-S (short) and CIC-L (long), of which CIC-L contains a unique extended N-terminal region (Lee, 2020). The regulation of CIC activity has been extensively studied in the context of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling. EGF treatment leads to ERK-dependent multisite phosphorylation of CIC, which inactivates CIC by inhibiting its binding to target DNA and inducing its cytoplasmic translocation (Dissanayake et al., 2011; Park et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2017). Moreover, EGFR activation also induces c-Src-mediated phosphorylation of the Y1455 on CIC-S to promote its cytoplasmic translocation (Bunda et al., 2020). Numerous studies have demonstrated that Capicua is critical for T cell development and homeostasis (Lee, 2020). Loss of CIC has been shown to attenuate TCR signaling in CD4+CD8+ double-positive (DP) cells, thereby impairing positive selection of thymocytes (Kim et al., 2021). In the periphery, CIC-deficiency promotes excessive Tfh cell differentiation and germinal center (GC) response in a T-cell-intrinsic manner through upregulation of the target gene ETV5. CIC has also been shown to play an important role in the generation of CD8+ resident memory T cells in the liver and to be essential for maintaining normal liver function (Park et al., 2019). Of note, both hematopoietic lineage-specific CIC-deficient (Cicf/f, Vav1-Cre) mice and T cell-specific CIC-deficient (Cicf/f, Cd4-Cre) mice exhibit lymphoproliferative and autoimmune-like phenotypes (Lee, 2020). Moreover, deletion of Cic in adult mice leads to the development of T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma (T-ALL) (Simón-Carrasco et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2018). These findings indicate that CIC plays a critical role in maintaining T cell homeostasis and raise the possibility that dysregulated T cell death may also contribute to the pathological consequences of CIC-deficiency.

Herein, we performed a survival-based, genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen to identify critical regulators of CWID. This screen identified CIC as the top candidate that positively regulates CWID in CTLL-2 cells, an IL-2-dependent cytotoxic T-cell line (Gillis and Smith, 1977; Nelson et al., 1997). Further experiments demonstrate that CIC is a specific regulator of CWID rather than a general mediator of apoptosis. Loss of Cic significantly attenuated IL-2 withdrawal-induced cell death in mouse CD8+ T cells, but not in CD4+ T cells. CIC mediates CWID independently of the canonical BIM pathway, and double-knockout of CIC and BIM abolishes CWID. Mechanistically, Capicua promotes CWID by facilitating Cullin-3/KEAP1–mediated BCL2 degradation and disrupting BAX-BCL2 interactions independent of its transcriptional repressor function. In a Listeria monocytogenes infection model, CIC-deficiency suppresses apoptosis of activated CD8+ T cells, resulting in increased numbers of these cells during the later phase of infection. Our findings suggest that CIC plays a vital role in CWID through a noncanonical mechanism, providing new insights into how the immune system safeguards against uncontrolled inflammation and immunopathology.

Results

Identification of CIC as a regulator of CWID by genome-wide CRISPR screens

To identify potential regulators of CWID, we established a pooled screening system in CTLL-2 cells, an IL-2-dependent cytotoxic T cell clone derived from C57BL/6 mice (Gillis and Smith, 1977; Nelson et al., 1997). Consistent with previous reports (Asselin-Labat et al., 2004; Grassilli et al., 1999), IL-2 deprivation induced dramatic apoptosis in CTLL-2 cells (Fig. 1(A)), suggesting that this is a robust in vitro model for investigating CWID. CTLL-2 cells were transduced with mouse CRISPR knockout pooled library containing 4 unique single guide RNAs (gRNAs) for each of the 19,674 murine genes, followed by IL-2 withdrawal for seven days. The surviving cells were then cultured in the presence of IL-2 for another 7 days to allow recovery, after which genomic DNA was isolated and subjected to next-generation sequencing (Fig. 1(B)). We then performed computational analysis of sgRNAs enriched in the selected cells compared with the control cells using the MAGeCKFlute pipeline (Wang et al., 2019). To investigate the roles of CIC in CWID in primary mouse T cells, we generated Cic-floxed mice (Fig. 2(C)) and crossed them with CD4-Cre transgenic mice to produce T cell-specific CIC-deficient mice (Cicflox/flox CD4-Cre; hereafter referred to as Cic-cKO). We activated naive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from wild-type (WT) or Cic-cKO mice in vitro with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 and expanded them in the presence of IL-2. After 7 days, cells were replated in fresh IL-2-free medium to induce CWID. The results showed that CIC-deficiency significantly decreased IL-2 withdrawal-induced cell death in mouse CD8+ but not CD4+ T cells (Fig. 2(D)), suggesting that CD4+ and CD8+ T cells may engage distinct pathways to induce apoptosis upon cytokine withdrawal. Collectively, these results suggest that Capicua plays an essential role in CWID in CTLL-2 and mouse primary CD8+ T cells.

CIC regulates CWID independently of its transcriptional repressor function

CIC is an evolutionarily conserved transcriptional repressor whose activity is tightly regulated by RTK and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway (Ballesteros-Gonzalez et al., 2025; Lee, 2020). Previous studies have shown that activation of extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase (ERK) mediates cytoplasmic translocation of CIC in mammalian cells thereby suppressing its transcriptional repressor activity (Dissanayake et al., 2011; Park et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2017). It is also well characterized that IL-2 activates ERK to maintain T-cell proliferation and function (Rokade et al., 2024). Thus, it is reasonable to hypothesize that during CWID, CIC is activated due to its release from IL-2-ERK-mediated sequestration. We next asked whether CIC-mediated gene repression functionally contributes to CWID. Actinomycin D (ActD) is a potent transcription inhibitor that blocks DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, thereby preventing mRNA synthesis and leading to gene suppression (Perry and Kelley, 1970; Sobell, 1985). We found that ActD suppressed the expression of CIC-target genes in CIC-deficient cells to a level comparable to that in wild-type cells following IL-2 withdrawal (Fig. 3(B)). However, knockout of CIC still inhibited CWID in the presence of ActD (Fig. 3(B)). These results led us to hypothesize that CIC regulates CWID through a transcription-independent mechanism. CIC acts as a transcriptional repressor through its DNA-binding activity, which requires its conserved high-mobility group (HMG)-box to confer binding to the promoters or enhancers of its target genes (Webb et al., 2025; Štros et al., 2007). By using a CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homology-directed repair (HDR) platform (Paquet et al., 2016; Qiu et al., 2022), we generated a Capicua HMG-box deletion (ΔHMG) CTLL-2 cell line (Fig. 3(C)). Compared with wild-type CTLL-2 cells, CicΔHMG CTLL-2 cells showed increased cell death upon IL-2 withdrawal (Fig. 3(D)), a phenotype opposite to that of CIC-deficiency (Fig. 1(D)). These results suggest that the HMG-box of Capicua is not required for its function in mediating CWID. Taken together, these results suggest that Capicua regulates CWID independently of its transcriptional repressor function.

CIC promotes CWID by enhancing Cullin-3/KEAP1–mediated degradation of Bcl2

CWID is primarily regulated by pro- and anti-apoptotic members of the BCL2 family (Kale et al., 2018; Larsen et al., 2017; Youle and Strasser, 2008), and relies much less on the Fas/Fas ligand (FasL) pathway (Hieronymus et al., 2000; Hughes et al., 2008). Pro-apoptotic BIM is essential for the execution of CWID and termination of acute T cell responses (Hildeman et al., 2002a, Hildeman et al., 2002b; Pellegrini et al., 2003). In the canonical CWID pathway, IL-2 deprivation disrupts the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (PKB/AKT) pathway, resulting in dephosphorylation of FoxO proteins. This dephosphorylation promotes their translocation to the nucleus, where they induce transcription of target genes such as BIM, thereby initiating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway (Asselin-Labat et al., 2004; You et al., 2006). In order to examine whether CIC acts within the canonical BIM-mediated CWID pathway, we generated CIC-, BIM-, and CIC/BIM double-deficient CTLL-2 cells using a CRISPR-Cas9 strategy (Fig. 4(A)). The results showed that knockout of BIM decreased the percentage of cell death following IL-2 withdrawal, although substantial cell death still occurred. Notably, knockout of CIC in BIM-deficient CTLL-2 cells further suppressed CWID and almost completely blocked cell death (Fig. 4(A)), suggesting that Capicua mediates CWID independently of Bim. Previous studies have shown that the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 also plays an important role in CWID (Charo et al., 2005; Hildeman et al., 2002a, Hildeman et al., 2002b). We found that knockout of CIC inhibited BCL2 degradation following IL-2 withdrawal (Fig. 4(B)), whereas the mRNA levels of Bcl2 were comparable between CIC-deficient and control CTLL-2 cells (Fig. 4(C)). In addition to its nuclear localization, Capicua also localizes to the cytoplasm and may accumulate in proximity to mitochondria (Chittaranjan et al., 2014; Lee, 2020). To investigate the mechanisms underlying CIC-mediated Bcl2 degradation, we isolated cytoplasmic extracts from CTLL-2 cells and performed Immunoprecipitation-Mass Spectrometry (IP-MS) to identify CIC-associated proteins in the cytoplasm (Fig. 5(A)). IP-MS analysis revealed that IL-2 withdrawal promoted the association of CIC with KEAP1 (Fig. 5(A)), a protein reported to mediate BCL2 degradation (Peng et al., 2015; Probst et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting analysis further confirmed that CIC was associated with KEAP1 and BCL2 upon IL-2 withdrawal (Fig. 5(B)). Previous studies have shown that KEAP1 serves as an adaptor for the Cullin-3 (CUL3)-based ubiquitin ligase and promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of BCL2 (Peng et al., 2015; Probst et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). We also found that knockdown of KEAP1 increased BCL2 expression in CTLL-2 cells (Fig. 5(C)). However, we observed that knockdown of KEAP1 may accelerate cell death upon IL-2 withdrawal, as evidenced by pronounced morphological changes under microscopy and a marked reduction in β-actin signal intensity (Fig. 5(C)). Consistent with previous reports showing that KEAP1 protects immune cells such as CD8+ T cells and NKT cells from apoptosis (Pyaram et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2024), we found that knockdown of KEAP1 enhanced cell death in CTLL-2 cells both before and after IL-2 withdrawal (Fig. 5(D)). These results suggest that although KEAP1 mediates BCL2 degradation, it plays a complex and potentially bidirectional role in the regulation of CWID. Coimmunoprecipitation and immunoblotting analysis showed that knockout of CIC inhibited the association between BCL2 and KEAP1 (Fig. 5(E)), indicating that Capicua is required for KEAP1 recruitment to BCL2. We also found that overexpression of CIC promoted Cullin-3/KEAP1-mediated degradation of BCL2 (Fig. 5(F)). Moreover, the results also showed that knockout of CIC enhanced the association between BCL2 and BAX (Fig. 5(E)), which is critical for BCL2-mediated inhibition of apoptosis (Czabotar and Garcia-Saez, 2023). CIC is required for death of activated CD8+ T cells in vivo

To investigate the roles of CIC in regulating activated T cell death in vivo, we infected sex- and age-matched wild-type and Cic-cKO mice with ovalbumin (OVA)-expressing Listeria monocytogenes (LmOVA) and tracked the responding T cells over time. We sacrificed the mice on days 7 and 14 after LmOVA infection and assessed the number of activated CD8+ T cells in the spleen (Fig. 6(A)). The results indicated that IFNγ+ CD8+ T cells expanded to a similar extent in both WT and Cic-cKO mice on day 7 (Fig. 6(B)). However, on day 14, the number of IFNγ+ CD8+ T cells was considerably higher in Cic-cKO mice than in WT mice (Fig. 6(B)). We also evaluated the kinetics of OVA-specific CD8+ T cells by staining with PE-conjugated H-2Kb OVA (SIINFEKL) tetramers. Consistent with the pattern observed in IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells, the number of OVA-specific CD8+ T cells rapidly increased to a similar extent in both WT and Cic-cKO mice (Fig. 6(C)). Discussion

CWID serves as a key checkpoint in the immune system to control the number of active T cells and limit inflammation during an immune response (Marrack et al., 2010). However, the blueprint of how this apoptotic process is intricately coordinated remains incompletely understood. To unbiasedly identify modulators of CWID, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen in CTLL-2 cells. This screen revealed multiple potential regulators of CWID, with CIC emerging as one of the strongest candidates. How the remaining candidates contribute to regulation of CWID will be investigated in separate studies. Moreover, we found that in primary mouse T cells, CIC plays a vital role in CWID in CD8+ but not in CD4+ T cells. One possible explanation is that distinct transcriptional programs exist between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, leading to differential dependence on specific cell death pathways. Previous studies have shown that these subsets exhibit distinct cell death dynamics in the context of BCL2 or BIM deficiency (Hildeman et al., 2002a; Hughes et al., 2008), suggesting that they may preferentially rely on different apoptotic regulators. Alternatively, despite comparable CIC expression levels, potential differences in subcellular localization or context-dependent activity cannot be excluded. Although previous studies have reported differential sensitivity to CWID among T-cell subsets (Larsen et al., 2017), the precise mechanisms that account for these subset-specific differences remain to be fully elucidated.

CIC contains an HMG-box domain that binds specific DNA sequences to repress target gene expression. Our results show that IL-2 withdrawal activates the transcriptional repressive function of CIC, possibly due to reduced ERK signaling. Previous studies have shown that CIC is required for proper T cell activation and function (Lee, 2020; Park et al., 2017, 2019, 2020), suggesting that loss of cytokine signaling may activate CIC and thereby efficiently terminate the immune response. However, our results show that CIC regulates CWID independently of its transcriptional activity; instead, it functions in the cytosol to regulate KEAP1/CUL3-mediated BCL2 degradation and BCL2–BAX dynamics. Consistently, CIC remains functional even when transcription is blocked, further supporting a transcription-independent mechanism. How CIC is regulated in this context remains unclear; in particular, whether IL-2 withdrawal triggers specific post-translational modifications (PTMs) that stabilize CIC in the cytosol and promote its interaction with KEAP1 warrants further investigation. In addition, deletion of the HMG-box does not impair CWID; instead, it enhances cell death. One possible explanation is that loss of the HMG domain leads to increased cytoplasmic accumulation of CIC, thereby promoting CWID. The long isoforms of CIC (CIC-L) share the same HMG-box and core regions with the short isoforms (CIC-S), but they differ in their N-terminal sequences (Lee, 2020). Recent studies suggest that the unique N-terminal region of Cic-L may confer distinct functions (Rodríguez-Muñoz et al., 2022). Although our study indicates that Cic-S and Cic-L play redundant roles in CWID, whether they are regulated through different mechanisms remains an important question for future investigation.

Although the PI3K-AKT-BIM pathway is well characterized in CWID (Asselin-Labat et al., 2004; Rosetti et al., 2022), our results indicate that CIC regulates CWID through a BIM-independent mechanism. Single knockout of either BIM or CIC suppresses CWID, whereas combined deletion of CIC and BIM abolishes CWID, suggesting that these pathways act in parallel. Our results suggest that CIC associates with KEAP1 and BCL2, promoting Cullin-3/KEAP1-mediated degradation of BCL2. Moreover, a recent study has shown that KEAP1 forms a complex with the PGAM5 to promote mitochondrial apoptosis (Zhao et al., 2024), whether CIC participates in this process remains to be determined. The function of KEAP1 in CWID appears to be highly complex. As an adaptor for the Cullin-3 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, KEAP1 recognizes multiple substrates, among which NRF2 is the most well-studied and plays a critical role in defense against oxidative stress. It has been shown that ROS accumulation induced by degradation of either BCL2 or NRF2 accelerates apoptosis (Adinolfi et al., 2023). However, our results, together with previous studies (Pyaram et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2024), support that knockdown of KEAP1 promotes T cell death, raising the possibility that additional KEAP1 substrates, such as IKKβ (Kim et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2009), contribute to cell death in a cell type- and context-dependent manner. In addition, our results also show that knockout of CIC enhances the association between BCL2 and BAX. One possible explanation is that, as a relatively large protein localized to mitochondria, CIC consistently associates with BCL2, potentially forming a stable platform to recruit the KEAP1/Cullin-3 complex for BCL2 ubiquitination and degradation. At the same time, due to steric hindrance, CIC may prevent BCL2 from interacting with BAX. Further structural studies will be required to test this hypothesis.

Using a Listeria monocytogenes infection model, we observed that CIC-deficiency reduced apoptosis in activated CD8+ T cells, leading to their accumulation during the late phase of infection. These findings suggest that CIC is required for death of activated CD8+ T cells in vivo. Based on our results, we propose a model in which CIC serves as a checkpoint that controls the number of active CD8+ T cells and limits inflammation. At the late phase of an immune response, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-2 decline, which induces activation of CIC's transcriptional repressor activity. This leads to suppression of target gene expression and consequently reduces T-cell activation and effector function. On the other hand, CIC interacts with KEAP1 and BCL2, promoting Cullin-3/KEAP1–mediated degradation of BCL2 and inhibiting the interaction between BCL2 and Bax, thereby facilitating CWID in activated CD8+ T cells. Through this dual mechanism, Capicua promotes the elimination of unnecessary activated CD8+ T cells, effectively terminating the immune response and maintaining immune homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Reagents and antibodies used in this study were obtained from the indicated sources: Recombinant human IL-2 (SL Pharm, S19991010); Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience, E-CK-A211); Actinomycin D (MCE, HY-17559); TrueCut Cas9 Protein (Invitrogen, A36498); Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (Thermo, 78835); Zombie Violet™ Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, 423113); Pacific Blue anti-mouse CD3 antibody (BioLegend, 100214); FITC anti-mouse CD8a antibody (BioLegend, 100706); APC anti-mouse CD8a antibody (BD Pharmingen, 553035); PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD3 antibody (BioLegend, 100220); APC anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody (Invivogen, 17-7311-82); PE-Labeled Mouse H-2Kb&B2M&OVA (SIINFEKL) Tetramer Protein (Acrobiosystems, H2A-MP2H7); Capicua antibody (Novusbio, NB110-59906); anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F3165); β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A2228); Bim antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2933T); Bcl2 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 3498T); Bax antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2772T); Bcl2 antibody (Proteintech, 68103-1-Ig); BAX antibody (Proteintech, 60267-1-Ig); KEAP1 antibody (Proteintech, 10503-2-AP); Anti-mouse Ig HRP (Rockland Immunochemicals, 18-8817-33); Anti-rabbit IgG HRP (Rockland Immunochemicals, 18-8816-33); HRP-conjugated Mouse anti-Rabbit IgG Light Chain (ABclonal, AS061); Prestained Protein Ladder 10-180 kDa (Thermo, 26616); Prestained Protein Ladder 25-400 kDa (Sevenbio, SW179).

Cells

CTLL-2 cells were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College (IBMS, CAMS/PUMC) and cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 × penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen), 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM Sodium pyruvate (Gibco), 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco), and 100 U/mL IL-2. HEK293T cwlls were originally provided by Dr. Gary Johnson (National Jewish Health, Denver, CO) and maintained in DMEM (Gibco) with 10% FBS and 1 × penicillin–streptomycin. Naive mouse CD4+ T cells or naive mouse CD8+ T cells were isolated using EasySep™ Magnetic Cell Separation (STEMCELL) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Mice

Cic-floxed mice on C57BL/6 background were generated by Animal Center of Medical Research Institute of Wuhan University, utilizing the CRISPR/Cas9 method. CicFlox mice were crossed with Cd4-Cre mice to generate conditional Cic-cKO mice. Mice were bred in a specific pathogen-free facility at the Medical Research Institute of Wuhan University. All mouse experiments were conducted with the approval of the Animal Care Committee at the Medical Research Institute of Wuhan University (MRI2025-LACA24).

Induction of CWID

CTLL-2 cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium without IL-2. After culture for the indicated periods, apoptosis was assessed by Annexin V/PI staining. For primary mouse T cells, naive CD4+ or CD8+ T cells were isolated from mice by magnetic cell separation. Purified cells were activated with plate-bound anti-CD3 (5 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 (2 μg/mL) antibodies and expanded in the presence of 200 U/mL IL-2 for 7 days. T cells were then washed with PBS and cultured in complete RPMI medium without IL-2. Apoptosis was assessed at the indicated time points by Annexin V/PI staining.

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen

CTLL-2 cells were transduced with the mouse CRISPR knockout pooled library (Doench et al., 2016) (Addgene #73632) and subjected to selection with puromycin (4 μg/mL) for 7 days. 5 × 107 CTLL-2 cells were washed with PBS and cultured in complete RPMI either with IL-2 (control) or without IL-2 (to induce CWID) for 7 days. The surviving cells were then cultured with IL-2 to allow expansion for an additional 7 days, after which they were harvested for genomic DNA extraction (Thermo, K0721). The sgRNA inserts were PCR-amplified, purified, and subjected to next-generation sequencing analysis (Novogene Co., Ltd.). Gene enrichment was analyzed using the MAGeCKFlute pipeline (Wang et al., 2019).

Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting analysis

Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting analyses were performed as previously described (Liu and Li, 2026), with brief details provided here. The cells were harvested and lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 10 μg/mL aprotinin, and 10 μg/mL leupeptin. Immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating lysates with the indicated antibodies or control IgG at 4 °C overnight. Immune complexes were subsequently captured with Protein G Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare). The precipitates were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by immunoblotting analysis using the specified antibodies.

qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara) and subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA with Moloney murine leukemia virus (MMLV) reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). Quantitative PCR was then performed to assess the transcript levels of the indicated genes. The results are shown as relative mRNA abundance normalized to Mouse Actb indicated in the figure legends. qPCR was performed using the following primers:

Actb: 5′-CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGG-3′ (forward) and 5′-TGCTGGAAGGTGGACAGTGAGG-3′ (reverse);

Etv4: 5′-CACAGACTTCGCCTACGACTCA-3′ (forward) and 5′-GCAGACATCATCTGGGAATGGTC-3′ (reverse);

Etv5: 5′-GCAGGAATACCATGACCCACTG-3′ (forward) and 5′- AGGATGACTGGCAGTTAGGCAC-3′ (reverse);

Dusp4: 5′-TACTCGGCGGTCATCGTCTACG-3′ (forward) and 5′- CGGAGGAAAACCTCTCATAGCC-3′ (reverse).

CRISPR–Cas9-mediated genome editing

CRISPR–Cas9-mediated genome editing was performed as previously described (Shi et al., 2025). For CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, double-stranded oligonucleotides encoding the target sequences were cloned into the lenti-CRISPR-V2 vector or the pLX-sgRNA vector (for double knockout). The constructed vector and packaging plasmids were co-transfected into HEK293T cells. Lentivirus-containing supernatants were harvested 2 days after transfection and used to infect CTLL-2 cells, followed by antibiotic selection with puromycin alone or together with blasticidin for at least 6 days. The following gRNA sequences were used:

gNC: 5′-GTAGTCGGTACGTGACTCGT-3’;

gCic(gCic-S/L) #1: 5′-GAAGCAGAAATACCACGACC-3’;

gCic(gCic-S/L) #2: 5′-AGAACGGCTACACGCAGTTG-3’;

gCic-S #1: 5′-GCGCCAGACGCGGGTATCAG-3’;

gCic-S #2: 5′-CCTGATACCCGCGTCTGGCG-3’;

gCic-L #1: 5′-TTCTATGAAGGAGTACCCGG-3’;

gCic-L #2: 5′-CCAGTGAAGGCTATGACCTG-3’;

gBim #1: 5′-GAACCGCAAGGTAATCCCGA-3’;

gBim #2: 5′-TCGCCGTCGGGATTACCTTG-3’;

For CRISPR-Cas9-mediated HDR, CTLL-2 cells were electroporated with Cas9-RNP complex (6 μg TrueCut Cas9, 3 μg EasyEdit sgRNA#1 and 3 μg EasyEdit sgRNA#2) and 2 μg of single-stranded donor oligonucleotides (ssODN) as template. At 48 h after transfection, single cells were isolated by flow cytometric sorting and seeded individually into 96-well plates. Genomic DNA was isolated, and PCR amplicons encompassing the targeted loci were sequenced to confirm the desired mutations.

Knockdown of target genes by siRNA

CTLL-2 cells (1 × 106) were subjected to electroporation with the indicated siRNAs. Immediately after electroporation, pre-warmed complete medium was added, and the cells were gently transferred out of the cuvettes. The siRNA sequences used in this study were as follows:

siNC: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’;

siKeap1 #1: 5′-GCAUCGACUGGGUCAAAUACG-3’.

Flow cytometry analysis

To assess surface marker expression, single-cell suspensions were incubated with antibodies specific for cell surface molecules at 4 °C for 30 min. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL PMA (MCE), 1 μM ionomycin (MCE), and 1 × monensin (Golgistop, BD Biosciences) for 4-6 h. After surface marker staining, intracellular staining was performed using the Cytofix/Cytoperm buffer set (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Mass spectrometry

CTLL-2 cells were left untreated or deprived of IL-2 for 12 h, followed by nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation. Cytoplasmic extracts were subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-CIC antibody, and the associated proteins were identified by mass spectrometry. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed by SpecAlly Life Technology Co. (Wuhan, China).

Listeria monocytogenes infection model

LmOVA (kindly provided by Dr. Cliff Yang) was cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium and harvested during the exponential growth phase. Age- and sex-matched WT and Cic-cKO mice were intravenously administered with 1 × 105 colony-forming units (CFU) of LmOVA in 100 μL PBS and were euthanized at the indicated day(s) post-infection. Spleens were mechanically dissociated in ice-cold PBS and filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to generate single-cell suspensions for subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using an unpaired Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups. For analyses involving more than two groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was applied as appropriate. GraphPad Prism 10 was used to analyze all data. Quantitative data in histograms are presented as means ± SEM. Asterisks indicate the level of significance, with statistical significance defined as ∗P < 0.05 and ∗∗P < 0.01.

Data visualization

The graphic abstract was created with BioGDP.com (Jiang et al., 2025), and the model in Fig. 1(B) was generated using Adobe Illustrator.
