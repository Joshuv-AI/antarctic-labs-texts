Evaluating Rotational Stability of the Visian V4b TICL in Patients with High Myopic Astigmatism
Authors: Sanjana Molleti, Hanna Pawlowski, Mina M Sitto, Kayvon A Moin, Phillip C Hoopes, Majid Moshirfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo evaluate the rotational stability of the Visian V4b toric implantable collamer lenses (TICLs) over a 12-month follow-up period and to assess refractive outcomes and potential factors associated with lens rotation at one U.S. institution.MethodsThis retrospective study included 48 eyes from 26 patients who underwent Visian TICL implantation between 2018 and 2021 at a single U.S. refractive surgical center. Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years old with myopia ranging from − 3.0 to − 20.0 D and astigmatism ranging from − 1.0 to − 4.0 D. Postoperative assessments were conducted at 1 week, 1 month, and 3, 6, and 12 months. Rotational stability was measured using standardized slit lamp photography with image overlay analysis. Visual acuity, refractive outcomes, and vault measurements were recorded at each visit. Vector analysis was performed, and potential factors associated with TICL rotation were analyzed using correlation analyses.ResultsMean (± standard deviation) implanted ICL sphere was − 11.4 ± 1.87 D, and mean implanted ICL cylinder was 2.00 ± 0.81 D. Of the implanted ICL sizes, the majority of eyes received a 13.2-mm ICL (26 eyes, 54.2%) and a 12.6-mm ICL (20 eyes, 41.7%). At 12 months, uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) was 20/20 or better in 83% of eyes, with a safety index of 1.21 and an efficacy index of 1.04. The mean absolute rotation at 12 months was 6.26 ± 6.57°, with no significant change between follow-up visits (all P > 0.05). A significant correlation was found between absolute rotation and ICL sphere, however, no significant correlations were observed between absolute rotation and preoperative sphere, white-to-white distance, anterior chamber angle, postoperative vault, or ICL size. At 1 week postoperatively, clockwise rotation occurred more frequently than counterclockwise rotation (P = 0.004), with no significant difference in rotation direction thereafter.ConclusionVisian V4b TICLs demonstrate excellent visual and refractive outcomes with acceptable rotational stability over 12 months. Overall, TICLs remain a predictable option for the correction of moderate-to-high myopic astigmatism, with a low risk of clinically significant rotation.Keywords: Astigmatism, Axis rotation, ICL, Myopia, Phakic intraocular lenses, Rotational stability, Toric, Undercorrection, Vector analysis, Visian

Introduction

The prevalence of myopia is projected to affect nearly half of the global population by 2050, representing a major public health concern [1]. Astigmatism frequently coexists with myopia and contributes to further degradation of visual acuity, particularly in cases of higher refractive error [2]. Although corrective options such as spectacles, contact lenses, and corneal refractive surgery are widely used, their effectiveness is often limited in patients with high myopia and significant astigmatism.

Phakic intraocular lenses (pIOLs) have emerged as an effective and reversible alternative for the correction of moderate to high myopia, offering excellent optical quality while preserving the natural crystalline lens and accommodation. The Visian toric implantable collamer lens (TICL; STAAR Surgical Co., Monrovia, CA, USA) was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2018 for the correction of myopic astigmatism, with indications spanning − 3.0 to − 20.0 diopters (D) of spherical equivalent and 1.0 to 4.0 D of cylindrical error [3]. TICLs provide simultaneous correction of both spherical and astigmatic refractive components, making them particularly advantageous in patients with high ametropia who are not ideal candidates for corneal refractive surgery.

A critical determinant of TICL performance is postoperative rotational stability. As toric lenses rely on precise alignment with the intended astigmatic axis, even small degrees of rotation can significantly reduce the effective cylindrical correction. It has been estimated that a 10° misalignment may result in approximately one-third loss of astigmatic correction, with progressively greater loss at higher degrees of rotation [4].

Previous studies have generally demonstrated favorable rotational stability of TICLs, with most lenses maintaining alignment within clinically acceptable thresholds [5–8]. However, the majority of these studies have been conducted in non-U.S. populations, often with variability in measurement methods and follow-up duration. As a result, there remains a relative lack of real-world data evaluating postoperative rotational behavior and vector outcomes of TICLs in a U.S. clinical setting.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the postoperative rotational stability of Visian V4b TICLs in a single U.S. surgical practice over a 12-month follow-up period. We assessed visual and refractive outcomes and performed vector-based analysis of astigmatic correction to better characterize alignment accuracy and refractive performance following implantation. Finally, we performed a correlation analysis to evaluate potential risk factors associated with rotational instability.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study included patients who had received Visian Toric ICLs (V4b; STAAR Surgical) implanted between 2018 and 2021 at Hoopes Vision in Draper, Utah. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York Institutional Review Board (#A20-12-547-823). All patients provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.

Patients aged ≥ 18 years with myopia between − 3.0 D and − 20.0 D and cylindrical refraction between − 1.0 D and − 4.0 D were included in the study. Other inclusion criteria were a minimum endothelial cell density corrected for age and aqueous depth (AQD) > 2.85 mm. Patients who had received TICL surgery in one or both eyes were included. Unilateral implantation was due to patients receiving other types of corneal refractive surgery, including laser assisted in situ keratomileusis or photorefractive keratectomy, in the fellow eye. Patients with a history of ocular diseases other than myopic astigmatism or those who underwent/received enhancements or bioptics were excluded.

Study Measurements

All patients underwent a complete preoperative ophthalmic examination, which included uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), manifest refraction spherical equivalent (MRSE), and slit lamp and dilated fundus examination. Biometric measurements, including axial length, white-to-white (WTW), AQD, and crystalline lens thickness, were obtained with the LENSTAR LS 900 biometer (Haag-Streit, Koeniz, Switzerland). Additional biometric measurements, such as sulcus-to-sulcus, crystalline lens rise, and anterior chamber angles, were obtained using the Presurgical VuMax ultrasound biomicroscopy (Sonomed Escalon, New Hyde Park, NY, USA). Keratometry and pachymetry measurements were obtained using the Pentacam HR device (Oculus, Arlington, WA, USA).

Slit-lamp photographs of the patients' fully dilated eye(s) were taken with Haag Streit BM 900 slit lamp (Haag Streit) during postoperative visits at 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, and 3, 6, and 12 months. In addition, measurements taken at each postoperative visit included UDVA, CDVA, intraocular pressure (IOP), MRSE, and vault size using the RTVue-XR Avanti optical coherence tomography system (Optovue, Inc., Fremont, CA, USA). A single grader who was not involved in the clinical care of the patient obtained rotation measurements for all included eyes. Rotation was measured relative to postoperative day 1 as immediate postoperative imaging was unreliable due to pupil constriction. TICL rotation was determined using Adobe Illustrator (version 26.0.3; Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) to overlay images of each dilated eye, align ocular landmarks, and mark the axes. On the slit-lamp photograph, a green line was drawn between the TICL markers and compared with the axis on postoperative day 1, denoted by a blue line (Fig. 1). This measurement was defined as the mean absolute rotation.

Slit-lamp photograph of a mydriatic eye with rotational measurements. To measure the rotation, the toric implantable collamer lense (TICL) markers were connected with a green line and compared to the axis of postoperative day 1, marked by the blue line

Each patient received one of four available ICL implant sizes (12.1, 12.6, 13.2, or 13.7 mm). Recommendations from the Parkhurst, Dougherty/Rivera STS, and FDA nomograms were compared to select the appropriate lens diameter using WTW and AQD measurements, and the STAAR Surgical Online Calculating and Ordering System (STAAR Surgical, Lake Forest, CA, USA was used for the ICL calculations.

Surgical Techniques

All surgeries were conducted by a single surgeon (MM). Approximately 1 week before surgery, each patient underwent Yttrium–Aluminum Garnet peripheral iridotomies placed at the 11 and 1 o’clock positions. Patients’ eyes were dilated with phenylephrine hydrochloride 2.5% and tropicamide 1.0% in the preoperative area on the day of surgery. With the eye(s) marked at the 0° and 180° positions, the surgeon planned the desired axis of the TICL. Two 1-mm paracenteses were performed, and dispersive viscoelastic was injected into the anterior chamber. With the pupil dilated, the TICL was inserted through a temporal 3.2-mm corneal incision and positioned in the sulcus at the predetermined alignment. The wound was then irrigated with a sterile balanced salt solution to remove any remaining viscoelastic material. The correct position of the toric lens was confirmed with visual inspection. Miochol-E (acetylcholine chloride; Bausch & Lomb, Bridgewater, NJ, USA) was injected intracamerally. Both peripheral iridotomies were confirmed to be patent, and the procedure was completed. Each patient received either ofloxacin 0.3% or moxifloxacin 0.5% 4 times a day and prednisolone acetate 1% 4 times a day for 1 week, followed by a weekly taper.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 17.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used to account for inter-eye correlation. GEE models were used to evaluate the association between time (preoperative, 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months, with preoperative as the reference) and outcomes, including rotational magnitude and refractive parameters (MRSE, sphere, and cylinder). An identity link function with a Gaussian distribution and an exchangeable working correlation structure was applied. Pairwise post-hoc contrasts with Bonferroni adjustment were performed to compare between time points. A separate GEE model with a binomial distribution and a logit link was used to assess rotational direction (clockwise or counterclockwise).

GEE correlation analysis was performed to assess potential factors associated with TICL rotation, including anterior chamber angle, spherical power of the TICL, preoperative sphere, and postoperative TICL vaulting. These factors were considered to be independent variables, with absolute rotational value used as the dependent variable. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Nine standard graphs of visual and refractive outcomes were created [9]. Vector analysis was performed using AstigMATIC software developed by Gauvin et al. [10]. Surgically induced astigmatism (SIA), representing the actual astigmatic change caused by surgery, target-induced astigmatism (TIA), representing the intended vector change in cylinder, difference vector, representing the difference in achieved and target astigmatism, and correction index (CI), or the ratio of SIA to TIA, were evaluated using this software. A CI > 1 reflects an overcorrection, and a CI < 1 represents an undercorrection.

Results

Patient Demographics and Implant Characteristics

This study consisted of 48 eyes (23 right, 25 left) from 26 patients (11 males, 15 females) (Table 1). Mean (± standard deviation [SD]) age at the time of surgery was 36.3 ± 4.6 (range 27–44) years. Unilateral and bilateral implantation occurred in four patients (15.4%) and 22 patients (84.6%), respectively. All patients (all 48 eyes) included in the study completed 12 months of follow-up. Preoperative mean sphere was − 7.96 ± 2.04 (range − 13.50 to − 3.75) D, and mean cylinder was − 2.10 ± 0.81 (range − 4.00 to − 1.00) D. All 48 eyes had a preoperative UDVA worse than 20/250, and 38 eyes had a preoperative CDVA of 20/20 or better.

Visual and Refractive Outcomes

At 12 months, 83% of eyes had a UDVA of 20/20 or better, and 67% had a UDVA of 20/16 or better (Fig. 2a). Thirty-nine eyes (81%) achieved the same or better postoperative UDVA than preoperative CDVA (Fig. 2b). The mean (± SD) efficacy index at 3 and 12 months was 1.02 ± 0.22 and 1.04 ± 0.24, respectively. In terms of safety, 96% of eyes showed no change or gained Snellen lines of CDVA (Fig. 2c). Two eyes (4%) lost 1 Snellen line of CDVA, although no eyes lost more than 2 lines. The mean safety index at 3 and 12 months postoperatively was 1.24 ± 0.15 and 1.21 ± 0.19, respectively.

Postoperatively, the mean sphere showed mild hyperopia by 12 months relative to baseline (P < 0.001) (Table 3). Mean (± SD) MRSE decreased from − 9.11 ± 1.90 D at baseline to − 0.08 ± 0.35 D at 12 months, and UDVA (LogMAR [ logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution]) improved from 1.50 ± 0.15 preoperatively to − 0.04 ± 0.11 at 12 months. There was a strong correlation between attempted and achieved MRSE (R2 = 0.97) (Fig. 2d). At 12 months, 85% of eyes were within ± 0.50 D of target refraction and all eyes were within ± 1.00 D (Fig. 2e). MRSE remained stable across all postoperative visits, with 13% of eyes exhibiting a MRSE change > 0.50 D between postoperative months 3 and 12 (Fig. 2f).

Mean postoperative residual cylinder was − 0.61 ± 0.40 D at 12 months (Table 3), with 60% of eyes within ± 0.50 D of the target refraction and 94% of eyes within ± 1.00 D (Fig. 2g). Correlation between TIA and SIA was R2 = 0.87 (Fig. 2h), indicating an undercorrection of 3%. The CI also showed a tendency toward undercorrection, with an arithmetic mean of 0.78 (Fig. 3). An angle of error within 5° of the intended axis was observed in 56% of eyes, and 93.8% and 97.9% of eyes were within 10° and 15° of the intended axis, respectively (Fig. 2i).

Astigmatic vector analysis at 12 months following surgery

Rotational Analysis

The mean (± SD) absolute rotations were 5.94 ± 6.06° at postoperative week 1 and 6.26 ± 6.57° at postoperative month 12 month, with no statistically significant change across time points (all P > 0.05) (Table 4). Approximately 60% of eyes demonstrated < 5° of rotation and 85.4% showed < 10° of rotation. One eye exhibited a mean absolute rotation of 39.82°, while four eyes had 0° of rotation at the postoperative month 12. There was no significant correlation between mean absolute rotation and vault over 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year (all P > 0.05). Mean absolute rotation was significantly correlated with ICL sphere (β = − 0.927, 95% CI: − 1.664 to − 0.191; P = 0.014). However, it was not correlated with ICL size (β = − 2.292, 95% CI: − 8.497 to 3.913; P = 0.469), lens rise (β = − 2.429, 95% CI: − 16.201 to 11.343; P = 0.730), anterior chamber angle (β = − 0.064, 95% CI: − 0.296 to 0.168; P = 0.587), WTW (β = − 0.542, 95% CI: − 5.658 to 4.574; P = 0.835), and preoperative spherical power (β = − 0.723, 95% CI: − 1.509 to 0.064; P = 0.072).

At 1 week postoperatively, clockwise rotation occurred more frequently than counterclockwise rotation (P = 0.004). There was no significant difference in rotation direction at the 1-month (P = 0.056), 3-month (P = 0.056), 6-month (P = 0.550), and 12-month (P = 0.900) time points. At 12 months, rotation direction was not significantly correlated with eye laterality (oculus dexter [OD] vs. oculus sinister [OS]) (P = 0.209).

Discussion

In this study, TICLs exhibited an acceptable degree of rotational stability and favorable visual outcomes over a 12-month follow-up period. The mean rotation of TICLs at the 12-month postoperative time point in the present study (6.26 ± 6.57°) is slightly higher than those reported in previous studies evaluating the V4b lens, although these other studies had shorter follow-up durations (all < 9 months) [6, 8, 11–13]. While this difference may suggest that rotational changes accumulate over time, our longitudinal analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant increase in rotation between postoperative time points. The greatest degree of rotation appears to occur during the first postoperative week; beyond that time point, changes in rotation may occur but are minimal and unlikely to be clinically significant. The US FDA Pre-Market approval of Visian V4b TICL found that 94.3% of eyes exhibited < 5° of rotation compared our study in which 58.3% of eyes showed < 5° of rotation at 12 months [3]. This difference may be due to differences in methodology as the FDA trial utilized estimated clock hour orientation to evaluate the parameters, while we utilized a combination of slit-lamp photographs and software markings. Additionally, our cohort included five patients with AQD < 3.0 mm while the FDA population excluded these patients, potentially contributing to increased degrees of rotation. Variation in surgical technique may also partially explain these findings, as all TICL implantations at our center were performed by one surgeon, potentially contributing to consistent differences in rotational outcomes. In general, however, we noted a majority of patients within 5° and 10° of rotation, highlighting the overall stability of the TICL.

We evaluated the Visian V4b TICL, but new technologies have been approved by the FDA in recent years. The V4c (EVO) ICL (STAAR Sugical Co.) represents a key design advancement which features a central port that eliminates the need for preoperative peripheral iridotomy and may reduce the risk of complications, such as postoperative angle closure glaucoma and cataract formation [14, 15]. Despite these design differences, our V4b cohort demonstrated comparable rotational outcomes to those reported in studies evaluating the V4c ICL, as shown in Table 5 [5, 6, 16–23]. For studies evaluating orientation techniques, only horizontal implantation lenses were included, as our study did not include vertical or oblique implantations. These findings suggest that both lens models demonstrate similarly favorable rotational stability, supporting the consistent performance of Visian TICLs.

Our study found that TICLs were more likely to rotate clockwise than counterclockwise during the early postoperative period, although this directional preference was not observed beyond 1 week postoperatively (Fig. 4). To our knowledge, this is the first TICL study to identify a significant association with rotational directionality. As all procedures were performed by a single surgeon, subtle and consistent surgical factors may have contributed to this pattern. Previous studies have reported a mixed consensus regarding the direction of rotation in TICLs [11, 19, 23]. Further investigation in larger, multi-surgeon cohorts is warranted to determine whether this represents a reproducible trend.

Several factors have been proposed to influence TICL rotation, including postoperative vault size, implant size, spherical power, foot plant position, implantation axis, pupillary dilation, and trauma [11, 13, 17, 20, 24–26]. In our study, no statistically significant correlations were found between vault and mean absolute rotation at any time points. One prior study by Sheng et al. observed a significant negative correlation, likely due to one patient with low vault measurements (< 100 µm), resulting in a clinically significant postoperative TICL rotation [13]. It is possible that the narrow range of vault measurements in our cohort may have limited the ability to detect such associations.

Notably, our study found that ICL sphere was significantly correlated with rotational stability, a finding similarly noted by Park et al. [11]. As the spherical power of the ICL increases, the thickness of the lens also increases, leading to a bulkier lens. The smaller absolute rotation seen with greater ICL sphere may therefore reflect the increased rotational stability seen with a heavier lens. In contrast, no correlation was observed in our cohort between lens size and rotational stability. The authors of some previous studies have observed that undersized implants are associated with increased rotational propensity due to insufficient fixation within the ciliary sulcus [13, 17]. The lack of correlation in our cohort likely reflects the limited variability in lens sizes (primarily 12.6 and 13.2 mm) and the small number of patients with low vault or undersized lenses, limiting our ability to detect this association. Although oversizing may improve stability, it is also associated with increased risk of complications, including elevated IOP and cataract formation [27, 28]. Optimization of lens sizing may enhance aqueous humor flow, stabilize anterior chamber angle, and improve refraction [29]. Discrepancies between WTW and sulcus-to-sulcus, as well as other corneal anatomical variations, may contribute to rotational instability that is not completely accounted for in current nomograms [30]. The incorporation of additional anatomical parameters or individualized approaches to lens sizing may improve the predictability of TICL fit and postoperative stability.

Clinically significant TICL rotation is generally considered to occur beyond 10°, with rotations exceeding 30° resulting in near-complete loss of astigmatic correction [4]. In our cohort, only one eye demonstrated a rotation exceeding 30° (39.82°), with UDVA of 20/32 and CDVA of 20/20. The patient also declined further intervention, suggesting that even large rotational instability may result in variable visual outcomes depending on patient tolerance and residual refractive error. Secondary intervention may be indicated if the rotation of the TICL exceeds 10°, the UDVA of TICL implantation is lower than the CDVA, and patients report visually significant residual astigmatism and other substantial visual symptoms [31]. Overall, enhancement following TICL should be reserved for symptomatic cases, primarily in patients with significant visual disturbances.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design. Although each TICL was carefully positioned intraoperatively, same day assessment of rotational alignment was not performed. Therefore, measurements on postoperative day 1 were used as the baseline, and any rotation occurring between implantation and the first postoperative evaluation was not captured. Immediate postoperative imaging was attempted, but image quality was insufficient for reliable rotational analysis using our image overlay method due to pupil constriction at the end of surgery, which often obscured the axis markings. Consequently, our study may not account for total rotation from the intended intraoperative axis. Nevertheless, the methodology has several strengths, including the 12-month follow-up period, which exceeds that of prior studies evaluating the V4b lens. Additionally, all postoperative assessments included pharmacologic dilation and slit lamp images at each postoperative visit, including postoperative day 1. This approach provides a more controlled and consistent methodology than implemented in prior V4b studies, which often relied on digital image analysis across fewer time points. Despite the small sample size (48 eyes), a post hoc power analysis confirmed an overall statistical power of 0.952. Inclusion of both eyes, rather than random selection of a single eye, may have reduced variability, but this approach was necessary to maintain the study’s sample size. GEE analysis was used to reduce bias arising from the use of both eyes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Visian V4b TICLs represent an effective option for correcting myopic astigmatism, with favorable visual outcomes and acceptable rotational stability over a 12-month period. While accurate preoperative measurements of parameters such as WTW distance and AQD remain critical for optimal lens sizing, postoperative rotation was minimal in most of the eyes included in the present study. Importantly, even in the presence of moderate rotation of the intended axis, visual acuity was largely preserved. These findings support a conservative approach to secondary intervention, with close monitoring during the early postoperative period to assess rotational stability. Further studies are needed to better define thresholds for intervention and to evaluate outcomes beyond 1 year.
