Introduction

The presented case of Mr. J, a 35‐year‐old male with combined penile fracture, urethral injury, and urethrorrhagia, underscores the complexity and severity of urological trauma resulting from blunt trauma to the genital area. This case report delves into the clinical presentation, diagnostic procedures, and surgical management of this rare and challenging condition. The aim of this report is to provide valuable insights into the evaluation and treatment of such complex urological injuries.

Case presentation

Mr. J, a 35‐year‐old male with no significant prior medical history or urological conditions, presented to the emergency department following blunt trauma to the genital area, which occurred due to a misstep during sexual intercourse. He complained of excruciating penile pain and urethrorrhagia.

Upon examination, Mr. J exhibited significant penile swelling, ecchymosis, and tenderness. A noticeable hematoma on the dorsal aspect of the penis was observed, and he displayed clear signs of urethrorrhagia, without acute urinary retention. To manage the urethrorrhagia, urethral catheterization was performed using a prudent Foley catheter CH 18.

In addition to the clinical presentation, an MRI of the penis was conducted to comprehensively assess the extent of the injuries and confirm the diagnosis. The MRI revealed a solution of continuity of the tunica albuginea of the left corpora cavernosa at their base (Fig. 1). This finding was associated with an adjacent hematoma of the penis and contiguous hematocele, further illustrating the extent of the penile injury.

Penile MRI showing the left CC displaying a discontinuity at the level of Buck's fascia (yellow arrow) with a hematoma (red asterisk). In (a) and (b), sagittal section, and in (c), transverse section.

Once the diagnosis of a combined penile fracture, urethral injury, and urethrorrhagia was confirmed, an emergent surgical repair was initiated to address these complex urological injuries. The surgical procedure involved incision and exposure with a lateral penile incision over the palpable site of the fracture. Careful dissection of the tunica revealed the fracture line, presenting as a discontinuity in the Buck's fascia (or deep fascia of the penis) and the albuginea around the corpora cavernosa. The exploration also exposed an extension of the Buck's fascia opening at the spongy body, which was injured, along with the urethral mucosa, exposing the Foley catheter (Fig. 2). After evacuation of clots, which were a potential source of infection and fibrosis, the repair was performed in two stages using absorbable 3.0 Vicryl suture. The initial phase entailed dealing with the urethra, encompassing both the closure and realignment of the urethra, along with a comprehensive evaluation and repair of the urethra. A rigid 20‐French silicone catheter was placed to facilitate urinary drainage and healing. The second stage addressed the corpora cavernosa, involving meticulous alignment and suturing of the fractured tunica albuginea (Fig. 3).

Intraoperative view showing the discontinuity of the spongy body with the two edges of the urethral mucosa (yellow arrow) separated by the urinary catheter (black asterisk).

Intraoperative view showing the suture of the fracture line in the cavernous bodies with resorbable braided Vicryl sutures in separate stitches (yellow arrow).

The postoperative course was satisfactory, with a reduction in penile swelling and hematoma. The urethral catheter was removed after 3 weeks, and normal voiding function was regained.

The patient's long‐term outcomes were closely monitored up to the 6‐month follow‐up. At this interval, erectile function had significantly improved, with no residual ED reported. Postoperative evaluations included urethrocystoscopy, which revealed a well‐healed urethra with no evidence of strictures or other anomalies. Additionally, uroflowmetry demonstrated satisfactory urinary flow. These comprehensive assessments underscore the effectiveness of the surgical intervention and the patient's positive recovery trajectory.

Discussion

Penile fractures are relatively rare, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 175 000 individuals. In most cases, the injury affects only one CC, accounting for approximately 86.3% of fractures. Bilateral corpus cavernosal tears are less common, occurring in about 13.7% of cases. Urethral injury, which complicates treatment, is present in 6.1% of cases. Complete urethral disruption is extremely uncommon, with only 22 cases documented before the pivotal study by Batura et al. 1

It typically occurs between ages 20 and 40. The causes vary by region. In the Western countries, most cases result from sexual intercourse, typically when an erect penis strikes the partner's pubic bone. In contrast, in Mediterranean and Middle Eastern countries, fractures often stem from manual bending of the erect penis, a practice called “Taqaandan,” aimed at hiding an erection due to cultural norms and limited sexual education. 2

Clinically, there is sudden intense penile pain, sometimes with a preceding cracking sound and immediate loss of erection. Physical examination typically reveals penis deviation towards the opposite side of the injury. Hematomas, usually limited to the penis, may occasionally extend to the scrotum and perineum if fascia is involved. Urethrorrhagia indicates urethral rupture. Post‐injury, the hematoma extends, leading to urinary disturbances, including painful, difficult, or impossible urination. Urethral obstruction from hematoma can lead to acute urinary retention. 3

In typical cases, no additional tests are necessary to diagnose corpora cavernosa fractures.

A retrograde urethrogram may be requested if urethral injury is suspected. 4

High‐resolution ultrasonography can play a role as an initial imaging modality due to its non‐invasive nature, availability, and ability to provide detailed anatomical visualization. It utilizes high‐frequency sound waves (typically ranging from 7.5 to 15 MHz) to generate images of the penile structures. This modality is particularly effective at identifying disruptions in the tunica albuginea, a dense fibrous layer surrounding the corpora cavernosa, which is commonly ruptured during a penile fracture. 5 The hallmark of a penile fracture on ultrasound is a hypoechoic or anechoic defect within the tunica albuginea, indicating a tear. Additionally, ultrasonography can detect the presence and extent of hematomas, which appear as heterogeneous, hypoechoic regions adjacent to the tear. These hematomas can vary in size and may be superficial or deep, depending on the severity of the vascular injury. 3

Ultrasonography also provides valuable information regarding the involvement of the corpora cavernosa and can differentiate between intratunic and extratunic hematomas. The use of color Doppler ultrasound enhances diagnostic capability by evaluating blood flow, thereby aiding in the assessment of vascular injuries or disruptions within the penile vasculature. This is particularly useful for identifying potential complications such as arteriovenous fistulas or pseudoaneurysms, which may occur following trauma. 4

MRI is rarely necessary and not routinely used as a first‐line imaging tool due to its higher cost, limited availability, and longer acquisition times. MRI is particularly useful in complex cases where ultrasonographic findings are inconclusive or when a more comprehensive evaluation of the penile anatomy is required. 5 It excels in delineating the extent of injuries to the tunica albuginea, corpora cavernosa, corpus spongiosum, and associated structures such as the urethra. The use of T1‐ and T2‐weighted sequences allows for the differentiation of acute and chronic hematomas, fibrosis, and other soft tissue injuries. MRI is also beneficial in assessing the integrity of the urethra and detecting urethral disruptions, which may not be as clearly visible on ultrasound. Gadolinium‐enhanced sequences can further assist in evaluating vascular injuries and inflammation. 5

Left untreated, corpora cavernosa fractures cause permanent penile curvature and ED. When combined with a urethral injury, the risks increase, including acute urinary retention, urohematoma infection, and urethrocavernous fistulas. 6

Treatment for complete urethral injury typically involves urgent surgical repair of the urethra and corpora cavernosa. 7 The preferred surgical approach is a distal circumferential incision in the balanopreputial groove, providing good exposure. For more proximal injuries, a midline ventral incision may be preferred. 5 After clot removal and wound cleansing, repair begins with urethra release and suturing. Urethral drainage is oftenly via a catheter, with suprapubic drainage for urinary diversion. 6

Post‐op care includes antibiotics and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs. To prevent erections, tranquilizers or estrogen therapy may be recommended. A follow‐up urethrocystography at 3 months helps monitor progress. 7

Long‐term outcomes following penile fracture repair, particularly when complicated by urethral injury, can vary significantly. Urethral rupture can lead to strictures, which occur in about 10%–15% of cases involving urethral injury and may necessitate additional surgical intervention. Urethral injury also increases the risk of ED to 20%–30%, compared to approximately 10% in cases without urethral involvement. 5 Additionally, complications such as infections, hematomas, and wound dehiscence are more frequent in patients with associated urethral injuries, with reported rates of 15%–25% for these issues. Urethral rupture, which occurred in 13.8% of cases, was associated with more postoperative complications in Ibrahim and al. study. 8

Age also significantly impacts long‐term outcomes. Older patients, particularly those over 40 years of age, are more likely to experience postoperative complications and have a higher incidence of ED. This correlation has been shown by Ibrahim et al., who evaluated long‐term outcomes of penile fracture repairs and found that age at presentation was the most significant predictor of postoperative complications and ED. This underscores the importance of considering age when predicting prognosis and managing patient expectations. Overall, the presence of urethral injury complicates recovery and increases the risk of both functional and aesthetic issues, emphasizing the importance of early and comprehensive intervention. 8

Conclusion

In summary, the case with combined penile fracture and urethral injury highlights the complex nature of urological traumas resulting from genital blunt trauma. Prompt diagnosis, precise surgical intervention, and postoperative care are essential for successful outcomes in these rare and challenging cases. This report underscores the importance of understanding and managing such injuries with diligence to ensure the best possible results for patients.
